## 14. The Court of Bells

The debt-broker's mask was the best lie I'd worn in twenty years, and I resented how well it fit.

Maren had made it from river-clay and a dead man's manners — she called it a *seeming*, and it sat over my face like a second skin that smelled faintly of grave-dirt and lavender. In the borderlands beyond my gate, where the rivers sang songs people used to know and the forests grew upside-down out of the sky, the seeming held. It would hold in the Court itself, she promised, so long as I did three things: spoke little, bowed often, and never gave anyone my name.

That last one was easy. I hadn't given anyone my name in two decades. I'd traded it away like a fool at a market stall, and the man who took it kept it in a ledger.

Vess.

I adjusted the broker's sash — Oskit's contribution, stolen off a corpse he swore had owed him money — and looked up at what the Undercourt called a door.

It was a mouth. That's not poetry. The entrance to the Court of Bells was an actual mouth, carved into the side of a hill that was itself carved into the underside of Vellharrow's mountain, lips of black stone parted around a throat of descending stairs. Bronze teeth. A tongue of polished obsidian worn smooth by centuries of feet. Somewhere deep inside, something breathed.

"Charming," Maren had said when we'd scouted it. "Most courts make do with gates."

"Most courts don't eat their guests," Oskit had said, which was his way of being encouraging.

Wren had said nothing at all. She'd just looked at the mouth for a long time, her hand pressed flat against her own chest, against the hollow where her soul used to hum before someone sold it out from under her. Eleven days ago she'd been an orphan stealing bread in Ashmarket. Now she was our map, because the First Tongue lived in her the way it no longer lived in me, and because the debt written on her soul pointed home to this place like a compass needle made of grief.

I'd left her with Sera and Maren two miles back, in a hollow of inverted trees whose roots clawed at the sky. She hadn't wanted to stay. She'd argued, actually — argued in the First Tongue, which she did now without noticing, the old words spilling out of her like water finding cracks — and I'd told her the truth, or most of it: that a child walking into the Court of Bells wasn't a rescue, it was a delivery.

What I hadn't told her was that I was more afraid of what waited inside than I'd been of anything in twenty years, Ministry inspections included. And I include the inspection where Inspector Quillon found my cellar wards singing.

*Little lies,* I thought, straightening my sash. *Small mercies. That's the whole business.*

I walked into the mouth.

* * *

The Court of Bells is a cathedral, if a cathedral were built by something that loved music the way a spider loves silk.

The nave went up forever — or down forever; direction gets philosophical down there — and from its unseen heights hung ten thousand bells. Great bronze monsters big enough to drown a house, and hand-bells no bigger than a thumbnail, and everything between, suspended on cords of woven sound that shimmered when you looked at them too long. They didn't ring. That was the horror of it. Ten thousand bells, silent as a held breath, all waiting.

Between them, on galleries of white bone-stone, moved the nobility.

I'll give the fae this: they dress beautifully. Robes the color of dusk and rust and deep water, faces like portraits left out in weather that doesn't exist, eyes that catch light and keep it. They drifted along the galleries in slow processions, pausing at alcoves where servants — bell-servants, their skins tattooed with musical notation — presented them with things on cushions.

Souls. They were presenting souls.

I saw one clearly as I passed beneath: a small blue flame cupped in glass, turning slowly, and inside the flame — I swear this — a suggestion of a face. An old woman's face, maybe. Or a young one. Hard to tell. The noble considering it tilted her head the way you'd tilt a bottle of wine to read the vintage, and the flame flared brighter, eager, and something in my chest turned over and died a little.

*Vintage.* That was exactly the word. These were cellars, and souls were wine, and somewhere below us all was the archive where the best bottles were kept.

"Broker." A bell-servant materialized at my elbow — they did that, appeared rather than arrived, as though walking were a courtesy they'd outgrown. Her notation tattoos crawled when she spoke. "Your appointment?"

"I have none," I said, in careful Undercourt-trade-speech, the pidgin of names and numbers Maren had drilled into me. "A broker of my standing requires no appointment. I require an introduction."

Her expression didn't change, but the tattoos did, rippling toward her throat. Amusement, maybe. Or appetite. "And your standing is?"

I produced the token Oskit had forged — a sliver of bone stamped with a sigil belonging to a debt-house that had, according to Oskit, been defunct for sixty years and unlikely to complain. "House Meridian," I said. "We buy distressed paper. Debts others have given up on."

"Distressed paper," the servant repeated, tasting the words. Around us, the procession of nobles flowed on, indifferent. One of them laughed at something, a sound like a spoon dropped down a well. "There is much distress here lately, broker. Much paper coming due." Her eyes — flat, silver, pupil-less as coins — slid over my seeming-face and moved on. "The Turning approaches. Everyone wants their accounts settled."

"So I've heard. Which is why I've come early. The wise broker inspects the cellar before the harvest."

That pleased her. I could tell because every tattoo on her body shivered at once, a ripple running from her scalp to her wrists, and she smiled with too many teeth arranged too neatly. "Follow. I will take you to the tasting galleries. If House Meridian buys distressed paper, there is a great deal you will want to see."

*I'm sure there is,* I didn't say. I bowed instead. Bowed often. Spoke little. Two of three rules observed already, and we hadn't even reached the wine.

* * *

They showed me the vintages.

I want to be honest about what that means, because honesty is the one luxury I can still afford: they showed me shelves of people. Alcove after alcove along the tasting galleries, each one lit by a lamp that burned without heat, and in each lamp a flame — blue, green, gold, and once, horribly, a pink so pale it was almost nothing. Souls sorted by quality, by age, by flavor profile. Servants moved among them with long silver tongs, lifting lamps, holding flames up to the light while nobles leaned close and inhaled.

One noble — a fat, soft-featured creature wearing a robe of living moths — held a golden flame near his mouth and breathed it in without drinking, just breathing, and the flame dimmed, and from somewhere far away came a sound I chose to believe was wind.

"That one is nearly spent anyway," the servant said, following my gaze. "Old harvest. The new stock is better. Younger. The city above grows careless, and careless cities are generous."

"The city above," I said, keeping my voice bored, "is Vellharrow."

"It is whatever we decide it is, after the Turning." She said it the way you'd say *the sky is blue*, and then seemed to hear herself, and her tattoos went very still. "But you are not here for politics, broker. You are here for paper. Come. The archive handles acquisitions."

The archive.

My heart, which I had been managing with professional calm for hours, decided to become a problem. I followed her down a spiral stair of black stone, past bells that grew smaller and denser as we descended, until the walls themselves seemed made of them — thousands of tiny bells mortared into the stone like fossils, all silent, all watching, if bells can watch, and down there I believed they could.

The archive door was iron, and on it was a lock shaped like an open mouth, and standing beside it, consulting a ledger taller than himself, was a clerk with a face like folded paper.

"My colleague from House Meridian," the servant announced. "Distressed paper. He wishes to view the holdings."

The clerk unfolded his face into something like a frown. "Holdings are catalogued by debt-number. Does the house have a number?"

"I have a name," I said. "A debtor's name. Wren. Of Ashmarket. Recently acquired — the paper is fresh, and my house wishes to verify the asset before the Turning settles all accounts."

Paper-face stared at me. Somewhere above, distantly, a single bell rang — one clear note that fell through the cathedral like a stone through water, and every noble on every gallery stopped moving until it faded.

Then the clerk turned to his ledger, licked a finger that left no moisture behind, and began to turn pages.

I had done it. I was inside. The plan — Sera's plan, refined by Maren, tolerated by Oskit, blessed by nobody — was working. Find the asset. Confirm it exists. Get out. Come back at the Turning with the whole company and a priestess who remembered rites older than the Court's manners.

Simple. Clean. Doomed, as it turned out, but I didn't know that yet.

"Wren," the clerk murmured. "Ashmarket. Fresh acquisition... yes. Lot forty-one, consigned by the Archivist's office, payable at the Turning." He looked up. "You wish to inspect the asset?"

"If permitted."

"Assets in the Archivist's private collection are not generally—" He paused. His paper face creased, and smoothed, and creased again, like a letter being read twice. "Actually. The Archivist has been expecting a broker."

The words went through me like cold water through a cracked wall.

"Has he," I said.

"For some days. He said a buyer would come sniffing at lot forty-one before the Turning, and that when he did, I was to admit him." The clerk closed the ledger. "He was very specific about the sniffing."

Every instinct I owned — twenty years of instincts, honed by Ministry inspections and quiet cellars and the discipline of never, ever being interesting — began screaming at once. This was wrong. This was a trap with my name on the invitation, except I didn't have a name, which meant the trap had been built for someone who knew exactly who I was under the clay.

"He expects me," I said slowly, "and yet I am not expected to introduce myself?"

"The Archivist needs no introductions." Paper-face unlocked the mouth-shaped lock, and the iron door swung inward on darkness and the smell of old paper and older cold. "Go down. He will find you. He always finds people. It's rather his whole profession."

I stood at the top of those stairs for exactly as long as pride allowed — which is to say, barely at all — and then I walked down into the archive of the man who owned my name.

* * *

The archive was not what I expected, and I had expected bad things, so being surprised was itself unpleasant.

No racks of flames. No cells. Instead: books. Endless books, shelved into a darkness the lamps refused to fully penetrate, spines labeled in the First Tongue and in scripts I didn't recognize and in some cases in no script at all, just impressions, like the books had been titled by pressure alone. The air hummed. Not with bells — with reading. With the particular silence of ten thousand volumes being attended to by no one.

And in the center of it, at a desk of black wood, sat a figure I knew.

Twenty years is a long time. Long enough to forget a voice, almost. Long enough to rebuild yourself around a hole and call the shape of the hole a life. But I knew the way Vess sat — spine curved like a question mark, hands folded on the desk as though the desk owed him money — and I knew the lamplight catching the edge of his spectacles, which he wore despite having no need of eyes, purely, I'd always suspected, for effect.

"Isandro Winterbourne," he said, without looking up.

The seeming didn't matter. The silence I'd kept didn't matter. Twenty years of gray, careful, charm-selling nothing didn't matter. He said my name — my true name, the one I'd paid him, the one that had lived in his ledger since the night I'd knelt at this same gate and begged — and it fit me like a key fits a lock, and every ward Maren had woven into my disguise came apart like wet paper.

I felt it go. Felt the clay-face slide off me like a mask in a wind, felt myself become visible, plain, *known*. A middle-aged man in a borrowed sash, standing in a dead man's shoes at the bottom of the world.

"You knew," I said.

"I always know." Vess looked up at last, and his eyes behind the spectacles were the same as they'd been twenty years ago: patient, dry, terribly kind in the way of a man about to explain arithmetic to a child who's about to lose everything. "You think I harvested from a thin spot in the veil for two decades without learning the taste of the man who made it thin? Every soul that came through, Isandro, carried a little residue of the bargain. A little *you*. You seasoned my collection." He smiled. "I should thank you. Instead I'm going to offer you a trade, because I'm sentimental, and because you're finally here, and because the Bell is nearly ready."

"I'm not trading anything."

"No?" He opened the ledger on his desk — the ledger, the one with my name in it, pages soft as cloth from handling — and turned it toward me. "Then let me show you what you're refusing. But first." He reached beneath the desk and lifted something into the lamplight.

A lamp. Small, plain, unremarkable — except for what burned inside it.

A silver flame, small and quick and bright, turning, turning. And in the heart of it, unmistakable now that I knew to look: a face. Narrow. Stubborn. About eleven years old, with mud still ghosting the edges of it, the way memory keeps what matters.

Wren.

Something in me that had been clenched for eleven days finally broke open, and what came out wasn't relief. It was rage, clean and total, the first pure thing I'd felt in twenty years.

"She's a child," I said.

"She's an *asset*," Vess said gently. "She's lot forty-one, consigned lawfully, payable at the Turning, and in four days she becomes a note in the Great Bell's chorus, along with several hundred of her fellow notes. Unless." He folded his hands again. The lamplight made his spectacles glow like two small moons. "Unless her account is settled by other means. You see, Isandro, the Bell cannot ring without a keystone. A name freely given, bound at the founding, the linchpin of the whole working. And I have spent twenty years wondering when its owner would come down my stairs to fetch it back."

He tapped the ledger. Once. Twice.

"Your name for the girl's freedom. That is the trade. Refuse, and I ring the Bell with her voice in the chorus and your city underneath it. Accept, and she walks out of here whole, and you—" he smiled, and it was almost tender, "—you become exactly what you've been pretending to be all these years. A man with no name, selling small mercies. Only this time, honestly."

The archive hummed around us. Ten thousand books. Ten thousand debts. Somewhere far above, the first bell of the evening vigil began to toll, and the sound fell through the dark like a verdict.

And I opened my mouth to answer — God help me, I genuinely did not know which word was coming —

— when every lamp in the archive flared at once, silver and gold and dying pink, and from the stair behind me came footsteps, and a voice I knew even better than Vess's, sharp with panic and Ashmarket vowels:

"*Isandro!* They've got Oskit — and Wren followed us down —"

Sera Quillon stood at the bottom of the archive stairs, Ministry coat torn, one eye swelling shut, holding a lantern with nothing burning in it.

Behind her, in the dark, something small was humming the First Tongue.

Vess closed the ledger with a sound like a kiss, and said, delighted:

"Well. The whole account arrives at once. How *efficient*."
