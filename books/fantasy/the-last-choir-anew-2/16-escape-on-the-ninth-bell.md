## 16. Escape on the Ninth Bell

Here is a thing nobody tells you about carrying your own soul in your hands: it's heavier than you'd think. Not in the way a stone is heavy. In the way a secret is heavy. In the way a name you've only just learned is heavy.

The lantern was made of glass and brass and one of Maren's old rites, and inside it my soul burned like a coin of silver fire — small, steady, mine. I could feel it through the glass. A hum. The same bell-note that had lived hollow in my chest since the night I signed that stupid paper for a loaf of bread and a lie, except now it wasn't hollow. Now it had something to ring against.

"Stop staring at it," Isandro murmured. "Stare at the door."

"I'm allowed to stare at it. It's mine."

"You're allowed to stare at it *after* we're not standing in the middle of the Court of Bells." He said it without moving his lips much, which was a skill he had, along with looking harmless and lying badly and knowing things about doors that no man should know. "Eyes forward. You're a debt-broker's clerk. Clerks don't gawk."

"Clerks don't exist," I whispered back. "Clerks are a story you invented an hour ago."

"Then be a well-behaved fiction."

We were standing in the antechamber of the Court of Bells, which is not a room so much as a held breath. Everything there is white stone and hanging chains, and every chain holds a bell, and every bell is listening. That's the part they don't put in the songs. The bells listen. They lean toward sound the way flowers lean toward light, and when you speak in the Court, you can feel them taking notes.

Somewhere ahead of us, past the archive doors, Vess the archivist was doing whatever archivists do — alphabetizing souls, probably. Cross-referencing griefs. And somewhere behind us, Oskit was supposed to be meeting us at the servants' stair after getting himself out of the holding cells where we'd found him, thin as a winter branch but grinning like he'd won something, which, knowing Oskit, he probably had. He always said you couldn't cheat a fae out of anything worth having unless you first let them think they'd cheated you.

And Sera Quillon was at the far door, the one we'd come in by, wearing her Ministry gray under a broker's cloak, counting guards with her eyes the way she counted everything: precisely, and as if their existence personally offended her.

"The ninth bell rings in four minutes," she said. "When it does, every soul in this Court will be required to attend the pealing. Even the dead ones. Especially the dead ones. That's our window."

"And if the window shuts on us?" Oskit had appeared at last, oozing out of a side passage with the smug look of a man who has recently stolen something small and excellent. His wrists still bore the marks of the cell-irons. He rubbed them like souvenirs.

"Then we find out what bells taste like," Sera said. "Wren."

I looked up. She crouched to my level, which she did more often than she probably meant to, and her eyes were doing the thing they did — sharp and gray and hiding something soft behind a door of their own.

"The locks," she said. "You're sure you can do it?"

"They're First Tongue," I said. "Everything down here is First Tongue, underneath. The Court just dresses it up in bells and ceremony because the truth embarrasses them." I'd figured that out over eleven days of listening — really listening — to the language that had come out of my mouth whole, the night I knocked on Isandro's door. It wasn't a language you learned. It was a language you remembered. And everything built below the mountain was built on remembering. "The locks are just words that agreed to hold. Words can change their minds."

"Can they?" Isandro asked quietly, and I knew he wasn't asking about locks.

* * *

The archive doors were taller than our shop, taller than three shops stacked, and they were made of a pale wood that I knew — everyone from Ashmarket would know it — was coffin-wood. Grown, not cut. Trees planted over graves until the roots learned the shape of sleep.

There were nine locks. Of course there were nine. This place never met a number it didn't want to make a ceremony of.

Each lock was a knot of script burned into the wood, and each script was First Tongue, old and formal, the way the gate under Isandro's cellar was old and formal. But where his gate spoke like a tired old man saying *no*, these doors spoke like clerks. *By order of. Entered into record. Let none remove.* Words that had been agreeing to hold for centuries and had never once been asked whether they wanted to.

I put my palm flat against the lowest lock.

It noticed me immediately. Cold spread up my arm like water finding its level, and a voice that wasn't a voice said, in the oldest grammar I'd heard yet: *Who unbinds?*

"Wren," I said. Just that. My name — the one I'd kept, the one they hadn't managed to file away. It felt strange and enormous in my mouth, like I was handing the door a candle.

*Wren,* the door repeated, tasting it. *The debtor. Your account is held within.*

"My account," I said, "is being withdrawn."

Silence. Somewhere above us, faint as a memory of a memory, the first bell began to gather itself. I felt it in my teeth. I felt it in the lantern, too — my soul leaning toward the sound like a child toward a window.

*Withdrawal requires,* said the door, and then it recited a list so long and so boring that even the fae who wrote it must have died of it eventually. Forms in triplicate. Sureties. The seal of a lord. The witness of two bells.

"No," I said.

The cold went very still.

"You heard me," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, because here's the other thing nobody tells you: when you're standing next to your own soul, you get brave in ways you don't recognize. It was like having someone older and calmer standing just behind my shoulder, someone who had already survived me. "No forms. No seals. No lords. I'm not asking the door to open. I'm asking the *words* to stop."

*Words do not stop.*

"Words are agreements," I said. "Maren told me. The old rites are all agreements — that's why they work, that's why they *ever* worked. Somebody agreed, once, and the world kept the promise." I pressed my hand harder against the burning script. "So I'm asking whoever agreed to hold this lock: you can put it down now. Nobody will think less of you. It's been a thousand years. You're allowed to rest."

And I sang it. Because First Tongue isn't spoken so much as sung, even when it comes out plain — there's a note under every word, and I'd finally stopped fighting it. I sang the word for *enough*. I sang the word for *done*. I sang the little falling phrase that means *you may go home now*, the one mothers use, the one Isandro had flinched at the first time he heard me say it, though he wouldn't tell me why for another week.

The lock sighed.

I swear on the lantern and everything in it, the lock *sighed* — a long exhale of cold air and old obedience — and the script dimmed from white fire to gray ash, and somewhere deep in the wood, a knot loosened that had been knotted since before Vellharrow had a name.

The second lock took longer. The third barely resisted; it had been waiting, I think, wanting an excuse. By the fifth, I was crying without noticing, the way you cry in cold wind, and by the seventh, Isandro had his hand on my shoulder, grounding me, murmuring the counter-chants Maren had drilled into him — he had a terrible accent, like a man singing in a language he'd only ever read — and it helped anyway. Agreement recognizes agreement, even badly pronounced.

The eighth lock fought. It was newer. Younger script, sharper edges, and under it I could feel a signature I already knew the shape of, because I'd seen it burned into a cellar door: *Vess.*

*This one is mine,* the lock said, in a voice like dry paper. *This one does not rest.*

"This one," I said, "is about to learn otherwise," and I did the thing Maren had forbidden, the thing Isandro had begged me to save, the thing I'd been carrying in my back pocket like a stolen knife: I called the ninth bell early.

Not rang it. *Invited* it. One note, pulled down through the stone, through the chains, through the listening dark — the smallest piece of the Great Bell's attention, borrowed for half a heartbeat. Every bell in the Court turned toward me at once. The eighth lock, being young and proud and made of the same stuff as everything else down here, could not help turning too.

And while it was turned — while it was *looking away from its post* — I sang it asleep.

* * *

The ninth bell rang while we were still inside the archive, and the world came apart politely.

That's the only way I can describe it. There was no crash. There was a moment when every chain in the Court drew taut, and every bell lifted its voice at once, and the sound didn't go through you so much as *rearrange* you — briefly, gently, the way a librarian reshelves a book. For the length of one peal, I understood everything: why the mountain was hollow, why the rivers up-country ran with old songs, why Isandro flinched at lullabies. Then the peal passed, and I was a girl again, on her knees in front of a shelf of small silver flames, and my hands were shaking, and the lantern was humming against my ribs like a cat.

"There," Isandro breathed. "Third shelf. Left of the ledger of debts unpaid. Do you see it?"

I saw it.

My soul was smaller than I'd imagined and brighter than I'd feared — a coin of silver fire no bigger than a hazelnut, sitting in a niche between a soldier's courage and somebody's grandmother's whole life, filed under a string of numbers. When I lifted it out, the niche didn't even resist. The Court was mid-peal; the whole archive's attention was elsewhere, stretched across nine bells and a thousand years of ceremony.

For exactly as long as it takes a bell to finish ringing, the Court of Bells did not notice us at all.

I opened the lantern. I tipped my soul in like pouring the last of the honey, and the flame settled onto its little wick of nothing, and the hum in my chest — the hollow one, the one I'd carried since Ashmarket — filled up so fast and so completely that I sat down hard on the archive floor and laughed and sobbed at the same time, which is a sound I recommend you never make in front of fae architecture, because the walls remember it forever.

"It's in," Isandro said. "Wren. It's in. Can you walk?"

"I can *fly*," I said, which was a lie, but a good one.

Oskit was at the doors, keeping watch with a stolen letter-opener and the confidence of a much better weapon. "Guards are at peal-devotions," he reported. "All of them. Even the ugly ones. Corridor's clear to the servants' stair. We go now, quietly, and in twenty minutes we're drinking something illegal in Vellharrow, and I'm telling this story wrong for the rest of my life."

"That's the plan?" I asked, hauling myself up.

"That's the *good* version of the plan," Oskit said. "There's also the version where everything goes wrong, but we don't discuss that one indoors."

We went.

* * *

The servants' stair was narrow and spiral and smelled of tallow and dust, and we were four turns down it when the bells stopped.

All of them. At once. Mid-peal.

You cannot imagine what that silence is like unless you have stood inside a cathedral of bells and heard them all choose, together, to shut their mouths. It wasn't quiet. Quiet is empty. This was *held*. The whole mountain leaned in around that silence like a fist closing.

And then a voice came down the stairwell after us, amplified by stone and ceremony and spite, and it was Vess's voice, dry paper and dry ink:

"*A withdrawal has been made from the archive. Unrecorded. Unwitnessed. Unpaid.*"

Oskit swore in three languages and one of them might have been dwarvish and all of them were heartfelt.

"*The debtors' stair is closed,*" the voice continued, almost gently, which was worse. "*Return the asset, and the matter may yet be entered into record as an error. Errors are forgiven. Theft is not.*"

We ran. I ran with the lantern held against my chest like a second heart, which by then it basically was, and Isandro ran beside me with a speed that twenty years of selling charms should have embarrassed out of him, and Oskit brought up the rear muttering about how he *knew* we shouldn't have discussed the bad version indoors.

The bottom of the stair opened onto the border gallery — the long white hall that ran toward the thin place, the seam in the world, the door-shaped nothing that Maren's rite had pinned open for us on the way in. I could see it from the top of the last flight: a vertical line of gray light, patient as a scar, with the smell of rain and chimney smoke coming through it. Home-smell. Coal smoke and fried onions and the particular damp of Vellharrow cellars. I have never in my life wanted anything the way I wanted that smell in that moment.

The line was closing.

Slowly — Maren was on the other side holding it, I could feel her rite straining like a rope in current — but closing, and between us and it, the gallery began to fill.

They came out of the walls, or near enough. Court guards, tall and gray-liveried, faces like unfinished portraits, and behind them the bell-servants, and behind *them* something bigger that I refused to look at directly because I suspected it would look back. They didn't rush. They didn't need to. They arranged themselves, the way you arrange furniture, the way you arrange a trap you've had a thousand years to practice.

"*The asset,*" said Vess's voice, everywhere now, from the bells themselves. "*Return the asset and pass. Keep it, and be kept.*"

Isandro stepped in front of me. Old habit, I'd learn later — the gatekeeper's reflex, body before door, door before self.

"We're leaving," he said, in his terrible accent, in First Tongue, loud enough for the whole gallery. "Freely. Which makes it not theft. Your own law: what is given freely cannot be stolen."

A pause. The whole Court considered this. I felt the bells turning it over, tasting it, checking it against ledgers older than mountains.

"*Given,*" the voice said at last, silk over gravel. "*Was it given, little thief? Did the debtor give herself her own soul back? Or did she take it, as her keeper before her took his freedom — as all your kind take, and call it liberty?*"

And that's when Sera moved.

She'd been at the rear the whole descent — rearguard, she'd called it, which I'd thought was modesty and understood, one breath too late, was arithmetic. She walked past us, past Oskit's grab and Isandro's shout, straight out into the middle of the gallery, and she did the single most Sera thing I have ever witnessed or ever will.

She showed them her Ministry seal.

"Inspector Sera Quillon," she said, clear and cold, in the trade-tongue, in *their* hall, "acting under sealed writ of the Kingdom of Vellharrow. This girl and this man are subjects of the Crown, currently engaged in licensed activity. Interfere with them, and you interfere with a treaty older than your Court. Touch them, and the surface world learns exactly what you harvest down here, and in what quantities, and from whose orphanages. I have the registers. I have the names. Try me."

It was a bluff. Most of it. Some of it. She'd falsified the registers herself, five nights ago, to get us down here — I'd watched her do it, hating every stroke of the pen like it was cutting her own hand. There was no writ. There was no license. There was a woman in a stolen cloak standing between a fae court and two people she'd known for eleven days, betting everything on the fact that bureaucracies fear exposure more than they fear defiance.

It worked.

Of course it worked. It was *beautiful*. The guards hesitated. The bells conferred. The big thing behind them actually recoiled, folding inward like a hand reconsidering. The gray line of the door flickered — wider, wider — and Isandro grabbed my wrist and Oskit grabbed my collar and we ran, all three of us, for the seam of home.

I was through. Shoulder, hip, the smell of coal smoke swallowing me whole —

— and I turned, because you turn, because some part of you always turns —

— and the door snapped shut on Sera Quillon's face.

Not on her scream. On her *stance*: feet planted, chin up, seal still raised, holding the line the way she'd held every line of her life, right up until the Court finished deciding that treaties were negotiable and inspectors were not.

The last thing that came through before the seam sealed was the sound of bells. A great many bells. Ringing something that was almost, but not quite, a laugh.

* * *

We landed in the cellar under the Shop of Small Mercies, among the dust sheets and the sealed door and the wards that flared gold at our arrival and dimmed again, satisfied. Maren collapsed against the wall, gray-faced, her rite spent. Oskit sat down on a crate and put his head in his hands. Isandro stood with his palm flat against the cellar door, pressing, as if he could push through it by stubbornness alone, back into a country that had already closed.

"Sera," I said.

Nobody answered. That was the answer.

"She stayed," I said. My voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone else's chest. "She stayed and they took her and we got out and she *stayed*—"

"Wren." Isandro turned. His face was the face of a man who has done this arithmetic before, twenty years ago, and gotten the same answer. "She bought us the door. The only thing we owe her now is to make it worth the price."

But I was looking at the lantern. At my soul, my silver coin, my freedom, burning safe and warm and *mine* behind the glass — and for the first time since I'd picked it up, it didn't feel like a victory. It felt like a bill. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in a cell of ringing silence, the woman who forged our passage was paying it.

And the lantern hummed — soft, low, and wrong somehow, off-key, like a bell that had heard bad news.

Later, much later, I would understand that hum. Souls know things. Mine had been in the archive; mine had heard what the Court intended for its new prisoner, and what the prisoner had already begun, even in chains, to plan.

But that night I only knew that we had won, and that winning tasted like ash and onions and a door that wouldn't open, and that eleven days of terror had taught me one true thing above all others:

The Court of Bells keeps accounts.

And so, starting that night, would we.
