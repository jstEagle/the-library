## 17. The Debt Comes Due

I have been arrested before. I have made arrests myself — hundreds, if the ledgers are honest, and I was always honest with my ledgers even when I wasn't with anyone else. So you would think I'd know what to expect from captivity.

What I did not expect was silence with a bell in it.

That's the only way I can describe the cell they put me in after the ninth bell's pealing tore me away from Isandro and the others and folded me up like a letter addressed to someone more important. The walls were white stone, seamless, warm as skin. No door that I could find, though one had clearly opened for whoever put me there. No window. No cot, no bucket, no courtesy of any kind. Just a room, and inside the room a sound so quiet I couldn't hear it so much as *contain* it — a single sustained note, like the moment after a bell is struck stretched out into forever, humming up through the floor and into my teeth and into the bones behind my eyes.

Ringing silence. The Undercourt's idea of hospitality.

I sat down cross-legged on the warm stone and did what I do best, which is inventory.

One Ministry officer, off the register, off the roster, and by now almost certainly off the rolls entirely. Sera Quillon, Inspector Third Class, who falsified the Turning-night registers to smuggle four criminals and a soul-lantern past her own colleagues. One coat, confiscated. One boot knife, confiscated. One hairpin I'd hidden in my collar seam, apparently not worth confiscating, which either meant their security was sloppy or it wanted me to have it, and in this place I'd learned not to bet on sloppy.

One plan, in progress, currently consisting of the word *survive* written very small at the top of an otherwise blank page.

I counted time by the note. It didn't change, but my body kept its own ledger anyway — thirst arrived first, then hunger, then the particular heaviness of eyelids that have been open too long against nothing. I don't know if it was six hours or two days. In the Undercourt I suspect the question is considered gauche.

When the door finally came, it didn't open. It simply stopped being a wall, the way a lie stops being convincing when you look at it long enough.

And there stood my host.

* * *

He was small. That was the first shock. Everything else about the Court of Bells had been built on the scale of cathedrals and ego — corridors tall enough to fly a kite in, nobility draped in robes that could have upholstered a house — but the creature in my doorway barely came up to my chest. He had a body like a man rendered in brass, joints visible, seams neat, with a face that was less a face than a suggestion of one, the way a bell has a suggestion of a mouth. Around his neck hung three small bells on a leather cord, each one wrapped in cloth.

A bell-lord. A minor one, if the title meant anything here, and I was already learning titles in this place were like coin — the smaller denominations got handled most.

"You are awake," he said. His voice was pleasant, mid-register, the kind of voice that reads proclamations. "Good. Unconscious prisoners are administratively inconvenient."

"Quillon," I said. "Inspector Third Class, Vellharrow Ministry of Order." Old habit. Give your name, your rank, assert jurisdiction. Then I heard how it sounded — asserting jurisdiction over a cell with no door — and something in me, some small exhausted honest thing, laughed.

The bell-lord tilted his suggestion of a head. "You find your situation amusing."

"I find it *legible*," I said. "Which is worse. Twenty years in the Ministry teaches you to recognize paperwork, and this—" I gestured at the ringing walls, the absent door, the whole elegant trap of it, "—this is paperwork. Someone filed me."

Something happened around his mouth, which I chose to interpret as the local equivalent of a wince. "You are not far wrong. You were taken under the provision of *materials useful to the Working*. Your classification is pending."

"Pending," I repeated. "For how long?"

"That depends," he said, "on what you are useful for."

And there it was. The opening. I'd spent twenty years reading people across interrogation tables — smugglers, forgers, one memorable necromancer who cried through the entire interview about his mother — and every one of them, guilty or innocent, wanted the same thing first. Not freedom. Not mercy. They wanted to be *seen accurately*. They wanted the record to reflect them as they understood themselves.

This creature had come to see me. Which meant I was not yet filed, and unfiled things can negotiate.

"Then let's establish the record," I said, and patted the warm stone beside me like we were two clerks sharing a bench. "Sit. Talk. My name is Sera Quillon, and unlike most of what comes through your Court, everything I say next will be true. I've found truth is faster. Lies require bookkeeping."

He sat. Carefully, folding himself down with the deliberation of a mechanism designed for standing. Up close he smelled like hot metal and, faintly, absurdly, of beeswax — polish, I realized. He maintained himself.

"You are the one who opened the registers," he said. "On the Turning night. We watched the city seal itself, and then we watched four shadows walk through the seam you left."

"We," I said. "The Court watches the Rite of Order?"

"The Court watches everything that touches the border." One brass finger tapped the stone, once, a soundless tap — or rather a tap whose sound went sideways, into the hum of the cell rather than the air. "Your Ministry builds a wall of law around Vellharrow every year, and every year the wall has the same shape, and every year the shape has gaps, because laws are drawn by people who need them to have gaps. We have watched your gaps for two hundred years."

Two hundred years. I thought of the archives back home, the tidy ledgers of the Order, all those confident seals and signatures. All those gaps, patiently mapped by things underneath the mountain.

"And tonight," I said slowly, working it forward, "you're watching *me*. Because I'm a gap that walks."

"I am watching you," he corrected, with a faint primness, "because I am the one assigned your file. I am Tolliver-Under-Nine, Keeper of Small Debts and Lesser Choruses. Do not be flattered; the assignment is considered dull. Prisoners of your type rarely sing."

"Sing," I said.

"When the Great Bell rings." He said it the way a tax clerk says *when assessments fall due* — flat, procedural, and underneath the flatness, something that was not procedure at all. Something tight. "At the Turning. All souls in the chorus sing. Including, if the archivists complete their work, yours."

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, assembled from fragments — from Maren's half-remembered rites, from Oskit's bitter dwarf-lore, from what Wren had pieced together in the Ashmarket before any of us believed her:

The Court of Bells intended, at the Turning of the Year, to ring the Great Bell with a chorus of harvested souls and rewrite the border between the worlds. Vellharrow would be the first payment. I had stayed behind on the ninth bell to hold a door shut with my body and a stolen writ, and I had known, holding it, that I was buying seconds and pricing them in myself.

What I did not know — what no one outside the Court knew — I began to get, piece by piece, from the small brass creature sitting cross-legged on the warm stone beside me, who talked.

They always talk. Give a lonely functionary an audience and eventually the file opens. I have built a career on this fact. I built it again now, sitting in a cell of ringing silence, asking small questions in a bored tone and letting him fill the silences, because Tolliver-Under-Nine had been Keeper of Small Debts for — I'd have guessed centuries — and nobody had ever asked him anything at all.

"The chorus," I said, somewhere in what might have been the second day. "The souls that ring the Great Bell. How many?"

"Nine hundred thousand and change," he said, with the pride of a man quoting figures he'd personally tallied. "Harvested across three centuries. Each one catalogued. Each one *bound*." He touched one of the cloth-wrapped bells at his throat, absently, the way a man touches a scar. "The binding is the work. Anyone can catch a soul. Keeping nine hundred thousand of them aligned, note against note, so that the Great Bell speaks instead of merely screaming — that is the achievement. The archivist's masterpiece."

"Vess," I said.

His head came around sharply. For the first time, the suggestion of a face became an expression, and the expression was fear. "You do not say that name lightly."

"I'm a government clerk," I said. "Names are the only thing I handle carefully."

We sat in the hum. And because I was watching — because watching is my trade the way lying is Isandro's and hoping is Wren's — I saw what his hand was doing. Still resting on the middle bell. Still touching it through the cloth. Not absently at all.

"Tolliver," I said, gently, the way you'd speak to a witness you didn't want spooked, "what's under the cloth?"

He didn't answer for a long time. The note in the walls seemed to lean in.

"They gave each of us keepers one soul to tend," he said at last. "Out of the chorus. To learn the craft of binding. Mine is called Anselm. He was a bell-founder, in the world above, three hundred years ago. He cast bells for the great cathedral in a city that no longer remembers its own name." A pause. "He is the third bell on my cord. When the Great Bell rings at the Turning, he will ring with it. Nine hundred thousand voices, one note, forever."

"And does he want to?"

The question landed on him like a dropped anvil. I watched it land. I watched him try to pick it up and find there was no handle.

"What does wanting—" he started, and stopped, and started again, smaller. "Souls in the archive are bound. Binding supersedes preference. That is the architecture."

"That's the *paperwork*," I said. "I asked about the soul."

"He sings beautifully," Tolliver said, which was not an answer, and was the most damning thing he could possibly have said, because I have heard that exact non-answer before. From Ministry officers describing the Rite of Order. From judges describing sentences. *It works beautifully.* Nobody says *it works beautifully* about a thing that is right.

* * *

Let me tell you what twenty years in the Ministry of Order teaches you, since I seem to be writing my memoirs on the walls of a fae prison.

It teaches you the law is a machine for converting conscience into procedure. It does not teach you this cynically; it teaches you practically. Every horror you will ever sign off on arrives pre-shredded into steps, each step reasonable, each form in triplicate, and by the time the horror is fully assembled it stands in front of you wearing your own signature, and the only honest response is to resign, and the only practical response is to initial page fourteen.

I had initialed page fourteen for twenty years. Falsifying the registers on Turning night was the first time I'd ever torn one out.

And here — in the white cell, in the hum, beside a brass creature guarding the soul of a bell-founder dead three centuries — here I finally understood what tearing the page out had cost and what it had bought. Because Tolliver-Under-Nine was initialed page fourteen. He was procedure given a face and a polishing cloth and a prisoner to process. He tended his bound soul and quoted his bound figures and did not ask what wanting meant, because asking was not in the architecture.

But he had touched that third bell every time he mentioned it. Procedure doesn't touch. Only grief touches.

"Tolliver," I said. "If Anselm could choose — truly choose, binding or no binding — would he sing for the Court?"

The silence went on so long I thought the cell had swallowed the question whole.

"He loved bells," Tolliver said finally. "Above. Before. He loved the making of them. He used to say — the archive keeps echoes, you know, residue, it leaks — he used to say a bell is the only instrument you play by hurting it, and therefore the only one that must never be played in anger." His brass fingers curled closed over the cloth-wrapped bell. "No. He would not sing for us. But the binding does not consult him. Nothing in the Court consults anyone. That is why the Court endures."

"That is why the Court *endures*," I agreed. "Now tell me why it falls."

* * *

I'll grant him this: he fought it. Not me — himself. I watched a minor functionary of the Undercourt spend what felt like hours wrestling with the shape of a sentence he had never permitted himself to think, while the cell hummed and I sat still as an auditor and waited, because waiting is also my trade.

"It is not a weakness," he said at last. "It is a design principle. Understand that. The founders wrote it into the Great Bell deliberately, because the first attempt — the first working, long ago, before the Court was a court — rang the Bell with *taken* souls, seized souls, and the Bell spoke nonsense. It cracked a mountain range. Sound made of unwillingness is noise, and noise cannot rewrite anything except itself. So they rebuilt the working on consent. The Bell must be rung by a chorus of willing souls. Willingness is the resonant frequency. It cannot be faked. Believe me, the Court has tried faking it for three hundred years, and the Bell knows, the way a lock knows a wax key."

"But," I said.

"But," Tolliver said, and his voice dropped until it nearly vanished into the hum, "willingness is a living thing, and living things change. A soul that consents on Tuesday may withhold on Thursday. The archive binds the souls' *presence*, but the ringing requires their ongoing yes — renewed at every peal, refreshed at the Turning, a chorus saying yes in unison, nine hundred thousand times over, sustained. The archivist calls this the maintenance problem. The maintenance problem is mine. Ours." His hand tightened on the bell. "Every keeper spends their existence keeping their soul willing. Comfort. Lullaby. Small mercies. We tell them the singing is an honor. Some of them, after centuries, believe it."

"And if one of them stops believing?"

"Then the chord fouls." The words came out of him fast now, a dam going, three centuries of classified maintenance manual pouring through a crack the width of one honest question. "One refusal among nine hundred thousand and the note bends. A handful and it breaks into dissonance. The Working fails. Not weakly — catastrophically. The Bell rings *false*, and a false ringing at the Turning would tear the border ragged, and the ragged edge would take the Court first, because the Court is closest, because the Court is *made of* the border—"

He stopped himself with an audible click, brass teeth meeting brass, and stared at me.

"You understand," he said, hollow, "that I have just committed treason."

"I understand," I said, "that you've just told me the truth, which in this building appears to amount to the same thing."

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only inheritance I have to give.

Tolliver-Under-Nine rose from the warm stone and unwrapped the third bell from his cord. Under the cloth it was small and dark and old, old iron, the kind of bell a founder makes last, the kind that outlives the cathedral. He held it in both hands like something that could go off.

"If the Working fails," he said, "Anselm is destroyed with everything else. Bound souls don't survive a false ringing. I have read the projections. I wrote some of the projections." His suggestion of a face turned toward me. "Unless."

"Unless," I said.

"Unless the souls are released before the Bell is rung. Freely. Willingness can be revoked — but it can also be *withdrawn in advance*, cleanly, if the binding is undone while the soul still consents to leaving. There are procedures. Of course there are procedures; I wrote several of them; they have never once been approved." He laughed, and the laugh was a terrible sound, like a coin dropping down a well. "Release requires the keeper's countersignature. Nine hundred thousand bindings. Nine hundred thousand keepers. Each one would have to choose."

"One is choosing right now," I said.

"Yes," said Tolliver-Under-Nine, Keeper of Small Debts and Lesser Choruses, traitor to the Court of Bells, and he pressed the little dark bell into my hands. It was warm. It was humming, very softly, the same note as the cell, and beneath the note — I swear this, and I have signed depositions under penalty — beneath the note there was a man. Tired. Patient. Three hundred years of patient.

"Anselm," Tolliver said to it, in the First Tongue, and I didn't know the language but the meaning arrived anyway, the way meaning does down here, sideways through the skin: *I am sorry. I am late. There is a woman above who tears pages out.*

The bell shivered in my palms.

Then Tolliver straightened, and the functionary came back over his face like a visor, and when he spoke his voice was pure proclamation again. "The prisoner Quillon attempted to bargain. She offered information regarding the surface world's defenses. I found her claims unverifiable. She remains uncooperative. This will be entered in the file."

"Will any of it be true?" I asked.

"Some of it," he said. "The best lies are mostly true. I believe your Ministry taught the Court that." And for one instant, under the visor, the suggestion of a mouth did the wincing thing again. "There is a debt-broker loose in the Court. Winterbourne. The archivist has him. If you value your strange gray friend, Quillon, you should know the archivist is not offering him a cell."

"No," I said. "She's offering him a name. His own. I've read that contract before. Everyone signs."

"Then pray he doesn't." The wall began to become a door. "And Quillon — the Turning is four days away. When the Court comes for its chorus, the chorus must be *ready to refuse*. All nine hundred thousand. Refusal spreads like fire or it doesn't spread at all. You have friends in the Ashmarket. Friends who carry lanterns." The light through the closing gap narrowed to a wire. "Tell them the Bell can only ring with a yes. And a yes—"

"—can be revoked," I finished.

The door became a wall. The hum resumed. I sat alone in the ringing silence holding a three-hundred-year-old bell-founder in my cupped hands, and I did the only sensible thing a woman of law can do when she has learned the rule that breaks all rules.

I broke protocol, and sang to it — badly, off-key, in no language at all — until it hummed along.

Four days. Somewhere above, Wren was carrying a lantern through the Ashmarket, and she had no idea yet that the war would be won not by force or theft or bargains, but by nine hundred thousand souls learning, at the last possible hour, the single most subversive word in any tongue:

*No.*
