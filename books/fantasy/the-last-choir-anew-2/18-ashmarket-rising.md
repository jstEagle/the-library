## 18. Ashmarket Rising

The lantern didn't weigh anything. That was the first strange thing about carrying your own soul around in a glass jar — you'd think it would drag at you, the way a debt does, but it was lighter than a loaf of bread. It was the lightest thing I'd ever carried, and I had to keep checking that it was still there, because my hands kept forgetting.

"You're doing the thing again," said Pip, walking backward in front of me through the Ashmarket muck so he could watch my face. "The looking-at-it-every-three-steps thing."

"I'm counting," I said.

"You've counted it eleven times since the tannery."

"Then I'm thorough."

He snorted and turned back around, which was lucky, because he walked into a fishmonger's stall and had to apologize to a woman who could have cracked walnuts on her forearms. The Ashmarket at dusk was its usual self: braziers smoking, knives arguing, children underfoot like leaves. Somewhere above us all, the cathedral bell began to ring the hour, and I felt it before I heard it — a hum in my chest, right where the hollow used to be, answering.

Except I wasn't hollow anymore. The lantern swung at my hip and the hum went *through* me instead of *into* me, the way a tuning fork goes through a table. Every bell in Vellharrow did it now. It had started three nights ago, after we came up out of Isandro's cellar smelling of river-songs and inverted forests, and it hadn't stopped. The city's bells were humming in their sleep. You could stand under the great bronze throat of Saint Verity's at midnight and hear it: not ringing, just *remembering*, like a dog dreaming of running.

The Court below was winding up for the Turning. Eleven days had become six. And everything made of metal and memory in this city could feel it.

"Stop," said Maren.

I stopped. She'd gone still by the mouth of Candlemaker's Row, her gray priestess's habit pinned up out of the mud, one hand raised like she was blessing traffic. She tilted her head. "Do you hear it?"

"The bells? Everyone hears the bells."

"Not the bells." Her jaw tightened. "The *choir*. Underneath. Listen properly — you have the ears for it now."

So I listened properly. I closed my eyes and let the First Tongue rise in me, that language that had come out of my mouth before I knew I knew it, and underneath the market noise and the bell-hum I heard what she meant. Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Thin as thread, coming up through the cobbles, through the well water, through the soles of my boots. Not singing. *Waiting.* The way a choir waits when the conductor has raised his hand and hasn't dropped it yet.

Souls. All the ones the Court had taken from this city for generations, quietly, one paper signature at a time. They weren't gone. They were being held in tune.

I opened my eyes. Maren was watching me with an expression I was starting to know — the look of a woman who had spent years telling herself the old rites were dead superstition and was now being proved catastrophically wrong on a weekly basis.

"Well?" she said.

"They're still there," I said. "All of them. And they're listening for something."

"For whom, more likely." She picked up her bundle. "Come on, then. If we're going to raise an army out of gutter-children and thieves, best do it before the bells finish waking up."

"That's exactly what I was going to say."

"No, it isn't. You were going to say something about bread first. You always do."

She was right. I bought bread.

* * *

You have to understand something about the Ashmarket orphans, and about me, because I was one of us until very recently and I remember exactly how we thought. We don't believe in kindness. Kindness is a hook with bait on it. A veiled merchant offers you bread and a warm room, and what you learn later is that the warm room is a ledger line and the bread was your own soul's purchase price. We believe in deals, in debts, in watching the exits. We believe that everyone wants something and the trick is finding out what before they take it.

So when I stood up on Oskit's fence-shop counter that night — his actual counter, which he complained about the whole time and then let me use anyway — with a lantern full of silver flame swinging from my fist, and told forty ragged children and a scattering of pickpockets, smugglers, and one extremely confused rat-catcher that I was going to lead them into a war against the fae under the mountain, I expected to be laughed out of the district.

What I got instead was silence, and then a small girl named Tansy, maybe seven, missing two teeth, who asked the only question that mattered:

"How much does it pay?"

And there it was. The Ashmarket catechism. I loved every single one of them in that moment, honestly, the way you love a mirror.

"It doesn't pay," I said. "It pays *back*. Everything they took. Everything they've been taking since before any of us were born." I lifted the lantern higher, and the silver flame leaned toward the crowd like it was curious, and forty pairs of eyes went wide as coins. "You've all signed things. Or someone signed for you. Papers you couldn't read, from people who were kind to you once. Am I wrong?"

Nobody laughed now. A boy near the back — Ferro, sixteen, already with the flat eyes of a man who'd been a courier for worse people than the fae — shifted his weight and looked at the door. But he didn't leave. That was the tell. In the Ashmarket, staying is the confession.

"My name got taken," I told them. "Not my name-name. The rest of me. The part that makes a reflection. The part that sings." I touched my chest. "There was a hole here where my soul should be, and I walked around with it for a month, and most of you have been walking around with holes too and calling it hunger, calling it cold, calling it bad luck. It wasn't bad luck. It was harvest. Somebody upstairs — downstairs, whatever — has been farming this city like a field, and the Turning of the Year is coming, and when it comes they mean to ring a bell so big it rewrites the border between their country and ours. With us as the payment."

Ferro spoke from the back. "And you're going to stop it. You. With a lamp."

"With a song, actually." I grinned, because I couldn't help it; the First Tongue was rising in my throat like carbonation. "But the lamp helps."

Maren stepped up beside me — she'd appointed herself my herald, or my handler, I hadn't decided which — and did the thing she did with old words, letting them fall slow and heavy like stones down a well. "The rites of choosing are older than the rites of debt," she said. "A soul given freely answers no summons but its own. If enough willing voices stand together at the Turning, the Great Bell cannot be rung against them. That is not faith. That is mechanics."

"She means it'll work," translated Pip.

"It will work *if*," said Maren, with the tone of a woman who had been defrocked largely for insisting on the ifs.

"If what?" Tansy demanded.

"If there are enough of us," I said. "If we're loud enough. And if we can get to the place where it matters before they get what they need to finish the job." I set the lantern down on the counter, dead center, and the silver flame stood up straight like a soldier. "Here's the deal. No papers. Nothing to sign. You walk away now, nobody follows, nobody remembers, and I'll buy the first round of chestnuts on the way out. Or you stay, and you learn a song in a language that was yours before anyone taught you to be afraid of it, and on the longest night we go make the loudest noise this city has ever heard."

Silence again. The lantern hummed. Somewhere far beneath our feet, so faint it might have been imagination, something answered — a thread of sound rising up through the foundations, hundreds of thin waiting voices, hearing us the way roots hear rain.

Tansy broke it, obviously. Tansy broke everything.

"What's the song called?"

I hadn't known until she asked, and then I did know, the way I knew the First Tongue — it arrived whole, like a memory of a place I'd never been. "*The Choosing,*" I said. "It's the oldest one. Older than the Court. Older than the debt. It's the song souls sing when nobody's making them."

Ferro came forward slowly, arms crossed, and looked into the lantern flame for a long moment. His reflection floated in the glass, faint and wavering — and beside it, for just a breath, I saw something else, a second shimmer, like a face behind a window.

"Huh," he said. "That's mine, isn't it."

"Yeah," I said gently. "They keep them close to the border. Close enough to call."

"Can I get it back?"

"That's rather the point of the entire enterprise," said Oskit from the doorway, limping in with a tray of chestnuts and the expression of a man whose shop had been annexed for revolution and who intended to invoice someone for it. "Eat. Sing. Try not to burn down my stock. In that order."

By midnight there were eighty of them in the yard behind the fence-shop, standing in the cold with their breath steaming, learning the first verse off Maren and me line by line. They were terrible at it. They were magnificent at it. Rat-catchers and cutpurses and children with chilblains, stumbling through a language older than kingdoms, and every wrong note they sang, the ground drank it up greedily and passed it down, and the hum under the city grew a little warmer. A little less like waiting. A little more like answering.

Pip found me afterward, sitting on the step with the lantern in my lap, watching the last stragglers wander home.

"You're crying," he said, with the tact of the very young.

"It's the smoke."

"There's no smoke."

"It's *metaphorical* smoke, Pip."

He sat down next to me and put his head briefly against my shoulder, the way you do when you're nine and hugs are still allowed but only if nobody mentions them. "My mum's in the ground," he said. "So it's not like they took her soul. But if the song works on living people too — like, if it keeps them from taking anyone else's —"

"Then it works on her behalf," I said. "Everything we do, we do on behalf of everyone they already got. That's how armies work. Mostly it's just grief with better organization."

He considered this. "Oskit says it's mostly complaining with better organization."

"Oskit is also correct."

We stayed there until the cold drove us in, and above us the cathedral bell tolled the second hour, and this time when the hum rose in my chest I could swear the tolling bent slightly toward it — toward us — like a flower toward a window.

* * *

On the fifth day before the Turning, Sera came back.

She arrived at Oskit's at dawn, out of uniform, which somehow made her more frightening, and she stood in the doorway with snow in her dark hair and said, without preamble: "The Ministry has sealed the registers for the Rite. Nobody in or out of the city from tomorrow nightfall. And Quillon's report — my report, the one I falsified — has been noticed. There's a man from the Provost's office asking questions about a charm-seller on Bellwether Lane, a defrocked priestess, and a missing child who is apparently no longer missing but is very conspicuously gathering crowds."

"So we're arrested by tomorrow night," said Ferro, who had appointed himself our pessimist-in-residence and took the duties seriously.

"We're *noticed* by tomorrow night," Sera corrected. "Arrest requires paperwork, and paperwork takes time, and I intend to keep losing it." She looked at me then, really looked, and her eyes went to the lantern at my belt and stayed there. Up close, in the gray morning light, I could see what the Court's cell had left on her: a faint tightness around her eyes, a new habit of touching her own throat as if checking that her voice was still hers. "May I?"

I held up the lantern. She studied the little silver flame for a long moment, and her face did something complicated — grief and fury and a kind of terrible relief, all fighting for the same square inch.

"It looks like my mother's locket," she said finally. "The way it moves. Like it knows you're looking."

"Do you want to—"

"No." Sharp, then softer. "No. What's done is done. Tell me about the song."

So I told her. All of us did — Maren with doctrine, Oskit with logistics and complaint, Ferro with numbers, Tansy with interruptions, me with the parts that only lived in the First Tongue. Sera listened the way she did everything: completely, silently, cataloguing. When I finished, she was quiet for a moment, and then she said the thing I hadn't let myself say out loud.

"The Court won't sit still for this. They know what's happening above them — the bells are humming, the ground is full of singing, their harvest is organizing. They have an archivist who's been planning this Turning for twenty years." She tapped the table. "They will not wait politely for the longest night. They'll come up early. They'll come for the gate, and the gatekeeper, and the name he's going to give them — and they'll come *before* we're ready, because that's what you do when your enemy is gaining strength. You strike while they're still terrible at it."

The yard went quiet. Even the hum under the cobbles seemed to lean in.

"And we're still terrible at it," Ferro pointed out. "We learned verse three yesterday. Tansy still thinks 'the hallowed deep' is 'the hallway sheep.'"

"It sounds the same!" Tansy protested.

"It does sound the same," I admitted.

Sera almost smiled. It was brief and crooked and gone, but it happened. "Then we have perhaps two days. Maybe three. Which means the plan changes. We don't wait for the Turning and march on the Court. We hold the gate. Whatever comes up — the host, the bell-lords, the archivist himself — they have to come through Isandro's door, and that door opens both ways, and Wren—" she looked at me, and her voice dropped into something that wasn't an inspector's voice at all, something older and more tired, "—Wren, they're going to demand him. His name. The linchpin. He'll give it, because he's decided to, and we both know it, and when he does—"

"When he does, we'll be there," I said. "All of us. Loud enough."

She held my gaze for a long moment, and then nodded once, the nod of an officer accepting a report she intends to disobey half of.

Outside, the sky over Vellharrow had gone the color of pewter, and the first flakes of the year's real snow were coming down. And across the whole city, all at once, every bell in every steeple began to hum — not the hour, not the quarter, nothing so orderly — a long low chord that rolled through the streets and rattled the windows and made the chestnut cart horses stamp and roll their eyes.

Maren stood up so fast she knocked over her chair. "That's not sympathy," she breathed. "That's not the Turning. That's a *summons*." Her eyes found mine, wide and certain and afraid. "Child, they're calling the chorus early. And they're calling it *up*."

Under the cobbles, faint and rising, the thousand waiting voices began — for the first time in a hundred years — to move.
