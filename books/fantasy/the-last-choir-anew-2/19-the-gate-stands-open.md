## 19. The Gate Stands Open

There is a particular quality to silence when it is about to end. I had lived with the cellar's quiet for twenty years — the hush of a door well-kept, the patient stillness of wards that hummed only for me. I knew its every texture. And so when that silence changed on the morning of the Turning — grew taut, like a drumhead before the stick falls — I was not surprised. I put down my tea, which had gone cold anyway, and went down the stairs to meet the end of my careful life.

Wren followed me. Of course she did. The girl had spent eleven days learning what her soul looked like in a lantern — a small silver flame, no bigger than a sparrow's heart — and she carried it now everywhere, cradled against her chest like something both precious and slightly dangerous, which is an accurate description of most things worth carrying.

"It's louder," she said.

It was. The gate beneath my cellar — old oak bound in iron, carved over with wards in the First Tongue, sealed by a bargain I had made when I was young enough to think a name was a small thing to spend — had begun to ring. Not sound, exactly. Resonance. The wood trembled at frequencies I felt in my teeth, in the fillings of them, in the scar tissue over my heart where Vess had once pressed his hand and said, *a pleasure doing business.*

"They're coming," I said. "Both courts."

"How do you know?"

"Because the gate only rings like this when it's being *summoned*." I set my palm against the oak. It was warm as a living thing. "And because I've spent twenty years learning every mood this door has. This one is new. This one is *expectant*."

Above us, muffled through floors of shop and street, the bells of Vellharrow were already humming. Not ringing — the Rite of Order forbade ringing until midnight — but humming, all of them, in sympathy with whatever vast instrument the Undercourt was tuning beneath the mountain. The whole city sounded like a wineglass waiting for a thumb.

Wren sat down on the bottom stair, lantern in her lap, and looked at me with the flat, unimpressed stare she deployed whenever I was being dramatic. She had learned it from Oskit, which told you everything about how our company had jelled.

"So we wait?" she asked.

"We prepare," I said. "Waiting is what you do when you don't have a plan. Preparing is what you do when your plan is terrible but you're going to pretend otherwise."

"That's the same thing."

"Welcome to adulthood."

* * *

They came at dusk, as I'd known they would. The fae are punctual about thresholds; dawn and dusk belong to them the way noon belongs to lawyers.

I stood in Ashmarket square with my ragged little company arranged around me — Maren in her defrocked vestments with chalk dust on her knuckles, Oskit limping but upright and complaining about it, Sera in her Ministry grays with her badge conspicuously left at home, and Wren at my right hand with her soul blazing in its lantern like a held breath.

The Court's host came up out of the ground the way frost comes up through cobblestones: everywhere at once, silent, inevitable. Bells first — servants of the Court whose bodies were half bronze, their throats clappers, walking in ranks and tolling softly with each step so that the very pavement rang beneath them. Then the nobility: tall figures in veils of woven sound, faces that kept almost resolving into something you could look at directly and then sliding away, like names on the tip of your tongue. At their center walked Vess.

He had not changed in twenty years. Archivists never do. He wore his gray robe and his ink-stained fingers and his expression of mild scholarly regret, as though he were here to collect an overdue book rather than an entire city.

"Isandro," he said, and my lost name wasn't in his mouth — he'd traded mine away too carefully for casual use — "you look well. Prison agrees with some people."

"I'm not in prison, Vess. I own a shop."

"A shop above a door." He smiled. He had always smiled like a footnote correcting you. "You were always better at keeping than at living. Shall we skip to the terms? You've had time to draft them, I'm sure. You were never spontaneous."

Behind him, the host waited. Behind me, my people waited. Between us, the air itself had begun to thin, the Turning pressing the two worlds together like pages in a closing book.

"You want my name," I said.

"The Court wants your name." Vess folded his hands. "Your true name is the linchpin of the Great Bell's working. Spoken into the bell at the Turning by a willing voice, it will let us rewrite the border between worlds. Your bargain, twenty years ago, made the gate thin. Your name will make it *ours*. Vellharrow becomes the first province of the Undercourt, and every soul in it — harvested, catalogued, rung — becomes part of the chorus. It is, I'll grant, ambitious. But then, ambition was always the Court's best note."

"And if I refuse?"

Vess gestured, gently, at everything. "Then we take the city the slow way. We have been taking souls from this city for generations, Isandro, one orphan at a time, one debtor at a time, and no one stopped us. We can continue indefinitely. The Turning merely offers us the express route." His eyes — if those were eyes — found Wren. Found her lantern. Something moved behind the mildness, and it was not kindness. "Though I notice the little debtor has recovered her property. That was not in the ledger."

"No," I agreed. "A lot of things tonight aren't going to be in the ledger."

Maren shifted beside me. Oskit's hand drifted toward the knife at his belt, which against this host would be roughly as useful as a spoon, but it was *his* spoon and he meant to bring it anyway. Sera stood very still, the way she did when she was memorizing everything for later testimony.

"Here are my terms," I said.

Vess inclined his head, indulgent. "Do go on."

"My name — freely given, willingly spoken, no coercion, no trickery, exactly as your Bell requires. In exchange: every soul your Court has ever taken from this city goes free. Every entry in your archive, every flame in every jar, every debtor's mark torn up. Wren's freedom isn't a term — hers was stolen and theft returns nothing to the thief. Her freedom is a *precondition*. The rest is the price."

A murmur went through the host — or rather, a *toll*, a low dissonance rolling through the bell-servants' ranks. Somewhere in the veiled nobility, several voices spoke at once, and the sound was like a library falling downstairs.

"You're asking," Vess said slowly, "for the dissolution of centuries of holdings. Do you understand what you're demanding? Souls are *capital*. They are collateral. They are—"

"They're people," said Wren.

She hadn't raised her voice. Eleven days ago she'd have hidden behind Oskit. Now she stepped forward, lantern high, and the small silver flame threw light across the front rank of the Court's host, and I watched bell-servants flinch from it — flinch, these creatures who had marched without fear — because somewhere inside each of them was a debt that had never been paid off, and her light was a reminder.

"They're *people*," she said again. "I know what it's like in there. I know what you do to them. And I know—" her voice caught, steadied, "—I know there are ones in your archive who've been waiting longer than I've been alive. Waiting for someone to come. Nobody came for them. So we're all here now, and he's offering, and you should be *ashamed* that someone has to offer."

Silence. Even the city bells overhead seemed to lean in.

"You've taught her to negotiate," Vess said to me, dryly. "How disappointing. She showed such promise as a debtor."

"She taught herself. I just gave her a language for it." I stepped forward until the ward-line at my back — Maren's work, hastily chalked that afternoon — hummed at my heels. "Well, Vess? You came for a bargain. Here is the only one on the table. My name, freely given, for the archive emptied and the gate sealed from both sides. Sealed *permanently*. No more thin spots. No more harvests. Your Court keeps its realm; we keep ours; and neither touches the other again while the mountain stands."

"And why would the Court agree to starve itself?" Vess asked. "Souls are our sustenance. Our music. Our—"

"Because the alternative," said Sera Quillon, stepping forward with a document in her hand, "is that the Great Bell doesn't ring at all."

She held the paper up. Even from ten feet away I recognized the format: a Ministry writ, heavy stock, official seal — forged, I happened to know, by her own hand three nights ago, beautifully.

"This," she said, "is a certified attestation, filed under the Rite of Order, declaring Isandro Winterbourne's true name *seized state property as of this evening*. Which means any use of it without Ministry sanction constitutes theft from the Crown. Your Bell requires a willing voice. A voice acting under compulsion of law is not willing. Argue with me in court — oh, wait. You can't enter courts. You can barely enter *doorways* anymore. That's rather the point of him." She nodded at me. "So. Either the archive opens tonight, or your linchpin rusts in a Ministry vault forever, and your Great Bell stays silent until the mountains wear down. Choose."

I had not known she was going to say that. I would later learn she'd drafted the forgery as a bluff, never expecting to use it, and had rehearsed the speech in a privy because her hands shook too much to do it anywhere visible. But standing there she was iron from the crown of her head down, and even Vess took a step back.

Which is when the second court arrived.

* * *

They came down the street from the north, where the Ministry's barricades had been — and the barricades were simply gone, unfolded like bad arguments, and the Ministry officers who had manned them stood aside in perfect, terrified discipline, because you cannot arrest what is larger than the law.

The Court Above — or what remained of it, the exiled gentry of the sunlit fae, the ones the old stories called the Fair Folk before the word got embarrassed — descended Ashmarket in procession. They were fewer, and older, and their beauty had the specific exhaustion of people who had lost a war long ago and never quite conceded it. At their head walked a woman crowned in hawthorn, and when she saw Vess her smile could have pared fruit.

"The archivist," she said. "Underfoot as always."

"Lady Rowan." Vess bowed precisely enough to insult her. "This is a matter of the Court of Bells."

"It is a matter of the *gate*." Hawthorn Lady's gaze swept past him, past the host, and settled on me with the weight of a century. "That door was sealed by a mortal, and mortals' bargains bind both courts, whether either of us likes it. If it is to be unsealed — or resealed — both realms must witness. So we have come to witness." Her eyes narrowed. "And to ensure the little brother below does not swallow the surface world out of spite. Continue, gatekeeper. You were making terms. Make them before *both* of us, and make them binding."

So there it was: the audience I had wanted and dreaded in equal measure. Both courts. Every harvested soul's fate hanging on words I hadn't finished drafting because I'd assumed I'd die before delivering them.

Wren's hand found mine. Small, cold, steady. "Say it," she whispered. "The way you say it in the shop when someone can't pay. You always find a way."

The way I said it in the shop. Twenty years of small mercies. All right, then. I turned to face both courts, and I spoke loudly enough to reach the back rows of the dead and the deathless alike.

"Here are the terms. One: my true name, given freely, willingly, spoken into your Great Bell or burned in your archive or wherever you keep such things — mine to give, no lien upon it, no trickery, witnessed by both courts. Two: the archive opens. Every soul taken from the world above — from Vellharrow, from Ashmarket, from every year your collectors walked — is released. Returned to their bodies where bodies remain. Freed to the turning of the world where they don't. Three: the gate is sealed from both sides, permanently, by the power of both courts jointly, so that no archivist, no collector, no lady of hawthorn, no clever mortal, ever thins it again. Four—" and here I paused, because this was the part I'd written in my head at four in the morning, staring at the ceiling, "—four: the freed souls owe nothing. No gratitude-debt, no memory-tithe, no bell-song extracted later as interest. Freedom means freedom, or the word is a lie and both your courts are built on it."

The hawthorn crown tilted. "And if we refuse?"

"Then I refuse *you*," I said, "and Inspector Quillon's writ stands, and your Bell is silent, and you may spend the next thousand years watching us live badly and freely through a crack in a door you cannot open. I've done it for twenty years. It's survivable. Ask anyone who's met me."

Someone in the Court of Bells' ranks laughed — an actual laugh, brief and rusty, from a bell-servant who immediately looked mortified, insofar as a throat of bronze can look anything. Vess shot the creature a glance that would curdle ledgers.

Lady Rowan turned to Vess. "Well, archivist? Your Bell, your linchpin, your choice. Or shall we stand here until the Turning passes and everyone's leverage melts?"

Vess looked at me for a long moment. Twenty years fell between us — the young fool I'd been, kneeling at this same gate, begging him to seal the door behind me; the ink-dry patience with which he'd explained what a name cost; the years of small thefts he'd committed through the crack I'd paid to close. I searched his face for the thing I feared most, which was triumph, and found instead something worse: recognition. He had known, all along, that it would come to this. Perhaps he had built it to. An archivist plans in centuries.

"The Court of Bells accepts," he said.

The toll that went through the host then was not joy. It was something older — relief, maybe, or grief, or the sound of a debt finally coming due that everyone had secretly hoped would be forgiven. The veiled nobility stirred. The hawthorn Lady raised one hand, and the air above Ashmarket split along a seam of gold, and both courts said the words of witnessing in languages that predated stone.

"Terms witnessed," said Lady Rowan.

"Terms entered," said Vess, and drew out the ledger — the actual book, black-bound, humming — and wrote as he spoke, and each word he wrote struck the cobbles like a dropped coin. "The name of the gatekeeper, freely given. The archive, opened. The gate, sealed both sides. The freed owing nothing."

"Then pay me," I said. "Give me back my name first. I want to know what I'm spending."

Something flickered across Vess's face — surprise, genuine, the first honest expression I'd seen on him in twenty years. "You want to *remember* it. Before."

"I've been nameless a long time, Vess. I'd like to meet the man I'm killing."

He opened the ledger to a page near the front, yellowed now, and read aloud — quietly, but in the First Tongue, which does not whisper — and the word he spoke slid into me like a key into a lock I'd forgotten my whole body contained.

And I remembered.

God help me, I remembered. A boy in a fishing village whose mother sang the First Tongue lullabies without knowing what they were. A young man who found the gate and the thing coming through it and made the only bargain available. A name that meant — it meant *the one who holds the door in winter*, and I felt tears on my face and didn't remember starting them, and behind me Wren made a small sound, and Oskit swore softly in Dwarvish, and Maren whispered, "Oh, you poor bastard, that's a *beautiful* name."

"There," said Vess, closing the ledger. "Now it's yours. Briefly." He extended his ink-stained hand. "Shall we?"

The bells of Vellharrow began, overhead, to ring the midnight hour.

* * *

The walk to the Great Bell took place underground, though it also took place sideways, and possibly inside a song. Both courts escorted me — the host of Bells on one side, the hawthorn retinue on the other, and my people behind me, because when Wren had started to follow, Vess had begun to object and Lady Rowan had said, in a voice like frost forming, "Witnesses, archivist. You agreed to witnesses," and that had been that.

The Great Bell hung in a cavern the size of weather. It was not bronze, or it was not only bronze; it was cast from everything that had ever been mourned in the world above, and it was cracked already, hairline, from the last time it had been rung — which, Maren breathed beside me, had been the day the border was first drawn, ages before cities, before kingdoms, before the word *fae* meant anything but *neighbor*.

Vess stood at the rope — there was a rope; of course there was a rope; bureaucracy abhors a gap — and turned to me.

"The name," he said. "Spoken willingly, please. The Bell knows the difference."

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands. I thought about the shop, the gray life, the twenty years of harmless trinkets and cold tea. I thought about a boy named for winter doors, and the woman who'd sung to him, and the fact that in a few minutes no one alive would remember that boy except the people I'd collected by accident — a child, a fence, a priestess, an inspector — who had somehow become, without my noticing, the reason any of it had been worth it.

"Wren," I said.

She looked up at me, lantern glowing, eyes wet and furious and brave.

"When this is done," I said, "someone has to water the plants in the window. Oskit will forget. He'll claim he didn't sign on for horticulture. Don't let him win the argument."

"He never wins the argument," she said, and her voice broke in the middle, and she laughed anyway, because that's the kind of person she was deciding to become, and I thought: *yes. That. Whatever my name buys, buy that.*

I faced the Bell. I filled my lungs with the cold of the deep places. And I spoke my name into the heart of the instrument that would unmake it —

—and that, as the first peal began to roll through the cavern and the archive doors below burst open and a thousand small silver flames rose singing into the dark, is a story for the next telling. Because the Bell did something none of us expected. The Bell answered.
