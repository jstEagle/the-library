## 20. The Last Choir

**Isandro**

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a bell rings. I don't mean the pause between the strike and the sound — that's just physics, air gathering its courage. I mean the silence a bell makes *on purpose*, the way it holds itself still, coiled, promising.

The Great Bell of the Undercourt had been holding that silence for twenty years. Waiting for me, as it turned out. Everything waits for me eventually; I'm very good at being waited for and terrible at arriving.

We stood in the Court of Bells' great nave — cathedral is too holy a word, though it had the architecture of one, all ribbed stone and impossible height — and above us hung the Bell itself, vast as a drowned ship, dark bronze shot through with veins of silver. And around its rim, suspended like moths in amber, hung the chorus.

Souls. Hundreds of them. Small flames, small lights, each one dimmed to a coal of what it had been. Wren's had been among them once. So, in a way, had mine.

"They can't ring it," Maren whispered beside me. She had ash on her face from the archive fire and looked, for the first time since I'd known her, entirely certain of something. "Not without willing voices. Sera was right. The Court needs consent."

"Then they'll manufacture consent," Oskit said. He was favoring his left side — the Hollow had held him three days, and it showed — but his hands were steady on his axe. "They always do. That's what contracts are *for*."

Below us, arrayed on the Court floor like guests at the world's worst wedding, stood the nobility of the Undercourt. And at their center, on a dais of black glass, stood Vess.

The archivist had not changed. That was the horror of it. Twenty years, and Vess wore the same gray robe, the same patient face, the same expression of a librarian who has found your overdue book and is *not angry*, merely disappointed, and would you care to discuss the fine structure?

"You came," Vess said, and the voice carried without effort, the way things carry down here where sound is currency. "I confess I hoped you would. It saves such tedious paperwork."

"I've been told I'm sentimental," I said.

Vess smiled. "You've been told many things, Isandro Winterbourne." And there it was. My name. My true name, spoken aloud in a room full of bells, and every soul-flame in the chorus flickered toward it like candles leaning into wind. "Shall we conclude our business? The Turning is upon us. The veil is thin. All I require is the name freely given, and the Bell will ring, and the border will be rewritten, and no one need be hurt who isn't already owed."

"And the souls?"

"Freed. As bargained." Vess gestured, and the chorus shivered in its amber. "Every one. Even the girl's. You have my word."

"The word of an archivist," Oskit muttered. "That'll hold up in court. This *is* the court. Still won't hold up."

I looked at the Bell. I looked at the little lights. Somewhere in that constellation was a girl from Ashmarket who had signed her name for bread, and somewhere behind my ribs was a hollow exactly the shape of a word I hadn't heard in twenty years, and both of those facts were about to become the same fact.

"Yes," I said. "Freely given."

And that is when Wren started to sing.

* * *

**Wren**

Here's what nobody tells you about the First Tongue: it isn't a language you learn. It's a language you *remember*. The first time I spoke it, in Isandro's shop, mud on my boots and no reflection in his mirror, it didn't feel like words coming out of me. It felt like words coming *back*.

So when I saw the chorus hanging in the Bell — all those little fires, all those people-shaped silences — I didn't think. Thinking is for people with plans. I opened my mouth and I sang the oldest song I knew, which is to say the oldest song there is, the one the gate hums when nobody's listening, the one the rivers of remembered songs were all trying to be.

It has no name in your tongue. In mine it's just — *yes*. That's the closest I can get. It's the song of choosing. Not *I owe*, not *I am bound*, not *take me, I signed*. Just: *yes, this, I choose this, I am here on purpose.*

The nearest flame flared.

Then the next one. And the next. Like dawn going along a street, window to window.

"No," said Vess, and for the first time in possibly centuries, the archivist sounded young. "The contracts—"

"We revoke them," I said, in the First Tongue, and the whole nave heard it, because down here everything true is loud. "All of them. We *choose* otherwise."

The amber around the flames began to crack.

* * *

**Sera**

I want it noted, for whatever record survives this night, that I did not cry. My eyes watered, yes, due to smoke from the burning archive, which I definitely did not set fire to with a stolen taper while two bell-servants were distracted by a dwarven fence shouting about invoices. The watering was environmental.

The registers of the Court of Bells were burning. Every contract, every debt, every soul-ledger Vess had kept for generations — Maren had walked into the archive an hour ago carrying the old rites like a lantern, and what she'd done in there, I only saw the results of: a room full of paper learning what fire knows.

Now the freed souls were singing.

I have attended the Rite of Order every year of my adult life. I have stood in Vellharrow's square in the cold and listened to the Ministry's bells and felt nothing, or told myself I felt nothing, which is the same discipline wearing different clothes. But this —

This was what the Rite was a forgery of.

The chorus came loose from the Bell in a slow rain of light, hundreds of them, drifting down through the nave, and where they passed, the nobility shrank back against the walls. Fae fear freedom. It took me until that moment to understand it: they are creatures of contract, and a contract requires two parties, and a soul that chooses cannot be owned. Every freed flame was a law they couldn't survive.

Wren stood at the center of it, small and mud-streaked and blazing, singing in a language the Ministry had outlawed before either of us was born, and I thought: *they were right to outlaw it.* Not because it's dangerous.

Because it's contagious.

Isandro caught my eye across the nave. He was walking toward the dais, toward Vess, and he looked — I've arrested murderers, smugglers, one memorable man who stole a bridge — and I know what guilt looks like, and what resolve looks like, and what terror looks like. He looked like all three at once, arranged into peace.

"Sera," he said, passing me. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. The shop. There's a false bottom in the counter. Oskit knows."

"Nothing's going wrong."

He almost smiled. "You falsified government registers to get us here. You set fire to an immortal archive. You're the most dangerous person in this room, Inspector Quillon, and I include the archivist."

"Flatterer," I said, and turned back to watch him walk toward the thing he'd spent twenty years hiding from, and I will admit here, in the privacy of my own head, that my eyes watered again. Environmentally.

* * *

**Oskit**

Right. So. For the record, since apparently everyone gets a turn narrating tonight: I never wanted to come underground, I never wanted to fight fae, and I *definitely* never wanted to be moved by anything. I'm a fence. My entire professional philosophy is that everything has a price and nothing has meaning, and I have run that philosophy profitably for forty years.

And then a bunch of little lights started singing, and one of them drifted past me, close, close enough to warm my beard, and it paused. Hovered. Like it was looking at me.

And I swear on my mother's forge, which is the only oath I keep, it hummed a bar of a song my grandmother used to sing over the anvil. A song I sold. Down in the Market of Borrowed Names, I sold a grievance, and the grievance was tangled all through with that song, and I thought I'd gotten a fair price, and now here was the song, free, floating past my face like it had never belonged to anyone but me.

"Go on," I told it, gruff as I could manage, which wasn't very. "Get out of here. You're free. Don't make it weird."

It made it weird. It circled me twice and then rose up to join the others, and I stood there in a cathedral under a mountain with wet eyes and an axe I hadn't needed, thinking: *well, that's the philosophy ruined.*

Above us, the Great Bell began to ring anyway.

* * *

**Isandro**

Of course Vess rang it. Contracts were ash, the chorus was free, and Vess rang it regardless — desperation dressed as procedure, the last argument of every failing power. The Bell swung on chains thick as rivers, and the first toll rolled out over the nave, and it was wrong. I felt it in my teeth. A Bell rung unwilling sounds like a door kicked open: all impact, no music.

But the working needed more than one toll. It needed the chorus. It needed *me*.

"Isandro Winterbourne," Vess said again, louder, over the dying echo. "Your name is the linchpin. Give it, and the Bell rings true, and the border falls, and Vellharrow is ours by morning. Refuse, and I take it, and the Bell rings broken, and Vellharrow is ours by morning anyway. You cannot save them by being stubborn. Stubbornness is not a currency here."

"You're right," I said. "Stubbornness isn't currency."

I climbed the dais. Twenty years of careful gray life, and it ended with me climbing stairs in front of the entire Undercourt, feeling like a man walking into his own funeral and finding it surprisingly well-attended.

"But you've misunderstood the transaction," I went on, "which embarrasses me, frankly, because I taught you half of it. You think a name given freely is the same as a name taken. It isn't. A taken name is a chain. A given name is—" I looked up at the Bell, at the freed souls wheeling above it like starlings, at Wren, still singing, still *choosing*. "It's a gift. And gifts work differently."

"What are you doing?" Vess said.

"Closing the account."

I reached up and laid my hand on the Bell.

And I gave it. Not the way Vess wanted — not poured into the working, not spent on rewriting borders. I gave it the way you give anything truly: completely, and to everyone. My name. The one my mother used when I was small. The one I traded away beneath this mountain when I was young enough to think a sealed door was worth more than a self. *Isandro Winterbourne.* Gatekeeper. Charm-seller. Liar, mostly, and lately, badly. All of it, all at once, into the bronze.

The Bell rang.

Not the broken toll Vess had forced. Something else. The note went out through the nave and through the mountain and through the thin places of the world, and every bell in Vellharrow answered it — I knew they would; I'd heard them humming for days — and the answer came back changed, softened, *chosen*. Because the freed souls caught the note as it passed and carried it, and Wren's song was woven through it, and the song was made of yes.

The Great Bell cracked.

A single line, from crown to lip, clean as a signature. The sound it made was not a toll. It was closer to a sigh — enormous, relieved, final. The border-working died inside it unfinished. Above us, hairline silver spread through the bronze like frost, and pieces began, gently, to fall, and where they fell they became light, and the light rose, and joined the rest.

Vess stood very still on the black glass.

"You could have kept it," the archivist said quietly. "Restored. Whole. I offered."

"You offered a cage with my name on the door," I said. "I've lived in a locked room for twenty years. I'm done with the arrangement."

And then the archive-fire reached the last of the ledgers, and Vess's own name — the true one, the old one, the one written at the head of every contract ever signed in this court — burned off the final page, and I watched an immortal creature discover what it feels like to be unrecorded. Vess did not scream. Archivists never scream. Vess simply looked down at empty hands, and then at me, with something I was surprised to recognize.

Envy.

The nave filled with rising light. The Court of Bells, that cathedral of debts, emptied of everything it had hoarded, and the last thing I remember clearly is Wren's song changing key — from *yes* to something warmer, something that sounded, impossibly, like a kitchen, like lamplight, like a shop door left open on a cold night — and my name dissolving into it, spent at last, spent *well*, and reaching for the hollow behind my ribs and finding it

finding it

finding—

* * *

**Wren**

He forgot.

That's the part nobody warns you about, when someone gives everything. They don't get to keep the receipt. Isandro stood on the cracked dais with the light pouring off him and looked at us — at me, at Sera, at Oskit swearing softly, at Maren with her ash-grey face — and there was nothing in his eyes but polite confusion, the look of a man who has woken in a room and can't think why any of us would be in it.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

And I did the only thing that made sense. I took his hand, and I started to sing.

Because here's the secret of the First Tongue, the one Vess never understood, the one the whole Court was built on forgetting: names don't live in people. They live *between* people. In the telling. His name wasn't gone. It was in me — I'd heard it sung; we all had — and in Sera, and in Oskit's grumbling, and in every freed soul rising home through the mountain. He'd given it to all of us. He'd made himself into a gift, and gifts don't vanish.

They get passed on.

"Come on, then," I said, in the First Tongue, and then again in plain Vellharrow speech, because some things deserve saying twice. "Let me tell you a story. It's about a man with a shop full of small mercies and a door in his cellar..."

Behind us, the mountain was quiet for the first time in an age. Ahead of us, the long stair home. And Isandro Winterbourne — nameless, blinking, held upright by a child and a disgraced priestess and a fence and a woman who'd betrayed her Ministry for him — let us lead him toward the light at the top, asking, in a voice gone soft with wonder:

"A door in his cellar? What was behind it?"

"That," said Sera, "is the best part."
