## 21. A Nameless Man in a Loud City

The trouble with losing your name is that nobody warns you about the plumbing of it. You expect the big things to go — the memories, the face in the mirror, the twenty years of careful gray living. And they do go, mostly, like water down a drain. But it's the small things that take you by surprise. The way your hands knew how to pick a particular lock. The taste you had for burnt honey. The fact that when Maren said *sit down before you fall down*, some traitor muscle in my legs obeyed her without consulting me at all.

I woke on a pallet in my own back room — or what I was told was my own back room — with the smell of woodsmoke and feverfew and someone's terrible attempt at porridge, and I did not know the ceiling above me, and I did not know the woman dozing in the chair beside the bed, and I did not know the small figure curled at the foot of the pallet like a cat who'd decided I was furniture now.

I did know, in the place where my name used to live, that I was empty.

Not hollow. There's a difference, and I learned it slowly over the weeks that followed. Hollow is what the Hollow were — scooped out, rung dry, left as vessels for whatever the Court cared to pour in. Empty is simpler and stranger. Empty is a room swept clean, waiting to see what gets put back.

"Water," I said. My voice cracked like an old door.

Wren's head came up off the pallet-foot so fast she nearly fell off the bed. "He's up. He's *up*. Oskit! Maren! He said a word!"

"He said *water*," came Oskit's gravel voice from somewhere beyond the door. "That's not a miracle, girl, that's thirst. Even the nameless get thirsty. It's one of the few things we've all got in common."

Then there were too many people in too small a room, and all of them were looking at me the way you look at a fire you thought had gone out, and none of them, not one of them, told me who I was.

I noticed that later. It took me days to notice it, because at first I didn't have enough self left to be curious. But when I did notice, I asked Sera about it — Sera being the sharp-eyed woman from the chair, who wore her Ministry coat with the buttons torn off now, which I gathered was either a scandal or a statement, possibly both.

"You knew my name," I said. "All of you did. You could have just told me."

Sera looked at me for a long moment. She has a way of looking at people that makes them feel audited. "Would you want it secondhand?" she said. "Your name, handed to you like a receipt?"

"I'd want *something*."

"You'll get something," she said. "But not from a file. Vess kept you in a ledger for twenty years. The Court kept you in an archive. The Ministry keeps everyone in registers." She tore a piece of bread in half and gave me the bigger portion, which I somehow understood was a habit of hers, and a telling one. "We're not going to keep you anywhere. We're going to give you back."

I didn't understand what she meant yet. Give me back to whom? I was standing right there, more or less, holding my bread like a man holding a rope over a very long drop.

* * *

Here is what I learned about myself in the first week, from sources I could not verify and began, against all my instincts, to trust:

That I owned a shop called the Shop of Small Mercies, and sold charms that did nothing, and that this had been a kind of honesty, since magic was outlawed and honest nothing was safer than dishonest something.

That under the shop was a door, and behind the door was a stair, and at the bottom of the stair was a gate that I had sealed with a bargain that cost me everything except the ability to make bargains, which should tell you something about how those negotiations go.

That a child had come to my door speaking a dead language, and I had gone down the stair after her, and come back up without the thing that made me me.

"And you *knew* this man," I said to Wren, on the seventh evening, when the fire was low and the others had gone to their various corners — Oskit to his cards, Maren to her prayers, Sera to whatever it is ex-inspectors do instead of sleeping. "Before all this. You knocked on his door."

"You let me sleep by the stove," Wren said. She was braiding a frayed bit of lantern-cord, her soul-lantern habit, though the soul was long since back where it belonged; she carried the lantern anyway, the way soldiers carry old scars. "You pretended you had a rule about orphans eating last, but you always cooked extra, so really you ate last. You were bad at pretending. That's the thing about him." She glanced up, quick and fierce. "You're bad at pretending to be hard. Everything else you can fake. Not that."

"Was he clever?"

"Oh, he thought he was."

"Was he?"

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Clever enough to get us all out of the Undercourt alive. Stupid enough to trade his own name for mine without asking anybody first." She pulled the braid tight. "So. Clever and stupid. Same as anyone worth knowing."

I lay back and stared at the ceiling and tried to feel like the man she was describing. What I felt instead was like a house after the owners have moved out — the shape of the life still visible in the nail-holes and the faded patches where pictures hung, but nobody home, and no forwarding address.

Grief, I learned, does not require a memory of its object. I grieved a man I couldn't remember the way you grieve someone whose funeral you missed: secondhand, by report, surrounded by other people's tears that wouldn't quite explain themselves to you. Some nights it was unbearable, grieving a stranger. Some nights it was worse than that. Some nights it was *relief*, and then I hated myself for the relief, and Maren would find me sitting up in the dark and say, in her defrocked-priestess voice, the one that had survived the loss of every institution that might have backed it: "You're allowed to be glad to be light, even if you miss the weight. Both are true. They've always both been true."

* * *

The city, meanwhile, was loud.

Vellharrow is always loud — it's a port town with delusions of grandeur and the bells to prove it — but that winter it was loud in new ways. The Ministry was in disarray, which turned out to look like this: inspectors on every corner asking questions nobody answered, proclamations nailed up and torn down within a day, and a slow, confused argument about whether the events of the Turning had been a fae incursion, a natural phenomenon, or a clerical error. I heard three different theories through the shop window — yes, I reopened the shop, more on that in a moment, I'm getting ahead of myself — and my favorite was the clerk's theory, passed along by Oskit with enormous relish: that a rounding error in the Rite of Order's registers had briefly unmade the border between worlds, and that the appropriate response was a strongly worded memo.

"They'll never admit what happened," Sera said, one gray afternoon, watching the street from behind the curtain. "Admitting it means admitting the Court harvested souls out of Ashmarket for two hundred years while the Ministry filed the missing children under *runaways*. There are men above me — were men above me — who signed those filings. Their names are in ledgers too." Her mouth went thin. "Some ledgers deserve to burn. Mine nearly did."

"What happened to your Ministry?" I asked.

"I resigned." She said it the way you'd say *I survived*. "Or I will have, once they finish deciding whether to arrest me for falsifying the Turning-night registers. Quillon's fate is under review. Quillon may spend a year writing reports about it. Quillon finds this almost funny." Almost. With Sera, *almost* was load-bearing.

And beneath all the civic noise — under the arguments and the arrests and the memos — there was the other sound. Or rather, the absence of it. The mountain was quiet. Every bell in the city rang true again, with no hum underneath, no sympathetic shiver climbing up from below. People said they slept better without knowing why. Children said the cellars felt less like cellars. On the night of the winter's first snow, the cathedral bells rang for the Turning's official commemoration, and the sound went out clean over the rooftops like a held breath finally let go.

I stood in the yard behind the shop and listened, and I felt nothing where something used to be, and I found — this surprised me — that I could bear it. The gate was sealed from both sides. Whatever bargain I'd made, whatever name had been the linchpin of the Great Bell's working, it was spent, dissolved into a song, done. The door in the cellar was just a door now. I checked it most mornings anyway. Habit, maybe. Or hope. I couldn't tell you which, and neither, I think, could the door.

* * *

It was Maren who started the storytelling. I don't know if it was deliberate or if it grew out of her the way her rites grew out of her — organically, stubbornly, from material the institutions had thrown away.

She caught me one evening trying to shave with a razor I clearly didn't know how to hold, my chin bleeding in three places, and she took it from me with a sigh that contained whole libraries of patience. "Sit," she said. "I'll tell you about a man who shaved like a barbarian for twenty years and never once cut himself, because his hands remembered what his head had lost, and tonight his hands have decided to sulk."

"That's not a story," I said, pressing cloth to my chin. "That's an insult."

"It's the opening of a story. All good stories start with an insult." She sat across from me, folded her hands, and became — I don't know how else to describe it — *liturgical*. "Once there was a man in the city of Vellharrow who sold charms that did nothing, and was honest, and alone, and did not know he was lonely because loneliness was the mortar between every stone of his life and you cannot see the mortar from inside the wall."

My hands went still.

"He had a cellar," she went on softly, "and in the cellar a door, and behind the door a debt twenty years old, and every night he went down and checked the wards the way other men check on sleeping children. And every morning he came up and opened the shop and lied kindly to his customers, and the kindness was real and the lies were harmless and the years went by like water, until the night a knock came in the First Tongue—"

"—and he thought, *no*," I said. My voice came out strange. "He thought, *not again, not after everything, I paid, I paid enough.*"

Maren smiled, slow as sunrise. "There he is," she said.

I hadn't meant to say it. The words had simply arrived, the way words arrive when someone reads you a story you half-remember from childhood — not memory exactly, but memory's shadow, cast forward. Something in my chest that wasn't a name and wasn't a soul and wasn't anything the archivists had catalogued shifted in its sleep.

"Again," I said. "Tell it again."

And that was how it became a ritual. Every evening, after the shop shut and the fire was fed, someone told me a piece of myself, like a bedtime tale told backward — not to send me to sleep but to wake me up, gently, night after night, story by story.

Oskit's installments were the best and the least reliable. Oskit told the chapters with himself in them twice as often as statistically warranted, and in his versions his escape from the Court of Bells involved considerably more swordwork and considerably less hiding in a wine cask. "Aye, ye were a hard bargainer," he'd say, dealing cards onto the table between us. "Ye got me to join yer mad quest by threatening to pay me full price for information. *Full price.* No fence in Vellharrow survives that kind of psychological warfare." He squinted at me over his hand. "Ye used to cheat at cards, by the bye. Badly. On purpose, I think, so I'd win. Don't look touched about it, it's undignified."

Sera's tellings were precise, dated, cross-referenced — little depositions of my life. "On the ninth of Deepmonth you lied to me about the resonance in your cellar. You were terrible at it. Your left hand does this—" she demonstrated, a small twitch at the cuff "—when you lie. It's doing it right now, incidentally, and you haven't lied yet, so draw your own conclusions." She told me about the Turning, the falsified registers, the cell of ringing silence, the bell-lord's vulnerability. She told me the parts where she'd been afraid. She was the only one who did that, told the fear straight, no varnish, and I understood eventually that she did it as a gift: proof that fear could be survived and still spoken aloud.

And Wren — Wren told the beginning, and the end, and she told them best.

"The man in the shop didn't believe in much," she'd say, curled by the fire, the lantern on her knee. "He believed in locks, and in checking things twice, and in feeding orphans by accident so they wouldn't have to be grateful. He didn't believe he was good. He thought goodness was for other people, people with cleaner hands. And then a girl came to his door with a hole in her chest where her soul should have been, and he looked at her and saw — this is the important part, don't interrupt — he saw his own fault looking back at him. Because the reason she could be taken, the crack in the world she'd fallen through, was a crack *he* had made, twenty years before she was born. And a lesser man would have closed the shutter. This man went downstairs."

"Did he come back the same?" I asked, every time. It was part of the ritual. I always asked; she always answered.

"No," Wren said, every time. "Nobody comes back the same. But he came back *with* us, and that's better than same." She'd poke the fire. "The archivist offered him his name back, you know. In exchange for me and the door. And he said no. First time in his life he'd ever refused a bargain, Maren says. And then he made the biggest one of all — gave the name away free, in front of both courts, on condition that every stolen soul walk out and the gate seal behind them." She looked up at me across the fire, her eyes bright and steady. "You spent yourself on us. So we're spending ourselves on you. That's how inheritance works. It goes both ways or it doesn't go at all."

* * *

The memories didn't return. I want to be honest about that, because honesty was apparently the one thing the man I'd been was famous for. There was no dramatic restoration, no midnight when the flood came back over the wall. What happened instead was slower and stranger: the stories accumulated. The versions of me told by four different mouths, each with their own errors and emphases and private edits, layered up inside me like river sediment, until one day I realized I *had* opinions about this Isandro person — found him brave and infuriating, admired his stubbornness, mourned his loneliness, and disagreed, respectfully, with several of Oskit's embellishments.

I was becoming a man assembled from testimony. A creature of hearsay. And here is the thing I discovered, the thing I suspect the Court never understood and Vess never wanted to: a name given freely by the people who love you weighs more than a name taken in a bargain. Vess's archive held my name cold and catalogued, a specimen pinned in a drawer. What Sera and Wren and Maren and Oskit were building in that back room was warm, and slightly inaccurate, and *mine* — ours — in the way that only shared things are truly owned.

One night near midwinter's end, Wren finished the tale — the whole arc, shop to gate to court to song — and the fire had burned down to that deep orange quiet, and nobody moved to bank it, and she said, "Do you remember any of it now?"

"Not the way you mean," I said. And then, because the ritual demanded honesty and had taught me to supply it: "But when you talk about him, my chest does something. Like a bell that's been struck very far away. I don't hear the note. I feel it."

Wren nodded solemnly, with the terrible authority of twelve-year-olds everywhere. "That's how the First Tongue works," she said. "You don't remember a word. You remember what it *did*. The archivists never figured that out. That's why their archive burned."

From her pocket she took a scrap of paper — she'd been scribbling for weeks, secretive as a magpie — and smoothed it on her knee. On it, in her careful, lopsided hand, was a line of the First Tongue, and beneath it, her translation, laboriously lettered:

*A door is a promise that the other side exists.*

"That's the ward," she said. "The new one. On your cellar door. Maren and I painted it while you were asleep — don't be angry, it's a welcome, not a lock, we checked with the mountain and everything. It's yours. Well." She grinned suddenly, and the firelight caught her, and she was for one moment the exact image of every story she'd ever told me about herself: mud-streaked, unbowed, impossible. "It's ours. But it's on your door."

I took the paper. My hands — the ones that remembered locks and razors and wards and the weight of a child lifted over a threshold — held it steady.

"So," I said, and heard in my own voice, faint but unmistakable, the note of a man beginning again, "what happens next? In the story. After the nameless man comes home."

Wren leaned forward, eyes shining, and said the words I would carry into every year that followed, the words that were, though neither of us knew it yet, the first line of a brand-new chapter:

"Well. First, we reopen the shop."
