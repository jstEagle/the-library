## 1. The Date That Wasn't His

Rain in Corvane doesn't fall so much as it settles in, the way a debt does. By midmorning it had soaked through my awning, my coat, and my last good mood, and I stood under a leaking canvas at the corner of Tanner's Row selling charms to people who mostly didn't believe in them, which is exactly how I like my customers. Believers want results. Skeptics just want to feel like they bought something, and feeling is a product I can deliver.

"Protection from drowning," I told a fishwife with forearms like mooring rope. "Wear it on your left wrist. The water respects symmetry."

She squinted at the little knot of riverstone and red thread in her palm. "My husband drowned."

"On his left wrist or his right?"

"...Right."

"There you go," I said, and she paid me three coppers for what had cost me nothing but string and a walk along the weir.

I want to be clear about something, since everything that happened afterward might make you think otherwise: that was the whole of my ambition. String, stone, patter, coppers. Fifteen years ago I'd walked away from something enormous, and I'd spent every day since building a life so small and so dull that no part of it could ever find me again. A stall on Tanner's Row. A room above a chandler's with a view of a wall. A name — Vane — that was common enough to hide behind, like a man standing in a crowd of men who all share his face.

I was good at being nobody. It's a trade like any other. You practice.

* * *

The bookseller's summons came at dusk, carried by a boy who looked like he'd been running since before the rain started, which in Corvane means he'd been running since roughly the age of the city itself.

"You're the Vane," he panted.

"I'm *a* Vane," I said. "There are forty on this street. Vane the cooper, Vane the widow, Vane who owes me two coppers—"

"He said the one who sells charms." The boy swallowed. "He said you'd know what that meant."

And there it was, that little cold hook behind my sternum, the one I hadn't felt in fifteen years. Because I did know. Old Hollis Fenner knew too — everyone on Tanner's Row knew the bookseller had been somebody before he was a bookseller, the way everyone knew not to ask about it. He'd been in the trade. The real trade. And people in the real trade didn't send boys; they sent signs, omens, debts. A boy was either an insult or an emergency.

"Where?" I asked.

"Candlewick Close. He said come now. He said—" the boy hesitated, clearly reciting, "—he said, 'Tell him the date matters.'"

I closed the stall. I left the charms in their box under the awning because I told myself I'd be back within the hour, and I want you to remember that lie, because it's the last comfortable one I told for a long time.

* * *

Candlewick Close smelled of tallow and wet ash, and Hollis Fenner's shop sat at the bottom of it like a tooth in a dying mouth. The sign above the door read FENNER & SONS, which had been a joke for thirty years, since Hollis had neither sons nor, apparently, much of a sense of humor left. Through the window I could see him behind his counter, propped up like a book someone had leaned against another book and forgotten.

He was dying. I don't mean he was ill. I mean dying was happening to him in the room, visibly, the way weather happens — his breath coming in long pulls, his skin gone the gray of old vellum. When he saw me he smiled, and it cost him.

"The charm-seller," he rasped. "Of Tanner's Row. Sit down, Vane. Standing rooms are for funerals and we're not quite there."

"I'd rather stand," I said. "I keep my visits short. It's a professional habit — customers linger otherwise."

"Wit. Good. You'll need it." He fumbled beneath the counter and came up with a bottle and two cups, pouring with a hand that trembled like a plumb line in wind. "Drink. It's terrible. Everything worth having tonight will be."

I didn't drink. I'm not proud of how long it took me to understand what kind of night this was, but even a coward can smell a funeral being arranged around him. "Hollis. Why am I here?"

"Because of the date." He said it simply, and turned the bottle so I could see the label: vintage older than me. "What's today?"

"Twelfth of Emberfall."

"And the year?"

I told him.

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd been dreading and hoping for in equal measure. "Then it's time." He pushed back from the counter and shuffled toward the back of the shop, and I followed him past shelves of ledgers and hymnals and books with their spines burned blank — the Church's doing, that; they used to burn the books themselves until someone pointed out that blank spines make people curious — to a case at the very back, iron-bound, locked with a key he wore around his neck like a saint's medal.

Inside lay a single book. Not large. Bound in something dark that wasn't leather, or wasn't only leather, with a clasp shaped like a closed mouth.

"Fifteen years I've kept it," Hollis said. "Not for me. I was paid to keep it, by a woman who said a Vane would come asking on the twelfth of Emberfall, and if no Vane came, to burn it unread." His eyes found mine. "You came on the date."

"Lots of things come on dates," I said. "Tax collectors. Fevers."

"Not like this." He lifted the book out and set it on the reading table between us, and I swear the lamplight bent toward it, subtle, like grass leaning in wind. "It's a prophecy, Vane. A century old, give or take. And it names tonight."

Everything sensible in me said leave. I want that on the record too. But dread and curiosity are conjoined twins, and curiosity always walks in front.

"What does it name tonight as?"

Hollis opened the clasp — the mouth-shape parted with a sound I decided not to think about — and turned to a page near the middle. "See for yourself. But touch it gently. It's particular about hands."

The page was heavy stock, ivory gone amber, covered in script that shifted the way watermarks do when you tilt paper toward light. At the top, a line in older ink, faded almost to rumor:

*On the twelfth of Emberfall, in the rain-year, the Vane comes to turn.*

Below it, fresher, darker:

*Let him who bears the name put hand to page, and the covenant speak its debtor's name aloud.*

"Charming," I said. My voice came out steadier than my hands deserved credit for. "Some century-old fraud wants me to touch a haunted ledger. Hollis, who wrote this? Who paid you?"

"A dead woman," he said. "That's all I ever knew. She paid in coin that predates the last king. She said the book would know its reader." He coughed then, a wet, awful sound, and gripped the table edge. "Vane. I've sold lies my whole life and true ones besides, and I can tell the difference better than any man alive. This one's true. I've never dared touch it myself."

"Then why show me?"

"Because I'm dying," he said, with the mildness of a man remarking on the weather, "and because I read the first page once, sixty years ago, by accident, and it's been waiting in me ever since like a swallowed hook. It said the Vane would come. It didn't say I'd have to watch him do it." His eyes were wet, whether from the coughing or something else I couldn't say. "I'm sorry. Genuinely. You seem like a man who built himself a small quiet life on purpose."

You could have knocked me over with a feather's shadow. "What makes you say that?"

"Nobody sells drowning-charms to fishwives for three coppers unless they're hiding from something that would pay better."

* * *

Here's what I should have done: thanked him for his time, recommended a physician, and gone home to my room above the chandler's where the only magic was the way damp got into boots no matter what you did.

Instead I looked at the page. *Let him who bears the name put hand to page.* And some old thing in me — some thing I'd buried under fifteen years of string and stone and patter — leaned forward like the lamplight had.

"It's just paper," I said, mostly to myself.

"That," said Hollis, "is what worries me."

I touched the page.

For a moment: nothing. Just old paper under my fingertip, dry and warm in a way paper shouldn't be, warm like skin. Then the warmth pulled, and I felt a sting, and I understood with the slow horror of a man watching his own blood bead up out of a fingertip that had no wound — the page was drinking. A thin line of red crawled into the fibers, spreading, branching, writing itself across the amber sheet in letters of my own blood, and the old ink rose up around it like sediment stirred in still water.

And the book spoke.

I don't mean a voice came out of it. I mean the words on the page moved, rearranged, and spoke themselves inside my skull, in the tone you use to call in a debt — patient, certain, and very tired.

***Isandro Vane.***

My name. Not the name on my stall license, not the name the fishwives knew. The name I'd been born with, the name I'd fed to a god fifteen years ago in exchange for a mercenary's bargain I've never once explained to anyone, because there was no one alive to explain it *to*. The name I thought I had drowned in a shrine that stopped answering.

The blood-script finished writing itself, and I read it standing up because my legs were no longer interested in participating:

*On the twelfth of Emberfall, in the rain-year, the covenant breaks.*
*The Choir falls silent, and silence takes the world's laws with it.*
*One named mortal stands at the seam: the Vane, twice-bound, once-forsaken.*
*Three fragments scattered: a song in a dead memory, a truth in a god's silence, a loyalty unchosen.*
*Let him gather them, or let both realms come unsung.*

The last lines were still bleeding themselves onto the page when the lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere up Candlewick Close, a whistle sounded — the short, doubled note that in Corvane means Inquisition, move along, nothing here belongs to you anymore.

Hollis grabbed my sleeve. His grip was stronger than a dying man's had any right to be. "Did it speak? Did it speak to *you*?"

"Hollis—"

"Listen to me." He thrust the book into my arms, and it was heavier than it had any right to be, warm as a sleeping animal. "They'll come for it. They'll come for anything the prophecy touches, that's what they do, that's all they are. Take it out the back, down the drain-walk, don't stop for anything with a white sash." He shoved a key ring after the book. "And Vane — Isandro." He said the name deliberately, tasting it, and I realized he'd known it all along, had probably always known, and had kept my small quiet life safe for fifteen years out of a kindness I had no way to repay. "Whatever it promised you once. Whatever you gave it. Tonight it comes due either way. Better you hold the debt than the debt holds you."

The whistles were closer now. Two notes, then answering notes from the far end of the close, the way wolves answer each other.

"Hollis," I said, "come with me."

He smiled again, that expensive smile. "Son, I've been dead since sundown. The rest is just the shop closing up." He turned back toward the front of the store, straightening his collar like a man preparing to receive customers. "Go. And when you see what's hunting you — and you will — remember it isn't the Inquisition you need to fear."

"Then what is?"

He paused at the curtain, silhouetted against the front window's gray light, rain streaking the glass like the world outside was trying to get in.

"The thing that wrote your name down a hundred years ago," he said, "and waited."

The front door rattled. Boots. White sashes. Hollis Fenner squared his shoulders behind his counter among his blank-spined books, and I went out the back into the dark with a prophecy drinking the last warmth out of my hands, and behind me I heard the door open, and a voice, official and cold, ask the old man what he was hiding.

And Hollis — gods rest him, wherever the silent ones keep their dead — laughed.
