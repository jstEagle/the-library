## 2. What the Inquisition Burns

I'd made it three streets from the bookseller's shop before I decided to go back.

That's the thing about dread — it doesn't announce itself. It just sits in your chest like a swallowed stone and waits for you to be stupid enough to test it. I had walked away from Old Pellam's shop with the particular lightness of a man who has convinced himself that nothing happened. A book spoke to me. Fine. Books speak to madmen all the time; Corvane is full of them, muttering at gutters and preaching to pigeons. I could be one more. Nobody burns a man for talking to himself.

But my feet kept slowing, and by the time I reached the corner of Tanner's Row I wasn't walking so much as circling, the way a dog circles a trap it can smell but doesn't understand. And then I smelled actual smoke.

Not pipe smoke. Not the tanneries' usual sour haze. Something sweeter, sharper — vellum burning, which is a smell I knew too well from fifteen years ago, though I'd have sworn under oath I didn't know why I knew it.

The shop was already lit when I came around the corner.

* * *

Let me tell you what an Inquisition raid looks like when you're standing on the wrong side of it, watching your livelihood go up in someone else's fire.

There were six of them, maybe seven, in their grey-and-black livery, faces behind half-masks that made them look less like men than like instruments someone was playing badly. They weren't looting. That was the first wrong note. Raiders loot; that's half the point of raids, whatever the doctrine says about purity. These ones were carrying things *out* of the shop and stacking them neatly in the street — books, ledgers, jars of ink — and then feeding them to the fire one stack at a time, deliberately, like a priest serving communion in reverse.

Old Pellam stood in his doorway between two raiders, hands bound in front of him with black cord, and he was not screaming. He was talking. Low, urgent, bent toward the youngest raider as if they were two friends arguing over dice. I couldn't hear the words over the fire, but I could read the shape of it: he was bargaining. He was always bargaining. Sixty years selling charms and charms-adjacent nonsense in a city where charms were illegal, and he'd survived on the simple principle that everyone can be bought if you find the right currency.

Whatever currency he'd offered, it hadn't worked yet. The young raider kept shaking his head, and the older one — the one with the captain's bar on his shoulder — kept looking at Pellam the way you look at a ledger that won't balance.

And then, in the middle of all this, I saw it. Leaning against the doorframe inside, past Pellam's shoulder, propped where no one had thought to grab it because it looked worthless: the prophecy book. My book. The one whose page had drunk my blood an hour ago and spoken my date back to me in letters that rearranged themselves like insects swarming into formation.

It was still there. Half the shop was ash and the thing sat untouched, patient as a coffin.

I want to be honest with you, because this account will probably be read by people who need honesty more than they need comfort: I did not go in there out of courage. I went in because I am a mercenary, and mercenaries are superstitious in the way gamblers are superstitious — we don't believe in luck, exactly, but we've each got one object we're sure is holding our death at bay, and when we lose it we die within the year. Everyone in my trade knows this. The charm-sellers of Corvane have gotten rich off it.

The prophecy book had drunk my blood. By every rule of the trade I'd lived by since I was nineteen, that made it mine now. Blood-price. You could no more leave it in a burning building than you could leave your own name.

So I did what mercenaries do. I found the alley behind the shop, counted the raiders' backs, waited for the captain to turn toward his neat little stacks, and went through the smoke.

* * *

Inside, the world had gone orange and wrong. Fire does something strange to a room full of paper — it makes the air itself seem to burn, heat you feel through your boots before you see a single flame, and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, so shadows stop meaning anything. I moved fast and low, past shelves that were already curling like leaves in autumn, and I grabbed the book off the floor.

It was warm. Not fire-warm — *alive*-warm, blood-warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and when my hand closed on the cover I felt it close back. Some part of me, some part I don't have a name for, tightened around my fingers like a fist made of paper and old glue. It remembered me. An hour ago it had tasted me. Now it held on.

I tucked it under my arm — you don't put a thing like that in a pack next to your rations; even I have limits — and turned for the window at the back of the storeroom, the one I'd noticed on the way out earlier because a mercenary notices exits the way a carpenter notices grain.

Except the exit had a problem. The problem was six feet tall, wearing grey and black, and holding a lantern.

She was young. Younger than me by some years, with the kind of face the half-masks are designed to erase — sharp, tired, ink-stained at the fingertips, which is not a look I associate with street-level Inquisition muscle. She had a satchel open at her hip and she was crouched over a shelf of Pellam's ledgers, and she looked up at me the way you look up when a cat knocks something over in the night: annoyed, then alarmed, then — this is the part I keep turning over — *recognizing*. Not recognizing me. Recognizing what I was carrying. Her eyes went to the book under my arm and stayed there, and her whole face changed, the way a river changes when something enormous moves under the surface.

"Put it down," she said. Not loudly. There was no theater in her voice, none of the sermon-cadence the Inquisition trains into its people. Just flat instruction, like a surgeon saying hold still.

Now, a smarter man would have noted her position between me and the window, calculated the odds, and done something clever. What I actually did was the oldest move in the mercenary book: I threw the nearest object at her head — a ledger, poor Pellam's careful accounts of forty years of illegal sales, may they rest in whatever afterlife keeps books — and while she flinched I went through the window feet-first, into the alley, into the rain, into the dark.

I heard her shout something behind me, but it wasn't the alarm-call I expected. No "raider down," no "stop him." It sounded like a question. Like she was asking the burning room a question, or asking herself one. I didn't stay to translate.

* * *

I ran. Of course I ran. Fifteen years of running from things has given me a kind of professional relationship with fear: we nod to each other in passing, we don't get sentimental. I cut through the tannery yards where the stink covers everything including pursuit, waded the drainage channel behind the dye-houses because dogs lose a scent in the effluent, and came out on Rope-Walk with wet boots, a stolen prophecy, and nothing else.

Nothing else. Let me sit with that for a moment, because it deserves sitting-with.

My stock was in that shop. Not much — I was never a rich man — but it was *mine*, assembled over years: luck-knots braided from rope I'd personally watched survive a hanging, fever-charms pressed from herbs gathered on the right side of dawn, the little tin soldiers I sold to mothers whose sons were conscripted, painted with a drop of my own blood so they'd come home. All of it contraband. All of it the only honest work I've ever done, if you can call a lie that comforts people honest. Gone. Every piece of it either ash or stacked in the street being fed to the flames by men who believed, sincerely, that they were saving the city from me.

And here is the thought that arrived while I was wringing out my socks under Rope-Walk bridge, and it arrived cold, the way the true ones do:

They didn't come for Pellam.

Think about it. The Inquisition has burned a hundred charm-shops in Corvane. It's routine work, the kind of raid they assign to new recruits to season them. But those raids are messy — doors broken, goods seized wholesale, the shopkeeper dragged off in irons to be questioned. This was different. This was *tidy*. They stacked the inventory before they burned it. They let Pellam talk. They left a worthless-looking book leaning against a doorframe untouched while they carried out armfuls of ordinary stock.

You don't tidy a raid unless you know exactly what you're looking for.

And what were they looking for? Not charms — Pellam's stock was the same dried herbs and knotted string every shop in the low quarter sells. Not Pellam himself; they barely seemed interested in him except as an obstacle. They were looking for a book. Or rather — and this is the thought that made my hands go cold around the leather cover — they were looking for whoever had touched that book tonight. Because someone had told them. Someone had watched an ordinary-looking man in a patched coat walk into a dying bookseller's back room, touch a century-old page, and walk out again changed, and someone had run straight to the Inquisition and said *him. Tonight. He's the one.*

Someone who knew my real trade. Not the charms — anyone could report a charm-seller, and half of Corvane would, for pocket-money. Someone who knew what I actually was. Who knew that under the patched coat and the market-stall patter there was a man bound to a silent god, a man who took contracts no honest soldier would take, a man with a name that meant something in certain rooms I'd spent fifteen years avoiding.

Someone who knew me.

I sat under that bridge and made myself count the list, because counting is what I do instead of panicking. People who knew both halves of Isandro Vane — the charm-peddler and the god-touched blade. I got to four before I stopped, because the fourth name was the one I didn't want to reach, and once you reach a name you can't unreach it.

Doss.

No. I shut the thought in a box and sat on the lid. Doss had pulled me out of a ditch at Kellin's Ford with an arrow in my thigh. Doss had held my hair back the winter I drank myself half-blind after Marisol. Doss ate at my table. You don't put a name like that on a list like that. You'd sooner accuse your own hands of treason.

But the box didn't lock well. Boxes never do, once doubt gets inside. I kept seeing it: Doss knew I visited Pellam. Doss knew what the old man really sold, underneath the licorice and the love-philtres. Doss knew my real name, the one I only used in the old days, the one that started with Vane. If anyone in Corvane could have drawn the Inquisition a map to me in under an hour, it was him. And if someone had offered him enough —

Stop. Sit on the lid.

The rain picked up, as rain in Corvane does when it wants to make a point, and I pulled my collar up and considered my options like a professional. Option one: run. Leave the city tonight, be in the hill country by morning, sell the book to some collector in Vail and start over under a new name. I've done it before. It's easier than it sounds and emptier than it looks. Option two: go to ground in the low quarter, wait out the search, pretend tonight never happened. Except the book wouldn't pretend. It was warm under my arm the whole time, warm as a heartbeat, and twice on the walk from the bridge it shifted against my ribs like something turning over in its sleep. Whatever had happened in Pellam's back room hadn't finished happening. The page had taken my blood and spoken, and spoken things I hadn't repeated aloud even to myself — *tonight*, it had said, and *the Vane*, and a third word I'd shoved down deep where I keep the things I refuse to think about. Things don't un-happen in my experience. They compound.

Option three was the shrine.

I didn't want option three. I want to be clear about that. The shrine of Vethra sits up on the cliff road above the tanneries, abandoned these fifteen years, and I have walked past it perhaps four hundred times in those fifteen years without going in, which takes a discipline most men reserve for vows of chastity. Because Vethra and I have an arrangement, Vethra and I: he gave me something once, when I was nineteen and desperate and bleeding in a place I don't talk about, and then he went silent, and silence is a debt collected slowly. You spend fifteen years paying interest on a god's voice.

But the book had said *tonight*. And the Inquisition had come *tonight*, tidy and precise, hunting a man they should never have known existed. Coincidence is a coin with two faces, and I've flipped it enough times to know when it's landing the same way twice.

So I sat under the bridge until the rain eased, and I felt the book breathe against my ribs — I'm telling you, it breathed, slow and patient, like it had all the time in the world and I had none — and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives after you've lost everything portable, that options one and two were the same option wearing different coats. Run or hide, either way I'd be spending the rest of my life looking over my shoulder for grey-and-black livery, waiting for the next tidy raid, the next precise tip-off. Whoever had sold me hadn't sold me once. They'd sold me *thoroughly*. My name was in their ledger now, written in the same ink they used for heretics.

Paranoia, the priests say, is a sin. So is getting caught. I chose the sin I could live with.

* * *

Toward morning the rain quit and the sky did that bruised-grey thing it does over Corvane, the color of old pewter, and I climbed the cliff road with the book under my arm and the city smoking gently below me. From up here you could see the whole low quarter laid out like a spilled purse, and among the chimneys and drying-yards there was one column of smoke still rising, thin and stubborn, from Tanner's Row.

Pellam's shop. Still burning, or smoldering, or whatever fires do when they've finished with a man's life and haven't been told to stop.

I didn't know if Pellam was alive. I didn't know if they'd taken him or killed him or bought him, and I couldn't go back to find out, because whatever else the raid had been, it had also been a message, and messages like that are addressed to whoever reads them. *We know what you are. We know where you sleep. We were tidy because we could afford to be.* The Inquisition doesn't waste precision on nobodies. Somewhere in a grey office, a file had opened with my name on it, and files like that don't close.

Halfway up the cliff road I stopped, because the book moved again — not the sleepy shifting from before but a deliberate press, insistent, like a finger tapping my ribs — and when I pulled it out to look at it, the cover was warm and the clasp had come undone on its own, and the page inside had turned itself to the one with my blood on it.

The letters had rearranged again. Last night they'd named my date. Now, in the pewter dawn, they'd settled into a single line, small and patient, in an ink that gleamed faintly the way blood gleams:

*The silent god remembers what he was made to forget.*

I read it three times. Then I looked up the road, at the ruined shrine of Vethra sitting on its bluff with the sea fog wrapping it like a shroud, and I laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh, the kind that scares crows — because of course. Of course. The book drinks my blood, the Inquisition finds me in a single night, and now the thing wants to introduce me to my god. Everything tonight was connected, and I was the thread running through all of it, and threads, in my experience, exist to be pulled.

Fifteen years I'd walked past that shrine. Fifteen years of discipline, of not-going-in, of keeping the debt unpaid and the door shut.

I went in.
