## 5. Pulled Through

There are three kinds of mornings in Corvane. There's the kind where you wake up poor and stay that way, which is most of them; there's the kind where you wake up rich and someone relieves you of it by noon; and then there's the kind where you wake up and everything you built over fifteen careful years comes apart in your hands before the bell rings the hour.

I'd had two of the first kind already this week. I got the third one on a Tuesday.

I knew something was wrong when I smelled pitch smoke from four streets off. Corvane always smells faintly of smoke — tallow, forges, the Tanners' Ward doing what the Tanners' Ward does to the air — but pitch smoke at dawn, moving *toward* me instead of drifting away from it, has a particular grammar. It says: somebody is lighting torches in a hurry, and torches lit in a hurry are never for reading by.

I was three doors from my room above the cooper's when I saw them.

Six riders, black-lacquered armor under grey cloaks, the sun-wheel of the Inquisition stamped on their pauldrons like a brand on meat. And behind them, walking because she apparently hadn't been offered a horse or hadn't wanted one, a woman with an archivist's satchel and an expression I recognized even at distance, because I'd worn it myself once: the look of somebody doing a job they've started having questions about.

I didn't know her name yet. I want that on the record. Whatever came later, whatever the Choir or fate or plain bad arithmetic made of us — that morning she was just the shape of my ruin, and I ran.

* * *

I ran the way you learn to run in the Vane trade, which is not heroically. Heroic running gets you caught. I cut through the cooper's yard, kicked a stack of hoops down behind me — sorry, Master Odell, I'll pay for those if the world continues — went over the wall into the dyer's yard, and came out the far side stained indigo to the hip, looking like a saint who'd fallen into his own miracle.

The book was against my chest under my shirt. The prophecy. The thing with my name in it and tonight's date inked a hundred years ago, drinking my blood when I touched it, speaking in a voice like a struck bell underwater.

I should have thrown it in the river. I've thought about that a great deal since. Every version of me that throws it in the river lives a long, dull, unremarkable life selling charms to grandmothers with rheumatic knees, and dies warm, and nobody ever hums about him anywhere.

Instead I kept it. Because I'm a fool, yes, but also because when it spoke, under the dread, under the very sensible terror, there had been a sound underneath its voice. A chord. Like the moment after the cracked bell rings, when the air remembers ringing and hasn't let go yet.

Fifteen years ago a god went silent, and I have spent every day since listening for the end of that silence. You don't stop being a man of faith just because the faith stops answering. You just get embarrassed about it, and carry it lower, next to the knife.

The dead-end was my own fault. I'll admit that. I've run these streets for years and I still took the turning behind the old wool exchange out of habit, forgetting the city had bricked it up two winters ago when the plague cart traffic got bad. Habit is a fine servant and a murderous guide. I hit the brick wall with both palms, felt the mortar grit, heard the hooves turn into the alley behind me, and understood with perfect clarity that my options had narrowed to: arrest, fire, or the wall.

So naturally I pulled out the forbidden prophecy book.

Look. Desperation is not thinking. Desperation is the absence of thinking, a white room with one door in it, and the door was the book. The raiders were dismounting. The archivist woman was saying something — "alive, Ravel wants him alive" — and the word *Ravel* landed in me like a hook in a fish's jaw, because I didn't know the name and somehow the book did, because the pages were warming against my ribs like a held bird.

"Isandro Vane," one of them called, and it was strange, hearing my name said aloud by a stranger, strange the way a mirror in the wrong room is strange. Fifteen years of being nobody's name, nobody's mark, just *the charm-seller*, just *Vane*, and now the whole world wanted to say all of me at once.

I opened the book.

I want to be clear that I did not open it to any particular page. I did not perform a ritual. I am not, whatever the Inquisition believes, some heretic scholar with sigils sewn into his lining. I opened a book in a blind alley because a book was the only door in the white room, and the book *took hold of me* the way drowning takes hold — no negotiation, no terms, just the water closing over.

My blood was on my fingers — I'd torn them on the bricks — and where blood met page the letters lifted. All of them. Every word of a century-old prophecy rose off the vellum like insects startled from a log, and hung in the alleyway between me and six armed servants of the Church, and sang.

That's the only honest word. They sang. Not music exactly, or not music the way we have it, which is sound arranged politely in time. This was the *material* of music before arrangement, the thread before the weaving, and it went through my chest, through my ribs, through whatever it is under the ribs that I'd always assumed was just fear and hunger and had apparently been waiting all along.

"The Vane," said the book, in every letter at once, in the bell-underwater voice. "Come and be read."

"No," I said, reasonably. "Absolutely not."

The world peeled.

I don't have a better verb and I've tried several. The alley didn't vanish; it *unmade*, the way a knot unties — the same rope, different arrangement, suddenly no longer holding anything together. The bricks went first, unraveling into lines of pale script. Then the smell of pitch smoke, which peeled away last of all and which I mourned, absurdly, because it was the smell of home. The riders shouted. The archivist woman reached for me — I remember her hand, gloved, close enough that I could see the worn place on one knuckle — and then even that went, folded up like a letter, and I fell.

Not downward. I want to say that clearly too, because falling is what my body insisted had happened and my body is a liar from a country where down exists. I fell *through*. Through the singing. Through the page. Through whatever thin membrane keeps Corvane's noise out of the places where noise was invented.

And then I landed, hard, on my knees, on a floor that was humming.

* * *

Here is what I saw, as much of it as language will hold, and language holds less than you'd hope:

A sky the color of the inside of a shell. No sun, but light everywhere, sourceless, the way light is in dreams and cathedrals. And architecture — gods, so much architecture — hanging in that light without foundations. Towers upside-down, their spires pointing politely at nothing. Bridges that arched toward each other and stopped mid-air, unfinished, patient. Stairways that spiraled into clouds of what I can only call *held notes* — visible sound, glowing faintly gold, drifting like pollen.

Everything was broken, and everything was suspended, and neither fact seemed to trouble the architecture at all.

Because the things weren't hanging on ropes or stone. They were hanging on song. I could hear it, now that I listened — a vast chord underneath everything, low and endless, and wherever the chord swelled, a bridge stood firm; wherever it thinned, masonry crumbled slowly upward into motes of light, like dust deciding to rise instead of fall. Broken towers hung by a single sustained note. Whole colonnades rested on harmonies.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen and it was dying, and I could tell it was dying the way you can tell a sickroom is a sickroom before anyone says a word — by the smell of it, by the held breath of it, by the places where the silence had already gotten in.

Because there were silences here. Holes. Gaps in the chord where whole districts should have been, and in those gaps there was nothing — not darkness, which is something, not emptiness, which is a shape, but *un-sungness*, an absence so total my eyes slid off it and my mind filed it under *nothing to see here* the way a tongue files a missing tooth.

I knelt on a bridge — an actual bridge, wide enough for a market, its balustrade carved with figures of singers whose mouths had all been chiseled smooth — and I was alone, and I did the reasonable thing.

"Hello?" I called, and my voice came out small and wrong, flattened, like a coin rubbed featureless.

The chord beneath me shifted. Just slightly. The way a sleeping man shifts when you say his name.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the balustrade, from the light, from the space just over my left shoulder where a person would stand if a person were going to speak — a voice.

"You're early."

I came off my knees and put my back to the nearest tower-foot and my hand to my knife, which is the posture of a man who has learned that beauty and danger are usually the same customer wearing different hats. "Early," I repeated. "For what?"

"For the reading." The voice was — I struggled with this for a long time afterward — it was *thin*. Not weak. Thin like the last coat of gilt on an old icon, worn down to the wood in places but still unmistakably gold. "The covenant names this night. You arrived at dawn. Mortal clocks and ours disagree, but even so, you're early."

Something gathered itself out of the light near the bridge's broken end. I expected — I don't know what I expected. A god, presumably, insofar as I'd ever let myself expect anything: vastness, wings, the whole theatrical inventory. What formed was small. Human-sized. A figure like a person made of the memory of a person, edges shimmering, face smooth as the chiseled mouths on the balustrade — no eyes, no mouth, and yet looking at me, and yet speaking.

"I know you," I said, and my stomach dropped, because I did, the way you know a melody from childhood without knowing its name. "You sound like —"

"Yes."

"He never answered. Fifteen years. I prayed at that ruined shrine until my knees were gravel and he never once —" My voice did something humiliating in the middle of the sentence. I pushed past it. "Who are you?"

"A remnant." The figure drifted closer, and where it passed, the chord under our feet brightened, briefly, like grass remembering rain. "When a god goes silent, he does not vanish. He leaves echoes. Most fade. I held." The smooth face tilted. "I held because someone had to open the door when the Vane came."

"The Vane." I laughed, and it came out jagged. "That's not — I sold charms. Small ones. Luck-knots. Wart-charms. Once I cured a horse of refusing bridges. That's the entire scope of my ambitions. Somebody wrote my name in a book a hundred years ago and everyone — the Inquisition, the Church, and now apparently *you* — has decided that makes me something."

"It makes you named," the Echo said simply. "In the Choir, naming is nearly everything. The covenant between your realm and mine was sung into being by three voices — mortal, divine, and the song itself. It bound your world's magic-laws to our music. While it held, your charms worked, your blessings blessed, the dead stayed properly dead, and reality kept its promises on both sides of the veil."

"And now?"

The Echo turned, slowly, to face one of the silences — one of the holes in the world — and I watched the nearest tower shudder as the chord strained around the gap like a crowd straining around an accident.

"Now it is breaking. Three fragments broke from the covenant when your god fell silent: Memory, Truth, and Loyalty. Scattered. Hidden. And every year the chord thins, and every year more of the Choir goes unsung, and when the last note fails—" the smooth face came back around to me, "—your world loses its magic-laws with it. Not its magicians. Its *laws*. The rules that let prayer land, that keep wards warded, that hold the shape of things."

Somewhere below us — below being approximate here — a section of colonnade finished crumbling upward into golden dust, and the gap where it had been widened, and the chord bent audibly to cover the hole, the way a choir covers for a singer who's died mid-verse, and I felt that bend in my teeth.

"Why me?" I asked. "Truly. Not prophecy-poetry. Why me?"

"Because the covenant requires a mortal voice given willingly," the Echo said, "and because your name is written, and because—" a pause, and something almost like gentleness entered that worn-gilt voice, "—because you have been listening for fifteen years, Isandro Vane. Silence answers the one who listens. It has been waiting for you to stop asking why and start asking *what*."

I looked out over the hanging city. The broken towers. The bridges to nowhere. The beautiful, catastrophic patience of it. Somewhere behind me, impossibly far away, a woman with a worn glove had been reaching for my collar, and six men with torches had been calling my name like it was a verdict, and a hundred-year-old book had eaten me alive.

"There's a way back," I said. It wasn't quite a question. I made it not-quite-a-question on purpose, because questions can be answered no.

The Echo was silent for a moment, and its silence had a texture I recognized with a sick lurch — the same texture as fifteen years of unanswered prayers at a ruined shrine.

"The way you came is closed behind you," it said finally. "The book brought you because the book is a fragment's shadow, and shadows only fall one direction. But the covenant, when reforged—"

"When *reforged*." I heard my own voice climb and didn't care. "You're saying the only road home runs through fixing your entire collapsing universe. That's — no. No. I'm a charm-seller. I cure horses of opinions about bridges. Find another Vane."

"There is no other Vane." The Echo drifted nearer, and up close its shimmering surface moved like the skin of a struck bell, trembling long after the strike. "There is only you, early, kneeling on the Meridian Bridge, in a realm that has perhaps a season left. The chord is failing. The silences are spreading. Your god hid something terrible rather than break the covenant's heart, and the hiding is killing both worlds, and you are the only living soul whose name the covenant still remembers."

Above us, the shell-colored sky flickered — actually flickered, like a lamp low on oil — and somewhere in the vast chord a note slipped, a half-tone flat, and every hanging tower in sight shivered at once.

"And if I refuse?" I asked quietly.

"Then eventually the music stops," the Echo said, "and neither realm is destroyed, precisely. Destruction is a shaped thing. It has edges. This will simply... cease to have been. Both worlds. Unsung."

The vertigo finally arrived, right on schedule, fashionably late after the awe. The bridge hummed under my knees. The light came from nowhere and everywhere. Fifteen years ago a god spoke to me once and then abandoned me, and I had hated him for it every single day, and now I stood — knelt — in the wreckage his silence had made, being told that the abandonment itself had been some kind of mercy, and that the bill for that mercy had my name on it in ink a century old.

I put my head in my hands. The chord hummed patiently through my palms, through my skull, through whatever it is under the ribs.

"Fine," I said, to my hands, to the god who wasn't there, to the worn-gilt ghost waiting beside me. "Tell me what broke. Tell me where the pieces fell."

The Echo brightened, and beneath us the Meridian Bridge rang like a bell being remembered.

"The first fragment fell into a memory," it said. "In your city. Held by a woman who inherited a song from a stranger's grave."

I looked up. "What woman?"

The smooth face turned toward the horizon — toward, though I couldn't see it, the grey smudge where Corvane sat under its vellum dawn, where six riders were reporting to a High Inquisitor that the Vane had vanished into thin air with the evidence, and where a woman with a worn glove was about to write my name in a ledger under the word *escaped*.

"Her name," said the Echo, "is Maerin."
