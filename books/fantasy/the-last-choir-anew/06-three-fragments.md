## 6. Three Fragments

I have been dead for a hundred and twelve years, and I would like to state for the record that it has not improved my patience.

The mortal arrived the way mortals always arrive in the Choir — badly. He came through the seam screaming, which is traditional, and landed on a staircase that was not currently attached to anything, which is less traditional but shows initiative. The stair held him because I asked it to, and then he lay there on his back in the half-light, breathing like a man who had been informed his lungs were optional and was arguing the point.

I watched him from the archway. Watching is most of what I do. It is not a role I auditioned for; it is simply what remained of me after the rest was spent. But I have gotten good at it. A century of watching teaches you things. It taught me that this one — Vane, Isandro, the name the book had bled out of him and sung into my realm like a coin dropped down a well — did not look at the wonders around him with wonder. He looked at them the way a man looks at a bill.

That, more than the prophecy, more than the blood on the page, told me he was the right mortal. Or the wrong one. In matters of covenant, the two categories have never been cleanly separated.

* * *

He found his feet eventually. That took some doing, because the Choir does not believe in floors as a moral principle. What stood beneath him was a plaza made of suspended sound — you could see the architecture holding itself up the way a note holds up a hymn, all of it hanging from melodies too vast to hear whole. Broken colonnades drifted past overhead, humming to themselves. A shattered bell tower turned slowly in the middle distance like a planet made of grief. Everything here is broken, and everything here still sings. That is the tragedy of the Choir in a single sentence, and I have had a hundred years to polish it.

"Where am I," he said. Not a question. Mortals say it like an accusation when they're frightened. This one said it like a man reading terms of service.

"The Choir," I said, stepping out of the archway.

He looked at me and did something interesting: he flinched, and then stopped himself from flinching further, and then studied me with the deliberate calm of someone who has decided fear is a luxury item. I appreciate that in a person. It's rare, and it's almost always expensive.

I should describe what he saw, since mortals always ask, and since I have had no one to describe myself to in a very long time. I am shaped like a man, roughly, the way a footprint is shaped like a foot. I am the color of the space between two notes. Where my face should be there is light doing an impression of a face, and where my voice comes from there is a hollow that echoes even when I don't want it to. I am, in short, what is left when a person gives everything they are to something larger, and the something larger keeps only the part it can use.

My name was Alderic. I mention this now because it will matter later, and because I have carried it alone for a hundred and twelve years, and carrying it alone has begun to feel less like honor and more like hoarding.

"You're the god," Vane said. "Vethra."

"No."

"You're something."

"I'm the receipt," I said, "for something the god purchased long ago. There's a difference, and the difference is going to be your problem shortly." I gestured upward, at the drifting ruin of everything. "Welcome to the place where reality gets its voice. And welcome to its bankruptcy proceedings."

* * *

He followed me because he had no better options, which is how most important journeys begin, whatever the ballads claim. We walked across the plaza — walked being generous; the ground rearranged itself under us in deference to songs we were standing inside, so that each step was a small act of negotiation — and I explained, in the manner of someone who has rehearsed a speech to an empty room for a century, what had happened.

Perhaps I should start further back. Dead men are allowed their digressions.

Once, there were two realms, and they were not separate. The Choir sang, and the mortal world listened, and what the mortal world heard became its laws — not its legal codes, though those too, but the deeper laws. Why fire burns upward. Why the dead stay dead. Why love persists against every rational argument. Your scholars call these natural laws. They are nothing of the kind. They are songs, sustained, and they require singers.

The covenant between realms was simple, as true things usually are. Each generation, the Choir would choose a mortal voice — not seize one, never seize one, that is the entire point and the entire failure — and that voice would come here willingly and be given over, wholly, to the singing. In exchange, the mortal world kept its magic-laws intact, and the gods kept their existence, and the chosen one kept... well. That is the part everyone agreed not to discuss.

Vethra was the god of silence. Not absence-of-sound — that's just quiet, and quiet is cheap. Silence. The held breath before speech. The pause that gives a sentence its meaning. Vethra's role in the covenant was to keep the space where the choice happened: the moment when a mortal stands before the offering and says yes or no with nobody pushing. Other gods sang. Vethra listened. It sounds like the lesser job. It is the greater one. Listening is where all meaning lives, and I say that as someone who has become mostly echo.

Fifteen years ago, the generation came due. A voice was meant to be chosen. And Vethra — who had watched the choosing longer than any other god, who understood the weight of the thing better than anyone because understanding weight was literally the office — went silent instead.

Not dead. Silent. There is a difference, and the difference is the reason your world's magic-laws have been rotting at the edges while your Inquisition burns books and calls it hygiene.

When Vethra fell silent, the Choir lost its listener. And a song without a listener frays. You know this. Every singer knows this. Play to an empty hall and hear how quickly the music dies in your own throat. The covenant-song began to unravel, and when it unraveled, it broke — not into silence, worse than silence, into pieces. Three pieces. Because covenants, like promises, are made of three things: what was shared, what was known, and what was owed.

We reached the edge of the plaza, where the architecture thinned into open dark, and below us — far below, or perhaps very near, distance is a courtesy the Choir extends rather than a rule it obeys — hung three fragments of light, turning slowly, each one singing a different third of a song that used to be one song.

"Those," I said, "are your life for the next while. Memory. Truth. Loyalty."

He looked down at them for a long time. Then he said, "I didn't agree to any of this."

"No," I said. "You didn't. Congratulations. You've understood the covenant faster than most theologians."

* * *

Here is what I did not say to him immediately, because I am a coward even in death, especially in death: that the fragments cannot be reforged by a god, by an echo, by any inhabitant of the Choir. We are the song. A song cannot sing itself. It needs a throat outside the music — a named mortal, one whose name is written in the old book, whose blood the pages remember, whose consent, freely given, is the hinge the whole covenant turns on.

And here is the second thing I did not say, which is worse: that if the fragments are not gathered and fused before the unraveling finishes, the break becomes permanent. Not dramatic. Permanent. The mortal world will not explode. Nothing so honest. The mortal world will simply lose its laws one at a time — the way a house loses heat in winter, quietly, room by room, until the people inside stop noticing they're cold because they've forgotten warmth was ever a feature of houses. Fire will forget why it climbs. Rivers will forget why they run downhill. Grief will forget why it ends. And the Church, which outlawed magic in the name of order, will discover that order was magic wearing work clothes, and their laws will unravel along with everything else, because their laws were built on the song whether they admit it or not.

Your Inquisition burned the shrines thinking they were putting out fires. They were tearing insulation out of the walls.

I told him the parts I could bear to tell. I told him about the three fragments, and I watched his face do arithmetic — the arithmetic of a man calculating what a quest costs versus what walking away costs, and finding both columns ugly.

"Memory," he repeated. "A fragment of memory. Whose?"

"A keeper's," I said. "In your city. She doesn't know she carries it. They rarely do. Memory hides in people much better than it hides in books — books get burned, as you may have noticed recently."

"And Truth?"

"Truth is kept in the Vault of Silence. Under your cathedral, ironically, behind the Inquisition's own seals. Your enemies are guarding the thing you need. You're welcome."

"And Loyalty?" He said the word carefully, the way a man handles a blade he suspects is enchanted.

"Loyalty isn't hidden anywhere," I said. "It's the only fragment that has to be declared. You'll understand later. Or you won't, and you'll fail, and everything will end gently and forever. Either way, let's not spoil it."

He laughed. Short, harsh, surprised at itself — but a laugh. A hundred and twelve years since I'd heard a mortal laugh in the Choir. The architecture leaned toward the sound like flowers toward sun. I pretended not to notice. The architecture noticed anyway. Everything here notices everything; that's what makes it holy and insufferable in equal measure.

"Why me?" he asked, and it wasn't rhetorical. That was the worst part. He genuinely wanted the answer. Most chosen ones ask it as a complaint. He asked it as a man checking the ledger.

"Because your name was in the book," I said. "Because the book drank your blood and the blood matched. Because fifteen years ago a silent god made you a bargain and you've been living inside its fine print ever since, whether you knew it or not. Because—" and here I nearly told him the truth, the real truth, the truth about the price, about what Vethra hid and why, about what giving a voice actually means — and I stopped. Not yet. He needed to walk part of the road before he could survive knowing where it ended. Every messenger learns this or breaks.

"Because what?" he pressed.

"Because you're the last name left in the ledger," I said. Which was also true. Both things were true. Truth is often a matter of which truths you stack on top.

* * *

We sat — sat being another courtesy — at the edge of the Choir, looking down at the three fragments burning their slow colors into the dark. From this distance they looked almost peaceful. Almost beautiful. Everything broken looks beautiful from far enough away. That's not wisdom, that's just optics.

"I had a life," he said finally. "Small. Bad weather, decent charms, one friend I trusted." His jaw did something complicated. "Had."

"The friend who sold you," I said. I had watched that too. I watch everything; it's my whole retirement plan.

"Don't," he said, and I didn't. Some wounds you leave covered. The fragment of Truth will do enough uncovering later, and neither of us will enjoy it, and I will be there pretending I warned him, because technically I will have.

Instead I said: "You can refuse. I want that on the record, witnessed by whatever's listening — and everything here listens, so consider it thoroughly witnessed. You can refuse. Walk back through the seam, such as it is, live out your span in a world slowly forgetting its own rules, and die ordinary. The Choir will not compel you. Compulsion voids the whole enterprise. That's not a technicality; that's the load-bearing wall."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I wait for the next name in the ledger." I paused. "There is no next name in the ledger."

"So 'refuse' means 'end the world politely.'"

"'End the worlds,'" I corrected. "Plural. Politely. Yes."

He looked down at the fragments again, and I watched him do the thing he'd done on the stair when he landed — flinch, master the flinch, convert it to study. I thought: this is a man who has been making deals with powers beyond his understanding since before he had the vocabulary for it, and has survived by treating awe as a negotiating position. The god chose shrewdly. Or desperately. With Vethra those were always the same word.

"What happens to you," he asked, "if I do this? If I gather them. Reforge it. What happens to the receipt?"

No one had asked me that in a hundred and twelve years. I had asked myself, of course. Nightly. It is one of the few entertainments available to the dead.

"I finish," I said. "Whatever finishing means. Songs end, Vane. Even covenant-songs. Especially covenant-songs. The question is whether they end resolved or cut off mid-phrase. I'd prefer resolved. I've been a suspended chord for a century and I would not recommend it."

He nodded slowly. Then, because he was who he was — a mercenary, a survivor, a man who checked exits before blessings — he said:

"Fine. Start talking. Memory first. Who, where, how do I get it, and what's it going to cost me?"

"It's going to cost you," I said, "exactly what everything costs. Something true about yourself. It always does. That's not cruelty; that's exchange rate."

He exhaled through his nose — mortal shorthand for *of course it is* — and stood, and the Choir shifted beneath him, attentive, hopeful, a ruined cathedral leaning toward its first congregation in generations.

Below us, the three fragments turned in the dark, singing their broken thirds. And somewhere beneath the fragments, beneath the songs, beneath even the silence that had once been a god — I felt the ledger turn its page toward Maerin's door, and the keeper who did not know what she carried, and the first price already waiting, patient as interest.

"Her name," I told him, "is Maerin. And Isandro Vane — take my advice, for whatever an echo's advice weighs: when she names her price, pay it. Pay it quickly, before you learn to love the memory you're spending."

He looked at me strangely. "Why?"

"Because I didn't," I said, "and you're standing in what that looks like a hundred years on."

The seam opened for him then, reluctantly, the way old doors do. He stepped toward it without ceremony, without heroics, without the speech the ballads would someday invent for him — and that, more than the blood, more than the name, more than the prophecy, is how I knew the covenant had chosen correctly.

The dark took him home. The fragments sang on. And I settled back into my watching, which was lighter now by exactly one secret, and heavier by exactly one hope.
