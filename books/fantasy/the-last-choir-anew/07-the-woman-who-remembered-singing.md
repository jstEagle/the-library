## 7. The Woman Who Remembered Singing

Corvane in the rain is a city pretending to be honest. Everything gleams, and nothing is clean. I came back to it the way you come back to a woman who's thrown a knife at you once — carefully, and with an exit already planned.

The Choir had spat me out three days ago at the edge of the Saltmarket, whole and unexplained, with the Echo's instructions rattling around my skull like dice in a cup. *Three fragments. Memory first. Find the keeper.* The Echo had a voice like a hymn played underwater, and the particular patience of something that has been dead for a very long time. It had also neglected, entirely, to mention where in Corvane one finds a memory-keeper. Gods, in my experience, are excellent at grand gestures and useless at directions.

But I'd sold charms in this city for fifteen years, and charms are just memories with a price tag. You learn things.

* * *

Maerin kept her shop above a dyer's yard off Tanner's Row, which meant the whole street smelled of vinegar and old blood, and no Inquisitor came within a hundred paces of it on principle. Clever woman. The stairs up to her door were narrow enough that a man my size had to climb them sideways, like a crab apologizing for existing.

The door was painted the blue of a bruise going green, and it opened before I knocked.

"I felt you coming," said the woman in the doorway. "You walk like a man carrying too much."

She was perhaps sixty, gray-haired, built like a barrel someone had loved too well — round-shouldered, soft-handed, with eyes the flat silver of coins worn smooth by handling. She looked at me the way a fishwife looks at a catch: appraising, unimpressed, already deciding what she could get.

"Isandro Vane," I said.

"No." She stepped aside to let me in. "That's what you're called. Come in before you drip on my landing."

Her shop was small and warm and impossibly full. Not cluttered — full, the way a throat is full of words. Shelves of jars, each labeled in a hand so fine it looked embroidered: names, dates, phrases. *The smell of the harbor, 3rd of Frostmoon. A mother's laugh, cut short. The color of a lie.* Between the jars hung ribbons, beads, locks of hair, each one humming faintly when the rain hit the window, as if the whole room were a struck bell deciding whether to ring.

I stood very still. Fifteen years of selling charms, and I had never once seen the other side of the trade laid out like a confession.

"You're a memory-keeper," I said, because apparently I was the sort of man who stated obvious things when terrified.

"I'm a woman who keeps what people can't carry anymore." She poured tea from a chipped brown pot without asking if I wanted any. "Sit down, Vane. You've got the look of a man who's about to ask me for something expensive."

I sat. The chair was soft as forgiveness.

"The Choir sent me," I said.

Something crossed her face — quick, there and gone, like a bird past a window. "Did it now." She handed me the tea. It tasted of rosemary and iron. "And what does the Choir want with an old woman?"

"Not you. What's inside you." I set down the cup. My hands were steady, which surprised me; everything else in me was shaking. "A song. The covenant's original song. They say it was held by a dead woman, passed down—"

"Passed to me," Maerin said quietly. "Yes. From a mother I never met, who took it from a grandmother she barely knew, all the way back to a singer whose name the song ate." She looked at me over her own cup. "Do you know what that means, boy? It means every woman in that line gave something to keep it. And you walk in here smelling of god-smoke asking me to hand it over because *the Choir told you to*."

When she put it that way — and she did put it that way, immediately and thoroughly — I sounded exactly like every fool who'd ever walked into a shrine demanding miracles. I drank my tea and let the silence do some work.

"The Echo says the Choir is unraveling," I said finally. "That when it goes, the laws that hold magic together go with it. Rain that falls upward. Fire that remembers being water. That kind of unraveling."

"And you believe a dead god's leftover?"

"I met it." I turned my cup. "It's the most honest thing I've talked to in fifteen years."

She snorted, but softly. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere below us the dyer's vats clanked, patient as digestion.

"Here's my problem with you, Vane," she said. "Songs aren't objects. You can't steal one, buy one, or charm your way into one. A song has to be *taught*, and teaching takes trust, and trust—" she pointed her spoon at me "—takes knowing what a man is made of. So. Before I decide whether to waste a winter on you: why you? Of everyone breathing, why does the Choir want Isandro Vane?"

"I don't know."

"Liar. Try again."

I thought about lying better. Then I thought about the Vault of Silence waiting somewhere ahead, and about how truth, apparently, was going to be the whole shape of this business, and I might as well start practicing on the hard audience.

"Because my name is in a prophecy," I said. "Written a century ago. Dated tonight — well, two nights ago now. Because a silent god bound himself to me fifteen years ago and then went quiet and left me holding the silence like a debt collector's note. Because I spent those fifteen years selling other people's discarded memories in charm form while telling myself it was honest work, and it wasn't, not entirely, because I knew what I was taking when I took them." I looked up. "And because I think the reason Vethra chose me is the same reason I can't stand looking at myself some mornings, and I'd very much like to find out if that's true or if I'm just drunk on doom."

The room hummed. Gently — the ribbons on the walls shivering, the jars murmuring their labels. As if the shop itself had leaned in.

Maerin studied me for a long moment. Then she refilled my cup, which I gathered was either approval or a stay of execution.

"All right," she said. "I'll teach you."

I exhaled.

"*If,*" she continued, "you pay."

"I have coin—"

"Coin." She said the word like it had personally wronged her. "Boy, I am the last memory-keeper in Corvane and possibly the world, and you offer me *coin*. No. The price is this: one true memory of yours. Given freely, fully, and forever. Not a copy. The original. I take it out, I keep it, and you will not remember it anymore. You won't miss it — that's the mercy of the craft — but it will be gone, and gone means *gone*."

I should have asked which one. Any sensible man would have asked which one. Instead I heard myself say, "What kind of memory?"

"That's my choosing," she said. "That's what makes it a price. If you pick it yourself, it's a trade. When I pick it, it's a payment." She set down her cup and folded her hands, silver eyes level. "So think carefully before you agree, Isandro Vane. Because I'll tell you what I see, sitting here. I see a man who has arranged his life like a room he never sits in — everything useful, nothing lived-in. Whatever I take, it'll be one of the load-bearing pieces. And the house may stand anyway. But it'll creak different."

* * *

I walked home through the rain that night without deciding anything, which is my usual method, and woke the next morning having decided, which is how the method always works.

I told myself it was simple arithmetic. One memory, however heavy, against the unraveling of two realms. Against streets turning to unsung matter. Against Sera Ilyen's face when she realized what her Church had done — no, strike that, she wasn't mine to count. Against the Echo's patience running out after a hundred years of it.

One memory. People lost memories all the time. Drink took them. Grief took them. Time took nearly everything eventually; I'd just be skipping the queue.

I told myself that for most of the walk up Tanner's Row, and the rain agreed with me, and the vinegar smell agreed with me, and only the narrow stairs disagreed, each step creaking under me like a witness.

She had the tea already poured.

"You slept on it," she said.

"I slept at all. The rest was argument."

"With whom?"

"Myself. I lost." I sat in the forgiving chair. "Which memory?"

"Mine to choose when I'm inside." She knelt across from me, close, and her hands — soft, dye-stained at the nails — hovered over my temples without touching. "This is how it goes. I'll open you like a book and read until I find the page worth its weight. You'll feel me there. Most people say it's like being remembered by someone who loves you. Some people say worse. Ready?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Good. Honest men take it easier."

She touched my temples, and the room went out like a candle.

Not dark — *inward*. I was suddenly aware of my own head from the inside, vast and lamplit, and shelves there too, God help me, shelves everywhere, and a small silver-eyed figure walking among them trailing her fingers along my spines. I felt every shelf she touched. Do you understand? Every memory she brushed, I *felt brushed* — the day I learned to swim, warm and chlorinated; the scar on my knee getting made; the taste of the first coin I ever earned honestly, which took less than a second to review.

She walked slowly. She was thorough. Somewhere in there I felt her pause at a door I didn't know I had, and press her ear to it, and frown, and move on — and I would have given anything to ask what was behind it, but you can't speak in there. You can only feel.

Then she stopped at one shelf and stood before it a long time, the way you stand before a grave.

"Oh," she said, inside my skull, gentle as snowfall. "*Oh, you poor stubborn man.*"

"What?" I couldn't hear myself, but I said it anyway.

"This one," she said. "This is the one."

And she lifted it out.

I want to describe what that feels like and I can't, properly, because describing requires the vocabulary of loss and I hadn't yet lost it — it was more like watching someone lift a stone out of a river and seeing, for one impossible moment, the whole shape of the current that had bent around that stone for years. A shape, a hole, a Vane-shaped absence, and then—

The candle relit. The room. The rain. Her kneeling in front of me, hands withdrawn, and in her palms a small glass jar glowing faintly amber, sealed with wax and a ribbon, and inside it — nothing visible, and yet I knew. The jar hummed. The whole shop hummed with it, harmonizing, and I sat in my chair feeling like a house with one wall missing that hadn't noticed yet.

"There," Maerin said softly. "Paid in full."

"What did you take?"

"You agreed it was my choice."

"Maerin."

She rose, corked the jar, and set it on a high shelf between *a mother's laugh, cut short* and something labeled in that embroidered hand: *the last honest winter*. She didn't answer me. She stood with her back to me a moment, and her shoulders — broad, barrel-strong shoulders — were shaking, just slightly.

"It was a good memory," she said at last, not turning. "It was the best one I found. That's the rule, Vane. I only ever take the best. Cheap payment insults both parties." She wiped her face with her wrist, briskly, a woman closing accounts. "Now sit up straight. We start tonight. The song is long, and it doesn't forgive sloppy students, and you—" she turned, silver-eyed, fierce, and pointed the spoon at me again "—you are going to be the sloppiest student I've ever taught, because you're missing a piece, and the song is going to try to pour itself into that hole and pool there instead of learning its shape."

"What piece?" My mouth was dry. "What did you take?"

"Tonight," she said. "We begin tonight. Bring bread tomorrow — real bread, none of that saltmarket sawdust — because you'll be here every evening until you can sing it asleep, standing up, bleeding, or all three."

She swept toward the back room, and I caught one more thing before she went: the jar on the high shelf, my amber little prison, had a label already written on it in that fine embroidered script.

I couldn't read it from the chair.

But I watched her glance at it as she passed — one quick, guilty flick of the eyes, like a woman checking a wound she'd caused — and I saw the label wasn't a description at all.

It was three words, and the first one was *why*.

Outside, the rain kept falling on Corvane, steady and honest, hiding everything. And behind me, on a shelf between strangers' treasures, the thing I had traded away hummed softly to itself — patient, amber, mine — waiting, the way all songs wait, for someone to give it back.
