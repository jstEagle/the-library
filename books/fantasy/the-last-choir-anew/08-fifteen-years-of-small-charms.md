## 8. Fifteen Years of Small Charms

People think memory-keeping is a soft trade. They imagine candles, tea, gentle voices. What they don't imagine is the weight. Every memory I hold has a body to it — a temperature, a smell, a gravity. My cellar under the dyer's shop on Tanner's Row holds four hundred and eleven of them, and some nights the floorboards creak under nothing at all.

Isandro Vane came down my stairs like a man descending into his own grave, which, in fairness, is what most of them are doing.

"You're Maerin," he said. Not a question. He'd been told by someone who'd been told by someone, the way word travels about people who deal in things that can't be weighed or taxed.

"I'm Maerin," I agreed. "You're the one who wants to learn a dead woman's song."

"I want the song. The woman can stay dead."

I liked him immediately. That should have been my first warning. Liking a client is how memory-keepers end up poor, or worse, invested.

"Sit," I said, and gestured at the chair by the lamp — the good chair, the one with the arms worn smooth by forty years of people bracing themselves. "Before we talk price, you should understand what you're asking for. The covenant-song isn't a tune. It's not something you hum while you work. It was sung into being when the world still had its original stitching, and it lives in me because it lived in her first."

"Her," Isandro said. "The dead woman."

"Aldissa Vane."

I watched the name land. He didn't flinch, exactly. But something behind his eyes went very still, the way a street goes still when a pickpocket works the crowd.

"Vane," he repeated.

"Distant relation, if the records are honest, and mine are. She was the last voice of the covenant before it started coming apart. When she died, her song had nowhere to go — so it went sideways, into a girl with a head for holding things." I tapped my temple. "Me. I was nine. It's been sitting behind my left ear ever since, humming when it rains."

"That sounds unbearable."

"It pays better than dyeing cloth." I leaned forward. "Here's the shape of it, mercenary. I can teach you the song. All of it, every verse, including the ones Aldissa only sang once. But a song like this doesn't live in the ear. It lives in the part of you that remembers *why*. And I can't put it there without taking something out. Room must be made. That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the little window near the ceiling, and somewhere above us the dyer's apprentices stomped around, arguing about mordants.

"What kind of something?" he asked finally.

"One true memory. Yours, freely given, whole and unedited. I'll hold it. You won't lose it forever — but you'll lose it for now. You'll walk out of here knowing you traded something, and knowing you'll never quite know what it felt like from the inside again." I spread my hands. "Most people pick something small. A first kiss. A grandmother's kitchen. Something they can afford to be without."

"And if I pick something big?"

"Then you'd better be sure it's worth a god's song."

* * *

He didn't decide that night. He came back three times over six days, each time with a different excuse — to ask about the acoustics of my cellar, to check whether the song could be written down (it couldn't; ink refuses it), to sit in the good chair and say nothing at all. On the third visit he brought a paper charm as payment for my time, a small folded thing that was supposed to keep moths out of wool.

"It doesn't work," I said, turning it over.

"No," he agreed. "It's a decoy charm. Sells beautifully. People want to believe they've protected something."

"You sell lies shaped like protection."

"I sell comfort shaped like charms. The distinction keeps me fed." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to honesty he'd offered yet, and I noted it the way I note everything: carefully, and against future need.

On the fourth day he sat down in the good chair and said, "The day Vethra spoke to me. That's the memory. Take that one."

I should have refused. Let the record show that a smarter woman would have refused. Fifteen years old, that memory would be — fifteen years of a man's central wound, the founding stone of whatever he'd built himself out of. Taking it wasn't taking a pebble. It was taking a keystone and hoping the arch held out of politeness.

"Why that one?" I asked. "It's clearly load-bearing."

"Because if I'm going to learn why a god sang the world together, I ought to remember why I bothered listening in the first place." His jaw worked. "And because it's the only thing I own that's worth the price. Everything else is decoy charms."

There are rules in my trade, most of them unwritten because writing them down invites Inquisitors. Rule one: never take a memory the client hasn't offered three times. Rule two: never take one they cry over beforehand — tears mean the self isn't done needing it. Rule three, the one I invented myself after a bad year: never take a memory you're afraid of.

Isandro had offered it twice. He hadn't cried. And I was, I realized, afraid of it — had been since he first said *Vethra*, since the song behind my left ear had shifted in its sleep like a dog hearing its name.

I took it anyway. God forgive the curious.

* * *

The taking itself is simple, which is the cruelest thing about it. He closed his eyes. I pressed two fingers to his temple and opened the door I keep behind my sternum, and he handed the memory across the way you'd hand someone an infant: carefully, with both hands, looking away.

For a moment he sat in the chair breathing, wearing the particular blankness of a man missing a room in his own house. Then he blinked, frowned faintly, and said, "That's it?"

"That's it."

"I feel like I've lost... a coat. One I liked. I know I owned it. I can't remember wearing it." He stood, tested his balance like a sailor finding his land legs. "The song. Teach me tomorrow?"

"Come at dusk. Bring your voice and nothing else."

He climbed my stairs and let himself out, and I sat alone in the lamplight with a fifteen-year-old memory cupped in my keeping, warm as a fresh loaf, heavier than it had any right to be.

Memory-keepers aren't supposed to look inside what we hold. It's rule four, and it exists because what we hold isn't ours, and because once you've seen the inside of someone's founding moment, you can't unknow it. You carry their weight home. You start saying their name gently even when they're not there.

But the song behind my left ear was awake now, and pulling, and I have never in my life been able to resist a door that opens.

I looked.

* * *

Rain. Of course rain — Corvane is rain with buildings in it. But this was fifteen years ago, and the rain fell on a younger city, and through a boy's eyes I saw it all slightly too large, the way the young see things, everything at the scale of its consequences.

He was seventeen. Thin in the shoulders, hungry in the specific way of boys who've learned not to ask. I watched through him as he ducked under the broken arch of a shrine I recognized — everyone in Corvane knows the shrine of Vethra, or knew it, the little drowned chapel off Cutler's Lane where the god of silence kept his court. Nobody went anymore. The god hadn't spoken in years even then; the priests had drifted off; pigeons held the lease.

The boy — Isandro, though I understood he didn't say his own name to empty rooms — knelt anyway. Not to pray. To steal. There was a silver offering-bowl on the altar, green with tarnish, and I felt his reasoning arrive whole and shameless: *a god who doesn't speak doesn't need silver.*

He reached for the bowl.

And the silence moved.

I don't have a better word for it, and neither did he. The shrine's silence — which had been the ordinary kind, the pigeon-and-dust kind — gathered itself like water finding a drain, and poured into the space between the boy and the altar, and became a presence so total that his heartbeat sounded like hammering on a door.

*You take,* said the silence, and it wasn't anger. It was something worse. It was *recognition.*

The boy froze with his hand on the bowl. I felt everything he felt, because I was holding the memory and the memory was holding me: the terror, yes, but underneath it — and this is the part I'll carry to my grave — underneath the terror, a relief so vast it was indistinguishable from grief. Someone had spoken. Someone had seen him reaching, and spoken, and the speaking meant he existed.

*I take,* the boy said aloud, to a god, in a stolen voice. "What of it?"

*Everything I have left,* said Vethra. *Take it. Please.*

Please. A god saying please. I sat in my cellar with my hand pressed over my mouth like a widow at a funeral, watching a seventeen-year-old boy bargain with a drowning man.

*They will tell you I went silent by choice,* the god said, and the words came slower now, each one costing something, a coin-counted voice spending down to nothing. *They will tell you gods withdraw, that silence is our dignity. Hear me instead, boy who steals from altars: I am not silent. I am hiding. There is a price written into the covenant, and it comes due in every generation, and it is paid in—*

The memory tore.

Not faded — tore, like cloth ripped along a seam, and through the rip I saw a fraction of a second of something else: a vast dark place hung with threads of singing light, and a shape like a throat made of architecture, and then—

Then the boy's own mind slammed the door, the way minds do, the way they protect themselves from truths too large for the skull that houses them. The memory sealed itself back up around the wound and presented the tidy version: *the god spoke to me, bound me, gave me purpose, then went silent.* Fifteen years of small charms built on top of a foundation with a crack running through it.

I came back to myself on the floor of my own cellar. I don't remember getting there. The lamp had burned low. My hands were shaking, and behind my left ear the covenant-song was singing — actually singing, softly, the first time in twenty-three years it had made a sound on its own initiative — and the sound it made was not a melody.

It was a warning.

* * *

Here is what I know now that Isandro Vane does not, and may never, and what I am supposed to do with that knowledge I genuinely cannot say:

His god did not abandon him. His god was *cut off mid-sentence* — mid-warning — by something with the power to tear a memory from the inside of a mortal mind across fifteen years and the width of a city. Something that did not want the price of the covenant spoken aloud. Something that is, I would bet my cellar and every memory in it, still listening for anyone who gets close to saying it.

And I have just taken into my keeping the only complete record of the attempt.

I thought about burning it. Memory-keepers can do that — press a held memory back into nothing, unmake it. It's considered a mercy, sometimes. I sat with the option for a long time, the way you sit with a knife and a rope and consider which kindness.

But the song wouldn't stop humming, and the humming had a direction now. It pointed down the stairs, out into the rain, toward a man selling decoy charms who believed his god had left him, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I could not do it. Could not unmake the one true thing he owns.

So instead I did what memory-keepers do. I built a box around it. Warded it, triple-sealed it, sank it deep in the cellar-dark among the four hundred and eleven, where no listening thing would find it easily.

And then, because I am apparently the sort of fool who takes keystones from strangers, I began to memorize the covenant-song's final verse — the one Aldissa sang only once, the one I'd never needed until now — just in case.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Somewhere across the city, a man slept without a coat he used to own.

And somewhere deeper than the city, something that had torn a god's sentence in half turned its attention, slow as a lighthouse beam, toward a cellar on Tanner's Row — and the song behind my left ear went silent as the grave, playing dead, praying to be overlooked.

It had never gone silent before. I didn't teach it that.
