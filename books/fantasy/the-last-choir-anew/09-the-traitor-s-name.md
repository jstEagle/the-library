## 9. The Traitor's Name

There are three ways to learn that your oldest friend sold you to the Inquisition, and I've had time since to consider all of them. You can be told by a stranger, which is clean but unbelievable. You can find the ledger yourself, which is thorough but slow. Or you can walk into the Drowned Sparrow at dusk and see him sitting in your usual corner with your usual drink already poured for you, wearing the face of a man who has been rehearsing an apology for two days.

I got the third way. I don't recommend it.

The Sparrow hadn't changed. That was the cruel part. Same crooked stools, same grease-fogged lamps, same barkeep who watered the ale and pretended nobody noticed. Fifteen years of coming here after jobs, and the room still smelled like wet wool and bad decisions. My boots left rain-marks on the floorboards as I crossed it, and every step felt louder than it should have, because I already knew — some animal part of me had known since the raid — who had given them my name.

Only one person alive knew both halves of my trade. The charms I sold openly were trinkets, knotted thread and river-stone, harmless enough that even the Inquisition's clerks shrugged at them. But the real work — the quiet work, the work Vethra's old covenant let me do before the god went silent — that half lived in exactly one other head besides mine.

Doss looked up when my shadow fell across his table. He didn't flinch. That was its own kind of confession. An innocent man would have grinned, waved me over, complained about the weather. Doss just sat there with his big hands flat on the wood like a man keeping himself from reaching for something.

"Your ale's going warm," he said.

"Then you'd better drink it." I sat down across from him. "Since you bought it."

He winced. Small, quick, gone. But I'd spent fifteen years reading this man's face across campfires and card games and the aftermath of jobs gone sideways. I knew his tells better than my own. That wince was the tell he made when a blade found bone.

"They came to the shop," I said. "Burned it. Old Tammen died in the smoke — did you know that? The bookseller. Seventy-one years old, and they burned him alive over a book."

"I heard." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"Heard from who?"

"Isandro—"

"From *who*, Doss?"

The barkeep glanced over. Doss shook his head at him, barely, and the man found something fascinating about the far wall. When Doss spoke again, he'd dropped his voice to the register he used for negotiating with people who might kill him, which, historically, was most people.

"Not here."

"There's no *here* after this," I said. "Whatever we are when we leave this table is whatever we're going to be."

* * *

We ended up in the alley behind the tannery, because Doss insisted, because he said walls in the Sparrow had been bought twice this year, and because some stubborn piece of me still took his word for things. The stink was tremendous. Rendered fat and lime and old blood. Fitting, I suppose. If you're going to have your heart opened up, best do it somewhere that already smells like slaughter.

Doss paced once, twice, then stopped with his back against the wall like he needed it to hold him up.

"Tammen sent for you," he said. "The bookseller. And I know what that means, because I've known what you are for fifteen years, Isandro. Since the shrine. Since you came back different." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Three weeks ago, men came to me. Not Inquisitors — the ones behind them. Gray cloaks. They had papers. They had names, dates, places. They knew about the shrine visit fifteen years ago. They knew about the silence. And they told me they were going to pull the thread whether I helped or not, and that the only question was whether my name was attached to yours when they pulled it."

"So you gave them mine instead."

"I gave them yours so they'd stop looking for mine." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage, and then immediately looked sick about it, which was the thing about Doss — he never had the stomach to be a proper traitor. He wore guilt like a coat two sizes too small. "And there's more, and you're going to want to hit me, so maybe sit down or brace yourself or whatever you do."

I didn't sit down.

"It wasn't coin," he said. "I didn't sell you for money, Isandro, I swear it on anything you want me to swear it on. They had Perrin."

Perrin. His brother. Nineteen years old, stupid as a sack of hammers, brave in all the ways that get boys killed. Doss had carried that boy on his back through two wars' worth of mud, had paid his debts, had taken his lashes once when Perrin deserted a posting — Doss had stepped up and taken thirty strokes meant for family, and come out of it with a back like a plowed field, and never once mentioned it again.

"They arrested him three weeks ago," Doss went on. "Contraband charms — the kind I used to move, before I went straight-ish. Ravel's people. And the gray cloaks came to my door with the arrest warrant in one hand and a quill in the other, and they said: the Vane, or the boy. One name for another. And Isandro—" his voice cracked, actually cracked, this man who'd held my splinted arm straight while a field surgeon dug shrapnel out of it without flinching, "—they were going to take you anyway. You understand? The prophecy, the book, all of it — they already had the date. All I did was confirm which Vane. All I did was pick which house they burned first."

"And Tammen?"

"Tammen was going to burn regardless. I'm sorry. Gods, I'm sorry, I—" He stopped. Looked at me with those tired brown eyes that had talked me out of killing three separate men over the years. "Tell me you understand. Lie if you have to. Just tell me you understand."

Here's the terrible truth I've carried since that alley: I did understand. That was the worst part. I understood completely. I could feel the whole shape of it — the gray cloaks, the warrant, the arithmetic where one name buys one boy — and I knew, with sick certainty, that if the tables had turned, if someone had put a knife to someone Doss loved and offered him my name as ransom, I'd have paid it too. Maybe slower. Maybe with more lying to myself first. But I'd have paid.

Understanding, it turns out, doesn't help at all.

"You should have come to me," I said.

"And you'd have said yes? Walk into the gray cloaks, hand yourself over, save Perrin?"

"I'd have said *something*. We'd have figured out something. That's what we do, Doss. That's the whole fifteen years. We figure out somethings together." My voice was doing something I hated, going thin and high. "Instead you did their math for them. You made their choice easy. You handed them the one name that mattered and let them burn an old man to cover the transaction."

"He was dying anyway! The gray cloaks said—"

"The gray cloaks *said*." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You know what I keep thinking about? Not the shop. Not the book. I keep thinking about how you poured my drink tonight. Sat in my corner. Ordered my ale. You wanted to confess. You've been sitting there for two days working up to it, buying my drink, rehearsing."

"Isandro—"

"*Don't.* Don't say my name right now. You don't get to say it like we're still us."

* * *

He swung first. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. For years afterward — if there is an afterward for a man like me — I'd remember that detail and hold onto it like a rail in a storm: *he swung first.* Because what came next was ugly, and I needed the ugliness to belong to him.

It wasn't a soldier's fight. Soldiers fight clean, angles and footwork, all the things Doss taught me. This was two men who knew each other's bodies too well, in a tannery alley, in the dark, and every move either of us made, the other had seen a hundred times. He feinted low; I caught his wrist because I'd caught that exact feint outside Fort Marren eleven years ago. I drove my shoulder in; he rolled off it because he'd taught me the roll. We broke apart, circled, breathing hard, and the whole time neither of us said a word, because what was there to say that the fists weren't already saying?

He got me against the wall eventually. Big hands around my collar, my heels scraping stone, and his face close enough that I could see he was crying without making any sound, tears just running down into his beard like his body had given up on dignity entirely.

"Perrin hangs in nine days," he whispered. "Nine days. Tell me what else I could have done. *Tell me.*"

And I couldn't. That was the hell of it. I hung there in his grip with my lungs burning and I searched for the answer and it wasn't there, because there wasn't one, because the gray cloaks had built the trap so that every road out of it ran through somebody's ruin, and Doss had simply chosen whose.

"Get off me," I said.

He let go. Stepped back, hands raised, and I slid down the wall to a crouch, coughing, tasting copper.

That's when I saw it. His coat had come open in the scuffle, and there, tucked inside the lining, folded small and soft with handling — paper. A page torn from a book, edges ragged, and I knew the raggedness. I'd watched Tammen's hands tremble over that binding. The prophecy. He'd taken a page off it before the raid, or after, or during — sometime — and he'd been carrying it against his heart like it was a debt note.

"What is that?" I asked, though I knew.

His hand went to his coat. Instinct, pure animal instinct, the same motion he'd make if I'd asked about stolen coin. And that motion told me everything his words hadn't: that whatever he'd told himself about the gray cloaks, about Perrin, about picking the lesser ruin — some part of him had kept a piece of the prize. Insurance. Leverage. Or maybe just the part of him that had always been a scavenger under the loyalty, the part that pocketed rings off dead men because the dead don't need them and the living might.

"Doss. What is that."

"You don't want to know."

"I have never in my life wanted to know anything more."

He hesitated. And then — and this is the part I still can't make peace with — he *smiled*. Sad, crooked, the old smile, the one from the card games, the one that used to mean *we'll laugh about this someday.*

"Then I guess you'll have to catch me," he said.

He moved fast for a big man. I was still half-crouched, lungs full of tannery air, and by the time I got my feet under me properly he was three strides gone, boots hammering the boards toward the alley mouth, and I lunged and caught nothing but the tail of his coat — no. No, worse than nothing. The coat tore. Came away in my fist, and he kept running, lighter by one coat, and the page went with him, tucked now against his bare shirt where I could see it flapping white in the lamplight from the street.

I stood in that reeking alley holding a torn sleeve and understood, finally, the shape of my whole situation. The Inquisition had my name. The prophecy book was hidden somewhere only I knew. And the one man who'd ever known all of me was running through Corvane carrying a piece of the only map home, headed — I'd have bet my remaining charms on it — straight for the gray cloaks, to trade again. A page for a boy. It was the same math. It would always be the same math with Doss, because Doss loved people in quantities, and love in quantities can be priced.

I let him go.

Not mercy. Let's be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my year. I let him go because my ribs were singing and because chasing him meant leaving the book unguarded, and because some cold practical mercenary corner of my brain had already done the arithmetic: a page without the rest is a fragment, and fragments point. If Doss delivered it to Ravel, then Ravel would know what the prophecy pointed at — and Ravel would move, and moving things can be followed.

Besides. There was a smaller, stupider, more human reason, and I've decided to own it.

I wanted him to have to live with it.

* * *

I walked back to the Sparrow, because I didn't know where else to go, and the city was too loud with rain to hear myself think anywhere else. The barkeep had saved my stool. He set the ale in front of me without a word, and I drank it warm, and it tasted like every bad night I'd ever spent in that room, and I sat there a long time watching the door.

Because here's the thing about Doss. Even now. Even after the alley, after the torn sleeve in my fist, after the page. Some traitor-logic part of my brain kept turning it over: *he swung first, but he cried while he did it. He sold me, but he bought my drink. He kept a page, but he kept it over his heart.*

Betrayal that comes from strangers is weather. You curse it and you move on. Betrayal that comes from love is architecture. It gets into the load-bearing walls. Every memory I owned of that man — the field surgery, the lashes, the card games, the time he carried me four miles with an arrow in my thigh and sang marching songs badly the whole way to keep me awake — every single one of them now had to be re-examined, weighed, re-shelved. Was the arrow-carrying real? Was any of it real? Or had I spent fifteen years loving a man who was always, underneath, the kind of creature who does arithmetic with people?

And the answer, which I arrived at somewhere around the third warm ale, is the one I didn't want: it was all real. That's what makes it unbearable. He carried me because he loved me, and he sold me because he loved his brother more, and both of those things are true in the same man at the same time, and there is no version of the world where one cancels the other out.

Love isn't a virtue. I'd thought it was, once, back when I believed in silent gods and simple ledgers. Love is just a force. Like gravity. It holds up bridges and it brings down buildings, and it doesn't care which.

The door banged open near midnight and a woman walked in shaking rain off a gray traveling cloak, and I knew her instantly from Tammen's description — the archivist, the Inquisitor's hound with the doubting eyes, Sera Ilyen. She scanned the room, found me, and crossed to my stool with the unhurried certainty of someone who has already decided how this goes.

"Isandro Vane," she said. It wasn't a question.

"That depends who's asking, and what happens to the answer."

"Sera Ilyen. Inquisitorial Archive." She sat down uninvited, laid a folded paper on the bar between us, and didn't touch her drink. "Your friend Doss delivered a page to the Cathedral an hour ago. High Inquisitor Ravel has it now. And whatever you think is coming — whatever you've braced for — it's worse than that. I've read the margins of that prophecy, Vane. My grandmother wrote them. And I need to show you what she found, before Ravel uses that page to open something that doesn't close."

The rain hammered the windows. Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled midnight, and beneath it — faint, almost imagined, the way a sound can be when you've started listening for it — I swear I heard something hum.
