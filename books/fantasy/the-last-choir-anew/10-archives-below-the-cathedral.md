## 10. Archives Below the Cathedral

The seal on the archive door was older than my orders, and it was meant to be broken only by someone who had already stopped believing.

I know that sounds like heresy dressed as poetry. It isn't. It's a practical observation. The door sat at the bottom of a stair that began behind the reliquary of Saint Aldemar — a saint whose miracles, I noticed as I passed his gilded bones, were all recorded in a hand suspiciously similar to the Church's official chronicler of the same century. The stair went down in tight spirals, each step worn concave by feet that had come this way rarely and reluctantly. At the bottom hung a lock of black iron with no keyhole, only a depression shaped like a hand.

You pressed your palm to it and it read you. Not your blood. Your *certainty*. That's what the old texts said, the ones we archivists were allowed to read: *the door opens to the faithful and bars the doubter.* I'd believed that once, the way you believe the floor will hold.

I stood there in the dark with my lantern and my grandmother's name in my mouth, and I pressed my hand into the cold iron, and I thought — honestly, plainly, without drama — *I don't know anymore.*

*Click.*

So much for the door's theology. Either it was broken after four hundred years, or it had always been lying about itself, or — and this possibility arrived late and unwelcome — the door had been built by people who understood that doubt is not the opposite of faith but its engine, and that anyone who came down these stairs at all would need to be let in eventually, because they'd finally started asking questions worth answering.

The hinges didn't scream. They exhaled, long and dry, like something that had been holding its breath since before my grandmother was born.

* * *

The archives below the Cathedral are not what novices imagine. No towering shelves, no drifting dust motes golden in shafts of light. The light down here doesn't drift; it waits. My lantern made a small stubborn circle and everything beyond it was a held breath. The air tasted of paper and stone and something underneath both — an old sweetness, like wax that had melted over candles burned for purposes no one recorded.

I knew the layout from catalogues. Wing One: canonical histories, approved and annotated until approval became architecture. Wing Two: liturgical records, tithing ledgers, the bureaucratic skeleton of faith. Wing Three was sealed even from sealed things — the catalogue listed it as "structural, no holdings," which is archivist-speak for *we are not telling you*, and every archivist knows that phrase is a map.

Wing Three it was.

Her notes had led me here. Grandmother Ilyen — Sera the elder, my namesake, dead eleven years and buried with full honors as a servant of the Church whose life had been, according to her memorial plaque, "a lamp of orthodoxy." I had grown up on that plaque. I had become an Inquisitor partly because of it, if I'm honest, because children want to be lamps too.

Then last week, sorting the bequest of another dead archivist, I had found a folio misfiled under "miscellaneous devotional" that was anything but devotional. Marginalia in a hand I knew better than my own — I'd learned my letters tracing hers, sitting on her knee with a quill too big for my fist. Her handwriting crawled all over a copy of the Vethrine covenant fragments, and in the margin of the last page, in letters small enough to hide in the binding groove:

*They will send you below when you start asking. Go to Wing Three. Ask who wrote the laws. Then forgive me, or don't.*

My grandmother had known. Had known I'd doubt, had known where doubt would lead, had left a note like a hand reaching back across eleven years of silence. And she had written *forgive me*, which meant she had done something requiring forgiveness, which meant —

Which meant I should stop standing in the doorway theorizing and walk in.

* * *

Wing Three smelled different. Not older — *truer*. The approved wings smelled of incense applied like perfume over sweat; this smelled of the sweat itself. Plain shelving, unadorned, packed with folios in no liturgical order at all. Chronological, maybe. Or by *author*. I pulled a spine at random and the title stopped my breath:

*Minutes of the Convocation on the Regulation of Unlicensed Song.* Dated one hundred and twelve years ago.

One hundred and twelve years ago is not scripture time. It is not ancient history. It is *great-grandparents* time. We teach the outlawing of magic as though it descended from the heavens with the rest of divine law — *the Choir's power belongs to the gods alone; mortal song is theft* — an eternal principle, handed down whole. But here was a meeting. Here were minutes. Here were men arguing about it around a table, which means there had been a moment when it was *not yet law*, which means it was made, and things that are made have makers, and makers have reasons, and reasons can be read.

I sat on the cold floor with my skirts pooled around me and read by lantern light like a thief, which is what I now was, technically, and found I couldn't muster the appropriate shame. Shame requires a stable self to attach to.

The minutes were dull in the way that damns. Committees are never more honest than when they're bored.

*Item the fourth:* reports of covenant decay in the eastern provinces. The Choir's manifestations diminishing. Shrines going quiet. The assembled fathers agreed this represented a crisis of *order*, not of faith — the covenant between realms was failing on its own, and when it failed fully, the laws magic held together would fail with it. Weather. Healing. The mending of walls. All of it hung on the covenant's continued existence.

*Item the seventh:* Brother Cassian proposes that rather than mourn the decay, the Church should *manage* it. If the covenant was dying anyway, then the powers leaking through its cracks could be attributed to the Church's own sacraments. Let the shrines go silent; claim the silencing. Let the healing wells run dry; sell bottled blessings. "A god withdrawing," Cassian had written, "is an opportunity for a church that does not flinch."

*Item the ninth:* the vote. Carried. Of course carried. And beneath the vote, in a later hand — a summary, a decision distilled: *the anti-magic edicts to be drafted and presented as ancient doctrine. Provenance to be obscured. Authorship to be attributed to the First Synod.*

Forged. Not forged in malice, even. Forged in *logistics*. They needed the people to stop seeking the fading gods before the fading became visible panic, so they invented a law older than memory forbidding the seeking, and then they enforced it with fire for a century because the lie required maintenance.

I set the minutes down. My hands were steady, which felt obscene. Somewhere above me people were praying to doctrines that were younger than some bridges.

And then I saw the second name in the record of the drafting committee, repeated again and again in the margins, the one assigned provenance — the one tasked with making the forgery look ancient, with aging ink and inventing lineage.

*Ilyen.*

Not grandmother. Great-great, maybe more — the family name like a thread running down through the generations into this room. My family hadn't just helped. My family had been the *craftsmen*. The forgers of antiquity. We had built the lie's bones and then spent a century serving as the lie's enforcement arm, Inquisitors generation after generation, burning books that might contradict a document our own blood had aged in vinegar and smoke.

Grandmother's marginalia floated up in my memory: *forgive me, or don't.* She had known because she had found this too. She had gone down these stairs, read these minutes, traced her own name through the forgery — and then climbed back up and served forty more years and raised my mother orthodox and put a lamp-plaque on her own grave.

That was the part that dropped the floor out from under me. Not the lie. Lies I could survive; I'd half suspected the shape of one for months. What broke something in my chest was realizing she had *chosen the lie again*, knowingly, at the end — and had still left me the map. Love and cowardice in the same handwriting. She couldn't defy the Church and couldn't quite bury the truth either, so she'd buried it *for me instead*, at a depth only a doubting granddaughter would dig.

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet and short, echoing off shelves of condemned truth. Somewhere in the dark a draft moved through the stacks like something turning over in its sleep.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the lantern drank most of its oil. Long enough that I stopped cataloguing my outrage and started doing the thing archivists do when grief has nowhere to go: I worked.

If the edicts were forged, the forgery had an original somewhere — the true doctrine it displaced, or the evidence of the covenant it denied. A cover-up leaves shadows. You forge a provenance, you must destroy the competing documents, and destroyed documents get *listed* — suppression manifests, burn orders, the accounting of erasure. Erasure is fastidious. Erasure keeps receipts.

Wing Three, far shelf, bottom row: a box marked only *EDICTS — SOURCE MATERIALS — DO NOT CIRCULATE*, which is the archival equivalent of a scream.

Inside: three items.

First, a fragment of true covenant-doctrine, pre-Convocation, beautiful and strange, describing the covenant as *a song given willingly, voice for voice, generation upon generation* — and I stopped reading there, because that phrase snagged on something Isandro Vane had said to me in a rain-soaked alley two nights ago, something I'd dismissed as heretic poetry. *It's not theft. It was never theft. Someone paid for it.*

Second, a burn manifest. Pages of titles, each with a date and a location and a signature authorizing destruction. Ilyen signatures. Generations of them, marching down the ledger like a family business.

Third — and my hands actually shook on this one, finally, the steadiness giving way — a single loose page, newer than the rest, in my grandmother's hand. Not marginalia this time. A proper note, folded, addressed: *For whoever comes next. There is usually a whoever.*

She'd written it knowing she'd serve the lie to her death, knowing she'd leave the truth to a stranger or a descendant. Inside, her script was hurried, urgent in a way her careful archival hand never was:

*The edicts are ours. The burning is ours. But listen to me — the covenant is not what the Convocation said it was, and it is not what the heretics say either. It is a debt being paid in voices, and it is falling due. Ravel knows more than he preaches. He has been below. Watch what Ravel does, not what Ravel says. And if a man named Vane crosses your path —*

The page ended. Not faded, not damaged. Ended, mid-sentence, as if she'd been interrupted, or as if she'd stopped herself, or as if she'd realized she was writing instructions for something she couldn't bear to finish imagining.

Vane. She had known the name. Eleven years ago, dead and honored and lying, she had known the name of a mercenary who tonight is somewhere in this city with a prophecy drinking his blood.

Above me, faint through fathoms of stone, the cathedral bell began to ring — not the hour, not the office bells. An alarm pattern. Three strikes, pause, three strikes. Raid called. Fire authorized.

And the target, when the criers' echoes confirmed it down the stairwell a minute later, was a bookshop in the Tanner's Ward belonging to a dying man named Oviden, who was reported to be harboring a document bearing *the Vane*.

Ravel wasn't hunting heresy tonight. Ravel was hunting a prophecy — and I now suspected he had read my grandmother's warning from the other side, and intended to make sure no one else ever did.
