## 12. An Inquisitor's Bargain

I had been an Inquisitor for eleven years, and in all that time I had learned exactly one universal truth about fugitives: they always go back for their belongings. Pride, sentiment, stupidity — call it what you like, but the pattern held. Men who had abandoned homes, names, and entire lives would still risk the noose for a spare shirt or a lucky charm.

So when I wanted to find Isandro Vane, I didn't hunt him. I hunted his habits.

The safehouse on Tanner's Row was one of four I'd mapped from the raid records — the ones that hadn't burned, because the raid records that mattered had been sealed by the same office that sealed everything else that mattered. I'd spent two nights in the archives below the Cathedral before coming here, and I will be honest with you, whoever you are, reading this: I had come out of those archives a different woman. Not shattered. Worse. *Reorganized.* The kind of different where you keep all your pieces but they no longer fit together in the shape they used to make.

I sat in the dark of the safehouse's back room for six hours, on a crate that smelled of tallow and old rain, with my grandmother's handwriting folded against my heart like a knife I hadn't decided how to hold.

At the seventh hour, the front door opened.

* * *

He came in the way a man comes home — which is to say, carelessly, because he believed the place was empty. I heard him set something down. Glass clinking. Charm stock. He'd gone back for his belongings after all. The universal truth held, and I felt a small, tired satisfaction that the world still obeyed at least one law.

I let him get both feet through the doorway of the back room. Then I lit the lamp.

"Evening, Vane."

He didn't startle. I'll give him that. He went very still instead, the way animals go still, and his eyes did a fast, professional inventory of the room — my position, the lamp, the door behind him, the window, the distance to each. I'd seen that same calculation in a hundred faces. Usually it ended with a run.

"Archivist," he said. "Sera Ilyen, right? The one who reads prophecy books like bedtime stories."

"The one whose job it is to bring you in." I stood off the crate, slow, hands visible. "Allegedly."

"Allegedly." He tasted the word. "That's a strange qualifier from someone holding a lamp and sitting in the dark of my house for seven hours."

"Six and a half. I arrived late." I turned the lamp up a notch so he could see my face clearly, see that I wasn't wearing the grey-and-silver of the Inquisition but plain wool, plain boots, no badge. "If I were here to arrest you, we'd both already know. There'd be six of them and one of you, and you'd already be bleeding."

"That's not the reassurance you think it is."

"It's not meant to reassure you. It's meant to establish that I'm alone, unarmed, and lying to you about nothing except my competence, which is considerable."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of one, the way a scar is the ghost of a wound. "Then say what you came to say, Archivist. I've had a long month, and I'm told it gets worse."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that everything I did next was against my training, my oath, my orders, and — I strongly suspected — my own long-term survival. I did it anyway. My grandmother's handwriting had a great deal to answer for.

"You found the Vault of Silence," I said.

His stillness came back, but different now. Sharper. "Say that again, quietly."

"The Vault beneath the old shrine district. The first door. The fragment of Truth that refuses everyone who comes to it with a lie in their mouth." I watched him. "You went. It refused you. You've been walking around Corvane for two days looking like a man who's been flayed and reassembled by someone in a hurry. I know, because I've been following you, and you're getting worse at noticing."

"I noticed you at the fish market."

"You noticed a beggar at the fish market. I was the woman buying herring I didn't want." I let that land. "The Vault has a second door, Vane. The first one tests. The second one *opens* — but only for someone who comes bearing proof. And the Church holds the only key that fits its lock, because the Church built the lock, because the Church built the Vault, and because the Church has been lying about all of it for a hundred years."

That last sentence I delivered evenly, without drama, the way you hand someone a live coal: quickly, and with the understanding that they can drop it or hold it, but either way it's theirs now.

He didn't drop it. He set it down carefully and looked at me. "You're an Inquisitor."

"I was."

"Were."

"I spent last week in the sealed archives under the Cathedral," I said, "reading documents that my own grandmother helped write. Documents that show the anti-magic laws weren't passed to protect people from the Choir. They were passed to protect the Church from the *covenant*. The Choir didn't break faith with us, Vane. We broke faith with it, on purpose, in a room full of very important men who then wrote themselves into history as saviors." I took a breath. "And then the Church spent a century burning anyone who remembered otherwise."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the rain had started again, soft against the shutters, the way Corvane's rain always sounds — apologetic, and insincere about it.

"You expect me to believe you," he said at last, "because you read a book."

"I expect you to believe me because you have no one else. Because your god went silent, your friend sold you, your charms are ash, and the only person in this city who knows what's actually happening is standing in front of you telling you the truth at the cost of everything she has." I heard my own voice go quieter. "I'm not asking for belief, Vane. I'm asking for a trade."

"A trade."

"You want into the Vault's second door. I can get you there — the key, the route, the seals, all of it. In exchange, you prove it to me."

"Prove what?"

"That the Choir isn't what we say it is." I stepped closer, and I watched his hand not move toward the knife at his belt, which took visible discipline. "My whole life, I was taught the gods are thieves. That their song is a hook and their realm is a mouth. That every miracle is a debt and every blessing is bait. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you — not because you've examined it, but because you'd fall apart if you didn't." My hand went, without my permission, to the folded pages over my heart. "Then I found my grandmother's handwriting in the margins of a forbidden prophecy, and the floor went out from under me, and I have been falling for six days. So here is my bargain, mercenary. Take me with you. Show me the Choir, the Echo, the fragments, all of it. Show me it isn't a mouth. And if it *is* — if you're carrying the end of the world under your arm like a stolen loaf — then I'll put you down myself, and I'll sleep afterward."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain. Lamp-hiss. The particular silence of a man deciding how much of himself to spend.

"You know what the first door showed me?" he said finally.

"I couldn't—"

"Everything I've lied to myself about. In order. With footnotes." He laughed, and it was a bad sound, a sound with rust in it. "Fifteen years, I told myself I took Vethra's covenant because I was chosen. Chosen. Like it was an honor." He looked at the lamp instead of at me. "I took it because I was fifteen and hungry and my father was dead and the god was the only thing that ever looked at me like I was worth something. The Vault showed me that. It showed me that everything I've built since — the charms, the anonymity, the quiet little life — is one long apology to a boy who just wanted to be seen." He looked up. "That's what Truth does, Archivist. It doesn't kill you. It just makes you live in the house it renovates."

"And you still want the second door."

"I still want the second door."

"Then we understand each other." I held out my hand. "You prove the Choir is worth saving. I prove the Church isn't. Between us, we might assemble something that resembles the actual truth, which is more than either of our institutions has managed in a century."

He looked at my hand like it might be a trap. Which was fair. It had been, in other people's mouths, for eleven years.

"Why not just arrest me?" he asked. "Ravel would make you Archivist-Prime. You'd have the whole archive. Every sealed book. Every answer."

"Because Ravel doesn't want answers." I said it flat, and I watched it hit him. "You've read the raid reports. You've seen how fast they moved on the bookseller — faster than the tip should have allowed. You've noticed the Church isn't trying to *contain* the Choir's collapse, it's trying to *reach* it. I've been in the archives, Vane. There are requisitions down there for ritual components that only make sense if you're trying to open a door from this side. The Church doesn't want to kill the gods. It wants to *replace* them. And it needs a named mortal with covenant-blood to do it." I let my hand stay where it was. "You're not a fugitive to them. You're a component."

The color went out of his face in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with arithmetic. I could see him doing it — adding up the raids, the tipped-off locations, the too-precise knowledge of his trade. Someone had been feeding the Inquisition more than his name.

"You knew that," he said slowly, "and you came alone anyway."

"I came alone because if I'd brought anyone, I'd be delivering you to Ravel in a cage, and I'd rather be hanged." I shrugged, and it cost more than the shrug showed. "My grandmother spent her life helping build the lie. I've decided to spend mine taking it apart. It's not noble. It's bookkeeping. Someone has to balance the ledger."

He took my hand.

His grip was a mercenary's grip — dry, calloused, brief, the handshake of a man who has learned that held hands are stabable hands — but he held it one second longer than the arithmetic required, and I understood that this was the whole of his trust, given all at once, against every instinct, the way you hand a stranger your only coin because the alternative is being alone in the dark with it.

"Conditions," he said.

"Naturally."

"You don't report to anyone. Not the Cathedral, not your confessor, not a diary with a very trusting lock. If we do this, we do it as two people, not an Inquisitor and her asset."

"Agreed. And you don't lie to me. Not about the Choir, not about the fragments, not about what they cost. I've had enough of institutions that hide the price of things."

Something crossed his face at that — fast, like a fish under ice — and I would spend a long time afterward wondering what it was. At the time I filed it as grief. It was, I think, guilt. He already knew what the fragments cost. He'd paid the first installment.

"Then we have a bargain, Archivist."

"Sera. If we're two people, use the name."

"Sera." He said it carefully, like a word in a language he was relearning. "The second door. When?"

"Tomorrow night. The Vault's seals renew at moonset, and the key —" I patted my coat, where a plain iron thing sat in an inner pocket, warm from my body, heavier than iron has any right to be — "the key only works for a few hours. After that, we'd be explaining ourselves to a locked door in a hole under the city, and Ravel's people would find our skeletons eventually and be very confused."

"Tomorrow night, then." He released my hand and looked at the lamp, the rain, the little ruined life of his safehouse. "You should know something before you commit."

"Tell me."

"Whoever sold my name to the Inquisition — I found him. He's my oldest friend. He did it to buy his brother's life, and I'd have done the same, and I hate that I'd have done the same, and he got away with a page of the prophecy." His jaw worked. "A page that's now in Inquisitor hands. So whatever your Church is planning, they have more of the map than we do. And the man who gave it to them thinks he's saving someone."

The rain picked up. I thought about my grandmother's neat, dutiful hand in the margin of a holy lie, and about how every catastrophe in history is just a good intention that met the wrong machinery.

"Then we'd best be faster than good intentions," I said.

He almost smiled. For real, this time. It was, I noticed, the first one I'd seen on him, and it changed his whole face — took ten years off and put ten years of exhaustion back on, simultaneously, which should not be possible and was.

"Get some sleep," I said, moving to the window to take the last watch, because old habits and because someone had to. "Tomorrow we rob the Church of its own door."

He grunted — the eloquent grunt of a man too tired for words — and I heard him settle onto the cot, boots on, knife in reach, the way mercenaries sleep, which is to say: not really.

I watched the rain come down on Tanner's Row and thought about doors. The Church's. The Vault's. The one my grandmother must have stood before, a century ago, holding her pen, deciding what to write.

Tomorrow we'd open one of them. And I had the sudden, cold certainty — the kind that archivists learn to trust above all documents — that whatever came through it had already begun moving.

Far off, past the rooftops, past the Cathedral spires, the sky over the old shrine district did something the sky is not supposed to do.

It flickered.
