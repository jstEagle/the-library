## 13. Doss Sings First

The page weighed less than a dead man's conscience.

I know because I've carried both. The page I'd torn from Isandro's prophecy book — careful tear, clean edge, the kind of thing you can tell yourself wasn't really theft because you left the rest of the book intact — sat inside my coat against my chest like a coal that wouldn't cool. And the other weight, the older one, was the memory of Garrick's face when they took him. My brother had smiled at me from the Inquisition cart. Smiled. As if being arrested were some joke between us, as if I'd already promised to fix it and he believed me.

I had promised. That's the thing about promises. They don't care what it costs to keep them, and neither do brothers.

So there I was, standing in the antechamber of the Cathedral of the Unbroken Word at an hour when honest men are asleep, waiting to hand over the only leverage in Corvane worth more than my brother's freedom, telling myself the same lie on a loop: *This is simple. This is trade. This is what men do.*

Isandro would have laughed at me. Not cruelly — that was always the worst part about him, that his laughter came with no teeth in it. He'd have said something like, *Doss, you great sentimental fool, you sold your soul for a man who'd have done it for free.* And he'd be right, and I'd have bought him a drink anyway, because that's how fifteen years of friendship works: you insult each other honestly and pay each other's tabs silently.

Except I hadn't sold my soul. Souls are grand. I'd sold something smaller and more permanent — his name, written in my own hand, on Church paper, with Church wax sealing it shut behind him.

The clerk who finally opened the inner door looked at me the way clerks look at mud tracked across clean floors.

"Name?"

"Doss Merren." Then, because apparently I wanted the sin notarized: "I'm here to see High Inquisitor Ravel. About the Vane."

Something changed in the clerk's face. Not respect. Hunger wearing respect's coat. He stepped aside so fast I nearly walked into the doorframe.

* * *

Ravel's study smelled of ink and cold iron, which struck me as honest, at least. Most powerful men prefer the smell of incense or money. This one worked in a room that smelled like instruments.

He was smaller than I expected. You build up these figures in your head — the High Inquisitor, the voice of the Church, the man whose writ could burn a street — and then he turns out to be a narrow, grey-faced fellow with ink-stained fingers and eyes that don't blink so much as shutter, like a shop closing for the night. He didn't offer me a seat.

"You have something."

Not a question. Good. Questions invite conversation, and conversation is where liars drown.

"A page from the Vane's book." I set it on his desk, flat, both hands steady — I'd practiced that in front of a cracked mirror, gods help me, practicing steadiness like a boy rehearsing a confession. "The original prophecy. There's more to the book, but this is the heart of it."

Ravel didn't touch it. He read it the way I'd seen butchers read meat, top to bottom, weighing.

"And the mercenary? Isandro Vane. Where is he?"

"Alive. Running. He has the book." I hesitated, and hated myself for hesitating, because hesitation is just fear wearing a thoughtful expression. "High Inquisitor, my brother—"

"Garrick Merren. Condemned under the Edict of Silence, sentence commuted pending—" Ravel waved a hand, and somewhere behind those shuttered eyes a ledger turned its own page. "Yes. Your arrangement stands. The name was genuine; the raid found what we expected to find. Your brother will be released within the week."

*Within the week.* Four words. Four words for fifteen years of friendship, for a man who once pulled me out of a canal with a hook through his own arm, for every campfire and bad wine and shared blanket in a frozen pass. Four words, and I felt lighter and filthier than I had ever felt in my life, like a man scrubbed clean in dirty water.

"That's all," Ravel said, dismissing me the way you'd shoo a cat off a table.

But I didn't go. Here's the thing nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't end at the transaction. You stand there afterward, holding your payment, waiting for someone to tell you it was worth it, and when they don't — when they just turn back to their papers like you're already forgotten — you find yourself lingering. Wanting to matter. Even as the knife, even as the coin-purse, wanting to matter.

"What will you do with it?" I asked. "The page."

Ravel looked up. And for the first time he smiled, and I learned something worse about him than any rumor: the smile was warm. Genuinely, terribly warm.

"Do you believe in the Choir, Master Merren?"

"I believe in getting paid," I said. "And in my brother."

"A practical man. Then let me be practical back." He folded the page with a gentleness I found obscene. "The Church teaches that magic is theft. That the old covenant made mortals beggars at the gods' table. That is what we teach the faithful, and most of them need it to be true, so true it becomes." He tapped the folded page against the desk, twice, three times, a metronome. "But the truth, Master Merren, is simpler and better. Magic isn't theft. It's *currency*. And for a century we have been a nation without a mint."

I understood then — not everything, not yet, the full horror was still an hour away — but I understood the shape of it. You know that moment when a friend tells you a joke and you laugh before you get it, and then you get it, and the laugh curdles in your throat? It was like that. Except the joke was my life.

"You're not going to destroy the Choir," I said.

"We're going to open it." Ravel said it the way other men say *good morning*. "Carefully. Deliberately. Under Church authority. The gods sang reality into being, and then they locked the songhouse and threw away the key — or one of them did, the silent one, the coward. We intend to pick the lock. And everything that flows out of that door flows through us."

*Through us.* Through the Church. Through Ravel. Power, unmediated, unapportioned, flowing into the hands of men who'd spent a century teaching everyone else that reaching for power was heresy.

And I thought of Isandro, running through the rain with a burning book under his coat, believing — because why wouldn't he believe it, everyone believes it — that the Inquisition wanted the magic dead. He didn't know they wanted it alive. Tamed, collared, milked. Alive the way livestock is alive.

"Will it hurt anyone?" I asked, and immediately wished I hadn't, because the question marked me. Practical men don't ask that.

Ravel studied me for a long moment with those shuttering eyes. "Everything worthwhile hurts someone," he said kindly. "That's how you know it's worthwhile. Go home, Master Merren. Collect your brother. Forget this room."

I went home. I did not forget the room. I lay awake listening to the rain and telling myself the new lie, the updated model, the deluxe edition: *Nobody gets hurt. He said carefully. He said deliberately. Men like that don't break things they want to own.*

Men like that don't break things they want to own.

I fell asleep repeating it like a prayer, and prayers, as any god will tell you, are just lies you say on your knees.

* * *

They came for me three days later.

Not with chains — worse, with courtesy. Two acolytes in dove-grey, hats in hand, standing in my doorway with the terrible politeness of the Church when it wants something. "The High Inquisitor requests your presence. He asks that you bring the rest of the book if you can arrange it."

"The deal was the name," I said. "The name bought my brother. We're square."

"Your brother," said the elder acolyte, gently, "is currently enjoying our hospitality in the lower vaults. Release papers take time. Signatures. Verification." He smiled with all the warmth of a locked door. "The High Inquisitor feels your continued assistance would expedite matters considerably."

You see how it works now? Of course you do. You probably saw it before I did — everyone sees it except the man inside it. The deal was never a deal. It was a hook, and hooks don't stop working just because the fish thinks the transaction concluded. Garrick was never going to walk out on four words. Garrick was bait now, and I was the line, and Ravel held the rod with those ink-stained fingers and waited for me to swim where he pointed.

I swam. Let's be clear about that. Whatever else this account is, it isn't an apology dressed as a story. I got the rest of the book — that part I'll carry to whatever afterlife will have me, and most of them won't. Isandro trusted me with his hiding places since we were nineteen years old, and I used every one of them, and I told myself *Garrick, Garrick, Garrick* the way a drowning man says a saint's name, and it worked exactly as well as prayers work.

When I put the book in Ravel's hands, he actually thanked me. That was the worst of it, up to that point. He clasped my shoulder like a father.

"You've served the faith well, Master Merren. Both of you will be remembered."

Both of us. He meant Garrick too. He meant it kindly. I want that on the record: the man destroying everything meant it kindly.

* * *

They performed the ritual that night, in the Vault beneath the Cathedral, and Ravel — gods rot him — invited me to watch.

"You should see what you helped build," he said, and I couldn't tell if it was cruelty or sincerity, and I've decided since that with Ravel the two were never separate rooms, just different lighting in the same one.

The Vault was round, carved from black stone that drank the lamplight, and the prophecy pages were laid out on the floor in a pattern I didn't recognize — not a circle, circles are safe, this was more like a wound. A spiral. Acolytes stood at intervals chanting the liturgy of silence, which even I knew was wrong, was backwards, was using a lockpick shaped like a hymn. And Ravel stood at the center holding the page I'd torn — the heart of it, the date, the name — reading aloud in a language that predated the Church by a thousand years, pronouncing it beautifully, fluently, like a man reading love letters he'd stolen.

The air began to hum.

Not sound, exactly. Sound has direction, source, manners. This was pressure. It came up through the floor and down through the ceiling and sideways through the walls, and my teeth ached with it, and the lamp flames stood perfectly still while everything else trembled. Above us — above the whole city — I felt the sky remember it was a ceiling over something vaster.

And then the Vault tore.

There's no honest word for it. The air split along a seam that hadn't existed, and through it came light that wasn't light and sound that was almost music — fragments of melody hanging in nothing, architecture suspended on chords, pillars built from sustained notes, a whole realm bleeding through the cut like yolk through a cracked shell. Beautiful. I'll say that even now, even here. It was beautiful the way a forest fire is beautiful from the far side of a river, and I was not on the far side of any river.

An acolyte screamed. Something had touched her — a stray note, a loose thread of song — and where it touched, she *changed*, briefly, horribly: her outline flickered, unsung, like a candle flame in wind, before the chant caught her again and stitched her back into ordinary flesh. She collapsed, sobbing, whole, mostly.

"Steady!" Ravel called, delighted, delighted, his face lit from below by the wound in the world, and I watched the hunger on him and finally, finally understood what I had fed. "Wider. Hold the seam wider!"

I grabbed his sleeve. I don't remember deciding to; my hand just went, the way a hand goes toward a stove. "You said carefully. You said deliberate. There are people up there—" I meant the city, Corvane, forty thousand souls sleeping under that flickering sky. "You're not picking a lock, you're gutting something alive—"

Ravel looked at my hand on his sleeve. Then at me. And his kindness, that awful warm kindness, didn't waver at all.

"Master Merren. Everything that lives can be farmed." He said it patiently, instructively, like explaining rain to a child. "The Choir is a field. The gods abandoned the harvest. We are simply... taking up the tools they left rusting." He turned back to the seam. "Your brother's papers are signed, by the way. First light. You may go home."

First light. Dismissed. Paid. Free.

I stood in the Vault of the Cathedral of the Unbroken Word, in the wound I had helped cut, watching a realm of singing stone bleed into my city, and I understood at last the full architecture of what I'd done — not the betrayal, that I'd understood all along, betrayal is simple arithmetic — but the *use*. Isandro's name. My hand. Garrick's freedom. Every piece of it had been a tool in a harvest I'd been told was a rescue, and the man holding the sickle had thanked me, warmly, sincerely, and meant it.

Good intentions. Mine, I mean. They'd been real — that's the trap nobody warns you about, that your intentions can be genuinely good and still be rope in someone else's hands. Love-shaped, Isandro would have called it, if Isandro ever spoke to me again, which he won't, which is correct.

Above us, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the bells of Corvane began to ring — all of them, unscheduled, unstruck, ringing because the sky was flickering and the city below felt it in its sleep and cried out without knowing it cried.

Ravel raised his arms into the light pouring from the seam, and the light bent toward him, eager, hungry, and he laughed — softly, warmly, the kindest sound I have ever been present for and the worst.

And far off, through the tearing sky, I heard — or imagined, I still pray I imagined — a single voice begin to sing back.
