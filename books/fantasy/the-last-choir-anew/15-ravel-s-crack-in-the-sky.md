## 15. Ravel's Crack in the Sky

*Isandro*

I want it on the record that I did not want to be standing in Cathedral Square when the sky broke. Nobody wants that. There is no list of reasonable life goals that includes *witness a cosmological catastrophe in person*, and yet here is how I ended up front row anyway.

Sera had dragged me there — or rather, she had walked there with the particular stride of a woman who believed she could still stop this, and I had followed because the alternative was being alone with my thoughts, which after the Vault of Silence was roughly as pleasant as sleeping on broken glass.

"They'll do it at noon," she'd said. "Ravel likes an audience."

"He gets one," I said. "Corvane never misses a hanging."

The square was full by the eleventh bell. Bakers with flour still on their sleeves. A pickpocket working the crowd with the serene confidence of a man who had not yet looked up. Children on their fathers' shoulders. All of them come to see the High Inquisitor perform a miracle, or a murder, or whatever you called it when a man in white-and-gold climbed a scaffold of consecrated oak holding a single torn page like it was a knife he'd sharpened for thirty years.

I knew that page. Half of page nine, the prophecy's margin-notes, the part that named the *doorway* rather than the door. Doss must have copied it true. My oldest friend, my brother-in-everything-but-blood, had handed the Church the one piece of scripture they'd spent a century failing to find.

And Ravel — Ravel was not reading from it. He had memorized it. That frightened me more than anything else in the square. Men who read rituals are following instructions. Men who recite them have made peace with what the instructions do.

"People of Corvane," Ravel said, and his voice carried the way only voices paid to carry can. "For one hundred years we told you magic was theft. We were wrong. It is not theft." He smiled. I felt my stomach drop through the cobblestones. "It is *inheritance*. And today we collect."

He began to sing.

Not well. That's the thing nobody tells you about men like Ravel — they don't need to sing well, because they've got a hundred years of stolen belief banked behind them, every terrified parishioner who ever whispered that the Inquisition must be right because look what happens to people who say otherwise. The song came out flat and grinding, a hymn run through a millstone, but the air above the scaffold began to *thin*, the way water thins over a drain.

"No," Sera said beside me. Quietly. Like a woman watching her own funeral procession. "No, that's not — the notes are wrong, he's inverted the third phrase, that's not opening, that's *tearing*—"

"Sera." I caught her arm. "Whatever you're about to do, do it after we're not standing directly underneath it."

She didn't move. Above us, the sky split.

I don't have language for it that doesn't sound like a drunk describing a shipwreck, so here is the honest version: the sky cracked along a seam that had always been there, the way you only see the join in a wall once the plaster gives. Through it came light that wasn't light — it was *sound*, visible sound, chords the size of weather rolling out of the wound in the world. And where those chords touched Corvane, Corvane stopped agreeing to exist.

The fountain went first. Not shattered — *unmade*. One moment it was stone and water, the next it was nothing at all, not even absence, just a patch of street where the eye refused to land. Then the baker's awning across the square, unraveling into threads of unsung matter that drifted upward like ash falling in reverse.

The crowd screamed. The crowd ran. The pickpocket dropped everything he'd lifted and ran faster than anyone, which I respected professionally.

"Maerin," I said.

Sera stared at me.

"The memory-hold. Her shop is four streets from here. If the Choir bleeds through this far—" I didn't finish it, because finishing it meant saying *the song*, the first fragment, the dead woman's inheritance Maerin had traded half her life to keep whole, and if that scattered—

We ran.

* * *

*Sera*

I have taken oaths in three languages, and until today I believed all of them pointed the same direction. Standing in the ruins of Cathedral Square with a heretic's hand around my wrist, hauling me toward a sorceress's shop while the sky hemorrhaged music, I finally understood that the oaths had been pointing at each other all along, and I had been standing in the angle between them calling it faith.

Ravel sang the covenant open wrong. I knew it was wrong the way you know a forgery — not because you're an expert, but because you've spent your life with the true thing and your hands remember its weight. My grandmother's marginalia had been in the prophecy book for a reason. She hadn't been cataloguing heresy. She'd been trying to *warn someone*, and the Church had sealed her notes in a basement and burned the messenger instead.

Now her Church had opened the heavens with a crowbar, and everything her family had helped build was coming down on ten thousand innocents.

Isandro Vane ran like a man who had done a great deal of running and resented each instance individually. "Which way?" I shouted.

"Left! And pray the Choir hasn't found the lane yet!"

"Why would the Choir care about one shop?"

"Because it's holding the only intact copy of the song that's supposed to fix this!" He vaulted a cart whose owner had abandoned it mid-market, cabbages scattering like green hail. "Maerin isn't keeping a book, Inquisitor. She keeps it *in her head*. She IS the hold. If the unmaking reaches her before the song does—"

I understood then why he ran the way he did. Not like a man fleeing death. Like a man carrying something breakable, somewhere behind his ribs, that he had already lost once and refused to lose twice.

The lane to Maerin's was still whole when we turned into it, and that was the worst part — the ordinariness of it. Laundry lines. A cat on a windowsill, deeply unbothered by the apocalypse occurring two streets over. And then the far end of the lane simply *stopped*, dissolved into that eye-refusing nothing, and the cat finally looked up and yowled and bolted, and even the cat understood before I did that the rules were gone.

"Maerin!" Isandro hammered the door. "MAERIN!"

It swung open on its own. Inside, the little shop that smelled of dried lavender and old paper was lit from within by a glow that had no source — the same visible sound, the same bleeding chords, leaking through the walls as though the memory-hold had become a second crack in the world, a smaller, kinder wound that the catastrophe had found and was now prying wider.

Maerin stood in the center of her own shop with her arms spread, and she was *singing*, holding note after note against the pressure like a woman bailing a flood with her cupped hands.

"You brought an Inquisitor into my house," she said without turning around. Her voice shook with effort. "Worse things have happened today, but give it time."

"The song," Isandro said. "It's breaking loose."

"Everything breaks loose." Now she turned, and her face was gray, and I saw that her eyes had gone silver at the edges, filled with reflected music. "A hundred years I kept it. A hundred years my mother kept it, and hers before her, waiting for the fool the prophecy promised." She smiled at Isandro — a real smile, exhausted and fond. "You took so long I nearly died of old age out of spite."

"Give it to me now. Whatever verse I can carry—"

"It doesn't work that way, love. You know it doesn't. Memory isn't a purse you empty." The floor shuddered. A shelf of charm-components slid sideways and dissolved mid-fall. "It has to be *given*, wholly, by a keeper who chooses—"

The crack reached the shop.

I felt it before I saw it — a silence moving up the lane like cold water rising, a place where sound went to die, and I understood with perfect clarity that Ravel's ritual wasn't just tearing the sky. It was eating the song out of the world itself, and everything made of song was coming apart, and Maerin Ilyen, last keeper of the covenant's first fragment, was made mostly of song.

"No," Isandro said.

"Yes," Maerin said gently. "Hold still. Both of you. This is going to be loud."

The memory-hold shattered.

I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe what happened in that shop, and I will fail, but the attempt goes: imagine a library burning in reverse. Imagine every memory Maerin had ever kept pouring out of her at once — not hers alone, but her mother's, her grandmother's, a hundred years of inherited recollection erupting outward in a storm of light and sound, and threaded through all of it, the Song. The original. The covenant's first voice. It scattered as it came, verse from verse, phrase from phrase, flung out into the ruined city like seeds from a burst pod, landing who-knew-where, taking root who-knew-how.

Maerin fell. Isandro caught her. Of course he did. Some men catch things; it's the entire architecture of them.

"Listen," she whispered, and blood at the corner of her mouth sang faintly when she spoke. "The verses went everywhere. Into people. Into *things*. The song won't be lost — it will be *hidden*, in ten thousand small hearts, waiting to be gathered." Her silvered eyes found mine, and I flinched, because she was seeing something in me I hadn't confessed to anyone. "Even into an archivist. Especially into you, girl. Your grandmother left you more than handwriting."

"Maerin." Isandro's voice was wrecked. "Take it back. Take back what I paid you — the day he spoke to me, take it back, trade it, I don't care—"

"Never." She smiled again. "Best coin I ever spent." Her hand rose, trembling, and pressed something against his chest — a small charm, crude, wooden, humming faintly. "Keep this. It caught one verse as I let go. Just one. Find the rest."

And then the light in her went out the way a lamp goes out — not violently, just *finished* — and Isandro Vane knelt on the floor of a dissolving shop, holding the body of a woman who had known him better than he knew himself, and screamed a wordless thing that the Choir, wherever it was, surely heard and counted among its verses.

Outside, the crack in the sky widened.

* * *

*Doss*

I watched it from the cathedral steps, from the place of honor Ravel had given me, close enough to see the gold thread in his cuffs, far enough to pretend I couldn't hear the city screaming.

My brother is free. I kept telling myself that. Torvin is out of the Church cells, his debt paid, his name cleared, because I sold one name — one name, one mercenary, one man who'd survived worse than betrayal — and in exchange my brother breathes free air tonight.

Except the man I sold was apparently the hinge of the universe, and the Church hadn't wanted him arrested. They'd wanted him *found*, because the prophecy needed a Vane present when they pried heaven open, and now the sky over Corvane was a wound wide enough to drive a wagon through, and streets were evaporating, and a woman's singing was audible in the ruin like a choir drowned mid-hymn, and Ravel stood on his scaffold with his arms raised and his face lit with a joy I recognized.

I'd seen that joy before. On Torvin's face, the day our debts were forgiven. Joy that costs someone else everything.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Ravel said, not turning. He knew I was there. He'd known since I stopped kneeling. "A century of lies, undone in an afternoon. They'll thank us, Doss. When we hold the Choir's power in mortal hands, they'll build statues."

"Statues," I said. My mouth tasted like copper. Somewhere in that smoke, Isandro Vane — who once carried me three miles with an arrow in his lung, who taught Torvin to dice, who never once asked me for anything except Tuesday nights and bad wine — was either dead or worse.

"You may go, if you wish," Ravel said pleasantly. "Your part is done."

My part. Yes. My part was done. My part had been done the moment I wrote Isandro's name on that paper, and everything since had just been the echo of it traveling outward, getting larger, until it cracked the sky.

I went. Down the steps, into the screaming, past a fountain that wasn't there anymore, into a city where the air hummed with escaped verses of a song older than the Church. And in a gutter, snagged on rubble, glowing softly like a coal that wouldn't die, I found a scrap of light that had shape and weight and sounded, when I picked it up, like a woman laughing.

A verse. Scattered. Looking for a home.

I stood there holding the end of the world in my fist, and I thought about my brother breathing free air, and about whether freedom bought this way was freedom at all or just a prettier cell, and about the fact that I could feel the verse humming against my palms like a heartbeat, choosing me, maybe, or testing me, and that either way I was now carrying something that belonged to the fix, not the ruin.

There was exactly one person alive who could gather these verses. Everyone knew it. Even Ravel knew it, which was why he'd wanted Isandro found and not killed.

Which meant that if Isandro Vane still breathed — and I would bet the only thing I had left, my guilt, that he did — then I knew where he'd go next, and I could get there first, and maybe, *maybe*, handing back one stolen thing was the first payment on an unpayable debt.

Or maybe it was too late, and the sky would finish cracking, and every choice any of us made would dissolve into unsung matter with the rest.

I put the verse in my pocket and started running toward the shrine district, feeling like the biggest fool in a city full of them, and hoping — with the desperate, shame-soaked hope of a man who had loved someone and sold him anyway — that fools could still be of use.

Behind me, the crack in the sky sang a note I almost recognized.

It sounded like Maerin. It sounded like laughter.

It sounded like a beginning.
