## 16. What Maerin Gave Back

There is a moment — I'd always imagined it would come later, much later, ideally in a bed with a view and someone weeping decoratively at my feet — when you realize the thing breaking apart inside you is not going to stop.

Mine came while I was still holding the teapot.

The sky over Corvane had cracked open like an egg, the Choir was bleeding through it in ribbons of unsung light, and my memory-hold — forty years of other people's treasured moments, shelved and dusted and kept safe — was coming down around me like a library set on fire by God Himself.

I remember thinking, absurdly: *the teapot will be fine.*

It was not fine. Nothing was fine. The pot shattered in my hands as the first wave of backlash hit me, and I sat down hard on the floor of my ruined shop amid the shards, because my legs had resigned their post without notice.

Here's what nobody tells you about keeping memories for a living: you never keep just one. You take a memory from a client, and you shelve it, and you think that's the end of it. But a memory is not a coin you can lock in a box. It's a lit candle. And if you hold enough candles long enough, some of their light gets into your wick. I have lived other people's weddings. Other people's funerals. A fisherman's last morning before the storm took him, which he'd given me precisely so that someone, somewhere, would remember he'd been happy once.

And now all of it — every shelf, every candle — was tearing loose.

I could feel them going. The baker's daughter's first snow. The old soldier's shame and his courage, filed under the same date. Isandro Vane's morning fifteen years ago, when a silent god spoke to him and changed the shape of his life — I'd only held that one for a few weeks, taken as payment, and even now, even as it slipped its shelf, I gripped it harder than any of the others.

Because inside it, I had seen something wrong. Something he needed to know.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Dying people are allowed to get ahead of themselves; it's one of the perks.

* * *

Let me tell you about Maerin, since Maerin won't be around to do it.

I was born in the Tallow Quarter to a mother who read fortunes badly and a father who was mostly rumor. I found out young that I could hold what other people couldn't carry — griefs too heavy, joys too bright, secrets that were crushing them flat. For a copper or a kindness, I'd take the weight and keep it. Not erase. Never erase. That's the difference between what I did and what the Church called theft. I didn't take anything away. I just carried it, the way a ferry carries a traveler across water without asking where they came from.

Forty years of that. Thousands of candles.

And then, three weeks ago, a mercenary with a face like weathered rope came into my shop and paid me with the single most important day of his life.

I want to be fair to him. He thought he was paying me with trash. "It's nothing," he said, setting the memory down on my counter like a man paying a debt with a button. "An old day. Take it and don't look at it too hard."

I looked at it too hard. It's the job.

I saw a boy of nineteen, hollowed out by hunger and by whatever hunger's older cousin is, kneeling in a ruined shrine to a god everyone said was dead. I saw the silence there — real silence, the kind that has texture — and then I saw it break. I saw Vethra speak. One word, maybe two. Enough to bind a covenant and buy a boy's survival at a price he didn't understand yet.

And I saw — this is the part, this is the wrong thing, this is what I've been carrying like a stone in my shoe ever since — I saw that the silence hadn't been empty. Behind the god's quiet, something was *hiding*. Something was being concealed, deliberately, the way you hide medicine's taste in honey. The boy felt none of it. But I wasn't the boy. I was standing outside the memory with forty years of practice at looking too hard, and I saw the shape of a secret pressed flat beneath the god's silence like a flower in a book.

Vethra hadn't gone mute. Vethra had gone *quiet*, the way you go quiet in a sickroom.

I meant to tell him. Truly, I did. But telling a man that his whole faith was built over a buried truth seemed like the kind of thing you should do gently, over tea, and then the Inquisition started circling him, and then the sky tore open, and now here I am on the floor of my shop with the teapot dead and my shelves coming down, and gentleness has officially left the city.

* * *

The song came apart at dusk.

That's how I'd describe it, though "dusk" is generous — the cracked sky made its own false twilight, gold-black and wrong. The covenant-song, the original one, the fragment I'd inherited from a dead woman whose name I never learned and whose voice I knew better than my own — it had lived in my hold, shelved among the memories, humming faintly whenever I passed. My grandmother-of-sorts, the woman who'd left it to me through channels I still half-don't understand. She had sung it into my keeping before she died, and I had promised to keep it until someone came who could carry it further.

Isandro Vane was supposed to be that someone. I'd taught him most of it. Most.

Then Ravel's ritual ripped across the city, and the backlash came through the walls of my hold like floodwater, and the song tore loose along with everything else.

I felt it go — felt the melody shred into pieces, scattering into the ruin of Corvane like ash off a pyre. And I did what any sensible dying archivist does.

I panicked. Briefly. Thoroughly. There may have been language involved that my fortune-telling mother would have charged extra for.

And then I stopped panicking, because panic is a luxury and I was bleeding light from places light shouldn't come from, and I realized something that turned my blood cold and hot at the same time:

I remembered it anyway.

Not the shelved version — the *memorized* version. Forty years of handling that song, teaching it note by note to a stubborn mercenary, singing it under my breath while I dusted. Somewhere in the last weeks, without deciding to, I'd copied it into the one vault no backlash can touch: the plain, mortal, unreliable, magnificent human skull.

Every note. Including the final verse.

Including the final verse, which I had *not* taught him. Which I had held back, partly to make sure he came back to me for another lesson — a keeper's petty insurance, I'll admit it — and partly because the final verse frightened me. It names its price. Plainly. Without metaphor. The first verses sing of Memory and Truth and Loyalty like a riddle. The last verse stops riddling. It says what the covenant costs, and who pays, and how willingly, and it is the reason, I understood in that terrible golden twilight, that Vethra went silent. The god hadn't hidden the price from cruelty. The god had hidden it the way you hide a diagnosis — to give the patient one more ordinary year.

I had memorized the truth of the whole covenant in case the fragment failed.

Well. The fragment had failed. Here I was, the backup copy, running low on time.

* * *

He found me by smell, I think. Smoke and spilled tea and the particular ozone stink of unsung matter. Or maybe the Echo guided him — the remnant of Vethra that had attached itself to his shadow like a loyal, melancholy dog. Either way, the door of my ruined shop groaned open and Isandro Vane stood in it, backlit by a broken sky, wearing the expression of a man who has run through a collapsing city toward a place he was afraid wouldn't have anyone alive inside it.

"Maerin." His eyes went over the wreckage, the scattered shelves, and then to me, sitting in the rubble with light leaking out of me like a cracked lantern. Something crossed his face that he'd have denied under oath. "You're —"

"Dying?" I said. "Yes. Do sit down, the chairs are mostly decorative now."

"Maerin —"

"Don't waste your last good minutes arguing with a professional. Sit."

He sat, on an upturned crate, close enough that I could see the Choir's strange light reflected in his eyes. Up close, dying looks worse than it sounds. I know, because I watched him watch me and revise several assumptions about how bad things can look.

"The hold broke," I said. "Everything scattered. The song, the memories, all of it. Gone into the streets."

His face fell in a specific way, and I recognized it, because I'd seen that same fall in a hundred clients when they came back to reclaim a memory and found I'd kept it safe. It's the face of a man watching his past blow away.

"But," I said, and raised one finger, which took more effort than raising a siege ladder, "you are speaking to the only archive in Corvane that fits behind these eyes. I memorized it, Isandro. All of it. Every note. Including the verse I never taught you."

"The final verse." His voice went careful. "Why didn't you teach me the final verse?"

"Insurance," I said. "And fear. Mostly fear, dressed up as insurance." I coughed, and it was not a normal cough; it came out as a faint thread of melody, which is never a good sign. "The final verse names the price of the covenant. A mortal voice, freely given, every generation. Everything tied to that voice goes with it. Name, memory, self. The whole ledger."

I watched him absorb it. Watched him understand why the Vault of Silence had refused him, why the truth-fragment had shown him visions instead of opening — it had been trying to teach him this same lesson in its own brutal way. Watched him understand, finally, what Vethra's silence had actually been.

"He was protecting us," Isandro said quietly. "All those chosen voices, generation after generation. He swallowed the cost himself rather than name the next one."

"He let the whole covenant rot rather than ask again," I said. "Fifteen years ago he broke his own rule and chose a starving boy, and then he couldn't bear to explain the bill. So yes. Your god is a coward and a saint, and the two categories overlap more than the Church admits."

That got a wet, cracked almost-laugh out of him, which was the point. Grief sharpens better against laughter than against solemnity. Ask any funeral cook.

"Listen to me now." I took his hand. His knuckles were scarred; everything about this man was scarred; I had seen the boy underneath them in his payment-memory and wanted, briefly, to go back in time and feed him. "The song lives in me, and I am leaving shortly. So we're going to do this properly, right now, and you are going to hold every note, because after tonight there is no second copy anywhere in either realm. Do you understand?"

"You're transferring it? Like a client's memory?"

"Worse. Better. This isn't storage, this is inheritance." I squeezed his hand. "A dead woman sang it into me, and I sang it into my bones, and now I sing it into yours, and someday — not soon, I hope, but someday — you'll understand why that's the only way songs survive. People. It was always people. Shelves burn. Skulls walk."

* * *

So we sang.

In a ruined shop, under a torn sky, with the Choir bleeding gold-black light through the cracks in the world and my own life guttering like the last candle on the shelf, Maerin of the Broken Hold sang the covenant-song entire to Isandro Vane, and he sang it back.

I won't pretend my voice was pretty. It kept fraying at the edges, unraveling into harmonics I wasn't putting there — the Choir leaning in to listen, I suppose, or perhaps the dead woman's remnant, checking her work. But the notes held. Verse by verse he took them: Memory, Truth, Loyalty, each fragment's theme braided into the melody like three threads making one rope. And then we reached the final verse, the one I'd hoarded, the one that names the price —

— and he faltered.

Of course he did. Any sane man falters when the song turns and looks directly at him and says *you*.

"I can't," he whispered. "Maerin, I heard what it says. Voice, name, everything. If I sing that verse —"

"If you sing that verse alone, nothing happens. Songs aren't contracts until they're offered." I gripped his hand tighter. "Right now it's just a song. Learn it. Carry it. What you *do* with it is the next chapter, and it isn't mine to write." I leaned closer. My voice was failing faster now; I could feel words dissolving on my tongue before they landed. "But listen. Listen to why I'm giving it to you like this."

"Why?"

"Because of what I saw. In the memory you sold me. The day Vethra spoke to you." His eyes widened — he'd forgotten, or tried to, that I'd ever held it. "I saw a boy kneel in a dead shrine with nothing left to believe in, and ask anyway. Nobody asked him to. Nobody forced him. He'd been robbed of everything except the stubbornness to hope, and he *still hoped*, and that's why the god answered. Not mercy. Not prophecy. Hope with nowhere else to put itself." I smiled, and my mouth tasted of copper and old honey. "That's why you, Isandro. That was always why you. Not because a book named you. Because you're the kind of man who prays in ruins."

"Maerin —"

"Last lesson. No charge." I pulled his forehead down to mine, the way my mother used to, the way I'd done for a thousand clients handing back their heaviest days. And I gave him back the memory.

Not the shelved version — that was ash on the wind with everything else. I gave him my *copy*: the memory as I had held it, seen from outside, with everything I'd noticed stitched into its margins. The boy in the shrine. The breaking silence. The hidden secret pressed flat beneath the god's quiet. And layered through all of it, the reason I'd kept looking too hard: because I had spent forty years watching people surrender their belief to me for safekeeping, and I had never once, not once, met anyone who earned it back the way this fool had.

"There," I breathed, as his breath caught — I felt the moment it landed, the boy returning to him, the shrine, the word, the *why*. "Now you remember why you believed. Keep that one yourself. Don't ever sell it to the likes of me."

* * *

Dying, it turns out, is mostly logistics.

I checked — as much as a dying woman can check — that he had the song whole. I made him hum the final verse back to me twice, and corrected him on the third bar both times, because even at the end I cannot abide sloppy archiving. I told him where Sera Ilyen might be found, and that she was worth the risk, which surprised him and would surprise her more. I told him the Echo was more than it seemed and would tell him itself, and to let it.

And then, because the light was coming out of me in earnest now, gold and slow, and the shelves of my life were nearly bare, I said the thing I'd been saving.

"Isandro. When you reforge it — and you will, because you're too stubborn to do otherwise — everyone will offer you loyalties. The god. The girl. Yourself. They'll dress it up as a choice between them." My vision was going soft at the edges, the ruined shop turning to watercolor. "Don't choose any of them. Choose the truest thing you know. Loyalty inherited is just habit. Loyalty *chosen* — that's the fragment that binds."

"How do you —" His voice cracked clean through. "How do you know that?"

"Because a dead woman sang it to me forty years ago, and I finally understand what she meant, and isn't that always the way with inheritances." I patted his scarred hand once, twice, a keeper closing a ledger. "Go on, then. The city's ending and you're the only man in it holding the whole song. Terrible responsibility. Wonderful company."

He didn't want to leave. I could see it, the big ridiculous man, wanting to stay and hold vigil over a dying woman in a collapsed shop while the sky came down. So I lied to him, gently, the last kindness in my inventory.

"I'll hold on till morning," I said. "Keepers always overestimate their stock. Go."

He went. At the door he stopped, and looked back, and said my name like it was worth something, and I confess I let him believe the lie, because a man walking into the climax of a century-old prophecy should be allowed one small warm untruth to carry in his pocket.

Then the door shut, and I was alone with the candles going out one by one, thousands of borrowed lights flickering home to wherever borrowed lights go.

It was fine, honestly. Better than the bed with the view. I had carried ten thousand memories and not one of them was mine, and at the end I found I didn't mind — because somewhere out in the broken streets, a man who prayed in ruins was humming a dead woman's song, and the third bar was correct, and the last verse was in his bones.

Somewhere, the Choir would remember that Maerin kept her promise.

I closed my eyes. The final shelf emptied.

And far above the ruined city, through the crack in the sky, I swear I heard it begin — the song, whole, in a voice roughened by scars, walking toward the only choice left that mattered.
