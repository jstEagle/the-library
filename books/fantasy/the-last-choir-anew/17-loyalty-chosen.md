## 17. Loyalty, Chosen

There is a particular kind of quiet that follows a catastrophe. It's not peace. Peace is earned. This is the quiet of a city holding its breath around a wound in its sky.

I stood in the ruin of what had been the Lanternmarket — though "ruin" undersold it. The crack Ravel's ritual had torn above Corvane bled light down onto the cobblestones, and where that light pooled, matter forgot itself. A fruit stall became a suggestion of fruit stall. A fountain hummed a chord and was suddenly, briefly, water again, then something older than water. The Choir's songs leaked through like blood through bandages, and everything they touched remembered being sung instead of built.

Maerin's body lay under a scrap of awning two streets back. I had covered her face with my coat because it seemed wrong to leave her looking at all that light without anything to soften it. Her memory-hold had shattered with everything else. The first fragment of the covenant — the song itself — had scattered into the air over Corvane like a handful of thrown grain.

And still, somehow, I could feel it. All of it. The verses she'd taught me sat in my chest like swallowed coals. The truth from the Vault of Silence sat beside them, heavier. And somewhere ahead, waiting, was the third thing.

* * *

The Echo found me at the edge of the Lanternmarket, standing on a cobblestone that was trying very hard to remain a cobblestone.

"You look terrible," it said. The Echo always looked like Vethra — or rather, like the idea of Vethra, a tall shape made of held breath and folded silence — but tonight its edges frayed, unraveling thread by thread as the Choir bled out above us. Fifteen years I'd prayed to a silent god. Now his ghost stood in front of me looking like a man whose house was burning down around him, which, in fairness, it was.

"I've had a day," I said.

"Two fragments," the Echo said. "Memory and Truth. You carry both."

"Don't forget the grief. That's practically a third."

The Echo didn't laugh. It never laughed. But something in its posture shifted — a softening, the way stone softens when you realize it isn't stone at all but someone leaning against a door, listening. "The third fragment cannot be carried," it said. "It cannot be found. I should have told you from the beginning."

"You should have told me a great many things from the beginning. Your god certainly should have."

"Yes." No deflection. No divine hedging. Just agreement, plain as bread. It unsettled me more than any thunder would have.

"So what is it?" I asked. "The third fragment. Loyalty, you said. Where do I dig?"

"You don't dig." The Echo raised one insubstantial hand toward the crack in the sky. Toward the crowd, too — because there was a crowd now. Corvane's people had come out of their houses to watch heaven bleed, and they pressed against the barricades the Inquisition hadn't finished erecting, hundreds of faces turned upward, lit blue and gold by unsung music. "Loyalty is not an object, Isandro Vane. It is a choice made in front of witnesses. The covenant demands declaration. Public. Binding. Renounce, and be bound to what survives."

"Renounce what?"

"One of three." The Echo's voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from a god-remnant is terrifying. "Renounce Vethra — your god, your patron, the silence that made you — and loyalty to him will bind the fragment. Or renounce the woman who hunted you and now stands beside you—"

I glanced back. Sera was thirty paces off, talking down two Inquisitors who clearly weren't sure whether she'd been promoted, captured, or possessed. She caught me looking and gave me a nod that meant *still alive, don't jinx it.*

"—and loyalty to her will bind it," the Echo finished. "Or renounce yourself. Your name, your history, your claim to any self at all. Give up Isandro Vane, and loyalty to what remains will hold the covenant together. Three doors. Choose one."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about choices: they're never really about the options. They're about what the options assume you'll accept.

I walked away from the Echo — just a few steps, enough to think, which has always been my most reliable tactical maneuver — and I did what I always do when the world gets too large: I thought about charms.

Fifteen years selling small magics in a city that outlawed them. Luck-knots and fever-binds and little woven things that kept nightmares honest. Every charm I ever sold was a lie with good intentions — *this will protect you*, when all it did was remind you that you wanted protecting. I'd told myself for fifteen years that I did it because it was easy money. Then the Vault of Silence had shown me the truth: I did it because the day Vethra spoke to me, I'd been so hungry to matter that I'd signed away a piece of myself for the privilege, and selling charms to frightened people was the closest I could get to giving back what I'd taken from myself. Every charm was an apology I couldn't say out loud.

Truth hurts. That's the whole point of it. If it doesn't hurt, it isn't true; it's just a story you like.

So I stood in the bleeding light and looked honestly at the three doors the Echo offered me.

Door one: renounce Vethra. Easy. Gods are easy to renounce; they're rarely present to object. And it would even be fair — he hid the covenant's price, let generations of chosen voices be spent in secret, went silent rather than confess. I had every right. But I thought of Maerin dying with my stolen memory in her hands, the day Vethra first spoke to me, and how inside it she'd seen the god wasn't cruel — he was *afraid*. Afraid for me, specifically, the way you're afraid for someone you've already failed once. Renouncing him would be satisfying and cheap, and cheap is the one currency the covenant doesn't accept.

Door two: renounce Sera. Harder. Harder because she'd earned her place beside me the hard way — by breaking her own oath, in public, in front of the Inquisition she'd served since girlhood. When Ravel's ritual tore the sky, she'd been the one who ran *toward* the falling streets pulling children out of them while her brothers-in-faith ran toward the ritual circle. Renouncing her would cost something real. Which meant the covenant would love it. Which meant it was exactly the kind of bargain designed to make sacrifice look like virtue.

And door three — renounce myself. My name, my past, everything. That one wasn't even a temptation. That was just suicide with better paperwork.

"They're all the same door," I said aloud.

The Echo drifted closer. "Explain."

"All three ask me to give something up and call the giving holy. Renounce the god, the woman, the man — pick a thing to cut off and bleed from. That's not loyalty. That's amputation with ceremony." I turned to face it fully. "You want to know why the covenant broke in the first place? This. Someone, generations ago, decided faith meant surrender. Give up your voice. Give up your name. Keep paying until nothing's left to pay with. Vethra went silent because he couldn't stomach telling the next one the price, and the Inquisition rose because people got tired of gods who took and never explained. The whole rot started with a choice shaped exactly like the one you're handing me."

"The covenant does require—"

"The covenant requires *loyalty*," I said. "Fine. I'm listening. But nobody loyal ever had to be told what to stop loving first."

* * *

The crowd had noticed us. Of course it had — a shimmering dead god arguing with a charm-seller makes an impression. Word moved through the press of bodies the way word does in Corvane: fast, distorted, hungry. By the time I climbed onto the broken base of the old bell-post — Sera of the Broken Bell, the district's named for it, some old saint nobody remembered — there were hundreds of faces below me, and more coming, and among them the blue-and-grey of Inquisitor patrols who hadn't yet decided whether I was a criminal or a miracle.

Sera pushed through to the front. "Isandro." Low voice, urgent. "Whatever you're doing, do it quickly. Ravel survived the backlash. He's regrouping at the Cathedral and he will come for whatever power is left standing when this is done. You do not want to be mid-ceremony when he arrives."

"Noted. Any chance you could arrest me instead? It'd buy time."

"Do it," she said, "or I swear I'll find out what you actually look like when you're serious."

So much for stalling.

The Echo rose beside the bell-post, tall as a steeple now, fraying into the light. Its voice reached every corner of the square without effort, the way a mother's voice finds a child in a crowded room.

"MORTALS OF CORVANE. THE COVENANT STANDS AT ITS END. ONE FRAGMENT REMAINS, AND IT CANNOT BE FOUND, ONLY DECLARED. LET THE NAMED ONE CHOOSE HIS LOYALTY BEFORE YOU ALL."

Silence. The real kind — the kind Vethra used to keep, vast and attentive.

I looked out at the faces. Fishwives and clerks, beggars and merchants, a few Inquisitors with their hands hovering near their weapons. Ordinary people watching their sky come apart. None of them had chosen any of this. Neither had I, if I was honest — a dying bookseller, a book that drank my blood, a god's ghost with a commission I never agreed to. Chosen without consent. That had been the whole rotten architecture of it, start to finish. Prophecy picks you. God marks you. Church hunts you. Everyone decides for everyone else.

The Echo waited. Somewhere behind it, faintly, I could hear the scattered song turning in the air above the city, waiting for something to hold it together.

"Loyalty," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. I stopped. Started again. "The covenant offers me three renunciations. My god. My friend. Myself. And it promises that whichever one I cut away will become the thing that holds the worlds together."

I held up my hand before the Echo could speak.

"But here's what fifteen years of selling charms in the rain taught me, and what one week of prophecy confirmed: nothing built on what you've cut away ever holds. You can't found a covenant on a wound. Believe me, I've tried — I built my whole life on the wound of what I gave that god, and look how well it kept."

Sera had gone very still at the foot of the bell-post. The Echo's edges trembled. Above us, the crack in the sky widened by a hair's breadth, patient, expectant, certain I'd choose.

"So I refuse the terms," I said. "All of them."

A murmur ran through the crowd. One of the Inquisitors actually drew steel.

"I won't renounce Vethra. He lied to me by omission and hid the price to spare me, which is cowardice wearing mercy's clothes — but he also gave a starving mercenary fifteen years of purpose, and purpose is not nothing. I won't renounce him. I renounced him years ago anyway, quietly, every time I cursed his silence. What's left after that isn't hatred. It's just grief, and grief is a kind of love that stayed too long at the grave."

"I won't renounce Sera Ilyen. She hunted me, arrested me twice—" "Once," she corrected from below. "Twice," I said, "once was implied — and then she read her grandmother's handwriting in a forbidden book and chose the harder road, alone, against everything that raised her. You don't renounce the person who taught you what courage looks like. You just hope you'd have been brave enough in her place."

"And I will not renounce myself. Not because I'm proud of Isandro Vane — the Vault showed me every corner of him and I'd burn half of it gladly. But because a covenant bought with self-erasure is just the same old theft with a holier receipt. A mortal voice given willingly — that was always the price, and willing means *whole*, not hollowed out."

I looked up at the crack, at the light spilling through, at the song turning in the air.

"So here is my declaration, Choir. Witness it." I spread my arms. "I give my loyalty to none of the three. I give it to the *truth*. To the thing underneath all three of them — the god's hidden price, the woman's broken oath, the man's ugly honest reasons. Whatever is true, I choose it. Even when it costs. Even when it unmakes me. Let loyalty to truth itself bind your fragment, because truth is the only thing in either realm that never needed me to defend it — only to stop lying about it."

For one heartbeat, nothing.

Then the light changed.

* * *

It came down through the crack like a struck bell — no, like the moment after a struck bell, the pure tone that lives in the metal afterward. The scattered song above the city stopped wandering. It *turned*, all of it, every scattered verse Maerin had memorized and died carrying, every note that had spilled from her shattering hold, and it came streaming toward the bell-post, toward me, threading itself through the air like a river remembering its bed.

The third fragment wasn't an object. It had never been an object. It was the square full of people hearing me refuse, and choosing — each in their small private way — to believe me. I felt it settle into my chest beside the other two fragments and fuse, click, *hold*, three notes becoming a chord, and the chord had a name older than any language: covenant.

The Echo made a sound I had never heard it make. It took me a moment to recognize it, because gods' remnants aren't supposed to weep.

"It accepts," it whispered. "In four hundred years, no chosen one has refused the terms. We thought refusal was the failure. It was the answer all along."

"Yeah, well." My knees were shaking and I gripped the bell-post to hide it. "Next time put it in the prophecy. 'The Vane will tell you to go to hell.' Saves everyone time."

Below me, Sera was staring up with an expression I'd never seen on her — not doubt, not duty. Awe, maybe, with fear underneath, the way dawn looks to people who've lived all their lives underground.

"Isandro," she said slowly. "The song. It's whole now. It's waiting. What happens next?"

And that was when the bells of the Cathedral began to ring — wrong, discordant, a rhythm I recognized from the Vault's deepest visions — and the crowd turned as one toward the sound, and I felt the fused covenant-song in my chest pull taut like a wire between me and whatever Ravel was building in the dark beneath his cathedral.

He'd felt it too. The moment the fragment fused, he'd felt it.

"He's coming for the song," I said. "And he knows exactly where it is now."

The Echo straightened, frayed edges blazing. "Then you must sing it before he reaches you, Isandro Vane. Tonight. Now. There is no longer any time to prepare you for what it costs."

I looked at Sera. She looked at me. Somewhere in the crowd, a child asked her mother what was happening, and the mother said, "I think it's starting," and neither of them knew how right she was.

"Costs," I said. "Of course it does. It always does. Fine." I took a breath, and the whole covenant-song rose in my throat like water finding its level. "Let's find out what I'm worth."
