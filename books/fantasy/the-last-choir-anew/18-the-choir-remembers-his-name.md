## 18. The Choir Remembers His Name

I have watched a great many mortals stand in the Choir and fail.

Forgive me if that sounds bitter. It has been four hundred years, give or take a decade I lost track of during the Second Unraveling, and in that time I have seen kings kneel at the threshold of the Vault of Silence and weep until their crowns rusted. I have seen saints turn back at the first suspended stairway because the architecture hummed a note they could not bear to hear. I have seen clever people — cleverer than Isandro Vane, though not by as wide a margin as he'd like you to believe — stand exactly where he was standing now, having gathered exactly what he had gathered, and open their mouths to declare something safe.

Loyalty to family. Loyalty to country. Loyalty to the warm, familiar shape of themselves.

The Choir is patient with all of them. The Choir is patient the way a grave is patient.

But Isandro Vane stood on the broken floor of the First Nave — which is to say, on nothing at all, held aloft by a chord that had been sustaining since before his grandmother's grandmother learned to walk — and he did none of those things. He looked up at the three fragments turning slowly above him like planets around an absent sun, and he said, in the flat, rain-soaked voice of a man who sells charms for coppers:

"You didn't offer me that one."

And the Choir stopped.

Not paused. *Stopped.* Every suspended bell tower, every hanging bridge of frozen song, every cathedral rib arcing overhead into darkness — everything went still, the way a room goes still when someone says the true thing out loud for the first time in centuries.

I should explain. When Isandro reached the third fragment's trial, the covenant offered him its ancient menu: renounce the god, renounce the woman, or renounce himself, and be bound to whatever loyalty survived. Those were the terms written into the covenant by its original architects, who were, I can say with some authority, very fond of menus. Mortals love a choice between pre-approved options. It feels like freedom while guaranteeing obedience.

Isandro Vane looked at the menu and declined it.

"I'm not renouncing Vethra," he said. "He lied to me for fifteen years, but he lied because he couldn't stomach the price either, and I know something about being unable to stomach things." His hand drifted to his chest, where Maerin's memory used to live before she gave it back to him along with her dying breath. "I'm not renouncing Sera. She broke her oath for the truth, and if I abandoned everyone who ever broke a rule for a good reason, I'd have to start with myself." A pause. Rain from a world away seemed to fall through his voice. "And I'm not renouncing myself. I've done enough of that for one lifetime."

The fragments turned above him. Waiting. The whole realm waiting, the way it had waited through my failure, and the failures before mine, and — I suspected, though I had never dared confirm it — the failures yet to come, if this one failed too.

"So here's my declaration," Isandro said. "I choose loyalty to the truth itself. Not to a god, not to a woman, not to the man I was or the man I wish I'd been. To the truth. Whatever it costs. Whoever it unmakes." He lifted his chin, and there was something in his face I recognized across four hundred years, because I had worn it once: the expression of a person who has finally stopped negotiating. "Take it or leave it."

* * *

You have to understand what happened next, and I will do my best, though describing the Choir accepting a declaration is rather like describing water accepting a fish.

The three fragments — Memory, which Maerin had carried in her skull like a candle flame carried through a storm; Truth, which had flayed Isandro open in the Vault and then, grudgingly, let him keep walking; and Loyalty, newly forged, still hot from the choosing — began to move toward each other.

They did not rush. Nothing in the Choir rushes; haste is a mortal invention and one of your better ones, frankly. They turned, and turned, and their orbits tightened, and the space between them began to sing. Not metaphorically. The gap between Memory and Truth produced a note, and the gap between Truth and Loyalty answered it, and the gap between Loyalty and Memory harmonized, and suddenly the First Nave was full of a chord that no throat had produced since the covenant last stood whole.

Isandro staggered. Any mortal would. The chord was reality's own key signature, the tuning fork the gods had used to sing streets into stone and rivers into running. I watched him plant his feet on the sustaining note beneath him like a sailor bracing on a deck, and I watched him refuse to fall, and I confess — four hundred years of watching, and I still felt my remnant-heart lurch.

Because the fragments fused.

Memory poured into Truth poured into Loyalty, and where they met they did not blend so much as *recognize* each other, the way two halves of a torn letter recognize each other across a table. And what rose from their fusion was not a light, exactly, and not a sound, exactly, but the thing the Choir had been starving for since the last voice fell silent: the covenant-song, whole, humming itself back into existence note by note like a fire remembering how to be warm.

The broken architecture began to mend.

I want you to picture this, because I have waited four hundred years to describe it to anyone who might understand. Stairways that had hung severed mid-arc for generations groaned back into continuity. Bell towers righted themselves. The great wound in the Choir's sky — the one Ravel's ritual had torn wider, but which had been scarring over wrong for a century before him — drew closed like a healing eye. And underneath it all, the song spread outward, past the Choir's borders, into the thin places, into Corvane itself, where I knew — I *knew*, the way the dead know weather — that unsung streets were re-becoming streets, that matter Ravel had unmade was being sung back into its old agreements with itself.

Isandro stood in the middle of it, soaked in borrowed music, looking smaller and more human than any man had a right to look while holding up two realms.

"Well," he said. His voice cracked on it. "That went better than the Vault."

* * *

Then he turned to me. Of course he did. There was only me left to turn to.

"You knew that would work," he said. It wasn't quite an accusation. Isandro's accusations always came dressed as questions, and his questions always came dressed as small talk. Fifteen years of selling charms teaches a man to hide the blade inside the pleasantries. "You've seen this before. The declaration. The fragments. You knew what I needed to say."

"I have seen many declarations," I said carefully.

"But not this one."

The chord sustained around us, patient, giving me time I did not deserve and had not earned. That is the cruelty of restored covenants: they restore everything, including your obligations.

"No," I admitted. "Not this one."

"Because?"

"Because the last mortal to stand where you're standing chose differently."

Silence — real silence, the kind I had been made of for four hundred years, the kind I had mistaken for home. Isandro waited. He was better at waiting than most mercenaries; it is the mercenary's second trade, after violence and before regret.

"My name," I said, and the name came out of me like a splinter that had lodged so long the flesh had grown around it, "was Elyse Vantry."

I felt him go still. Anyone in Corvane would have gone still. The Vantrys were a singing family once — chapel singers, street singers, the kind of family whose blood remembered melodies their minds had never learned. Four hundred years ago, the covenant frayed for the first time, and the Choir called for a voice, and Elyse Vantry climbed the suspended stairs of the First Nave with her family's songs in her throat and her family's blessing in her ears, and she declared her loyalty —

— to the god. To Vethra, then young-voiced and radiant, then worth believing in.

"The fragment bound to my faith," I told him. "And my faith was real. Don't mistake me — I don't regret loving him. But I bound the covenant to a *person*, Isandro, and persons change, and gods change worst of all. When Vethra began to doubt — when he first learned what the price would ask of the next generation — my loyalty strained. And when he went silent, my loyalty *broke*. And everything I was holding came down with it."

I gestured — a habit of a body I hadn't worn in centuries — at the mended nave, the healed sky, the fused song burning quietly above us like a second dawn.

"This is what I couldn't finish. I sang what remained of the covenant into its long decay. I bought the realms four hundred years of slow unraveling instead of quick ruin, and I told myself that was mercy, and perhaps it was. But I could not reforge what I had bound to a breaking heart. So I stayed. What was left of me. An Echo. A remnant. The Choir's oldest ghost and its most patient failure."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Rain from the mortal world fell somewhere far below us, in a city that was busy re-learning its own name.

"You're the last voice," he said. "The one Vethra couldn't forget. The reason he hid the truth — because he'd already taken *you*."

"He took me willingly," I said. "Which made it worse. You cannot rage at a door you opened yourself." I let the old grief pass through me, the way one lets weather pass; after four centuries it moves through quickly, like a cat. "Yes. I am the debt's previous payment. And you are the next. And we are standing in the space between, and there is something I must ask you, and I have rehearsed it for four hundred years and it still tastes like ash."

"Don't ask," Isandro said.

I blinked. Remnants can blink; it surprises people.

"You heard me." He stepped closer, and up close I could see what the song was already doing to him — the faint translucence at his edges, the way his eyes kept losing focus as memories untied themselves and drifted upward like sparks. The price had begun. Of course it had begun; the covenant does not wait for permission twice. "You don't need to ask. I figured it out in the Vault. I figured it out when Maerin died. Everything in this rotten covenant gets paid by somebody, and I'm the somebody who's standing here." His jaw set. "So don't ask me. Ask yourself whether you can live with it. Again."

"Isandro—"

"And one more thing." He was smiling now, that crooked, charm-seller's smile that had talked a thousand suspicious customers out of their coppers, except his eyes were bright with something that was not salesmanship. "When it takes me — when it takes all of it — someone should remember that I chose this. Not the prophecy. Not the god. Not the menu. Me. Can your Choir hold that? Can it hold a man who walked in knowing the bill?"

The chord swelled to answer him. The whole Choir leaned in — every mended tower, every healed arch, every note of the fused covenant-song bending toward one stubborn mortal standing on nothing, offering everything.

"Yes," I said. And then, because I owed him honesty and had spent four centuries paying interest on its absence: "It is the only thing it has ever truly wanted. Not obedience. Not sacrifice. A voice that knows the price and sings anyway." I extended what remained of my hands — remnant-hands, echo-hands, hands that had failed this exact moment lifetimes ago. "Elyse Vantry couldn't finish it. But Elyse Vantry can teach you the descant. The part the covenant keeps off the page. The part that makes it *yours* instead of merely *paid*."

He took my hands. They fit, strangely, around each other — the dead and the almost-dead, the failed and the willing.

"Then teach fast," Isandro said, glancing up at the song, which was already reaching for him, tender and terrible, patient no longer. "I get the feeling it doesn't do encores."

Far below, in the mortal world, a crack split the sky over Corvane — Ravel's last spite, or the covenant's final labor pain, I could not tell — and the Choir shuddered, and the song turned toward Isandro Vane like a tide turning toward the shore.

I began to sing.
