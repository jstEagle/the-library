## 20. The Song Given Willingly

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes after you've watched a man be unmade by his own silence. It isn't peace. It's the silence of a room where someone has just stopped screaming — everyone still breathing, everyone listening to themselves breathe.

That was Corvane's sky now. Ravel's crack had sealed itself when he fell, or rather when the crowd's witness pulled the belief out from under him like a rug from a standing acrobat, and what remained was a bruise-colored evening with the Choir showing through it in places. Not bleeding through anymore. *Showing* through, the way light shows through a curtain that's been washed too thin. Streets that had been unmade were knitting back slowly, unsung matter remembering its shape like a dog returning to its master's door.

And above the broken plaza, held in the air by nothing so crude as architecture, hung the covenant-song. Three fragments fused into one thing: Maerin's memory of melody, the Vault's truth, and my declaration — loyalty to truth itself, which sounds noble until you realize what truths cost.

The Echo hovered beside me, translucent as breath on glass.

"It wants a voice," I said. "Say it plainly. You owe me that much."

"I owed your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother that much," the Echo said, "and I never paid it either. Yes. A mortal voice, freely given. And everything tied to it." A pause, the way there always is before the worst part. "Every memory that lives in the voice. Every word you ever spoke that made you who you are."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it, but I did. Fifteen years as a mercenary teaches you to laugh at bad odds; apparently nothing teaches you to stop.

"So not just my singing voice. My *voice*. The one that argued with Doss over dice. The one that told Maerin her tea tasted like boiled leather. The one that said Sera's name for the first time last night and felt like a boy saying it."

"Yes."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward," the Echo said gently, "there will be no afterward that remembers itself."

* * *

Sera found me at the edge of the plaza, sitting on a step that had been a temple once and then a tavern and then rubble and was now, briefly, all three. She sat down without asking. Her Inquisitor's coat was scorched down one side and she'd torn off the insignia herself — I'd watched her do it, watched the crowd go silent as she renounced the oath, watched Ravel come apart like wet paper when ten thousand witnesses stopped believing in his authority at once. There's a lesson in that about what power actually is, and I intend to forget it along with everything else.

"You don't have to," she said.

"The Choir unravels. The mortal realm's magic-laws go with it. Every charm, every ward, every prayer that works — gone, and worse than gone, *never having worked*, which means half the city's foundations unmake themselves retroactively. I've read the book. Well. I've been drunk through the book."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." I turned the small charm over in my fingers — Maerin's charm, the one she'd pressed on me years ago, worthless tin shaped like a bell. "You meant: find another way. There isn't one. Believe me, I looked. I looked the way Doss looks at an exit."

"Isandro—"

"Don't say my name right now." I said it lightly, and it wasn't light at all. "I want to keep a few minutes of hearing it. If you say it like that, soft, like it matters — I'll spend my last hour hoarding the sound instead of learning the song."

She was quiet. Sera is very good at quiet; it's how she interrogates, how she grieves, how she loves, if I'm reading the evidence correctly, and I have been careful with that evidence because I couldn't afford to believe it too soon.

Then she said, "Teach me the first verse. If you're going to do this alone, at least I'll carry the beginning of it. Someone should remember the song even if no one remembers you."

Which is the moment I understood that Sera Ilyen would outlive me and would choose to hurt forever rather than forget, and that this was her version of the same sacrifice, and that we are, both of us, catastrophically stubborn people who deserve each other in some better world than this one.

"All right," I said. "First verse. It goes—" and I sang it, low, the way Maerin taught me, and the air around us went still the way air does around a struck bell.

* * *

The Echo brought me up. That's the only word for it — the Choir doesn't have stairs, it has *willingness*, and the melody carried me the way water carries a leaf, up past hanging bridges strung on sustained notes, past towers built of chord, past the great hollow place where Vethra used to stand.

Vethra's absence was still there. Absences have weight here; silence is a material, and the god's silence was load-bearing. I stood in it and felt fifteen years of faith rearrange itself one final time — not forgiveness, exactly. Something more tired. He hid the price to spare us, and his mercy broke the world, and mine will fix it, and neither of those sentences makes either of us right.

"I'm ready," I told the Echo.

"No one is ready. That's why it must be given freely. A ready voice is calculating. Yours is only terrified, which is honest." The Echo drifted closer, and for a moment I saw through it — saw the face beneath the remnant, young, mortal, a singer from six generations back who had stood where I stood and failed, or succeeded differently, I still didn't fully understand which. "One kindness before you begin. The memories won't leave all at once. They leave note by note, each verse taking what it needs. So you will feel yourself going. Choose what you look at while it happens. It's the only choice left."

"What did you look at?"

"A river," the Echo said. "I grew up on a river. I thought: if I must become nothing, let the last thing be something that was never mine to lose."

I nodded. Then I opened my mouth, and I gave them the song.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about dissolving: it feels like being *read*. Like the most patient listener in existence finally getting your whole story, every verse, and taking notes in the margins of you.

The first verses took small things. The taste of Corvane rain — gone, and I felt it go, a gray flavor lifting out of me like steam. The name of the street I'd sold charms on for nine years. The specific ache of an old scar on my knee, and then the fall that caused it, and then the year it happened. Each loss arrived with a strange courtesy, a little bow, *thank you for this, we'll take it now.*

I kept singing. Maerin's melody rose through me, and the Vault's truth harmonized underneath it, and my declaration rang over the top like a bell over a choir, and below us both realms began to move. I could see it — Corvane spread out beneath the thinning veil, streets re-knitting, the cathedral's shattered spire drawing itself back up out of its own rubble like a man deciding to stand. The Choir's broken architecture settled onto its melodies and held. Two worlds lacing shut along a seam of song, stitch by stitch, and every stitch was a piece of me.

The middle verses took bigger things. Doss — not the betrayal, strangely, that stayed longest, but the summer we were nineteen and stole a boat and got lost on the reservoir and laughed ourselves sick paddling in circles. Gone. I reached for it and found smooth wall where the memory had been, and I understood suddenly why the dead in old stories always ask to be remembered: not for vanity. Because being remembered is the only afterlife with any texture to it, and I was spending mine as mortar.

Then it took Maerin. Her laugh, her terrible tea, the way she'd said *one true memory, mercenary, that's my price, don't haggle.* I sang through the losing of her and it was the hardest verse, harder than the truth-verse, harder than the fear, because she had given me the song knowing I'd use it to erase her, and she'd memorized the final verse anyway, dying, so that I could finish. Grief sharpened into resolve — that was the beat, wasn't it? I could hear the plan of my own life clicking into place like a lock, and I hated how neat it was, and I sang anyway.

Sera lasted longer than anyone. Of course she did. She was the newest thread and the strongest-woven. I sang the verse that should have taken her and it held, and held, and the Echo made a sound beside me that might have been warning or might have been wonder.

"She's tied to your voice now," the Echo said. "You spoke her name into the covenant when you declared. The song can't take what you gave away freely."

"Then it takes the rest."

"There is very little rest."

"I noticed," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, and the crack went into the song and became, of all things, the loveliest note in it. The Choir shivered. Below, the last seam between realms closed with a sound like a city exhaling.

* * *

Near the end there wasn't much of me left to do the noticing. I was mostly music by then, a tune wearing the last rags of a man. But the Echo had promised me a choice about what to look at, and I spent it well.

I looked at Sera.

Not at a memory — most of those were gone, harvested verse by verse — but at her, actual her, down on the plaza steps where I'd left her, looking up at the mending sky with her hand pressed flat over her heart like a woman holding a door shut against a wind. She couldn't see me. Or — and this is the part I'll spend whatever remains of me wondering about — maybe she could. Because just as the final verse gathered itself, just as the last of Isandro Vane stood up inside the song to be spent, she lifted her head and looked directly at the place where I was, and mouthed something.

I didn't need the words. Some things survive translation out of a self.

The final verse took my name first. *Isandro* — gone, and with it the man who'd flinched the first time a bookseller said it like a prophecy. Then *Vane*, the family name I'd never earned and finally, tonight, had. Then the rest, the whole inventory: the mercenary, the charm-seller, the liar, the believer, the boy at the shrine fifteen years ago who'd asked a silent god for help and gotten a debt instead.

What was left sang the last note.

It was very small. It sounded, if I'm honest, like a tin bell charm humming in a pocket.

And then—

* * *

Nothing. No — not nothing. Nothing is a thing, and this was less organized than that. Warmth. Rain somewhere, eventually, on a face that didn't know whose it was. Hands that knew knots and coin-weights and the trick of palming a charm, though their owner couldn't have said where the knowledge came from, the way you can't say where you learned to swallow.

A city. Good smells, bread and wet stone. People who didn't know him, moving around him like water around a post. He stood in a market square with empty pockets and a full chest and no idea why his eyes were wet, and decided — the way you decide things when you have nothing else to decide with — that it was probably the onions.

In his pocket, something small and metal. He took it out. A charm, cheap tin, shaped like a little bell. He turned it over twice. It hummed, faintly, when he wasn't quite paying attention — stopped whenever he looked straight at it, like a shy animal.

"Huh," he said, in a voice that had never sung anything larger than a barroom chorus, and put it back in his pocket.

Across the square, a woman with a scorched coat and no insignia had stopped walking. She was staring at him with an expression he couldn't read — grief and relief fighting to a draw, or something past both. For a strange moment he thought she might come over. Might say his name, if he had one.

She didn't. She smiled — a small, wrecked, private smile — and turned away, and let him keep the only thing he had left:

Tomorrow.

Somewhere far above the weather, in a realm rebuilt on a willing voice, something ancient and vast and newly mended hummed a single name into its shining architecture, over and over, gentle as a lullaby, patient as stone.

It sounded almost like *Vane*.
