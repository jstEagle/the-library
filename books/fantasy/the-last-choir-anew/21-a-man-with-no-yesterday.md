## 21. A Man With No Yesterday

The sky over Corvane has been whole again for nineteen days, and I still flinch when I look at it.

Old habits. For eleven years I was an Inquisitor, and Inquisitors are trained to look at things the way butchers look at livestock — for the flaw, for the weakness, for the place where the knife goes in. I looked at the heavens for a fortnight expecting a crack to reopen like a wound someone picked at, expecting Ravel's work to come undone because nothing that beautiful stays fixed. But the sky holds. The masons hold. Even the Choir's new embassy — a building of white stone that hums if you press your ear against it, which children do constantly and officials pretend not to notice — holds.

Corvane rebuilds the way Corvane does everything: badly, loudly, and faster than anyone thought possible. The streets Ravel unmade have been re-sung — that's the official term now, *re-sung*, and you can watch the crews doing it, robed envoys of the Choir standing in the rubble with their hands open, humming matter back into shape while mortal laborers haul away what can't be salvaged. There are arguments about whether the new cobblestones count as holy ground. There is a tavern called the Silent God already, three weeks old, selling a drink called the Vane's Mercy that tastes like regret and honey. Isandro would have hated it. Or loved it. He had that kind of humor — the kind that could go either way depending on how much he'd had.

I resigned my commission nine days after the fall of Ravel. It wasn't dramatic. I didn't break my oath on the cathedral steps the way I did that night — though they tell me the moment I did it, when my voice joined the crowd's witness and Ravel's ritual came apart like wet paper, is already being painted onto walls all over the city. In the murals I am taller and my hair is better behaved. In the murals there is only one figure kneeling where the High Inquisitor stood. In truth there were pieces of him for a long time afterward, drifting down like ash, and none of us spoke for what felt like an hour.

No. My resignation happened in an office, across a desk, from a woman named Prefect Callas who inherited the Inquisition's wreckage and was trying very hard to make it into something that wasn't a weapon. She asked if I was sure. I said yes. She asked why, and I told her the truth: "Because I spent my career hunting theft, and it turns out I worked for thieves." She signed the paper without another word. I think she was relieved. Someone had to be the first, and better a Sera Ilyen than someone with less reason to be angry.

My grandmother's notes are in the city archive now, under glass, labeled honestly for the first time: *Marginalia of Adelisa Ilyen, archivist, regarding the manufactured statutes of 311.* My family helped build the lie. My family helped keep it for four generations. And my grandmother — this is the part I return to at night, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone — my grandmother knew, wrote it down in a cipher she must have hoped someone would one day read, and then went home every evening and said nothing and raised children who raised me in the faith of her silence.

I don't hate her. That surprises me more than anything else about this year. I understand her, which is worse. She was afraid. Fear is the Church's oldest building material, and we were all living inside it.

* * *

I found him on a Thursday, in the Lanternmarket, in the rain.

I hadn't been looking. I want that on the record, even if the record is just this page and just me. I had spent three weeks deliberately not looking — avoiding the Lanternmarket, avoiding the shrine district, avoiding the faces of men with dark hair and mercenary builds, telling myself it was grief and grief passes and anyway he isn't dead, exactly, so what is there to grieve? A man who isn't dead but is gone. A man whose name the whole city knows and whose self no one saved. The accords named him in the preamble. *In gratitude to Isandro Vane, who gave freely.* They carved it into the embassy's lintel. Tourists touch it for luck. His name has never been more famous or belonged to him less.

But it was raining, and the Lanternmarket is the shortest walk between the archive and my rooms, and I was tired of taking the long way around my own life. So I went through the market, hood up, and there — between a woman selling roasted chestnuts and a boy with a cart of umbrellas that kept blowing inside out — there was a stall.

Small charms. That's all. Wards against damp, charms for safe travel, little knotted cords that were supposed to help milk keep and headaches lift. The kind of trade Isandro built for himself after Vethra went quiet, the honest-seeming front over the real work. The stall was modest and slightly crooked and the man behind it was mending a cord with his teeth while he talked price with a grandmother who was clearly going to buy nothing.

He was thinner than I remembered. Everyone is thinner than I remember lately; memory fattens the people it loves. His hair was longer. There was a scar on his jaw I didn't recognize — no. Wait. I did recognize it. I recognized it from Maerin's account of him, from before, from fifteen years ago, which means the scar survived everything else. The Choir took his name, his past, his memories tied to his voice, and left him a scar like a signature he couldn't read.

I stopped walking. Rain ran off my hood and dripped onto my collar and I stood there like an idiot while the grandmother haggled and lost, bought nothing, and shuffled off satisfied with herself.

He looked up.

Here is what I had prepared myself for, in the weeks I spent not preparing: that seeing him would undo me. That I'd cross the market, take his face in my hands, and say something ruinous — *you knew me, you chose me, you gave everything and I'm the one carrying it now and I don't know how to put it down.* Here is what actually happened:

He looked up, saw a stranger staring at his stall in the rain, and smiled the polite smile of a man who sells things.

"Can I help you, miss? The travel wards are my best seller, though between us I think it's the knot people like more than the magic."

His voice. His voice was the same voice. That was the cruelest part, and I don't think he'll ever know that, and maybe that's the mercy — that the thing I recognized most, the thing that made my throat close, was the one thing he got to keep.

"No," I managed. "Just looking."

"Take your time. Everything's honest except the luck ones, and those are only half a lie."

Wry. Warm. A stranger's kindness worn comfortably, like a coat broken in by someone else. I picked up a charm — I don't even remember reaching for it; my hands decided things without consulting me that day — a small knotted cord with a sliver of pale wood threaded through it. Wood from the shrine district, probably. From the rebuilt shrine of Vethra, maybe, though the god's silence had lifted along with everything else, and the new covenant didn't need shrines anymore. It needed voices. Anyone could sing now. That was the whole point. That was what he bought.

"That one's for remembering," he said. "Or forgetting. Honestly the old woman I learned the knot from wasn't clear, and I've had both results, so it depends what you're in the market for."

"What do you mean, both results?"

He grinned. "Sold one to a man last month who'd been trying to quit gambling for years. Came back Tuesday to buy six more. Says he can't remember why he ever liked cards." He turned the cord over in his fingers. "And I sold one to a widow who wore it to her husband's grave every day for a month and says she remembers him clearer now than she has since he died. So." He shrugged. "Remembering, forgetting. Depends on what you're holding on to."

"And which are you holding on to?"

It came out before I could stop it — too sharp, too specific, the question of a woman who knew exactly what he was holding and what it had cost him. Something flickered across his face. Not recognition. Recognition would have undone me. Just a salesman's instinct that the customer had gone strange, that the sale was delicate now, handle gently.

"Funny," he said slowly. "You say that like you know me."

"I don't," I said. And it was true, in the way that mattered most: I didn't know this man. This man sold charms and joked with grandmothers and had never heard the Choir scream or felt a god's silence lift off his chest like a hand. I had known Isandro Vane, and Isandro Vane was the price of this man's ordinary afternoon.

"Where'd you learn the trade?" I asked, because I couldn't help myself, because some part of me needed to map the edges of what was left.

"Don't know, actually." He said it easily, without the grief it should have carried, and that ease was its own kind of wound. "That's the joke of me. Woke up nineteen days ago in a boarding house on Tanner's Row with a bag of tools and a talent for knots and no idea where either came from. The landlady swears I've lived there two years. There's a ledger with my handwriting in it." He laughed, short and puzzled. "Doesn't feel like mine. But it pays the rent, so here I am."

Nineteen days. The day the realms knit. He hadn't existed as himself before that morning; the boarding house ledger was written in a hand the covenant had invented for him, a history retrofitted around the hole where he used to be. The Choir hadn't just taken his memories. It had built him a scaffolding to stand in, a small fake past, kind and seamless. Somewhere in the Choir, the Echo — the remnant of the last given voice, the one who asked him to finish what it couldn't — was presumably tending all of this. Making sure he landed softly. Making sure no one told him.

"You could ask them," I said. "The Choir. People ask them things now. They answer."

"Do they?" He considered this with genuine curiosity, head tilted, rain dripping from the edge of his stall. "What do they sound like?"

"Like singing. Like a lot of voices that used to be one voice."

"Hm." He looked out at the rain, at the market, at the crooked skyline with its new seams of white Choir-stone. "Seems rude to bother them. Everyone says they paid enough." He said it lightly, the way you'd repeat gossip about a distant war. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Sometimes I hum something I don't know the words to. Wake up with it. Drives me mad trying to place it." He shook it off and smiled at me again. "Anyway. You wanted the charm or not? First one's free today. Rain-day rates."

I should have walked away. I have replayed this a hundred times and I always arrive at the same conclusion and the same failure: I should have paid for the charm, thanked him, and never come back, because mercy means leaving the wound alone, because love expressed as restraint is still love, because he earned his blank page with everything he was and I had no right to write on it.

I took the charm.

* * *

I've been back four times now. I tell myself it's surveillance. I'm very convincing.

He doesn't know me. Each time, I am a stranger — the serious woman who buys charms and asks odd questions and never stays long. He's started calling me "the archivist," because I let that much slip, because a person needs a name to give and mine was the only thing I had left that felt honest. He likes to argue prices down for me even though I'd pay double. He told me about a dog he's adopted, a brindled mutt he found in the rubble district who follows him to the stall and sleeps under it. He named her Bell. He doesn't know why the name came to him. He said it just *arrived*, fully formed, like the knot-work, like the humming.

Bell. Sera of the Broken Bell. Maerin's memory-hold shattered in the catastrophe; Doss told me once, drunk and hollowed out with guilt, that Isandro used to call me that behind my back, before we were allies, when I was just the Inquisitor hunting him — *the broken bell, all noise, no ring.* Doss cried when he told me. Doss is doing penance work in the rubble district now, hauling stone for the re-singing crews, and he won't accept forgiveness from anyone, least of all himself, and his brother writes him letters he doesn't open. But that's his chapter, not mine.

The point is: I named his dog without meaning to. Or he named it, from somewhere, from the sediment of himself that even the covenant couldn't reach. Things survive. Not memories, exactly — not whole — but residue. The scar. The knots. The song he hums without words. The name of a bell that broke.

Yesterday I watched him give a charm to a child for free and tell her, gravely, that it only worked if she was brave at least once a week, and she nodded like this was a contract with terms she intended to honor, and I had to turn away and pretend to examine chestnuts until my eyes stopped burning. Because that was him. That was always him. Strip away the prophecy and the god and the war and the terrible arithmetic of sacrifice, and underneath it all Isandro Vane was a man who charged fair prices and lied charmingly to children for their own good. The Choir took everything tied to his voice, and it turns out a man is more than his voice. A man is also what his hands do when no one's keeping score.

So here is what I've decided, here on this page, in ink, where decisions become harder to unmake:

I will not tell him.

Not tomorrow, not when the guilt gets loud, not even if he asks — and someday he might ask, because the humming hasn't stopped, because sometimes when I linger at the stall he looks at me a half-second too long with an expression like a word on the tip of his tongue, and I see him almost reach for something and then decide not to. If he ever truly asks, really asks, I don't know what I'll do. But I will not offer. I will not be the one who takes the payment back. He bought a world with his yesterday. The least any of us can do is let him keep the purchase.

Instead I'll do something smaller. I'll keep coming to the market on Thursdays. I'll learn his new rhythms instead of mourning his old ones. Maybe I'll buy a bad umbrella from the boy next door so he stops fighting it in the wind. Maybe one day, months from now, I'll ask him to teach me the knot that remembers, and I'll tell him it's for a friend I lost, and it will be true in every way he doesn't know about.

And last night — I'll write this down because otherwise I'll convince myself I imagined it — last night, as I was leaving, he handed me a charm I hadn't asked for. Small, pale wood, the remembering-forgetting knot.

"For you," he said. "No charge. You've got the look of someone carrying too much, and I've got the feeling these help with that." He paused, frowned slightly at his own hands, at the cord, as if they'd moved without him. "Strange. I don't usually give those away."

"Why not?"

"Don't know. Feels like giving away something that isn't mine to give." He shrugged and the frown cleared, sun through cloud. "Take care of yourself, archivist."

I walked home in the rain with the charm closed in my fist, and halfway down Tanner's Row, when I wasn't listening — when I was thinking about nothing, thinking about chestnuts and umbrellas and the price of rope — it hummed.

One note. Soft. Patient. Like a voice that had been singing a long time and could afford to wait for the rest of the choir to find it.

I stopped under an awning and held it up to the grey light, and I swear to every god still singing, it hummed again — and this time, beneath the note, so faint I might have dreamed it, there were syllables. Two of them. Shaped like a name.

I closed my hand around it and did not listen.

Not yet.
