## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fence post had rotted through at the base again, and Quentin Coldharbour knelt in the wet grass with her sleeves pushed up and her hands black to the wrist, thinking that a hundred years was a very long time to keep mending the same fence.

She dug around the post with a trowel until she could lever it free, then set the new one—cedar, split and pointed by her own hand two winters ago, seasoned under the lean-to—and tamped the earth down around it with the flat of the blade. Not the sword. She never used the sword for work. The sword hung inside on its pegs above the hearth, wrapped in oiled cloth like something sleeping, which was more or less what it was.

"Third one this year," she said aloud, to nobody. The valley didn't answer. It never answered. But talking was a habit she'd stopped fighting somewhere around year forty, when the silence had begun to feel less like peace and more like a room with all the furniture covered in sheets.

The morning came up gray and soft over the eastern ridge, the way it did most mornings, fog pooling in the low fields like milk someone had forgotten to pour back. Quentin straightened, pressed her fists into her lower back, and surveyed her kingdom: forty acres of orchard going wild at the edges, a garden gone to seed for the season, a stone cottage with a slate roof she'd re-laid twice, a well that never ran dry, a stream that never flooded. A valley cupped between two ridges like water in a palm.

The Hollow. That's what she'd come to call it, though once it had probably had another name—the name of whoever owned it before, or whatever it was called on maps of a country that—

She stopped that thought the way you'd stop yourself from probing a tooth. Carefully. With practice.

*Magic cannot enter,* she reminded herself, as she did every morning, a litany worn smooth as a prayer bead. *And nothing leaves without my leave.*

That second part wasn't strictly true. Apples left, in baskets she carried down the goat track to the road at the valley's mouth, where she traded them—when anyone came—for salt, flour, nails, news. People left, too: peddlers, pilgrims, the occasional lost hunter who'd wandered off the high road and found himself inexplicably weeping among her pear trees. They always left. What they never did was remember much. That was the other rule, the one she'd learned slowly and paid for dearly, in the early years, when she'd tried to make friends.

The Hollow forgot people. Not all at once—a face here, a name there, the reason they'd come, the color of a coat—but steadily, the way a river smooths a stone. Visitors walked out of the valley lighter than they'd walked in, and by the time they reached the next town they remembered only an orchard, an old woman, a kindness. Sometimes not even that. Quentin had watched a peddler stand at the valley mouth one autumn evening, looking back up at her with polite blankness, and she had understood that to him she was already becoming weather. Something seen. Nothing kept.

So she'd stopped trying to be kept. She grew her apples. She kept her fences. She sharpened a sword that no longer sang, and she told herself this was enough, and for whole seasons at a stretch, she almost believed it.

* * *

The whetstone made its small patient sound in the kitchen that afternoon: shk, shk, shk, like a slow heart.

Quentin worked the edge of a skinning knife first, then the scythe, then the billhook, laying them out on the oilcloth in order of size, because order was a thing she could make even when nothing else cooperated. Outside, rain had started—not hard, just enough to bead on the window and run down in long silver threads. The fire ticked. The kettle muttered.

Last, because she always saved it for last, she took down the sword.

It was a beautiful thing and had been a terrible one, and both facts lived in her hands every time she touched it. Long, slightly curved, forged in some smithy whose smoke had stopped rising a century ago. The grip was ray-skin gone ivory with age. Along the flat, where another blade might carry a maker's mark, runes were cut deep and filled with silver that had never tarnished, because the tarnishing would have required time to touch them, and time had never quite dared.

A swordspeaker's blade. Hers. The last one, because she was the last one.

She unwrapped the cloth from the hilt—she kept the edge wrapped separately; no sense dulling it against dust—and drew the whetstone along the steel in long, even strokes. The sound it made was just the sound of metal on stone. That was all. For a hundred years it had been only that.

Once—she let herself remember this exactly once a year, on the eve of her anniversary, and today was that eve—it had sung. Not metaphor. Actually sung: a note below hearing that lived in the bones rather than the ears, and when it sang, words came with it, hers and not hers, the old oath rising up through her chest and out of her mouth in a voice like a bell being struck underwater. *By root and by ruin. By the door and the dark behind it.* She couldn't say the whole thing anymore. She'd tried, in the early decades, standing in the middle of the orchard at midnight, and what came out was a shape without substance, a key ground down past its teeth. The oath needed magic the way a voice needs breath, and the Hollow gave her nothing but quiet.

Which was the point. Which was why she'd chosen it.

Shk. Shk. Shk.

"You're just a knife now," she told the sword, wiping it down. "An expensive, ugly-tempered knife." The runes caught the firelight and threw it back cold. She wrapped the blade, hung it up, and put her back to it, the way you turn your back on a grave you've decided not to visit more than once a year.

Supper was barley and the last of the salt pork and an apple baked in the coals, sweet and soft as a lie. She ate at the table by the window because she liked watching the rain come down through the lamplight, and halfway through, she caught herself setting out a second cup, and stopped, and looked at it, and put it back on the shelf with exaggerated care, as though the gesture meant nothing, as though she hadn't performed it four thousand times over a hundred years.

Loneliness in the Hollow was a strange animal. It didn't howl. It purred. It curled up beside you at night warm as a cat and purred you to sleep, and that was the trap, because contentment and loneliness made nearly the same sound, and after enough years you could no longer tell which one was keeping you company.

"I'm fine," she said to the empty chair, mildly defiant. "I'm perfectly fine."

The valley, naturally, said nothing.

* * *

The rules, for the record—and she kept records, eleven volumes of them now, in a cramped hand that had started confident and grown smaller—were these:

One. Magic does not enter the Hollow. Spells slid off it. Curses broke against the ridgeline like waves on rock. Enchanted things that crossed the boundary came in mute: charms went dead, conjured fires guttered, talking animals stood blinking and ordinary and confused about why their throats hurt. And swordspeakers' oaths, which lived half in the world and half in the speaker, went silent at the border and stayed silent within.

Two. The Hollow forgets. Names, faces, purposes—all of it wore away in visitors' minds within a day or two of leaving. Quentin alone remembered everything, because she was not a visitor, and because whatever she was, she was bound to the place in ways she preferred not to examine closely on ordinary evenings.

Three. Nothing in the valley aged quite right, including her. She'd come here at what she'd then have called late middle age, gray already threading her hair, knees already complaining. A hundred years later her hair was still threaded gray, not white; her knees complained on the same damp days. Time moved in the Hollow the way light moved through deep water—present, but thinned.

There had been a fourth rule once. She'd written it down in volume one, and then crossed it out so thoroughly the page had nearly torn, and she had not reread it since. Some rules you write so you can forget you know them.

She closed volume eleven and shelved it with the others and banked the fire, and that was when she heard the bells.

Not real bells. There were no bells within fifty miles. This was memory-bells, the kind that ring in the skull on certain evenings, and she knew exactly what evening it was, because she had been counting toward it since midsummer the way you count toward a storm you can't do anything about.

Tomorrow she would have been in the Hollow one hundred years.

She stepped outside into the rain-washed dark. The clouds had torn open along the west and the first stars were out, sharp and indifferent, and the valley lay below her doorstep in bands of blue shadow: orchard, field, stream, and beyond them, faintly visible even now, the pale line of the old boundary stones circling the whole basin like a scar.

"A hundred years," she told the stars. "You'd think I'd feel different."

What she felt was the way you feel the night before leaving a house you've lived in forever—even if you're not leaving, even if there is nowhere else, the body keeps the anniversary anyway. Somewhere in her chest, under the ribs, in the place where the oath had once lived like a second heartbeat, something turned over in its sleep.

She stood there a long while, letting the cold work into her shoulders, doing the arithmetic she always did on this night and always hated: of everyone who had sworn beside her, everyone whose voices she could still hear in the cadences of the old rites, everyone who might conceivably have survived what came after—how many? She wrote the number fresh each year in the front of volume twelve, and each year the number was the same.

One.

Her.

Then, because the night was clear and her legs wanted walking, she took the lantern and went down through the orchard to check the boundary stones, which was a ritual with no purpose, since the stones had not moved in a century and would not move tomorrow either, but rituals were the only furniture she had left that belonged to her old life, and she held on to them the way drowning sailors hold on to anything that floats.

The stones were where the stones always were. Twelve of them, waist-high, unmarked to any eye but hers—on each one, visible only when you already knew to look, was a single rune: the sigil of severance, the mark they cut into things they wanted to cut off from the rest of creation. She laid her palm against the nearest. Cold granite, damp moss, nothing else. No hum. No whisper. Just stone, holding up its little corner of an invisible wall.

"Still there," she reported to nobody. "Still mine."

On the way back, passing the third row of pear trees, she noticed the light.

Down near the valley mouth, where the goat track met the road, there was a smudge of orange bobbing in the dark. Lantern-light, moving. Coming up the track.

Quentin stopped between the trees and went very still, the way she'd once gone still in other years, in another country, listening for worse things than travelers. Peddlers came sometimes—two, three times a year, drawn by her apples and by the odd gravity the Hollow exerted on people who traveled alone at night and took wrong turnings. But they came by day. Nobody climbed that track after full dark. Nobody except—

Except, once every decade or so, somebody who arrived without seeming to decide to arrive. Somebody the road itself seemed to have handed over, the way a current hands a leaf to an eddy. In a hundred years it had happened four times, and three of those four had changed her life, and not gently.

The light bobbed closer. She could make out a figure now, slight, hooded, leading a pack-mule that picked its way down the steep track with the resigned air of an animal that had given up questioning its owner's choices. A trader, maybe. A refugee from the weather. Or—

The wind shifted, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath both, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something that had no business being anywhere within a hundred miles of the Hollow:

Ozone. The smell magic makes when it dies.

It shouldn't have been possible. Magic couldn't enter the valley; that was rule one, rule zero, the law underneath all laws. Whatever the traveler carried, it should have gone dead at the boundary stones, dumb as any charm.

But the smell was there, thinning with every step the stranger climbed, fading like a voice going away down a corridor—which meant it hadn't died at the stones at all. It had followed the stranger in anyway, clinging to them like a scent, like grief, like something the valley couldn't quite wash off.

Quentin set down her lantern on the grass, deliberately, so her hands would be free, and waited by the gate with her arms crossed and her heart doing something complicated she refused to name.

"Ho, the orchard," the traveler called, when the lantern-light finally reached her—a youngish voice, neither man's nor woman's, cheerful in the way of someone who has talked to themselves on roads for a very long time. "I'm told there's an old woman up this valley who trades apples for stories. I've got stories to spare."

"Who told you that?" Quentin asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later.

The traveler pushed back their hood. Rain-dark hair, quick eyes, a smile with too much intelligence in it for comfort. Behind them, the mule sighed.

"That's the strange part," the traveler said. "Nobody did. I just—" They frowned, as if hearing the sentence for the first time as they said it. "I woke up three towns back with your name written in a book I've never seen before. And tonight's date circled under it."

* * *

Later—much later, after tea, after the mule was bedded in the barn, after the traveler had fallen asleep in front of the fire mid-sentence, worn out by whatever strange errand had carried them up a mountain track at night—Quentin sat across the room and watched them breathe, and did not sleep at all.

They had a pack, and in the pack, they'd said, was the book. They hadn't offered to show it. She hadn't asked. Both of them had understood, with the wordless precision of two people who have spent their lives keeping secrets, that some doors are better left shut until morning.

But she had seen the cover, just for a moment, when they'd shrugged the pack off in the doorway. Water-stained leather. Gilt lettering rubbed almost to nothing.

And beneath it, embossed deep enough to survive the water, the sigil she had spent a hundred years not dreaming about: a circle broken by a single vertical line.

The mark of a country that no longer existed.

Quentin sat in the dark with her back to her sleeping sword, and listened to the rain begin again, and thought: *tomorrow I will have been here a hundred years, and today the past has climbed my mountain to find me.*

Outside, far down the valley, one of the boundary stones made a sound.

It had never made a sound before.
