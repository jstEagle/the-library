## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The peddler came up the valley road at dusk, when the light went the color of old honey and the fences threw shadows long enough to trip a man twice his height.

Quentin saw him first from the garden, a smudge of motion on the road that had no business carrying motion. Nobody came to the Hollow. That was the whole point of the Hollow. In a hundred years she could count her visitors on one hand without opening it all the way: a lost shepherd who'd wandered out three days later insisting he'd never seen a valley at all, a hawk that wasn't a hawk, and once—she didn't count this one—a voice on the wind that said her name in her mother's accent and was probably wind.

The peddler walked with a pack that looked too heavy for him and moved like it weighed nothing. He was young. They were always young, the ones who found their way in, because youth was its own kind of hole in the world's attention.

She put down the trowel. Wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though she hadn't grown anything worth aprons in years—the soil here tolerated beans and refused anything sweeter—and went to meet him at the gate.

"You're lost," she said.

He blinked at her. He had the flat, washed-out look of someone who'd been walking much longer than he'd planned. "Is this—" He turned around, as if the road behind him might have changed while he wasn't looking. Roads did that. Not here, but he wouldn't know that. "I was following the river."

"There's no river."

"I was following where the river was," he said, with the particular despair of the very tired, and something in Quentin's chest shifted an inch, the way it always did when the outside world leaked in wearing a human face.

"Come in," she said. "You can have soup and a night under a roof. Then you walk back the way you came and you don't come again."

He nodded the way people nod at kindness they've stopped expecting, and followed her inside.

* * *

His name was Tam. He was maybe twenty, with chapped hands and the smell of wet wool and lamp oil, and he talked the way lonely people talk when someone finally feeds them—about the fair at Marrowfield, about his sister who kept the ledger, about the roads going strange this year, folk saying the maps were wrong, towns not where they'd been. Quentin listened with half an ear and ladled soup with the other half, and let none of it past the outer wall of herself. The world outside was always going strange. It had been going strange since before he was born. She'd simply stopped being invited to watch.

Her sword hung above the hearth, the way it had hung in every house she'd built or borrowed in the Hollow. It was called Vesper, though she almost never used the name anymore, the way you stop using a dead friend's name because it costs more each time. It didn't sing. It had stopped singing ninety-seven years ago, when the oaths went quiet and the country that held them went wherever erased things go. It was just steel now. Good steel. She sharpened it anyway, every week, on a whetstone gone smooth as river rock, because a woman needed rituals and that one was hers.

Tam stared at it over his soup. "That's a fine blade."

"It's a heavy blade."

"You a soldier?"

"I'm a gardener," Quentin said, "with opinions about fences."

He laughed, and something about the laugh was so ordinary, so unmagical, so gloriously free of prophecy, that she let herself enjoy the evening. This was the trick of the Hollow, she told herself, the bargain she'd struck with it: magic couldn't follow her in, and neither, mostly, could meaning. Things happened here—beans grew, fences mended, visitors left—but things didn't *mean*. A hundred years of Tuesdays. She had earned a hundred years of Tuesdays.

In the morning she fed him eggs and pointed him down the road south. He shouldered his pack, hesitated, and dug into it.

"For the soup," he said, and pressed something into her hands before she could refuse. "It's water-struck. Worth nothing to me now. But you strike me as someone who reads."

Then he was walking, and then he was a dot, and then the road was empty the way roads in the Hollow were always empty, and Quentin stood at her gate holding a book.

It was small, octavo, bound in gray cloth gone soft as skin. Water had gotten into it somewhere along its long life; the cover bloomed with brown continents of mold, and the pages were furred at the edges, stuck together in clumps. On the spine, in flaking gilt: *Prognostications of the Century, Being a True Account of What Is To Come, Set Down in the Year 1923 by the Choir Scribes of —*

The rest of the title was eaten by water damage. The name of the place had been there. The name of the place was gone.

Quentin stood at her gate for a long time. The wind moved the grass. Somewhere a fence post creaked, the third one from the corner, the one she'd been meaning to replace since spring.

She went inside and did not open the book. She set it on the table between the salt cellar and the lamp, the way you'd set down something venomous but interesting, and she spent the day doing everything else. She replaced the third fence post. She sharpened Vesper, though it wasn't the day for it. She pulled weeds that didn't need pulling. When the light failed she lit the lamp, sat down across from the book, and glared at it like an opponent across a chessboard.

"You're nothing," she told it. "You're a peddler's stock. You're mold and paper and some dead fool's guesses."

The book said nothing. Books from 1923 were good at that.

She opened it.

* * *

The pages smelled of pond and time. The script inside was dense, formal, two-columned, the work of people paid by the prophecy. Most of it was the usual gruel—kings dying, harvests failing, a comet in the year whatever, doom arriving on schedule the way doom does in books written by professionals. The water damage made islands of the text. Whole sentences drowned. Quentin read the way you read through rain-streaked glass, catching faces in the gaps.

She told herself she was reading out of boredom. She was a hundred years old, give or take the parts she didn't count; boredom was a legitimate reason to read a moldy book. Her hands were steady. Her hands were completely steady.

Near the middle, a heading: *Concerning the Survivors of the Sundering, Who Are Anchors, and the Hour of Their Unbinding.*

The word *survivors* went through her like a splinter entering skin—painless on entry, deepening on its own.

The column below was damaged badly. She got fragments. *...the oath-bound shall endure beyond the erasure... seven anchors named... the last swordspeaker among them...* Her eyes snagged on *last swordspeaker* the way a sock snags on a nail, and she read the next line four times without it getting any clearer, because the line after it was submerged in a brown tide, and when the text surfaced again it was already talking about something else, some duke, some winter.

Seven anchors. She knew that word from nowhere and everywhere. It rang against a door in her that she'd nailed shut so long ago she'd forgotten the door existed.

She turned pages. The book went on prophesying dukes. She went back. She read forward again, hunting the drowned sentence, and this is how she found the calendar section at the back—pages of dates, each with its note, the scribes working through the century like farmers planting fields.

And there, in the middle of the page, a date circled twice in ink gone rusty with age.

Tonight's date.

Not approximately. Not the right season, the right month. The date. The same arrangement of numbers she'd watched arrive a thousand mornings in the Hollow, the anniversary she privately refused to celebrate, the eve of her hundredth year behind walls magic couldn't cross.

Beside the circle, in a different hand—smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper—two words.

*Coldharbour sings.*

Quentin did not move for a long time. The lamp burned. A moth ticked against the glass chimney. Outside, the valley did what the valley always did: nothing, beautifully, forever.

*Coincidence,* she thought.

Coldharbour was not a rare name. There must be hundreds of Coldharbours. Thousands. It meant cold harbor; it was practically generic; any coastal town with a bad winter could claim it. And *sings*—choirs sang, bells sang, kettles sang. The scribe could have meant anything. A festival. An eclipse. Some duke's wedding, since the book seemed fond of dukes.

She noticed she was gripping the edge of the table.

She noticed her true name sitting behind her teeth like a stone she'd swallowed a century ago. Because the book hadn't said Coldharbour, not exactly. The water damage had taken letters, yes—but the hand that wrote those two words, the hurried hand, had known. She felt it the way you feel a stranger using your childhood nickname: the word fit a lock only one key ever touched. Whoever wrote *Coldharbour sings* had meant *her*, Quentin Coldharbour, last of a rank of one, and had written it in 1923, sixty-one years before she'd finished crawling into the only hiding place left in the world.

"That's impossible," she said aloud, and her voice sounded fine, level, reasonable, a gardener's voice, a woman with opinions about fences.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached professional interest, the way you'd watch someone else's hands, someone whose story this wasn't.

Because here was the thing she knew, the thing every oath-bound child learned with their first lesson: prophecies about the oath-bound weren't predictions. They were appointments. The Choir Scribes hadn't guessed the future—they'd *read* it, off the oaths themselves, the way you read a scar to know how a bone broke. If this book named tonight, then tonight had been decided before she was born, and everything she'd built—the fences, the beans, the hundred years of Tuesdays—wasn't a life she'd chosen but a fuse she'd been standing on, burning at the speed of an ordinary day.

"No," she said again, and this time it wasn't denial, it was refusal, and refusal was the closest thing she had left to an oath.

She closed the book. She picked it up to throw it in the stove.

She didn't throw it in the stove.

She stood there holding it, arms cocked, and understood that she couldn't—not because it was precious, but because it was *information*, and Quentin Coldharbour had survived a century on exactly three things: stubbornness, whetstones, and never, ever throwing away information. Even information shaped like a hook. Especially information shaped like a hook, because hooks were bait, and bait meant a fisher, and after a hundred years of silence she wanted—God help her, some starved animal part of her *wanted*—to know who was still fishing these waters.

Someone had sent this. Peddlers didn't find books; books found peddlers. Someone out there in the erased dark knew her name and her date and had wrapped both in gray cloth and set them walking toward her gate.

She put the book down. She banked the fire. She lay in bed with Vesper propped against the nightstand, which she hadn't done in decades, and listened to the valley breathe.

Sleep came slow and shallow. And in the hour before dawn, when even the Hollow forgets to pretend, Quentin dreamed—not of the past, not of the war, not of the singing, but of a door. Just a door. Standing alone in a meadow, frame and leaf and lintel, no wall around it, no house, no path. Old wood, black iron hinges. And from underneath it, faint as breath on winter glass, came a sound she had spent one hundred years not listening for.

A choir, humming.

She woke with dawn in the window and the word already in her mouth, tasting of iron and ash, and she said it out loud to the empty room because saying it made it smaller:

"Tonight."

Outside, on the south road, far off but coming closer, she heard wheels.
