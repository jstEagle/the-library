## 3. The Oath That Will Not Sleep

The dream came the way weather comes to the Hollow: all at once, and wrong.

Quentin woke with her hand already on the knife under the pillow, which was the old habit speaking before the woman could. She lay in the dark of the cottage she had built twice — once properly, once after the first version collapsed into a bad joke of carpentry — and listened.

Nothing. The valley's particular nothing: no owl, because owls were magic-adjacent and the Hollow starved them out; no wind worth naming; just the creak of her own house settling around her like an old dog turning in its sleep. A hundred years of that sound. She could have mapped every joint and nail by ear.

But something had woken her. Something below the sound of nothing.

She got up, lit the lamp, and stood a long time listening to herself breathe. The prophecy book sat on the table where she'd left it, still open, still showing that circled date beside a name she hadn't spoken aloud since the world ended the first time. She had turned it face-down before bed. It was face-up now.

She did not remember turning it back over. She told herself firmly that she must have, that memory at ninety-nine was a leaky bucket, that peddlers' books did not rearrange themselves in the night.

The book said otherwise. The book, water-stained and patient, seemed to be waiting for her to admit what it knew.

"No," Quentin told it, conversationally, because talking to yourself was one thing but talking to books was another, and she liked to keep her madness sorted into categories. "No. Whatever you think is going to happen tonight, I've survived worse on less warning."

Her voice sounded thin in the empty room. It always did. That was the arithmetic of the Hollow: perfect safety divided by zero company.

She made tea she didn't want, because ritual is a kind of armor, and that was when the sword began to hum.

* * *

It hung above the hearth where it had hung for decades — a plain blade with a grip worn to the shape of her hand, unremarkable except that swords are never unremarkable, and this one least of all. In the old country they would have called it by its name, and the name would have answered. Here it was furniture. Here it was dead weight and good steel and a hundred years of silence.

Now it hummed.

Not loudly. Not like song. Like a struck bell heard through three walls — felt in the teeth more than the ears, a single low note that rose and fell as though the sword were breathing. As though it were remembering how.

Quentin set down her cup very carefully. Her hands were doing the shaking thing again, the thing they'd started doing when she read the circled date, and she hated them for it. A hundred years of composure, undone by two days of a wet book and a peddler with clever eyes.

"Stop that," she said to the sword.

It didn't stop. If anything the note deepened, gathered harmonics, and beneath it — or through it, or inside her own skull where the note lived — came words.

Not the sword's words. She knew the difference. The sword had never spoken; *she* spoke through the sword, oaths given shape and edge. What she was hearing now was older and stranger. It was the oath itself. Her oath, the one she'd sworn at nineteen with her knee on cold flagstone and her whole life held out in her cupped hands like an offering:

*I am the door's keeper and the dark's delay. I hold the line my sisters drew—*

It had been silent since the erasure. That was the whole mercy of the Hollow: magic couldn't follow her in, and her magic lived in the oath, so the oath slept, and she slept, and a century passed like one long held breath.

Now it was whispering names.

*Vessa Corrow,* it said, soft as ash settling. *Vessa Corrow holds.*

Quentin's cup cracked against the table. She didn't remember dropping it.

Vessa Corrow had died at the Fall. Everyone said so. Vessa had been rearguard at the western gate while the sky came apart, and Quentin had run east with the last survivors and never looked back, and looking back was the only proof anyone ever had of anything, so Vessa Corrow was dead because everyone needed her to be, because if Vessa had lived then Quentin's running had been—

*Ilse Marnett holds,* said the oath.

"Ilse drowned." Quentin's voice came out flat and hard, a blade laid flat on stone. "Ilse drowned crossing the Stair, forty years before the Fall. I carried her boots."

*Anselm Bright holds. Tam Osgar holds. Mereth of the Nine Holds.*

Nine names. Nine names in the space of ten breaths, each one delivered in that patient whisper, each one a person she had mourned or buried or fled from or — worse — simply stopped thinking about, because a century is long enough to bury anyone, even the living.

They were alive. They were *holding.* Wherever the word *holds* meant anything — wherever there was still a line to keep — nine other oath-bound survivors were keeping it, scattered through a world that had deleted their country from every map and memory except their own.

And the oath knew them. The oath had always known them. It had been carrying their names this whole time like seeds in a closed fist, waiting for some condition to be met, some door to crack open — a peddler's book, perhaps. A circled date. A name written in a dead language beside tonight.

"No," Quentin said again, and this time it wasn't to the book.

Because here was the thing, the shameful thing, the thing she shoved down deep where the Hollow couldn't see it: somewhere under the terror, under the animal certainty that this was how it ended, a small voice in her was singing.

*Nine others.* Ninety-nine years alone, and there had been nine others all along, holding lines in the dark, saying her name maybe, keeping faith with a country that no longer existed. She wasn't the last. She had never been the last. She was only the one who hid.

That hope was obscene. Hope was what got people killed in the last days — hope was why Vessa stayed at the gate, why Ilse tried the crossing, why half the choir sang themselves to pieces holding a seal for a country that had already decided to stop existing. Survival had required her to become someone who did not hope, and she had done it so thoroughly she'd called it peace.

And now a sword was humming on her wall, and the small voice was singing anyway.

*Mereth of the Nine falters,* said the oath, and the whisper changed. Not louder. Closer. As if someone had leaned in near her ear. *Mereth of the Nine falters. The line thins. The door remembers.*

There it was. The phrase from the book, arriving in a voice older than the book. *The door remembers.* Tonight's date, circled in faded ink. And now a faltering sister whose name she hadn't spoken in eighty years, calling across whatever distance remained.

Quentin stood in her kitchen in the smallest hour of the morning, an old woman in a nightshirt with tea going cold and a cracked cup at her elbow, and understood — with the terrible clarity that only comes when the body knows before the mind consents — that the Hollow had stopped working.

Or rather: the Hollow had worked because the wound it covered was stable. The erased country had been a clean cut, cauterized, sealed. But the seal was failing — Mereth's faltering told her that, the sword's waking told her that, the book told her that — and the Hollow wasn't a sanctuary. It was a scab. And scabs do not protect you from the thing underneath. They only tell you where it is.

Magic couldn't enter the valley. But the oath wasn't outside magic. The oath was hers, grown into her like roots into a foundation, and if the seal was cracking wide enough for her own buried magic to wake —

Then everything she'd hidden from could reach her here. Everything she'd been hiding *from* was already in the room.

* * *

She dressed. Breeches, boots, the wool coat she mended every autumn whether it needed it or not. She took the sword down off the wall.

It weighed what it had always weighed, which is to say nothing much until you swore something, and then everything. The humming stopped the moment her hand closed on the grip — not silenced, but attentive. Listening. Waiting for her the way a dog waits at a door it has scratched at for years.

"Don't get comfortable," she muttered. "We're not friends anymore."

The pommel was warm. Swords don't warm. She held it anyway.

Outside, the Hollow lay silvered under a moon too thin to be honest, meadows and fences and her crooked orchard all exactly where she'd left them, exactly as they'd been for a century. Safe. Still. A painting of a life hung in a frame of mountains.

She walked the fence line because her feet chose it without consulting her, and halfway along the eastern wall, where the ground sloped toward the pass that led out — the pass no road used, the direction she had come from and never gone back to — she felt it.

A pull. Gentle as a hook behind the sternum. The same direction the whispering came from, the same direction as everything: the door, the seal, the country-that-wasn't, the nine holding names. All of it tugging on her like tide on a moored boat, and the mooring rope was her oath, and the rope was fraying.

*Mereth of the Nine falters.*

If Mereth failed — if one anchor let go — what happened to the rest? To the seal? To the eight still holding? To the ordinary people of the ordinary world, who slept tonight in towns built on ground that remembered being something else?

To her?

Quentin pressed her free hand flat against the fence post and breathed until the shaking slowed. This was the discipline of a century: fear arrives, fear is catalogued, fear is set aside for later, and later never comes because later is when you're dead.

Except later had come. Later was tonight. The date was circled.

"I should burn the book," she said aloud.

She wouldn't. They both knew it — she and the sword, she and the oath, she and the small singing voice. Burning the book was what the hiding-woman would do, and the hiding-woman had been useful, had kept her alive through a hundred quiet years, but the hiding-woman's lease was up. Someone had sent that book to survivors. Someone wanted the anchors found. And Mereth of the Nine was faltering right now, tonight, in the space between one breath and the next, and Quentin Coldharbour had sworn — nineteen years old, knee on stone, whole life in her hands — to hold the line her sisters drew.

The line was drawing taut. It was pulling her out of the valley.

She went back inside. She wrapped bread and cheese and the last of the smoked fish in cloth, filled both waterskins, took the flint and the oilcloth and the whetstone she'd used more for comfort than function all these years. She packed the way she'd packed at nineteen: fast, ruthless, expecting to leave things behind that she'd grieve later.

Last, she took the book. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, heavier than a book should be, heavy the way a key is heavy, which is to say not at all, which is to say immeasurably. She wrapped it in oilcloth and put it against her spine where she'd feel it move.

At the door she stopped. Looked back at the room. The cracked cup on the table. The chair shaped to her body by decades of sitting. The hearth swept clean. A museum of one woman's survival, curated by a coward, and beautiful, God help her, it was beautiful, and leaving it felt like dying, and staying *was* dying, just slower and with better furniture.

"The door remembers," she said, testing the words in her own mouth.

The sword, slung across her back, gave one low note of assent.

Quentin Coldharbour stepped out of her cottage, pulled the door shut, and did not lock it. There was no one left in the valley to steal from her, and besides — some part of her, the part already walking toward the pass, already counting the hours since Mereth's name had whispered *falters* — suspected she was coming back. Or suspected she wasn't. Either way, locks were for people who believed doors still worked the way they were supposed to.

The moon had set by the time she reached the eastern slope. Below her, the Hollow spread out in the grey pre-light, complete and contented and false as a painted backdrop. Above her, through the pass, the air had a texture she hadn't felt in a hundred years: the faint static pressure of the world where magic lived, reaching toward her like a hand recognizing a hand.

The oath stirred against her ribs, warm as a second heartbeat, and whispered one more time — not a name this time, not a report, but something almost like instruction, almost like pleading, in the voice of every sister who had ever knelt on cold flagstone beside her:

*Come hold the line. Come quickly. We are so few, and it is so hungry, and the door remembers.*

Quentin tightened the straps on her pack, settled the sword, and began to climb.

Behind her, in the cottage by the dead hearth, the prophecy book lay wrapped and packed, and the fire she'd banked for the night guttered and went out — the first fire in the Hollow, in a hundred years, to die unattended.
