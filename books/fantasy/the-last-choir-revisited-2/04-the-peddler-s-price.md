## 4. The Peddler's Price

Quentin did not sleep.

She sat at her table with the prophecy book open in front of her and a pot of tea going cold beside it, and she watched the circled date like a hawk watches a field mouse. The ink had bled where the water got in, but the circle itself was deliberate—drawn twice around, pressed hard enough to dent the page beneath. Someone had wanted this date found. Someone had wanted *her* name beside it, in letters that spelled Quentin Coldharbour with an archivist's neat hand, not a peddler's scrawl.

By the time the light came up gray over the eastern ridge, she had made herself three promises, and she said them aloud because saying things aloud was how she kept them.

*I will find the peddler.*

*I will not show fear.*

*I will not leave the valley until I understand what's coming for me.*

The third promise was the one that cost her. A hundred years of discipline said: pack light, walk fast, be gone before whatever this is finds its feet. But the book had her true name in it, and true names were not things you outran. She'd learned that lesson at nineteen, on a night she still could not think about without her hands going cold. You could cross oceans from your name. It would wait for you, patient as water, on the far shore.

So instead of packing, she sharpened her sword.

It was a morning ritual older than her life in the Hollow, though the ritual had changed. Once, the whetstone's stroke had drawn a sound out of the blade—a low harmonic, almost a word, the way a wet finger draws a note from a glass rim. The swordspeakers called it waking the steel. Her oath lived in that sound; when the blade sang, the oath answered, and between them they could do things Quentin had spent ninety-nine years pretending she hadn't done.

Now the stone moved over metal and produced nothing but the ordinary shriek of an edge being honed. The sword was just a sword. It had been just a sword since the day she crossed into the Hollow, because magic could not follow her here, and an oath was magic, and so the voice in the steel had gone silent as a dead man's letter.

She finished the edge anyway. Habits were load-bearing.

Then she put on her coat, took down a walking staff, and went out to hunt a peddler.

* * *

Wren had come through the Hollow two days ago, which was itself a small impossibility. The valley had exactly one road in and one road out, both of them long, both of them watched by nothing but weather, and Quentin knew every face that used them the way a lighthouse keeper knows ships. In a century she had catalogued perhaps forty visitors: surveyors who never surveyed, a lost shepherd, a tax man from a government that no longer existed, pilgrims of one superstition or another who always left disappointed because the Hollow granted no miracles. It only granted silence.

The peddler had arrived on foot with a cart too heavy for one person to pull, and had somehow pulled it anyway. Quentin had watched them from her kitchen window with the particular attention she gave anything that broke the valley's rules, and had decided—wrongly, she now understood—that a cart and a stranger were merely a cart and a stranger.

They had stayed one night at the old waystation by the ford, sold nothing to anyone, bought nothing, spoken to no one, and left before dawn heading south along the river road. Two days later, Quentin found their book in her woodshed.

Not left behind. *Placed.* She was nearly sure of it now. It had been sitting on the chopping block, square to the grain of the wood, spine toward the door, open to the marked page. Nobody abandoned a book that way. Somebody presented one.

She followed the river road south.

The Hollow in autumn was beautiful in the way of places that have stopped trying to impress anyone. Birch gone gold, rowan gone red, the fields cut and stubbled, the air smelling of leaf-rot and coming frost. Quentin walked it fast, staff clicking, and let the landscape do what it always did: remind her that she loved this place, and that loving it had never once been the same thing as being happy in it.

Around midday she caught sight of the cart.

It was stopped on the road ahead, half in shadow under a leaning alder, and the peddler was standing beside it doing something fussy and small-handed to one of the wheels. From a distance they looked like any traveling tinker: broad-brimmed hat, coat the color of undyed wool, a figure built for going unnoticed. Quentin slowed her pace and studied them the way she'd learned to study everything, back when studying things quickly and correctly was the difference between dying and eating dinner.

Something about the wheel was wrong. She couldn't say what. The peddler wasn't repairing it; there was no tool in hand. They were touching it, or arranging it, or—she squinted—drawing on it? No. Too fine for drawing. Marking it. The way you'd mark a map.

*Of course,* she thought, and her stomach dropped through the road. *A mapmaker. Of course it would be a mapmaker.*

Because that was the other thing about the book. She hadn't let herself look closely last night—fear makes a coward of the eyes—but this morning, before leaving, she had gone through it properly, and the thing was full of maps. Hand-drawn maps of places Quentin knew had been erased from every atlas in the world. Maps of a country that officially had never existed, drawn with such confidence and such grief that the pages seemed to ache. And in the margins, in the same neat hand that had written her name: coordinates. Bearings. Distances. As if someone were charting a route to something, and the something had a name that the water damage had eaten, except for one surviving fragment at the bottom of the final page:

*—Door of the Last Ch—*

Quentin had read that fragment eleven times. She had read it standing up, sitting down, holding the book at arm's length, holding it against her chest. Eleven times. And each time, some part of her that had been quiet for a hundred years shifted in its sleep and began, very softly, to hum.

She approached the cart the way you approach a snarling dog. Sideways. Slowly. With an exit already chosen.

"You're a long way from anywhere," she said.

The peddler looked up. Their face was young—younger than hers by decades, though everyone's was—and unremarkable in the specific way of people who have practiced being unremarkable. Brown eyes, wind-chapped cheeks, a scar through one eyebrow old enough to have gone silver. They met her gaze without flinching, which was the first thing about them that unsettled her. Strangers in the Hollow usually flinched. Strangers usually flinched at *her*, eventually, once they figured out what she was. This one looked at her like she was a problem they'd been expecting.

"Quentin Coldharbour," the peddler said.

Not a question. A statement, delivered flat, delivered first, before she'd given her own name—which meant they had known it already, which meant the book was no accident, which meant—

"Who are you?" Quentin said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later.

"Wren." Just that. No family name, no trade, no origin. "I'm the one who brought you the book."

"I know. I found it in my woodshed."

"No," Wren said, and there was something in their tone—not apology, not quite honesty either, but the careful weight of someone setting down something fragile—"you didn't. I've been carrying that book for six years. I've tried to give it away eleven times. It won't stay given. It comes back to me until I deliver it right."

"Deliver it right to whom?"

"To whoever's name is in it." Wren turned their hat brim down against the light and looked at her sideways, the way she was looking at them. Two wary animals, each waiting for the other to bolt. "Your name is in it. So it's yours. That's all I know, and believe me, it's more than I wanted to know."

Quentin stood there in the road and felt the ground tilt under an argument she didn't yet have words for. *The book found you,* she thought. *That's what they're telling me. The book found you.* She had spent a century believing the Hollow was the one place the past couldn't reach. Magic couldn't cross the boundary. Oaths dissolved at the treeline. Whatever had been done to her country, whatever had been done to her name, none of it could follow her down into this fold of hills.

And yet a book had walked in carrying her true name like contraband, and a stranger had carried the book, and neither of them should have been possible.

"How did you get in here?" she asked. "The valley doesn't let things in. Not things like that book."

"I don't know," Wren said. "I've been asking myself that since the ford." Then, quieter: "Maybe it doesn't stop things. Maybe it only stops certain things. Maybe a book isn't magic. Maybe it's just paper and somebody's handwriting, and the valley has no opinion about paper."

"That's a generous theory for someone who seems to know a lot."

"I know almost nothing," Wren said, "and I would trade most of what I do know to know less. Is that honest enough for you?"

It was, barely. Quentin had interrogated people before—in another life, under another sky, with methods she did not miss—and she knew the texture of a real answer versus a managed one. Wren's answers had the texture of real ones. Ragged, incomplete, slightly ashamed. Liars were smoother. Liars enjoyed themselves.

But honest or not, Wren was still standing between her and the only information in the world that mattered, and they knew it, and Quentin could see them deciding to charge for it.

She decided to get ahead of the price.

"Tell me about the Door," she said. "The Door of the Last Choir. Tell me what you know and I'll owe you a favor. A real one. I keep my debts."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then they laughed—one short, dry note, entirely without humor.

"A favor," they repeated. "From the last swordspeaker. Do you know what I could do with a favor like that, in the world outside? Do you know what people would pay?"

"I'm not in the world outside. Neither are you, at the moment."

"No," Wren agreed. "At the moment I'm in a valley where my oaths go quiet and my hands work properly and I slept eight hours last night for the first time in six years. At the moment I'm the richest mapmaker alive." They turned the hat brim up again, and Quentin saw that their eyes were red-rimmed, tired in a way that sleep didn't fix. "And that's the price, actually. That's the whole price. I want passage. Through the valley. Permission to stay inside it while I finish a job."

"Finish a job," Quentin said slowly. "What job?"

"Mapping it." Wren jerked a thumb at the hills around them, the birches, the river, the ridgeline going amber in the afternoon. "All of it. Every fold, every ford, every fence line. Properly. On paper that will survive."

"Why?"

"Because your valley shouldn't exist." Wren said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "I don't mean that as an insult. I mean it as a fact of cartography. There is no geological record of this depression. No survey ever drew it. No kingdom ever claimed it. It appears on no map made in the last four hundred years, and I have seen maps that predate the maps. And yet here it is, with a road, a ford, a waystation, a hundred years of your firewood smoke in its sky. Something made this place. Something made it *recently*, in the scale of things, and then made sure no one wrote it down." They paused. "Places that get unwritten leave scars, Coldharbour. I chart scars. It's most of what my oaths are good for anymore. And this—" they spread one hand at the entire visible world "—is the largest scar I've ever stood inside."

The wind moved through the alder overhead, and Quentin heard, underneath it, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the sound of her own oath turning over in its sleep. Not awake. Not yet. But restless. Listening.

"The Door," she said again. "You mentioned it. Or the book did. What do you know?"

"I know where it is," Wren said. "Approximately. I know what it's supposed to do—I think. I know that the ghosts at your southern boundary aren't random, whatever story the valley tells about them, and I know that they're guarding something, and I know that the something has a name, and the name is the Door of the Last Choir." They looked at her straight on, and for a moment the practiced blankness fell off their face entirely, and what was underneath was frightened. Genuinely, humanly frightened. "And I know that the book marks tonight's date next to your name, and I know what happens to places like this when the dates in books like that arrive. I've mapped three of them. None of the three exist anymore. Not ruined. Not abandoned. *Unwritten.* No ruins, no records, no memories in the neighboring villages. People who grew up on their borders can't tell you what's missing, only that looking in a certain direction feels like looking into a gap in a tooth."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. She thought of the orchard behind her house. She thought of the way the fog came off the river some mornings in shapes that looked almost like streets.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I send you on your way and bar my door?"

"Then you'll be alone in here when it starts," Wren said, "and I'll be alone out there, and we'll both find out separately whether being alone was the mistake." They shrugged, and the shrug was honest too, which somehow made it worse. "I'd rather not find out separately. That's the whole of my offer. My knowledge for your valley. Passage, shelter, and the right to draw it before it goes."

"Before it goes," Quentin repeated.

"Everything goes," Wren said. "The question the book is asking—the question whoever sent it is asking—is whether anything of this place gets to *leave*. Whether you do." They picked up the cart handle, testing it, and Quentin saw the fine dark lines inked along their knuckles, coordinates and bearings tattooed or written into the skin itself, a mapmaker who carried their trade in their body. "So. Do we have a bargain?"

She should have said no. Some clear-eyed, disciplined, hundred-years-surviving part of her laid out the case neatly: the stranger was a risk, the story was unverifiable, the book might be bait, the whole thing might be a trap set by whatever remained of the country that had bound her oaths, and the correct move—the move that had kept her alive this long—was to close the shutters and let the date pass unmarked.

But another part of her, the part that had hummed when she read the fragment, the part that had whispered names in the night, the part that had been lonely for a century and known it and said nothing—that part had already answered.

"Yes," Quentin said. "Passage. Shelter. Stay as long as the mapping takes. In exchange, everything you know about the Door, shared freely, held back nothing." She hesitated, then added the clause she'd been circling in her head since the ford: "And if it comes to leaving—if the valley really does end—we leave together, or not at all. I don't run faster than the people who told me the truth."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Whatever they saw, they didn't laugh at it, and they didn't thank her for it either. They just nodded once, slow, like a person accepting terms they'd expected to be worse.

"Together or not at all," they echoed. "Spoken like an oath."

"It's not an oath," Quentin said, too quickly. "Oaths are what ruined me. It's a bargain. Bargains can be broken."

"Sure," Wren said mildly, taking up the cart. "Keep telling yourself that."

They started south along the river road, the peddler pulling their impossible cart and Quentin walking beside it with her staff and her silent sword, and the afternoon leaned gold across the valley, and everything looked exactly as peaceful as it had for a hundred years, and Quentin Coldharbour found that she no longer believed in the peace even slightly.

They made camp that night in the lee of the waystation, and Wren unrolled their maps across an overturned crate, and Quentin bent over them, and the first thing she saw—before the coastlines, before the mountains, before the vast white space where a country used to be—was a single line of ink running along the bottom margin of the oldest sheet, in a hand steadier than Wren's, in a hand that matched the one that had written her name in the prophecy book.

It said: *The door remembers what the country was made to forget.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost a plea:

*Ask the ghosts why they stayed.*

Quentin looked up from the map. "Wren. Who wrote this?"

Wren stared at the line for a moment, and then went very still, and then said, in a voice that had just discovered its own bad news:

"I don't know. It wasn't there yesterday."
