## 6. Ghosts of the Broken Choir

The valley's edge was not a line. Quentin had learned that in her first year, when she'd walked the perimeter the way you'd walk a fence, expecting wire or stone or at least a change in the air. There was nothing. The pines simply thinned, and the ground went soft with moss, and then the moss gave way to a gray scree that had never been part of any map she'd seen. Beyond it, the world resumed—hills, cloud, the distant glitter of a river she could not name because naming it would have been a kind of leaving.

She had stood on that scree a hundred times over ninety-nine years and felt nothing but the particular loneliness of a woman looking at a door she couldn't open.

Tonight she stood there with Wren beside her and the sword humming against her back like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

"They're here," Wren said. Not a question. Wren had said *they're here* three times since sundown, each time with the flat certainty of someone reading coordinates off a chart only they could see. "At the tree line. Past the third stand of birch. You won't see them until they want to be seen."

"I've lived here a century, peddler. If there were ghosts at my border, I'd know."

"Would you?" Wren turned to look at her, and in the last light their face did something complicated—not quite a smile, not quite pity. "You knew this valley was a wound. You knew your oath was waking. What exactly do you think you *do* know about this place, Coldharbour?"

Quentin didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They walked down through the birches together, and the sword's hum climbed half a tone, and Quentin thought: *it knows them.* The blade had not sung since the erasure—it had gone silent the day her country stopped existing, the way a voice goes silent in a house where everyone has died—but for two days now it had hummed, low and constant, and she had told herself it was the prophecy book, the date, the dread. Now she understood it was singing toward something. Or away from something. Swordspeakers' blades sang toward oaths, and oaths were what waited past the birches.

The ghosts stood in a rough crescent at the valley's edge, where the scree met the first true grass of the outside world, and there were more of them than she could count at a glance—thirty, forty, perhaps more, standing in the blue hour like people waiting for a coach that had already left.

They were not wisps. They were not the pale shivering things of fireside stories. They looked *finished*. Solid as memory is solid. A woman in a quartermaster's coat with brass buttons gone green. A boy who might have been fifteen once, wearing the collar of a choir school. An old man whose hands were folded in front of him with the terrible patience of someone who has practiced waiting for a very long time. Their clothes were of a cut Quentin hadn't seen since before the erasure—the high collars, the asymmetric buttons, the sashes of the civil choirs—and seeing them was like hearing her mother's voice come out of a stranger's mouth.

The nearest one stepped forward. She was tall, gray-haired, dressed in the field grays of a war that had officially never happened, and she moved with the economy of someone who had once commanded people and had never entirely unlearned it.

"You're the swordspeaker," she said.

"I'm nobody," Quentin said, which was the first lie she'd told in a hundred years, and it came out smooth, worn smooth, like a stone she'd been turning in her pocket all century.

The ghost almost smiled. "I'm Sable. I held the eastern gate at Vell. You'll have heard of Vell."

"No one's heard of Vell. No one's heard of anything." Quentin kept her hand away from the sword hilt, deliberately, the way you keep your hand away from a dog that's growling. "That's rather the point."

"It is." Sable looked past her, at the valley—at the darkening shape of the Hollow, the smoke from Quentin's chimney rising straight up in the windless evening. "So it's true. It really holds them out. Magic can't cross."

"Nothing crosses. That's what makes it mine."

Sable's gaze came back to her, and there was something in it that Quentin recognized and hated recognizing: the look of a person assessing a resource. She'd seen it on officers' faces. On quartermasters'. Once, horribly, in a mirror.

"You've been safe in there for a hundred years," Sable said. "While we—" she gestured, a small precise gesture that took in the whole crescent of ghosts, the scree, the birches, the hundred years, "—stood here."

* * *

They would not come closer. Quentin understood why within minutes, though no one explained it, and the understanding arrived in her body before her mind—a cold spreading under her sternum as she watched the boy in the choir-school collar take one step toward the valley line and stop as if he'd hit glass. He didn't fall. He just stopped, and his face did something young and lost, and he stepped back into the crescent without complaint, the way you step back from a stove you've touched before.

"Broken oaths anchor us," Sable said, when Quentin asked. They had settled—she and Wren—on the scree side of the line, and the ghosts had arranged themselves around them at a distance that was either courtesy or quarantine. "An oath is a rope, swordspeaker. Spoken whole, it ties you to something—to a country, a duty, a person—and the tying is what gives it power. Break the oath and the rope doesn't vanish. It frays. And every frayed end hooks into you." She held up her hand, and in the ghost-light Quentin saw them: thin dark lines running up from the ghost's fingers into her sleeve, like veins drawn in ink. "We broke ours to survive the erasure. We spoke the words of release without meaning them, or we abandoned our posts, or we—" Sable paused, and the pause had weight, the weight of a confession rehearsed so long it had gone hollow. "There are as many ways to break an oath as there are people to break them. The erasure gave us all a choice, and we chose ourselves, and the frayed ends caught. Now we can't go forward into the world—we're tied to the wreck of the country that made us—and we can't go back into your valley, because the valley refuses everything the country touched. Including us."

"Including me," Quentin said quietly. "I'm still bound. My oath never broke. Why can I live there?"

"Because you're not *in* the country anymore, are you?" It was the old man who spoke, the one with the patient hands. His voice was dry as ledger paper. "You're in the hole it left behind. The valley isn't shelter, girl. It's the socket where the tooth was pulled. Magic can't follow you in because magic belongs to the country, and the country is gone, and the valley is the shape of its absence." He looked at her with eyes that held no malice at all, which was somehow worse. "You haven't been hiding from the world. You've been hiding inside the wound."

Wren made a small sound—not quite a laugh. Quentin shot them a look. "Something funny?"

"The mapmaker thing I mentioned," Wren said. "Places that don't exist leave shapes. I've been charting shapes my whole career and calling them anomalies." They shook their head slowly. "I charted this valley once. Forty years ago. I drew it as a blank with edges. I remember thinking: what a strange place, to be so precisely nothing."

"That's the country for you," the old man said. "Precise even in death."

"Enough, Corvel." Sable's voice wasn't sharp; it was tired in a way that had been tired for so long it had become architecture. She crouched down—ghosts crouching, Quentin thought absurdly, ghosts with knees and patience—so that her eyes were level with Quentin's. "You came to the edge tonight because the book found you. Don't bother denying it. The book finds all of you eventually. It found eleven survivors before you. We counted."

The cold under Quentin's sternum spread outward. "Eleven."

"Four came looking for the Door. Three turned back. Two went mad at the border—you may have heard them, some nights, if the wind comes from the east. Two—" Sable's jaw tightened. "Two tried to force the Door open with broken oaths, and the Door took the attempt personally. There are marks on the threshold now. Scorch-marks shaped like people."

"And you're telling me this," Quentin said, "out of neighborly concern."

"I'm telling you because you're the last whole oath in existence, and I need you to understand what that means before you do something stupid with it." Sable rose. Behind her, the crescent of ghosts shifted, a field of grass moving in wind Quentin couldn't feel. "The Door of the Last Choir opens for one thing only. Not blood. Not keys. Not force—ask the two who tried. It opens for a whole oath, freely sung, by a voice that means every word. Do you understand? Every word. Sung aloud, unbroken, by someone whose oath was never broken." Her ink-veined hand opened, palm up, an offering or a plea. "There is exactly one such voice left in the world, swordspeaker. It's humming against your spine right now."

* * *

They built a fire on the scree, because Wren insisted fire helped—*not for warmth,* they said, *for witness; things confess to fires*—and the ghosts drew closer to it without drawing closer to it, ringed around the light at the exact radius their anchors allowed, so that the flames threw shadows of people who cast no shadows themselves. It was, Quentin thought, the most honest image of survival she had ever seen: everyone warmed by a thing none of them could touch.

The stories came slowly, then all at once, the way water comes through a dam.

The quartermaster woman had signed the release-oaths for four hundred soldiers and spoken her own falsely, crossing her fingers behind her back like a child, because her daughter was on a boat and boats needed mothers. The boy in the choir collar—he was called Fen—had run from the evacuation muster because his older brother had already been taken as an anchor and Fen could not be the second son the country spent. The old man, Corvel, had been an archivist, and he had burned pages—actual pages, sworn records—and the burning had been his breaking, and he described it with the flat precision of a man reciting inventory: *eleven ledgers, one codex, the 1908 census of the western provinces.* He had burned them so the devouring god would have less to read, he said, and then he said, quieter, *or so I told myself,* and then he said nothing else for a long time.

Quentin sat with her arms around her knees and listened, and the shameful flicker of hope she'd carried since the oath began whispering names—that she was not the last, that somewhere others endured—curdled and curdled again, because these were the others. These were what endurance looked like from the outside. Not survivors. *Remnants.* People who had paid for their continuing with the only coin the erasure accepted, and who now stood at the edge of a valley that wouldn't have them, holding frayed ropes hooked into their chests, waiting for a Door that required the one thing none of them possessed.

"Why tell me all this?" she asked finally. The fire had burned down to a red heart. "Why does any of it matter to you whether I reach the Door?"

Sable was sitting closest, close enough that the firelight should have lit her face and didn't. "Because the Door doesn't only open," she said. "It *remembers*. Tonight, according to your book, is the night it remembers. And when it remembers, it pulls. Everything anchored to the country feels the pull—the broken oaths most of all. We will be dragged toward that threshold whether we will it or not, swordspeaker, and if it opens while we're being dragged—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "If it opens wrong, it doesn't lead out. It leads *down*. To the thing the country sealed itself away from. The god. The reason for all of it."

"The book didn't mention a god," Wren said.

"The book was written by optimists," Sable said.

Quentin looked at the ghosts ringing the fire—the quartermaster with her crossed-finger penance, the boy Fen watching her with an expression she refused to identify because identifying it would require her to feel it, Corvel with his burned-ledger hands folded in his lap. A hundred years she had believed herself the cost of survival. The last one standing, paying alone. And here was the arithmetic she'd never done: that her wholeness had been purchased by their breaking, that the country had spent its people unevenly, and that she had slept warm in the wound while they stood at the rim of it, hooking their fingers into the earth.

"What happens," she said, "if I sing?"

Sable's head came up sharply. "You don't—"

"If I walk to this Door tonight, tomorrow, whenever it remembers, and I sing my oath whole, the way it was given to me—what happens? To me. To you. To whatever's down there."

The silence stretched. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Then the Door opens clean," Sable said at last, carefully, each word set down like a foot on ice. "And the seal—the last working piece of the country's magic—comes undone, gently or otherwise. And everything anchored to the country goes through it or goes with it. Us included." Her ink-lined fingers curled closed. "You would free us, swordspeaker. All of us. Whatever we are by then."

Behind her, Wren said, very quietly, "And the god."

"Yes," Sable said. "And the god."

Quentin sat with that. The sword hummed against her back, patient as Corvel's hands, and for the first time in a hundred years she understood what it was saying. Not *fight*. Not *flee*. It was singing the way a choir sings at a funeral—toward the door of the church, out into the ordinary light, carrying the box.

She stood. The ghosts rose with her, thirty-odd remnants moving as one, and the sight of it raised the hair on her arms.

"Then I'll need to know where it is," Quentin said. "The Door. Exactly where, and how to reach it before tonight ends."

Sable smiled then, and it was the smile of a woman about to hand over a debt she'd been carrying too long. "That," she said, "is going to cost you."
