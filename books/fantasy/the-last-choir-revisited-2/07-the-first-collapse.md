## 7. The First Collapse

Wren woke to the smell of apples that weren't there.

They lay in the loft of Quentin's cottage with the blanket pulled to their chin and the prophecy book under their head like a stone pillow, and the whole room smelled of orchard—ripe fruit, bruised windfalls, the green bite of leaves crushed underfoot. Wren knew every smell a valley could make. This one was wrong for the season, wrong for the hour, wrong the way a name is wrong when someone says it in a voice you've only ever heard in dreams.

They sat up. The loft boards creaked their usual creaks. Below, Quentin was already moving—she moved before dawn the way other people breathed—and Wren heard her stop mid-step.

"Quentin?" they called down.

No answer. That was answer enough.

Wren came down the ladder fast, one hand on the rail, and found her standing at the open door with her back very straight and her hands hanging loose, which on Quentin Coldharbour was the posture of a woman holding something down by force.

The orchard was gone.

Not burned. Not cut. Not flooded or struck by lightning or any of the hundred ordinary violences an orchard can suffer. Gone. Where forty apple trees had stood in crooked rows since before Quentin was born—since before she'd been *old*, anyway—there was now a meadow of pale grass that had never been planted by anyone, growing in soil that had never been turned, sloping gently toward the stream as if it had always been exactly that shape. As if the orchard had been the interruption and this was the correction.

Wren had seen maps lie. They had drawn maps that lied, professionally, for money. But they had never seen the world itself revise its own memory.

"The stream's moved," Quentin said. Her voice was level. Too level, like a floor walked on carefully because you know what's underneath. "It used to bend east around the far row. Now it goes straight through where the Gravensteins were."

"How long have you lived here?"

"A hundred years." She said it flatly, the number worn smooth from handling. "I planted some of those trees myself. In the forties. Fifties. I don't—" She stopped. Wren watched her reach for the memory the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and watched her hand close on nothing. "There were Gravensteins," she said, less surely. "I'm nearly sure there were Gravensteins."

*Then the memory of the orchard.* Wren had read the phrase in the book without understanding it, the way you read a diagnosis in a language you almost speak. They understood it now, standing in the doorway with the smell of apples still caught in the rafters above them—the last place the orchard existed, a ghost made of scent, already fading.

"It took the trees first," Wren said. "Now it's working on you."

Quentin didn't turn around. "It?"

"The erasure. Whatever ate your country. It doesn't stop at borders, Quentin. It stops at *finished*. And whatever magic holds this valley together—" they gestured at the meadow, the grass, the gentle lying slope— "it isn't finished. It's a bandage over an amputation. Bandages rot."

Finally she looked at them. Her eyes were dry and furious and frightened in equal measure, and Wren thought: *there she is. The last swordspeaker. Not the woman who mends fences. The woman who has spent a century holding a door shut with her spine.*

"I buried my wife under the third tree from the gate," Quentin said.

Wren said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a knife.

"I buried her there because she loved the Gravensteins, and now I cannot tell you what a Gravenstein looks like, and I do not know if the grave is under the grass or under the stream or nowhere at all, and I am *standing here* telling myself it's fine, it's just weather, valleys change—" Her jaw worked. "I've been here a hundred years and I never once asked why the fog never lifts past the ridge. I never asked why my sword stopped singing the day I crossed the boundary. I called it peace."

"You called it slow," Wren said.

That landed. He saw it land, saw her flinch and then, being who she was, absorb the blow and file it somewhere useful.

"Yes," she said. "Slow. A hundred years of slow." She stepped out into the meadow, into the grass that hadn't existed yesterday, and knelt, and pressed her palm flat against the soil. Wren watched her face and understood that she was listening for something—a hum, a heartbeat, the faint seismic note of a seal under strain—and that what she heard made her go white around the mouth.

"It's not just the orchard," she said quietly. "I can feel it. Under everything. Like a tooth that knows the dentist is coming."

* * *

They spent the morning doing inventory, and the inventory was worse than the orchard.

Wren walked the valley's edge with their satchel of instruments—compasses that pointed at things no compass should point at, ink that dried black in the presence of erasure, a folding rule their grandmother had sworn could measure absence—and marked what was missing against what Quentin remembered. It was not a long exercise. It was a horrifying one.

The millstone by the north ford: gone. Quentin remembered grinding grain on it for thirty years; the ford now ran smooth and unbroken, as if no miller had ever quarried a stone. The leaning fence post she'd carved her wife's initials into: gone, and worse, the fence itself now ran straight through that stretch, unbroken, uncarved, as though it had always been built by someone with better patience. The hollow where she'd hidden during the first year, starving, eating roots: gone, filled in with honest earth.

And the memories went with them, or frayed beside them. Quentin would remember a thing clearly, then remember it less clearly, then remember that she remembered it, the way you remember a dream by its outline after the color drains out. By noon she was describing her own wedding and got the season wrong twice. By early afternoon she described it correctly again—but Wren noticed she'd begun checking with them first. Looking at their face before she committed to a detail. Using Wren as her map.

*I know what that's like,* Wren thought, and hated knowing it. *Outsourcing your own continuity to another person. I did it for eleven years. It's how you end up selling someone's location to save yourself—you start believing you're only real where they confirm you.*

"You're doing it too," Quentin said suddenly. They were walking back along the eastern ridge, and she'd stopped, and her eyes had that flat bright quality of someone who has decided to be angry instead of afraid because anger has better footing.

"Doing what?"

"Watching me. Checking me. You keep glancing at me when you write in that book of yours, like you're making sure I still match the description." She held out her hand. "Let me see it."

Wren's arms closed around the satchel before their brain authorized the motion. Old reflex. Eleven years of keeping the maps safe had built the reflex into the bone.

Quentin waited. She was good at waiting; a century in a valley will teach you.

Slowly, feeling like a man handing over his own pulse, Wren passed her the field book. She opened it to the page they'd been working on all morning—the inventory page, the ledger of losses—and read it top to bottom while the wind moved the impossible grass.

*"Orchard, gone. Millstone, gone. Fence carving, gone. Hidden hollow, gone. Wedding, unstable. Wife's name—"*

She stopped.

"Wife's name," she read aloud. "You wrote *wife's name, unstable*." She looked up. "What was her name, Wren?"

And Wren—who had heard the name yesterday, had heard it three times, had written it down themselves in their own careful cartographer's hand—opened their mouth and produced nothing. Not a blank, exactly. Worse than a blank. A shape where a name should be, the way the meadow was a shape where the orchard should be. They knew the name had existed. They could feel the absence of it like a missing tooth, tongue going again and again to the gap.

"I wrote it down," they said, and their voice cracked in the middle, which embarrassed them so much they kept talking to cover it. "Yesterday. Before we left the cottage. I wrote it down because I have a rule—I write down names, I always write down names, it's the first law of my trade, a place is only lost when its name is lost—"

She turned the page.

There it was, in Wren's own ink: *wife's name:* and then a smear, a water-blur, as if the page had been rained on, except the rest of the page was dry and the sky had been clear for two days. The ink hadn't run. The ink had been *unwritten*. Someone—or something—had reached into a bound book in a locked valley and edited it.

Below the smear, in fresher ink, in handwriting that was Wren's but slightly wrong, slightly too confident, someone had written: *no record.*

"No record," Quentin repeated. She laughed, one short terrible syllable. "My wife has been dead sixty years and she's just been killed again, and the coroner's report says *no record*." She handed the book back. Her hand was steady. Wren noted, with the part of their mind that mapped everything including people, that her steadiness was the most frightening thing about her. "How long do we have? Before it gets to something I can't afford to lose?"

Wren did the arithmetic they didn't want to do. The orchard had gone overnight. The memories were fraying within hours. The pattern wasn't linear—it was accelerating, the way a collapse accelerates, each failure loading the next.

"Days," they said. "Maybe less. Maybe it depends on—" they hesitated, because the next words were ones they'd been circling since dawn— "on whether anything here is anchored. Quentin, listen to me. Valleys don't erase themselves. Something erased your country, and this valley is what's left of the wound, and wounds close. From the outside in. The orchard was the outside." They tapped the ground with their boot. "We're standing on the inside. And I don't think the closing cares whether we're standing on it."

"Then I leave."

"Just like that."

"No." She was already turning back toward the cottage, already moving with that terrible efficient stride. "Not just like that. But yes. I have spent a hundred years learning every rock in this valley and I will not spend my last day in it memorizing the way it forgets me. Get your book. We're going to the edge. We're going to find your Door."

Wren didn't move. Because there was a thing they hadn't told her yet, and the thing had teeth.

"Quentin. The Door of the Last Choir isn't in the valley."

Her stride hitched. Didn't break. "You said—"

"I said I knew about it. I said I'd take you to it. I didn't say where it was, because until this morning I wasn't sure, and because—" they made themselves say it plainly, because lies had gotten heavy in their pack years ago and they'd sworn off carrying them— "because the Door is at the edge. Past the ghosts. Past the oath-breakers who couldn't get through. And Quentin—the reason they're stuck there, the reason they're ghosts at all, is that the Door opens for a whole oath freely sung, and theirs are broken, and yours—" they watched her face as it arrived— "yours is the oldest intact oath left in the world. Which means the Door wants you. Which means everything between here and there wants you too. Including, I suspect, the thing that's eating the valley. An anchor that walks toward the exit drags the whole seal with it."

Silence, except wind in grass that had no right to exist.

"So if I stay," Quentin said slowly, "I get unwritten. And if I go, I might tear the last of my country open on the way out."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this *now*."

"I'm telling you now because this morning I watched a dead woman's name get erased out of a book I was holding, and I decided I'd rather be honest and useless than useful and quiet." Wren met her eyes. "Also because I'm coming with you, and you deserve to know what you're walking into with me behind you."

For a long moment Quentin Coldharbour said nothing. Then she looked past Wren, out across the meadow where her orchard had been, where her wife's grave might or might not still be, and Wren saw her do the thing that defined her—weigh survival against something larger, and hate the scale, and use it anyway.

"Then we leave tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I'm going to sit under where the third tree used to be, and I'm going to try to remember her name on my own, without your book, without anything. If it's going to take her, it can watch me hold on as long as I can." She started down the slope. "And Wren—if the Door needs a whole oath, then I need to know exactly what happens to an oath when it's sung at a threshold. All of it. No more surprises."

"There's one more surprise," Wren said, hating themselves. "But it keeps until tomorrow."

She paused. Looked back. And for the first time since the orchard vanished, Quentin Coldharbour smiled—not kindly, not warmly, but the way a blade smiles when it comes out of oil.

"Then tomorrow's going to be a very full day," she said, and walked down through the grass that was eating her home, toward the spot where her dead had been, to stand guard over a name with nothing but her own stubborn memory and one night left to hold it.

Behind her, at the valley's northern edge, so faint that Wren might have imagined it, the fog on the ridge began, very quietly, to lift.
