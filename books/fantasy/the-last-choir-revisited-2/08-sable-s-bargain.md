## 8. Sable's Bargain

The ghosts kept their dead hours at the valley's edge, where the grass grew in the shape of footprints that had never finished being made. Quentin had counted them the night before—forty-one figures standing in the frost like fence posts someone had forgotten to pull—and she counted them again now as dawn came up gray and reluctant behind the eastern ridge.

Forty-one. The same. Ghosts did not multiply. They did not leave. That was rather the point of them.

She stood at the boundary with Wren a half-dozen paces behind her, and the boundary itself was a strange thing to look at directly: not a wall, not a line, but a softness in the air, the way a window goes vague when you breathe on it. On this side, the Hollow. On the other, whatever the world had become since the country that made her oaths stopped existing. Between the two, the ghosts stood in their loose half-moon, and at their center, Sable.

She had died well, Quentin thought. That was the horror of it. Sable had the posture of a woman who had once commanded rooms—a broad-shouldered ghost with a soldier's cropped hair and a scar that the erasure had preserved along her jaw like a signature. She had been, Wren's research suggested, a captain of the last choir guard, the ones sworn to hold the Door. And she had broken that oath. She had run. And now she stood at the edge of the valley she'd fled toward, unable to take one more step in any direction, forever.

"You slept badly," Sable said. Ghost voices carried strangely—close and far at once, like someone speaking through a wall they were leaning against.

"I slept fine," Quentin said.

"You slept with your sword on the bed. I watched the light of it under your door until the small hours." Sable's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "The dead notice light, Coldharbour. It's most of what we have."

Quentin didn't apologize. She was a hundred years past apologizing for caution. Instead she looked past Sable at the other ghosts—the ones who swayed slightly, like reeds, the ones who stood rigid, the one young man near the back whose hands kept opening and closing around a sword hilt that wasn't there anymore. Her oath had been whispering their names for three days now, a litany under her skin, and she hated how well she was coming to know them. Maren. Osk. the twins, Pell and Pellam. The young one was Tam. Tam, who had been nineteen when he broke his oath and nineteen forever after.

"You said you wanted to talk," Quentin said.

"I said I wanted to make you an offer." Sable folded her arms. "Wren. You can come closer. I know you're writing everything down. I can see the way your eyes move. Left to right, left to right, like a woman copying out a page."

Wren came forward without embarrassment, which was one of the things Quentin was learning to tolerate about them. They stopped at her shoulder, notebook already open, pencil behind their ear like a carpenter's nail.

"An offer," Quentin said. "Ghosts don't have anything to offer. That's what being a ghost means."

"Most ghosts." Sable spread her hands, and Quentin saw that the frost on the grass beneath her had died in a perfect outline of her shadow, black and precise. "But we are not most ghosts, are we? We are the ghosts of the Last Choir's guard. We held the Door of the Last Choir. We were the last living people to see it, to touch it, to hear it close." Her voice dropped. "We know where it is."

The words went through Quentin like cold water through cloth. She kept her face still. A hundred years of hiding had taught her that much, at least: the still face, the flat eyes, the body pretending to be a wall.

"You said the Door opens only for a whole oath freely sung," she said. "You told us that three nights ago. You didn't mention you knew where it stood."

"I didn't mention it because knowledge is the only coin we have left, and I don't spend it on the first visit." Sable's eyes—gray, patient, terrible—moved to Wren. "Your mapmaker friend has been very useful. Very clever. But cleverness isn't what the Door wants. The Door was built to be found by the sworn, not the shrewd."

"And you'd just give me the location," Quentin said. "Out of the goodness of your death."

"I'd trade it." Sable stepped closer, and the frost died in a new outline, and Quentin felt the boundary hum against her skin like a struck wire. "Here is my price, swordspeaker, and I'll only say it once. You carry our confession out of the valley."

"Your—" Quentin stopped. "Confession."

"A confession," Sable said, "is not words. Not for people like us. You of all people should know that. An oath is a weight. A broken oath is a weight that never sets down. We have carried ours for—" she paused, and Quentin watched her do arithmetic she clearly hated— "eighty-one years. We cannot cross the boundary. We cannot set them down. They are nailed to us."

"So you want me to carry them for you." Quentin laughed, short and ugly. "No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need the terms. I know what a burden of guilt made literal looks like. I've been carrying my own for a century, and it weighs enough." She turned. "Wren. We're leaving."

"Quentin." Wren's voice was quiet, but it had that mapmaker's precision, the way they could draw a line through fog. "Hear the terms. We don't have to accept. But we've spent three days trying to find the Door with nothing but my charts and your memories of a country that was erased before either of us was born. Sable is offering the one thing that can't be charted."

Quentin stood with her back to the ghosts and looked out at the valley—the crooked fence she'd mended a hundred times, the orchard, the cottage with its singing sword on the wall, all of it soft in the early light. Three days ago the orchard had still been there. She hadn't let herself think about it. She was going to have to let herself think about it eventually, but not now.

"Say the terms," she said, not turning around.

"You carry our confession out," Sable said. "All of it. Forty-one broken oaths, gathered into one weight, and you bear it through the Door. Not metaphorically. The Door of the Last Choir was built to take weight—it was a door for the whole country's guilt, once, in the old rites. It can take yours. It can take ours, if ours is carried by a living, sworn hand."

"And what does that cost me?"

"Nothing," Sable said. "That's the beauty of it. The weight passes through you. It doesn't lodge. You carry it the way a river carries a boat."

"Nothing," Quentin repeated. "Nothing is never the price. I've been alive for a hundred and thirty years, and I have never once been offered something for nothing. What does it cost, Sable? What aren't you telling me?"

Behind her, silence. The particular silence of forty-one people who had agreed on a story in advance.

Quentin turned around.

Sable's face had not changed, but the ghosts behind her had shifted—small movements, a ripple of discomfort through the half-moon. Tam the young one had stopped opening and closing his hands. Maren, the oldest of them, a ghost worn thin as paper, was looking at the ground.

"There is a cost," Sable said. "There's always a cost. The weight passes through you, yes. But while you carry it, you carry it. Forty-one broken oaths, Coldharbour. Forty-one voices. They will speak to you. Not constantly. Not cruelly. But they will speak. You will know what Osk did to get away. You will know what Maren abandoned. You will dream their nights. And the Door—" she paused. "The Door will know you're carrying them. It may be slower to open. It may test you harder. A door built to weigh guilt does not lighten its scales for the courier."

"That's it?" Quentin said. "Voices in my head and a harder door?"

"That's it."

"It's not," Wren said.

Everyone looked at them. Wren had stopped writing. They stood with the pencil motionless in their hand, and their face had gone the particular pale gray of someone reading terrain wrong.

"Quentin," Wren said. "Look at the frost."

Quentin looked. The dead grass in the ghosts' outlines—the black shadows printed into the frost—she'd seen them, catalogued them, thought nothing of it. But now she saw the pattern of them. They weren't random. They weren't the shapes of standing people.

They were letters. Old script, the script of the erased country, the script in her oath-book and on the Door's old seals. She could read maybe a third of it, and the third she could read said: *we confess.*

"They've been writing it into the ground for years," Wren said quietly. "Every night. Every frost. I charted the valley's edge last spring, before I ever met you, and I thought the patterns were drainage. They weren't drainage. They were a message, repeated, forty-one bodies wide, every cold morning, for decades." Wren looked up at Sable. "How long have you been trying to say this to someone?"

"Since the frost began coming to the valley," Sable said. "Sixty years. You're the first who read it."

Quentin felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a plow hitting stone.

*We confess.* Sixty years. She had lived ten miles from this boundary for a hundred years. She had walked the ridge above it. She had never once come down to look, because looking meant acknowledging, and acknowledging meant hoping, and hoping was the one crop the Hollow couldn't grow. She had let forty-one people stand in the cold and write the same sentence into the ground every winter morning, and she had never come to read it.

"Why?" she said. Her voice came out wrong. "Why confess? What did you do that needs this much confessing?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its captain's flatness. What was underneath it was worse.

"We ran," she said. "That's the shape of it. The country was being erased—you know this now, your archivist friend told you, the seal, the god, all of it. The Choir was singing the last verses. The Door was closing. And the guard—" she made a sound that was not quite a laugh— "the guard ran. Forty-one of us, sworn to hold the Door until the last voice finished the last verse, and we heard the god coming, the way you hear a storm coming, in your teeth, in the water in your cup. And we ran. We broke the circle. We let the Door close early. Unfinished."

"And that matters because—"

"Because an unfinished verse is a crack." Sable's eyes were steady on Quentin's. "Because the seal has been leaking for a hundred years through the hole we left. Because every collapse, every place that stops existing, every name that fades—some part of that is the god, finding the seam we made when we ran." She spread her hands. "We didn't destroy the country. But we left the latch open. And we have known it. And we could not say it, because we are dead, and the dead in this valley can only write in frost."

The boundary hummed. Quentin's oath, dormant for decades, stirred against her ribs like a sleeper turning over, and she felt it listening.

"You want absolution," Quentin said.

"No." Sable's answer came fast and hard. "I want you to understand what I'm actually offering, because you keep thinking this is a trade and it isn't. The location of the Door is nothing. Wren could find it in a year, maybe two, with enough charts and enough luck. What I'm offering is what we know about the Door—what it weighs, how it tests, what it does to people who come to it carrying guilt. Forty-one lifetimes of watching it work. And in exchange I'm asking you to do the one thing no ghost can do." Her voice cracked, just at the edge, just enough. "I'm asking you to carry our words to a door that can hear them. We have been shouting into frost for sixty years, swordspeaker. You would be the first person in eighty-one years to carry our confession anywhere it might be received."

* * *

They walked back from the boundary in silence, Wren charting, Quentin not talking, which Wren had learned to let her do. The morning was warming. Somewhere behind them the ghosts stood in their half-moon, and Quentin did not look back, because she knew that if she looked back she would see Tam's hands opening and closing on nothing, and she was already carrying too much to add the sight of it.

At the fence line, Wren finally spoke.

"Don't do it."

Quentin stopped. "You read the frost. You were the one who read it. And now you say don't."

"I read the frost, and I also read Sable." Wren leaned on the fence, and for once they didn't look like a mapmaker; they looked tired, and young, and like someone who had once sold another survivor's location to save their own skin and had never fully forgiven themselves. Quentin knew that story now. It had come out on the road, in the dark, the way confessions do. It was one of the reasons she trusted them, which she was aware was a strange arithmetic. "Sable told you the weight passes through you like a river carrying a boat. Quentin. Nothing about guilt passes through anyone. I would know. I've been carrying one small boat for thirty years and it has never once been light."

"You think she's lying."

"I think she's telling the truth as she understands it, which is worse." Wren's pencil turned over and over in their fingers. "Forty-one oaths. Forty-one voices. You've heard one voice your whole life—the sword—and it nearly drove you out of your mind before you learned to close the door on it. Now multiply that by forty-one, and make every one of them a regret instead of a duty. And then walk into a door that weighs guilt, carrying forty-one guilts that aren't even yours." They shook their head. "There are other ways to find the Door. My charts. Time. We don't need this."

"The orchard stopped existing four days ago," Quentin said. "The valley is coming apart. We don't have a year. We might not have a month." She looked out at the fence, at the crooked post she'd straightened so many times the wood had memorized her hands. "And there's something else you're not accounting for."

"What's that?"

"Sixty years." Quentin turned to face Wren fully. "Sixty years of writing *we confess* into the frost. You don't do that to be forgiven. I've thought about it the whole walk back, and I know what that is, because I've spent a century not doing it. That's not a plea for absolution. That's a plea for a witness. They don't want to be let off. They want it to be *known*. They want one living person to carry the truth of what they did out of this valley and put it down somewhere it counts." Her throat tightened. "I know what that is because it's what I'd want. If I'd run. If I'd broken everything. I wouldn't want forgiveness. I'd want someone to know."

"Quentin—"

"And here's the part you really won't like." She said it before she could stop herself, which was how her truest things always came out. "I know what they did. I understand it. The god was coming, Wren. In their teeth, in the water in their cups. I've spent a hundred years running from a country that no longer exists, and I have never once had to hear it coming. They heard it. They were nineteen and twenty-five and forty, and they heard a god coming, and they ran." She swallowed. "I would have run too. That's why I have to carry it. Because I'm not carrying the guilt of strangers. I'm carrying the guilt I'd have earned."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The pencil stopped turning.

"You're going to say yes," they said finally.

"I'm going to say yes."

"And if the weight doesn't pass through? If it lodges? If you get to the Door carrying forty-one voices and you can't sing your own oath over them?" Wren's voice was steady, but their hands on the fence rail were white. "Then we lose. Everything. The valley, the Door, you. All of it, because you felt sorry for ghosts."

"Yes," Quentin said. "That's the risk."

"That's not a risk, that's a—"

"It's the only trade on the table, Wren. And they've paid sixty years of frost for it. The least I can do is pay a few bad dreams." She pushed off the fence. "Besides. You told me once that guilt is a weight that never sets down. You were wrong. It sets down the moment someone else agrees to know it. I've watched you these past weeks. You've been lighter since you told me about the survivor you sold. Not fixed. Lighter. That's all this is. That's all they're asking. Someone to know."

Wren looked at her for a long time, and something in their face shifted—not agreement, not surrender, but the particular expression of a person updating a map to include terrain they'd hoped wasn't there.

"You're going to be the death of me," they said.

"No," Quentin said. "I'm going to be the death of me. You're just going to be nearby when it happens. That's the job."

* * *

The ghosts had not moved. Of course they hadn't. She stood before Sable in the late light, the boundary humming against her skin, and Wren a pace behind her, arms crossed, radiating the silence of a formal objection entered into the record.

"I'll do it," Quentin said.

Sable's face did not change, but the whole half-moon of ghosts did—a ripple, a shiver, forty-one bodies remembering in the same instant that they had once been people who could hope.

"Then hear the last of it," Sable said, "because I said I'd only make the offer once, and the offer includes the whole truth." She raised one hand, and the frost at her feet died into a new line of letters, and this time Quentin could read all of it, because the oath in her ribs had woken and was reading it for her, translating in a voice like a struck bell.

*The Door is not where the maps say. It never was. It is under the mountain that isn't there anymore, and the way down opens only for those who bring it a truth heavy enough to balance the one it keeps. We will show you the road. We will walk it with you as far as the boundary lets us. And Coldharbour—*

The letters stopped. The frost held its shape. Sable looked at her, and for the first time since Quentin had met her, the captain's composure failed completely, and what was underneath was just a tired dead woman who had been standing in the cold for eighty-one years.

"The god knows we're talking," Sable said. "It has known since the night the orchard went. It's been listening through the cracks, through us, through everything. Which means it knows what you're about to carry. Which means it will do everything it can to stop you—or worse, to help you, in ways that serve it." Her voice dropped. "And there is one more thing. The last thing. The thing we wrote into the frost every winter and never once finished, because finishing it felt like a door closing on us forever."

"Say it," Quentin said.

Sable's ghost-hands closed at last around a sword hilt that wasn't there, and held it, and steadied.

"The Door will ask you what you're carrying," she said. "And when it does, you must tell it the truth. Not our confession. *Yours.* The Door doesn't weigh what you bear for others, Coldharbour. It never did. It weighs what you bear for yourself. And if you walk up to it carrying forty-one borrowed guilts to hide behind—" her gray eyes held Quentin's, merciless and kind at once— "it will know. It will know you came to save the world so you wouldn't have to save yourself. And it will not open."

The boundary hummed. Behind Quentin, Wren exhaled slowly, and somewhere far beneath the valley, under the mountain that wasn't there anymore, something old and patient and hungry turned its attention toward her like a lighthouse beam.

Quentin stood very still in the dying light, forty-one ghosts waiting on her answer, and understood at last that the heaviest thing she would carry through that door was the one thing she had spent a hundred years refusing to pick up.

"When do we start?" she said.
