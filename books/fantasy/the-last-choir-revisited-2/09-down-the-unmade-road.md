## 9. Down the Unmade Road

Wren had mapped forty-one countries that no longer existed, and every single one of them had been easier than this.

They stood at the valley's edge at dawn, the three of them—Quentin with her sword wrapped in sacking like a body prepared for burial, Sable's confession bound against Quentin's ribs in oilcloth and cord, and the forty-one ghosts trailing behind them like a funeral procession that hadn't decided who was in the ground. Wren unrolled their kit on a flat stone: compass, which spun uselessly; sextant, which pointed down; and the book from 1923, which had become their only reliable instrument, its pages rearranging themselves each morning to show the road as it would be by nightfall.

"The trick," Wren said, "is to not look back."

Quentin looked at them. "That's it? A hundred years of cartography and the trick is don't look back?"

"The roads behind us aren't roads anymore. They're *absences* shaped like roads. If you watch one dissolve, your mind tries to hold the shape of it, and holding the shape of an absence is how you get unwritten. I've seen it happen." Wren cinched the last strap on their pack. "A colleague of mine. Halvard. He turned around once on the Salt Steps to check his own footprints. By evening his mother couldn't remember his face. By morning—" Wren shrugged, a small precise motion. "By morning there was no morning."

"Cheerful," said Sable, drifting forward through the grey light. The ghost's edges smoked faintly, the way they always did near dawn, as if the sun were trying to boil her off the world. "Lead on, mapmaker. We're dead already. We'll take our chances looking back."

"That's exactly my point," Wren muttered, and started walking.

* * *

The first road out of the Hollow was a farm track, and it behaved itself for nearly two hours. Then, around a bend where a stone wall should have continued along the hillside, the wall stopped—not ended, not crumbled, but *stopped*, mid-stone, the top half of the final rock hanging in air over nothing, and past it there was a meadow that had never been planted, growing grass that had no seeds, under a sky with one too few colors in it.

Wren felt it before they saw it: a tugging behind the sternum, like a fishhook set in the bone.

"There," they said, and knelt, and did the thing they'd done perhaps six hundred times in a long and dishonest life. They pressed their palm flat to the earth and spoke.

"I, Wren of the Cartographers' Guild, oath-sworn in ink and salt, do witness this place. It was called Marrowfield. Wheat grew here. A woman named Odile Voss kept bees at the south hedge, and her honey won ribbon at the Lammas fair four years running. I witness it. I chart it. Let it be remembered if nothing else is."

The words came out of them the way blood comes out of a cut—warm, involuntary, costing something they couldn't count until later. And the ground answered. Not much. A shiver through the grass. The absent color in the sky flickered briefly toward amber. The hanging half-stone settled onto solid earth with a sound like a sigh.

It would hold for a day. Maybe two. Then the wound would open again, because you cannot stitch a country shut with one tongue, and the country behind them was bleeding out faster than any Wren had ever charted.

"What did that cost you?" Quentin asked. She'd come back up the track when she noticed Wren had stopped. Her eyes were doing the thing they did—cataloguing, weighing, deciding whether Wren was an asset or an encumbrance.

"A headache," Wren lied, and stood, and kept walking.

Behind them, the forty-one ghosts passed over the mended stretch of road without disturbing a blade of grass, and every one of them looked back, and none of them unraveled, and Wren decided not to think about what that meant about the living versus the dead.

* * *

By the third day the roads had stopped pretending.

They walked through a forest where the trees grew in rows because someone had planted them, and then through the same trees again, and then a third time, except each iteration was subtly wrong—the bark smoother, the birdsong a half-tone flat, the shadows falling the wrong way—and Wren understood they were walking through the same memory of a forest wearing itself thin, like a song played until the needle wore the groove smooth.

"We can't navigate by landmarks," Wren said, calling a halt at what might have been noon or might have been dusk. The light wouldn't commit. "Landmarks are dissolving faster than we move. From here on, we navigate by oaths."

Quentin sat down on her pack like a woman settling in for bad news. "Explain."

"Every oath-bound survivor is a thread tied to the country's spine. You feel it—I know you do, you've felt it since the book arrived. When I speak a witnessing, I can feel the other threads. Tension, slackness. Where they pull tight, the seal is holding. Where they've gone slack—" Wren drew a finger across their throat. "We walk toward the tension. Toward whatever's still anchored hard enough to hurt."

"And the Door?"

"The Door is where all the threads were tied off. The knot at the end of the rope." Wren met her eyes. "Which means every step we take toward it, we're walking toward the loosest thread in the world. Yours."

Quentin was quiet for a moment. Somewhere off in the wrong-shadowed trees, a bird sang its flattened note and got no answer.

"You could have mentioned that before I agreed to carry a sack of ghosts' confessions into the teeth of it," she said finally.

"I'm mentioning it now." Wren busied themselves with the book, turning pages that turned themselves. "Besides, you'd have come anyway. You're the type."

"What type is that?"

"The type that picks up heavy things." Wren glanced at the oilcloth bundle against her ribs. "I've been watching you carry Sable's confession for three days. You sleep with it. You talk to it. You're letting it weigh on you properly, the way it's meant to. Most people would have found a way to put it down." They closed the book. "It makes you either the most honest person I've traveled with or the biggest fool, and I genuinely haven't decided."

"Take your time," Quentin said, and rolled into her bedroll facing away from him, and Wren lay awake a long while listening to forty-one ghosts whisper among themselves in a language made entirely of apology.

* * *

It was the fifth day when the road asked its price.

They came down out of the worn-thin forest into a valley that Wren knew—knew intimately, had charted twice, had slept in once with a fire going and a friend across from them—and the valley was fine. Whole. Grass green, stream running, an inn standing at the crossroads with smoke rising from its chimney and a sign creaking in a wind Wren could hear.

Wren stopped so abruptly that Quentin walked into their back.

"That inn burned down," Wren said. "Forty years ago. I charted the ruins."

"It looks very much not burned down," Quentin said.

"It's not *real*. It's remembered. Something here remembers it so hard it's standing." Wren's hands had gone cold. Because there was only one thing in this whole unraveling country with enough will left to remember an inn into being, and it was an oath, and there was only one oath Wren knew of tied to this valley, and—

"No," Wren said aloud, to nobody, and walked toward the inn.

The door was open. Inside: low beams, a peat fire, a pot of something stewed and steaming, and a man sitting at the corner table with a mug of small beer, looking up with an expression of mild, patient surprise, as though he'd been expecting this visit for forty years.

"Halvard," Wren said.

"Wren." Halvard smiled. He looked exactly as he had at thirty-two, the year Wren had last seen him, the year before the Salt Steps and the footprints and the morning with no morning. "You look terrible. Sit down. The stew's real enough to eat. Mostly."

"You're dead."

"I'm *slack*," Halvard said. "There's a difference. My thread went loose when I lost my name, but the oath didn't let go all the way. So I drift. And sometimes—" he gestured at the inn, generous, sorrowful, "—sometimes I remember things into standing. Can't help it. It's the last thing my oath does. It keeps what I loved."

Wren sat down because their legs told them to. Quentin stayed by the door, hand nowhere near the wrapped sword but aware of it. The ghosts crowded the windows outside, and even Sable, Wren noticed, looked away from the warmth inside as if it were a brightness she couldn't bear.

"You're the survivor whose location I sold," Wren said. No preamble. No softening. The confession had been climbing their throat for five days and it came out ugly and fast, the way such things always do. "In the Year of the Grey Ledger. Someone came asking—guild brokers, they said, verifying census rolls. They paid in charting rights, in access to the sealed atlases. I gave them your safehouse in Coriander Row. I told myself you'd moved on anyway. I told myself everyone eventually moved on." Their voice cracked down the middle. "Three weeks later the safehouse was ash and you were missing and I took the atlas access and I *charted with it*, Halvard, I built half my reputation on maps drawn with money I earned selling you."

The fire popped. The stew smelled wonderful and Wren could not have swallowed a spoonful to save their life.

Halvard considered them for a long moment. Then he laughed—a real laugh, tired and fond, the worst possible response.

"Oh, Wren. I know."

"You—"

"Who do you think sent them a false trail afterward? Who do you think scrubbed your name from the brokers' ledger so the guild never learned what you'd done?" Halvard leaned forward. "I sold your location right back, you idiot. To the same people. Told them you'd gone north. Bought myself a decade. We're the same kind of coward, you and I. That's why the book found us both."

The silence in the remembered inn was absolute. Outside, forty-one ghosts pressed closer to the glass.

"So this is the price," Wren said numbly. "The road brought me here to make me say it."

"The road doesn't make anyone do anything. That's what I tried to tell the others, back when there were others." Halvard drained his beer and set down the mug, and the mug stayed exactly where he put it, which was somehow the saddest thing Wren had ever seen. "But roads do *remember* what happened on them. And you can't walk a road clean while you're carrying that. Neither can she." He nodded at Quentin. "Ask her what she's carrying besides the confession. Ask her sometime, when she can answer."

"Halvard—"

"I'm fading, Wren. This inn costs me everything I have left. By tomorrow I'll be a slack thread drifting, and by winter I'll be nothing, and that's *fine*, that's what happens to people like us." He smiled again, and this time it was only tired. "But you're not fading. You're the healthiest thread I've felt in fifty years. Whatever you are, whatever you didn't tell the swordswoman yet—you're *anchored*, Wren, harder than anyone I've ever charted. Ask yourself why. Before you reach the Door. Because if you go through it carrying secrets the way you carry maps, it will know. Doors like that one always know."

He stood. The inn flickered around him like a candle in wind.

"Stew's good," he said. "Eat something. You look terrible."

And the inn was ruins. Had been ruins the whole time—Wren was suddenly sitting on a charred beam in a roofless shell, weeds in the hearthstone, and the stew smell was gone, and Halvard was gone, and on the blackened table where the mug had been lay a single object: a brass map-weight, guild-stamped, the one Wren had lost at Coriander Row sixty years ago.

Quentin crouched beside Wren on the ruined floor and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything she could have said.

"He forgave me before I confessed," Wren managed. "Do you understand how unbearable that is? I rehearsed that confession for five days. I earned the right to be forgiven slowly."

"Yes," Quentin said quietly. "I know exactly how that feels."

She picked up the map-weight and handed it back to Wren. Her fingers, Wren noticed, were shaking—but whether from fear or from the weight against her ribs, from whatever Halvard had meant by *ask her what else she's carrying*, Wren couldn't tell.

* * *

They walked until full dark, because neither of them wanted to sleep in that valley.

It was Quentin, surprisingly, who broke the silence. "The ghosts. They've gone quiet since the inn."

"They do that around strong memories. It's like—" Wren searched. "Like dogs around a sleeping bear."

"Mm." A hundred paces of silence. Then: "Sable says the Door is close. Three days. She can feel the threads pulling tight, she says. Like harp strings."

"She would know. She's been listening to them snap for a century."

Another silence, longer. When Quentin spoke again her voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of someone stepping carefully across ice.

"Wren. When we get to the Door. Whatever it asks for. Whatever it *knows* about us." She didn't look at Wren. "Are you going to be able to walk through it honestly?"

Wren thought of Halvard's fading smile. Of the healthy thread in their own chest, anchored hard, harder than any survivor's should be. Of the question they had been refusing to ask themselves since the day the book found them.

"I'm going to find out," Wren said.

Ahead of them, the road bent around a hill that appeared on no map Wren had ever drawn—and Wren had drawn maps of places that had never existed, so that was saying something—and beyond the hill, faint as a struck bell, they heard singing.

Dozens of voices. In harmony.

Coming from the direction of the Door.
