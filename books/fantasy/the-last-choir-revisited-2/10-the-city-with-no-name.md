## 10. The City With No Name

The road ended the way a sentence ends when the writer loses nerve.

One moment Quentin was walking on packed earth between hedgerows gone feral with a century of neglect; the next, the hedgerows were simply *suggestions* of hedgerows, translucent at their edges, and the earth beneath her boots had the soft give of paper that has been rained on and dried too many times.

"Stop looking at it," Wren said from behind her.

"I'm not—"

"You are. You're staring straight down the middle of it like a woman reading a will." Wren came up beside her, map case slung across their chest, hands loose and ready. They had not slept since the collapse of the orchard two days ago, and it showed in the bruised skin under their eyes, in the way they kept touching the strap of the map case as if checking for a pulse. "It exists when nobody watches. That's the whole trick of this place. The capital was unmade so thoroughly that its absence became load-bearing. Look away and the streets remember themselves. Look too hard and they fall down."

"And how do you navigate a city you can't look at?"

Wren smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Badly. Until now."

Quentin turned her face deliberately toward the gray sky, letting her peripheral vision do the work, and the city assembled itself out of the corner of her eye: spires, first, black against cloud, then the long slope of avenues descending toward a river she could hear but never quite catch moving. It was like watching a photograph develop in reverse — or forward, rather, the world deciding to exist only where she wasn't paying attention.

At her hip, the sword was silent. It had been silent since the valley, except for the humming, which had grown less like a hum and more like a held breath.

Sable's confession rode against her spine in an oilskin packet, sewn into the lining of her coat by candlelight while the ghosts watched. She had carried it four days now and had already learned what carrying guilt made literal felt like: heavier every morning, as though someone added a coin to it each time she slept. She did not open it. She had promised.

"The archive is in the Spire Quarter," Wren said. "Or it was. The prophecies were written there — all of them, officially. Every prophecy that ever bound this country came out of one building, which should tell you something about prophecy."

"What should it tell me?"

"That it was a bureaucracy." Wren said it lightly, but their jaw was set. "People think of prophecy as weather, as fate, as something that falls on you. It was clerks, Quentin. Ink quotas. A waiting list. Someone had to file the book that named tonight."

They walked into the city without naming it, because it had no name anymore; the erasure had taken even that. The streets accepted them the way water accepts a stone, closing behind.

* * *

By midmorning Quentin had learned the rules of walking through a place that existed only unobserved.

Rule one: don't look directly at anything you need. She navigated by reflections — in puddles that appeared and vanished, in dark windows, in the polished face of a fountain that stood dry and full of coins from a country no one remembered minting. In reflection, the city held still, as though mirrors were a kind of mercy it permitted itself.

Rule two: don't count things. Doors, windows, steps — the numbers slid if she counted them, and sliding numbers made her dizzy in a way that felt less physical than moral, as if arithmetic itself were being unwritten around her.

Rule three, Wren gave her without being asked: "If a street starts to go, walk backward off it. Backward is memory. Forward is intention. This place trusts memory."

"That's nonsense," Quentin said.

"It's cartography," Wren said, and stepped confidently backward down an alley that had begun, at its far end, to fray like rope.

She followed them. Behind her own heels, cobblestones solidified one by one, and ahead of her toes the same cobbles softened into possibility. Walking this way felt like walking through her own past — solid where she'd already been, terrifying everywhere she hadn't.

They stopped at noon in a square where a statue should have been. There was a plinth. There was a shape of absence above it, man-sized, heroic in silhouette, and around the plinth a ring of grass that grew in perfect circles, like ripples.

"Someone erased him specifically," Wren murmured. "Not the square. Him."

"How can you tell?"

"Grass doesn't grow in rings unless something *burned* here. Erasure isn't deletion, it's fire. It leaves scorch marks in causality." Wren crouched, pressed two fingers flat to the grass, and closed their eyes. Their lips moved — not words Quentin could catch, but the cadence of an oath, the old liturgical rhythm she knew better than her own heartbeat.

When Wren opened their eyes, they were pale.

"Well?"

"There was a man here who held the country together," Wren said. "A speaker of some kind. And they took his name out of every stone, and the stones have been trying to say it ever since. That's the ringing. That's why the grass grows in circles. He's still trying to be remembered." Wren stood, brushed off their knees with unnecessary force. "Your erasers were thorough, Quentin. Thoroughness takes planning. Planning takes institutions."

Quentin looked up at the empty plinth and felt the first cold thread of something that was not fear. She had been afraid for days now — afraid in the orchard's absence, afraid on the unmade roads, afraid each dawn when Sable's confession gained its new weight. Fear was familiar. She had lived beside it for a hundred years the way a farmer lives beside a river that floods.

This was different. This was anger arriving on schedule, punctual as a debt.

"Come on," she said. "The archive."

* * *

The Spire Quarter announced itself by silence.

Every other part of the ruined city had a sound — wind through hollow windows, the ghost-river's almost-noise, the tick of masonry settling into whatever compromise it maintained with nonexistence. But the Spire Quarter was quiet the way a sickroom is quiet, and the spires themselves were wrong: too tall, too slender, leaning inward toward each other overhead like conspirators, so that walking beneath them felt like walking through a throat.

The archive building did not lean. The archive building stood perfectly, insultingly straight, its white stone unstained by the hundred years that had rotted everything else. Quentin stopped ten paces from its doors and understood why.

"It's been maintained," she said.

"Observed," Wren corrected quietly. "There are ghosts inside. Enough of them, watching often enough, and a building stays real. It's the one architecture the dead are good at." They glanced sideways at her. "You feel that at your back, right? Your cargo?"

She did. The confession against her spine had warmed, the way Sable herself had been warm — the warmth of things that burned instead of living. The ghosts of the broken choir had known about the archive. Of course they had. Sable had given her directions to the Door, but she had also given her — buried in the confession Quentin hadn't read, or perhaps simply in the way Sable had gripped her hand too long at parting — a warning she was only now decoding.

*Don't trust what's written there,* the grip had said. *But read it anyway.*

The doors of the archive were oak, and they opened before Quentin touched them, inward, silently, onto darkness that smelled of ink and dust and, underneath both, faintly and impossibly, of lilies.

"After you," Wren said, and for once their voice had no irony in it at all.

* * *

Inside, the archive was a lie built out of shelves.

The entry hall ran farther than the building's exterior could contain — she checked, twice, from the doorway, refusing to step fully in until she'd confirmed the geometry was cheating — and the shelves rose into a dark so complete it seemed manufactured. Lamps hung from chains at intervals, each burning a flame that gave light without heat, and by that light Quentin saw the books.

Thousands. Tens of thousands. Leather spines in regimented rows, each stamped with a year, running backward along the wall: 1922, 1921, 1920 — and then, where any sane library would have continued, a gap. A gap exactly wide enough for one year's volumes, flanked by empty brass brackets shaped like hands, as if the missing books had been held and then let go.

"The year of the erasure," Wren whispered. "They kept everything except the year it happened."

"No." Quentin was already walking. Her voice came out flat and strange to her own ears. "No, look at the dust."

The shelves flanking the gap were dusty. The gap itself was clean — not swept clean, but *tended* clean, the way a shrine is clean, the way a grave is kept.

"They didn't remove those books," she said. "They come back. Someone puts them back, over and over, and someone else removes them again, and they've been doing it for a hundred years, a war fought in a bookshelf—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "Where are the current volumes? Where's the writing done?"

Wren pointed with their chin toward the far end of the hall, where a single lamp burned lower than the rest, and beneath it a desk, and behind the desk —

Behind the desk sat a figure so thin it might have been a folded shadow, and it was watching them with the patience of furniture.

Quentin's hand went to the sword. It did not sing. It did not even hum. It lay against her hip like a bar of cold iron, and she realized, with a lurch, that in this building her oath could not reach it — that here, at the heart of where oaths were written, hers had no jurisdiction at all.

"You're the archivist," she said.

The figure inclined its head. When it spoke, its voice was the sound of pages turning. "I am what's left of the position. The last seven holders refused to die until the filing was finished, and the filing will never be finished, so here we are." It gestured with a hand like a dried leaf. "You've come about the prophecy. 1923. Tonight's date. Your name."

"How do you know my—"

"Child." The archivist's voice gentled into something horribly kind. "I wrote it. Or the position wrote it, through me, the way a pipe carries water. We wrote every prophecy in this room. Do you know what a prophecy is? Practically?" It did not wait for her answer. "A prophecy is a work order. We told the country what would happen so that the country could prepare. And when the country decided to erase itself, we wrote the erasure's schedule. Including the part where the anchors fail. Including tonight."

The word landed in Quentin's chest like a dropped stone.

"Anchors," she repeated.

The archivist turned its head — slowly, with the creak of old binding — and looked past her, at Wren, at the door, at the whole ruined world outside, and then back at her with eyes like punched holes in paper.

"You haven't read the ledger yet," it said. Not a question. Almost pity. "Then I'll tell you what the books in the gap say, the ones they keep removing and we keep replacing, because the truth always comes back on its shelf eventually. The erasure wasn't an accident, swordspeaker. It wasn't a catastrophe. It was voted on. Debated for eleven years. Minuted. There are minutes." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "And it wasn't done to save everyone. It was done to save *some*, and the some who drew the short straws were never told what they'd been conscripted to hold shut."

Somewhere deep in the archive, in the dark beyond the lamps, a shelf groaned — the sound of a book forcing its way back into a gap that powerful people wanted empty.

Quentin's fear, which had ridden her for a hundred years and four hard days, stood up and left the room. What remained in its place was hot and clear and had teeth.

"Show me the ledger," she said. "Show me the names of the anchors."

The archivist unfolded itself from behind the desk, joint by joint, taller than it had any right to be, and gestured down the dark aisle with a hand like a moth.

"Walk with me," it said. "And bring your friend. The ledger remembers everyone who enters — and it has been waiting a very long time for yours."

Behind them, unnoticed by either Quentin or Wren, the clean gap in the shelf filled, silently, with a single black volume whose stamp bore the date of tonight.
