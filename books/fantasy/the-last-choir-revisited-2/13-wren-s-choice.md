## 13. Wren's Choice

Wren had known for three days that they could walk through the Door alone.

They hadn't told anyone. They'd barely let themselves think it in full sentences, the way you don't say a thing out loud because saying it makes it yours. But the knowledge sat in them like a swallowed stone, cold and specific and heavy: *the anchor ledger has one name on it, and it isn't mine.*

Quentin was the lock. Sable's ghosts were the tumblers. The archivist had said so with the flat certainty of a man reading his own obituary. Every oath-bound survivor still breathing was a pin holding the erased country shut against the thing underneath, and Quentin — last swordspeaker, hundred years in the Hollow, a woman who had sharpened a silent sword every morning of a century rather than admit it would never sing again — was the biggest pin of all.

But Wren? Wren had sold their oaths piecemeal decades ago. A mapmaker who charts places that no longer exist pays for the privilege by unbinding from places that do. There was nothing left in them anchored enough to hold. When the Door opened, the seal wouldn't miss them. It wouldn't even notice they were gone.

They could walk through. Tonight. Tomorrow. Any night between now and the end of everything.

And instead here they were, sitting on a fallen lintel in the shadow of the Door of the Last Choir, whittling a stick into a pencil point that would never be used, watching Quentin sleep badly by a fire she'd built too small because she didn't want the light to reach the ghosts at the tree line.

*You're going to tell her,* Wren thought. *That's the whole problem with you. You always tell her.*

The fire popped. Quentin's hand twitched toward the sword even in sleep — an old reflex, a century deep. Watching it made something in Wren's chest hurt in a way they refused to name, because naming it would make it a debt, and debts were how people got owned.

"Your stick is dead," Quentin said, without opening her eyes. "You've been carving the same knot for an hour."

"It's not dead. It's resting."

"Mm." She sat up, hair silvered by moonlight, face lined by everything. "Talk or sleep, Wren. You're bad at pretending, and I'm worse at ignoring it."

So much for easing in.

* * *

"I can go through," Wren said.

They'd walked away from the fire first, out onto the shelf of broken road below the Door, where the world thinned to gauze at the edges and you could see the ordinary country waiting beyond — real grass, real weather, a horizon that behaved. Quentin followed. Of course she did.

"Say that again."

"The Door opens for a whole oath freely sung. That's what opens it. But passing through—" Wren spread their hands, the cartographer's habit, measuring the space between things. "Passing through only requires that nothing holds you back. I checked the ledger. Twice. My name isn't in it. It hasn't been in anything since I sold my binding to buy passage off the Salt Coast. I'm the freest person left in this entire wound of a country, and I mean that as an indictment."

Quentin stood very still. In the Hollow, stillness had been survival; out here it looked like a woman deciding how much truth she could carry in one night.

"You could just leave," she said. "Walk out. Be done."

"Yes."

"And instead you're telling me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And there it was. The question Wren had been whittling around all evening, all week, maybe all their life. They could have lied. Lying was practically their native language — maps were just lies you could navigate by. But lying to Quentin had stopped working somewhere around the orchard that stopped existing, and Wren had found, to their considerable annoyance, that they preferred it that way.

"Because I want you to come with me," they said. "Not as anchors. Not as oath-bound companions chained together by a dead country's paperwork. As two people who choose each other. And I can't ask that honestly if I'm hiding the fact that I have the option you don't."

The wind came down off the Door like a long exhale. Somewhere behind them, faint as memory, the ghosts murmured in the trees.

"And if I can't come?" Quentin said quietly. "If the only way through is singing myself empty — no magic, no oaths, no sword-voice, ever again? You've seen what I am without it. I don't know what I am without it."

"You're a woman who mended fences for a hundred years in a valley that couldn't love her back," Wren said. "You'll manage."

"That's not—"

"Wren's Second Rule of Cartography," they said, because jokes were armor and they were going to wear all of it. "Never trust a map drawn by someone who thinks the territory owes them anything."

"Is there a First Rule?"

"'The map is not the place.' Which is relevant, actually, because—" Wren crouched, pulled the stub of charcoal from their coat, and started sketching on a flat stone. "Listen. This is why I stayed. This is the actual reason, not the noble-sounding version."

The stone filled with lines. The valley. The Door. The seal beneath it, which the archivist had described as a knot and which Wren now drew as exactly that — a knot of oaths, each strand a survivor, each survivor a name in the ledger, all of it cinched tight around the sleeping dark of the god.

"Everyone assumes the strands have to be cut," Wren said. "Sable thinks so. Cut the anchors, open the door, pour through. The archivist thought so — that's the whole design, escape hatch for the willing, everyone else stays tied to the mast. But I've spent my whole career charting places that stopped existing, and here's the thing nobody wants to hear: *nothing truly ends.* Places don't vanish. They get overwritten. And an overwrite can be revised."

Quentin stared at the stone. "You're saying you could redraw the knot."

"I'm saying a knot can be untied. Slowly. Strand by strand. Each anchor released properly, transferred, absorbed into the structure itself instead of ripped free. It's the difference between cutting a rope and splicing it. Cutting collapses the seal and lets the god out through the front door. Splicing keeps the seal while letting the person walk away." Wren looked up. "It's never been done. It might not work. It will take time we may not have. But it means you don't have to sing yourself hollow to be free. Nobody does."

For a long moment Quentin said nothing. Then she sat down on the broken road beside the sketch, cross-legged, like a child at a lesson, and Wren's chest did the painful unnamed thing again.

"How long?" she asked.

"Honestly? Years. Maybe decades. The god's getting louder under the seal; the archivist said the erasure is accelerating. We might not have decades. We might not have months." Wren turned the charcoal over in their fingers. "Which is why I'm telling you about the Door tonight. Because you deserve to know all your options, including the ones that are terrible, before I start asking you to bet your existence on a theory from a mapmaker who once sold a friend's location to save their own skin."

"That was different. You've paid for it."

"I'll always be paying for it. That's not the point." Wren sat down next to her. The stone between them held its little impossible diagram of hope. "The point is: I could walk through tomorrow and never think about any of this again. No ghost choir, no devouring god, no ledger with my name absent. I'd be safe, and light, and gone." They paused. "And every hour of the rest of my life I'd know I left you standing at a locked door when I had a set of picks in my pocket."

* * *

Quentin cried. Wren pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of courtesy, and after a while she pretended not to notice that they'd noticed, and that mutual fiction held until she could speak again.

"No," she said.

Wren waited.

"What you're describing — untying me strand by strand — that's surgery. On the seal. With the god underneath it, awake enough now to talk." Her jaw tightened; Wren knew she was hearing it again, that tired reasonable voice through the cracks. "Every time you loosen a thread, it gets a little more room. You said yourself it might take years. You'd be kneeling at that knot with its breath on your neck for years."

"Yes."

"And if it wakes while you're mid-splice?"

"Then I'll have bought you whatever time I bought you, and I'll die knowing more about knots than anyone in history."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke, which is the terrifying part." Wren rubbed their eyes. "Quentin. Look at me. I am offering you a way out that doesn't cost you yourself. I need you to understand that I'm not doing it out of duty. I don't have duty; I sold it. I'm doing it because when you sang the names of the other survivors and your hands shook, I wanted — " They stopped. Started again, plainer. "I want a life after this. I want one with you in it. Not the oath-bound you, not the lock, not the last swordspeaker. *You.* The fence-mender. The terrible haggler. The woman who feeds strays she pretends not to feed. If the price of that is years of careful work at the edge of a hungry dark, then the price is cheap and I'd pay double."

Quentin looked at them for a long time. The moon moved. The Door did not.

"You're asking me to let you risk everything for me," she finally said. "After I spent a hundred years learning that accepting help is how people get hurt."

"I'm not asking you to accept help. I'm asking you to accept company. Help implies I expect gratitude." Wren shrugged. "I expect argument. Mostly about my technique."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob, and Wren counted it among the great victories of their life.

"And if I refuse?" she said softly. "If I say no, splice someone else, untie Sable's ghosts first, untie the ones who broke their oaths to survive — and leave me as the last strand, the final lock, because someone has to hold it and I've held it longest?"

"Then I stay anyway," Wren said. "Untying the others. Making the seal as light as I can make it. And when the end comes — whenever it comes — I'll be standing next to you at that door, and we'll find out together whether I can walk through while you can't." They met her eyes. "I won't leave first, Quentin. That's the offer. Not rescue. Company."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet and furious and alive.

"You're a fool," she said.

"A mapmaker. Same thing."

"I haven't refused yet," she said. "Notice that. I haven't said yes either. Ask me again tomorrow."

"I'll ask you every day," Wren said. "Until one of us runs out of tomorrows or you get sick of hearing it."

"That could be soon."

"Noted."

* * *

They walked back toward the fire together, and that was when Wren felt it — a wrongness in the ground, a wrongness in the air pressure, the particular flattening sensation they'd learned to read the hard way, the way a mapmaker reads a landscape about to be redrawn.

The cracks in the seal were widening.

Underfoot, faint but unmistakable, the god's voice rose through the stone like heat through a floor. Not words this time. Something worse. It was humming — a slow, patient tune, almost gentle, and Quentin went rigid beside Wren because she recognized it. Wren saw the recognition hit her like a blow.

"What is it?" they asked. "What's it humming?"

Her voice came out thin.

"It's the second verse of my oath," she said. "The one nobody taught me. The one I've never heard sung." She turned toward the Door, and the moonlight caught her face, and she looked less afraid than awake. "It knows the unbinding clause, Wren. It's been sitting under our feet the whole time, learning the song that opens its cage — and now it's *practicing*."

Far off in the trees, the ghosts' murmur cut off all at once, like a held breath.

The tune hummed on beneath them, patient as erosion, and Wren understood with mapmaker's certainty that whatever time they'd thought they had had just been spent.
