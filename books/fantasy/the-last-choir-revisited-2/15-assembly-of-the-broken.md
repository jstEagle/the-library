## 15. Assembly of the Broken

Wren had mapped a hundred gatherings of the dead and never once been invited to one.

They came up out of the archive as the light failed, Quentin's hand on the ledger like it was the only solid thing left in the country, and the ghosts were already waiting in the square below—hundreds of them now, more than Wren had ever seen in one place, drawn up out of the unmade roads and the collapsing orchard-edges by whatever signal the seal sent when it cracked. They stood in loose ranks among the ruins of the capital, translucent in the last gray light, and the sound of them was the worst part. Not moaning, not wailing. Murmuring. The low, patient murmur of people who had waited a long time for something and could feel it coming.

"Tell me you have a plan," Wren said.

"I have a direction," Quentin said. "It's mostly downhill."

"That's not a plan."

"It's what I've got."

The square had been a mustering ground once—Wren could see it in the bones of the place, the wide flagstones worn by boots that no longer existed, the plinth at the center where some statue had stood before erasure took its name along with everything else. Now it was a stage, and the dead were assembling on it, and Wren felt the old cartographer's instinct rise in their chest: *chart this, chart this now,* because places where this many ghosts gathered developed a shape, a gravity, and if you didn't map them fast they'd map you.

They pulled the stub of charcoal from their coat and opened their field book against their forearm, and that was when Sable came forward through the crowd, and every ghost in the square went quiet at once, the way water goes still around a dropped stone.

"You brought the anchor," Sable said. Not to Wren. To Quentin. And there was something in the way she said it—not *you brought your friend*, not *you brought the mapmaker*—that made Wren's charcoal stop moving.

"She's not an anchor," Quentin said. "She's with me."

"Everything is an anchor," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

She drifted closer, and Wren saw her clearly for the first time in full light—or as close to full light as the dying day allowed. In the valley's edge fog she had been a shape, a voice, a warning. Here, in her own city, she was almost solid: a tall woman in the rotted finery of someone who had been important once, a general or a judge or a choir-master, with hands that still remembered holding things. Her eyes were the color of the space between stars, and they moved from Quentin to Wren and back again with the unhurried attention of someone counting.

"The god spoke to you," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"It did." Quentin didn't flinch. Wren had learned, over these weeks of unraveling roads, that Quentin never flinched; she just got quieter, the way deep water gets quieter before it drops off a shelf. "You knew it would."

"We hoped." A ripple went through the assembled ghosts—not disagreement exactly, but the memory of disagreement, fossilized into posture. "We hoped it would offer. We hoped you would listen long enough to understand what was being asked. And then we hoped you would come back here and tell us what you learned, because—" and here Sable's voice changed, softened, became something terribly reasonable, "—because you are the only living soul who has heard it and walked away, Quentin Coldharbour. That makes you the most valuable person in this country. Possibly the most valuable person who has ever been in this country."

"And what does everyone else think I'm valuable *for?*" Quentin asked.

Sable smiled, and Wren thought: *oh no.*

"Come down among us," Sable said, spreading her arms to the square, to the hundreds of murmuring dead. "You've traveled our roads. You've carried our confession. You've earned the right to hear it from all of us, not just me. Come and stand in the Assembly of the Broken, and let us tell you what we want."

* * *

They stood in the middle of the muster-ground because Sable asked, and because Wren could see no way to refuse without turning the whole square hostile, and because—and this was the part Wren admitted only to themselves, in the privacy of their own skull—they wanted to hear it too. They had spent their whole life trading secrets across tables, reading the gap between what people said and what they wanted, and the gap in this square was wide enough to drive a caravan through.

The ghosts closed ranks around them. Not threateningly. Worse than threateningly: reverently. Like pilgrims around a relic.

"You should know," Sable said, pacing a slow circle, addressing the crowd as much as Quentin, "what our ancestors did. What *we* did. When the devouring god came up out of the sea-trench and the armies broke and the prayers failed, the Council voted to erase the country. To unmake itself rather than be eaten. And the oath-bound—the swordspeakers, the singers, the mapmakers, the oath-sworn of every craft—we were left behind as anchors. Living locks on a door that was never meant to open again." She paused. "Some of us accepted this. We stayed. We held. We died holding, and our ghosts held after us, because a broken oath cannot be set down until it is finished or forgiven.

"But some of us ran."

The word fell into the square like a coin into a well. Wren watched faces turn away, watched shoulders hunch, watched a hundred years of shame move through the crowd like wind through wheat.

"I ran," Sable said simply. "I broke my oath to the Choir and I fled toward the Door, and the erasure caught me at the threshold, and I have stood at the edge of this valley for a hundred years knowing exactly how far I got. Every ghost you see here broke something to survive. That is why we are called what we are called. That is why we gather."

Quentin's voice, when it came, was very quiet. "And now the seal is failing. And the Door is close enough to touch."

"Now the seal is failing," Sable agreed. "And the Door remembers. And here is what we want, Coldharbour, and I will say it plainly, because you have earned plainness." She stopped her circling. She faced Quentin directly. "We want to go home."

A murmur of assent rolled through the square, soft as rain.

"Not to haunt it. Not to rattle chains in ruined cities. We want back into the world. We want fields and weather and other people. We have been locked outside existence for a century, and the Door is the only hinge, and you—" her starless eyes fixed on Quentin, "—are the only key. Open it, Coldharbour. Sing whatever you have to sing. Walk through. And let us follow you into the morning."

Wren felt the relief come first—that was the trick of it, the thing they would be ashamed of later. Relief. Because it sounded *right*. It sounded like justice. These poor broken souls, locked out of the world for a century, asking only for passage—

And then the cartographer's mind caught up with the feeling, the way it always did, three steps behind the heart and twice as sharp, and Wren looked at the square again, really looked, at the ranks of the dead standing in formation, at the weapons some of them still carried, at the way the murmuring had organized itself into something with cadence, with *drill*—

"Sable," Wren said. "Who taught you to stand like that?"

The square went quiet.

"What?" Sable said.

"Formation," Wren said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Shoulder-width intervals. Sight-lines to the perimeter. You're not standing like refugees, you're standing like a *regiment*. Refugees cluster. Soldiers deploy." They turned slowly, mapping it, hating themselves for mapping it. "I've charted forty battlefields, Sable. I know what an army looks like from above. This is what an army looks like."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Sable's face that was not grief and was not shame. It was older than both. It was patience.

Then Quentin said, in a voice Wren had never heard her use, flat and cold as a blade laid on stone:

"Finish what wars, Sable?"

And the square did not answer, which was answer enough.

* * *

"You told her," Wren said. "In the valley. You told her about the Door, you gave us the road, you sent your confession with her like a *gift*—" They were walking fast, away from the square, though Wren wasn't sure anymore whether they were retreating or being permitted to leave. "It was bait. All of it. The confession was a leash."

"The confession was true," Sable said, drifting beside them, and Quentin on her other side, silent, terrible, carrying the ledger like a shield. "Every word of it. Do you imagine guilt stops being real because it's useful? I regret what we did. I regret the wars we started when the erasure began—the border lords who decided that if the country was going to die, they'd die winning, and dragged their oath-bound regiments into a last war against each other while the god ate the coast. I broke my oath trying to stop them. That's why I'm here. That's why any of us are here." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Wren hated that it sounded genuine, hated that they couldn't tell anymore.

"But," Quentin said.

"But," Sable admitted, "there are ghosts in this square who marched in those wars and never stopped marching. There are generals here, Coldharbour. There are border lords' champions who have spent a hundred years telling themselves they were betrayed, that the country abandoned *them* first, and if they could just get back into the world they would settle it. Finish it. Win it, finally, posthumously." She spread her hands. "I am not their master. I am their *spokesman*. Do you understand the difference? I speak, and they decide, and lately what they have decided—"

"Lately what they've decided is that when you open the Door," Wren said, "they don't walk through behind you into the world. They pour through. All of them. With everything they remember about making war." They stopped walking. Turned. "And the world has forgotten the erased country entirely, hasn't it? No histories. No warnings. No one alive knows the names of these generals or the borders they fought over or the grievances they're still carrying." They laughed, short and ugly. "Gods. You wouldn't even be invading. You'd be *newly discovered ancestors*. Every crackpot crown in the world would be fighting to claim your bloodline."

"Which is why it must be done carefully," Sable said. "Which is why it must be done *gently*, and with a guide, and that guide was meant to be you, Coldharbour. A hero stepping out of legend with a host of the faithful dead—"

"No," Quentin said.

One word. It hit the square behind them like a dropped bell, and Wren realized the ghosts had followed, silently, and were gathered in a wide ring around the three of them in the ruined street, and had been for some time.

"No?" Sable said.

"No." Quentin turned to face the whole ring of them, and Wren watched her do the thing she did—stand smaller, get quieter, plant herself like something that had grown out of the ground and intended to keep growing there. "I will sing the verse. I will open the Door. But I will not lead an army out of a grave. Whatever unfinished wars you're carrying, you finish them here. Among yourselves. Or you lay them down. Those are the terms."

"You don't set terms," said a new voice, from the back of the ring—a big ghost, a soldier's ghost, armor fused to him like bark to a tree. "You're a lock, anchor. Locks don't negotiate. Locks *open.*"

"And if I refuse?" Quentin said. "If I stay in the valley? If I sit on my seal and hold it shut for another hundred years?"

The soldier-ghost smiled, and his smile was the worst thing Wren had seen since the god's voice. "Then we wait," he said. "We're very good at waiting. But you should ask yourself, little lock, what happens to a seal when the god beneath it gets hungry, and the only person willing to feed it is standing in this square offering her a better deal than yours."

Silence.

Wren looked at Quentin. Quentin looked at the ledger in her hands. And Wren understood, with the sick clarity of a map suddenly revealing that north had been wrong all along, that they were standing in the exact center of the trap—not the god's trap, not even Sable's trap, but the trap the whole country had been building for a century: a door that could only open outward, and every desperate thing on either side of it pushing.

"There's another way," Wren said.

Everyone looked at them.

"The Door needs a whole oath freely sung. That's what you told us in the valley, Sable. Your words." Wren stepped forward, heart hammering, mouth running ahead of sense the way it always had, the way that had gotten them sold out and sold *others* out and somehow here. "But it doesn't need to be *her* oath. And it doesn't need to be sung *alone*." They were thinking of the hidden verse, of the first swordspeakers who wrote mercy into the architecture, of every map they'd ever drawn of a place that no longer existed—places that survived because someone kept drawing them. "An oath is a chord, not a note. One voice opens it. But nothing in the law says the voices have to be *alive*."

The square of the dead went very, very still.

Sable's starless eyes widened, and for the first time in a hundred years, the spokesman of the broken looked genuinely afraid—not of Quentin, not of the god, but of the possibility taking shape in the air between them.

"You'd sing *with* us?" she whispered.

"I'd sing *over* you," Wren said, "if it comes to that. But yes. If she'll have us." They looked at Quentin. "Well, lock? Want a chorus?"

Above the ruined street, the gray light thickened, and somewhere below the world, something vast and patient began to count faster.
