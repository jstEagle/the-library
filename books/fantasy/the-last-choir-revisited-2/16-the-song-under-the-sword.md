## 16. The Song Under the Sword

Quentin did not sleep.

She sat in the ruin of the archive with the ledger open on her knees and her own name staring up at her like a wound that had learned to read. *Coldharbour, Quentin. Anchor. Ninth of Nine.* The ink had not faded in a hundred years because ink written into a seal does not fade. It waits.

Wren slept against a fallen shelf, their map-case clutched to their chest even in dreams, and above them the city with no name breathed in and out of existence — streets assembling themselves whenever she looked away, dissolving when she looked too long. A country made of not-being-seen. She had lived in its reflection for a century and never known.

"You're a lock," Wren had said, before sleep took them. Not cruelly. Wren was never cruel; they were only ever accurate, which was sometimes worse. "Every step you take toward the Door is a tooth turning in the mechanism. You've been winding yourself tighter for a hundred years."

*Then I stop walking,* Quentin had thought. *I go back to the Hollow. I sit down in the grass and I don't move until—*

Until what? Until the seal failed anyway, because it was failing regardless, because the orchard was already gone, because Sable's ghosts were already circling the Door like wolves who remembered being men. There was no version of this where she went back to mending fences. The fences were gone. The valley was going. Everything she had built her long quiet life on was a room in a house that was being demolished around her, slowly enough that she'd mistaken the trembling for weather.

So she didn't sleep. She read.

* * *

The ledger gave her the shape of the cage. What she needed now was the key, and keys, in Quentin's experience, were always kept by the people who built the locks. The first swordspeakers. Her predecessors. The nine who had sworn the original oaths and bound themselves into the seal alongside a country's worth of desperate magic.

The archive had a section for them. Of course it did. Every institution keeps a shrine to the people it spent.

She found it three aisles deep, behind a door that only existed when the building felt generous: a narrow room lined with sword-oils and oath-grammar, training manuals with margins full of dead handwriting. The air smelled like the inside of the Hollow — cold stone and iron and the particular nothing-smell of a place where magic couldn't follow. Someone had warded this room against the erasure itself. Someone had wanted these books to survive even if the country didn't.

Someone had wanted *her* to find them.

She told herself that was coincidence, too. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

The manuals were organized by generation. First Oath at the front, in a hand so formal it looked printed. Quentin worked backward through a century of diminishing script — each generation of swordspeakers writing smaller, faster, as if they could feel time closing over them — until she reached the last entries, dated weeks before the erasure. The hand there was cramped, urgent, and signed with a name she knew from childhood stories: **Aldous Vane, First Speaker of the Final Circle**.

The final entry wasn't about swordwork at all.

*If you are reading this, the seal has begun to fail, which means you are one of the anchors, which means I am sorry. We built the oaths in haste and hid the remedy in shame. No anchor should learn this from a book. But books are the only things that outlive our courage.*

Quentin sat down on the floor. The stone was cold through her clothes. She kept reading.

*An oath, properly spoken, has two movements. The binding verse, which every swordspeaker knows — we taught it to children before they could write. And the unbinding verse, which we wrote into the structure of the first oath and then agreed, all nine of us, never to speak aloud. Not because it is forbidden. Because it costs everything, and no one consents to everything until they must.*

Her hands had gone still on the page. Outside, somewhere in the shifting streets, the god's voice was murmuring through a crack in the world — she could feel it the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth, that low pressure of something vast and patient wanting in.

*The unbinding verse cannot be forced on another. It cannot be sung in anger or in fear; the words will simply refuse the throat. It must be chosen. Freely. Completely. Sung by an anchor who understands what it takes: the oath leaves you, and it takes the magic with it. All of it. Not the oath alone — the whole inheritance. The speaking-of-swords, the warding, the small bright workings. Everything the blood remembers. After the last note, you will be as any ordinary person. You will be free, and you will be nothing special, and those are the same sentence.*

*We wrote it for exactly this night. We hoped no one would ever need it. But if you do — if you are the last one standing at the Door with the seal coming apart in your hands — know that the first speakers loved you. We left you a way out. We just couldn't make it painless. Nothing true ever is.*

Below the signature, in letters spaced carefully apart, as though the writer had been shaking:

*The verse is hidden where all our deepest work is hidden. Under the sword. Under the song. Sing your binding oath slowly, and listen beneath it. Listen for the second voice.*

* * *

Quentin walked back through the breathing city to the campsite with the manual pressed to her chest like a splint. Wren woke when her boots scuffed the marble — they always woke, they slept like a knife sleeps — and read her face in the dark.

"You found something."

"I found the exit." She crouched beside them. "There's a second verse. Hidden under the first oath. An unbinding clause. If I sing it—" Her voice caught, and she was annoyed at herself for letting it. "If I sing it freely, completely, it releases me from the seal. From being a lock. And every other anchor with it, maybe. It breaks the mechanism instead of breaking me."

Wren sat up slowly. In the grey half-light of the half-existing city, their face was careful. "And?"

"And it strips all my magic. Forever. The oath, the speaking, everything. I come out the other side an ordinary woman with a sword she can't talk to." She laughed, short and ugly. "A century of hiding so I could keep the one thing that makes me *me*, and the way out is giving it up."

"Quentin." Wren said her name the way they drew maps — precisely, with attention to the dangerous parts. "Is that a cost you can pay? Honestly. Not bravely. Honestly."

She wanted to say yes immediately. Bravely. That was the reflex — a lifetime of swallowing fear and calling it discipline. But Wren was watching her with those cartographer's eyes that saw through terrain to truth, and lying to them had gotten harder since the road, since the confession about the survivor they'd sold, since trust between them had become something repaired rather than intact. Repaired things deserve honesty. It's part of the maintenance.

"No," she said. "Not yet. Ask me again after I've heard it."

"That's the right answer." Wren almost smiled. "The wrong answer would have been fast."

* * *

Dawn in a city that exists only when unobserved is a strange negotiation. Light came through windows that weren't sure they were windows, and Quentin stood in the middle of the ruined plaza with her father's sword drawn — the sword that hadn't sung since the Hollow swallowed her, the sword she'd sharpened every morning for a hundred years the way other people pray.

She planted the point in the cracked stone. She put both hands on the pommel. She closed her eyes.

*Under the sword,* Aldous Vane had written. *Under the song.*

She began the binding oath. The one her mother sang her before she could walk. The one that had been whispered into the family line for nine generations, worn smooth as a river stone by repetition:

*I am the edge that holds the line.*
*I am the word that outlasts the wound.*
*By blade and breath and borrowed light,*
*I keep what must be kept.*

She sang it slowly. Slower than she'd ever sung it. And beneath it — not with her ears but with some older listening, the listening that lives in the marrow where the magic pools — she heard it.

A second voice.

It had been there all along. Every time she'd ever spoken the oath, it had been underneath, the way a riverbed is underneath the water, the way grief is underneath a long and busy life. The first verse rode on top of it and drowned it out, the way a loud promise drowns out a quiet truth. But sung slow — sung *listened to* — the second verse rose through the first like bones rising through soil after rain.

*What was given may be given back.*
*What was sworn may be unsung.*
*I lay down the edge. I lay down the word.*
*I keep nothing, and am free.*

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well, and she felt how deep the water was. This was no trick clause, no lawyer's loophole. This was a door built into a door — the first speakers had known that someday the seal would need opening from the inside, and they had hidden the key in the only place no enemy would think to look: in the mouths of the prisoners themselves, disguised as the song of their captivity.

She stopped singing. The plaza hummed. Somewhere far below, through miles of seal and stone and silence, something enormous shifted in its sleep, and Quentin understood suddenly, viscerally, why the god's voice had been getting louder. The lock knew its key had been found.

"Quentin?" Wren, from the doorway. Alarmed. "Your sword — it's—"

She opened her eyes. The blade was glowing. Not the battle-glow she remembered from her youth, hot and hungry. This was softer. Lamplight. The light of a thing saying goodbye.

"It knows," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "It's known longer than I have. Swordspeaker steel is oath-stuff, Wren. It's been holding the second verse inside itself for nine generations, waiting for someone to sing slow enough to hear it."

"What does it cost?" Wren asked, because they would. Because they always asked the real question while everyone else was still admiring the plan. "Exactly. Say it out loud. Both of us need to hear it."

Quentin looked down at her hands on the pommel. Swordswoman's hands. Scarred, steady, a hundred years old and strong as rope. Hands that had held the only magic left in a broken lineage, the last ember of a country that had voted itself out of existence.

"If I sing it," she said, "I lose the speaking. The warding. All of it. Forever. No exceptions, no refunds — Vane is clear on that. I don't become an ordinary swordspeaker. I become an ordinary *person*. I'll grow old the normal way. I'll die the normal way. And everything I've been — the last swordspeaker, the keeper, the lock — all of that ends mid-note." She swallowed. "And I won't be able to carry Sable's confession with anything but my legs and my mouth. No magic to shield it. No power to force anyone to listen. Just a woman telling an uncomfortable story to strangers."

"Good," Wren said.

She stared at them. "*Good?*"

"Good that it hurts. Good that it costs the real thing and not the fake thing." Wren crossed the plaza toward her, limping slightly on the leg the doorframe wound had stiffened. "You've spent a hundred years paying a price you never agreed to, Quentin. Being the lock. Hiding in the Hollow. Watching everyone you might have loved die or forget you. That was the *fake* price — the one the erasure charged without asking. This one you get to choose. Chosen prices are different. They're heavier going down and lighter after." They stopped in front of her, close enough that she could see the exhaustion under their eyes, the map-lines of worry. "But I need you to choose it for the right reason. Not because it's noble. Not because you're tired. Because it's the only door that opens."

Quentin looked at the sword. The glow was fading gently now, settling, patient. A century ago it had sung battle-hymns in her grip and she had believed that song was her purpose. Now she understood the hymn had always been a duet, and she'd only ever heard half of it.

"The right reason," she said slowly, "is that staying bound doesn't save anyone. The seal fails either way — the archivist said it, the orchard proved it, the god's voice proves it every night. My magic in the lock isn't holding the door shut anymore. It's just... rattling. Adding noise to a collapse." She turned the blade, watched the lamplight run along it. "But if I sing the unbinding — freely, completely, the way it demands — the lock opens *clean*. On my terms. Maybe the country comes apart gently instead of violently. Maybe the ghosts go free instead of tearing through the Door like a flood. Maybe—" and here was the thought she'd been circling all night, the shameful flicker of hope grown into something steadier, "—maybe nobody else has to be unwritten. Not Wren. Not the survivors. Not the ones still asleep somewhere, dreaming they're safe."

"And you?"

"I get to be free," she said. "And nothing special. Same sentence."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was rough. "You know what the worst part is? A month ago I'd have told you it was suicide. Giving up the only power you have, in front of a hungry god and a choir of angry ghosts." They shook their head. "Now I think it might be the first sane decision anyone's made in this whole cursed country."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the preparation was mostly silence.

Wren drew maps that didn't need drawing — routes from the plaza to the Door of the Last Choir that existed only on paper, contingency lines for contingencies, the compulsive geometry of a person whose hands needed work so their heart didn't have to speak. Quentin cleaned the sword. Oiled it. Wrapped the grip with fresh leather from her pack, working the strips tight the way her mother had taught her, and realized halfway through that she was doing it the way you prepare a body.

She sang the binding oath once more, just to hear the second voice beneath it, just to be sure. It rose obediently, quietly, patient as geology. *What was given may be given back.* Yes. Still there. Still hers, in the strange sense that the way out had always been hers — hidden in her own mouth, waiting a hundred years for her to stop singing loudly enough to notice.

Toward dusk, Sable came.

The ghost didn't arrive so much as condense — the air at the plaza's edge thickening, greying, taking the shape of a tall figure in the rotted finery of a country that no longer existed. Sable's face was the worst part, still: handsome and hollowed, the face of someone who had broken their oath to live and had been living inside that brokenness ever since, the crack running through them like a fault line through marble.

"We felt it," Sable said. Their voice was wind through a burned house. "This morning. Something moved in the deep structure. The seal shivered, little anchor. What did you find?"

"A way out," Quentin said. She didn't see the point of lying; Sable could probably taste intention the way sharks tasted blood. "For me. For the other anchors. For the ghosts, if it works as I hope."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes — hunger, or its ghost. "And what does it cost, this way out?"

"My magic. All of it. Forever."

Sable smiled, and the smile was the most terrible thing Quentin had seen in a hundred years of terrible things, because it was *relieved*. "All of it," the ghost repeated softly, tasting the words. "You'd stand at the Door stripped bare. Powerless. Among the gathered dead." The smile widened by a degree. "You'll want allies, then. Protection. A chorus, even — you'll need voices for whatever ritual this is, won't you? Unbindings want witnesses. Want company."

"I hadn't decided yet."

"Oh, you will." Sable began to thin back into the evening air, and their last words arrived like a blade laid flat against the throat, not cutting yet, just resting there. "Tonight the choir gathers to discuss your little discovery, swordspeaker. All of us. Every broken soul at the wall. Come and hear what your allies have been planning while you were reading old books." A pause, and the wind-voice dropped to almost tenderness, which was worse. "Come and hear what we've been planning *for* you."

The ghost was gone. The plaza stood empty except for the two of them and a sword full of goodbye-light.

Wren looked at Quentin. Quentin looked at Wren.

"Well," she said, and slung the sword across her back, and felt the second verse humming against her spine like a promise she was finally ready to keep. "Let's go meet the choir."
