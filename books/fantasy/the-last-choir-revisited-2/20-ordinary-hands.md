## 20. Ordinary Hands

The bread burned because Quentin forgot it.

Not because the oven was strange, though it was—gas, not wood, a blue flame with no temper and no opinion—but because she had spent a hundred years knowing when things were done by listening. The sword on the wall of her cottage in the Hollow used to hum when the stew needed salt, when the storm was three hours out, when the fence post was about to rot through. It had hummed for everything except her own loneliness, which it apparently considered beneath its dignity.

Now there was no hum. There was only smoke curling off a ruined loaf in a rented kitchen above a chandler's shop in the town of Marrowgate, population four hundred and eleven according to the sign at the bridge, and Quentin Coldharbour standing barefoot on cold flagstones thinking: *this is what I traded it for. Bread.*

She opened the window and set the loaf on the sill to cool, or rather to be forgiven. A woman passing below glanced up at the smell and gave her the look people give bad bakers—not pity, exactly. Just the ordinary arithmetic of a stranger's judgment, delivered without ceremony. Quentin found she could bear it. That surprised her more than anything else that morning.

* * *

The first month she had counted her losses like coins: the sword's voice, gone; the oaths, unbound; the valley, unwritten along with the country it had been a wound in; the god, forgetting itself somewhere beneath a meadow. Against that column she had set one entry: alive. Unmagic. Undocumented. Wren had called this a fair trade and then immediately tried to renegotiate it into something larger, and Quentin had said no, and Wren had said *all right*, and had stayed anyway, mapping the new world from a room down the street until the roads pulled them elsewhere.

Alone again now. But it was a different alone than the Hollow's alone. The Hollow's alone had been a held breath. This one had neighbors who complained about her chimney smoke.

She learned cooking the way she'd once learned swordwork: badly, then stubbornly, then adequately. She burned things. She under-salted things. She made a fish stew that made her eyes water for reasons that had nothing to do with the fish, because the last person she'd cooked for had been Wren, and before that, a hundred years before that, someone whose face the erasure had taken but whose taste for thyme had somehow survived inside her hands.

Grief, she discovered, came without anesthetic now. In the old country—and it was *old country* now, the way you'd say *old religion*, something buried but load-bearing—there had always been magic to soften it. An oath could hold sorrow at arm's length. A swordspeaker's vow could turn mourning into duty, duty into motion. Now grief just sat down across from her at the table while she ate, uninvited, patient, and she had to look at it with nothing between them but a candle.

She got better at looking. Not faster. Just less flinching.

* * *

The confession weighed four pounds.

Sable had pressed it into her hands at the Door—or what had been the Door, in the last hour when ghosts were becoming voices becoming weather—and it had arrived as words, and words, in the Hollow's economy, had weight. Quentin had carried it out of the collapsing country the way you carry water: carefully, both hands, aware of every slope.

It went like this:

*I, Sable of the Ninth Choir, broke my oath on the third day of the erasure. I fled while my sisters sang. I told myself the country was already dead, that survival was the last loyalty left. I have carried this lie for longer than I carried the truth, and the truth is this: I ran because I wanted to live more than I wanted to be good, and I would do it again, and I am sorry, and the sorry changes nothing, and I am leaving it here anyway, with her, because she is going where I cannot go.*

There was a name attached. A line of descent. Sable had died—or stopped, or finished—in whatever year ghosts use for calendars, but she had lived first, in the flesh, long enough for a daughter, and the daughter for a son, and so on down the years like a fuse burning very slowly toward the present.

The name at the end of the fuse was Odile Vasseur. Chandler. Marrowgate. Population four hundred and eleven.

Quentin had known since the second week. She had walked past the chandler's shop eleven times, telling herself she was casing it, learning the rhythms, choosing the moment. On the twelfth time she admitted she was hiding, and hiding was the thing she had sworn off at the threshold of a door that remembered, and so she went in and bought a candle she did not need.

"You're the one renting above me," Odile said. She was perhaps sixty, broad-handed, with Sable's jaw—Quentin saw it now, unmistakably, the way you see a river in a map's blue thread. "You burn things."

"I'm learning."

"Hm." Odile wrapped the candle in brown paper without being asked. "Learning's loud. Most people quit before it gets quiet." She looked up. Her eyes were ordinary brown and entirely unmagical and Quentin felt the loss of that word like a tooth. "You want to say something. You've got the face for it."

"I do," Quentin said. "But not over a counter."

Odile named a price for the candle that was too high, and Quentin paid it, and they agreed on Thursday.

* * *

Thursday smelled of beeswax and tallow. Odile worked while Quentin sat on a stool among the hanging candles, all those small patient flames waiting to exist, and read the confession aloud.

She had thought about not reading it aloud. About handing it over like a parcel. But Sable had said *leaving it here, with her*, and *her* meant the descendant, and a confession read silently is a document, while a confession read aloud is a debt changing hands. Quentin had spent a century as the last speaker of oaths; even stripped of the power, the grammar remained.

So she read it, standing, in a shop that smelled of honey and rendered fat, to a woman trimming wicks, and when she reached the part about *I wanted to live more than I wanted to be good*, Odile's knife stopped moving.

When it was done, the silence had a texture. Beeswax has a texture. So does silence.

"That's it?" Odile said finally.

"That's all of it. Every word."

"My great-great-grandmother ran from something." Odile resumed trimming, but slower. "Family story. Nobody ever said from what. My mother said deserters, my aunt said debt." She snorted. "Turns out it was worse than either and nobody knew."

"Would they have believed it? If she'd told them?"

"No." Odile said it flatly, without cruelty. "That's why she didn't. That's why it's you telling me, a stranger, a hundred years late." She set down the knife and looked up, and it was Sable's jaw set against Sable's old question. "Why you? Why does a stranger carry this?"

"Because I asked her to let me. Because—" Quentin stopped. The honest answer was long. The honest answer involved the Hollow, and the ledger with her name in it, and a door that had opened only once everyone stopped trying to survive alone. "Because I know what it costs to keep a sorry past its expiration date. Somebody should deliver it. Even late. Even to the wrong address."

Odile studied her for a while. Then she took a candle stub from the workbench—a mispour, lopsided, the kind shops sell in bundles of twelve—and handed it over.

"For your windowsill," she said. "You'll need light. Learning's loud."

Walking home, Quentin realized her hands were shaking, the way they'd shaken over the prophecy book a lifetime ago, and understood that this was the other thing: some prices you pay in coin, and some you pay in installments, and today's installment had cost exactly one confession, two shaking hands, and the discovery that a stranger's jaw can be an inheritance you can see but never touch.

* * *

Winter came early that year, or late, depending on whether you trusted the almanac or the geese. Quentin trusted the geese.

She had gotten good enough at bread to stop burning it, most days, and bad enough at solitude to start accepting invitations. The chandler's Thursday had become a fixture: Odile taught her wick-dipping, Quentin taught her a stew, and neither of them mentioned the confession again, which was its own kind of keeping it.

One such Thursday, near dark, Odile wiped down the counter and said, casually, the way people say enormous things: "There's a traveler asking after you at the inn. Been asking since Tuesday. Won't give a name. Says—" and here Odile's mouth twitched, "—says you'd know them by their maps."

Quentin stood very still among the candles, all those small flames waiting.

She knew exactly one cartographer in all the wide unmapped world. She knew exactly one person who charted places that didn't exist, who had redrawn reality to buy her minutes at a door, who had been gravely wounded anchoring a doorframe and had walked away from the offer of a shared road with a smile and a *when the roads pull me back, they'll pull me back to you*.

She also knew that Wren did not travel anywhere without a reason, and that in the old life, reasons arriving unannounced had always meant trouble wearing a friendly coat.

"They're still there?" Quentin said.

"Drinking tea. Slowly. Like someone waiting for a nerve." Odile untied her apron. "Well? You going down, or do I have to carry a message like we're in one of your books?"

Quentine looked at her hands—ordinary hands, flour-scoured, wax-flecked, capable now of bread and grief and delivered confessions, and of nothing else, and therefore of everything that couldn't be done any other way—and reached for her coat.

"I'll go down," she said. "Some doors," she added, mostly to herself, "you have to walk to twice."

Below, in the inn's yellow window, a figure sat hunched over a spread of papers, drawing coastlines that Quentin was almost certain had not existed yesterday.
