## 21. Where Wren Goes

The world, it turned out, had edges after all.

Wren had spent a lifetime mapping places that didn't exist, and now they spent their mornings mapping places that did, and the second job was harder. The first had required only memory and a steady hand. This required walking. Miles of it. Roads that went somewhere, rivers that kept their names, towns where the people had never heard of the Last Choir and never would, and every one of them a small miracle Wren recorded in ink because miracles deserved documentation.

The new maps sold well. That surprised them. There was a hunger in the ordinary world for accurate things, Wren supposed—people tired of guessing where they stood. Wren understood that hunger from the inside. They'd spent a hundred years anchored to a country that had voted itself out of existence, charting its ghost-roads with oaths that let them draw the unmade, and now that the oaths were gone—sung away, unbound, spent like a fortune on a single night—they had nothing but their legs and their eyes and a satchel of good pens.

It was enough. That was the strange part. It was enough.

Their shoulder still ached where the doorframe had gone through them. The healers in the river town called it a miracle they kept the arm. Wren called it arithmetic: everything costs, and they had paid less than they owed. The scar was a long pale seam from collarbone to elbow, and on cold mornings it pulled, and Wren had decided to think of it as the world's signature on a contract they'd finally gotten right.

They were three months out from the valley—or the meadow where the valley had stood—when the letter caught up with them.

* * *

It found them in a coastal town whose name changed depending on which sign you read first, a place of herring boats and salt-rotted piers, and the letter was waiting at the inn because Quentin had a talent for finding people who didn't want to be found, a talent that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with stubbornness.

Wren knew the hand before they broke the seal. That careful, blade-straight script. The letter was short.

*I'm in Millbrook. The confession is delivered. Sable's granddaughter wept and then made me tea, which I think is how grief works in this world. I'm staying at the inn with the crooked sign. Come if you want. Come if you don't. But come.*

No signature. As if a signature were a chain, and Quentin Coldharbour had spent a century learning what chains did.

Wren read it four times. Then they paid for a room they didn't use, sat by the window with their maps spread out, and did not sleep.

*Come if you want.*

They had asked her, at the threshold of the Door, in the last hour before the singing, whether she'd walk out with them. She had said: *I don't know what I am without the oath. Let me find out alone first.* And Wren, bleeding against the doorframe, holding reality together with a cartographer's dying art, had said: *Then find out. I'll be on the road.* Because that was the whole of what they'd learned from a hundred years of survival—you could not make a person whole by standing next to them. You could only be there when they finished the work themselves.

Three months. She'd had her three months.

Wren packed the maps.

* * *

Millbrook was two weeks inland, a market town with a mill that no longer milled anything and a bridge old enough to have opinions. The inn with the crooked sign was called the Drowned Lamb, which Wren felt was an omen of some kind, though about what they couldn't say.

Quentin was in the yard behind it, of all places, mending a fence.

Wren stood at the gate and watched her for a long moment before she noticed them. She looked—different. Not older. Looser, maybe, the way a rope looks after you've cut the load off it. Her hair was shorter. Her hands, those sword-calloused hands that had held a blade that sang, were currently wrestling a fence post with a mallet, and losing, and swearing at it in a language Wren suspected she'd learned from the ghosts of the broken choir, which was to say fluently.

"You're holding the mallet wrong," Wren said.

She didn't startle. He'd have been disappointed if she had. She finished her swing, drove the post crooked, and turned around with dirt on her cheek and an expression she was trying very hard to make neutral.

"You're supposed to be mapping the coast."

"I mapped the coast. It has a coast. I wrote that down." Wren came through the gate. "The fence is winning."

"The fence has been winning for a week." She looked at their shoulder, at the way they favored the left arm without seeming to notice they did it. "You're walking well."

"I'm walking fine. You're fencing badly. We've all changed."

That got the almost-smile, the one that used to be rare as rain in the Hollow and had gotten, if anything, rarer since. Wren had learned to treasure it the way they'd once treasured a road that appeared on no chart.

"I got your letter," they said.

"I know. I wrote it."

"Fourteen words asking me to come and not one explaining why."

Quentin set the mallet down. She wiped her hands on her trousers and looked past Wren at the hills, at the ordinary, unenchanted hills, and for a moment she was a hundred and some years old and it showed in her face like weather.

"Because I don't know how to ask," she said. "I spent a century not asking. Asking is—" She stopped. Started again. "In the valley, everything I had was given by the oath. Belonging, purpose, the sword's voice. I didn't earn any of it. I was *assigned* it. And now—" she gestured at the crooked fence, the inn, the whole indifferent world, "—now I'm learning to earn things. And earning is slow and humiliating and I'm terrible at it. And I thought, if I asked you to come, it would be like reaching for the oath again. Taking the easy belonging. The kind you're given."

"And now?"

"Now I've been here three months and the fence is still crooked and I make bread that could stop a siege and I've delivered a dead woman's confession and I still wake up some mornings reaching for a sword that's just a sword." She met Wren's eyes. "And I've learned that I can do it. The earning. Alone. I can do it alone."

Wren heard the word land where she'd aimed it. *Alone.* Not a boast. A foundation stone.

"Good," they said. "That's the whole point. You can't come with me because you need me. You'd have to come because you choose to."

"Is that what you're asking? That I choose to?"

"I'm asking you to come map the world with me." Wren kept their voice level, the way they'd learned to keep a line steady on a chart in bad weather. "Not as oath-bound companions. Not because a prophecy circled a date or a country bound us to each other before either of us was born. There's nothing left binding us, Quentin. No seal, no ledger, no god underneath it. Whatever we are to each other now, we have to make it from nothing. From scratch. Like the fence." They paused. "Like the bread."

"The bread is a war crime."

"Then we'll suffer together. That's rather the definition of companionship, I've found."

She laughed—short, startled out of her—and then pressed her lips together as if the laugh had escaped without a permit. Wren watched her master it and thought: *a hundred years of hiding teaches you to treat joy like a signal fire.*

"I'm not good company," she said. "I'm learning to cook and I'm bad at it. I grieve loudly. I have nightmares about doors."

"I draw maps for a living and talk to my pens. I was wounded anchoring a doorframe and I have dreams where the geography argues with me." Wren shrugged, and the scar pulled, and they ignored it. "We'd be terrible together. It'll be the best thing either of us has ever done."

Quentin looked at the road. The road ran out of Millbrook past the dead mill, over the bridge, westward into country Wren had half-charted and half-invented, and it stood there in the late light being ordinary and infinite, and Wren watched her look at it the way she'd once looked at the Door.

"That's what it is, isn't it," she said quietly. "For me. Every road is the Door now. Every road is the thing I either walk through or stand in front of for another hundred years."

"Yes."

"And you'd wait. If I said not yet."

"I'd wait. But I'd keep moving while I waited, because I'm not a fence post, and also because I'd want you to have somewhere to arrive."

* * *

They sat on the inn's bench as the light went long and gold, and Quentin told Wren about the granddaughter—Sable's granddaughter, a schoolteacher with ink-stained fingers who had wept over a confession a hundred years old and then, because the world is strange, asked Quentin to stay for supper. Wren told Quentin about the town where two rivers met and the maps disagreed about which was which, and how Wren had settled it by asking a nine-year-old who fished there, because the child was the only one who'd actually looked.

"You're better at this than you were," Quentin said. "The ordinary world."

"I had a good teacher of what not to do. Me. A hundred years of me." Wren turned their pen over in their fingers—an ordinary pen now, no oath in it, no magic, just good steel and a point they kept themselves. "You know what I keep discovering? Everything I did with magic, I can do without it, it just takes longer. Charting a coast takes weeks instead of an afternoon. Earning trust takes years instead of an oath. Loving someone—" they stopped, and made themselves finish, "—loving someone takes a lifetime instead of a binding. And the lifetime version is the only one that's actually real."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. The mill wheel turned without grinding anything, creaking like an old joint.

"When the oath left me," she said at last, "there was a moment. After the singing. When all that silence came in where the voice used to be. And I thought: this is what I was afraid of my whole life. Not the god. Not the erasure. The silence. And it was—" she searched for it, "—it was fine. It was just quiet. All that terror, and the thing on the other side of it was *quiet*."

"I know. I was there."

"You were bleeding."

"I was there," Wren said again, "which is the point."

* * *

In the morning, Wren packed their satchel in the yard while Quentin stood at the gate, and the fence behind her leaned at its hopeless angle, and the road ran west, and the whole scene arranged itself with the terrible clarity of a map: a person, a road, a choice.

"I've drawn you a map," Wren said. "Of the western country. It's not finished. There's a stretch along the high fells I haven't walked, and a river the ferryman swears changes course when no one's watching, which I now believe is possible even without magic, because rivers are like that."

"You're saying the map has blank spaces."

"I'm saying the map has room in it." Wren held it out. "That was always the difference between us, you know. I charted what didn't exist. You guarded what did. And now there's just—country. Unfinished. Waiting for someone to walk it and say *here, this is real, I stood on it.*"

Quentin took the map. She unrolled it halfway, looked at the blank stretch of fells, and Wren saw her finger hover over it—hover, and press down, just slightly, as if testing whether the paper would hold her weight.

"I'm not the last swordspeaker anymore," she said. "There's no such thing. There's just a woman who used to be one, standing at a gate, and the gate isn't magic, it's just a gate, and on the other side of it is a road."

"That's the most hopeful thing you've ever said."

"Don't ruin it."

"I would never."

She rolled the map closed. She looked at Wren—looked at them the way she'd looked at the Door of the Last Choir, at the seal, at the god's cracked voice, at every threshold of her long buried life—and Wren stood very still and let her look, because you could not make this choice for another person. You could only be there when they finished it.

"I'm not saying yes," Quentin said.

"I know."

"I'm saying—" she stopped. Started. Stopped again, and Wren watched a century of silence war with three months of earned words. "I'm saying the fence will keep. The fence has kept for a week; it can keep for a season. And I'm saying that if I walk out this gate, I walk out because I choose to, and I'm not choosing yet, and you have to let that be a real answer and not a riddle."

"It's the realest answer I've ever been given."

She almost smiled. Then she went inside to get her sword—just a sword now, just steel—and Wren stood alone in the yard with the crooked fence and the long road and the morning coming up gold over the fells, and waited, and found, to their own surprise, that the waiting was not the old kind of waiting, the hiding kind, the hundred-years-in-a-valley kind.

It was the kind that comes before a door opens.
