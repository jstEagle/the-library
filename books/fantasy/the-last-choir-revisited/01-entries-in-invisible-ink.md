## 1. Entries in Invisible Ink

The last words of a dying fisherman, it turned out, tasted of brine and copper. Sebastian Sterling knew this because the guild's intake ledger required him to record the *character* of every bottled memory that crossed his desk, and character, in the Guild of Improbable Things, was a matter of taste. He dipped the glass stopper against his tongue—standard procedure, gloves on, breath held—and wrote in his small, immaculate hand: *Salty. Regretful. Notes of an unfinished letter.*

He had catalogued four hundred and eleven bottles since dawn. The shipment from the Widow's Coast had come in overnight, forty crates of them, each bottle no bigger than a thumb and glowing faintly the way all last words glowed—that soft amber of a voice with nowhere left to go. Somewhere in crate nineteen was the final utterance of a woman who had spent her whole life wanting to tell her sister she forgave her. Somewhere in crate thirty-one was a child's last word, which was always the hardest entry, and which Sebastian saved for last on principle, the way you save the bitterest medicine.

*Child, female, approx. six years,* he wrote. *One word: "again." Context unknown. Recommend permanent archive, restricted shelf.*

He blotted the page. He checked the blotting twice.

This was the discipline of Sebastian Sterling, junior cataloguer, ninth year of service: he did nothing memorable. His handwriting was deliberately unremarkable—a clerk's hand, one of ten thousand clerk's hands. He arrived at the guildhall at the same minute every morning and left at the same minute every evening, and between those minutes he was so thoroughly, so *professionally* forgettable that the doorman had once introduced himself to Sebastian for the third time in a year. Sebastian had shaken the man's hand warmly and said, "Sterling. A pleasure," as though for the first time, because it was safer to be met than remembered.

In nine years he had not appeared in a single guild record beyond the minimum required by charter. No commendations. No complaints. No debts. In a house that traded in bottled memories and secondhand miracles, where every soul had a price and most had already spent it, Sebastian Sterling's ledger line read like an empty room: *No assets. No liabilities. No history of note.*

It had taken him four years to make it read that way, and he guarded it the way other men guarded gold.

The trouble with a life built on not being written down, he had learned, was that nobody wrote you down even when you wished they would.

He finished the fisherman's entry and allowed himself the small ritual he permitted at midday: he took out the letter.

Not a real letter. She had never been one for letters. It was a scrap of vellum, soft as cloth from handling, on which Agatha Winterbourne—or Mother Thorn, as she'd insisted the neighbors call her, or *that hedge-witch on the fen road*, as they'd called her when they thought she couldn't hear—had written a single line in her slashing, impatient script:

*Keep your name small, boy. Small things don't get eaten.*

No signature. No date. No explanation of why she'd pressed it into his hands at fourteen years old, shoved him onto the mail coach to the city, and stood in the road watching him go with an expression he had spent nine years failing to decode. Fear? Calculation? That terrible tenderness that looks, from close up, exactly like coldness?

The coach had gone around the bend and she had still been standing there. He knew because he'd looked back until looking back made him sick. He had never stopped looking back, really. He had simply gotten better at doing it invisibly.

*Small things don't get eaten.* He folded the vellum along its worn creases and put it away in the inner pocket, over his heart, where it had lived through nine years of ledgers. Then he went back to work, because work was the wall he had built between himself and everything else, and walls only held if you laid every brick.

* * *

The afternoon brought the prayer.

Later—much later—he would understand that it should have been impossible. The guildhall's wards were the oldest thing about the old building, older than the charter, older than the masters' chairs, older than the rumor that the mountain behind the city had a hollow heart. They were woven into the mortar itself, and their entire purpose was simple: nothing that *asked* could get in. Prayers were requests, and requests were openings, and openings were how the old things had gotten their hooks into the world back when the world was younger and stupider. The founders had known this. The wards sang a low note day and night, a note that said, in the grammar of wards: *no one is home, no one is listening, go ask elsewhere.*

Sebastian had worked beneath that note for nine years without once hearing it. You didn't hear a wall unless it cracked.

It was nearly dusk. He was re-inking his pen—the third time, because the ink kept going thin, which he noted with mild irritation and attributed to cheap stock—when the sound came through the floor.

Not a voice. Not exactly. More like a voice heard through several feet of stone and one very long distance: a woman's voice, frayed to threads, saying the same thing over and over in the cadence people used when they had stopped believing anyone was listening but couldn't afford to stop asking.

*Bring him back. Please. Bring him back, bring him back, bring him—*

Sebastian sat very still.

The wards were supposed to shred such things before they reached the first landing. This one had come through like water through a rotted plank. And it was *answered*—that was the part that made the hair on his arms rise, that made the pen slip from his fingers and roll off the desk. Because after the third repetition, something answered. Not in words. In weight. The air in the archive room grew heavy the way air grows before a summer storm, and every bottle on the shelves around him flickered at once—all four hundred and eleven last words flaring amber together, like candles catching a draft—and then settling.

And then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, a sound like a ledger being opened.

Sebastian did what he was built to do. He told no one. He finished his entries. He locked the archive, signed the duty book with his forgettable hand, and walked home through streets gone strange with early dark, telling himself the whole way that it was nothing, that wards failed sometimes, that old buildings groaned, that a clerk's job was ink and inventory and nothing else.

He almost believed it by the time he reached his rooms. He fell asleep repeating it like a rosary.

* * *

The ledgers had rewritten themselves overnight.

He knew it before he reached his desk, because the archive smelled wrong—ozone and candle smoke and underneath it something older, something like the inside of a grave that has been kept warm. The lamps were lit. He had blown them out himself; he always blew them out himself; checking them twice was part of the discipline.

Every ledger on the central table lay open. Every page had changed.

His own hand stared back at him from entries he had never written. The fisherman's bottle was logged as *delivered*, recipient illegible, the ink smeared as if written by a wet finger. The child's last word—*again*, restricted shelf, permanent archive—was now marked *withdrawn*. Date of withdrawal: tomorrow.

Tomorrow.

Sebastian stood in the doorway for a long moment, one hand flat against the frame, doing the arithmetic of dread. Then he crossed the room and pulled the intake ledger toward him, because if the world had gone mad, at least it had gone mad in a language he could read.

The pages were full of prayers.

That was the only word for it. Where shipments had been, there were now petitions, hundreds of them, entered in a hand that was almost his and wasn't—slightly too elegant, slightly too eager, the handwriting of someone imitating a clerk who had never existed. Each entry followed the guild's format perfectly. Item: one prayer. Provenance: the Widow's Coast, the fen country, the capital slums. Condition: *desperate*. Price:

The price column stopped his breath.

Because the prices weren't in coin. They were in the old notation, the notation every apprentice learned in theory and no living clerk had ever seen used—the notation for transactions with parties *below*, the notation the charter called *Undercourt terms*. Firstborn sons. Voices. Shadows. Names.

Names, freely given or otherwise.

His eyes moved down the column the way a man's eyes move toward a wound. And there, near the bottom of the third page, in ink that was still drying, still glistening, still *warm*—

*Item: one soul, male, age five at time of sale. Sold by: A. Winterbourne, hedge-witch, fen district. Purchased by: the Undercourt. Term of holding: until called. Status:*

Status: *secured against default.*

Default, the entry explained, in that almost-his handwriting, would be declared upon the extinguishing of the last flame of Vaskalorth.

Sebastian read it four times. His hands were steady. Nine years of practice; his hands knew how to be steady when the rest of him was falling. He became aware, distantly, that he was whispering her words like a charm—*small things don't get eaten, small things don't get eaten*—and that the words had stopped working, or perhaps had never meant what he thought they meant, because she hadn't told him to stay small to keep him safe from being noticed.

She had told him to stay small because something enormous already owned him, and small things, sometimes, are overlooked long enough to run.

Outside the archive windows, the mountain stood black against the graying sky. Pilgrims had begun gathering on its slopes—he'd noticed them yesterday, god-touched wanderers in patched gray, singing in a language the wards usually swallowed. This morning the wards let the song through untouched, and it sounded, from this distance, almost like a choir rehearsing.

Almost like a choir preparing for a funeral.

Sebastian closed the ledger. He did not run for the masters. He did not raise the alarm. He did the thing that had kept him alive for nine years: he erased every trace of his presence from the room, straightened the bottles, relit the lamps exactly as they'd been, and walked out with his forgettable face arranged into mild clerical boredom.

Then, alone in the stairwell, out of sight of everyone, he put his hand over the inner pocket where her single sentence lay folded, and he finally let himself shake.

Nine years of safety. She had bought him nine years, and paid with the only thing she had that was hers to sell.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, in a court that dealt in names, a debt was waiting—patient, patient, patient—for a dragon's fire to gutter out.

He had perhaps a season. Maybe less.

Sebastian wiped his face, descended the stairs, and went to find out what a Vaskalorth was.
