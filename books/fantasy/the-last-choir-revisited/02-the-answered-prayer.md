## 2. The Answered Prayer

The prayer arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a shipment of grief.

Sebastian knew it was trouble before he opened the crate. Grief came to the guild in many containers—letters, locks of hair, jars of river water from the place someone had died—but it always weighed more than it should. This crate weighed like a sin. The manifest said *bottled last words, twelve units, client: M. Corvane, Thistlefen*. The manifest lied. There were eleven bottles and one thing that was not a bottle at all: a scrap of paper, pressed flat between two wax seals, covered in handwriting that was not handwriting.

It was a prayer. He could tell because the ink moved when he wasn't looking at it directly, the way ledgers did when they were ashamed of themselves.

Prayers weren't supposed to reach the Guild of Improbable Things. The wards on the receiving room ate them for breakfast; that was half the guild's business model. People prayed constantly, carelessly, the way they breathed, and nine prayers out of ten dissolved into nothing because there was no one left to hear them. The old gods were gone. Everyone knew this. The guild's entire trade in secondhand miracles depended on everyone knowing this, because a world with living gods needed no middlemen.

The tenth prayer got bottled and sold as a curiosity. The eleventh—

The eleventh had never happened before.

Sebastian sat very still in the receiving room with the moving ink in his hands and listened to the building breathe around him. Somewhere above, Master Quillon was arguing with a supplier about the going rate for a saint's yawn. Somewhere below, the vaults hummed their usual note of patient money. Everything was ordinary. Everything was fine.

He read the prayer anyway. Curiosity was a flaw in his design; he'd never managed to file it down.

*Give me back my boy,* it said. *I will pay anything. I will pay anything. I will pay anything.*

Three times. Prayers repeated themselves when the pray-er meant it, Odile-the-archivist had once told him—not singer Odile, whom he hadn't met yet, but old Odile who'd kept the dead-letter drawer until she died in it. *Repetition is emphasis, dear. Emphasis is collateral.* Sebastian had filed that away as trivia. Now it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because the prayer was not dissolving. It was not even fading. The ink moved with purpose now, crawling toward the edge of the paper like something walking home, and beneath it, faint as breath on glass, new letters were surfacing:

*Received.*

*Terms accepted.*

*Delivery pending.*

Sebastian did what he always did with things that frightened him. He catalogued them. He fetched a fresh ledger, entered the prayer under *Anomalies, Unpriced*, noted the date, the client, the weight of the crate against its manifest, and the fact that the words *delivery pending* had been written by no hand present in the room. Then he locked the prayer in the anomaly cabinet, which was warded three ways, and went to lunch, and did not taste any of it.

* * *

He learned what the prayer had bought four days later, from a news-sheet a courier left on the counter between transactions.

**THISTLEFEN: SEVEN CHILDREN DEAD IN ONE NIGHT. VILLAGE BLAMES FEVER. FEVER, SAY SURVIVORS, WALKED AND KNOCKED.**

Seven children. Every firstborn under the age of ten in a village of perhaps forty households. The article was thin on detail—the Thistlefen correspondent had apparently fled—but thick on the kind of wrongness that made Sebastian's professional instincts stand up and howl. The children had drowned. All seven. In their beds. Their lungs full of black water, though the nearest deep water was the millpond, and the millpond, the article noted, had been found empty.

M. Corvane of Thistlefen. A mother. Sebastian pulled her file from the client registry with hands that were almost steady.

Marguerite Corvane, widow, firstborn son Tam, age six, lost to the millpond in spring flood. Client of the guild for eight months. Purchases: one bottled lullaby (her own mother's), one secondhand courage (mild), one consultation regarding *the possibility of return*, price quoted, declined as too high, re-approached twice since.

She had declined. She had been told the price—a true resurrection, the only miracle the guild refused to broker—and she had walked away. Twice.

And then she had gone over the guild's head. Past the wards. Straight down.

Sebastian took the prayer out of the anomaly cabinet and looked at it properly this time, and the third line of surfacing ink had grown while it waited in the dark:

*Delivered.*

*Balance outstanding.*

Underneath, in smaller letters, like a footnote written by something that enjoyed footnotes: *First payments received with thanks. Seven of nine-and-forty.*

Nine-and-forty. Every firstborn in Thistlefen. Not seven. Forty-nine, eventually, unless someone intervened, and the something that had made the bargain counted infants still in the womb, counted children not yet conceived, counted with the patience of a creditor who owned time itself.

Sebastian closed the cabinet and stood in the dark of the archive with his heart going like a trapped bird, and understood two things with terrible clarity.

One: the gods were not gone.

Two: something under the mountain had just taken an order from a grieving woman in a village too small to appear on most maps, which meant the old powers were not merely awake. They were *open for business*. And the guild—the guild whose entire existence rested on the premise that miracles were extinct and bottling them was harmless antiquarianism—had just become, without knowing it, a storefront.

He should have reported it. That was procedure. Anomaly, unpriced, escalate to the masters.

Instead he did the other thing, the thing that would have gotten him flayed if anyone found out. He went looking for the contract.

* * *

Every bargain the guild ever witnessed lived in the deep vaults, and every bargain had a shadow copy in the night-ledgers—the ones kept in invisible ink, the ones only three people alive could read. Sebastian was not supposed to be one of the three. He had made himself one of the three years ago, quietly, the way he made himself everything: unasked, unnoticed, essential and invisible both at once.

The night-ledgers told him what he already feared. The Thistlefen contract had not come through the guild at all. It had been *notarized elsewhere*—and the notation, when he coaxed it up out of the invisible ink, was a seal he had never seen but recognized the way you recognize a word from a language you dreamed in: a circle broken by a descending line, like a door opening downward.

Beneath the mountain. Undercourt. The words arrived in his mind unbidden, wearing familiarity like a stolen coat.

There was a mirror-contract. There was always a mirror-contract; debts had reflections the way souls did. Sebastian followed the chain of references down through six ledgers, each older than the last, into the section of the vault where the air smelled of cold stone and the candles burned blue because they'd been lit before he was born. The oldest ledgers were chained shut. His keys opened locks they had never been cut for—he had copied them all, of course, invisibility required contingency—and the chains fell away with a sound like a held breath released.

The debt-ledger lay on a lectern of black wood. It was enormous, bound in something that was not leather, and it was warm.

That was the detail that broke him, later. Not the contents. The warmth. Ledgers were not warm. This one was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and when Sebastian opened it, the pages turned themselves to the right place, helpfully, hungrily, as if it had been waiting for him specifically and was pleased.

The entry was old. Fifty years old, give or take. The ink had faded to brown, but the hand was fresh in his memory because he had spent nine years forging documents in a style descended from it: the hedge-witch's hand. Sera Winterbourne's hand. The woman who had found him half-dead in a hedgerow at eight years old, taught him letters and ledgers and the thousand small arts of being overlooked, and then vanished one morning leaving nothing but a cold hearth and a note that said *be small, be safe, be missed by no one.*

The entry read:

*One soul, juvenile, unnamed. Received from S. Winterbourne, hedge-witch, in exchange for: apprenticeship secured, ward extended, nine years' protection from collection. Debtor: the child, upon naming or upon death of guarantor flame, whichever comes first. Collateral accepted. Interest accruing.*

And below it, in a child's careful, wobbling letters—the letters of an eight-year-old practicing his signature because his mistress had told him a name was the first thing a clerk must learn to write well—

*Sebastian Sterling.*

His own name. His own hand. He remembered writing it. He remembered the afternoon: rain on the cottage roof, the smell of nettle tea, Sera watching him with an expression he had filed, all these years, as pride.

She had had him sign a ledger. She had told him it was practice. And the ledger had been *this* ledger, or its twin, and the ink had drunk his name down into the dark beneath the mountain, and for nine years he had built his whole careful life on top of the fact of it without knowing, a house on a cellar he didn't have.

*Interest accruing.*

The dragon. Whatever slept beneath the mountain, whatever fire the Court's power was sealed behind—that was the guarantor flame. When it died, the debt came due. He had learned the word *Vaskalorth* only days ago, and here it was in the old entries, threaded through the ledger like a vein of ore: *guarantor flame: VASKALORTH, status: burning, burning, burning.*

Status: burning. Present tense. For now.

Sebastian put his hand flat on the page, over his own childish signature, and felt the warmth of the ledger rise to meet it, patient and pleased, like a hand closing over his from underneath.

"Hello," the ledger seemed to say. "There you are. We've been keeping your seat."

He took his hand back. He copied the entry—all of it, every clause, every cross-reference—invisible ink on invisible paper, because knowledge was the only currency no court could repossess. Then he re-chained the ledger, relocked the locks, climbed the stairs past the humming vaults and the arguing masters, and stood for a long moment in the receiving room among the crates of bottled last words.

Forty-nine children. One of them had been him, once, in the only way that mattered: a soul, signed away, priced at an apprenticeship.

Sera. What did you do? What were you so afraid of?

But he knew. He had known since the stairwell, since the shaking, since the sentence she'd left him. The gods had wanted him for something worse than a debtor. She had sold him small to keep him from being taken large.

It didn't feel like mercy. It felt like a bill, arriving fifty years early.

In the anomaly cabinet, behind three wards, the prayer-paper had gone quiet and blank, its work finished. But as Sebastian watched, one final line surfaced, delicate as frost:

*Next payment due upon extinguishing of the guarantor flame. Debtor will be collected in person.*

Debtor. Him.

Sebastian packed a bag that night—ledgers, keys, forged credentials, the folded sentence from Sera's note—and left a note of his own on his desk, in his own neat hand, dated three days in the future, saying he'd gone to audit the Thistlefen accounts. By the time anyone thought to check, he'd be gone.

The mountain was four days east. The dragon, if the ledger told true, was dying.

He intended to arrive before the flame did.
