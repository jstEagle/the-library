## 3. What the Dragon Remembers

Maren had learned to measure time in embers.

Not years—years were a clerk's invention, useful for taxes and grudges. Embers. A dragon's fire did not die all at once; it banked itself, folded inward, the way an old man folds his hands. Vaskalorth had been folding for nine years now, and each morning Maren climbed the switchback path to the caldera with a bucket of rendered tallow and a copper poker, and she counted what was left of him.

This morning there were seven coals.

She knew them individually, the way you know the freckles on a lover's shoulder. The great one at the center, deep red as a wound that never closed—that was his heart-coal, and it had been the size of a wagon wheel when she first came to the mountain as a girl of fifteen with chapped hands and no family worth naming. Now it was small enough to cover with both palms. The six around it were dimmer, gray at their rims, breathing slow.

"Good morning, old ruin," she said.

The coals pulsed. Not light—warmth has its own grammar—and Maren felt the greeting settle into her chest like a swallow of hot wine. Nine years of mornings, and he had never once failed to answer. That was the thing about dragons nobody wrote down in the bestiaries: they were courteous. Even dying, even hollowed down to a hearth's worth of themselves, they answered.

She knelt on the black glass floor—it had been stone once, before his first fire turned it to mirror—and fed the tallow along the channels cut into the caldera's rim. The fat caught, crept, found the heart-coal, and flared. For one bright moment the whole chamber lit gold, and Maren saw him the way he used to be: the vast suggestion of wings in the smoke-shapes overhead, the long skull of shadow cast against the far wall, jaws that could have held a cathedral.

Then the flare died back and there were only seven coals again, and the dark came in close around her shoulders like a coat.

"Seven," she told him. "We're at seven."

*Seven,* said the warmth, and it carried something that might have been amusement or might have been arithmetic. Dragons were precise about their endings.

Maren sat back on her heels and let herself do the thing she did every morning and hated every morning: she counted backward. Seven coals. If the pilgrims stayed away, if the wind off the peak didn't carry too much cold iron from the mining camps, if her tallow held out—if, if, if—he might last until spring. Maybe into summer. And when the last coal went out, the seal went with it, because that was the arrangement, older than any guild charter, older than most kingdoms: a god's power locked beneath the mountain, and a dragon's fire the lock, and a keeper's line the hand upon the lock.

Her line. Which was to say, her, alone, since her mother died, which was to say, her, alone, since always, if she was honest, because her mother had been half a keeper at best and mostly a drunk.

"You could have picked anyone," she said to the coals. "You could have picked someone with better arms."

*I picked someone who talks,* the warmth said. *It gets very quiet down here.*

"It does not get quiet. You snore."

*I do not snore. I smolder. It is different.*

It was, she conceded, different. She'd slept in the caldera more nights than not these past three winters, wrapped in a fireproofed blanket that smelled of sulfur and old leather, listening to the slow tick of cooling scale-skin somewhere below the floor where the last of his body lay folded like a letter no one would ever open again. Because that was the other thing about dragons: the fire went first, but the body kept. His body was still down there, vast and patient, holding the shape of himself the way a glove holds the shape of a hand. When the fire left entirely, the body would go to stone. He'd told her once, early, when she was young enough to cry about it, that this was not so bad. Stone, he said, is just a decision made permanent.

She had hated him a little, for saying it so gently.

*

The first pilgrim arrived on a Tuesday, which Maren considered typical. Trouble always came on the dull days, when you'd stopped expecting it.

She saw him from the ridge above the caldera mouth—a speck on the pilgrims' road, then two, then by week's end eleven, then too many to count without arithmetic she didn't want to do. They came up the mountain in ones and twos and little devout clusters, dressed in travel-gray and ash-colored wool, and they did not behave like the usual mountain traffic. The usual traffic wanted the hot springs, or the view, or to be able to say they'd stood on a volcano. These ones walked slowly. These ones stopped at the treeline and faced the peak and *listened*, heads tilted, like dogs hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears.

God-touched. That was the word her mother had used, spat sideways, when Maren was small. People who'd been near an answering prayer and come back changed—quieter, or louder, or with a look in their eyes like a house with all its lamps lit and nobody home. Her mother had said they smelled the way a struck match smells right before it catches.

Maren smelled it now, some evenings, drifting down from the pilgrims' camp. Struck matches. Hundreds of them.

"They're praying," she told the coals that night. "Down at the treeline. I can hear them through the rock. Not words. Just—the wanting part."

The heart-coal flared, guttered, steadied. In nine years she had learned his moods in temperature, and this was the one she liked least: the tight, banked heat of an animal deciding whether to be afraid.

*They are not praying to me,* he said.

"I know what they're praying to."

*Do you?* The warmth spread thin, thoughtful. *Ask yourself who taught them the road, keeper. Pilgrims do not find mountains. Mountains call pilgrims. And something beneath us has begun to have a voice again.*

She didn't want to think about that. She thought about it anyway, lying awake in her blanket, listening to the tick of his cooling skin and, beneath it, fainter, a rhythm she had never heard before this season—a pulse under the pulse, like a second heartbeat buried in the mountain's bones. The seal was his fire. But a lock, she understood suddenly, lying in the dark with her heart going hard, is only ever as strong as the door it's set in. And doors could be worked loose from either side.

"Vas." She hadn't called him that since she was a girl; he tolerated it the way old men tolerate nicknames, as a private kindness. "If your fire goes out while they're still up here—"

*Then the Court will not need to break the lock,* he said. *It will simply find it open.*

*

The guild man came on the ninth day of the pilgrims, in the hour before dawn, when the caldera light was all gray and the coals were at their lowest.

She heard him on the switchbacks—no attempt at stealth, which marked him as either very confident or very desperate—and she was waiting at the caldera mouth with the copper poker held low and casual, the way you hold a thing you intend to use.

He was thinner than she expected. Guild men, in her experience, arrived in threes, wearing good boots and worse smiles, carrying writs and ledgers and the particular hunger of men who catalogued miracles for profit. This one came alone, in a coat that had been mended well but often, and he stopped at the edge of the black glass floor and looked at the seven coals with an expression she couldn't place at first.

Then she placed it. Grief. The specific, unguarded grief of someone seeing a grave they weren't warned was a grave.

He didn't know he was showing it. That was the interesting part. Everything else about him was arranged—his posture, his voice, the careful blankness of his face—but the grief slipped out underneath like water under a door.

"State your business," Maren said.

"I'm looking for answers." His voice was quiet, pitched for her and not the mountain. "About the Undercourt. About what happens when—" He stopped. Looked at the coals again. "When the fire goes out."

"And what makes you think the fire's going out?"

"Because prayers are being answered," he said, "and the ledgers are rewriting themselves, and a woman's drowned son came home with river-water still in his lungs and every firstborn in her village gone." He said it flatly, factually, the way clerks say terrible things, and Maren felt the poker go heavy in her grip. "I traced the transaction. It leads here. Beneath here."

"Traced it how?"

"With ink," he said, and something in how he said it—in how carefully he said nothing else—made her want to laugh and hit him in equal measure.

"Guild," she said. "Show me your seal."

He hesitated. Just a breath. Then he drew back his sleeve and pressed his thumb to the inside of his wrist, where the guild mark sat in its wax-and-ash ink: the open ledger, the key laid across it, the small impossible star above the pages.

Maren knew that seal. Every keeper knew it, because keepers remembered things the rest of the world was paid to forget. Ninety years ago, a guild delegation had come up this mountain to negotiate the terms of the seal—what the guild would pay, what the guild would leave alone—and her grandmother had served them tea and listened at the door. The terms were still in the family memory, passed down like the copper poker: the guild got its trade routes and its bottled wonders, and in exchange, the guild's ledgers would never record the mountain's true name, and the guild's agents would never climb past the treeline.

And here one was, standing on the black glass, seal bared, having climbed past everything.

"You're breaking your own charter," she said.

"Yes," he said simply. "It seems my name was already in someone else's ledger. So I've decided to stop being careful about whose rules I'm breaking." He looked at the coals again, and this time the grief on his face didn't slip out from under anything. It just was. "His name is Vaskalorth. Isn't it? I read about him when I was an apprentice. There was a page. Someone had written down his last recorded words, from the sealing, ninety years ago. Bottled last words—we deal in them." His jaw tightened. "I catalogued them myself, four years ago. Shelf nine. I remember thinking whoever transcribed them had beautiful handwriting, and wondering why a dragon's dying words were filed under *miscellaneous*."

The heart-coal flared.

Maren felt it happen—felt the warmth leap toward the stranger like a dog hearing its name after years, felt the whole chamber heat five degrees in a heartbeat, felt the tallow channels catch unbidden and burn blue-gold. The man staggered. So did she. In nine years, Vas had never reached for anyone but her.

*That handwriting,* the warmth said, and the voice filled the caldera, vast and cracked and unmistakably awake, *was mine.*

Maren stared at the coals. "What?"

*Ninety years ago, keeper, I dictated my own ending to a guild scribe, because a dragon should choose what the world remembers.* The heat rippled, and beneath it ran something Maren had never felt from him before—something that took her a moment to name, because she had never once felt it in him. Longing. *They filed me under miscellaneous. I have been miscellaneous for ninety years.* The warmth turned, gathered, fixed on the thin man with the mended coat. *Clerk. You have read my words. You have carried them in your head across nine years of shelves. Do you know what that makes you?*

"A customer?" Sebastian said weakly.

*A witness,* said Vaskalorth. *And witnesses are the only currency the Undercourt cannot counterfeit.*

Maren looked between the coals and the clerk—the dying god-lock and the man who broke his own charter to climb past the treeline, both of them staring at each other across the black glass like two halves of a sentence finally finding each other—and she felt the thing she had been refusing to feel for nine days finally arrive, unwelcome and warm: hope. Wary, dangerous, probably stupid hope.

She lowered the poker.

"Right," she said. "Somebody start explaining. And then somebody tell me why the pilgrims at the treeline started singing an hour ago, and why the second heartbeat under this mountain has gotten faster every night since they arrived."

Sebastian went pale in the gray dawn light.

"Singing," he repeated. "What kind of singing?"

"The kind," Maren said, "with harmonies. And clerk—I don't know much about gods, but I know this: choirs don't gather unless something's about to be born. Or something's about to be dug up."

Below them, faint through the mountain's bones, the second heartbeat quickened.
