## 4. A Choir of Small Gods

The Guild of Improbable Things had survived nine hundred years on a single principle: nothing is free, and everything has a price tag. Sebastian had built his whole life inside that principle like a hermit crab in borrowed shell. So when he came down from the mountain and found the guild's great trading floor full of people singing, he understood immediately that something fundamental had broken.

They were gathered around the central dais where the Auction Bell hung—clerks, factors, two junior appraisers whose entire job was putting honest prices on dishonest goods—and they were kneeling. Not all of them. Maybe forty of the three hundred souls who worked the floor. But forty was enough to make the sound carry, and the sound was harmonized.

Harmonies took rehearsal. Harmonies took *organization*.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the mezzanine with his ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate, and he counted. Counting was what he did when the world went wrong. Forty-one kneeling. Twelve standing watchfully apart, which meant twelve more who hadn't decided. And up on the masters' gallery, behind the frosted glass of the Council Room, he could see silhouettes moving in patterns that suggested argument rather than ceremony.

One of the kneeling clerks looked up and saw him. She smiled. It was Tam Wilder, third-year apprentice, a boy who once wept over a mispriced jar of bottled courage. He smiled at Sebastian the way you smile at someone you've already forgiven for not joining you yet.

"Master Sterling," Tam called out between verses. "We're doing morning devotions. You're welcome to—"

"I have inventory," Sebastian said, and fled to the counting house.

* * *

The counting house was his sanctuary: shelves of bottled last words, drawers of secondhand miracles sorted by efficacy and decay, the long oak tables where he'd spent nine years making himself indispensable and invisible in equal measure. He locked the door, sat down, and opened yesterday's ledger with hands that were almost steady.

The ledgers had rewritten themselves again overnight.

He'd known they would. Maren's news about the pilgrims, the singing, the quickening heartbeat under the mountain—all of it pointed one direction, and the ledgers were barometers. They always had been; he'd simply never understood what pressure they measured until now. Page after page of entries had sprouted new marginalia in ink no clerk would admit to using:

*Bought: one answered prayer. Paid: see attached.*
*Attached: everything.*

And in the shipment logs—the records of goods flowing through the guild's trade routes—new destinations had appeared. Routes that had run to coastal cities now showed terminus markers beneath the mountain. The guild's arteries, redrawn. Somebody upstairs was rerouting the circulatory system of the improbable world toward whatever was waking below.

A knock at the door. Three raps, then two, then one—the old pattern meaning *friend, but be careful*.

"Who's there?" Sebastian asked.

"Nobody," said a voice he didn't know. "That's sort of my point."

He opened it anyway. Standing in the corridor was a woman he recognized from guild history more than guild presence: Odile Vane, the god-touched singer. She'd been famous before Sebastian's time—a voice that could carry prayers so cleanly the gods themselves were said to lean down and listen. Then she'd lost it. The official record, which Sebastian had cataloged himself in his first year, read simply: *Vane, O. — instrument forfeited per contract terms. See sealed annex.* The guild loved euphemisms almost as much as it loved ledgers.

"You're supposed to be exiled," he said.

"The exile order was rescinded this morning." She walked past him without invitation, trailing the smell of woodsmoke and cheap wine, and surveyed his counting house with an appraiser's eye. "Rescinded by the same masters who are currently voting on whether to convert the eastern trade routes into pilgrimage corridors. They want their choir back. Turns out a guild that trades in prayers wants its own prayer-singer on staff when the gods come calling."

"They can't just—" Sebastian stopped. Of course they could. The masters owned the routes, the wards, the very concept of guild membership.

Odile picked up a bottle from his desk—one of the last-word bottles, labeled in his own hand—and turned it in the light. "You cataloged my forfeiture," she said. "I checked. Your handwriting. Very neat. Very invisible. You write like a man trying to leave no fingerprints on his own life."

"What do you want?"

She set the bottle down and looked at him directly for the first time, and Sebastian saw what nine years of cataloging had taught him to see: the exact shape of a debt being worn. It sat on her the way grief sits on some people—not as an expression but as a posture, a way of holding the skull.

"I want to know what you found under the mountain," she said. "Because I watched your face when you came through the gates this morning, clerk, and I've seen that face before. In mirrors. That's the face of a man who just learned he's collateral."

* * *

He told her. He didn't decide to; his mouth simply started moving somewhere between the second and third question she asked, and by the time he finished, the light through the counting-house windows had gone from morning-gray to noon-pale. He told her about the debt ledger, his childhood signature, his mistress's name beside it as seller. He did not tell her about the vessel-shaped hole where that story should have ended, because he still didn't understand it himself.

Odile listened the way stone listens—completely, without interruption. When he finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"My sister," she said finally. "Her name is Liss. When we were girls, she drowned in the Millbrook flood. I prayed. Not to the small gods everyone keeps in jars—the old ones, the ones under mountains. I sang to them. And they answered." Her hand drifted to her throat, an unconscious gesture, protective. "Liss lived. My voice was the price. And here's what nobody tells you about paying a debt to the old gods: they don't close the account. They keep it open. Because an open account means you might pay again."

"And Liss?"

"Liss serves them now. Willingly. She sings in their choir under the mountain." Odile smiled, and it was the worst smile Sebastian had ever seen. "My sacrifice bought her life, and she spent that life joining the thing that took mine. That's the joke. That's the whole rotten joke of inheritance, Sterling. Debts don't die with us. They roll downhill. Blood to blood. My parents owed something I never saw the ledger for, I paid it, and my sister is paying the *interest*."

Outside, faint through the walls, the morning choir had started again. More voices today. He could hear it even here, even behind the counting house's soundproofed doors—wards designed to keep miracle-echoes out, not devotion in.

"The masters are going to give them the trade routes," Sebastian said. "As temples."

"Some already have. Master Corvane signed over the Salt Road this morning. Firstborn tithes, if the rumors hold." Odile leaned against his desk, arms crossed. "So here's my proposal, clerk. There are maybe thirty of us in this building who haven't knelt. Thirty people who remember what these things devour. We're scattered across cellars and night shifts and exile orders that got quietly rescinded. Meanwhile the kneelers have the dais, the bell, and the Council."

"That's not a proposal. That's a eulogy."

"It's a *choir*," Odile said. "Just a different kind. You know what I learned carrying prayers for ten years? A single voice gets heard once and forgotten. But voices in agreement—voices that hold the same note at the same time—that's how you wake things up. Or put them back to sleep." She tapped the ledger on his desk. "You're the only person in this building who reads the deep ledgers without flinching. You found your own signature in a debt book. Which means you know how those books work. Which means you might know how to unwork them."

Sebastian looked at her. Nine years of practice said: stay small, stay filed, stay alive. Stay a footnote.

But he'd seen his own name in ink he recognized, sold by the only person he'd ever loved, and he'd stood on a mountain with a dying dragon while the ground beat like a second heart. Smallness had not saved him. Smallness had just made him easier to steal.

"The vault," he said slowly. "Under the counting house there's a vault. Original contracts. Every deal the guild ever brokered—including, if the indexing holds, the Undercourt's side of every bargain." He met her eyes. "If I'm collateral, then somewhere down there is the paper that says so. Paper burns."

Odile's ruined throat moved as she swallowed. For the first time since entering, she looked less like stone.

"There it is," she said softly. "There's the note I've been waiting nine years for somebody else to hold."

A bell rang overhead—not the Auction Bell, but the smaller one, the summons chime that meant the masters wanted all senior clerks on the floor. Sebastian rose, and Odile rose with him, and together they stepped out into a corridor where the hymn had grown loud enough that the walls hummed with it.

On the trading floor below, Master Corvane stood at the dais in vestments none of them had seen before, and beside him, robed in gray, stood a figure Sebastian recognized from the mountain treeline: one of the pilgrim-singers, smiling with too many harmonies in her face.

"Brothers and sisters of the Guild," Corvane announced, his voice carrying the oily warmth of a man who believes he is making history, "the gods beneath the mountain extend their hand. And the first offering they require—" his gaze swept the floor and settled, with terrible precision, on the mezzanine— "is the clerk who has been hiding from them for nine years. Master Sterling. Come down. Come down and be *counted*."

Beside him, Odile breathed a word that was definitely not a hymn.

"Well," she murmured. "Sing or burn, clerk. Pick fast."
