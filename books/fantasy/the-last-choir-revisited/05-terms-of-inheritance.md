## 5. Terms of Inheritance

Odile had not sung in nine years, and the silence had grown into her like a second skeleton.

She felt it now, standing in the guild's flooded undercroft with rain drumming the grates above—felt it every time she opened her mouth and what came out was only a woman's voice, flat and mortal and hers. That was the cruelty of it. The Court hadn't taken her voice. They'd taken everything *around* it: the resonance, the reach, the thing that had let a note carry through stone and into whatever listens beneath the world. What remained was enough to speak. Just barely. Enough to order ale. Not enough to pray.

"Again," she said.

Sebastian stood among the wine racks, ledger clutched to his chest like a shield he'd brought to the wrong war. He'd rolled his sleeves up. He looked, she thought, like a man doing arithmetic at his own funeral.

"I don't understand how a song opens a door."

"It doesn't open it. It *reminds* the door that it was once something else." She crossed to him, took the page he'd copied from the debt ledger—the one bearing his own name in his own childish hand—and held it up between them. "Everything down there is contract, Sterling. The Court thinks in terms. So you don't break in. You present terms. And the old god-songs are just that: language dressed as music. Every hymn is a clause. Every verse is a signature."

"And if I sing one wrong?"

"Then the Court hears a man misfiling its property." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful. "They're very forgiving of clerical errors. It's what makes them gods."

He flinched. Good. Fear kept clerks alive; she'd watched braver men than Sebastian walk into the Undercourt's antechambers whistling, back when her voice could still make the mountain hum along.

"Now. The first hymn is called *The Unfastening*. Three verses. You'll sing the third alone." She set her hand flat against the wine rack, against the old stone behind it. "I'll give you the melody. Don't try to feel anything. Feeling is for congregations. You are a filing system with lungs."

* * *

She taught him the way she'd been taught, which was the way no one should be taught: by someone who loved the songs too much to teach them gently.

The Unfastening went into him slowly. He had a clerk's ear—precise, unlovely, exact—and by the third hour he could hold the descending phrase without letting his voice slide into pity. Pity was the trap. The hymns were built to be sung by people who wanted something, and want leaked into the notes like water into chalk, and the Court tasted want the way sharks tasted blood.

"You're thinking about your mistress," she said, when he faltered on the fourth repetition.

"I'm thinking about nine years," he said. "Nine years she let me believe I was worth an apprenticeship."

"Mm." Odile sat down on an upturned cask. Her knees ached. Everything ached; silence was heavy cargo. "My sister thought she was worth a cure."

Sebastian went still. He was good at stillness. It was probably why he'd survived the ledgers so long—the stillness of a man who has made himself small enough to slip between the lines.

"Tell me or don't," he said. "But I've learned this week that secrets compound interest."

She almost laughed. Almost. "There's a song for that too. *The Confessional*. The Court loves it. Sings itself, almost."

So she told him.

Her sister Liss had been born wrong—not wicked, just thin at the edges, a child the world seemed to be slowly erasing. Fevers like tides. Winters that nearly finished her twice before she turned ten. And their village, being poor and faithful, did what villages do: it prayed. And Odile, twelve years old with a voice that made dogs go quiet and old men weep, prayed loudest of all. Not to the new quiet gods. To the ones underneath. The ones who had never stopped listening, because they had never once been given a reason to sleep.

Something answered.

Not with ruin—that came later, that came as fine print. It answered with *terms*. Liss would live. Whole, hale, boringly healthy, blessed with a life so sturdy she'd outlast everyone who loved her. And the price, itemized, witnessed, signed in a twelve-year-old's voice because a child's voice binds tighter than any seal—

"The Court doesn't take money," Odile said. "Money is fungible. They take what can't be replaced. My voice was the collateral. Liss's life was the loan." She looked at her hands. Clerk's hands now. A singer's calluses long since softened into nothing. "Twenty-two years of payments, and I sang my last verse at nineteen, and the door closed, and my sister walked out from under the mountain *cured*, and I have hated her for eleven years with a purity I can only describe as religious."

"She didn't choose it?"

"That's the question, isn't it. The one I carried down the mountain like a stone in my boot." Odile stood, paced, turned. "Two years ago I went back. Found the pilgrim road, followed it past the wardstones, all the way to the antechamber where the Court lets the faithful kneel. And there she was. Standing at the gods' shoulder. *Willingly*, Sterling. Dressed in vestments, holding a censor, smiling at me across twenty feet of consecrated dark."

"What did she say?"

"'You sound tired, Odie.'" Odile heard her own voice go very flat, very even, the voice of a woman reading an inventory. "'You should rest. It's so much easier here, once you stop fighting the terms.'"

* * *

Sebastian didn't speak for a long time. When he finally did, it wasn't comfort. It was worse. It was arithmetic.

"The drowned boy's mother," he said. "The prayer that started all this. The Court took every firstborn in her village."

"To pay for one resurrection. Yes."

"But it wasn't random." He was pacing now, and she recognized the gait—a man walking through a ledger, checking columns. "Firstborns. Blood-debts. And your sister's life bought with your voice—your mother's gift, passed to you—" He stopped. Turned. "Odile. The Court collects inheritance."

The words dropped into the undercroft cold as well-water.

"Debt passed blood to blood," he said. "That's why they wanted children's signatures. That's why my mistress sold my soul instead of her own—it wasn't mercy, it was *structure*. A soul sold by a parent binds the child. A debt incurred by a bloodline stays with the bloodline until it's discharged or extinguished." His voice had gone quick, clerk-quick, and underneath the speed she heard something cracking. "It's not a court. It's a *creditor*. It lends life and collects generations. Your sister isn't cured, Odile. She's *collateral that pays interest*. And someday—"

"Someday her children," Odile said quietly. "If she ever has them. If she ever *wants* them, after a lifetime of standing next to the thing that owns the deed." She laughed, short and joyless. "Gods. Eleven years I thought she chose them over me. And maybe she did. Or maybe she looked at the terms the way you look at a ledger, Sterling, and understood that the only way to keep the debt from reaching anyone else was to stay close to the book."

Sebastian sat down hard on the cask.

"My mistress," he said. "In the vault. There'll be terms. Whatever bargain she struck—there'll be a schedule. A collection date. And if inheritance is the mechanism—"

"Then whoever she bought those nine years from will come looking at her blood." Odile let it land. Let him feel it. "At *you*. Not because you owe it, Sterling. Because you're the account."

He stared at her, and she watched the last comfortable lie of his life die: the idea that hiding was safety. That invisibility was armor. That a man who kept his name off every page could keep it off *this* page.

"The hymn," he said at last. His voice was steady. Barely. "Teach me the rest of it. All three verses. And then whatever comes after."

"There's always something after. The Court writes in appendices."

"Good." He stood, wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and picked up the ledger again—and this time he held it differently, less like a shield, more like a weapon he was learning to grip. "Because if they collect through blood, then I want to know every clause they think I inherited. Before they do."

Odile looked at him—this careful, buried, half-frightened man who had come to her wanting a key and was leaving with a map—and felt something in the silence where her voice used to live shift, like ice before it breaks.

"One more thing you inherited tonight," she said. "The third verse of the Unfastening is sung in the debtor's own name. You'll have to *say* it, Sebastian. Out loud. Down there. In front of them."

His jaw tightened. "Then I suppose," he said, "it's time someone read me aloud."

Above them, through the grates, the rain stopped—all at once, unnatural, like a held breath—and far off toward the mountain, something began, very faintly, to sing back.
