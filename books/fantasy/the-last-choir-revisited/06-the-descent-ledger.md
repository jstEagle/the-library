## 6. The Descent Ledger

The vault beneath the Guild of Improbable Things had no door.

That was the first thing they'd learned, the three of them huddled over Odile's stolen floor-plans in the dead hours after midnight: you did not open the Descent Ledger's vault. You *descended* into it. The guild's founders had built it downward on purpose, past the wine cellars and the reliquary of unsold miracles, past the sealed well where the guild's charter was said to sleep in a lead coffin. The deeper you went, the less the guild believed in you — and the vault sat at the bottom of all that disbelief, where a person could pass through stone like a rumor.

"You'll need to stop being certain of yourselves," Odile said. She stood at the mouth of the stairwell with her hood back, and even without her voice there was something about her face that made instructions sound like prophecy. "The last forty feet are a matter of opinion. Hold mine hand if yours shakes."

Sebastian held her hand. He told himself it was practicality. Her grip was warm and callused from nine years of silence, and he hated how much steadiness he took from it, how easily his body had decided she belonged to him before his mind had signed anything.

Behind them, Maren carried the ember.

She'd brought it down from the mountain in a brazier of black iron strapped to her back like a pilgrim's burden, and it had changed her — or rather, it had made visible what had always been there. The light moved under her skin now, faint as banked coals. Vaskalorth's fire was guttering; everyone knew it. But a guttering flame is still a flame, and in the dark below the guild it threw their shadows up the walls, huge and wavering, three travelers tall.

"The masters will feel it," Maren said quietly. "A dragon's ember, this deep in their foundations. It's like ringing a bell in a chapel."

"Then we're fast," Sebastian said. "We're ledger-clerks. We're very good at fast."

It wasn't entirely a lie. Nine years of cataloging bottled last words had taught him that every transaction left a trail, and that the oldest trails were written twice — once for the record, once for the truth. The guild's surface ledgers said the Undercourt contracts were held under triple seal in the Hall of Assizes. But Sebastian had spent years noticing what other clerks were trained not to notice: that the Hall of Assizes was a performance, a room built to be burgled by people who needed to believe they'd found something. The real archive went down. Down was where the guild kept its shame, and shame, unlike gold, is never inventoried honestly.

* * *

The descent took an hour, though Sebastian would swear afterward it took a night and part of a winter.

The stairs were cut in a spiral so tight that each turn showed them only three steps ahead, and the walls began to sweat ink. That was the old guild-work — the founders had fed the stone on miswritten contracts until the rock itself learned to remember, and now it wept faint script when it was frightened. Sebastian ran his fingers along it as they passed. Fragments of bargains gone bad. *I give my second sight for my daughter's wedding.* *I give my shadow, freely walking.* *I give the year I cannot name.*

"Stop reading," Odile murmured. "The walls read back."

He stopped. Mostly.

At the bottom, the spiral opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and the chapel comparison was not accidental, because the guild's founders had built the vault as a kind of anti-church: instead of an altar, a table. Instead of saints, shelves. And instead of a god, the Descent Ledger itself — a book the size of a tomb lid, chained open on its stand, its pages turning themselves with a slow, patient sound like breathing.

Maren set the brazier down. The ember flared once, tasting the air, and every shelf in the room leaned toward it the way flowers lean toward sun.

"It remembers fire," Maren said. Her voice had gone rough. "Everything down here remembers fire. That's why they buried it."

Sebastian approached the great book with the particular dread of a man who has spent his life avoiding his own reflection and is about to meet it in a public square. The contract he sought — his contract, the one his hedge-witch mistress had signed with his soul as currency — would be recorded here in the Court's own hand, mirrored into the guild's deepest copy the way debts echo between creditor and debtor.

He found the index carved into the lectern's edge. Surnames by decade. He knew the decade. He'd been dreading the decade since he was seven years old and learned to spell his own name in secret, in candle-smoke, so no ledger could catch it.

*Stirling.* There. One entry. The ink was brown-black and old, but it was still wet at the edges. Debts, Sebastian had learned, never fully dry.

He turned to the page.

And there it was — the signature he'd been hunting across two chapters of his life. Not his. Hers.

*Mirella Vane,* in her sharp, slanted hand, the same hand that had taught him letters on a slate, the same hand that had pressed a hot poker into the guild seal on his wrist and told him *this means you're nobody's, which is the safest thing a boy can be.*

But it was not the contract that stopped his breath. Contracts he understood. Contracts were architecture, load-bearing lies, and he'd have read hers with the cold competence of a clerk.

What stopped him was the confession bound into the margin — because the Undercourt does not take collateral without recording provenance, and provenance, in the Court's ledgers, is always written in the seller's own blood-ink, unedited, forever.

Sebastian read it standing perfectly still, the way he'd learned to stand still as a child when the gods' attention swept the valley like a searchlight. Odile came to his shoulder. Maren came to his other side. Neither spoke. The pages turned their slow breathing sound.

The confession said:

*I, Mirella Vane, hedge-witch of the Vane line, do sell into the keeping of the Undercourt the soul of the boy Sebastian Sterling, whom I have raised these four years, and I do it knowing the price and paying it gladly, because the gods returning under the mountain have marked him for their vessel, and the Court alone keeps contracts the gods must honor. Let them have his soul when the debt is called. Let him keep the nine years between. A soul is a thing they can hold. A life unlived is a thing they cannot. I have watched what they do to vessels. I choose that he hate me rather than become their house.*

*If he reads this: the seal on your wrist was never to mark you as sold. It was to mark you as claimed — by me, first, before anything older could claim you. Forgive me the order of it. I had no good options, only a choice between which monster got you, and I chose the one that deals in paper.*

Sebastian did not remember sitting down. He was on the floor of the vault with the confession still hovering in front of his eyes, printed on the inside of them now, permanent as a brand. His hands were shaking. Nine years of meticulous invisibility — the blank spaces in every register, the name withheld, the careful small life — and all of it, all of it, had been bought with the coin of his own soul by a woman who had then walked out of it, leaving him to believe himself abandoned rather than armored.

"Fury," Odile said softly, crouching beside him. "That's the first stage. Fury is honest. Stay in it a while."

"She let me think—" His voice cracked on it. "Nine years. She let me think she didn't want me."

"She wanted you alive." Maren's hand settled on his head, brief and heavy, the way she touched Vaskalorth's flank. "There are worse inheritances than anger. I've carried a few."

He laughed, or something near it, wet and ugly. "She traded my soul for my childhood. She *shopped* for monsters. She picked the one that files paperwork."

"And it worked," Odile said. "You're here. You're free enough to be stealing yourself back. That's more than most vessels get."

It was true, and the truth of it was worse than the lie had ever been, because the lie had given him someone to hate cleanly, and the truth tangled love and betrayal into a knot no blade could find the center of. He knelt there on the cold stone and grieved the woman twice over — once for the abandonment that had never happened, and once for the sacrifice that had, and both griefs were for the same woman at the same time, and he understood suddenly why the old prayers warned against looking too long at your own ledger.

Then the ember screamed.

Not Maren's ember — the brazier flared white, and every chain in the vault rang at once, and above them, far above, through sixty feet of ink-sweating stone, Sebastian heard the guild wake. Bells. Voices. The particular thunder of masters descending stairs with authority in their stride.

"They know," Maren said, already hauling the brazier onto her back. "The fire announced us. Or the book did." She nodded at the Descent Ledger, whose pages had stopped turning. All of them faced outward now, every page in that vast tome angled toward Sebastian like eyes.

Odile pulled him up by the collar. "Later," she said. "Grieve on the stairs. Run first."

They ran. Behind them the vault doors — the ones that weren't doors, the ones that were a matter of opinion — began, slowly and with terrible certainty, to exist.

Sebastian clutched the torn page against his chest, his mistress's confession, the one document in the world written in her blood-ink, proof of the worst and best thing she ever did. Above, the bells kept ringing, and beneath the bells, faint but rising, he heard something else: singing. Many voices, chanting in the old cadence, coming up from under the mountain itself.

The masters hadn't just discovered the theft.

They'd already sent word down.
