## 7. Where the Fire Gutters

Maren had learned to read the ember the way other people read weather.

Forty years in the caldera, and Vaskalorth's flame had moods the way a hearth had moods—sulky on wet mornings, greedy before storms, patient as old stone most days. She'd learned its language in spoonfuls: which woods fed it, which songs settled it, which silences meant *hold still, keeper, I am thinking*.

Tonight it spoke plain.

The flame that had burned down from a bonfire to a coal to something you could cup in two hands guttered once—twice—and then rose, all at once, straight up like a spear. The light went white. Maren's shadow was thrown against the far wall of the cave, huge and wavering, and for one terrible heartbeat she thought: *he's going out.*

Then the color came back, wrong. Not the deep gold of dragonfire but something colder underneath it, a blue-white thread running through the gold like frost through blood.

"No," she said, out loud, because there was no one else to say it to, and because some things needed saying even when they were true. "No, old man. You hold."

Vaskalorth's eye opened. It took him a long time now—the lids moved like doors in a house nobody lived in anymore—but they opened, and the eye that found her was still the eye she'd known since she was nine years old and foolish enough to climb the mountain looking for the god everyone said had died here.

"Keeper." His voice came through the stone more than the air, a vibration in her boot-soles, her teeth. "Something has come up the mountain."

"Pilgrims. I know. I've watched them for weeks."

"Not pilgrims." The flame shuddered, and the blue thread brightened. "Debts. They are walking debts, little keeper. The Court is calling in what it is owed, and everything it touches comes up the mountain to kneel."

Maren looked at the fire again—at the frost-thread pulsing in time with something far below, slow as a heartbeat under floorboards. "That's what's feeding off you."

"That is what has always been sealed by me." A sound moved through the cave that might have been laughter, if laughter could be made of gravel and regret. "I did not tell you, in the beginning, how long the sealing would ask of me. I told myself I would find another way before it mattered. Dragons are liars, keeper. It is our first language."

"You told me you were dying," she said. "You let me spend my whole life on a lie with an ending."

"I let you spend your whole life on a truth with a shape you could carry." The eye closed, opened. "I am sorry. Both of those sentences are the same sentence. Choose whichever lets you sleep."

She wanted to shout at him. She had shouted at him before, over smaller things—a stolen goat, the year he ate her mother's loom—but the words wouldn't come, because the flame chose that moment to gutter again, and this time when it steadied, it had shrunk. Visibly. A coal becoming a bead. A bead becoming—

"How long?" she asked.

"With them pulling from below?" Vaskalorth considered. "Days. Perhaps fewer. When I go out, the seal goes out, and everything I have been holding shut for nine hundred years will stand up and stretch."

* * *

They came at dusk three days later, and Maren knew it before she saw them, because the birds left the mountain in a single silent river, flowing east, not one of them singing.

From the caldera's rim she counted forty figures climbing the pilgrim road in formation—not the ragged, weeping processions she'd watched for weeks, but ordered ranks in grey robes, and at their head walked Master Corvane of the Guild of Improbable Things, whom she remembered as a soft-spoken clerk who'd once haggled her down three coppers for a jar of bottled thunder. He carried a staff now instead of a ledger-stick, and the staff was black wood banded in silver, and the frost-light of the Undercourt ran up it like sap.

She should have rung the warning bell. There wasn't one; there never had been; her only defenses were a mountain's bad roads and a dragon's reputation, and both had failed.

"They've come for Sebastian," Vaskalorth said. He didn't open his eyes. Speaking cost him visibly now; each word dimmed the flame. "The guild masters who knelt. The Court wants its debtor delivered before the last light fails."

"He's coming here anyway. He sent word through Odile—he'll arrive within the week."

"Then the Court is early," said the dragon, "and so must we be."

"We?"

The eye opened all the way, and for the first time in months there was something in it besides exhaustion. Something that might, in a younger creature, have been called mischief.

"Maren Ashfell. Forty-one years you have fed me, sung to me, argued with me, and buried my shed scales under the rowan tree like a good daughter. Have I ever asked you for anything?"

"You ate my mother's loom."

"*Asked.*"

She stood at the rim of his bed of coals, hands on hips, and felt forty-one years settle onto her shoulders all at once, and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, exactly what he was about to ask, and why she was going to say yes.

"The seal doesn't need me alive," he said. "It needs my fire. Fire can be carried. It has never been carried because no carrier could survive the descent into the Court—and because I never trusted anyone to bring it back." The flame bent toward her, gold and frost. "I trust you. Bring it down into the dark. Feed it to the hymn the singer knows. And when it is done—" 

He stopped. The flame flickered.

"When it is done, there won't be anything to bring back," Maren said.

"When it is done," Vaskalorth agreed, gently, "there won't be anything anywhere. That is rather the point. The gods end, or everything does. I have done the arithmetic many times across nine hundred years, and I have never once gotten a different sum."

"And I'm supposed to just carry you down there like a lantern."

"Like the best lantern ever made," he said, "and I will complain about the handling the entire way, because I am a dragon, and dignity is our second language after lying."

Maren laughed. It came out cracked and wet, nothing like a laugh, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand and hated him and loved him in the same breath, the way family does.

"If you die while I'm carrying you," she said, "I swear on the rowan tree I will dig up every scale and make them into soup bowls."

"A fitting fate." The great head lowered until his brow nearly touched her, and his breath washed hot and cold at once. "Little keeper. You kept me. No one has ever kept anything so well. Whatever happens below, that was not wasted. Nothing kept is ever wasted."

* * *

Taking the fire was not like taking a candle-flame.

It was like being asked to hold a river. She knelt in the coals—they did not burn her; they never had; that had always been part of the keeping—and pressed both hands around the last of Vaskalorth's flame, and the fire poured into her chest and sat down behind her sternum like a second heart, furious and enormous and *old*. She gasped. Her skin lit faintly along every vein, gold shot through with frost-blue, and she understood suddenly why the guild had bottled miracles instead of drinking them: some things change you at the root.

Behind her, Vaskalorth's body dimmed like a landscape at dusk. Still vast. Still himself. But quiet in a way that frightened her worse than any roar.

"Hurry," he said. The word barely stirred the air. "And keeper—if you meet a boy down there with ink on his fingers and fear in his pockets—tell him the debt he fears is not the debt he thinks. Tell him to read the confession twice."

"What confession?"

But the eye had closed, and the flame in her chest pulled her forward, down the mountain path, away from the caldera, toward the guild road and the distant smudge of lamplight where the town lay under a sky already bruising purple with frost-light rising from below like the aurora gone wrong.

* * *

She reached the guild at midnight and found it already taken.

Grey-robed masters at the doors. Corvane's black staff raised over the courtyard, where clerks knelt in rows with their ledger-books burning in a ceremonial heap—nine years of trades and bargains going up as smoke that smelled wrong, sweetish, like offerings. And held between four kneeling masters like a sacrifice already half-consecrated:

Sebastian Sterling.

He wasn't struggling. That was the worst of it. He hung in their grip with his chin up and his eyes moving fast—counting exits, counting faces, cataloguing even now, even at the end—and when his gaze found hers across the courtyard, something in his face broke open with relief so naked it hurt to witness.

*Maren,* his mouth shaped. No sound. But the frost-light flared, and the masters turned, and Corvane smiled the smile of a man who has just found the last item on his list.

"The keeper," Corvane called, "and the ember. The Court thanks you for delivery, madam. Both debts arrive together. How efficient."

Maren looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked at Maren. Nine years of guild caution versus forty years of a keeper's stubbornness, and neither of them hesitated.

"Now," he said, and tore free.

What followed was not a battle; it was a burglary conducted at speed, which was, she would later think, the most guild thing she had ever witnessed. Sebastian threw ledgers—actual burning ledgers—into the faces of the masters holding him. A small woman with ink-stained cuffs (Odile? No—Odile was already at the cellar door, waving them frantically) tripped a master with a perfectly placed foot and stole the keys off his belt mid-fall. And Maren walked through the middle of it with a dragon's last fire beating inside her ribs, and wherever she stepped, the frost-light recoiled, hissing, because the Court's power had never once, in nine hundred years, met anything it couldn't buy—and fire cannot be bought. Only spent.

They reached the cellar. Odile slammed the door. Somewhere above, Corvane began to sing, and the mountain answered, and the whole guild groaned on its foundations like a ship changing course.

"The descent vault," Sebastian panted. "Under the ledgers. There's a stair that goes down past the foundations—I found it in chapter six of the charter nobody reads. It goes all the way."

"To the Court," Maren said.

"To the Court." He looked at her then—at the light running under her skin, at the way her shadow burned gold at the edges—and his voice dropped. "That's him. That's Vaskalorth. Maren, if you come down there, the fire gets spent. All of it. There's no version where he survives this."

"I know." She rolled up her sleeve and showed him the glow along her veins, steady as banked coals. "He did the arithmetic. So did I." A pause. "He also says: read the confession twice. Does that mean anything to you?"

Sebastian went very still. Then, slowly, he touched the satchel at his hip—where, she realized, a stolen page rode against his ribs, close as a second heartbeat.

"It might," he said quietly. "It just might."

Above them, the singing swelled. The door glowed frost-white at its seams. Odile began, low and steady, the first notes of the unbinding hymn, and the stair into the dark opened its throat beneath their feet.

Maren put her hand flat against her chest, against the ember, and said goodbye to the mountain, and to the rowan tree, and to nine hundred years of a liar who had told her one true thing after all:

*Nothing kept is ever wasted.*

"Down," she said. "While there's still fire to spend."
