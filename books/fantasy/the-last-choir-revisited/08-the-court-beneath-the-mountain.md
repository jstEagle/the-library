## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stairs went down for a very long time.

I counted them at first—clerk's habit, catalog everything—and lost my place somewhere past four hundred when the walls began to breathe. Not metaphorically. The stone expanded and contracted in slow rhythms, like the ribs of something sleeping, and each exhalation carried warmth up from below that smelled of woodsmoke and beeswax and, underneath it all, of old paper left too long in a damp room.

Maren walked ahead with the ember cupped in an iron lantern, her knuckles white around the handle. Vaskalorth's last flame had shrunk to something the size of a robin's egg, but it threw light farther than it should have, and where that light touched the breathing walls, they flinched.

Odile hummed as she descended. Not a song—the Court had taken her songs—but the skeleton of one, melody stripped down to intervals, the way you might sketch a face from memory. The walls breathed in time with her. I tried not to think about what that meant.

"Tell me again why we're walking into the mouth of this," Maren said without turning around.

"Because the contract is here." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "The original page. Everything else is copies, and copies can be argued with. The original has to be *unmade*."

"And the gods who hold it are just going to let us walk in and burn their ledger."

"No," I said. "They're going to offer me a job."

She stopped on the stair and looked back at me, and in the ember-light her face was all hollows and stubbornness. "You've been reading the negotiation notes before we got here."

"I've been *their* clerk for nine years, Maren. I know how they write." I touched the satchel at my hip, where Odile had sewn the unbinding hymn into the lining in thread pulled from her own hem. "They don't take. They *offer*. That's what makes them worse than anything with teeth. Teeth you can run from. An offer follows you home."

* * *

The stairs ended in a door.

I say *door*, but it was more honest than that: it was a page. A single sheet of vellum, tall as a cathedral arch, blank on both sides, standing upright in the dark with no wall around it. When Maren raised the lantern, the ember's light sank into the vellum instead of reflecting off it, and letters began to surface—slowly, the way drowned things rise.

**SEBASTIAN STERLING. ADMITTED.**

Not asked. Admitted. The way you admit a patient. The way you admit a debt.

"Don't read them," Odile said quietly. "Whatever else appears on that page, don't read it aloud. Words spoken in the Court become terms."

I put my hand flat against the vellum. It was warm. It was warm the way a hand is warm, and for one vertiginous moment I felt it press back, gentle, almost fond.

Then the page opened like a gate, and we stepped through into the Undercourt.

* * *

I had expected a cavern. What I got was a library.

It went on farther than light could follow—shelves carved from the living rock, rising out of sight, every one of them crammed with ledgers, scrolls, tablets, knotted cords, clay cylinders, things bound in leather I refused to identify. The whole history of bargain-making, shelved and dusted and *kept*. Somewhere in that dark, pages turned by themselves, a sound like distant wings.

And between the shelves, they were waiting.

I don't know how else to describe the old gods except as *readers*. They had shapes—you could catch a shoulder, a crown, a long fold of shadow that might have been a robe or a wing—but the shapes kept resolving into something else, the way a word repeated too many times dissolves into noise. They were vast and they were patient and every one of them held a book, and every book was open, and every open page was turned toward me.

The largest of them spoke first. Its voice didn't come through the air; it arrived already inside my skull, formatted, typeset, as if I'd always known it.

*Sebastian. You kept our accounts beautifully.*

"I kept *no one's* accounts," I said. "That was rather the point of me."

*Every entry you made, you made in our grammar. Every price you calculated used our arithmetic. Nine years, child. You thought you were hiding from us. You were apprenticing.*

My mouth went dry. Because it was true—that was the horror of it, the thing I'd been circling since the night the ledgers rewrote themselves. Bottled last words, secondhand miracles, prayers brokered at three percent: none of it was neutral trade. It was liturgy with a commission structure. I hadn't been hiding in the guild. I'd been *practicing*.

Maren shifted beside me, the lantern held high, and the ember hissed—a small, furious sound, like a cat backed into a corner. The nearest god drew its shadow-shape back from the light with something that might have been courtesy.

"We're not staying," Maren said. "We're here for one page. Then we go."

*One page,* the great god agreed, and its voice was honey poured over iron. *Of course. But first—since you have come so far, since you have carried fire so faithfully—let us make our offer. We would be poor hosts otherwise. And we,* a pause, a turning of pages somewhere in the dark, *are never poor.*

* * *

They showed me, and this was the worst part—they didn't tell me, they *showed* me, the way you'd show a man a house he was thinking of buying.

A hall filled with people who knew my name. Not the name I'd scrubbed off every ledger, not Sterling-the-invisible, but *Sebastian*, said warmly, said in chorus, said by choirs trained in vaulted ceilings built from the bones of the mountain. I stood in the middle of it—I wasn't standing anywhere, I was still in the library, but I *stood there*—and a hundred voices rose together, and the sound they made was the sound I'd been listening for my entire life without ever once admitting it.

*Belonging,* the god murmured. *You catalog other people's families because you were never given one. We will give you ours. We will give you theirs—all of theirs. Every worshipper under the mountain, every client who ever whispered a wish over a guild counter. They will be your congregation and you will be their vessel, and you will never eat alone, never sleep alone, never be a marginal note in anyone's book again.*

I saw power next. Not crude power—not armies—but the power I'd actually wanted, if I was honest, which I was being, horribly, in that place. The power to *amend*. To reach into the world's ledger and correct the entries that had broken my heart: the hedge-witch's cottage, the night she left, the nine empty years. One signature. One line drawn through grief. I could feel my hand itching for a pen that wasn't there.

*And your name,* the god said, soft now, intimate, a hand on the back of the neck. *You sold yourself into namelessness to be safe. We understand. Safety is a fine thing. But safety is not the same as being loved, is it? Give us your vessel—your hands, your voice, your years—and we will carve your name into the mountain itself. Children will be named after you. Your name will outlast the language it's written in.*

"That's not an offer," I heard myself say. My voice sounded very small in all that dark. "It's a purchase order. I've written ten thousand of them. I know the format."

*All offers are purchase orders,* the god said, and I felt it smile, though gods don't have mouths. *The only question is whether the buyer is kind.*

* * *

And then she was there.

Wren. My mistress. My hedge-witch, my whole childhood, the woman whose leaving had hollowed me out and whose confession—*I bought you nine years*—had refilled the hollow with something worse. She stood between two shelves in the grey dress she'd been buried in, except she wasn't buried, she was *held*, collateral, a soul in escrow, and she looked exactly as she had on the morning I turned thirteen and she kissed my forehead and walked north and never came back.

"Sebastian." Her voice frayed at the edges, the way voices do in dreams. "Oh, love. Look at you."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "Don't do the voice. Don't stand there looking like her."

"It is her," Odile said softly behind me. "That's the cruelty of it. They keep what they hold *intact*. It's worth more intact."

Wren crossed the space between us—or the space crossed itself; distances in the Undercourt were negotiable—and took my face in her hands, and her hands were cold and real and I hated that they were real.

"Accept it," she whispered. "Please. Nine years was all I could buy you, and I've watched every one of them from in here, and I am so tired, sweetheart. Say yes. Become their vessel and they'll release me—we'll both be free, we'll be *together*, you'll have the family you—" Her thumb traced my cheekbone, the way she used to when I had fever. "You've spent your whole life keeping your name off the page. Let someone finally write it *large*."

And God help me, some traitorous cellar of my heart said *yes*. Said it easily, gratefully, the way a lock says yes to its key. Here was everything: the choir, the belonging, the power to amend, the mother returned, the name in stone. All I had to do was stop being me. Hand over the steering wheel of my soul and ride along forever in a body that sang for gods who ate prayers and answered them with ruin.

Every firstborn in a village, gone for one drowned boy. That was the arithmetic I'd be signing.

"Wren." I caught her wrists. Held them. "What did you teach me the first week? Before any spell, before any herb. Do you remember?"

Her eyes flickered. Something behind them moved—not her, something wearing her patience.

"You taught me to read the *whole* contract," I said. "Especially the parts written small. Especially the parts that cost the other fellow." I leaned close. "So read this one, mistress. What happens to the vessel when the gods are done with it?"

Her hands went still against my face.

"What happened to the last one?" I pressed. "Where is he shelved?"

And Wren's expression broke—not into grief, exactly, though grief was in it, but into something older and fiercer, the look she'd worn the day she signed me away: a woman doing terrible mathematics and hating every digit. Her lips shaped one word, silent, meant only for me.

*Run.*

Then the thing behind her eyes finished putting on her face, and smiled with her mouth, and said in her voice:

*"He's on the third shelf. Would you like to meet him?"*

Behind us, at the top of the long stairs, faint as a struck match, Vaskalorth's ember flared—and every shelf in the infinite dark shuddered, and somewhere far above, a dragon who had already given everything prepared to give the last of it.

The great god closed its book.

"*Terms,*" it said, "are now open for discussion."
