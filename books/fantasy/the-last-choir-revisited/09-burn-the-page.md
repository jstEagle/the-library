## 9. Burn the Page

The Undercourt did not have a ceiling. It had a throat.

Sebastian stood in the center of it with the stolen contract pressed to his chest, and above him—where a ceiling should have been—the mountain's roots curled and flexed like a gullet deciding whether to swallow. The old gods were everywhere and nowhere: a pressure behind the eyes, a sweetness in the air like fruit rotting into wine, a chorus of voices that never quite resolved into words. *Vessel. Vessel. Vessel.* They had been singing that word since he crossed the threshold, patient as interest accruing.

Beside him, Maren held the ember in its iron lantern, and the lantern shook in her grip. Not from fear. From grief.

"He's almost gone," she said. "Vaskalorth. I can feel it. Whatever we're going to do, we do it now."

"Then we do it now." Odile stepped forward, and the darkness around her seemed to lean away. Even voiceless, she had a way of occupying sound—of making silence feel like her decision. She pressed her hand flat against Sebastian's sternum, over his heart, and looked up at him. "The hymn needs two voices. One to call the debt, one to release it. I lost my voice paying for my sister's life. You're about to spend yours buying it back." Her mouth curved, crooked and fierce. "Symmetry. The old gods love symmetry. It's the one thing they can't counterfeit."

"I thought you said your voice was gone for good."

"I said it was *collateral*. Collateral isn't destroyed, Sebastian. It's held." She tapped her own throat. "And we're standing in the vault where everything held is kept."

The Court had taken it from her nine years ago, when she sang her sister free. It sat somewhere in this dark—a voice in a jar, a song in a bottle, shelved among the last words and secondhand miracles Sebastian had spent his life cataloging. He had spent nine years learning where the Guild of Improbable Things kept everything. The Undercourt, it turned out, kept things the same way. Greed was greed, whether it wore a merchant's face or a god's.

They had found the jar an hour ago, in a niche of black stone behind the throne of roots. Odile had not opened it yet. She had just held it, both hands, the way you hold something that might still be beating.

"Sing," Sebastian said. "And when you can't hold the line, I'll take it."

"You can't sing."

"I've been reading hymns in the margins of ledgers for nine years. I know every unbinding verse the guild ever suppressed." He managed a smile. "I'll be flat. The gods will suffer."

Odile laughed—a small, cracked sound, the last sound her borrowed silence would ever make—and pulled the stopper from the jar.

Her voice came out like a bird released from nine years in a bottle: ragged, wild, furious with living. The first note she sang was not beautiful. It was *true*, and it went through the Undercourt like a blade through wet paper. The chorus of gods stuttered. The pressure behind Sebastian's eyes flinched.

*There it is,* he thought. *The sound they took a voice to stop.*

She was singing the unbinding hymn—the real one, the one the guild's founders had cut into pieces and scattered through their ledgers so no one could ever sing it whole. Sebastian had spent three nights reassembling it from fragments, in the margins, in invisible ink, in the shorthand of clerks who had died believing they were only filing paperwork. He knew it the way he knew everything: secondhand, on paper, at a safe distance.

Now there was no distance.

He opened his mouth and joined her.

His voice *was* flat. It cracked on the second verse and went sour on the third, and none of it mattered, because the hymn was not a performance. It was a contract. That was the secret at the heart of it, the thing the guild had always known and never said aloud: every song was a ledger entry, every prayer a signature, every name a debt. And a contract could be *broken*—but only by the parties who signed it, in the presence of a fire hot enough to burn what was written.

Maren opened the lantern.

The ember woke.

Vaskalorth's last flame had been dying for a hundred years, guttering down to a coal the size of a fist, and in the light it threw, Sebastian saw the Undercourt as it truly was: not a hall but a *hoard*. Shelves of bottled voices. Racks of bottled years. Jars of firstborn laughter, of wedding vows, of the particular silence after a door closes on someone who isn't coming back. The old gods did not devour souls. They *filed* them. They were the first guild, the original ledger-keepers, and every prayer ever answered was an entry in their books.

And there, on the central dais, in a lectern of fused bone and gold: the master ledger of the Court. His page in it. The contract his mistress had signed with a name that was also his name.

*Sebastian Sterling. Sold. Paid in full. Term: nine years of safety, vessel-hood deferred until the fire fails.*

The fire was failing now. He could feel it in Maren's trembling hands, in the ember's light going from gold to orange to the deep red of a closing wound.

"Odile," he said, between verses. "The lectern."

"I see it." Her voice was doing something impossible now—singing harmony with itself, the nine lost years layering over the present, so that she sounded like a choir of one woman at two ages. "The hymn holds them. The gods can't move while it's sung. But Sebastian—" and here her voice broke, not from the singing but from what came after, "—the contract has to be *fed*. To the fire. By your hand. And the fire takes what it burns. All of it."

"I know."

"You don't. Vaskalorth told me, before we came down. The flame that burns a god's ledger doesn't stop at the page. It burns the *charter*. Everything the Court is anchored to. The guild's founding seal. The trade routes, the vaults, the wards—" She swallowed. "The guild dies with them. Everything you've been hiding in for nine years."

The chorus of gods had gone quiet. Not defeated. *Listening.* That was worse. That was the sound of a creditor hearing a debtor say something interesting.

Sebastian thought about the guild. The smell of ink and tallow. The ledgers with their cracked spines. The clerks who had become his family without either of them signing anything. He thought about how he had spent nine years keeping his name off every page, and how it had never once kept him safe—it had only kept him *unclaimed*, which was a different and lonelier thing.

And he thought about what Odile had told him, three nights ago, in the dark above the vault: *A name freely given is the only inheritance that cannot be stolen.*

"Good," he said.

He walked to the lectern. The gods did not stop him. That was the trap, of course—he understood it even as he walked into it. They *wanted* him to hesitate. They wanted him to stand before his own page and weigh it, because a soul that hesitated was a soul that could still be bargained with, and bargaining was the only prayer they had ever truly answered.

The page was written in ink he recognized. The same brown-black ink his mistress had used for everything—her invoices, her recipes, the single letter she'd left him when she vanished, which he had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Her handwriting was in the margin, small and cramped:

*Forgive me. I could not keep you safe and keep you mine. I chose safe.*

"You chose wrong," Sebastian said aloud, and his voice did not crack, and it was not flat.

He lifted the page from the ledger.

The Undercourt screamed—not the gods, but the *structure* of it, the mountain's throat closing, the shelves shivering, ten thousand bottled souls rattling in their jars like hail against a window. The hymn was still being sung. Odile had not stopped. Maren had not stopped holding the lantern steady, though her arms shook and tears cut clean lines through the ash on her face. The ember in its cage had gone almost white, the way fire goes at the very end, when it spends everything it has left on one final brightness.

Vaskalorth was spending his last century on this. On Sebastian's page.

Sebastian fed the contract into the flame.

It caught at once—not like paper, like *memory*, the way a thing burns when it was never really paper at all. He held it until the heat reached his fingers and kept holding, because a contract had to be surrendered whole, and he was not going to hand the gods a single scrap of himself to keep.

The fire took the debt. Then it took the term, the signature, the margin note in brown-black ink—*I chose safe*—and for half a heartbeat Sebastian saw his mistress's handwriting glow gold in the flame, and he could have sworn the letters rearranged themselves into something else. *I loved you.* Or maybe that was only what fire does to anyone who watches too long.

Then it took the charter.

He felt it go. Not heard—*felt*, the way you feel a tooth leave the jaw. The guild's founding seal, nine hundred years old, dissolved somewhere far above. The wards on the shipping routes went out like lamps at dawn. Every contract the guild had ever brokered, every bottled memory and secondhand miracle, every debt in every ledger in every vault—released. Not destroyed. *Released.* Somewhere above him, ten thousand clerks were waking to ledgers full of blank pages, and Sebastian found, to his surprise, that he was laughing.

The gods' chorus came apart mid-note.

It did not scream. It *unraveled*—a chord held too long finally letting go, each voice drifting off to wherever unbound things go. The pressure behind his eyes lifted. The sweetness in the air curdled and faded, and the Undercourt's shelves stood dark and ordinary, full of nothing but glass and dust and the faint smell of old prayers finally allowed to be only words.

The mountain's throat closed gently, like a mouth relaxing in sleep.

And the ember went out.

Maren made a sound Sebastian would never be able to describe afterward, and never wanted to. She knelt with the empty lantern in her lap, and the lantern was just a lantern now, and somewhere far above them a dragon who had held a door shut for a hundred years was finally, at long last, allowed to rest. She did not cry the way people cry at funerals. She cried the way people cry at the end of a very long shift, when the work is done and the hands finally remember they are tired.

"He said—" She stopped. Started again. "At the end. He said the fire wasn't a cage. It was a *promise kept*. He said someone should keep one more."

Odile's voice, whole and ragged and hers, said quietly: "Then we'd better find out what."

Sebastian looked down at his hands. They were empty. No contract. No debt. No page in anyone's book, anywhere in the world, with his name on it. Nine years of meticulous invisibility, and now the whole architecture of hiding was ash, and he was standing in the ruins of the only life he'd known, voice hoarse, fingers blistered, flat-singing, ledger-less.

He had never felt so completely, catastrophically *visible*.

And he had never felt so much like himself.

* * *

They climbed for a long time. The passages that had led down in terror led up in silence, and the silence was a good silence, the ordinary kind, the kind that is only the absence of noise and not the presence of something listening.

When they emerged, dawn was breaking over the mountain, and the guild was burning.

Not all of it. The god-worshipping masters' wing was a pillar of black smoke—the charter's death had unmade their claims, their authority, their carefully hoarded divinity, and they had apparently taken it the way gods take things: badly. But the eastern range, the clerks' quarters, the low long hall where the junior ledgers were kept—those still stood, and there were people on the steps, and as the three of them came down the mountain road, filthy and hollow-eyed and carrying an empty lantern, the people on the steps began to murmur.

Sebastian knew what they were seeing. The clerk who kept his name off every page. The singer who lost her voice and got it back. The keeper of a dead dragon. He knew, because he had spent nine years watching how stories moved through the guild, that by nightfall there would be a dozen versions, and by winter there would be a hundred, and none of them would be true, and one of them would probably be sung.

Odile bumped his shoulder with hers. "They're going to want a new guild, you know. A house with no charter, no seal, no debts. Someone will have to write the first ledger."

"Let them want," Sebastian said.

But he was already reaching, without quite meaning to, for the little book in his coat—the blank one he'd carried for nine years and never written in, because anything written could be held, and anything held could be claimed.

The pages were still blank. The binding was still warm from his body. And for the first time in his life, Sebastian Sterling—no vessel, no debtor, no hidden name—wanted to write in it.

At the top of the first page, in the brown-black ink he'd carried all this way without letting himself think about why, he wrote a single line:

*Ledger of the Guild of Freely Given Names. Entry the first:*

And then he paused, pen hovering, because the next word was the hardest word he had ever written, harder than any hymn, and the clerks were coming down the steps to meet them, and Odile was humming something new, and the mountain stood quiet behind them with nothing left inside it but stone.

He wrote his name.

And below it, in smaller letters, in ink that was only ink: *given, not taken.*
