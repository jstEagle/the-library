## 10. A Name Freely Given

The guild had burned for three days before the rain came to argue with it.

Sebastian stood in what had been the counting room and watched the water find its way through the collapsed roof, dripping onto a floor of blackened oak that still held the ghost-shapes of the old ledgers' shelves. Everything above the third stair was gone. Everything below was drowned. The Guild of Improbable Things had spent two hundred years trading in bottled memories and secondhand miracles, and in the end it had been unmade by a single page of vellum fed to a dying dragon's fire—his page, his debt, his choice—and the whole architecture of improbable commerce had come down like a house of cards discovering gravity.

He should have felt grief. He felt something adjacent to it, the way you feel weather through a window.

"Mind the beam," Maren said, coming up behind him with an armload of salvaged slate. She moved through ruins the way she'd once moved through Vaskalorth's cavern: with the unhurried certainty of someone who had already survived worse. The dragon's keeper had ash in her hair and a burn scar wrapping her left forearm where the last ember had kissed her on its way down into the dark, and she carried both like decorations.

"The beam can mind itself," Sebastian said.

"That's how buildings fall on people."

"They mostly fall on people who deserve it."

Maren snorted and set her slates down anyway, angling them away from the drip line with a precision he suspected she'd learned tending eggs. "Odile's back in an hour. She found a roof. Well—a roof-adjacent structure. The old tithe barn on the east road." She paused. "She says it has good bones and terrible acoustics, which for her is practically a love letter."

Sebastian looked at the wet black floor, at the shelf-ghosts, at the place where the master ledger had sat for two centuries cataloguing every soul bought, sold, borrowed, and quietly misplaced. He thought about what Odile had said on the walk out of the mountain, when they were all too exhausted to lie: *You didn't destroy the guild, Sebastian. You destroyed what the guild was holding down.*

He hadn't understood it then. Standing here, he was beginning to.

* * *

The tithe barn smelled of hay rot and old incense, which turned out to be a better combination than anyone expected. By the second week they had a fire going, tables made from doors laid across barrels, and eleven clerks sleeping in lofts that creaked like ships. The clerks had come back one and two at a time over those first days—not the masters, never the masters; the masters who hadn't knelt to the returning gods had scattered to other cities and other ledgers, and the ones who had knelt were learning, slowly and publicly, what it cost to worship things that devour. No. It was the clerks who returned. The ink-stained, underpaid, invisible people who had spent their careers making other people's names disappear.

They came back because Odile sang to them.

Not god-songs. She refused those now, or rather she kept them the way you keep a knife—wrapped, oiled, out of reach of children. What she sang in the evenings was older and smaller: work songs, road songs, a lament for a dragon whose name most of the world would never learn. Her voice had come back to her in the Undercourt, pulled out of the Court's collateral vaults in the same hour the gods' power guttered and died, and it was not the voice she'd lost. It was rougher. Lower. A voice that had been owned and taken and given back, and knew the difference between all three.

"People keep asking me," one of the clerks said—it was Tam, the youngest, who had once bottled last words for a living—"what we are now. If the guild charter burned with everything else. If we're even allowed to exist."

"We're allowed," Sebastian said. "We just aren't chartered."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is who holds the paper."

Tam looked at him for a long moment, and then at the big empty book Odile had bought from a stationer in the valley town—blank pages, good rag paper, a spine that would open flat—and something shifted in his face. Understanding arriving the way understanding always did in that first year: sideways, unwelcome, and then suddenly obvious.

"So we write our own."

* * *

They argued about it for nine days. Properly, loudly, with diagrams scratched in charcoal on the barn wall. Some wanted to rebuild the guild exactly as it had been, minus the soul-trading, as if the soul-trading had been a wart and not the heart. Some wanted no book at all—no records, no names, nothing written down ever again, because every ledger they had ever known had been a weapon pointed at somebody. Old Wren, who had catalogued miracles for forty years, stood in the middle of the argument and said the truest thing anyone said that season:

"A blank page isn't safe. A blank page is just a page nobody's decided to be honest on yet."

So they decided.

The new house—the House of Kept Names, though everyone just called it the barn—would keep one kind of record only: names, freely given, with the story attached. Not debts. Not contracts. Not collateral. If you gave your name to the book, you wrote beside it what it meant to you, and the clerks' only duty was to remember it truly, and to read it back to you if you ever forgot. That was the whole trade. Memory for memory. Nothing bought, nothing sold, nothing owed.

It sounded small. It took Sebastian months to understand that it was the most radical thing the guild had ever done, because every institution he had ever known ran on the principle that a person could be separated from their own story and the story traded separately. His entire life had been proof. A hedge-witch had sold his soul before he could write. An Undercourt had held it in a vault for thirty years. He had grown up meticulous, invisible, keeping his own name off every page because every page belonged to someone else.

Now there was a page that belonged to whoever wrote on it.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the burning—or the freeing; the clerks argued about which word to use, and eventually settled on both, entered twice—he sat down at the long table with the great open book in front of him and did the thing he had spent his whole life avoiding.

He picked up the pen.

His hand shook. Not from fear, exactly. From the strangeness of it—the muscle-memory of thirty years of careful erasure fighting against a single deliberate act. Around him the barn was quiet. Rain on the roof. Odile humming something low in the loft, not a song so much as the shape of one. Maren sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, because Maren sharpened knives the way other people prayed.

*Sebastian Sterling,* he wrote.

And then, beneath it, because the rule was the rule:

*I was sold before I could speak, and I hid for thirty years, and I burned a god-court to get my name back, and I am giving it here, freely, to people who will keep it honestly. I am the clerk of my own account now. Let this page be read back to me on any day I forget what I paid and what it was worth.*

The ink dried. Nothing happened. No thunder, no rewriting, no ward flaring, no god stirring beneath any mountain. That was the miracle, he realized—the absence of consequence. For the first time in his life, writing his name changed nothing except the world's knowledge of him, and the world could be trusted with that, because the world was eleven clerks and a singer and a dragon-keeper and a barn full of hay rot and old incense.

Maren leaned over his shoulder. "Sterling," she said. "Never asked. Is it your real name?"

"It's the real one now."

"That's the right answer." She straightened up, took the pen, and wrote *Maren, keeper of Vaskalorth, who carried the fire down and brought the light back up,* and her handwriting was terrible and she didn't care, and Sebastian watched her and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since he was six years old.

* * *

That night, alone, he lit a candle.

It was a stub of yellow wax he'd carried out of the mountain in his coat pocket, taken from the Undercourt's collateral vaults, from the shelf where his mistress's soul had been held for thirty years in a jar labeled in her own handwriting. The gods were dead or sleeping or simply elsewhere—the clerks still argued—but the jars had opened when the Court fell, and what came out of them had gone wherever souls go when no one owns them anymore. Hers had gone without waiting. Without lingering. Without, he sometimes thought bitterly, so much as a note left behind.

But she had left notes. That was the thing he'd finally understood, reading her confession in the vault by stolen lamplight, fury and tenderness tangled so tightly he couldn't tell which was which. She had sold him to buy him safety. She had let him hate her for thirty years because hatred was armor, and armor was safer than grief, and grief was safer than hope, and hope was the one thing the gods could use. Every cold lesson, every withheld kindness, every year of silence after she walked out of his apprenticeship and out of his life—it had all been the same bargain, struck over and over: *let him be angry, let him hide, let him survive.* She had spent her soul the way she'd spent everything, on a boy who would never thank her, and she had done it knowing that.

He set the candle stub on the windowsill, lit it, and watched the flame stand up straight in the draft.

"For Wrenna," he said aloud, because her name deserved air. "Who bought me time with everything she had, and never told me the price, and never once lied about anything that mattered."

The candle burned down while he watched. When it went out, the smoke rose straight for a moment, and then bent toward the mountain, and then dispersed, and he chose—deliberately, the way you choose to believe in mercy—to take it as a signature.

* * *

Spring came to the valley. The barn got a proper door. Children from the village started leaving notes pinned to it—*please remember my grandmother liked bees*—and the clerks entered them, gravely, in the back pages, because the House of Kept Names did not distinguish between large memories and small ones. Odile began teaching again, not god-songs but plain singing, voices raised in a room with terrible acoustics because terrible acoustics meant everyone heard everyone. And on the mountain above them, the pilgrims stopped coming. Whatever had stirred beneath the stone had settled, starved of bargains, starved of prayers priced in blood. Maren hiked up once a month to sit in the cold cavern where Vaskalorth had burned, and she reported each time that the dark there was only dark now. Ordinary dark. The kind you bring a lantern to.

On a bright morning in the second spring, a stranger climbed the east road to the barn. Young, travel-worn, carrying a satchel of bottled memories she'd inherited from a hedge-witch who had died owing debts to no one—which was, the stranger said, unusual in the trade, and worth asking about.

She knocked. Odile answered. And the stranger looked at the great open book on the table, at the names standing in it freely given, and asked the question that Sebastian had been waiting his whole life for someone to ask:

"How do I start a page?"

Sebastian slid the pen across the table.

"You begin," he said, "by telling the truth."
