## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink froze before it dried.

Ivo Winterbourne had learned to work with the cold the way other people learned to work with weather—accepting it, adjusting to it, never quite forgiving it. In the guildhouse at Ashmoor, a converted granary whose walls still smelled faintly of rye and rat, the winter accounts were kept in a room above the stables where a brazier could be coaxed without setting the thatch alight. Ivo sat wrapped in two cloaks and a blanket that had once belonged to a man who traded his own reflection for passage out of a debtors' war, and they wrote with quick, economical strokes, dipping the pen fast, before the well could skin over.

Outside, Ashmoor lay under its first real snow. The town sat on the border the way a coin sits on an edge—half in one country's habit, half in another's neglect—and the Guild of Impossible Things had kept a house here for sixty years precisely because no one looked twice at a border town. Maps showed a river bend and a toll road. They did not show the guildhouse, because the guild paid cartographers to forget it, and because forgetting was the one trade Cael Sarn's neighbors had never lost their appetite for.

Ivo turned the page.

*Entry forty-one,* they wrote. *Winter quarter. To Maren Ostler, of the tannery row: one shadow, stolen fair from a moneylender in Corvess, delivered intact and weeping at the edges. Received in exchange: three months' lodging, a promise of silence, and one jar of pickled quince.*

The shadow had been difficult. Shadows always were. It had sulked in its crate for the whole journey north, pooling against the slats like spilled night, and when Maren had finally unbound it in her cellar it had clung to her ankles with the desperate gratitude of a thing returned to its owner after too long among strangers. Moneylenders' shadows were the worst. They learned greed.

*Entry forty-two.* The pen scratched. *To the widow Ansel, of the mill road: one year, borrowed against her own old age, to be repaid with interest when her grandson comes home from the sea. Received: nothing yet. Noted as a gift, pending.*

Borrowed years were gentler work than shadows but heavier in the hand. You could feel them through the ledger paper sometimes—a warmth, a pulse, like holding a bird. Ivo had recorded eleven of them this season. The guild did not lend years often; Serah Quill called it "spending the future to buy the present," and she said it the way she said most true things, flatly, daring anyone to argue.

Ivo blew on the page. The ink held.

There was comfort in this—the only kind Ivo trusted. Numbers did not lie, and if they lied, they lied in ways you could catch. Every impossible thing the guild bought or sold or smuggled across a border that itself was half a rumor went into these books, and because the books existed, the guild existed, and because the guild existed, the people it sheltered existed: the deserter with a god's name stitched into his skin, the woman who had been erased from three census rolls and one marriage, the child who remembered a country that officially had never been. Forty years ago Cael Sarn had been dissolved—not conquered, not destroyed, *dissolved*, the way a debt is dissolved, by decree—and everyone in it had been told, gently and then not gently, to stop being from somewhere.

The guild was where they stopped pretending. And the ledgers were the proof that pretending had happened at all.

Ivo finished entry forty-three—a haunted bell, sold to a monastery that wanted to be haunted—and set down the pen to warm their fingers over the brazier. The coals ticked. Somewhere below, Caspian Ondu was arguing with the stable cat about a fish head; his voice carried up through the floorboards in low, patient Ondu cadences, the way water talks to a hull.

It was almost peaceful. That should have been the first warning.

* * *

The ruined shrine stood behind the tannery row, which was why no one visited it anymore except Ivo, and why even Ivo came rarely. It had been a shrine to Veth once—one of hundreds scattered through the old country, small stone alcoves where travelers had whispered debts and grievances to a god who never answered, because Veth was the silent god, the god who listened and kept what he heard. When Cael Sarn was dissolved, the shrines were supposed to have been pulled down. This one had simply been left to fall, which amounted to the same thing, slowly.

Ivo went there on the last evening of the audit, out of habit more than devotion. Habit was the closest thing Ivo had to prayer.

Snow had drifted against the shrine's broken arch. Inside the alcove, the stone basin meant for offerings was full of grey ice, and the carving above it—a closed eye, worn nearly smooth—had filled with white so that it looked, briefly, like an open one. Ivo brushed the snow away with a bare hand. The stone was cold enough to burn.

They hadn't meant to speak. But the day's entries were still in them, all those small debts, and something about the frozen basin and the blind carved eye loosened the words.

"Nothing new to report," Ivo said quietly. It was what they always said. A joke, once, between themself and a god who couldn't hear. "Same debts. Same debtors. The widow Ansel's year is still outstanding. Maren's shadow is settling in well." A pause. The snow made the world soft, distant, like sound heard through a wall. "You'd have liked the bell."

And the voice said:

"You would have liked the chapel, little Wren."

Ivo did not move. Later, going over it, they would catalog every detail with ledger-precision: the quality of the light, unchanged; the snow, still falling; the absence of footprints other than their own. There was no one behind them. There was no one anywhere. The voice had come from inside the stone—from the basin, from the ice, from the worn-smooth eye—as if the shrine itself had cleared its throat after forty years of silence.

"Who—" Ivo began, and stopped, because the question that mattered wasn't *who*. The question that mattered was the name.

*Wren.* No living person knew that name. Ivo had buried it deeper than the guild buried its houses. It belonged to a kitchen with a copper kettle and a mother who sang while she worked, to a chapel that burned, to a childhood that had ended the way Cael Sarn ended—by decree, dissolved, everyone instructed to stop being from somewhere. Ivo had been nine years old. Afterward there had been an oath, and an orphanage, and eventually a ledger, and the girl or boy or whoever little Wren had been was folded away so thoroughly that Ivo themselves could barely find the seams.

No living person knew it. Which meant—

"I'm dead," the voice said, and it sounded almost amused, almost gentle, the way an old friend sounds when they've caught you in a lie you told as children. "Or near enough. Dead gods don't answer prayers, Ivo Winterbourne. Everyone knows that. So either I am not dead, or you are not praying."

The ice in the basin cracked. A single line, straight down the middle, like a seam being opened.

"Your accounts are in order," the voice said. "All but one. There is a debt on your family's line that has waited forty years for payment, and the waiting is over. The collectors are coming, little Wren. For the guild. For the ledgers. For you."

Ivo's breath hung in the air, a small ghost that faded and left nothing behind. Their hands, they noticed distantly, had gone very steady—the steadiness of someone gripping a rail in high wind. "We don't owe anything," they said. "I keep the books. I would know."

"You keep the books of the guild," the voice said. "I keep the books of everything else. Ask your Guildmaster what she has not told you. Ask her what the Unmapped Room is mapped *unto*." The crack in the ice widened, and beneath it, impossibly, Ivo heard water moving—deep water, patient water, though Ashmoor had no river deep enough to drown a cup. "Come find me where the capital went under. Bring the heir's key. And bring—"

The voice stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-word, the way a door stops a sentence, and the silence that rushed in behind it was so total that Ivo heard their own heartbeat stumble. The snow went on falling. The eye above the basin stayed open now, snowless, watching.

Ivo stood in the ruin for a long time, long enough for the cold to reach the marrow, and then walked back through the blue dusk to the guildhouse without once looking behind them—which took discipline, because every instinct they owned was screaming to look.

Inside, the brazier had burned low. The ledger lay open on the desk where they'd left it.

Ivo froze in the doorway.

The page had changed. Entry forty-one, the stolen shadow, was exactly as written. Entry forty-two, the borrowed year, untouched. But below entry forty-three, in a hand that was Ivo's own and yet not—slightly older, slightly surer, the handwriting of someone who had been keeping accounts far longer than twenty years—new lines had appeared:

*Entry forty-four. To Veth, silent god, from the house of Winterbourne: one covenant, sworn and unpaid. Forty years' interest accrued. Collector dispatched.*

And beneath that, smaller, in letters that seemed to sink into the paper as Ivo read them:

*Payment due at the Door.*

Ivo stood with the pen in their hand and did not write anything, because there was nothing in the guild's tradecraft for this—no procedure for a ledger that kept itself, no rate of exchange for a childhood name spoken aloud by stone. Outside, the snow kept falling on Ashmoor, on the tannery row, on the ruined shrine with its open eye, and somewhere south, in a country that was not supposed to exist anymore, bells that had been silent for forty years began, one by one, to ring.
