## 5. Collectors

They came at the hour when the mist off the fens is thickest, and they came without horses, which was the first wrong thing Ivo noticed. The road into Hollowmere had been mud and rut for three days' walking; no one arrived there on foot unless they wanted to arrive quietly.

Ivo was in the chapel loft above Pell's sleeping cell, going over the notes from three days of questions, when the dogs in the village began to howl—not in alarm but in something worse, a low communal keening, as if they were mourning something that hadn't happened yet. Then the bells in the empty tower rang once, all together, though no rope had been pulled, and stopped mid-peal like a breath held too long.

Ivo went to the slatted window and looked down into the churchyard.

There were five of them standing among the leaning gravestones, arranged with the terrible neatness of inked figures on a survey chart. Grey coats, grey gloves, faces so unremarkable that Ivo's eyes slid off them twice before managing to hold them—the way one's gaze slides off a blank margin. Each carried a leather case strapped across the chest, and each case bore a stamp Ivo knew from guild briefings but had never seen in the flesh: a compass rose with its north point struck through.

The Cartographic Office. The people who unmapped the world.

Below, in the vestry, Pell was lighting the morning candle to Veth, as he did every morning, as he had done every morning for forty years in a country that officially did not exist. Ivo saw the flame gutter as the five figures crossed the yard in perfect step, saw Pell turn, saw the old priest's face go through surprise and then—horribly—through recognition, and then through something that looked almost like relief.

*They've come at last,* that face said. *I always knew someone would.*

"No," Ivo breathed against the window slats. "No, you fool, run."

But Pell set down the taper, straightened his stole, and opened the vestry door to them like a host greeting guests.

* * *

What followed, Ivo watched the way one watches a drowning: wholly, uselessly, memorizing everything.

The Collectors did not break down doors. They did not need to. The eldest of them—a woman whose coat was cut a half-inch finer than the others', whose case was bound in black rather than brown—stepped into the vestry and produced a document. Even at this distance, through old glass and fog, Ivo could see it was beautiful: heavy paper, copperplate script, an official seal that seemed to shift when looked at directly, the way water shifts over stones.

Pell read it. His shoulders shook—not with fear, Ivo realized, but with laughter, or something adjacent to laughter.

"You can't arrest a man in a country that doesn't exist," he said, loud enough for Ivo to catch the words. "That's the whole point of what you did. You made us *nowhere*. There's no law here. There's no jurisdiction."

The woman said something brief. Her voice carried strangely, flatly, like a voice heard through a wall.

"Ah," Pell said. "Yes. But you see, that's the flaw in your arithmetic." He raised his hands, and the candleflame behind him stood up tall and white. "A place erased still has to be *kept* erased. Every day. Forever. That's not nothing. That's work. And work—" his voice cracked open with forty years of it, "—work is prayer. You've been praying to Cael Sarn every day of your lives, gentlemen, madam. You just call it filing."

One of the younger Collectors moved then—fast, economical—and there was a sound like a page being torn out of a very large book. The candle went out. The light in the vestry changed, becoming thinner, grayer, the light of a place being *edited*.

And Pell—Pell did not scream. He said something in Old Sarnic, four syllables, a blessing or a name, and then the Collectors closed around him like a ledger closing, and where five figures had stood in the churchyard there were now six, and the sixth walked between the others with his head bowed, not struggling, and Ivo understood with a sick lurch that to the Office this was not an abduction. It was a *correction*. A marginal note being removed.

The bells rang again as they left—all of them, every tower in Hollowmere, a great dissonant peal that no hand pulled—and then stopped forever, and Ivo felt the silence where the sound had been the way one feels a tooth pulled from the jaw.

* * *

Ivo did not follow them. That was the discipline, the first law of guild tradecraft, beaten into every apprentice before they were taught anything else: *the witness who follows becomes the second prisoner.* Instead Ivo did the harder thing. Ivo sat down in the hay of the loft, shook until the shaking was spent, and then thought.

The Collectors had come for Pell because Pell was loud—a priest praying openly in a resurrected village, a beacon burning in a country the Office had spent forty years darkening. But Ivo was louder, in a way the Office could measure. Ivo was a ledger-keeper whose ledgers were annotating themselves. If the Office could hear prayers being answered, it could certainly hear debts being written.

Which meant Ivo was already in their files. Somewhere in some grey archive there was a card with a struck-through compass rose on it, and on that card, in copperplate: *Winterbourne, I. Guild of Impossible Things. Covenant-line. Observe. Retrieve.*

*Covenant-line.* The word from Pell's scripture, surfacing now like a drowned body. The Office would want a covenant-holder even more than it wanted a priest—because if the Door of Unnaming existed, only a covenant-holder could open it, and the Office's whole purpose, its whole *bureaucratic soul*, was to make sure that door stayed shut. Erasure was their sacrament. An open door was heresy.

So: they would come for Ivo next. Not today—the Collectors moved like weather, slow and total—but soon, and from more than one direction.

Ivo counted assets the way Serah had taught counting: coldly, honestly, without flattery. One: knowledge of the fen paths, which appeared on no map living or dead. Two: the guild sign worked into the buckle of the left boot—worthless against the Office, who knew all the guild's signs, but useful against everyone else. Three: a head full of things the Office wanted, which was either an asset or a death sentence depending entirely on who reached it first.

Four: nothing else. No army. No god willing to speak. A silent god and a burning country and a road south toward Ashmoor that might already be unwriting itself.

Fear sat in Ivo's chest like swallowed riverwater. And beneath the fear, rising slowly through it like a bubble through deep water, something harder. Resolve. Because Pell had gone out of that vestry with his head bowed and a blessing on his lips, and the blessing had been for *Ivo*—Ivo knew it, felt it in the marrow, four syllables in Old Sarnic that meant roughly *may you be remembered by what remembers you*—and Ivo would be damned by every dead god in Cael Sarn if that blessing was going to be wasted on a coward hiding in a hayloft.

* * *

Ivo left Hollowmere by the corpse-road, the old funeral track that ran along the fen's spine, because corpse-roads predate maps and resent being mapped; even the Office's instruments slid off them. All that day Ivo walked through a country remembering itself, and the remembering was accelerating. Hedgerows stood where hedgerows hadn't been last week, grown decades tall overnight. A milestone surfaced from the turf bearing distances to cities that were underwater. Twice, Ivo passed villages that had been ruins in the guild's oldest records and were now merely poor—roofs mended, smoke rising, children staring at the stranger on the corpse-road with the frank suspicion of people who have recently become possible again.

In one such village, an old woman pressed bread into Ivo's hands and asked, "Are you from the Office?"

"No," Ivo said. "I'm running from them."

"Then eat fast," she said, "and walk faster. They took the priest from Hollowmere yesterday. They took the miller's daughter from Corndy the day before—she'd been healed of the wasting sickness, you see. They take anyone the gods have *touched*." She lowered her voice. "My grandmother told me about them, before the Dissolution. She said they don't kill you. They put you in a file. And a file is worse than a grave, because a grave lets you rest."

It was near dusk when Ivo found what the old woman's warning had been building toward: a Collector, alone.

He stood at a crossroads ahead, consulting a case-bound instrument—a brass theodolite mounted on a tripod, its lens pointed not at the land but *into* it, as though surveying the ground underneath the ground. He was young, or young-faced, and he worked with the absorbed patience of a clerk balancing columns.

Ivo should have circled wide and gone on. Guild tradecraft said so. But the theodolite was aimed at the corpse-road itself—at the exact stretch of it Ivo needed—and as Ivo watched, the young man made a small notation, and the road *dimmed*. Not vanished. Dimmed, like a lamp turned down, like a word going faint on aging paper. In an hour that stretch would be gone, and with it Ivo's fastest path south, and perhaps with it the memory of every foot that had ever walked it.

So Ivo did the second-hardest thing in tradecraft, the thing taught after the first law: *if you must engage a stronger enemy, engage him as a problem, not as a fight.*

Ivo walked out onto the crossroads openly, hands visible, wearing the expression of a minor official having a bad day.

"Surveyor," Ivo said, in the flat bored tone of bureaucracy addressing bureaucracy. "Third District reassessment. Why is my route being amended without a countersignature?"

The young Collector looked up, and for a moment his face did what all their faces did—slid, blurred, refused to hold. Then it settled, puzzled. "Countersignature?"

"Every amendment requires one. Section Twelve of the Standard Procedures." Ivo had never seen the Office's procedures. But every bureaucracy in the world was the same animal, and guild work meant impersonating customs men, tax assessors, plague inspectors, and once, memorably, a bishop. Bureaucrats trusted procedure the way priests trusted liturgy. "You're erasing a classified route. If it's in your schedule, show me the schedule. If it isn't, you're in violation, and *your* name goes in the report, not mine."

The young man hesitated. And in that hesitation Ivo saw it—the crack every functionary carries, the small terror of having done something unsanctioned. He fumbled his case open, produced a folio, began flipping pages with suddenly unsteady fingers, checking whether he needed a countersignature, whether he had ever needed one, whether Section Twelve existed—

And while he checked, Ivo stepped past him, laid two fingers on the theodolite's brass frame, and did the smallest possible piece of guild work: a hairline fracture in the spirit of the instrument, invisible, patient, the kind of sabotage that takes three days to surface. By the time the road finished dimming, the machine would be blamed, not the route.

"File it under pending review," Ivo said, already walking. "You'll hear from the District."

Behind Ivo, pages rustled in panic. Ahead, the corpse-road ran south through the gathering dark, faint but holding, faint but *there*.

* * *

Ivo walked through the night, and somewhere past midnight, sheltering in the shell of a tollhouse with a fire no larger than a cupped hand, the pieces finally assembled themselves into the shape they had been trying to become all day.

The Office was hunting the resurgent Cael Sarn—that was certain. They were collecting the touched, the healed, the prayed-for—that was certain. But collection wasn't the purpose. Purpose was never collection; purpose was always *containment*. And there was exactly one thing in all the world that could contain a country coming back from erasure, one mechanism, one door.

"They're going to Vael Corin," Ivo said aloud to the fire, and the fire leaned as if listening. "They're going to close it."

The Door of Unnaming, beneath the drowned capital. The Office wanted it sealed forever—which meant the Office knew where it was, which meant the Office had known for forty years, keeping it on no map precisely so no one else could ever find what they guarded. And the same knowledge lived now in Ivo's own skull, planted there by Serah Quill and a self-annotating ledger and a dead god's covenant written into a family name.

Two powers, racing for the same door. One wanted to walk through it. One wanted to brick it up behind the world.

And between them, walking south through the night with blistered feet and a stolen hour of advantage, one ledger-keeper who was beginning to understand that they were not the hunter in this story, nor yet the hunted, but the *key*—and that keys do not get to choose which lock they are turned in, unless they move first.

Ivo fed the fire one more stick, watched the flames stand up tall and white the way Pell's candle had stood, and made the decision the way debts are entered in a ledger: plainly, permanently, in ink.

South, then. To Ashmoor. To warn the guild.

And if the Collectors followed—they would find that the Guild of Impossible Things had survived forty years of being nowhere, and had learned, in that time, a great many ways to make trouble for the people who kept the maps.
