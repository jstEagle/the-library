## 6. The Drowned Capital

Serah Quill had kept the guild alive for thirty years by knowing, before anyone else did, when a thing was about to stop existing.

She felt it now in her teeth, the way she felt weather coming off the moors. Ashmoor was going out. Not burning, not falling—going *out*, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. She stood at the window of the Unmapped Room and watched it happen to the street below: the cobbler's shop across the lane flickering, its sign reading first COOPER & SONS, then nothing at all, then a blank stretch of plaster that no one walking past seemed able to look at. A woman stepped around the doorway as though it were a wall. She had done it every day of her life, Serah supposed, without ever once seeing a door there.

The Cartographic Office had come to Ashmoor.

Not with soldiers. With clerks. That was worse. She could see them from here if she leaned—a grey carriage drawn by grey horses parked at the corner of Tanner's Row, and two figures in dust-colored coats taking measurements of a street that was, even now, agreeing to be less than it had been. One of them held an instrument like a sextant crossed with a pair of scissors. Every time he raised it to his eye, something small died. A chimney. A cat. The name of a cat.

"Guildmaster." Tamsin Hale, their forger, stood in the doorway with ink on her knuckles and fear she was trying very hard to file away somewhere orderly. "The eastern quarter's gone. Not destroyed—gone. I walked down Well Lane this morning and I couldn't tell you what's on either side of it. My feet remember the walk. My head won't hold the houses."

"And the ledgers?"

"That's why I came for you." Tamsin's voice dropped. "They've started writing themselves again. But not debts this time. Directions."

* * *

Ivo Winterbourne rode into Ashmoor at dusk, and the town did not know them.

That was the first horror. They had lived in Ashmoor four winters. The stable boy who watered their horse looked through them twice before Ivo pressed a coin into his palm and watched recognition arrive late and grudging, like a guest who'd forgotten the address. At the guild's front door—the door on Fetter Street, which existed only to people who owed something—Ivo had to knock three times before the wood consented to be a door.

Inside, the Unmapped Room smelled of tallow and panic. Half the guild was there: Tamsin with her ruined hands, old Merrick the fence, the Ondu twins back early from a salvage run, salt still crusted in their hair. And Serah Quill at the head of the table, older suddenly, as though the town's unwriting were taking years off the ledger of her face.

"You went into Cael Sarn," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The village of Lowmere. A dead god healed a dying child there. The roads are coming back, Serah. The bells ring in empty towers. The land remembers itself, and every answered prayer names someone we've sheltered." Ivo set their satchel on the table. "And the Office is here. I passed their carriage on Tanner's Row."

"We know." Serah's jaw tightened. "They arrived three days ago. They're not hunting us yet. They're *surveying*. Which is how they hunt things they intend to erase completely." She pushed a ledger across the table toward Ivo. "Yours."

Ivo opened it and felt the floor tilt.

Their own handwriting—there was no mistaking the narrow, patient script they'd kept accounts in for twenty years—had filled pages Ivo had never written. Not trades. Not debts. A map, drawn in words:

*Vael Corin lies beneath the water. The Door stands under the drowned cathedral of Saint Vessia, where the choir stalls still stand though the singers do not. Only the covenant-holder may open it. Only the covenant-holder may pay.*

"I didn't write this," Ivo said.

"No," said Serah. "You didn't. Veth did. Or the country did. Or whatever is left of the thing your family bound themselves to four hundred years ago." She looked at Ivo then with an expression Ivo had never seen on her—not the cool arithmetic of the Guildmaster, but something rawer. Regret, maybe. Or the shape regret makes when it has been rehearsed too long. "Sit down, Winterbourne. There's more, and you're going to hate me for all of it."

* * *

She told it plainly, because Serah Quill had never learned any other way to say a cruel thing.

The guild had known about the Door of Unnaming for longer than Ivo had been alive. It was in the founding records—in the papers old Mother Grieve had carried out of Vael Corin the year before the flood, the year before Cael Sarn was dissolved and struck from every map in the world. The guild itself had been built on that knowledge. Its whole trade in impossible things, its whole survival in the gaps of the world, rested on one certainty: that somewhere beneath the drowned capital there was a door that could take a person out of every record ever kept. Insurance. Escape. The last resort of the last resort.

"And you never told me," Ivo said.

"You weren't ready."

"I've kept your books for twenty years, Serah. I've buried three members and laundered the money for two funerals apiece. When exactly was I going to be *ready*?"

"When the ledgers told us who you were." Serah didn't flinch. "Mother Grieve's records say the Door answers only to a holder of Veth's covenant. The Winterbournes held it. We checked, over the years. Quietly. There were two children of that line left in the world, and we found where both of them had gone." Her voice gentled by a degree, which from Serah was nearly an apology. "One of them took holy orders and died in a fire at a chapel in the hills. The other signed on with a mercenary company and then, eventually, with us."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, faintly, a bell rang in a tower Ivo was no longer certain Ashmoor had.

"So the prayers," Ivo said slowly. "Every prayer that gets answered, naming our people—that's not the god hunting us. It's the god calling in a debt. Through me. Because I'm the collateral."

"It would seem so."

"And the Office wants the Door closed forever. If they reach it before I do—"

"They'll erase Vael Corin so thoroughly that even the water will forget it drowned anything. And everything the Door might have freed goes with it. Every erased place. Every erased person." Serah folded her hands. "Including, I suspect, everyone the old gods have named. The Office doesn't distinguish between a resurgent country and the people it's coming back inside of."

Merrick spoke from the shadows near the hearth, his voice dry as old paper. "Then send a party. Send the twins—they know Vael Corin's canals better than the fish do. Send half the guild."

"No," said Serah.

The word fell like a coin into a well.

"The Door opens for one," she said. "The records are clear. A covenant-holder, alone, or not at all. Anyone else who touches the threshold becomes part of the payment." She turned back to Ivo, and now she did flinch, just slightly, just enough for Ivo to see the cost of what she was asking written plainly on her. "I'm asking you to go alone, Winterbourne. Down into the water, into a city that's been dead forty years, past the Office, past whatever Veth has become down there in the dark. Alone. Because if you don't, everything we've built—every person this guild has ever made unfindable—gets found."

"And if I refuse?" Ivo asked. "If I just walk away? Take my chances outside the guild?"

Serah held their gaze. "Then I hope you're faster than the Office's clerks. And I hope the god doesn't mind being owed."

It was betrayal wearing duty's coat, and they both knew it. Ivo thought of twenty years of ledgers, of the guild's quiet mercy, of every soul Serah had sheltered—and thought also of Pell, the priest who never stopped praying, rotting in some Office cell if he still lived at all. Thought of a mother's hymn they couldn't quite remember, sung in a chapel that burned.

"You planned this," Ivo said quietly. "From the beginning. You took me in because of whose blood I carry."

"I took you in because you were starving and good with numbers," Serah said. "What I *planned* came later. That's the difference between mercy and strategy, Winterbourne, and I've never pretended to be a merciful woman." She rose. "The twins will take you as far as the coast. Caspian Ondu knows the flooded streets—he dove the cathedrals before the Office made it illegal. He'll get you to Saint Vessia's. After that, you go down alone."

Caspian Ondu lifted his head from where he'd been studying the self-written map. Salt-haired, broad-shouldered, with the calm of a man who had made peace with drowning long ago. "High tide's at midnight," he said. "We can be at the water by dawn."

Ivo looked down at the ledger, at their own handwriting saying things they had never written, and understood at last that they had never been the author of their own account. Someone else had opened the book. Someone else had written the first line. And now the whole balance was coming due.

"Pack light," Caspian said, and almost smiled. "The dead city doesn't like luggage."

Outside the window, the cobbler's shop finished forgetting itself. The street where Ivo had bought bread four winters running dimmed like a lamp going out—and in the space where it had been, faint as breath on glass, Ivo saw water. Dark water, moving.

Vael Corin, calling ahead of them.
