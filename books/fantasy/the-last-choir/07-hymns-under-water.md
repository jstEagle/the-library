## 7. Hymns Under Water

The boat that took us out of Ashmoor was called the *Patient Widow*, and she rode low in the water like something that had already given up. Caspian Ondu stood at her stern with his arms crossed over his chest, watching the drowned city rise out of the morning fog the way other men watch a lover's window.

"You'll want to breathe wrong at first," he said. "Everyone does. The water down there is old. It remembers being rained on by a country that doesn't exist, and it resents strangers."

"I've been resented before," I said. "By better things than weather."

He laughed, short and rough, and spat over the side. He was a broad man, soft-spoken, with the calm of someone who had made his peace with drowning as a profession. His guild mark was inked not on his skin but on his gear—a small impossible thing, a key drawn inside a circle, stitched into the shoulder of his diving coat. He'd been sent by Serah Quill, which meant he was trustworthy in every way that could be measured, and dangerous in all the ways that couldn't.

"Serah says you're the covenant-holder," he said, not looking at me. "Says the Door won't open for anyone else."

"Serah says a great many things."

"She also says you argue." He checked the valve on his air tank with thick, careful fingers. "So let's not. The city's down forty fathoms, and the singing starts at thirty. Past that, I can't promise what you'll hear or whether it'll be true."

"What will we hear?"

Caspian looked at me then, and there was something almost gentle in his face, the way there is gentle in the faces of men who work funerals.

"The choir," he said. "Vael Corin never stopped singing. That's why they drowned it."

* * *

We went down at noon, when the light came through the water in long grey columns, like the pillars of a church that had been knocked flat and laid end to end across the sea.

The first fathoms were only cold. Then salt. Then a pressure that settled on my chest like a hand—patient, enormous, waiting to be asked something.

At twenty fathoms the fish changed. They began to move in patterns too deliberate to be feeding, circling us in slow processions, and I understood that we were being watched, or escorted, or counted. At twenty-five, the first rooftops rose out of the murk: slate and copper gone green with centuries, chimneys standing like grave markers, a weathervane still turning though there was no wind down here, only current, only memory moving in slow tides.

At thirty fathoms, the singing started.

It did not come from anywhere. It came from everywhere—the water itself, vibrating against my mask, humming up through my bones. It was not one voice but many, layered and braided, rising and falling in harmonies that no living throat could hold. It sounded the way light looks through stained glass. It sounded like a hymn sung in a language I had never learned and somehow knew.

I stopped swimming.

Caspian's hand closed on my arm—not pulling, just anchoring. He tapped twice on his own chest: *keep going*. But his eyes behind the glass were wide, and I knew that even after years of diving here, the choir still reached him. It reaches everyone. It is simply that most people are not carrying what I was carrying.

Because I knew this song.

Not the words. Not the melody, exactly. Something underneath both—the shape of it, the way grief has a shape, the way a room you grew up in has a shape that your feet remember even when your mind has burned the map. Somewhere under forty years of ledgers and oaths and careful, careful forgetting, some part of me was singing along.

We descended past a bell tower lying on its side, its bell mouth open toward the surface as if shouting upward. We passed a market square where the stalls had become reef, anemones blooming from awnings like strange lit candles. And always the singing wound around us, closer now, more distinct—I could pick out voices within it, if voices is the word. One sang low and steady, like a mother at a cradle. One rang bright and sharp as struck bronze. And beneath them all, so deep I felt it rather than heard it, was a silence shaped exactly like a voice. A silence with intention. A silence that was listening.

Veth.

The silent god. The god of the empty shrine where a whisper had been answered aloud. The god whose debts were writing themselves into my ledgers in a hand I recognized and refused to name.

We swam down the nave of a cathedral whose roof had folded open like hands unclasping, letting the grey light pour through onto the altar below. Fish hung in the transepts like thoughts suspended. Along the walls, in stone worn smooth but not smooth enough to erase them, ran carvings: figures kneeling, figures singing, figures holding up a child toward something vast and patient.

I looked at the child in the carving and my chest went tight inside the diving coat.

The child was holding a ledger.

* * *

Below the cathedral, the water changed.

I don't know how else to say it. It thickened, darkened, took on weight—not the weight of depth but the weight of attention. The choir fell away voice by voice until only that listening silence remained, and it pressed against me from all sides, gentle and total, the way snow presses on a roof.

Caspian stopped at a doorway carved into the bedrock itself, a threshold older than the city above it. He turned to me and made a gesture I knew from guild tradecraft: two fingers to the eyes, then outward. *Watch.* Then a fist against his heart. *I'll wait.*

He would go no further. Later, he told me, no diver ever had. The door was not for him. It had never been for anyone but—

But I'm ahead of myself.

I went through alone.

Beyond the threshold was a chamber that the water did not seem to touch. Oh, it was flooded—everything here was flooded—but the water hung motionless, suspended like held breath, and in the center of the chamber stood a single chair of black stone, facing away from me, and on the chair sat a figure made of the space where sound should be.

I knew it was Veth the way you know your own name in a dream: without evidence, without doubt.

*I have kept your account,* it did not say. The words arrived not in my ears but in my memory, already lived, already remembered, as though they had happened years ago and I was only now catching up. *Forty years. You balance beautifully. You were taught well.*

"Taught by whom?" My voice came out strange in the still water, flattened, private.

And Veth answered the only way a silent god can answer.

With memory.

* * *

It did not flood me. That's the word everyone would reach for, but it isn't right. Flood implies violence. This was an opening, slow as dawn, of doors inside me that I had nailed shut so long ago I'd forgotten the nails were mine.

A chapel. White stone, whitewashed inside, smelling of tallow and cut grass. Morning. I am small. My hands are small. There is a hymn being sung above me and I know every word because I have known nothing else. My mother's voice is in the singing—no. Not my mother. A woman who is singing *as if* she is my mother, and the difference matters, though I don't yet know why.

The chapel burns.

I am outside in the cold, wrapped in a blanket that smells of smoke, and men in the grey coats of the Cartographic Office are walking the perimeter with instruments that hum. They are erasing the chapel even as it burns—unwriting the ground it stood on, the road that led to it, the parish records, the births. There is a list. My name is on the list. Not *Ivo*. The other name. The childhood name. The one the answering voice spoke at the ruined shrine in Ashmoor, the one no living person should know.

And a woman kneels in front of me in the smoke. Not the woman who sang. Another woman—tall, tired, wearing the travelling coat of the Guild of Impossible Things, with ash on her cheek and tears cutting clean lines through it. She holds my face in her hands.

"They will erase you," she says. "Unless something older than their instruments claims you first. Forgive me. Forgive me, little one. This is the only door left."

She carries me into the ruined shrine—to Veth's shrine, the empty one, the silent god's—and she sets me down before the altar, and she speaks the words of covenant in a voice that shakes.

But they are not her words to speak.

They are mine. They were always meant to be mine. The covenant of Winterbourne and Veth, sworn generations back, renewed each generation by the heir's own voice, freely given—and I am the heir, and I am four years old, or five, and I am sobbing, and I do not understand, and the woman says the words *for* me, in my place, with my name in her mouth.

And Veth—who cannot lie, who cannot even speak—accepts.

The oath takes root in me like a hook taking root in a fish. Everything that follows unfurls from it: the forgetting, arranged between the woman and the god so that I would not grow up grieving; the guild, which sheltered me as it shelters all things unwritten; the ledgers, which I took up because balancing accounts was the one prayer my hands remembered how to make.

I never chose Veth.

Someone chose for me. Someone loved me enough—or feared enough, or grieved enough—to sell my voice before I knew I had one, and call it salvation, and it *was* salvation, and it was theft, and both of those things are true forever, in the same breath, the way a hymn is joy and sorrow sung at once.

The memory closed like water closing over a dropped coin.

I knelt on the floor of the drowned chamber, in the held breath of the sea, and I wept into my mask for a childhood that had been taken twice—once by fire, once by mercy—and for the self I had built on top of the wound without ever knowing it was a wound. Every choice I had called my own. Every careful line in every ledger. All of it built on ground someone else had signed away.

*You are angry,* Veth did not say.

"No," I said, and found it was true. "I'm *accounted to*. There's a difference. Anger would mean I thought I'd been cheated of something that was mine."

*And was it not?*

"The oath wasn't mine. But everything I made inside it was." I lifted my head. Through the mask, through the still water, I looked at the figure of listening silence on its black stone chair. "You've been patient forty years. Be patient a little longer. Take me to the Door."

The silence moved—or seemed to—and the chamber's far wall, which had been wall, became corridor, which became stair, descending into dark where the water finally, gently, let go of me entirely.

And far below, faint as a pulse, I saw it: a seam of light in the bedrock, drawn straight as a ruled line.

The Door of Unnaming.

Waiting for its debtor.
