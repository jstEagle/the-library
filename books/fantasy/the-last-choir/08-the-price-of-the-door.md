## 8. The Price of the Door

The stair went down for a long time.

Not long in the way of distance—I have walked a day and a night without resting, and I know what long is—but long in the way of a held breath. Each step landed in silence so complete I could hear my own pulse knocking at my ears, could hear the water of Vael Corin far above, pressing down through stone like a congregation leaning forward in its pews. The light of the Door rose to meet me as I descended, that ruled seam of pale gold widening by increments, until it was not a seam but a line, and not a line but the edge of something.

The stair ended at a threshold of black basalt, worn smooth as a tongue. Beyond it lay a chamber no architect had made. The walls were bedrock, yes, but they had been *addressed*—the way one addresses a letter—and the rock had answered by becoming legible. Faint script ran through the stone in veins, older than any alphabet I knew, older perhaps than alphabets. It did not spell words. It spelled *names*, I understood, though I could read none of them. Every name that had ever been unmade came here to be filed.

And set into the far wall, floor to ceiling, stood the Door.

It was not grand. That was the first cruelty of it. After the drowned cathedrals, after Veth's hall of listening dark, I had expected something monumental—bronze and bone and burning script. Instead it was a door such as might open onto a modest house in a modest town: oak gone silver with age, iron hinges green with verdigris, a latch worn bright by hands. Only the line of light around its edges betrayed it, gold as a ledger rule, precise as an accountant's pen. Through the gap beneath it I could see nothing—not dark, not water, not stone. Nothing, in the way that a blank page is nothing: full of everything that has not yet been written.

I stopped three paces from it. Some instinct older than my oath told me not to touch it yet.

"You've come."

The voice did not come from the Door. It came from everywhere the silence wasn't—from the script in the walls, from the water in my boots, from the hollow behind my ribs where forty years of quiet prayer had made a room. Veth did not appear. Veth had never appeared. But the chamber filled with attention the way a bell fills with sound before it rings.

"I've come," I said. My voice sounded small and human and absurdly loud. "What does it cost?"

I am a keeper of accounts. I have spent my life learning that every door has a price, and that the honest ones tell you before you knock. So I asked as I would ask of any creditor: plainly, with my hand ready for the blow.

The answer came not as words but as understanding, which is how gods speak when they wish to be cruel or kind and cannot always tell the difference.

*Yourself.*

"No," I said. "Be specific. I deal in specifics."

And the god, patient beyond all patience, was specific.

It showed me. Not told—showed, pressing the knowledge into me the way a seal presses into wax. The Door of Unnaming opens only for a covenant-holder; that much Serah had known, that much Pell's broken scripture had promised. But opening is not passing through. To pass through is to be unwritten—to step out of every record, every memory, every mind that ever held you. And the toll, the price chalked against the account, was this: the covenant-holder must surrender the whole of their memory of self. Every recollection in which Ivo Winterbourne appeared. Every face of their own remembered face. The mother's hymn. The burning chapel. The first time they held a ledger and understood that numbers could be a kind of mercy. All of it, paid out through the Door like coin through a grate, until on the far side there would stand a person with skills and habits and no idea whose they were.

Free, yes. Nameless, yes. But also *no one*—a shape walking elsewhere with someone else's handwriting all over it.

"That's not freedom," I whispered. "That's just erasure with better manners than the Office's."

*Is it?* The attention shifted, almost curious. *The Cartographic Office erases you and keeps the map. The Door erases you and gives you the territory. Choose your thief.*

I laughed—or made a sound like laughing, thin and cracked in that sealed place. Forty years I had served a silent god, and now that it finally spoke, it spoke like a guildsman haggling.

"There's another way." I turned from the Door, addressing the whole listening dark. "You offered once already, in the cathedral above. You said you would pay the debt yourself. A god's payment." I made myself say it. "Say what it costs."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls, the old names seemed to lean closer.

And Veth showed me that, too.

A god's memory of self is not one life. It is a country's worth of lives—the prayers of everyone who ever knelt, the harvest-blessings, the funeral rites, the small desperate bargains struck over cradles and deathbeds across four hundred years of Cael Sarn. For Veth to pay my debt, Veth would have to unmake all of it: pour itself through the Door, name by name, prayer by prayer, until the god was spent and the land that remembered itself—the roads reappearing, the bells ringing in empty towers, the dead child healed in a village that officially did not exist—all of it would fold back into blankness. Cael Sarn erased a second time. Not by bureaucrats this time. By mercy. By a god paying a mercenary's bill.

Pell's face rose in me unbidden—that stubborn, sun-cracked priest who had prayed into forty years of silence because someone had to keep the account open. He had given his whole life to the return of his country. And I could spend it in an afternoon to save my own skin.

"No," I said.

*You have not heard the terms fully—*

"I heard them perfectly. You'd erase everything Pell prayed for. Everything those people died believing in. You'd make him a fool retroactively." My hands were shaking. I looked at them and let them shake. "I've kept ledgers for impossible things for twenty years, Veth. I know a fraudulent entry when I'm asked to sign one. The debt is mine. It stays mine."

The attention withdrew a little—not offended, I thought, but *moved*, if a silence can be moved. In the walls, the ancient names glowed faintly, like embers under ash.

And then the water above us shivered.

I felt it before I heard it—a pressure change, a wrongness in the deep, the particular displacement of many bodies entering flooded streets in formation. I had felt that rhythm before, in Ashmoor, when the Cartographic Office came with their instruments and their blank grey coats: surveyors' boots, marching in time, each step a small act of cartography, measuring the world toward its disappearance.

They had found Vael Corin.

"They're here," I breathed. "How—they'd need the drowned city charted, they'd need—"

*They followed you,* Veth said, without accusation, which was worse. *Or they followed the Door. It does not matter. They are coming, and they bring their blanks with them.*

I could hear it now through the stone: a low hum, the sound of erasure working, the same note I'd heard as Ashmoor's streets unwrote themselves. Above me, cathedrals were being un-sung. The old gods' music, the hymns under the water, were going quiet quarter by quarter, like candles pinched out by a careful hand.

And there was nowhere to go. Behind me, the Door. Before me, the stair, and rising up the stair, the Office.

I drew my knife—habit, uselessness—and turned to face the corridor.

They came down in file, six of them, lamps hooded grey, their faces the faces of clerks: tired, competent, incurious. At their center walked a woman with a ledger of her own, bound in white, and when she saw me she did not draw a weapon. She simply opened her book and began to write, and where her pen moved, the script in the chamber walls began to fade.

"Step away from the Door," she said. "In the name of the Surveyor-General. This anomaly will be closed. You will be—" she consulted her page, "—adjusted."

I did not step away. I did the arithmetic instead, fast and cold, the way Serah taught me: six agents, one knife, one god who could not act without unmaking a nation, one Door that cost everything I was. There was no column in which I survived this. There was barely a column in which I *died* usefully.

Then the water behind the agents caught fire with lamplight, and someone shouted, and the file broke.

He came down the stair like a man descending into his own funeral and refusing to be solemn about it—robes streaming, salt-crusted, one eye swollen half-shut, hands still bound at the wrist with surveyor's cord he had clearly not waited politely to be freed from. Pell. Alive. Freed—by whom, from where, I would never learn—and armed with nothing but a broken-off piece of brass instrument casing gripped in both bound hands like a censer.

"Pell—"

"Ivo!" His grin was terrible and glorious. "You're *alive*. The god found me, did I tell you? Forty years of silence and then it *found* me—" He saw the Door. He saw the white ledger. He understood, because priests understand sacrifice the way I understand interest, instantly and without flinching. "Go," he said. "Open it. Whatever it costs—it's yours to pay, heir of Winterbourne. That's the whole of the covenant. Someone has to hold the account, and it was always going to be you."

"It costs everything I remember of myself—"

"Good," he said fiercely. "Memory was never the point. *Faithfulness* was." And he turned his back on me, on the Door, on his own rescue, and raised the brass shard against six grey coats and their writing woman, and began—in a cracked, joyful, drowning-man's voice—to sing.

The hymn of Veth. The one my mother sang. The one I had spent forty years pretending I didn't know.

The agents hesitated. Erasure, I realized, requires the thing erased to hold still. A singing priest does not hold still. Pell met the first of them with the brass shard and his shoulder and his whole furious body, and they went down together in a tangle of grey and salt-white robes, and the second agent raised something that gleamed, and I screamed and lunged and was caught—held—pinned by arms that wrote as they gripped, letters crawling up my sleeves—

"VETH!" I roared into the dark. "Don't you dare pay for me! Don't you dare erase them! Do you hear me? The debt is MINE—"

And in the last of my sight, Pell looked back at me over a clerk's shoulder, blood in his smile, and mouthed the words of the final verse. *Account received. Account closed.* Then the white ledger's pen came down, and the world went narrow, and grey, and quiet—

—and the Door, unopened, unlatched, untouched, swung inward on its own worn hinges, spilling light like a ruled line drawn through the dark, waiting for me to choose what I would pay.

* * *

I stood at the threshold with agents' hands on my arms and Pell's song dying in the air and the blank page of elsewhere breathing cold against my face.

Everything I remembered of myself. Or a country. Or nothing at all—if I could find a fourth entry no god and no Office had thought to forbid.

Somewhere behind me, faintly, the hymn began again.
