## 1. The Weight of Other People's Yesterdays

The rain in Vael never fell clean. It came down through the smoke of a thousand chimneys and the ash of the tanneries across the river, and by the time it reached the cobbles of Sallow Lane it tasted of copper and old secrets. Edmund Winterbourne stood beneath a sagging awning that smelled of rotting canvas and watched the woman cross the street toward him, and he thought — as he thought every time — that this would be the last one. That after tonight he would find some other trade. Something clean.

He had thought it forty-one times before. He kept count.

Maren Ostrell was a cloth merchant with a shop on Cordwainer's Row and a husband dead eleven months and a grief so heavy she could no longer stand upright beneath it. She had come to him through the usual channels — a whispered name passed from a midwife to an apothecary to a boy who sold information outside the Temple of Small Mercies — and she had paid half up front without haggling, which told Edmund everything about how far she had already fallen. Desperate people bargained. The truly broken simply opened their purses.

She stopped under the awning beside him, close enough that he could smell the lavender she'd dabbed over something less pleasant underneath. Fear-sweat, mostly. They were always afraid, right up until the moment they weren't.

"You're him," she said. Not a question. Her eyes stayed fixed on the rain, on the gutter-water running black between the cobbles, anywhere but his face. "The taker."

"I prefer *practitioner*," Edmund said mildly. "Taker sounds like I do it without consent."

"Don't make jokes." Her voice cracked on the word and she pressed her lips together, hard, a woman holding shut a door against a flood. "Please. Just — do it. Before I lose my nerve."

"Madam." He turned to face her fully, and let his voice drop into the register he'd learned long ago, the one that sounded like warm oil poured over a wound. "What we discussed. One memory. Your choosing. Lifted whole and clean, no fragments left behind, no damage to what surrounds it. You will remember that your husband lived. You will remember loving him. What you will not remember is the night he died, or the year since — not the shape of it, not the weight. The hole will be smooth as river stone. In time you'll forget there was ever a hole at all."

"And you keep it." She said it like an accusation, because it was one. "You walk away with the worst day of my life in your head. Or in — whatever you keep them in."

"In glass," Edmund said. "In glass, and then in someone else's keeping entirely. I don't linger in my own inventory, madam. It's bad for the constitution."

It was true, more or less, and it was also the most comfortable lie he owned. He did linger. That was the trade's dirty arithmetic: every memory he lifted had to pass *through* him first, had to live behind his eyes for the hour or the day it took to decant it properly into a vial. Forty-one extractions. Forty-one worst days worn like borrowed coats, each one leaving lint and threads behind in his seams. He carried a widow's vigil at a deathbed that wasn't his. A deserter's sprint through burning orchards. A child's last sight of a mother walking into the sea. They sat in him like stones in a riverbed, smoothed by nothing, and some nights they rose in his dreams wearing his own face.

But Maren Ostrell didn't need to know that. Nobody who paid him needed to know that.

"Here?" she asked, glancing up the lane. The street was empty except for a dray horse tied outside a shuttered pie shop and the usual Vael shadows doing their patient work along the walls.

"Here's fine. Rain covers a multitude." He drew off his right glove finger by finger, unhurried, letting her watch. Professionalism was theater; he had learned that early. A man who fumbled made clients bolt. A man who performed ritual made them grateful. His bare hand looked ordinary enough — long fingers, ink-stained cuticles, a scar across the knuckles from a knife fight he'd won by being luckier than brave. But when he turned the palm upward and spoke the two syllables of the taking-word, low and soft as a secret, the skin of his palm went silver, faint as moonlight through fog.

Maren stared at it. "Does it hurt?"

"The truth? No. That's the obscene part." He held out his hand, palm up, between them. "Grief is the easiest thing in the world to carry out of a person. It wants to go. Grief is loyal to nobody but itself. Now. Think of the night. Don't fight it. Let it rise."

She took his hand. Hers was cold and chapped from years of handling bolts of wool, and it trembled against his like a bird deciding whether to trust the air.

And Edmund closed his eyes and *took*.

---

It always began the same way: a sensation of falling upward. The memory came off her in layers, the way wet silk peels from skin. First the small things, the furniture of the scene — a bedroom with a crack running along the ceiling plaster, a cup of willow-bark tea gone cold on the sill, rain against glass exactly like the rain falling now, as though all the rain in Vael were one continuous event eleven months long. Then the man. Aldous Ostrell, fifty-two, gray at the temples, laughing at something in a book before the coughing started. Then the coughing itself, weeks of it, rendered in the terrible high fidelity that only love can record: the sound his breathing made at three in the morning, the color his lips went, the exact weight of his hand going slack in hers — not heavy, that was the thing she could never forgive the world for, he didn't grow heavy, he grew *light*, as if dying were a kind of lifting-away —

Edmund breathed through it. He had learned to breathe through other people's deaths the way divers breathe through drowning.

Then the burial, and the empty house, and the eleven months compressed into a single gray pressure, and underneath it all, at the very bottom of the grief, the thing she hadn't told him about, the reason she'd finally scraped together his fee: a letter in Aldous's handwriting she'd found tucked in his desk a month after the funeral, unfinished, addressed to her, saying things she couldn't bear to read again and couldn't bear to have read only once.

He took all of it. The contract said one memory, and technically it was one memory — grief is greedy, it grows tendrils through everything adjacent — and he pulled it free in one long silver thread and felt it settle into him, cold and enormous, a glacier calving into his chest.

Maren swayed. He caught her elbow and steadied her, and when she opened her eyes they were wet but *clear*, clearer than they'd been all evening, and she looked at him with the polite blankness of a woman trying to recall why she was standing in the rain with a stranger.

"Forgive me," she said slowly. "Did we — were we meeting? I feel as though I've forgotten something."

"You dropped your glove," Edmund said, and handed her back her own hand's warmth by pressing her fingers briefly, kindly, and letting go. "Safe travels, madam. Mind the puddles."

She thanked him, puzzled and light — *lighter*, that was the word, she moved like a woman whose shoulders had been unburdened by invisible porters — and walked off up Sallow Lane with her step already quickening, already forgetting why it had ever been slow. By the corner she paused, frowned faintly at nothing, touched her breastbone as if checking for a wound that wasn't there anymore.

Then she turned the corner and was gone, and Edmund stood alone under the rotten awning with a dead man's deathbed sitting behind his ribs like swallowed ice, and he counted.

Forty-two.

"*Last one*," he murmured to the rain, and the rain, which had heard that particular lie from better men, went on falling.

---

Master Corvane's establishment occupied the fourth floor of a counting-house on Quill Street, above a moneylender and below a man who claimed — loudly, in pamphlets — to have invented a machine for turning seawater into wine. The stairs smelled of tallow and ambition. The door at the top was plain oak with a brass plate reading only *C., Antiquities & Curiosities*, which was rather like calling a viper a ribbon.

Corvane received him in a room lit green by shaded lamps, its walls hung floor to ceiling with glass. Hundreds of vials, thousands, racked in velvet-lined cabinets like a library of stolen souls — because that, Edmund reflected not for the first time, was precisely what it was. Some glowed faintly. Some were dark as tar. One, in a case apart from the others behind warded glass, burned a deep and restless blue that made Edmund's teeth ache when he looked at it too long. Corvane caught him looking, as Corvane always caught him looking, and smiled his collector's smile — the smile of a man who has never once paid a fair price and sleeps beautifully anyway.

"Winterbourne." Corvane did not rise from his desk. Rising was for men who needed to seem taller. "You look terrible."

"It's raining."

"It's always raining. It's Vael." Corvane extended one manicured hand, palm up, an echo of Edmund's own gesture an hour ago that he was certain was deliberate. "And? Was the merchant's worth the trouble?"

Edmund set the vial on the desk between them. Decanting had taken forty minutes in a rented room on Tanner's Walk — the memory flowing out of him down his arm into the prepared glass, silver threading into silver, until the vial held a complete widow's year of sorrow, stoppered and sealed and labeled in his neat hand: *Ostrell, M. Death of husband, with letter.* He felt lighter himself, now, the way he always did after decanting, and the lightness always disgusted him more than the carrying had. Relief at setting down another man's burden was just theft compounding interest.

Corvane held the vial to a lamp. The memory inside shifted like smoke with a grudge. "Oh," the collector said softly, and Edmund's stomach tightened, because he knew that softness. Softness in Corvane meant appraisal, and appraisal meant the number was about to be wrong in one direction or the other. "Oh, this is *fine*. The letter detail — you didn't mention a letter in your message."

"She found it after. It was tangled in the root. Came with the grief."

"Untangled?"

"Whole. Clean. It reads complete." He hesitated, and hated himself for hesitating, because hesitation was a tell and Corvane collected tells the way he collected everything else. "I read part of it. During decanting. You can't help it, some of it bleeds through the—"

"I know how your trade works, Winterbourne. I invented three quarters of its market." Corvane set the vial down and steepled his fingers. "A merchant's grief. Common stock, ordinarily. A hundred crowns on a generous day, and I'd have haggled you to eighty. But a grief with an unread letter in it — a grief with *words from the dead inside it* —" He clicked his tongue. "There are four people in this city who would pay five hundred for that without blinking, and one of them is me, and I am going to pay six."

Edmund blinked. "Six hundred."

"Six hundred crowns. Coin, not script." Corvane was already drawing a strongbox from beneath the desk, already counting, his rings clicking against the coins. "Because you're going to tell me, Winterbourne, over a drink I'm about to pour, where you found a client desperate enough and rich enough to sell something this raw. Because griefs like this cluster. Where there's one widow drowning in a house full of letters, there are others. And I—" he glanced up, green lamp-light making his face skull-like and pleasant, "—am feeling acquisitive."

Six hundred crowns. It was more than double his asking rate. It was a year of rented rooms and bad stew and gloves that didn't wear through at the palm. It was, he understood with a clarity that arrived cold as the vial had, the sound of a hook going into water near a fish that should have known better.

"That's very generous," Edmund said carefully.

"Generosity is just investment with better manners." Corvane slid the coins across the desk, then produced — casually, as an afterthought, which meant it was nothing of the kind — a second object. A leather map case, supple, travel-worn, its buckle worked in a style Edmund didn't recognize: interlocking thorns around a circle. "Tell me. In your line of work. Have you ever handled anything you couldn't lift?"

The question landed strangely, the way a key lands in a lock you didn't know you had. Edmund thought of the vials, the silver thread, the forty-two burdens. "Everything can be lifted," he said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Mm." Corvane smiled again, and this smile had weather in it, distance in it, something almost like longing. "Almost everything. There are things in this world that refuse to be taken, Winterbourne. Old things. Things that take *back*." He tapped the map case once, lightly, and pushed it out of sight beneath a ledger as though it had never been there. "Drink?"

Outside the window, far below on Quill Street, the rain went on falling, and somewhere down in that gray wash a woman named Maren Ostrell was walking home light as paper, and somewhere else a boy was telling a man in a doorway about a practitioner on Sallow Lane, and Edmund Winterbourne took the drink, because refusing a free drink from Master Corvane was the sort of thing that got a man remembered, and being remembered in Vael was worse than being robbed.

He drank. He pocketed six hundred crowns that weighed too much. He told himself, forty-three times' worth of conviction, that next week he would find another trade.

He was still telling himself that, three days later, when the bleeding stranger broke down his door and died on his floor with a name in his mouth that Edmund had spent twenty years paying other people to make him forget:

*"Thornfell keeps."*
