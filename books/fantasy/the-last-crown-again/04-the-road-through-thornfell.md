## 4. The Road Through Thornfell

The map did not want to be folded.

Edmund discovered this on the second morning out of Vael, when he tried to tuck it back into its leather case and the vellum resisted his hands — not stiffly, as old parchment does, but with a kind of muscular reluctance, like a sleeping animal being turned over. He had learned not to touch it more than necessary. His fingers ached faintly whenever they lingered on its surface, the way a tooth aches before it decides to become a problem.

He rode north through country that grew steeper and poorer by the hour, trading the fat river valleys for scrubland where the pines came down to the road like an audience waiting for something to happen. The imperial checkpoints began at Marrow Ford. He'd expected them. What he hadn't expected was how many there were, or how far from the capital the empire's grey reached.

At the third checkpoint he paid a toll he couldn't afford and let a bored sergeant read his forged papers twice. The man's eyes were flat as coins. Behind him, two soldiers were questioning a carter about a courier — *a man in a brown coat, riding hard, three days ahead of you* — and Edmund understood that the story of the dying stranger had traveled faster than he had.

*Thornfell keeps.*

The words still surfaced at odd moments, usually just as he was falling asleep, arriving with the intimacy of a voice in the next room. He had tried, twice, in the privacy of roadside inns, to do what he did best: press his memory-sense against the map and take whatever it held. Both times it had been like pressing his palm against a river. Something moved underneath, vast and cold, and none of it came away in his hands.

For thirty years, Edmund Winterbourne had never met a mind he could not open. Now the first thing to refuse him was a piece of paper.

* * *

The pass called the Shepherd's Throat was the only route the map's first marker allowed, and it was also the one road in all the borderlands that Edmund knew without knowing why he knew it.

That was the trouble with an erased childhood. It didn't leave you empty. It left you furnished with things whose provenance you couldn't account for — a fear of deep water, a fondness for honeyed barley bread, and now, apparently, an intimate knowledge of a mountain track he had no memory of ever walking.

He knew, for instance, that the Throat narrowed to a hand's width of stone above a drop of four hundred feet, and that the safe path hugged the left wall where the rock overhung, though no sign marked this and every instinct argued for the right. He knew that the third switchback hid a spring behind a curtain of moss, sweet water, drinkable even in high summer. He knew that if you whistled a certain rising phrase at the cliff face near the summit — he caught himself actually doing it once, three notes, up-up-down, absurd — the wind coming off the peak would answer with something almost like harmony.

He stopped whistling so abruptly that his horse shied.

*Who taught me that?*

There was no answer. There was never any answer. There was only the strange, sour feeling of being a house where someone else had arranged the furniture — everything in its place, nothing bearing his fingerprints.

He had built a life on other people's yesterdays. He knew precisely what a stolen memory felt like from the outside: the smooth place where a grief had been lifted, the scar-tissue shine of a mind healed too quickly. He had never once considered what it looked like from the inside. Now he was living in one, and it was worse than he'd imagined. Worse because it wasn't absence. Absence would have been clean. This was presence without explanation — knowledge like teeth marks in an apple, evidence of someone who had been here before him and eaten his life down to the core.

*Someone took my childhood,* he thought, riding into the shadow of the pass. *And whoever they were, they were thorough. And thorough people are afraid of something.*

The thought kept him company for the rest of the day, and it was not comforting company.

* * *

He came down out of the Throat at dusk on the fourth day, into a valley the map said was called Hollowmere, and smelled the burning before he saw anything else.

Not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke has bread in it, resin, the ordinary sweetness of hearths. This was older and uglier — thatch gone to char, grain stores, animal hair. A village burned thoroughly and then left to cool.

Ashfall drifted on the wind like dirty snow. It settled on his horse's mane, on his sleeves, in the creases of his knuckles, and he found himself wiping his hands on his coat again and again without deciding to.

Hollowmere sat in the crook of a stream, twenty-odd houses around a well, and it had not simply burned. It had been *emptied*. Doors stood open. A cradle lay in the road, unburned, tipped on its side. In the smithy, the forge was cold but the tools were gone — not looted, he realized after a moment, but *taken*, carried off deliberately, along with every blade in the village. Someone had wanted these people disarmed before they died, or wanted their weapons for themselves.

And someone had wanted them dead at all.

Edmund walked the street slowly, leading his horse, and the professional part of his mind — the part he hated and relied on — went to work reading the scene the way he read faces across a buyer's table.

The burn patterns were wrong for accident and wrong for panic. Fires set in rows, methodically, starting at the valley mouth and working uphill. The villagers had run toward the mountain, not away from it; the deepest trample-marks in the mud led up the eastern slope toward a goat track. Toward Thornfell. They had known where safety was supposed to lie, and they had died reaching for it.

Here and there, half-buried in ash, were small things the fire had spared: a clay whistle shaped like a bird. A child's boot. A wedding ring fused to a scrap of bone.

Edmund crouched beside the ring for a long time.

He had taken memories like this from people. Not many — he told himself not many — but some. A widow who wanted the drowning gone. A father who paid double to forget which of his children he'd failed to save. He had always told himself it was mercy, that he was a surgeon of pain, that the collectors who bought those yesterdays were harmless eccentrics paying for secondhand sorrow the way rich men paid for paintings.

Standing in Hollowmere, he understood for the first time that a memory is not a painting. It is a room. Take the grief out of a grieving woman and you have not healed her; you have left her standing in a stripped room, touching walls, wondering why she is standing there. He had done that. He had done it for coin, and the coin had bought him this horse, this coat, this road north into a valley full of ash.

"Gods," he said quietly, to no one. "What am I?"

His voice sounded obscene in the silence. He made himself stand, made himself keep moving, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant sitting down in the ashes and not getting up.

* * *

It was near full dark when he found the first grave.

It was fresh — days old, not weeks — a mound at the treeline above the village, marked with a rowan branch tied with white cloth. Then a second grave beside it. Then a dozen. Then, at the edge of the clearing, a long trench with a plank at its head bearing a single burned-in word:

*HOLLOWMERE. REMEMBER US OR AVENGE US.*

Someone had buried these people *after* the soldiers left. Someone had come back.

Edmund's skin prickled. He scanned the treeline, the dark ranks of pine, the pale scars of birch. Nothing moved. But the graves told him what the village itself could not: the empire had not come here for taxes or reprisal or the ordinary cruelties of occupation. They had come asking questions. And when the answers weren't good enough, they had burned the questioners.

Which meant the questions were about Thornfell. Which meant he was walking, alone and conspicuous, along the exact path everyone in this war was already traveling.

He was turning back toward his horse when he saw the light.

A thread of it, deep in the trees upslope — not firelight, exactly. Too steady. Too green. He should have mounted and ridden on. Every sensible instinct said ride on. But the light sat in a hollow directly beside the goat track the villagers had died running toward, and the map's first marker, he was almost certain, lay beyond the ridge above it.

He tethered the horse and climbed.

* * *

The hollow held a shrine.

That was the only word for it. A ring of stones around a spring that glowed faintly green — mountain magic, the old kind, the kind the empire had spent three centuries stamping out and never quite managed to kill. Offerings littered the ground around it: bread going hard, a lock of hair, a soldier's badge pried off a grey uniform and nailed to a tree as a warning or a trophy.

And kneeling at the spring's edge, so still she might have been another stone, was a girl.

She was perhaps nineteen, thin as a winter branch, dressed in men's clothes cut down and patched with sacking. Her hair had been shaved close on one side and hung ragged past her jaw on the other, and across her cheekbone ran a burn scar, glossy and new. She held a bow across her knees with the ease of someone who had recently needed it often. She had not turned around. She had not moved at all.

But she said, in a voice scraped raw by smoke: "You're standing on Joss's grave."

Edmund froze. He looked down. Beneath his boot, half-hidden by needles, lay a small marker stone, scratched with letters.

"I'm sorry." He stepped back carefully. "I didn't— I found the village. I saw the trench. I didn't know anyone had—" He stopped. Every sentence he started sounded like a lie, because every sentence he might honestly say sounded worse. *I steal memories for a living. I'm following a map I can't read to a crown I don't believe in.* 

The girl rose. She turned. Her eyes were the color of wet slate and they went through him like a spear through wet canvas — cataloguing his coat, his boots, his city softness, his horse's expensive tack visible downslope through the trees.

"You're not imperial," she said. "They don't apologize." A pause. "You're not Choir either. We bury our own facing the peak." Another pause, longer, and her hand drifted to the bow. "So what are you?"

"A traveler," Edmund said.

"No one travels this road now." She took a step toward him, and the green light of the spring moved on her scarred face like water. "No one comes through Hollowmere unless they're looking for something in the mountains. The greys came looking three days ago. They asked my mother where the Crown slept." Her voice did not break. That was somehow the worst thing about it — the absolute flat control, grief compressed until it could cut. "My mother told them she'd never heard of any Crown. My mother was telling the truth. They burned her anyway."

Edmund's mouth was dry. "I'm sorry," he said again, uselessly.

"Stop saying that." She raised the bow now, unhurried, and put an arrow to the string with the same terrible calm. "Tell me what you're carrying instead. Everyone who comes through here is carrying something. Papers. Gold. Orders." Her eyes dropped to the leather case at his hip, and he watched her read it the way he read other people — instantly, totally, the way the desperate learn to. "Or maps."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere below, his horse shifted and stamped.

"I could tell you it's nothing," Edmund said. "You wouldn't believe me. I could tell you it's mine, and that would be true, in the sense that a man gave it to me while he was dying and I've been running with it ever since." He held her gaze. "I could give it to you. If you tell me what it leads to."

Something flickered in the girl's face — not softening, nothing so gentle, but recognition. The look of someone seeing a piece of a pattern she'd been staring at too long.

"Dying," she repeated. "Brown coat? Rode through here six nights ago with imperial riders a mile behind him?" Her breath came out slow. "We pulled an arrow out of him. He wouldn't stay. Said the Choir had to know before the greys closed the passes." The arrowhead lowered an inch. "He said a name before he went. Mother Ivenna sent runners after him. They found blood on the road south and no body."

"Thornfell keeps," Edmund said quietly.

The girl went very still.

"That's what he whispered to me," Edmund said. "I don't know what it means. I don't know what's on this map — I've tried, and it won't let me see. But the empire burned your village hunting it, and a collector in Vael offered me more money than I've earned in five years just for the chance to bid on it, and I think—" He heard his own voice steady in a way that surprised him. "I think whatever it is, it's worth more than all of us. And I think I'm being hunted by people who burn villages, and I can't outrun them alone."

The girl studied him for a long moment. The spring glowed between them, green as new leaves, green as something growing in the dark.

"Sera," she said at last. Not a greeting. An offering, grudging as the bread on the stones. "Sera of the Ash Choir." The bow came down, but her hand stayed near the quiver. "You'll walk ahead of me to the hold, and if you make one move I don't like, I'll put an arrow in your spine and let Mother Ivenna decide what you were carrying."

"And if she decides it's worth killing me for?"

"Then you'll have died knowing something," Sera said, turning up the goat track toward the ridge, toward the mountains black against the stars. "Most people in Hollowmere didn't get that much."

Edmund looked back once at the glowing spring, at the little graves, at the burned-in plank with its two impossible demands — *remember us or avenge us* — and then he followed her up into the dark, carrying a map that refused to be read, toward the only people in the world who might understand what he'd become.
