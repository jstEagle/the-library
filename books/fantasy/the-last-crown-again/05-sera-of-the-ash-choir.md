## 5. Sera of the Ash Choir

Sera had learned to count in ashes.

Three days since the village of Hollowmere had stopped existing, and she could still count its bones from the ridge above: the black ribs of the meetinghouse roof, the collapsed spine of the mill, the scatter of smaller shapes that she refused, with a discipline that hurt worse than looking, to name as houses. Smoke still rose from the deepest of the ruins, thin and grey and patient, as though the fire were a living thing that had simply lain down to sleep among the wreckage. The snow on the upper slopes had melted back from the heat of it, leaving a bald scar on the mountain's shoulder visible for miles — visible, Sera thought bitterly, from anywhere at all. Whoever had burned Hollowmere had not cared who saw.

She crouched among the wind-stunted pines with her bow across her knees and watched the smuggler's road thread down through the pass below, and she counted other things instead: eleven arrows remaining, a knife, half a loaf of black bread gone hard as slate, and four days since she had slept more than an hour at a time.

Grief, Mother Ivenna had told her once, long before any of this, is a fire you either feed or put to work. Sera had not understood then. She understood now. Her grief had become a kind of engine, cold-burning and tireless, and it ran on a single fuel: *someone did this, and someone will answer for it.*

The Ash Choir had taught her how to make it sing.

* * *

They called themselves a choir because they had no weapons worth the name and no army worth the fear, only voices — watchers on the passes, runners between the holds, singers who moved through the high country humming the old Thornfell airs so that any loyalist within earshot would know friend from stranger. Sera had been sworn for two years, since the night she'd knelt in the crypt beneath the chapel at Hollowmere and Mother Ivenna had pressed ash to her forehead and said the words: *What burns may be rebuilt; what is forgotten is lost forever.* She had taken the vow thinking the empire would come someday, in some abstract future, the way one thinks of winter or death. She had not expected it to come on a Tuesday, in the morning, while she was hauling water.

She had been at the well when the riders came over the eastern rise. Imperial grey, twelve of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times and would do it many more. She had dropped the buckets and run, and the running had saved her life, and she had hated herself for every step of it — hated the way her legs had carried her up the sheep-track without asking her permission, hated that she had looked back exactly once and seen the first roof catch, hated most of all that in that single glance she had seen a figure in the square, small, wearing her neighbor Maren's yellow shawl, and had kept running anyway.

That was the arithmetic she lived by now. One glance. One shawl. Kept running.

So when the man came down the smuggler's road alone, walking with the careful economy of someone who knew these paths but wished he didn't, Sera did what the Choir had trained her to do: she went still, she went silent, and she waited to see who else followed him out of the trees.

* * *

He was not imperial. That much was plain even at distance. No grey, no armor, no soldier's rolling gait. He wore a dark travelling coat worn shiny at the elbows and a scarf pulled up against the mountain cold, and he moved like a city man doing his best impression of a countryman — watching his footing too carefully, flinching at the wrong sounds, ignoring the right ones. A raven called somewhere upslope and he didn't mark it; a branch cracked under his own boot and he froze like a startled deer. City, then. Vael, maybe, or one of the river towns. Soft hands, if the way he tucked them into his sleeves meant anything.

But soft men did not walk the smuggler's road to Hollowmere three days after it burned. Soft men did not come to the high passes at all, unless they were lost, and lost men did not move with this particular directionality — always angling downhill, always toward the ruined village, always toward the thing the Choir existed to guard.

Sera counted to two hundred after he passed beneath her stand of pines. No others emerged from the treeline. No second man lagging behind, no outriders on the ridges, no glint of grey among the birches. Either he was alone or his companions were better than imperial soldiers ever were, which was its own kind of answer.

She slipped down through the trees the way Ivenna had drilled into her — weight on the outside of the foot, breath timed to the wind, never crossing open ground where your shadow could betray you — and came out onto the road twenty paces behind him, arrow nocked, drawn to her cheek before her boots had finished finding their stance.

"Stop," she said.

He stopped. He did not spin, which surprised her; most men spun, presenting their backs or their bellies, all instinct and no sense. This one stood very still, shoulders dropping a deliberate inch, hands rising slowly away from his body with the studied calm of someone performing the idea of harmlessness rather than feeling it.

"I'm not armed," he said.

"Everyone is armed." She let the silence stretch. "Turn around. Slowly."

He turned, and she got her first proper look at him: younger than his voice, older than his face should have allowed. Dark hair going grey early at the temples, or perhaps dusted with the road. Eyes the colour of wet slate, and in them something she recognized with an unpleasant jolt — the flat watchfulness of a person accustomed to assessing exits. She had seen it in smugglers. She had seen it, once, in an imperial tax-assessor who had come to Hollowmere smiling and left with a ledger full of names.

"You're a long way from any city," she said.

"So are you." His gaze flicked — quickly, professionally — to the bow, to her, back to the bow. Calculating distances. She tightened her draw a fraction and saw him register it.

"That's not an answer," she said.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Below them, far down the valley, the smoke from Hollowmere bent eastward on the wind, and he followed its line with his eyes, and Sera saw him see it — saw the ruin register in his face, not with a soldier's satisfaction or an official's indifference but with something colder and more complicated. Recognition. The look of a man confirming an item on a list.

Her grief turned over in its sleep and opened one eye.

"Why did you come here?" she asked, and her voice came out lower than she intended, scraped raw at the bottom.

"I'm passing through."

"To a burned village. Alone. On a dead man's road." She took one step closer, keeping her feet on the stones where her own footing was sure. "The last person who passed through this valley 'passing through' carried imperial writs and a company of greycoats. So you'll understand why my hospitality has gotten short."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly, though there was some, banked and managed. Calculation, yes. But underneath both, briefly, something that looked almost like grief of his own, surfacing and shoved down again before she could be sure.

"I heard about the burning," he said quietly. "In the lowlands. They said it was bandits."

"They lied."

"They usually do." He swallowed. "Were there survivors?"

The question hit her sideways, in the place she kept the yellow shawl folded away, and for one dangerous heartbeat her aim wavered — not outward but inward, toward the memory, and she hated him a little for making her feel it, hated the gentleness in the question, because gentleness was worse than cruelty just now, gentleness got under the armor.

"Some," she said. "Not enough."

* * *

He tried to leave. Of course he did. She had known he would — had watched the calculation complete itself behind his eyes, weighing the odds of a dark-haired woman with a bow against whatever urgency drove him down the mountain. When he shifted his weight onto his back foot, she put an arrow into the path six inches from his boot so fast he flinched at the sound rather than the impact.

"The next one doesn't miss," she said. It was a lie. She was a good shot, not a perfect one, and at this range in this light she might as easily have put it through his throat as past his heel. But he didn't know that, and she watched him decide not to test it, and she filed the decision away: he valued his life highly, but not so highly that he panicked. He bargained with danger. That made him a merchant of some kind, or worse.

"Walking," he said carefully, "seems like a poor use of both our afternoons."

"Then talk fast."

"About what?"

"About why a city thief—" she let the word land, watched him absorb being read correctly, "—is carrying a map case inside his coat, left side, against the ribs, heavy enough that he keeps checking it with his elbow every time the ground gets steep."

He went very still again. Different stillness this time. The stillness of a man whose whole world has just narrowed to the size of a leather tube.

"I don't know what you—"

"Don't," Sera said, and her voice cracked on it despite everything, despite the discipline, because she was so tired, and the smoke was still rising down there, and Maren's shawl was still yellow in her memory, and she could not bear one more lie today, not even a competent one. "Please don't. I have spent three days pulling children out of ash and deciding which bodies go in which graves, and I am standing on a mountain holding a bow on a stranger, and the only reason I haven't loosed is that something in how you carry yourself says you're running *from* the people who did it. Give me one true thing. One. And I'll give you the benefit of whatever doubt I have left."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere below, the ruin smoked on, patient as a held breath.

He looked at her for a long moment — really looked, the professional assessment falling away layer by layer until what remained was just a hollow-eyed man on a cold road, holding himself together with habit and nothing else.

"If I show you," he said, "you'll want to take it. And if you take it, they'll take it from you, and they'll burn whatever's left of you the same way they burned that village. I'm not refusing you. I'm telling you what knowing it costs."

"We buried the cost three days ago," Sera said. "Show me."

A muscle worked in his jaw. Then, moving with exaggerated slowness, hands high and visible, he reached inside his coat and drew out the map case — leather, scarred, dark along one seam with a stain she recognized instantly and would know anywhere, the way any child of the high country knows blood dried into brown-black lacquer.

He held it out to her on his open palm. An offering. Or a trap. On this road, in this season, the two looked identical.

"Take it," he said. "Look. But understand — it wasn't given to me freely either. A dying man put it in my hands and died doing it, and imperial riders have been hunting me since, and I don't fully know what I'm carrying. I only know that everyone willing to kill for it believes it's real."

Sera did not lower her bow. Not entirely — she was sworn, and the Choir's first law was *never wholly disarm* — but she eased the tension in her draw and stepped forward, and she took the case from his palm without letting her eyes leave his face.

The leather was warm from his body. The bloodstain was old, days old, stiff at the edges. She turned the case over once, twice, and found what her fingers had already promised her they would find: pressed into the underside of the flap, faint, nearly worn away by handling, the sigil she had knelt beneath two years ago while Mother Ivenna's thumb drew ash across her brow.

A crown. A thorn-crown, circlet of briars, the old royal seal of Thornfell.

The mountain seemed to lean in around her. The smoke, the snow, the wind in the pines, the stranger with his raised hands and his tired liar's eyes — all of it receded, and what remained was the seal, and the words spoken over her in the crypt, and the shape of the duty she had sworn herself to.

*What burns may be rebuilt; what is forgotten is lost forever.*

"Where," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, distant and ringing, "did you get this?"

"A courier," Edmund Winterbourne said. "Dying. In my rooms, in Vael. He was followed." He hesitated, and she watched him choose, deliberately, to give her one more true thing. "His last words were *Thornfell keeps.*"

The words went through her like cold water through a crack in stone. *Thornfell keeps.* Not a sentence. A name. A place. The phrase every member of the Choir learned first and spoke last, the countersign that opened the hidden doors of the loyalist holds — and a stranger was saying it to her on the smuggler's road, saying it lightly, not knowing its weight, the way a man might handle a reliquary believing it a snuffbox.

She lowered her bow all the way. It felt like lowering a drawbridge.

"Sera," she said.

"What?"

"My name. You'll need it, if you're coming with me." She slung the bow across her back and tucked the map case inside her coat, against her own ribs, where it sat warm and heavy as a second heart. "There's a hold two valleys north. People who can tell you what you're carrying better than I can — and protect you from the ones hunting it, for a while, maybe. The road there is known to me and to no one else you're likely to meet." She turned and started up the track, then paused, not looking back. "I'm trusting you, city man. Understand what that costs me. My village burned three days ago, and I am choosing to believe you had no part in it, and if I learn otherwise, the mountain is large and full of quiet places."

Behind her, silence. Then footsteps, following. Cautious ones. A man walking into a bargain he didn't yet understand the terms of.

Sera climbed, and counted her blessings the way she counted everything now: one map, one stranger, one chance that the thing Maren and all the rest had burned for might still be kept. And ahead of them, two valleys north, hidden behind the waterfall at Greyhollow, the Ash Choir waited — Mother Ivenna waiting among them, who would need to hear everything, and decide everything, and whose eyes Sera dreaded seeing darken with the news she carried.

Because Sera had seen the sigil. And Sera had already begun, in the secret ledger of her heart, to write the only entry that mattered:

*It's real. And it's moving.*
