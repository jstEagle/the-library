## 6. The Choir's Bargain

They took him down into the mountain by way of a door that did not exist.

Edmund had lived in Vael long enough to know that every city kept its lies — the shuttered windows that were never shuttered at night, the cellars that went deeper than their deeds allowed. But Thornfell country lied differently. Here the lie was older than the stones themselves, and it was carved into the rock with the patience of someone who had expected to be forgotten and had built accordingly.

Sera walked ahead of him with a lantern hooded in oiled leather, its light leaking out in thin amber slivers. Behind him came two more of the Ash Choir, silent as ashfall, their grey cloaks dusted white with snow. None of them spoke. They had not spoken since the ambush on the ridge, since Sera had pressed her knife against his throat and then, seeing the map case against his chest, drawn back as if the leather itself had burned her.

The path descended through a crack in the mountainside so narrow that Edmund had to turn sideways, his shoulders scraping stone that wept cold water. The air changed as they went down — lost its bite of pine and snow and took on something mineral, something ancient, like the breath of a cellar that had been sealed since before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath.

Then the crack opened, and there was light.

Not lamplight. Not firelight. A pale glow rose from veins in the living rock itself, threading through the walls like frozen lightning, and by its strange luminescence Edmund saw that they stood in a cavern that had been made into a town. Stairways spiraled up into darkness where homes must be. Walkways of black timber crossed the space on iron chains. Smoke curled from vents cut high in the ceiling, and somewhere below, water moved with a sound like slow applause.

"The Hollow," Sera said, without turning. "Don't gawk. It makes you look like a spy."

"I am a spy," Edmund said. "Of a sort."

"Then gawk convincingly."

* * *

They fed him first. That was the part he distrusted most.

The hall they brought him to was low-ceilinged and warm, dug deep into the rock and lined with benches worn smooth by generations of sitting. They gave him barley soup with marrow fat floating on it in golden coins, dark bread, tea brewed from something sharp and resinous that burned pleasantly going down. He ate because refusing would have been more suspicious than eating, and because three days of smuggler roads had hollowed him out.

Around him, people watched. Not openly — these were mountain folk, and mountain folk had learned three centuries ago how to watch without seeming to. But he felt it all along his spine, the weight of eyes measuring him: the cut of his coat, too fine for a pilgrim; his hands, soft at the fingertips in the way of a man who worked with small things rather than heavy ones; the map case, which they had let him keep but which sat between them now on the bench like a third guest no one wanted to acknowledge.

A girl of perhaps ten years stared at him from across the hall until an older woman pulled her away. Edmund caught himself wondering what memories the child carried, what griefs or glories she'd trade someday in a Vael alley for coin enough to eat, and he hated himself for the thought. It was the merchant's reflex. Everything looked like inventory when you'd been in the trade long enough.

He set down his spoon. "Your Mother Ivenna," he said to Sera. "She'll want to see me before I've finished chewing, won't she."

Sera almost smiled. Almost. It flickered across her face like the hooded lantern's light through the door-crack and was gone. "You're learning the mountains faster than I expected."

"That wasn't flattery. That was indigestion."

"She'll see you when she sees you." Sera turned her bowl in her hands, and for a moment the vigilance dropped, and Edmund saw underneath it a tiredness so vast it seemed geological. "Eat your soup, memory-thief. Whatever she offers you, you'll want your strength."

* * *

Mother Ivenna received him in a chamber that had been a shrine once, and might have been again, depending on whom you asked.

The room was round, its walls hung with banners gone grey with age — the sigil of the stag-and-mountain repeated on each one, stitched in thread that had once been silver and gold. Candles burned in niches around the circumference, dozens of them, and their flames bent inward toward the center of the room though there was no draft. Edmund noticed that immediately. He noticed everything about rooms; noticing was how he survived them.

In the center, beneath a skylight of thick crystal through which no starlight fell, sat an old woman in robes the color of woodsmoke. Her hair was white as the snowfields above them and braided in a single rope over one shoulder. Her hands rested on the head of a walking staff of black ironwood, and her eyes — pale, nearly colorless, like frost on glass — found Edmund the moment he entered and did not release him.

"So," she said. Her voice was dry and unhurried, the voice of someone who had buried more people than she could count and had stopped keeping track. "The thief comes down from the world above. Sit, Edmund Winterbourne."

He did not sit. "I didn't give you my name."

"No. You gave it to a dead man's ears in Vael, and a dying man repeats what matters." She tilted her head. "You told the courier you could hide him. You said you knew roads the empire's maps don't chart. He believed you enough to spend his last strength reaching your door. That is either a testament to your skill or a condemnation of his judgment, and I have not yet decided which."

"You interrogated me already?"

"We interrogated the map." Her colorless eyes glittered. "It refused us. It refuses everyone, we are told, except the hand that carries it willingly — and even then only grudgingly, like a mule crossing a river." Something moved behind her face, quick and hungry and quickly mastered. "But you carry it. Three hundred miles of imperial patrols and smuggler cuts, and you carry it still, unopened by anyone but you."

"It doesn't open for anyone else," Edmund said. "I tried to show Sera. The seals won't break for another hand. And my own—" He hesitated. This was the part he had turned over and over during the climb, the part that frightened him more than Rell's riders, more than the burning village, more than anything. "My own touch slides off it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I steal memories for a living. Skin to paper, a heartbeat's contact, and I can lift what a person remembers of any document they've seen. I've copied royal seals that way. Bank ledgers. Confessions. I touched this map for the length of a prayer and got nothing. Not resistance. *Nothing.* As if it isn't written in ink at all. As if it's written in something my art can't grip."

"And that frightens you," Ivenna said.

"It should frighten you. Your whole order exists to guard that thing, and it cannot be guarded against me — or against anyone like me. If I can't read it, I can't protect it. I can only carry it. There's a difference."

"Yes," the old woman said softly. "There is. And that difference is precisely why we need you."

She rose then, slowly, leaning on the ironwood staff, and crossed to one of the grey banners. With one finger she traced the faded stag-and-mountain, and the candle flames all leaned a little further inward, as if listening.

"Do you know what the Ash Choir is, Edmund? Truly?" She did not wait for his answer. "We sing. That is all. Every dawn and every dusk, in this hollow and a dozen others scattered through the range, we sing the names of the dead kings of Thornfell — all nine hundred and forty-one of them, every name, in order, without end. We have sung them since the fall. Mothers teach daughters the verses before they teach them prayers. We sing so that the names do not die, because—" her finger stopped on the banner's heart— "because names are the oldest magic there is, boy, older than crowns, and the empire knows it. Why do you think they burned every record? Why do you think they salted the grave-fields? An empire can survive a rebellion. What it cannot survive is a *remembering.*"

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. He was thinking, absurdly, of his vials. Of the little glass phials in their velvet case, each one holding a stranger's yesterday, glowing faintly with borrowed color. He had always told himself memory was currency. He had never considered it might also be ammunition.

"You want the Crown," he said.

"I want what the Crown *is*." Ivenna turned to face him fully, and in the candlelight her frost-pale eyes held something that was not madness, which was somehow worse — it was patience, patience refined over a lifetime into something like steel. "The Last Crown does not make men strong. It makes men *whole*. It holds what was lost. When the last king broke it — when he hid it rather than let the emperor wear it — he did not merely conceal an object. He concealed the memory of an entire kingdom. Its laws. Its songs. Its borders. Its *name*, spoken properly, with the old vowels. All of it sleeps under this mountain, folded inside that circlet like a letter inside an envelope. Restore the Crown, restore the reading of it, and Thornfell rises again — not as ghosts. As a nation. As law and harvest and school and song. The villages that forgot themselves will remember. My people will stop singing to the dead"—her voice cracked, just barely, a fault-line in the stone of her— "and start singing to the living."

The candles guttered, all together, though again there was no wind.

Edmund heard himself say, carefully, "And the war."

"What war?"

"The one that ends when the Crown comes out of the ground. Empires don't surrender their provinces because a circlet hums. You put that thing on someone's head, and within a season there will be Grey Company columns in every pass, and within five years there will be mass graves, and within twenty there will be an Ash Choir singing *new* names. Nine hundred and forty-one will look like a rounding error."

For a long moment the old woman simply looked at him. Then, to his surprise, she nodded — slowly, gravely, as if he had passed some examination he hadn't known he was taking.

"You see clearly," she said. "Better than most of my own. Yes. There will be a price in blood. There is always a price in blood; the question is only who pays it and for what. The empire takes our children for its legions, our grain for its granaries, our language for its gutters, and calls the taking peace. I am offering the reverse of peace. I am offering the *bill*." She came closer, and her voice dropped to something intimate, terrible, reasonable. "I am an old woman, Edmund Winterbourne. I will not live to see the kingdom restored. I ask only to see it *begun*. To hear the Crown speak its first true word. Let the generations after me pay the butcher's bill — they will pay it gladly, for freedom, as ours paid it for survival. Is that so monstrous?"

"It's so *familiar*," Edmund said quietly.

Ivenna paused.

"In Vael," he said, "the collectors talk exactly like that. Not the small ones — the great ones. Master Corvane and his kind. They buy memories wholesale, entire lifetimes, and they speak of restoration. Of returning to people what was taken. Of making the past whole again." He met her frost-colored eyes without flinching. "What they mean is that they want to *own* it. The past is power, Mother. Whoever holds a people's remembering holds the people. You're not asking me to carry a map. You're asking me to carry a crown to your head, and you're dressing it as mercy."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled through fathoms of rock, the Choir had begun to sing — the dawn verse, hundreds of voices rising through the stone in threads, and Edmund felt the sound enter his chest and settle there like a splinter.

"You are not what I expected," Ivenna said at last. There was no anger in it. There was something almost like respect, and something almost like pity. "Good. The ones who come easily are the ones the empire breaks easily." She lifted her staff and gestured toward the chamber door, dismissal and summons both. "So here is the bargain, thief, plainly, since plainness seems to be your tongue. Carry the map to the vault beneath the Weeping Gate. Guard it with your life, which I understand you value highly. In exchange: my Choir shields you. Rell's riders will not find you while you walk under our protection. Food, road-knowledge, shelter in every hold from here to the peak. And when the Crown stands uncovered before you — *if* it accepts your hand, and the old texts say it answers to certain blood — then we will speak again about what it should be used for. You will have a voice in that council. Perhaps the loudest voice. Perhaps the deciding one."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave tonight, alone, with the map, and every rider in the Grey Company between you and the lowlands. We will not take it from you." Her lips thinned. "The Choir has rules older than the empire. The map goes freely given or not at all. But I will tell you what I tell my grandchildren, Edmund: freely given and cornered into giving are cousins, and the family resemblance is strong."

* * *

He walked the hollow afterward, because sleep would not come and because he did not trust himself to be still.

The place hummed with life in its crevices — children chasing one another along the chain-walkways, a smith's hammer ringing somewhere, old men playing a board game with pieces carved from antler. Ordinary life, dug into the dark like a root. He passed the shrine-hall where the dawn singers were finishing, and through the doorway he saw Sera among them, standing in the last row, her mouth moving with the others' though her eyes were open and fixed on nothing. Singing the dead kings' names. Singing her village into the list.

*Everyone here believes,* he thought. *That's what makes them dangerous.*

He had spent his whole adult life among people who believed nothing — collectors who bought sorrow by the ounce, clients who sold their weddings to pay their debts, fences who weighed grief on jeweler's scales. Believers unsettled him. Believers would do anything, and call it faith. Corvane believed in the perfect archive. Ivenna believed in resurrection. Both of them, he suspected, would burn the same number of villages to reach their altars.

And yet.

He climbed a stairway to a ledge overlooking the great cavern and took out the map case, and for the hundredth time ran his thumb along the seal that would not break for any hand but his. The leather was warm. It was always warm, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and he had begun to think of it the way one thinks of a debt collector waiting at the door — with dread dressed as indifference.

*Ally or cage,* he thought. *Corvane's offer or Ivenna's. The restored self or the restored kingdom. Everyone wants to give me back to myself so they can use what they find.*

Below, the Choir sang the final names of the dawn verse, and the candles in the shrine leaned inward, and the mountain held all of it — the living and the named dead and the sleeping thing beneath — in its long stone patience.

He was still standing there when Sera found him, her face tight, her hand resting on the knife at her belt in the way that meant bad news traveled fast in the Hollow.

"Mother Ivenna wants us both," she said. "Now. There's been word from the eastern scouts." A pause, and her voice dropped. "Grey riders. Moving fast. And Edmund — they're not searching anymore. Someone told them where we are."

* * *

Later, much later, when the hold was burning and Mother Ivenna's staff lay broken on the shrine floor, Edmund would remember this night as the last moment the bargain had seemed like a choice.
