## 7. Memories He Never Sold

Sleep did not come to the Ash Choir's hold so much as it ambushed him.

Edmund had meant to keep watch through the night — old habit, older fear — but the hold's air was warm with woodsmoke and pine resin, and the stone walls drank sound the way sand drank rain. Somewhere below, a Choir sister was singing in a low, cracked voice, a lullaby or a lament, he could not tell which; the melody kept folding back on itself like a river doubling home. He lay on a straw pallet in a cell that had probably been a monk's before the mountain forgot its monasteries, and he told himself he would rest his eyes for a moment only.

The dream took him before he finished the thought.

He was small. He knew it the way one knows things in dreams — not by seeing his own hands, though they were small and grubby and missing a fingernail where a door had caught them, but by the height of everything. The tower rose above him impossibly tall, its stones going up and up into a sky the color of hammered pewter, and he was standing at its base with his neck craned back and his heart hammering with a child's particular terror: the fear of being late.

*Run, little wren.*

The voice came from everywhere. A woman's voice, warm as bread left near a fire, frayed at the edges as though she had been shouting for a long time. He turned to find her and there was nothing — only smoke beginning to thread down from the upper windows of the tower, thin white ribbons at first, then fat black ropes of it, and the smell arrived all at once, tar and burnt feathers and something sweeter underneath that his dreaming mind refused to name.

*Run, Edmund. Don't look up. Whatever you hear, don't look up.*

He ran. His feet slapped cold flagstones. Behind him the tower made a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard, a groan like a giant waking, and glass burst somewhere above and rained down around him in bright, silent shards. He wanted to look up. She had told him not to look up, and so of course in the dream he looked up —

— and woke.

Edmund sat bolt upright on the pallet, breath sawing, one hand already at his belt where he kept the thin blade he'd never once needed against men but had used twice on rats. The cell was dark. The singing below had stopped, or perhaps it hadn't and he simply couldn't hear it over the blood in his ears. Sweat had pasted the shirt to his back. The map case lay where he'd left it, wedged between the wall and his hip, and his fingers found it without being asked, confirming its presence the way a tongue returns again and again to a chipped tooth.

*A dream.* He said it inside his own skull like an incantation. *Only a dream.*

But it wasn't. That was the thing that sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone. Dreams were noise, dreams were the mind dumping its slops, dreams were other people's memories gone sour in his keeping — he knew dreams better than most men alive, because for eleven years he had poured other people's lives into his own head by the ladleful, grief and first loves and childhood summers that belonged to merchants' daughters and dying soldiers and one collector in Vael who paid extravagantly to remember being a boy in a cherry orchard that had never existed. Edmund Winterbourne knew exactly what borrowed memory felt like from the inside. It felt like wearing someone else's coat: the shape was right, the warmth was real, and none of the pockets held your things.

This dream fit him like skin.

He knew the tower. Not as an image — as a body knows a staircase in the dark. He knew the particular lean of its western wall, the way the third-floor window bulged outward where some long-ago fire had warped the frame. He knew the flagstones were cold because they'd been quarried from the mountain's shaded face, and he knew the smell of burning feathers because the tower had kept doves, hundreds of them, white ones —

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw stars.

*I have no childhood.* That was the founding fact of himself, the bedrock under everything. He had woken to himself at what the priests at Vael's charity house guessed was seven or eight years old, sitting on the steps of the Temple of Coin with no name, no history, no grief — a blank coin, the priest had called him, unstruck, waiting for the mint. And Edmund had built a life in that blank space the way water builds a life in a crack: memory-work had come to him easily, suspiciously easily, sliding into other minds like a key into oiled locks, lifting out their yesterdays without leaving a scratch. Other thieves needed years to learn the touch. He had needed an afternoon.

He had told himself it was talent.

Lying in the dark of the Choir's hold, sweat cooling on his ribs, Edmund considered — for the first time honestly, without the professional's shrug he wore over the thought like an apron — that it might not have been talent at all. That a lock so easily opened might be a lock that had been opened before. That a man with no childhood might not have been born empty, but emptied.

*Don't look up. Whatever you hear, don't look up.*

She had known his name. In the dream she had known his name, and it had not surprised him, and that was the worst part — that some deep sedimentary layer of himself had accepted *Edmund* from her mouth as ordinary, as worn-in, as a shoe takes the shape of a foot.

* * *

He gave up on sleep when the grey light began to seep through the arrow-slit window, and went looking for work, because work was the only prayer he'd ever trusted.

The hold was larger than it had seemed in the dark. It occupied the gutted shell of some fortified waystation carved halfway into Thornfell's shoulder — he'd seen that much when Sera had led him in blindfolded two days ago, which struck him now as either prudence or insult depending on how much she suspected about him, and he was beginning to suspect she suspected a great deal. Morning found the place busy in a quiet, disciplined way: sisters and brothers of the Choir moving through vaulted halls with lamp-oil and bundles of arrows, mending, counting, preparing. No one lounged. No one laughed loudly. Grief, Edmund thought, watching a broad-shouldered woman sharpen a billhook with strokes slow enough to be meditation, can be run like a garrison, if you post sentries on it.

He found Mother Ivenna in the old chapel, if chapel it could be called. There was no altar, no icon — only a round depression in the stone floor, swept clean, ringed with nine unlit candles, and above it a ceiling open to the mountain through a collapsed section, letting in a column of morning air that moved even when the valley below was still. Ivenna knelt at the ring's edge. Her white braid hung to her waist. She did not turn when his boots scraped the threshold, but she said, "You slept badly."

"You heard me."

"The whole east hall heard you." Now she turned, and her eyes did the thing they always did, the thing that unnerved him more than any soldier's stare: they looked at him as though he were a text written in a language she was still translating. "You said a word, over and over. Do you know what you said?"

"No."

"Neither do we. It isn't Low Speech. It may be Old Thornic." She rose with the care of a woman whose knees had opinions. "Say it for me."

"I don't remember saying anything."

"Then say the word you woke with in your mouth." She watched him with terrible patience. "Everyone wakes with a word, child. Most are nonsense. Some are prayers. A few are names."

And it was there, of course — it had been sitting behind his teeth since he opened his eyes, tasting of smoke. Edmund heard himself say it before he decided to:

"*Vaelora.*"

Ivenna went very still. Stillness, in a hold full of people trained to stillness, was itself a kind of shout. Two sisters in the doorway stopped mid-stride. Somewhere a lamp guttered.

"Where," Ivenna said quietly, "did you learn that word?"

"I didn't learn it anywhere." His voice sounded strange to him, flattened. "It's just — it was in the dream. There was a tower burning. A woman telling me to run. She called me—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Lied badly, then, catching himself, didn't lie at all: "She called me Edmund. My name. Before I had it."

Ivenna crossed the chapel slowly and took his chin in her hand, tilting his face to the light the way a jeweler checks a stone for flaws. Her grip was dry and strong. "Open your mouth."

"What?"

"Teeth. Show me."

Baffled, he showed her. She studied his teeth like a horse trader, then his eyes, leaning close, and whatever she searched for there she must have found, because she released him and stepped back and made a small gesture — two fingers touched to her sternum, then flung outward — that the nearest sister answered instantly, a bow, a whispered word, both of them leaving the chapel at speed.

"You will tell me this dream," Ivenna said. "All of it. Every stone."

So he did. Standing in the candle-ringed chapel with morning pouring through the broken roof, Edmund described a tower he had no business knowing: the lean of the west wall, the bulged window, the doves, the flagstones quarried cold. He described the voice. He described — and here his own throat tightened around it, surprising him — the feeling of being late, a child's panic, the absolute conviction that someone upstairs was waiting for him and he had dawdled and now the smoke was coming down the stairwell and —

He stopped.

"Someone was upstairs," he said. The chapel's silence received the words and gave nothing back. "In the dream, someone was upstairs, and she wouldn't let me go back for them."

Ivenna lowered herself onto a stone bench, suddenly looking every year of her age and several she hadn't yet earned. "Vaelora is not a person," she said. "It is the name of the high tower at Cairn Hallow, the summer seat of the Thornfell kings. It burned three hundred years ago, on the last night of the war. With the royal family inside it." She looked up at him, and for the first time the translation-failure in her gaze resolved into something plainer and far more frightening: recognition. "Every child of Thornfell learns that story, Edmund Winterbourne. We sing it in the Ash Choir until our voices crack. But we learned it as history. You appear to have learned it as a Tuesday."

"That's impossible." He heard how thin it came out. "I told you — I'm nobody. I woke on temple steps with nothing. No name, no—"

"No memories," Ivenna said. "Yes. So you've said." She folded her hands. "And I have lived seventy-one years among people who guard the Crown's secret, and I will tell you what I know of erasure, since apparently no one else in your life has. Memory does not leave a mind cleanly. It leaves scars the way fire leaves scars in timber — the grain remembers even when the surface is planed flat. A man who lost his past to fever forgets gradually, and the forgetting has soft edges. But a man whose memories were *taken*—" her eyes held his, merciless, "—a taken man dreams in straight lines. He dreams the truth he cannot reach awake. The mind keeps knocking on a door that has been painted over."

Edmund's hands had gone cold. He became aware of them distantly, as though they belonged to someone standing beside him.

Because she was describing him. Eleven years of it, laid out in another woman's words as neatly as goods on a stall: the dreams that came too vivid, too structured, the same scenes recurring with the same details — the bulged window, the missing fingernail, the woman's voice — while other men's dreams scattered like starlings. The ease of the memory-touch, sliding into strangers' minds as if his own had a door propped open somewhere, waiting. The hollow at his center he'd spent a decade filling with other people's yesterdays, telling himself it was appetite, ambition, the simple economics of the trade.

A door painted over. And he had spent his whole adult life walking through everyone else's doors because he couldn't find his own.

"There's a test," Ivenna said. "Old. The Choir keeps it for... contingencies." Something moved behind her face — reluctance, or pity, or the particular exhaustion of a commander weighing whether a weapon should be shown to the person holding it. "It requires the map."

"The map resists me." He said it flatly. He'd told her already, the second night, when she'd asked what he knew of it. "I tried to read it the way I read everything else and it shut me out. Nothing has ever done that. Not once in eleven years. It's the first thing I've ever touched that I couldn't take from."

"Yes," Ivenna said. "And you should ask yourself why a dead courier bled his last breath delivering a thing that refuses the greatest thief in Vael — unless it wasn't refusing you at all." She rose. "Unless it was waiting."

* * *

They brought the map case to the chapel at midday, and Ivenna cleared the room of everyone but the two of them, and then made Edmund do the thing he had been avoiding since the courier died in his rented room with imperial riders' hooves still echoing off the cobbles.

He unrolled it across the swept stone floor and knelt over it, and let his consciousness sink into that familiar descent — the loosening, the opening, the thief's touch that slid beneath the surface of a mind like a knife beneath a purse-flap. The map was not a mind. He knew that. But memory clung to objects the way damp clung to stone, and Edmund had lifted a hundred secrets from letters, lockets, wedding rings — the residue of hands and hopes soaked into things. He reached for the map's residue.

And the map reached back.

There was no other honest word for it. His awareness plunged — not into smoky residue, not into the courier's dying impressions, but *down*, through vellum and ink, through the paper into something vast and patient and old beyond reckoning, and for one vertiginous instant Edmund Winterbourne stood inside a darkness that was aware of him, that turned toward him the way a sleeper turns toward a sound, and he felt it examine him — felt it recognize—

*Little wren,* said a voice that was the woman's voice and was not, that was smoke and bread-warmth and three hundred years of dust. *You came back after all.*

He tore free.

He came back to himself on his hands and knees on the chapel floor with blood in his mouth where he'd bitten his tongue and Ivenna gripping his shoulders, shouting his name, and the candles around the ring — unlit, all nine of them, he would swear it, unlit when he knelt — burning with a low blue flame that cast no heat and no shadow.

"The map," Ivenna was saying. "What did the map show you?"

"It didn't show me anything." His voice came out in pieces. "It *knew* me. It spoke with her voice. The woman from the dream. It said—" and here the vertigo crested again, the floor of his self tilting, because he understood, finally, with the clarity of a man falling, what had been true all along and what he had built his entire crooked livelihood on top of rather than ever once looking down at:

"It said I came back. As if I'd been there before. As if the map had been *mine*."

Ivenna sat back on her heels. Around them the blue flames bent, all nine together, toward the map as though leaning in to listen.

"Three hundred years ago," she said slowly, "when the last king saw the war could not be won and could not be survived, the royal house did not merely hide the Crown. They hid the *bloodline*. Children were carried out of Cairn Hallow in smugglers' carts. Their memories were taken — by the royal mnemonicans themselves, willingly given up, the whole past struck away so no inquisitor could follow a name to a cradle. The children were scattered. The taking was meant to be permanent." Her hand closed over his wrist, hard, an anchor thrown to a drowning man. "The Choir has sung for three centuries that the line ended. We sang it because believing otherwise was unbearable. But the Crown answers to Thornfell blood alone, Edmund. And there is a reason the map would not open to my sisters, or to me, or to any soul in this hold."

The blue flames bowed lower.

"There is a reason it opened for you."

Far above, through the broken roof, the mountain wind changed direction — and with it, faint and wrong, came a sound Edmund had learned to dread on the road from Vael and hoped never to hear again this deep in Thornfell: horns. Many horns, pitched in the Grey Company's marching cadence, closing along the valley road.

Mother Ivenna was on her feet before the echo faded, and the gentleness had left her entirely.

"They've found us," she said. "Get the map, heir of Thornfell. Pray you're worth what it cost us to keep it."
