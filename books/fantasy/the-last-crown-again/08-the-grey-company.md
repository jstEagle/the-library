## 8. The Grey Company

Captain Aldric Rell had learned long ago that mercy was a discipline, not a feeling.

He practiced it now, standing in the cold of the abandoned granary outside the village of Hallowmere, watching his soldiers drag the two scouts across the threshing floor. The prisoners were young. That was always the hardest part. The girl could not have seen more than nineteen winters; the boy beside her was older but thin in the way of men who had been running for a long time. They wore the grey-and-ash livery of the Ash Choir beneath rough traveling cloaks, and they had made the mistake every loyalist eventually made: they had believed the empire's reach ended where the paved roads did.

Rell knew better. The empire's reach ended nowhere at all.

"Names," he said, though he already had them. Corporal Vess had pulled them from a waystation ledger two villages back — Sable and Maren, registered as couriers, carrying salt permits that had been forged by someone competent enough to be interesting. Interesting people were Rell's trade. He collected them the way other men collected coins.

The girl spat at his boots. The boy said nothing, which was more telling. Guilty people who stayed silent had been coached, and coached people had something worth protecting.

"Water for them," Rell said. "Food, if they'll take it."

His lieutenant, a broad woman named Osk with frost-scars on her knuckles, raised an eyebrow. "Captain?"

"They've been in the hills three days. If we're going to ask them questions, they should be able to answer." He said it without warmth and without cruelty, which was the only way he trusted himself to say anything these days. "Mercy first, Captain Osk. It makes everything after it cleaner."

The granary smelled of rot and old grain dust, of mouse droppings and the sweet chemical tang of the engine being unloaded from the supply wagon behind him. Four men carried it in pieces, assembling it on the threshing floor with the reverence of priests erecting an altar. Rell watched the brass ribs go up one by one, the copper cradle at their center, the array of needles no longer than a fingernail, each one polished to a mirror shine. The Extraction Engine, Mark Seven. The empire had built six before it, and each had been retired for reasons the manuals described in careful bureaucratic language — *operator fatigue*, *signal degradation*, *subject mortality* — language that meant what all such language meant.

Mark Seven worked beautifully. Rell had certified it himself. He had signed the document with the same hand that had once signed his mother's death notice, and some nights he thought there was very little difference between the two signatures.

* * *

They gave the prisoners an hour. Rell spent it in the granary's loft, going over the map of the region by lamplight, marking the places where Choir activity had thickened in the past season like blood pooling under a bruise. Thornfell province. Three hundred years after the fall, and still the mountains bled loyalty.

It was not sentiment that kept him here, though his superiors in the capital certainly thought so. Let them. He had stopped needing to be understood around the same time he had stopped needing to sleep well.

Below him, the girl — Maren — was talking to the boy in low voices. Rell did not need to hear the words. He had interrogated enough pairs to know the shape of the conversation: *don't tell them anything; whatever they offer, don't; if it comes to it, bite your tongue.* And underneath it, quieter, the thing neither of them would say aloud: *I'm afraid of what they'll take.*

That fear was justified. Rell wished, not for the first time, that it weren't necessary. But necessity had never once asked his opinion.

Osk climbed the ladder and crouched beside him. "Corporal Vess found something in the boy's pack. Hidden in the heel of a spare boot."

She handed it down: a scrap of oilcloth, folded small, and inside it a strip of birch bark marked with charcoal. Not words — a notation. A sequence of peaks drawn in profile, and beneath the third peak, a symbol Rell recognized from a file he was forbidden to remove from imperial archives and had removed anyway, years ago, the night he decided this posting would be the last one he ever served.

A crown. Drawn with thorns.

"Thornfell," Osk said.

"Thornfell," Rell agreed.

"You believe they're scouts for the Choir's main company. Heading for the mountain."

"I believe they're the fingers, and somewhere ahead of us is the hand." Rell turned the bark over. On its back, in a different hand, smaller and shakier: a name half-written and scratched out. He could make out four letters. *Edm—*

He stared at it for a long moment. Then he put the bark in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the things that mattered, and climbed down the ladder to begin his work.

* * *

He started with the girl.

This was procedure, not preference — the young broke faster, and a broken first subject softened the second — but Rell would have started with her regardless. Not because she was weaker. Because she was angrier, and anger was a door that opened inward. You only had to push.

They strapped her into the copper cradle with her consent, more or less. Rell explained it to her first, as he always did, kneeling so their eyes were level, his voice pitched to carry no farther than the space between them.

"The engine doesn't hurt, Maren. I want you to understand that, because everyone believes it hurts, and the belief is worse than the thing. What it does is *read*. Your memories are written in you — in the patterns your mind makes when it remembers. The needles listen to those patterns and copy them out. Like pressing a key into wax." He held up his hand, palm out, unthreatening. "What happened tonight stays in your head. Nothing is removed. Nothing is damaged. In the morning you will remember every moment of this conversation, and you will hate me, and that is your right."

"You're lying," she said. Her wrists strained against the leather. "Everyone knows what those machines do. People come out of them *empty*."

"People come out of the old ones empty. The ones the Church used, during the Reclamation — yes. Those were butchery dressed up as sacrament." He let a little weariness show, because it was true, and truth was the sharpest tool he owned. "I have run this engine forty-one times. Ask my own soldiers what I do afterward. I check every subject personally. I feed them. I write a report on their condition and I am accountable for it to the Ministry of Interior Integrity. If I harmed you, Maren, I would be tried by the same courts that would try you." A lie of omission, carefully shaped: the courts would weigh his service heavily, hers not at all. But the architecture of the statement was sound, and soundness was what conviction required.

"Why does the empire care about two couriers?" she asked. And there it was — the anger opening inward, the question that wanted answering more than it wanted fighting. "Salt permits. That's all we were carrying."

"Salt permits forged by a hand that has forged other things," Rell said gently. "You know what the Ash Choir is. You swore yourself to it. So you know what they guard, and you know why men like me cross frozen provinces to stop it." He rose. "Last chance to give it freely. Anything given freely costs you nothing. Anything taken—" He stopped himself. Corrected. "Anything taken is taken gently, but it is still taken."

"Burn," she said, with feeling.

Rell nodded, as if she'd agreed to terms. "Very well."

He took his seat at the engine's console — a plain wooden chair, deliberately, because the empire liked its instruments of terror to look like furniture — and set the needles. They went in at the nape, seven points, barely a pinch; he watched her flinch and catalogued the flinch, filed it under the cost of doing business. The brass ribs began their slow rotation, humming a note below hearing, a note Rell felt in his molars and in the scar tissue over his left kidney, where a Choir blade had found him eleven years ago at a place called the Weeping Gate.

The image-pool filled. Silver liquid, quicksilver-bright, and across its surface the girl's memories began to move like weather.

Rell did not watch the pool the way his technicians did — hungry, greedy, faces lit blue-white with reflected lives. He watched it the way an auditor watches ledgers. There was a discipline to extraction, and the discipline was this: you looked for the pattern, not the person. The person was none of your business. The person was a lock, and you were paid to care about what was behind the door.

Maren's childhood came first, as it always did — nearest the surface, least defended. A farm. Goats. A mother singing off-key. Rell skimmed it with professional detachment and permitted himself exactly one breath of something like tenderness before moving on. This was mercy too, he told himself. The alternative — the old method, the Church's method — was to burn the surface memories away entirely just to reach the depths. Mark Seven touched nothing. Mark Seven only listened.

Then the training. The Choir's hidden hold — glimpsed only in fragments, as he'd expected; the Choir blindfolded its own initiates on approach, a caution Rell respected even as he planned to defeat it. Then the oaths, sworn over ash. Then the assignment: *carry word south, find the grey-eyed man, tell him the route is open.*

Rell leaned forward. "*The grey-eyed man.*"

"He means nothing," Maren whispered. She was crying now, silently, tears sliding sideways into her hair. "Please. He's nobody."

"Everyone is somebody, Maren. That is precisely the problem." He adjusted a dial. The pool deepened. Memories rose in strata, older and older, the way an archaeologist digs — and here Rell did something he almost never permitted himself, something Osk would have frowned at. He slowed the engine down. He let the girl's life play at reading speed rather than skimming speed, because he had learned that the deepest rooms of a mind were guarded by doors that only opened for patience.

And there — in the sediment, in the place where initiates were taught the reason for their oaths — he found it.

Not her memory. Older. Borrowed, worn smooth by a hundred retellings, the way a river stone is worn by water: the story every child of the Choir learned at their grandmother's knee. And through Maren's telling of it, Rell saw the original as the Choir preserved it, and the seeing was like pressing his eye to a keyhole three centuries deep.

* * *

He saw a throne room the color of dusk.

He saw a young emperor — no, not an emperor yet, a warlord, a conqueror with frost-grey eyes and a voice like a landslide — standing before a captured king of Thornfell. The last Thornfell king, small and grey-faced in chains, and the conqueror laughing, because what was a mountain kingdom to a man who had never lost?

He saw the Crown brought out. Taken from the vault as spoils. The warlord's advisors begging him not to touch it — *the kings of Thornfell did not wear it as ornament, lord, they wore it as a yoke wears an ox* — and the warlord laughing again, because he had broken kingdoms, and what was a circlet of metal?

Rell watched, through the borrowed lens of a dead grandmother's story filtered through a living girl's faith, as the founding Emperor Aurelian set the Last Crown upon his head.

And the Crown — the Crown *listened*.

That was the horror of it. That was the thing no imperial history book recorded, the thing scrubbed from every archive Rell had ever searched, the thing that existed only here, in the ash-oaths and firelight stories of the people his empire called rebels. The Crown did not grant visions. It did not whisper. It *remembered*. Every mind that had ever touched it, every king, every war, every grief of three hundred years of Thornfell kings — it held them all, and it poured them into the wearer like a dam breaking. Not as knowledge. As *experience*. As lived fact, total and inescapable.

Rell watched Emperor Aurelian — through the memory of a story — kneel. Watched the conqueror clutch his skull and scream a scream that had no end, because the Crown had shown him every death he had caused, from inside, from the dying man's own eyes, and it did not stop showing him, and it could not be taken off, because a mind flooded that deeply forgets how hands work.

Three days. The story said three days the Emperor lay screaming while his physicians despaired and his generals debated killing him for the good of the succession. And on the third day the Crown was pried loose — and Aurelian rose changed. Quiet. Grey. He never conquered again. He spent thirty years building walls instead of breaking them, and on his deathbed he left a single commandment, sealed and sworn by every inquisitor since:

*The Crown must never be found. And if it is found, it must never be understood — for understanding is how it kills.*

*We do not fear it because it is powerful,* the dying emperor had said. *We fear it because it is merciful, and we are not.*

Rell sat back from the console. His hands were steady — they were always steady; steadiness was another discipline — but it took effort, and he noted the effort the way he noted all costs.

Beside him, Maren hung limp in the cradle, weeping without sound, and he realized the engine had shown her the story too, had played her own inheritance back at her while it copied it into silver. Her eyes found his.

"Now you know," she whispered. "Now you know what your emperor saw. And you still serve them."

"Yes," Rell said.

"How?"

"Because someone has to stand between that thing and the world," he said. "Because a hundred thousand people died in the last war the Crown presided over, and I have read the casualty rolls, Maren — actual names, actual villages, actual children — while your Choir remembers only its glory." He stood, signaling the technicians to withdraw the needles. "Your story says the Crown broke our emperor. My records say it also broke your kingdom, from the inside, king by king by king, until there was nothing left but ash and oath-singers. Both things are true. That is what memory does — it keeps both truths and lets you choose which to worship." He unbuckled her wrist straps himself, an act no other inquisitor performed, and did not examine why. "I have chosen. Forgive me."

She looked at him for a long moment — hatred and pity and something almost like recognition — and then turned her face away.

Rell moved to the boy, Sable, and the needles, and the hum, and the pool.

* * *

The boy gave them the route.

Not willingly — Rell never asked for willingness twice; it wasted time and cheapened the asking — but the engine found it, folded in the sediment beneath his fear: a pass called the Shepherd's Stair, a smuggler's road, a burned village where the Choir meant to regroup, and beyond it, higher, a landmark the boy had never seen but had been taught to seek. *The Weeping Gate.* Water falling upward out of the mountain. Stone that sang.

And threaded through it all, recurring like a motif in music, a name the boy had heard his handlers speak with reverence and urgency in equal measure:

*Winterbourne.*

The thief. The memory-thief of Vael, who had gone to ground with something in his possession that had the entire Grey Company riding north through the snow. Rell lifted the birch bark from his breast pocket and looked again at the scratched-out letters. *Edm—*

"Wake her," he told Osk. "Feed them. Put them somewhere warm and under guard, and when we march at dawn, leave them alive with food enough for three days. Write it in the log under my seal."

"And if the Ministry asks why we release confirmed Choir assets?"

"Because I am not in the business of making martyrs," Rell said. "Dead rebels breed ten rebels apiece. Living ones mostly go home and plant turnips." He gathered his maps, his lantern, the scrap of bark with its half-name. Outside, snow had begun to fall, fat and silent, erasing the road south toward Thornfell one flake at a time.

Osk lingered at the tent flap. "Forty-two," she said quietly. "That was your forty-second extraction. You checked the girl twice tonight. You're checking the boy now, through the canvas. Captain — when does the auditor start keeping his own ledger?"

Rell did not answer. He was looking at the birch bark, at the four letters, and thinking about a story in which the most dangerous thing in the world was not power but *memory* — and realizing, with the cold clarity that was his only remaining prayer, that somewhere ahead of them a man was walking toward Thornfell carrying both.

"Break camp at first light," he said. "We ride for the Weeping Gate."
