## 9. Betrayal in the Hold

Edmund woke to the sound of a door he had not heard open.

In Vael he had slept through riots. He had learned, in the years since his trade had made him both valuable and despised, to sleep like a knife balanced on its edge — one ear always listening for the particular quality of wrongness that preceded violence: a floorboard creaking under too much weight, a breath held too carefully, silence where there should have been the ordinary music of a living house.

The hidden hold beneath Thornfell's shoulder was never silent. It breathed. Water moved somewhere in its deep galleries; the Choir's people murmured in their sleep behind woven screens; the great hearth in the common hall ticked and settled as it burned down toward morning coals. Mother Ivenna said the mountain sang to them if they were quiet enough to listen, and Edmund, who had spent his life taking other people's songs out of their heads, had begun — uneasily — to believe her.

Now the breathing of the hold had changed.

He lay still on his pallet in the narrow cell they'd given him, staring at the low stone ceiling, and counted what was missing. The sentry at the eastern stair should have shifted his weight every quarter hour; his boots were new and he hadn't yet learned to stand quietly. Nothing. The woman two cells down who talked in her sleep — a scout named Maren who dreamed aloud in fragments about a farmhouse and a river — her voice was gone too, though not the way sleep ends. It was gone the way a room goes when everyone in it has left quickly.

And underneath it all, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the smell of smoke that wasn't hearth smoke.

Edmund rose without sound. He'd kept his boots on. He had told himself it was habit, the wariness of a man who slept in rented rooms in cities full of collectors and creditors, but some part of him — some part that remembered things he could not remember — had known better. He took the map case from beneath the loose flagstone and slung it across his chest, the strap biting into him, and eased his door open an inch.

The corridor beyond was lit by a single guttering lamp, and standing beneath it with a hood drawn up and a face he recognized from three days of careful watching was Tam, one of Ivenna's runners, a boy of perhaps seventeen who had shown Edmund how to work the water pump on his first night and laughed at his incompetence.

Tam was holding the eastern door open.

Beyond it, in the blue dark before dawn, torches were coming down the mountainside. Not many. A dozen, maybe fewer, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who did not need to hurry because someone inside had already done the hard work for them. Grey cloaks. Grey hoods. And at their center, walking with the terrible calm of a man attending a scheduled appointment, a figure whose face Edmund knew only from Sera's spitting hatred and the Choir's whispered warnings: Captain Rell.

* * *

Later, Edmund would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail, because memory — real memory, honest memory — does not work the way stolen memory works. Stolen memory is clean. It comes out whole, a vial of amber light, everything in its place. But the next ten minutes arrived in shards, and every time he tried to fit them together they cut him differently.

He remembered grabbing Tam. He remembered the boy's eyes going wide, the smell of coin-sweat on him — actual coin, Edmund's thief's nose cataloguing it even as horror rose in his throat, silver and old copper, imperial mint — and Tam saying, in a voice cracked down the middle, "They have my sister. They had my sister before I ever—" and then Edmund hitting him, once, with the edge of his hand, because there was no time, and the boy folding against the wall with a sound like grief itself.

He remembered running for the common hall, where Ivenna kept the old horn she swore she would blow only once in her life.

He remembered fire.

Not from Rell. Not yet. The fire came from below — from the deep galleries, the flooded ones, where the Choir stored what little they had and hid the youngest of them — and Edmund understood, with the cold clarity that had served him all his life in back alleys and bad bargains, that the traitor had sold more than a route. Someone had opened the lower doors. Someone had let them in at the roots, and now the hold was dying from underneath while the Grey Company walked in calmly at the top, and between the two jaws of it were forty sleeping people and an old woman and a horn.

"Edmund." Sera, materializing out of a side passage with her bow strung and no arrows in her quiver yet, her hands moving with the terrible efficiency of someone doing arithmetic with death. "The east stair?"

"Gone. They're on it." He grabbed her arm. "Fire below. How do we get them out?"

Her face did something complicated and awful. For one heartbeat she was seventeen again in a burning village, and he watched her choose, deliberately, painfully, not to be. "There's a chimney shaft off the drying loft. Old smoke vent. Narrow. Children fit."

"And everyone else?"

"The mountain fits," she said, "if you know its doors." And she turned and ran toward the hall, and Edmund ran after her, because he was beginning to understand that he would run after her into anything now, and hated himself a little for the timing of the discovery.

* * *

Mother Ivenna stood in the middle of the common hall with the horn already at her lips.

She was not blowing it. She was looking at the far doorway, where grey shapes were beginning to gather like weather, and she was smiling — a small, dry, terrible smile — as though the empire had finally confirmed something she had suspected for decades.

"You're awake," she said to Edmund, without turning. "Good. Thieves make the best survivors. Take the girl and go."

"I'm not leaving—"

"You are." She turned then, and the lamplight caught the deep seams of her face, and Edmund saw that she was not afraid, and that this was the most frightening thing he had ever seen. "Listen to me, boy, because I will say this once and I do not repeat myself, even for kings." Her hand closed on his wrist, old fingers strong as root-wood. "That map is not paper. It is a promise, and promises must arrive. Whatever else burns tonight — whatever I burn tonight — the map goes on. Do you understand me? You carry it. You don't read it, you don't sell it, you don't weep over it. You carry it."

Behind her, the first of the grey shapes crossed the threshold, and the hall filled with the sound of steel leaving leather.

"Sera!" Ivenna's voice rang off the stone like a bell cracking. "The loft! Now!"

Sera was already moving, dragging a half-asleep child by each hand. Edmund looked at the old woman. The old woman looked back.

"I had a son once," she said, conversational, almost kind, as the greys came on. "I traded his future for forty lives during the last purge. It seemed a fair price. It has taken me thirty years to stop checking my sums." She lifted the horn. "Go and be worth it."

Then she blew.

The sound was nothing like a horn. It went through the stone of the mountain and through the bones of everyone standing in it, a note so deep and so old that the lamps guttered and the dust leapt from the rafters, and Edmund felt it in his teeth and in the hollow places inside him where his childhood used to be, and something in those hollow places *answered*. Just for a heartbeat. Just a single pulse of recognition, warm and vast and grieving, rising up from far below like a whale surfacing from the dark of the sea.

Then Ivenna dropped the horn, drew the long knife from her belt, and walked toward the Grey Company, and Edmund ran.

* * *

The drying loft was chaos — children crying, two Choir women hauling bodies up through the trapdoor, Sera shoving the smallest of them into the black mouth of the chimney shaft and telling them, in a voice that did not shake until the very end of each sentence, *climb, climb, don't look down, climb.* Below them the hall had become a roar. Above them the sky was going grey at the edges, dawn arriving with the worst possible timing, painting the mountainside silver for anyone with eyes to see.

Edmund pushed children up the shaft. Small hands, small boots, the smell of woodsmoke and terror. He did not think about the hall. He thought about the hall constantly. Every scream that climbed up through the floorboards he sorted, helplessly, professionally, the way he sorted everything — *that one is pain, that one is rage, that one is a woman calling a name* — and he understood with a clarity that felt like punishment exactly what was being purchased with each of those sounds. His survival. Sera's survival. The map's survival. The same currency he had dealt in for fifteen years, except now he was on the selling end of the transaction and the price was being paid out of other people's futures, and there was no collector waiting at the end of this alley, only snow and distance and a promise he didn't want.

A woman reached the top of the shaft bleeding from the shoulder — Maren, the sleep-talker, the farmhouse-and-river dreamer — and fell gasping onto the loft boards, and Edmund pulled her clear and saw her face and knew, before she spoke, what she had come to say.

"The lower gallery," she rasped. "It was Tam. He gave them the tide-door code. But Captain Rell—" she coughed, wet and horrible, "—Rell wasn't looking for the map. Not first. He asked her — he asked Mother Ivenna — for *names*. He wanted the names of everyone who'd ever carried it, and she wouldn't give them, and he said—" Maren's eyes fixed on something very far away. "He said, 'I am sorry, madam. Mercy is a heavy instrument.' And then he—"

Screams swallowed the rest. Or maybe Maren simply stopped. Edmund never learned which.

Sera's hand closed on his collar. "Up. Now. We're the last."

They went up the chimney shaft into the freezing dawn, and below them the hold of the Ash Choir burned from the inside out, flame climbing the vent after them like a thing alive, and Edmund climbed with a dead woman's promise strapped to his chest and a boy's confession lodged under his ribs — *they had my sister before I ever* — and he climbed, because that was the whole of his skill and the sum of his life. Climbing out. Surviving the transaction.

* * *

They gained the tree line as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and from there, from a shelf of granite hung with rime, they looked back.

The hold was not visible — it was built into the mountain's flank, disguised as rock — but its entrances smoked, thin grey threads rising into the blue morning air, and the Grey Company was deploying across the slope below in disciplined lines, sweeping, searching, patient as frost. Somewhere among those lines was Captain Rell, and somewhere among the ashes was Mother Ivenna, and Edmund found that he could not make himself feel it properly. That was the rot in him, he thought. Fifteen years of lifting grief out of other heads and handing it away had left him unable to hold his own. He stood on the frozen rock and waited for devastation to arrive, and instead what arrived was inventory. *Map secured. Sera secured. Enemy disposition known. Escape routes: south ridge, or down through the scree.* His mind doing what it always did, turning horror into logistics, and he hated the efficiency of it, hated it the way a scarred man hates his own numb skin.

"They'll follow the vent shafts," Sera said. Her voice was scraped hollow. Ash was in her hair. She had not cried, and he could see the effort it cost her, see it sitting in her jaw and her white-knuckled fists like swallowed glass. "The children are scattered. Three rendezvous points. If any of them reach the second point, the Choir survives. On paper." She stared at the smoking mountain. "On paper."

"We can go back down. At night. We could—"

"*No.*" It came out of her like a blade. Then quieter, worse: "No. Forty-one people bought us this much of a head start. I am not going to spend them for nothing, and neither are you." She turned and looked at him, and in her eyes was the question he had been dreading since the first torch came down the mountainside. "Forty-one people, Edmund. An old woman who hadn't slept in two days. Children who trusted us. All of it gone, and the Grey Company knew exactly where to knock." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Someone sold them the route. Sold them the *tide-doors*. Who else knew them? Who else had been inside long enough, been *trusted* enough?"

The wind moved the pines below them with a sound like the sea.

"Tam," Edmund said. "I saw him. He opened the east door. He said they had his sister." He heard his own voice and was disgusted by it — level, professional, a witness giving testimony. "Sera, I hit him. I left him in a corridor that's ash right now, and I don't know if I killed him or if they did, and either way—"

"Either way you survived," Sera finished. "Again." She said it without cruelty, which made it unbearable. "Everyone who stands near you dies buying you time. The courier. Ivenna. My village wasn't you, but sometimes at night I wonder." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "I need you to understand that I'm telling you this while choosing to stay beside you, because if you learn it later you'll think I stayed out of blindness, and I won't be blind. Not anymore. Not for anyone."

Edmund looked at her, and then at the smoking mountain, and the guilt in him finally broke loose of the inventory and became something with teeth. Because she was right, and because it was worse than she knew. Survival purchased with other people's blood wasn't just his curse. It was his *craft*. He had made a life of it. He had priced other people's grief by the dram and sold it to men who drank it like wine, and he had told himself it was honest work, cleaner than most, at least the sellers consented — and now here he stood on a frozen ledge above a burned sanctuary, wearing a dead woman's promise across his chest, and every rationalization he owned lay in the ashes below with the rest of the Choir.

"I'm going to give it back to them somehow," he said. Not a plan. A vow, spoken to the smoke. "Every coin Corvane ever paid me. Every vial. Everything I took. I'll—"

The words stopped.

Because down on the sweep-lines below, a rider had broken from the Grey Company's formation and was climbing toward the tree line — alone, fast, following a path no tracker should have found that quickly — and even at this distance Edmund recognized the cut of the coat, the easy arrogance of the seat in the saddle. He had seen that silhouette a hundred times in Vael, arriving at his door with too much money and too many questions.

Master Corvane had come to Thornfell. And he was riding directly toward them.

Sera followed his gaze, and her hand went to her knife. "Friend of yours?"

"No," Edmund said, and the word tasted like a lie, because in every way that mattered, it was. "Worse."
