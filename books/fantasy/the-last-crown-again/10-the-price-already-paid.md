## 10. The Price Already Paid

The snow began an hour past the tree line, fat flakes that fell without wind, as if the mountain were deciding slowly whether to bury them.

Edmund climbed with the map case strapped tight against his chest and Sera three paces ahead, her boots finding purchase on scree that should have swallowed them both. Behind them, far below, a smudge of black smoke still rose from the Choir's hidden hold — the place where Mother Ivenna had stood in a burning doorway and told him to *run*, and he had run, because running was the one skill his whole life had taught him well.

He did not look back anymore. Looking back made the arithmetic worse.

They crossed a shoulder of granite where the snow lay thin as spilled salt, and Sera finally stopped. Not because she needed rest — she hadn't asked for it — but because the path narrowed between two leaning stones, a place where a body could stand its ground, and Edmund understood she had been waiting for exactly such a place since they'd fled the hold.

She turned. Her face was ash-grey in the failing light, streaked where tears had frozen and been scrubbed away, more than once by the look of it.

"Say it," she said.

Edmund pulled his cloak tighter. "Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying for two days." Her voice was flat, scraped clean of anything soft. "You keep opening your mouth when you think I'm asleep. You start sentences and swallow them. I watched you do it over Ivenna's body. So say it now, Winterbourne, while my hands are cold enough that I might listen instead of hitting you."

* * *

Sera had counted the dead the way other people counted coins.

It was a habit from the village — Ashfall, before it was ash — where her grandmother had taught her that grief uncounted became grief unmanageable, a debt with no ledger, and a debt with no ledger would ruin you faster than any tax the empire levied. So: eleven villagers burned in the raid that took her home. Two Choir scouts lost on the eastern passes before Edmund ever came among them. Mother Ivenna, who had held the door of the burning hold with a song in her throat and fire climbing her robes like it recognized her. And Tam. Little Tam, sixteen years old, who had carried messages between the safeholds and laughed at his own jokes before anyone else could, and who had died in the first volley because the traitor's note had named the route he walked.

All of them dead within a season of one grey-coated man or another learning their names.

And then there was this: every one of those deaths had happened *near* Edmund Winterbourne, or after him, or because of the thing he carried. The scouts had died tracking the map's path before he held it — but Rell's company had only begun sweeping the high country in earnest once word spread of a thief out of Vael with a leather case strapped to his chest. The hold had burned the third night after he arrived. The traitor, Bren, had confessed under questioning that he'd sold the route because he believed Edmund would lead the empire to them all anyway, better to cut the rot out early.

Sera didn't know if Bren had been right. She knew that believing it had gotten Bren's throat opened by Rell's own hand, according to a dying scout's last whisper — the empire did not forgive its instruments — and that changed nothing about the arithmetic.

Eleven. Two. One mother of the Choir. One boy who whistled off-key.

And now there was her, climbing into the teeth of winter beside a man whose eyes never stopped moving, whose hands were always half-curled like something waited to be lifted out of the air, and who could not look at her for longer than four seconds without flinching.

She had told herself it was shock. Grief. The particular cowardice of men who had never buried anyone. But she had watched him at the fires these last nights, and it wasn't cowardice in his face. It was recognition. He looked at her the way a man looks at a door he knows is going to open onto something bad.

So when the path narrowed between the two leaning stones — old boundary markers, her grandmother's maps said, from the time before Thornfell kings, before everything — Sera turned and demanded the truth, and watched him decide, visibly, whether to lie.

That was the moment she knew she would stay anyway. She just wanted to know *what* she was staying for.

* * *

"I steal memories."

The words came out smaller than Edmund intended. Above the snowline, sound behaved strangely; the mountains seemed to take his confession and carry it away somewhere, so that even he barely heard himself admit it.

Sera didn't move. Snow gathered on her shoulders. "I know about the trade in Vael. Everyone past the border towns knows. Memory-thieves, mind-wipers. They say it's mostly the desperate who buy it. Widows who can't carry it. Soldiers." A pause, deliberate as a blade laid flat against skin. "Which are you selling to? Or which are you?"

"Both. Neither." He heard how useless the words were and forced himself to be precise, because precision was the last professional dignity he had left. "I lift them. Grief, mostly. Sometimes shame, sometimes love that's gone rotten in the keeping. People come to me because they want to stop remembering, and I take it out, and I seal it in glass, and I sell it to collectors who pay for the taste of other people's pain." His mouth twisted. "There's a man in Vael called Corvane. He pays too well. I always thought that meant something was wrong with the merchandise. Turns out it was wrong with the merchant."

"And your own past?" Sera's voice didn't soften, but it changed key — the interrogator's key, the one that left no room to hide. "The thief with nothing in his pockets. You mentioned it once, at the hold. You said your memory was like a house with the furniture sold off."

"I did say that." Edmund breathed out, and watched the steam of it shred in the cold. "Because it's true. There's a hole where my childhood should be. Twenty-odd years of nothing before I surfaced in Vael with a name and a talent and no idea where either came from. I told myself I'd sold it. Drunk years, desperate years — I assumed I'd traded my own yesterdays the same way I traded everyone else's, and forgotten the transaction along with everything else." He shook his head slowly. "But I've started having fragments. Since the mountains. A tower burning. A woman singing — a lullaby in a language I don't speak but understand. And the smell of this stone." He put his bare hand against the boundary marker beside him. "This exact stone, Sera. Like I've stood here before, small enough that I had to reach up to touch it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, an eagle rode the thermals, screaming once.

"So you're telling me," Sera said at last, each word placed like a foot on ice, "that the man carrying the map to the Last Crown — the man whose blood the Choir's oldest songs say must open the vault — cannot remember why that might be."

"Yes."

"And everyone around him dies."

"That," Edmund said, "is what I've been trying not to say for two days."

* * *

He told her all of it then, standing between the boundary stones while the light went from pewter to iron.

He told her about the courier bleeding out on his floor, the name *Thornfell keeps* whispered like a password into a lock. About the map that refused his touch — the first thing in fifteen years of thieving that his gift couldn't peel open, that sat behind his eyes like a locked room in a house he suddenly suspected was his own. About fleeing Vael through smuggler roads, hunted by men in imperial grey, feeling for the first time in his life like the vial instead of the hand holding it. About the burned village he'd walked through, and the small charred shoes he still saw when he closed his eyes.

About Ivenna. About the door. About running.

"I want you to understand what I am," he said, "before you decide whether to walk south without me. Because I've done the count too, Sera. I'm not stupid, whatever else I am. Every life near mine gets spent. The courier. The Choir. Your Tam." He saw her flinch at the name and made himself continue, because mercy here would be the cruelest thing he could offer. "Rell isn't hunting the map alone. He's hunting the man who carries it, because somewhere in the empire's ledgers there's a page with my family's name on it, and I think Corvane has read it, and I think that's why he paid too well for fifteen years — keeping his investment close. I am not a person, as far as any of them are concerned. I'm a container. And containers get broken."

"Then give me the map," Sera said. "Walk away. Go south, go anywhere, live."

The words hung between them, and Edmund found — to his genuine surprise — that the answer was already formed, had maybe been forming since a dying stranger pressed leather into his hands.

"No."

"Why not?" Her hand had drifted toward the knife at her belt, though her eyes said the gesture was habit, not threat. "Give me one reason that isn't guilt. Guilt is cheap. Everyone in Thornfell runs on guilt."

"Because I tried being nobody." His voice cracked on it, and he let it crack; there was no performance left in him worth protecting. "Fifteen years, Sera. Fifteen years of being hollow and calling it professionalism. Of taking other people's pain because mine was missing and I couldn't even mourn it properly. And then a dying man gave me this—" he touched the case over his heart "—and for the first time since I can remember, which admittedly isn't far, something mattered. Something chose me. Or something I chose. I honestly don't know the difference anymore. But I know that if I hand you the map and walk south, I'll spend the rest of my hollow life wondering if I ran again, and I have run from everything my entire existence, and I am so tired, Sera. I am tired down to the marrow."

Snow settled between them. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its edge — not warm, never warm yet, but level. Human.

"My grandmother used to say the mountain doesn't care what you deserve. It cares what you carry." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes. "You're carrying stolen goods, Winterbourne. Other people's deaths, your own missing years, a king's crown in a leather tube. That's a lot of weight for one thief."

"It is."

"Good." She turned up the path. "Then you'll move slow enough for me to keep up. And Edmund—" she glanced back, and for one unguarded second the vigilance fell away and he saw only a woman who had lost everything twice, "—when we find whoever erased your childhood, I want to be there. Not for you. For the principle of the thing."

Despite everything — despite the smoke still rising behind them, despite the dead he owed — Edmund laughed. It startled him, the sound, rusty as an unused hinge.

"Noted," he said, and followed her up into the snow.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen pillar-stone, half-buried, its carved runes worn to suggestions. Sera built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and they ate hard bread and colder cheese, and neither of them spoke until the dark was complete.

It was Sera who broke it. "The lullaby. The one in the language you don't speak. Sing it."

"I can't. I don't have it. I have the shape of having it, like a footprint after the bird's flown."

"Hum the shape, then."

And because he owed her honesty and had nothing else to pay with, Edmund hummed — four notes, five, a descending phrase that felt older than the fire, older than the empire — and Sera went very still across the flames.

"What?" he asked.

Her face was unreadable for a long moment. Then she reached into her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord, a small bone whistle, blackened with age.

"The Choir teaches it to every child," she said quietly. "We call it the King's Cradle. We thought it was ours. A relic of the loyalists, passed down since the fall." Her thumb moved over the whistle without blowing it. "My mother sang it the night the soldiers came to Ashfall. She said it was a promise — that someone would come back to the mountain, someday, and end the waiting."

The wind moved through the pass above them, and far off — so far it might have been imagination, might have been the settling of the snowpack — the mountain answered. A low tone, deep in the rock, like a struck bell heard through a wall of stone. Once. Twice. Then silence.

Sera looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the map case against his chest, which had grown, in the last mile, faintly warm.

"Tell me that was an avalanche," he said.

Sera did not tell him that.

She banked the fire instead, and sat with her knife drawn and her back straight, watching the dark where the sound had come from, and Edmund sat watch beside her, humming four notes to himself like a prayer he didn't believe in, while beneath their feet — patient as a debt, warm as a hearth — something old turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
