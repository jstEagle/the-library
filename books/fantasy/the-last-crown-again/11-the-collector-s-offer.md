## 11. The Collector's Offer

The high country above the snowline had a way of making silence feel like surveillance.

Edmund had learned that in the last three days — learned it the hard way, flinching at every crack of ice, every groan of wind through the passes, every shadow that slid across the snow and turned out to be only a hawk. He had spent years teaching himself to move unnoticed through Vael's crooked districts, to be a ghost among crowds. But crowds were easy. Crowds gave you a thousand faces to hide behind. Up here there was nothing between him and the sky but thin air and his own guilty heartbeat, and the map rode against his ribs like a second heart, heavier than any vial he'd ever carried.

He'd made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen boulder, a shelf of stone half-buried in drift, where a fire no larger than two cupped hands could burn without announcing itself to the valley below. Sera was on watch somewhere above him — she never slept when he did anymore, not since the hold, not since Ivenna's face going grey in the smoke. She moved like something born to these mountains, which he supposed she was, and every hour she spent looking down the slope for imperial grey was an hour she wasn't looking at him with that question in her eyes: *what are you?*

He didn't know how to answer it. That was becoming the whole of his life.

The fire had burned down to a red eye of coals when he heard the footsteps.

That was wrong in itself. Three days of Sera's company had taught him what mountain footsteps sounded like — deliberate, rolling from heel to ball of foot, placed like a woman setting stones across a river. These steps came up the switchback trail light as falling snow, unhurried, almost courteous, and Edmund's hand went to his knife before his mind caught up with the recognition already blooming cold in his stomach.

He knew that walk. He'd watched it cross a collector's salon a hundred times, watched it pause before shelves of other people's lives.

"Master Winterbourne." The voice drifted out of the dark, warm and dry as old paper. "You've been difficult to find. Though I suppose that's rather the point of you."

Corvane stepped into the coal-light as though the night had simply decided to produce him. He looked exactly as he had in Vael — the same immaculate grey coat, absurdly clean for a man on a mountain; the same narrow face with its expression of perpetual mild interest; the same silver chain at his throat holding a single lens that caught the firelight and threw it back in fragments. Only his gloves were different. New ones. Kid leather, pale as peeled bark.

Edmund was on his feet with the knife out before he'd decided to move. "How."

"How did I find you?" Corvane smiled the small, apologetic smile of a man being asked to explain arithmetic to a child. "The same way everyone finds anything in this world, Master Winterbourne. Someone sold it. A drover outside Ashford saw a man and a woman climbing north with a dead woman's cloak pin. A ferryman remembered your face because you paid too precisely — thieves always do, they count everything twice." He tilted his head. "And one of Mother Ivenna's people, may she rest, kept notes. Very thorough notes. People who guard secrets always do; it's the first thing I look for."

"You're alone?"

"I am always alone. It's why I'm good at what I do." Corvane glanced at the tiny fire, then at the stone beside it, and sat down without invitation, arranging his coat with the care of a man settling into a box seat. "Sit, please. I haven't come to take anything from you. That will be obvious shortly, and I'd rather not conduct this conversation with you crouched over that knife like a dog over a bone."

"I've seen what happens to people who sit down with you."

"Yes," Corvane said pleasantly. "They get paid."

Above them, faintly, a pebble clicked loose and rattled down the scree. Sera, listening. Edmund let three seconds pass — long enough for her to know he knew, long enough for her to decide whether to put an arrow through this stranger's throat. She didn't. Not yet. He could feel her deciding.

He sat. He kept the knife.

* * *

Corvane produced a flask from inside his coat — silver, dented, older-looking than its owner — and poured something into the cap that steamed and smelled of plum and pepper-wine. He drank, sighed with genuine contentment, and regarded the stars for a moment as if consulting them.

"Do you remember our transaction? In Vael. The merchant's grief."

"I remember everything I sell," Edmund said. "It's the job."

"Mm. And did it trouble you? The widow's sorrow. Selling a woman's love for her dead husband so that a bored magistrate could wear it like a coat on winter evenings."

"It pays better if it doesn't trouble me."

"That," Corvane said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said to me in a year, and I pay people to be honest." He set the cap down and laced his gloved fingers together. "I'm going to tell you a story now, and I want you to listen, because unlike most stories, this one is about you, and every word of it is true, and I can prove it."

Edmund said nothing. The map pressed against his ribs. He thought of Sera above him in the dark with her bow, and of Rell somewhere behind them with his engine and his quiet voice, and of the burned village, and of the dream-fragments that had been coming to him since the Choir's hold — the burning tower, the smell of pine pitch, a woman's voice singing something low and urgent that he could never quite hold onto past waking.

"Twenty-three years ago," Corvane began, "a man came to me in Vael. Not for coin — I didn't deal in extraction then, only acquisition, and he knew it. He came to make a deposit. He wanted me to keep something for him. Something he couldn't destroy and couldn't risk keeping." Corvane's eyes came back from the stars and settled on Edmund with a weight that felt physical. "A memory. A very large one. An entire childhood, extracted whole — every year of a boy's first eleven winters, taken out cleanly, by a craftsman, at the family's own request and the family's own cost."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, wind moved through the pines like water.

"The boy's name was in the memory," Corvane went on softly. "So was the tower. So was the woman singing, and the reason she sang, and the name of the house he was born in, and the shape of his mother's hands, and the oath his father swore over a sleeping child — that if the empire or the loyalists ever came asking after the blood of Thornfell, there would be no blood left to find. No memories. No name. A boy who woke up knowing a trade and nothing else, with coin enough to learn it and no past to contradict the learning."

Edmund discovered that his mouth had gone dry. "You're lying."

"I have never once lied to a seller," Corvane said, and there was, for the first time, something almost gentle under the silk of his voice. "Bad practice. Lies devalue the goods. Ask anyone in my profession." He reached slowly — telegraphing every inch of the motion, the way one moves near a strange dog — into his breast pocket, and drew out a case. Not leather. Glass and brass, a case the size of a prayer book, and inside it, nested in black velvet, a vial.

But not one of Edmund's little trade vials, the thumb-length phials he filled in back alleys. This one was long as a forearm, its glass thick and old, its stopper sealed with wax stamped by a sigil Edmund's body recognized before his mind did — a crown made of thorns, rendered in a style three centuries out of fashion.

Inside the glass, the memory moved. They all moved — he'd known that since his first week in the trade, though collectors preferred their clients ignorant of it. Light within light, slow currents of color, silver and gold and one deep vein of green that pulsed like a heartbeat. Like a forest. Like summer in a high green valley between mountains.

Edmund's hand rose without his permission. He stopped it halfway.

"You feel it," Corvane observed. "Of course you do. You have the gift; proximity is enough. What does it show you?"

"Nothing." His voice came out rough. "It shows me nothing. It's just light."

"Liar," Corvane said, with real warmth. "It's showing you a kitchen. Stone floor, copper pots, morning sun through a window facing east toward a peak with a broken crown of rock. There's flour on someone's hands. You're very small and you're standing on someone's feet while they work, and they're laughing." He reclosed the case, and the loss of it hit Edmund like a door shutting. "That is eleven seconds of what's in that vial. Eleven seconds out of eleven years."

The wind had stopped. Or Edmund had stopped hearing it.

"Why," he managed. "Why are you telling me this. Why do you *have* it."

"Because the man who deposited it paid me for three things. Safekeeping, secrecy, and delivery — delivery only to the heir himself, upon proof, upon his own request. He was very specific. He said: *if he ever asks for it back, it means he's found the road home, and then he'll need to know who he is.*" Corvane spread his pale-gloved hands. "I am a man who honors his contracts, Master Winterbourne. It is the entire foundation of my reputation, and my reputation is the only currency that cannot be stolen."

"And the price."

"Ah." Corvane smiled again, and this time there was no apology in it at all. "There it is. You see, I knew you'd ask. The thief always asks about price before he asks about the thing itself — it's how I knew, twenty years ago in Vael, watching you lift a merchant's grief with those careful little hands of yours, exactly whose son you were. Your father asked about price first, too."

"Corvane."

"The map," Corvane said. "Deliver the map to me. Whole, unopened by anyone but yourself — I know it resists you, don't look surprised, I know a great many things about what that map does and does not permit — delivered into my hands, here or wherever we next meet, and the vial is yours. Every winter of your childhood. Your mother's voice. Your father's face. Your name — your true one, the one that isn't Winterbourne." He leaned forward slightly, and the lens at his throat caught the coals and burned. "Everything that was taken from you, returned. All I require is that you give up the one thing standing between this empire and a war, and give it to the only man in Vael who wants it for no reason worse than to own it."

"To *own* it," Edmund repeated. "You'd hang kingdoms on a wall."

"I'd hang them in a *case*, properly lit, away from hands." For a moment something flickered behind the collector's mild face — not madness, Edmund realized, but conviction, cold and polished as the lens he wore. "Do you know what crowns do, Master Winterbourne? Living ones? They don't rest on heads. They *drive*. Every king who touched the Last Crown thought he was choosing, and every choice he made afterward was chosen *for* him by three hundred years of dead men whispering through gold. Your own family understood that. It's why they cut their own son hollow rather than let the blood answer the call." He tapped the glass case once, lightly, with one gloved finger. "In my keeping, it drives no one. It sits in the dark and hums to no one. I am not offering to use it, boy. I am offering to end it — the way you end a fire, the way you end a song. Put it where no voice can reach."

"And the empire? Rell? The Grey Company tearing the mountains apart — you think your glass case stops them?"

"The empire," Corvane said, "cannot want what cannot be found. And once the map is mine, it will not be found." He said it simply, the way men say things they have arranged to be true.

Edmund stood. His legs felt borrowed, unsteady, someone else's legs. Above them on the ridge, he heard — or imagined he heard — the whisper-soft draw of an arrow half-nocked, held, released. Sera, waiting on his word.

"You've given me nothing," he said. "A story and a pretty vial. My father's contract — prove it. If you're so scrupulous, show me proof."

Something moved through Corvane's eyes then that Edmund had never seen in them, in all the years of buying and selling in that lamplit salon: hesitation. Genuine, human hesitation, gone almost as fast as it came.

"One fragment," Corvane said quietly. "Free of charge. As a mark of faith — and because, whatever you choose, you deserve to carry one true thing into that choice." He opened the case again, and this time he drew the vial out, and held it between them, and did something with his thumb against the wax seal — a pressure, a whispered syllable, a craftsman's trick — and the glass warmed and brightened, and the memory inside reached out the way memories did when they were handled by someone with the gift, and it found Edmund like a hook finding meat.

One image. One breath.

A tower room, high in a house of grey stone. Firelight. A man kneeling to bring his face level with a child's — a man with Edmund's own deep-set eyes and a beard going early grey, and his hands, his broad scarred hands, resting on the child's shoulders, and his voice saying words that Edmund heard across twenty-three years and eleven thousand paces and the ruin of everything:

*"Whatever they tell you you are — whatever name they give you, whatever trade they put in your empty hands — you are the last leaf of a tree worth saving. And someday, if the Crown calls, you will have to choose what a Winterbourne—" what a— "*

The name dissolved. The image dissolved. The vial dimmed, and Corvane sealed it away in velvet and brass and closed the case with a click that sounded, in the enormous silence of the mountain night, like a lock turning.

Edmund was shaking. He stood in the coal-light with a knife in his hand and his whole self trembling around a hole shaped exactly like the rest of that sentence, and Corvane watched him with something that was almost — almost — pity.

"Consider it," the collector said, rising, brushing snow from his immaculate coat. "Not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and so should the archer on the ridge, who has been very patient and whom I salute." He inclined his head toward the darkness above, courteously, horribly, and Edmund's blood went cold at the confirmation. "Three days. I'll be at the shrine below the Weeping Gate — you'll find the road knows the way, even if you don't. Bring the map, and walk out of these mountains a whole man. Don't—" and here Corvane paused, gathering his coat around him against the wind, and finished softly, "—don't die hollow for a kingdom that would eat you alive."

Then he walked back down the switchback into the dark, unhurried, light as snowfall, until the night folded over him and he was gone.

Edmund stood alone for a long time. Then he took out the map, and unwrapped it, and looked at it by coal-light — the inked peaks, the marker at the Weeping Gate, the route that refused to be stolen, that stayed in the world's memory and out of his — and understood, all at once, with terrible clarity, why it had resisted him.

The map wasn't guarding itself from a thief.

It was waiting for an heir.

Above him, silent as ash, Sera came down the scree, bow in hand, and looked at his face, and stopped.

"Edmund," she said. "Who was that man?"

He opened his mouth to say *no one*, the old reflex, the professional reflex — and found he couldn't. Not anymore.

"He knew my mother's laugh," he said.
