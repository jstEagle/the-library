## 12. Who Was Edmund Winterbourne

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Corvane spoke again.

They had made camp in the shell of a shepherd's cot high above the treeline — a dry-stone ruin that smelled of old smoke and sheep and cold. Sera slept sitting upright against the far wall, her knife loose in its sheath, one hand curled around it even in exhaustion. She did not trust the collector. Neither did Edmund. But Corvane had walked into their camp alone, unarmed, hands open like a man approaching a horse he didn't want to spook, and he had brought food, and fuel, and above all he had brought the one thing Edmund could not buy, steal, or imagine: a small glass vial, no longer than his thumb, filled with a slow silver drift of light.

*Yours,* Corvane had said. *A taste.*

Edmund held it now between two fingers, turning it so the firelight caught the suspended gleam inside. He knew vials the way a priest knows scripture. He knew their weights, their colors, the way memory-silver moved when it was fresh versus when it had been kept too long. This one was old. Decades old, at least. The silver within it had gone thick as honey, moving only when he breathed on the glass.

And it was warm. Not from the fire. Warm the way a hand is warm.

"You've been staring at it for an hour," Corvane said. He sat across the hearth with his long coat spread around him like a roosting bird's wings, gray-haired, elegant, utterly at ease in a ruin at four thousand feet. "Most men would have drunk it by now."

"Most men know what they're drinking."

"And you don't." Corvane smiled without warmth. "That is precisely my point, Master Winterbourne."

*Don't drink it.* Sera had said it before sleep took her, low and fierce, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise. *He doesn't give gifts. He sets hooks.*

She was right. Edmund knew she was right. He turned the vial over once more, felt the warmth pulse faintly against his fingertips like a second heartbeat, and set it down on the stone beside his knee — deliberately, visibly, the way a man lays down a card he intends to win with later rather than now.

"Tell me why it's warm," he said.

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the gap in the roof, a thin keening sound, like something mourning. Somewhere down the valley, very far off, an eagle cried once and went silent.

"Do you know how memory-taking actually works?" Corvane asked instead. "Not the trade patter. Not what the guilds tell their cutters to recite while they work. The truth of it."

"I know what I do. I reach where I'm not wanted, I find the shape of a thing, and I lift it out whole."

"Yes. And where do you find it? What is the place you reach into?" Corvane leaned forward, and the firelight carved deep shadows under his cheekbones, making his face briefly skull-like. "Every cutter I've ever employed described it differently. A dark room. Deep water. A corridor of doors. But they all describe the same thing: a place that isn't theirs. A vault that belongs to someone else. They reach into another mind and take from shelves that were built before they arrived."

Edmund said nothing. His mouth had gone dry.

"Now ask yourself," Corvane said softly, "what kind of man has a vault already built inside him. Shelves. Doors. A dark room with a lock on it — before any thief ever came knocking."

The wind keened. The fire popped. Edmund became aware, with terrible slowness, of a sensation he had carried his whole life and never named: that hollow feeling behind his breastbone, the sense of standing in an empty house where the furniture had been arranged once, by someone, for some purpose, and then taken away. Every memory-thief felt their own minds as strange territory. He had always assumed it was the trade. The cost of reaching into other people's heads was that your own began to feel borrowed.

Hadn't it?

"Your childhood," Corvane said, "was not simply sold, Master Winterbourne. It was *removed*. Surgically. By professionals. There is a difference, and your hands have known it your entire career even if your mind refused to read them." He gestured at the vial. "When you lift a grief from a merchant, the space it leaves is soft. New-cut. It heals over with fog. But when you press your own mind — you've told me this, years ago, in Vael, drunk enough to be honest — when you go looking for yourself, you find walls. Cut stone. Old work. Someone walled up a wing of your own house and left you living in the rest."

Edmund's fingers had closed around the vial without his deciding to. It pulsed against his palm like a trapped bird.

"The woman's voice," he said hoarsely. "In Chapter Seven—" He stopped himself; there were no chapters in a life. "On the road through Thornfell. In the burned village. I remembered — fragments. A tower burning. A woman singing. Stone under my hands, and someone telling me to hold on, hold *still*, little one—"

"Your mother," Corvane said.

The word landed in the silence of the ruined cot like a stone dropped down a well. Edmund heard it fall a long way before anything answered.

"She sang," Corvane went on quietly. "Thornfell women sang the old mountain-lays, the ones the empire banned three hundred years ago and never quite succeeded in killing. Your mother sang them to you in a tower on the eastern face, in the last year of the war, while the imperial engines climbed the pass below. You remember the song. That fragment survived because she hid it inside a lullaby's rhythm, and rhythm is the last thing erasure reaches." He tilted his head. "You've hummed it your whole life, haven't you? Without knowing why. Some tuneless thing you catch yourself doing when you're tired."

Edmund realized his lips were parted. That somewhere in his chest, barely voiced beneath the wind, he *had* been humming. Three notes, falling, rising, falling. His whole life — every landlady in Vael who'd complained about the tenant who whistled the same six notes over and over, every night, like a man praying in a language he'd lost.

"What was her name," he whispered.

"Maren Winterbourne. Born Maren of the line of Aldric Thornfell, ninth of that name." Corvane let the firelight move across his face. "Which makes you, Edmund — Edmund of the line of Aldric, tenth of that name. The Last Crown does not merely sit in its vault waiting for whoever is clever enough to find it. Crowns of the old making are bloodbound. They answer to the line that made them. And every other throat that line ever fed is closed now — burned at Thornfell, purged in the valleys, scattered so thin the empire believed the tree dead." He smiled, and it was almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "The tree wasn't dead. It was pruned. One branch, hidden in a city of thieves, stealing memories for coin."

The vial trembled in Edmund's grip. Or perhaps it was his hand. Perhaps it was both.

"That's not possible," he said. "If I were—if anyone knew—the empire—"

"Would have killed you before you could walk. Yes. Which is why nobody knew. Which is why *you* don't know." Corvane rose, unhurried, and paced to the broken window, where the night sky hung vast and salted with stars above the black shoulder of the mountain. "Consider the elegance of it. Who hides a prince? Not in a palace. Not among loyalists who might one day remember what they're guarding. You hide him in the one place no one will ever look — inside himself. Take everything. Give him nothing. Let him grow up a stranger wearing his own name. Let him become a man whose entire talent is *not knowing*. The perfect disguise isn't a mask, Master Winterbourne. It's an empty room."

Edmund looked down at the vial. Silver drifted behind the glass, thick and slow, and for the first time in his life he understood what he was holding — not merchandise, not leverage, but a splinter of himself, pried loose by someone else's tools and sold into a collector's cabinet like a coin from a dead king's purse.

"Who took them," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. Flat. Strange. "My memories. My childhood. Say it."

Corvane turned from the window. "Does it matter?"

"*Say it.*"

"Your mother did." The words fell clean and merciless. "With help from the Choir — Mother Ivenna's predecessor, and two cutters whose names died with them. The tower was falling, Edmund. Rell's master's master had found the eastern pass, and there was no escape for a woman and a child who both knew what the boy was. So she chose the only hiding place left." He paused. "She took your past so that your future would survive it. I am told—" and here, for the first time, something flickered across the collector's composure, something almost human, almost sorry, "—I am told she wept the whole time. And that the last thing she put into the vault before she sealed it, the last shelf she stocked, was her own face. So that someday, if you ever got the key back, you would know who had locked the door. And why."

Edmund could not breathe. The ruined cot, the fire, the sleeping woman with her knife — all of it had gone distant, painted on the far side of glass. Inside him, behind those walls Corvane had named, something shifted. Not opened — he would have known, would have *felt* an opening, the way he felt every theft — but shifted, pressed, like a weight leaning against a door from the other side.

The humming had stopped. In its absence the silence was enormous.

"So," he said finally, and his voice cracked on the single syllable, and he hated it, and let it crack. "The Crown answers to me."

"It has waited three hundred years for you."

"And everyone who wants it — the Choir, the empire, *you* — needs me alive. Needs me willing. Because it won't sing for thieves or emperors or grieving zealots." He looked up, and found his own face had gone cold and professional, the merchant's mask sliding home over the ruins beneath, and he was grateful for it, grateful for the hollow craft of it. "So this is what I am. Not a thief. A key. Everything I've done — every memory lifted, every grief sold, every coin taken for the contents of strangers' hearts — none of it was a life. It was a man pretending to be empty so well he convinced himself."

"Or," Corvane said, "a man surviving. As his mother intended. Do not confuse the cage with the crime, Edmund. You were locked in that room by love, not malice."

"Love." Edmund laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "She walled me off from myself and you kept the key in a cabinet in Vael, and you call it love."

"I call it what happened. What you call it is the first choice that has ever truly been yours." Corvane crouched, bringing his eyes level with Edmund's, and produced from his coat a slim case of black lacquer — and inside it, nested in velvet, a dozen vials. Silver light. Old silver, decades old, moving like slow weather. "Her face. The tower. The song entire. Your father's voice — yes, you had one; he died holding the gate so the cutters could finish their work. Your first snowfall. Your name, spoken aloud, the way she said it." He laid the case on the stone between them, open, glowing faintly like a reliquary. "This is the free fragment's family. The rest of you — the full restoration, everything the Choir's people took that night, everything I have spent thirty years gathering piece by piece — waits in Vael. One delivery. The map, the location, your cooperation. And you walk out of my shop a whole man, Edmund of the line of Aldric, with thirty years of your own life returned and a crown waiting to know your hand."

Behind them, steel whispered against leather.

Sera stood at the wall, awake — had been awake, Edmund realized, for how long? — with her knife drawn and her eyes fixed on the case of vials as if it were a nest of snakes. Her face in the firelight was terrible and still.

"He's lying," she said. "Or worse — he's telling the truth. Either way it's the same trap."

"Is it?" Corvane did not rise, did not reach for any weapon. He simply looked at Edmund, patient as stone. "Ask her what the Choir intends to do with you, once you've opened the vault for them. Ask what Mother Ivenna plans for the last heir of Thornfell once the Crown is on his head — whether a restored kingdom needs a king who remembers being a memory-thief, or a king remade into what the Choir requires. Everyone in this valley has a plan for what goes inside you, Master Winterbourne." He gestured at the vials, at the warm little light of the first one still clutched in Edmund's fist. "The only question left is whose version of you gets to live. Mine. Hers. Or the empty man's."

Sera took one step forward, knife low. "Touch him and—"

"You'll what? Kill the last hope of everything your Choir bled for?" Corvane smiled at her, mild and unbothered. "Go ahead. Explain to Ivenna's ghost."

But Edmund wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at the vial in his hand — the warm one, the first one, the taste — and slowly, deliberately, watching both of them watch him, he raised it to the firelight one last time.

Then he drank it.

It went through him like winter water, like a bell rung inside his bones — and the world tore open. A tower, burning against a violet sky. Stone hot under small palms. Smoke that tasted of pine and iron. And a woman kneeling before him, soot-streaked, her hands cupping his face, singing the six falling notes even as tears cut channels down her ash-gray cheeks — and her mouth shaping words the memory was too damaged to carry, except the last of them, the ones she pressed hardest, the ones that had survived thirty years in glass:

*Forget us, little king. Forget us and live.*

The vial slipped from his fingers and shattered on the stone.

Edmund knelt in the ashes of a shepherd's cot, thirty years old and five years old all at once, weeping for a mother whose face was already fading from him again — because one vial was a taste, only a taste, and Corvane had told him so from the beginning, hadn't he, the hook baited with exactly enough —

Sera was beside him. Her hand on his back. Her voice shaking with something between fury and fear: *"What did it show you?"*

Edmund lifted his head. Through the blur, past Corvane's waiting smile, toward the black mass of the mountain looming over them all — the mountain that had been calling him since the Weeping Gate, that had been calling him, he understood now, his *entire life*.

"It showed me who I am," he said. "And it showed me why I can never let any of you decide that for me."

Corvane began, softly, to laugh.
