## 13. Sera's Choice

Sera had learned to count her griefs the way other people counted coins — by weight, not number.

There was the grief of Ashford burning: one. Her mother's voice in the smoke: two. The Choir's hold going up in flame with Mother Ivenna still singing inside it: three, and that one she carried like a stone sewn beneath her ribs. She had learned that griefs did not lighten when you named them. They only became easier to carry, the way a soldier's pack grows familiar against the spine until you forget it is there at all — until you set it down, and discover how crooked your back has become.

Edmund Winterbourne was a new kind of weight. He was the first one she had chosen.

She watched him now from across the fire they'd built in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, high on the shoulders of Thornfell where even the imperial riders' horses balked at the scree. He sat with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the way he'd been sitting since Corvane left them. Since the words. *The last heir of the Thornfell line. The Crown will answer to his blood alone.*

His palms were empty. His face was the face of a man who had opened his own chest and found someone else's heart beating there.

*You could have told me,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because he had told her — last night on the snowline, shaking, honest for the first time since she'd put a knife to his throat among the ashes of her village. He had confessed the memory-trade, the vials, the merchants and widows and deserters he'd emptied like cellars. He had confessed the hole where his childhood should have been. What he had not known — what none of them had known — was that he'd been robbed before he'd ever learned to rob.

Corvane had known. That was the thing that made her teeth ache. The collector had carried the truth of Edmund Winterbourne around inside himself like a jewel in a locked box, waiting for the moment it would be worth the most. And now he'd shown them a single facet of it — free, he'd said, *free of charge* — the way a fisherman chums the water.

"Say it," Edmund said quietly. He hadn't looked up. "Whatever it is. You've been circling it since dusk."

Sera fed a stick to the fire. Sparks climbed toward the stars and died halfway, which felt about right.

"Ivenna used to say the Crown doesn't choose kings," Sera said. "Kings choose the Crown, and the Crown chooses what they'll pay." She turned the stick, watching the bark curl and blacken. "She believed if the right blood took it up, the price would be different. A just price. She believed the old kingdom could come back whole."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting across from the right blood," Sera said, "and he's a man who sold other people's yesterdays for drinking money."

Edmund flinched. It was small — a tightening around the eyes — but she saw it, because she had spent three years learning to read men in the half-second before they decided whether to lie. He didn't lie. That was the worst of it. He simply nodded, as though she'd confirmed something he'd already written on his own headstone.

"Yes," he said. "That's who I am."

"It's not all of who you are."

"It's enough of who I am."

Sera stood. The cold came through her boots immediately; it always did, above the snowline, an honest cold that asked nothing and forgave nothing. She walked to the edge of the hollow and looked north, down the black shoulder of the mountain, to where the valley lay hidden. Somewhere down there were the survivors from the burned villages, the families the Choir had been shepherding toward the high passes for a month. Somewhere behind them, Captain Rell was climbing with his grey company and his memory-eating engine, patient as frost.

Three roads. She had been walking them all day in her head, and every one of them went through the same man.

* *

The first road was the Choir's road. It was the easiest to see because she had been born onto it, more or less — born into a village that kept the old feast days and sang the ash-songs at midwinter, raised on stories of the Thornfell kings who had bound the mountain's wild magic into mercy instead of war. When the empire burned Ashford, the Choir had found her kneeling in the ruin of her mother's kitchen, and Mother Ivenna had crouched down beside her and said, *Grief is a coal, child. You can warm your hands on it or you can burn a house down. Either way, it's yours.* The Choir had taught her the passes, the signs, the songs that were also maps. The Choir had given her mission in place of meaning, and for three years that substitution had held.

But Ivenna was dead, and the hold was ash, and the Choir was eleven people scattered across four valleys with no leader, no vault, and no plan beyond the map folded against Edmund's ribs. And the Choir's road, followed honestly, led here: to Edmund taking up the Crown and restoring the kingdom, becoming the vessel of everything Ivenna had prayed for. A king made from a memory-thief. A crown answering to blood that didn't know itself.

Could she kneel to that? Could she sing the investiture hymns over a man whose pockets rattled with stolen years?

She thought of Ivenna again — really thought of her, past the icon she'd become since the fire — and remembered that the old woman had lied too. Small lies, necessary lies, the lies of anyone keeping a secret for three centuries. Ivenna had never told Sera what the Crown cost its wearers. Never mentioned the visions, the hunger of it, the way it ate its kings slow. Maybe she hadn't known. Maybe she'd known perfectly well and judged the price worth paying, the way the empire judged its engine worth the minds it emptied.

Everyone believes their own cruelty is arithmetic, Sera thought. Everyone balances their ledger with themselves on the profitable side.

The Choir's road wanted her to hand Edmund to the Crown the way Corvane wanted to hand him his memories — as a tool, a key, a means. She looked back at him across the fire. He was watching her now, saying nothing, giving her the room she needed, and that patience of his — that terrible, professional patience, the patience of a man who waited out other people's pain for coin — was being used, tonight, for free.

* *

The second road was vengeance, and vengeance was simple, and that was precisely why she didn't trust it.

Rell had burned nothing himself — that was the obscenity of it. Rell had never struck a match. He sent others, or he sent engines, or he sent men with orders so clean they felt like mercy: *we are only removing dangerous memories, we are only preventing a war.* Sera had heard one of the grey-cloaks say it while she lay under a dead goat in the ruins of Ashford, breathing through her sleeve. *We're sparing them, brother. A mind without the past can't grieve.* As though the mind were a house you could strip for the owner's good. As though emptying a man were saving him.

If she took the second road, she knew exactly how it would go. She would use Edmund. She would let him take up the Crown — because the Crown was power, and power could kill Rell and break the Grey Company and burn the engine and everyone who had ever worn imperial grey — and afterward, when the war was won, she would find out what the Crown had left of him. She had seen what the mountain's magic did to those it moved through. She had seen the Choir's oldest members, the ones who'd touched the deep stone too long, sitting in corners smoothing smooth stones for hours, smiling at nothing.

Vengeance would spend him. Vengeance would spend him gladly, and call the price fair, and she would be no better than Rell with his engine, no better than Corvane with his ledger of souls — a woman who had found a man made of other people's memories and decided he was currency.

And underneath that — underneath the strategy and the arithmetic — was the thing she had been refusing to look at since the snowline, since he'd knelt in the snow and told her the truth with his hands flat and open like a man showing he held no weapon.

She trusted him.

Not blindly. Not anymore. But trust re-forged is stronger than trust first cast; any smith will tell you that folded steel holds a truer edge. He had lied to her by omission for weeks, and then he had stopped, and the stopping had cost him everything he pretended to be. She had watched him choose honesty with no guarantee it would buy him anything but her knife. Men did not do that often. Men she had loved had done it never.

*I've begun to trust him,* she thought, and the thought frightened her worse than Rell's trackers, because trust was the one door through which grief could enter twice.

* *

"You're doing the thing," Edmund said.

"What thing?"

"Where you decide my life in silence and don't tell me the verdict." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "I used to charge for that. People paying good silver to have their choices taken away. Easier than choosing."

"Is that what you want?" Sera came back to the fire and sat, close enough to feel its heat on her shins. "Someone to choose for you? Because Corvane offered to give you yourself, Edmund. Your whole past, restored. Everything they took. You could know who you were before any of us. And you told him no."

"I told him not yet."

"That's not nothing."

"No," he agreed. "It's not nothing. It's the opposite of nothing. It's—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands. In the firelight she could see how thin he'd gotten on the climb, the sharp architecture of his cheeks, the shadows like bruises under his eyes. "When I take a memory, I feel it. All of it, once, like a wave breaking. Grief, love, shame — whatever the person paid me to lift. And for a moment I understand their whole life from the inside, and then it fades, and I'm me again, and I'm glad when it fades." His voice dropped. "What terrifies me isn't getting my past back. It's finding out what's in it. That I was someone I can't live with. That the erasure wasn't theft. That it was mercy, and I've been walking around for twenty years wearing a mercy I never earned."

Sera was quiet a long time.

Then she said, "My mother used to bake bread on festival days before dawn. I remember the sound of the oven door. That's the memory I keep returning to, since Ashford. Not her face. The oven door." She stared into the flames. "I would sell years of my life to hear it again. Real years. So when I say I understand wanting your past — believe that I understand it from the inside."

"Sera—"

"But I also know what Corvane is," she went on, harder now, because the tenderness in her own voice had startled her and she needed to get ahead of it. "He showed you one truth for free because a partial truth is bait. He wants the map. He wants the Crown found, or found first, or found controllable. Every gift he gives moves you toward his hand. So here is what I need from you, Edmund Winterbourne, heir of Thornfell or thief of Vael or whoever you turn out to be." She leaned forward. "I need you to promise me that you will never trade with him. Not your memories, not the map, not the choice of what happens at the Weeping Gate. Whatever you learn about who you were, you learn on your own terms or not at all. Can you promise that?"

He looked at her for a long moment. The fire popped. High above, wind moved through the crags with a low tonal hum — the singing stone, the Choir called it, the mountain dreaming aloud — and for a heartbeat it sounded almost like voices.

"I promise," he said.

"And if the Crown offers you the same bargain? If it shows you your childhood, your name, your mother's oven door — everything, all at once, in exchange for wearing it?" Her throat tightened. "Because it will. Ivenna told me that much before the end. The Crown trades in memory, Edmund. It's made of it. It buys kings the same way you bought grief from widows."

Something moved behind his eyes — fear, yes, but under the fear a bleak recognition, the expression of a man meeting his own reflection in a mirror he'd been avoiding for years.

"Then I suppose," he said slowly, "I'm finally on the other side of my own counter. Let's hope I drive a harder bargain than my customers did."

"That's not funny."

"No," he said. "It's not."

* *

She did not sleep. Toward the coldest hour she rose, checked the map case where it rode against his side even in sleep — his hand curled around the strap like a child's — and climbed to the mouth of the hollow to watch the dark.

The mountain breathed below her. Far to the south, a pinprick of torchlight moved along the lower smuggler's road, steady, unhurried, wrong. Too disciplined for refugees. Too few for a supply train. She counted, lost the light behind a ridge, counted again when it emerged. Nine points of flame, moving in step.

Grey Company discipline. She'd know the cadence anywhere; she'd watched it burn her village.

They had perhaps a day. Less, if the trackers were reading the same stone-song the Choir used, and Rell's engine could pull routes out of captured scouts the way Edmund pulled memories out of merchants. Which meant the Weeping Gate tomorrow, or not at all. Which meant the flooded galleries, the singing stone, the vault of kings at the bottom of the world — with nine torches climbing behind them and the map's final marker still unread.

Sera stood in the freezing dark and made her choice properly this time, not as an accident of survival, not as a debt, not as the momentum of three years of grief pointed at a convenient target. She chose the man, knowing what he'd been. She chose the mission, knowing it might kill him. She chose vengeance last, set down like a heavy pack she might need later but refused to let steer.

And she understood, standing there, that this was the difference between faith and blindness. Blindness had followed Ivenna into a fire. Faith would walk beside Edmund into the dark with her eyes wide open, ready to stop him with her body or her knife if the Crown began to eat him — and ready, God forgive her, to follow him in if it came to that.

Behind her, Edmund stirred in his sleep and murmured something. A name, maybe. A woman's voice, he'd said — fragments of a burning tower. His erased childhood, leaking through the cracks Corvane had pried open.

Sera turned. Down in the dark, the nine torches had gone out.

All nine at once.

They weren't stopping to camp. They were dousing their lights to climb unseen.

"Wake up," she said, already kicking snow over the fire. "We're out of mountain."
