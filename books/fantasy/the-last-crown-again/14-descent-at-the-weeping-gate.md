## 14. Descent at the Weeping Gate

The map changed when the mountain came into view.

Edmund had carried it for weeks now — through smuggler roads and ash fields, past burned villages and frozen rivers — and it had always been leather and ink, stubborn and silent, refusing his touch the way a locked door refuses a thief. But on the morning they crested the last ridge before Thornfell's peak, he unrolled it across his knees in the shadow of a wind-bent pine, and the ink was moving.

Not flowing. *Breathing.* The lines of the mountain's interior shifted like veins beneath skin, the marked route pulsing with a faint blue luminescence that hadn't been there yesterday. Where once the map had shown only the peak and its approaches, it now showed what lay beneath: galleries, shafts, flooded chambers rendered in silver thread, descending farther than Edmund could follow with his eye. At the heart of it all, a single unmarked space — a blankness in the ink, as if the map itself declined to say what waited there.

"The Weeping Gate," Sera said beside him. She said it the way people said the names of the dead.

"You've heard of it."

"My grandmother sang about it. A lullaby." Sera's breath fogged in the thin cold air; her eyes never left the peak. "I thought it was a story to frighten children away from the mines. *Don't go under the mountain, little one, the mountain weeps for what it keeps.*" She glanced at him. "I didn't think I'd ever be the little one."

Below them, the valley of Thornfell opened like a cupped hand — terraced slopes gone wild with three centuries of neglect, a river braiding silver through the floor of it, and scattered among the ruins, smoke. Refugee fires. Families who had fled the empire's advance and sheltered in the old kingdom's bones because there was nowhere else. Rell would come for them eventually. Rell came for everything eventually.

And Rell was close. They'd seen the Grey Company's outriders two days ago, a thin dark line moving along the western pass like a scar knitting itself shut. The trackers moved wrong — too smooth, too coordinated, men who had given up their own rhythms to become instruments. And where they walked, Edmund felt it against his skin like a change in pressure: the engine. Whatever Rell used to pull memories out of living minds left a wake in the world, a flatness, a silence where feeling should be.

He'd felt it once before, from a distance, when they passed through a village the Company had already visited. The people there had smiled and answered questions and remembered nothing worth keeping. Their eyes had been clean. Empty-clean, like scrubbed stone.

"We go in tonight," Edmund said. "Before they reach the valley floor."

Sera nodded and began rolling the map, but Edmund caught her wrist — gently — and held it a moment longer than he needed to. She looked at him. He almost told her then, what he'd been carrying since Corvane's fragment had cracked his identity open like an egg: *I'm the heir. The Crown will know me. I don't know if that makes me its key or its prisoner.*

Instead he said, "If something happens down there—"

"Nothing is going to happen."

"Sera."

She pulled her wrist free, not unkindly, and finished rolling the map. "If something happens down there," she said, "you'll still owe me eleven years of my life I spent learning to hate the empire instead of learning to live. So don't die cheap." She stood. "Come on. There's a mine entrance above the tree line. My grandmother's song named it."

*What song?*

But she was already walking, and the mountain waited, and some questions you save for the dark where they belong.

* * *

The entrance was a wound in the mountainside half-swallowed by scree, and it breathed.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed as they squeezed through the gap — the air moving, slow and steady, in and out, warm and mineral-sweet, so that the mountain seemed less a dead thing than a sleeping animal. His lantern flame leaned toward the depths as though drawn by the inhale.

Inside, the passage descended in long switchbacks cut by hands, not water — stairs worn to shallow bowls by feet that had climbed them centuries ago. Old kingdom work. The walls were dressed stone veined with something that caught the lantern light and gave back a dull gold, and every few paces a carved face looked out from the rock: kings, Edmund guessed, or the men who served them, their features smoothed by time until they all wore the same expression, which was patience.

His skin prickled. Not fear. Something older. Recognition without content — the body remembering what the mind had been emptied of.

"This way feels right," he said, and hated how much it sounded like a confession.

"It should." Sera's voice echoed strangely, doubled and tripled off unseen surfaces. "You were born under this mountain. Somewhere in there." She pointed down. "Corvane's fragment—"

"Was four sentences and a name. It isn't a childhood, Sera. It's a receipt."

They walked on. The stair gave way to a gallery, the gallery to a hall whose ceiling vanished into darkness overhead, and here the first singing stone found them.

It started as a hum at the edge of hearing, the note you mistake for your own blood. Then a second voice joined it, a fifth above, and Edmund stopped and raised the lantern and saw them: pillars of pale stone rising from floor to unseen roof, dozens of them, each one trembling — actually trembling, minutely, visibly — and each one giving off a tone. Together they made a chord that was not music exactly but was close enough to hurt, a chord like a word half-remembered from a language spoken before you were born.

"What are they?" His voice came out hushed without his permission.

"Load-bearing." Sera touched one pillar and the tone bent around her fingers, went sweet and minor. "The Choir's oldest songs are mining songs, did you know that? We thought we were keeping the old kingdom alive with our voices." Her laugh had no humor in it. "Turns out we were practicing."

Edmund laid his palm flat against a pillar and felt the vibration crawl up his arm and settle behind his sternum, and for one vertiginous instant he was somewhere else — a tower room, firelight, a woman's voice singing this exact chord an octave higher while small hands pressed over his ears—

He snatched his hand back. The vision folded up like a fan. His heart slammed.

"What?" Sera was watching him. "What did you see?"

"A woman singing." He kept his voice level. Professional. The old mask slid on easily; it had always fit too well. "Firelight. Nothing else."

"That's more than you had yesterday."

"That's the problem." He turned away, toward the downward passage. "Every piece I get back is a debt someone else paid. Let's keep moving. The Gate should be near the flooded galleries according to the map."

"And after the Gate?"

"After the Gate," Edmund said, "we'll see what the mountain wants to remember."

* * *

The water began as sound.

They heard it before they saw it — a vast dripping, echoing from everywhere and nowhere, a rhythm too irregular to be natural and too patient to be anything built. Then the passage opened into the first flooded gallery, and Edmund understood the name.

The ceiling wept. Water gathered along the vaulted stone in beads and threads and fell in slow silver lines into black pools below, and each drop struck the water with a small clear note, and thousands of drops together made a sound like rain heard from inside a coffin. The galleries stretched ahead in linked chambers, the water waist-deep in places, chest-deep in others, and the lantern light couldn't find the bottom anywhere.

"Cold?" Sera asked, already wading in, sword belted high.

"Yes."

"Good. Cold keeps you honest."

They went in. The water took Edmund at the thighs, then the waist, and the cold closed around him like a fist, and the singing of the drop-notes changed with every step they took, rippling outward, so that the whole drowned hall knew where they were and sang their progress to whatever else lived down here. He tried to move quietly and understood quickly that quiet was a currency this place didn't accept. You could not sneak through a cathedral. You could only walk through it like a sinner.

Twice the galleries narrowed to passages barely wide enough for their shoulders, water at their chins, lantern held overhead, and both times Edmund felt the claustrophobia rise in him like floodwater of its own — the stone pressing in, the past pressing in, three hundred years of buried kingdom and buried family and buried self all narrowing to a channel he had to squeeze through whether he wanted to or not. This was the thing no one told you about descent: it wasn't dramatic. It was just narrow, and wet, and dark, and there was no way through except through.

In the third gallery, they found footprints.

Grey mud on a shelf of dry stone, boot-shaped, military-regular, days old at most. Sera crouched over them and went very still.

"Rell's trackers came down ahead of us." Her voice had flattened into the register Edmund had learned meant violence coiled and waiting. "Or something else wearing their boots."

"How many?"

"Four. Maybe five. Heading the same direction we are."

Edmund looked at the prints, then out across the black water, listening. Beneath the drip-song, far off, he thought he caught it again — that pressure-flatness, that scrubbed-stone silence. Closer than it had been on the ridge. The engine was underground now. Which meant Rell wasn't waiting at the valley floor for them to emerge. Rell was already in the mountain, following the same map by other means, or following *them*, and the thought arrived cold and certain: the engine could hear the singing stone too. Every pillar, every drop-note, every echo of their passage — the machine would read it all like a page.

"He knows we're here," Edmund said. "Maybe not where. But here."

"Then we stop being polite about it." Sera waded past him, cutting the water into silver shards. "My grandmother's song had a second verse. About a door that opens for blood, and stays shut for everything else. If the Gate's real, we reach it before they do, and we put stone between us and that engine."

"And if the door doesn't open?"

She didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They came to the Weeping Gate near dawn, though dawn was a rumor this far underground — they knew it only because the drip-song had been changing for hours, slowing, deepening, the notes spacing themselves apart like a heartbeat easing toward sleep. The final gallery opened without warning, the passage simply ending at a wall that was not a wall.

It was a door the size of a cathedral frontage, cut into the living rock and carved so densely that the eye failed trying to read it: ten thousand figures ascending spirals toward a crown at the top, generations upon generations of carved people, and every face among them different, every face awake. Water ran down the whole surface in countless channels the masons had followed rather than fought, so that the door appeared to be crying — weeping in ribbons, weeping silver in the lantern light, and the sound it made was not dripping at all up close but something structured, harmonic, a chord held for three hundred years without drawing breath.

At its center, at the height of a man's reaching hand, was a hollow shaped like nothing Edmund had ever seen and exactly like something he had known his whole life without knowing it: a depression in the stone, smooth-walled, waiting, sized for a palm.

His palm. He knew it the way you know your own name in a dream — instantly, totally, wrongly.

"Don't," Sera said quietly. She had seen him looking.

"The map led us here."

"The map led *you* here." She stepped between him and the door, and the weeping water ran down the carved faces behind her like a curtain of witnesses. "Edmund. Everything that brought you to this mountain — the courier, the map, Corvane, the Choir, all of it — everyone moved you here like a piece on a board. Ivenna. Corvane. Maybe even me. One person in this whole story gets to decide what happens next, and it has to be you, freely, or none of it means anything. So decide now. Not with your hand. With your head."

Edmund stood in the weeping chord and felt the mountain breathe around him, felt the singing stone hold its endless note, felt the hollow in the door wait the way a lock waits — patiently, indifferently, forever if necessary. Behind him, far back through the flooded galleries, the silence-that-eats-feeling was getting closer, and with it the flat clean eyes of men who remembered nothing worth keeping.

His head said: *walk away, find another road, let the Crown sleep.*
His head also said: *the refugees in the valley, the burned villages, Sera's eleven years — the Crown decides none of it unless someone claims it first.*

And underneath both voices, older than either, a woman's voice singing an octave above the stone.

He reached forward —

— and froze, because the water on the door had gone suddenly still. Every ribbon of it. Every channel. Three hundred years of weeping stopping at once, the whole carved surface holding its breath, and in that enormous silence Edmund heard, distantly, from the flooded dark behind them, boots. Many boots. Moving fast.

Sera drew her sword. "They found us."

"No," Edmund said, staring at the door, at the hollow shaped like his own hand, at the carved king at the top whose face — now that the water had stilled — was no longer anonymous. Now that the water had stilled, Edmund could see that the face at the top of the spiral was his own.

"They found the door."
