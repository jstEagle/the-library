## 17. Crown of Thorns, Crown of Memory

The Crown sat on its pedestal of black stone like a thing asleep — which was worse than if it had been awake. Awake things could be fought. Sleeping things waited.

Edmund stood at the edge of the vault and did not move toward it. Behind him, the Weeping Gate boomed again as Rell's sappers drove another wedge of grey-fire into the ancient doors, and the sound rolled through the cavern like a mountain clearing its throat. Dust sifted down from the ribs of the vaulted ceiling, catching the pale light that leaked from the walls themselves — the singing stone, Sera called it, the mountain's own slow heartbeat made visible.

"Edmund." Sera's voice came from the doorway behind him, tight as a bowstring. "The Choir's last stand won't hold them long. Ivenna's people are buying us minutes. Minutes."

"I heard you the first time."

"And you're still standing there looking at it like it might look back."

"It is looking back," Edmund said quietly. He hadn't known he was going to say it until the words left him, but they were true, and truth had a way of arriving in his mouth uninvited lately. The Crown was not sleeping. It was waiting. It had been waiting for three hundred years in this cold room under the world, and it knew — somehow, in whatever way a crown could know anything — exactly whose feet stood before it now.

He could feel it the way he felt memories when he lifted them: a pressure against the inside of his skull, a door with someone standing on the other side. Except this door was vast. This door was a cathedral.

Rell's voice echoed faintly from above, distorted by stone: *"—the valley, all of it. Every mind. Mercy, gentlemen, is simply thoroughness applied early."* The engine's hum followed, that wrong, wet whirring Edmund had heard through two captured scouts' stolen eyes. He knew what it did. He had done smaller versions of it himself for coin. And Rell meant to turn it loose on an entire valley full of refugees who had never held a map, never spoken Thornfell's name, never done anything but be born near the wrong mountain.

Sera crossed to his side. In the stone-light her face looked carved from ash, the grief she carried worn smooth into something almost like calm. "You don't have to touch it," she said. "We can run. There are ways out — Ivenna showed me—"

"There aren't ways out for four hundred people in the valley below. You know that." He swallowed. His hands were shaking, and he hated them for it. "Corvane wants it sold. Rell wants it buried under a thousand emptied minds. The Choir wanted it raised. And I—" He laughed, a thin ugly sound. "I don't even know what *I* want. I don't know what I wanted when I was seven years old. Someone took it from me."

"Then don't decide as the boy you were. Decide as the man you are."

"The man I am steals other people's yesterdays for money, Sera. That's not a recommendation."

"No," she said. "But you've been paying it back since the day a dying stranger bled on your floor. I've watched you do it." Her hand found his — rough fingers, burn-scarred, steady. "Whatever happens when you touch that thing, remember that I saw you first. Before any crown told you who you were."

From the gate above came a crack of splintering stone, long and final, and then the sound of boots — many boots — beginning their descent.

Edmund Winterbourne reached out and lifted the Last Crown from its pedestal.

***

It was like plunging his hand into a river that turned out to be an ocean.

The first thing was a name — not his name, but the first name, the one at the root: **Aldric**, and Aldric was standing on a hilltop in a green country that no longer existed, feeling the weight settle onto his brow for the first time, and feeling — this was the horror of it, this was what Edmund would never be able to unknow — feeling *grateful*. Grateful, because the Crown took the memory of his dead son's face and made it bearable. Because the Crown held everything, and so nothing hurt quite as much as it should have.

Then Aldric died, and the weight passed on, and Edmund fell through centuries like a stone through water.

He saw Torvhen the Bold, who used the Crown's memory to hold every treaty ever sworn and broke every one of them anyway, because knowing a promise's history taught you precisely where it would tear. He saw Maren the Quiet, who wore it for sixty years and spoke less each decade, until at last she communicated only in gestures, having discovered that language itself was just a clumsy archive, inferior to the thing on her brow. He saw the twins, Edda and Eddas, who shared it between them, passing it back and forth across a table each morning, and grew to be strangers to each other because they remembered different halves of the same life.

And always underneath, always humming, the mountain. The Crown did not merely hold the memories of kings. It held the mountain's memory too — the slow granite centuries, the deep veins of that old singing magic the empire had spent three hundred years pretending was superstition. Thornfell was not built *on* the power. Thornfell was where the power slept, and the Crown was the pillow beneath its head. Wear it, and the mountain dreamed through you.

Edmund tried to let go. He couldn't find his hands.

*Let go,* something said — or perhaps he said it, or perhaps the Crown was showing him the memory of every king who'd thought the same thought at the same moment, a chorus of drowned men all reaching for surfaces that weren't there. *Not yet,* the chorus answered. *You asked. You touched. You inherit.*

And then the flood found the part of him that was *his*, the blood-thread running down three centuries to a burning tower, and Edmund stopped being a thief falling into an ocean and became instead a man being shown his own house.

***

He saw his family.

Not the Winterbournes of Vael, the gray-faced moneylenders he'd half-remembered inventing for himself out of fog and shame. The other line. The line that had kept the Crown when the kingdom fell, generation after generation, keepers without crowns, hiding in plain sight while collectors and empires sniffed at the mountain like dogs around a grave.

He saw his grandmother — or a woman who was his grandmother, though her face kept sliding into other faces, ancestors stacked behind her like reflections in facing mirrors. He saw her carry a child up the tower stairs on the night of the burning. He saw the child's face.

His face.

*No,* Edmund thought, and the thought rang through the vault like a struck bell, because the Crown heard thoughts, the Crown was made of thoughts, the Crown was thought given metal and thorn.

*Yes,* said three hundred years of kings, gently, terribly. *Watch.*

The tower burned because they set it alight themselves. He watched it happen from six angles at once — from the grandmother's eyes, from the child's, from a servant's, from the eyes of the imperial agent bribed to believe his own lie. The last war had been ending badly; the empire's engines had grown hungry enough to threaten the valley itself; and the royal line had made the choice their founders had always known might come. They would erase themselves. Not die — *erase*. Take the heir beyond the reach of every extraction engine and every ambitious loyalist, strip him of name and blood-memory both, scatter him into the lowlands like seed. Let the Crown sleep with no living hand that fit it. Let the war end not in victory but in forgetting.

"We chose this," his grandmother's voice said, and the voice was coming from inside Edmund's own chest now. "Remember that much, little one, even when you can't remember us. We were not robbed. We paid. There is a difference, and someday — someday you will stand where we stood, and you will understand why we paid, and you will hate us for it, and that too is all right."

They had hired a memory-thief to do it. A good one. One of the best in Vael, whose name Edmund suddenly knew, whose hands he suddenly remembered — no. Whose hands *he had*. 

The vertigo hit him like a physical blow. He was on his knees. He didn't remember kneeling. The Crown's light filled the vault, gold shot through with veins of singing blue, and somewhere very far away Sera was shouting his name and Rell's boots were ringing on the stair, and none of it mattered, because Edmund was remembering the moment of his own erasure from both sides at once.

He remembered the thief's hands on his temples, gentle, professional, the practiced tenderness of a man who did this work well and hated it slightly more each year. And he remembered — because the Crown remembered, because the Crown had been watching, the Crown had *always* been watching, even hidden, even sleeping, some thread of it trailing the scattered heir like a dog too loyal to stop following —

He remembered the thief hesitating. Just a moment. Just one heartbeat before the cut.

And in that heartbeat, something had passed between them. Not a memory. A promise. A small dark vial, palmed, hidden, kept — the seed of Edmund's whole self, carried away by the only man greedy enough to steal it and strange enough to keep rather than sell.

Corvane. Even then. Even thirty years ago. The collector had collected *him*.

Edmund understood everything at once, the way drowning men understand the sea. Corvane had not found him by chance in chapter one of this ruin. Corvane had tended him like an investment, pointed him like a tool, and waited for the day the map and the man and the moment aligned. The offer of restoration had never been mercy. It was a harvest.

And yet —

And yet the horror braided itself with something else, something that felt disgracefully close to transcendence, because the Crown was still pouring, and what it poured now was the reason. He saw the last war's final days as the kings had seen them: the valley burning, the engines singing their wet song over villages, children walking out of their homes with smooth blank faces and their mothers screaming names they no longer answered to. He saw the choice his family made as the only door left in a burning house. He saw that the Crown could have ended the war in a night — could have unmade the emperor's mind, could have rewritten the empire from the top down — and that every king who had seen that possibility had also seen what lay on the far side of it: a throne built from stolen minds, a kingdom of puppets, the mountain's dream wearing human faces forever.

Three hundred years of kings had refused. His family had refused. They had chosen oblivion over that particular immortality.

Now the Crown rested on the brow of the last heir of the line that had surrendered everything to bury it, and the empire's boots were on the stairs, and the collector's web was drawn tight, and the choice that had been made once in fire was standing up again, wearing his face, asking itself the same question.

*What will you do, little keeper?*

The Crown's voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It was patient as geology, warm as a hearth, and it offered him everything — his name, his childhood, his mother's voice, the tower before it burned, twenty years of stolen self laid out like a feast — and it offered it freely, because it was his inheritance, because blood called to blood, because that was what crowns *did*.

All he had to do was keep it.

Above, the vault doors gave way. Grey light spilled down the great stair. Captain Rell's voice, calm and terrible, floated into the singing dark: "There he is. There's our thief."

Edmund rose. The Crown blazed on his brow, and he could feel the mountain dreaming beneath him, vast and slow and waking, and he could feel every memory he had ever stolen from every grieving soul in Vael coiled inside him like coins in a purse — and he understood, finally, with total clarity, what the payment would be.

Sera reached him first, blood on her cheek, her knife out, planting herself between him and the descending greys as if a knife could matter now. She looked up at him — at the light pouring off him — and her face did something complicated: awe and fear and love all fighting for the same ground.

"Edmund," she said. "What did it show you?"

"Everything." His voice came out strange, resonant, doubled. "Everything, Sera. And I know now how it ends. I know what it costs." He looked past her, at Rell descending with his engine cradled in its harness like a fat white grub, at the soldiers fanning out with weapons they didn't understand, at the door through which Corvane's shadow would come sooner or later, patient as ever, to collect.

He lifted his hands toward the Crown.

"Sera," said Edmund Winterbourne — the last keeper of Thornfell, the thief, the orphan of his own making — "whatever happens next, don't tell me my name. Promise me. Whatever I beg. Whatever I forget. Don't give it back."

Her eyes went wide. "Edmund—"

"Promise."

Below them, Rell raised his hand, and the engine began to sing.
