## 18. The Breaking

The vault was singing.

Edmund felt it through the soles of his boots, through the marrow of his shins, through the cage of his ribs — a low harmonic that was not sound at all but memory given weight, three centuries of kings pressing their yesterdays against the inside of his skull. The Last Crown sat upon its plinth of black glass at the center of the chamber, and it did not gleam. It *looked*. The thorn-work band of it turned slowly, impossibly, its barbs drinking the light of Sera's lantern, and Edmund understood with a thief's certainty — the same certainty he'd felt lifting a merchant's grief, a soldier's shame, a child's last summer from their heads — that the thing knew he had come for it.

Behind him, at the threshold where the Weeping Gate's flooded galleries gave way to this dry cathedral of stone, Captain Rell stood in the ruins of the door his engineers had burned through. The Grey Company filled the passage behind him, forty men in imperial grey, torchlight sliding off their lacquered masks. Rell himself had removed his. He looked tired. That was the worst of it. He looked like a man at the end of a long shift, a physician closing a ledger.

"Edmund Winterbourne," Rell said, and his voice carried in the vault the way a stone carries into a well. "Or whatever name you were born under. It hardly matters now."

"It matters," Edmund said. His own voice sounded thin to him, scraped hollow by everything the Crown had already shown him on the descent — the visions that came unbidden, unearned, like coins spilled from a cut purse. "You just don't know why."

"Then let me tell you why *I* am here." Rell stepped forward, and his men parted around him without a sound. In his hands he carried the extraction engine — the thing Edmund had heard about in whispers all the way up the mountain, the brass-and-bone apparatus that had emptied the minds of two Choir scouts in an afternoon, leaving them smiling and vacant as fresh snow. "I have read the old dispatches. I know what this Crown did to our founding emperor — how it sat on his head for one night and left him weeping in a garden for eleven years until his own ministers smothered him with a pillow. I know what it does to every mind that touches it. And I am telling you, thief, with no cruelty in me at all: it must be broken. Not kept. Not worn. Not sold." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Sera, standing at Edmund's left with her knife drawn and her knuckles white around the grip. "Your friend's Choir believes they can wear it back into power. They are wrong. Everyone who reaches for this thing believes they can hold it. No one has. No one will."

"So break it," Edmund said. "If that's what you came for, take your hammers and—"

"I will." Rell raised the engine. Its coils began to whisper, a sound like breath drawn through teeth. "But the mountain is saturated with it. Three hundred years of leaking enchantment soaked into the rock of Thornfell, and my engine must burn it out at the root — which means burning out everything else rooted here. The valley below. The villages. The four hundred refugees your Choir sheltered in the deep holds, who came down from the passes believing themselves safe." He said it plainly, without apology, and that plainness was more horrifying than any threat could have been. "Every mind in this valley will wake tomorrow with nothing. No names. No mothers. No grief worth grieving. I call that mercy, Winterbourne. A clean page. An end to wars before they can be remembered into being again."

"You call it mercy," Sera said, and her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "My village. My sisters. You'd take even the *ashes* from us."

"I would take the wound," Rell answered. "Yes."

And then, from the ruined doorway behind the Grey Company, another voice — smooth, unhurried, horribly familiar.

"Gentlemen. And lady." Master Corvane stepped past the soldiers as if he owned them, as if he had purchased them along with the road, his rings catching the light, his smile the same one he wore when he counted out coin for other people's sorrow. "Forgive the theater. I did try to reach you first, Edmund. On the mountain. With a better offer than either of these men carry."

Edmund turned. Something in his chest had gone very still, the way it went still before a lift — before he slid his fingers behind a stranger's eyes and took.

"Say it anyway," he said.

Corvane spread his hands. "The Crown answers to your blood. You know that now. Which means it can give back what was taken from you — every stolen year, every erased morning, the name your mother whispered over your cradle in a tower that burned. All of it, whole, yours again. You walk out of this mountain *someone*, Edmund. Not the hollow man who sells grief by the vial. Not the thief who cannot remember why his own hands shake. A man with a yesterday. A lineage. A crown, if you want it — or simply the memory of being loved once, which I suspect is worth more to you than any throne." He leaned closer, and dropped his voice to the register he used for closing deals. "Rell offers you oblivion. The Choir offered you a leash. I offer you *yourself*. There has never been a price like it. There never will be again."

The vault hummed. The Crown turned on its plinth, slow as a sleeping eye opening.

And Edmund — Edmund who had spent fifteen years taking memories from strangers because his own were a locked room, because the emptiness in him was a hunger that only other people's yesterdays could briefly fill — stood in the silence and let himself feel the whole shape of it. Corvane was right. That was the obscenity of it. Every word was true. He could be whole. He could be *Edmund*, whoever that had been; he could hold his mother's voice again, real and warm instead of half-remembered through the Crown's cruel glass.

He thought of the merchant in the Vael back-alley, weeping gratitude for the loss of her daughter's death. He had told himself it was kindness. It had been theft with clean hands.

He thought of Mother Ivenna dying in the burning hold, buying his escape with her life, trusting him with a map she should have burned.

He thought of Sera — of the night above the snowline when she'd asked him who he was and he'd finally told the truth, and she had stayed, not blindly, not kindly, but with her eyes open, which was the only kind of staying that had ever meant anything to him.

"No," Edmund said.

Corvane's smile flickered. "Think—"

"I've done nothing but think since the courier died in my room." Edmund's voice rose now, filling the vault, and the mountain seemed to lean in to hear it. "I've thought about selling this secret. I've thought about destroying the map and running south. I've thought about wearing it, God help me — I've stood here and let it show me every king who ever bore it, and do you know what they all had in common?" He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, ragged. "*They all thought they could carry it.* Every one. And every one paid in the same coin — everyone else's memory, everyone else's tomorrow, traded away so they could keep their own precious name."

He looked at Rell. "You want it broken. So do I. But not with your engine, and not at the cost of four hundred minds in the valley. Not one more person pays for my choices. Not ever again."

He looked at Corvane. "And I don't want myself back badly enough to buy myself with this. If I need a whole life to be worth something, then I wasn't worth much to begin with."

Then he looked at the Crown.

It was waiting. Of course it was waiting. It had been waiting three hundred years for someone of the blood to come down into the dark and pick it up, and it had shown him its kings, and its wars, and — last night, in the flooded gallery, unbidden — the truth beneath all of it: that his family had done this to *themselves*. Had erased their own heir, scattered their own history, walked willingly into forgetting, because the only way to end the war the Crown fed on was to end the line that could claim it. His emptiness had been a door his ancestors locked from the inside.

They had chosen unwritten over inherited.

He could too.

"Sera," he said quietly.

"No." She had read him already; of course she had. Her knife-hand dropped. Her face did something terrible and brave. "No, there's another way, there's always—"

"There isn't. And if there were, it would cost someone else, and I'm finished spending other people." He took her hand. Her fingers were cold and shaking and gripped his like she could anchor him to the world by force. "Tell them who I was. After. Tell me, if some part of me is still in there to hear it. That's the only inheritance I want — the kind that's given, not taken."

"Don't," she whispered. "Please. I just found you."

"I know," Edmund said, and smiled, and it hurt more than anything the Crown could have shown him. "That's why it works. That's why it's worth it."

He turned from her, from Rell's frozen face, from Corvane's dawning horror, and he climbed the three steps to the plinth, and he lifted the Last Crown of the Thornfell kings in both hands.

It was lighter than grief. That was his first thought, absurdly. Lighter than grief, heavier than sin, warm as a held hand.

*Are you certain,* it asked — not in words, but in the pressure of every king who had ever worn it, all their doubts and hungers laid against his skull like a congregation leaning forward. *Are you certain, child of the blood?*

"I'm a thief," Edmund said aloud. "And I'm robbing you."

He set it on his head.

The world opened.

Not light — *everything*. Three centuries poured into him at once: Aldric Thornfell crowned in the rain, laughing; Aldric twenty years later ordering a village burned to starve a rebellion, and remembering, forever, the smell. The nine kings after him, each one older faster, each one carrying more dead. The Crown's nature laid bare at last — it was not power, it was *keeping*. It kept everything, hoarded every memory of every wearer, grew fat and heavy and hungry on them, and it could not stop, and it could not die, and it made its bearers into vaults exactly like itself: full, and locked, and alone.

*This is what I am,* it sang inside him. *Take me and never lose anything again.*

"That's the problem," Edmund said, tears freezing on his cheeks in the vault's cold air. "Everything I've ever kept, I kept instead of living."

And he reached — with the strange, practiced grace of fifteen years' trade, the thief's touch turned inward at last — and he *took*.

He took from the Crown. All of it. Every stored reign, every buried war, every whispered coronation oath, three hundred years of hoarded yesterdays — he ripped them loose from the thorn-band the way he had once lifted a widow's grief, and he did not keep them. He *gave* them. He flung them outward, downward, into the living rock of Thornfell itself, into the roots of the mountain where the old magic slept, unbinding the Crown's hoard into stone and water and deep cold earth until the rock itself groaned with the weight of returned memory and the singing in the walls became, for one impossible moment, ten thousand voices saying *thank you*.

The Crown screamed. He felt it scream. He held on.

And the price came for him, as prices do.

His stolen stock went first — the vials of his trade, years of other people's lives he'd carried in his head like contraband. The merchant's daughter. The soldier's shame. A hundred small sorrows he'd pocketed and told himself he'd sell someday. They poured out of him and were gone, and he barely noticed, because next came—

His name. *Edmund.* The word dissolved like sugar in rain. He grasped for it and found only a shape where it had been.

His childhood. The tower. The burning. A woman's voice — *my love, my little winter king* — fading, fading, the melody losing its words, then its warmth, then itself.

Sera's face. No — not gone, never gone, but stripped of everything but *now*: he knew her hand was holding his, knew it mattered, knew nothing of how it had come to matter.

The vault. The map. The courier's blood on his floor. All of it draining away like water through open fingers, and through the roar of loss one thought remained, small and bright and entirely his own, the last thing he chose rather than remembered:

*Let it go. Let it all go. Someone else can carry the yesterdays now.*

The Crown shattered.

Not loudly. Like frost cracking off a branch at dawn — a soft, crystalline exhalation — and the shards did not fall but sank, dissolving into the black-glass plinth, into the stone floor, into the bones of the mountain, and the great harmonic that had sung through Thornfell for three hundred years resolved at last into a single clear note, and then into silence.

Real silence. The first in centuries.

Edmund swayed. The world was enormous and new and had no furniture in it. Hands caught him — strong, familiar-in-the-way-of-just-now hands, a voice calling a name he did not recognize as his own but understood, somehow, was being given to him freely, the only inheritance anyone had ever handed him without a price.

He looked up into a stranger's tear-streaked face and tried to say thank you, and found he had forgotten most of the words, and said what remained:

"Who... was I?"

Above him, in the doorway, Rell lowered his dead engine and stared at the quiet mountain with the expression of a man whose faith had just been outdone by a thief. Beside him, Master Corvane was already backing into the dark, empty-handed, calculating losses.

And Sera held the hollow man in her arms, in the silence after the breaking, and began — word by word, piece by piece — to tell him who he was.

* * *

Far below, in the deep galleries where four hundred refugees slept, the mountain breathed out three hundred years of returned memory — and somewhere in the dark, an old woman dreamed her mother's lullaby for the first time in fifty years, and woke weeping, and did not know why she was smiling.
