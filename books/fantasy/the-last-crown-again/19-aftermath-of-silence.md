## 19. Aftermath of Silence

Sera did not remember walking out.

She remembered the light — the terrible, singing white of the Crown unbinding itself into the stone — and then she remembered cold air and wet granite under her hands and the sound of her own breathing, which was all that remained of the world for a long while. The Weeping Gate had gone dark. The galleries that had hummed with old voices for three hundred years were silent, and the silence was not empty. It was full, the way a bell is full after it has been struck, the way a room is full after someone has left it.

Edmund lay where the vault's threshold met the flooded gallery, on his back, half in the water, his face turned up toward the ceiling he could no longer see anything in. His eyes were open. That was the worst part, at first — the eyes open, fixed, empty as two coins — until Sera knelt in the freezing shallows and pressed her fingers to his throat and found the slow, stubborn pulse there, faint as a moth against glass.

"Edmund." Her voice came out ruined. She had been screaming, she realized. She didn't know when. "Edmund, wake up."

Nothing. Not the nothing of death, which she knew intimately, which she had counted in the faces of her burned village — this was a different nothing. This was a man whose candle had been carried out of the room entirely, leaving only the wax behind. She shook him. She put her mouth to his ear and said his name again, and then, because she did not know what else to do, she began to say everything else: her own name, the name of her village, the names of the dead of the Ash Choir, Mother Ivenna's name, spoken like a litany, like kindling, like something that might catch if she fed it enough.

Around her, the mountain kept its new quiet.

It was Rell's voice that finally broke it — Rell's voice, hoarse and stripped of all its polished certainty, echoing down from the vault's threshold where he stood with his grey company gathered behind him like men who had walked into a church and forgotten why they'd come.

"It's dead," the captain said. Sera turned. In the gloom she could see the memory-extraction engine sprawled across the stones where his soldiers had hauled it, its brass arms slack, its lenses dark, weeping a thin residue of silver that pooled and would not cohere. Two of his men were prodding it with the wary gestures of people touching a corpse. "The engine. And the—" He stopped. He could not say *the Crown*. His jaw worked around the word and gave it up.

Sera got to her feet slowly. Her legs shook. She put herself between Rell and Edmund's body without deciding to, the way her hand went to a knife without deciding to, and stood there in the dark water breathing like a cornered animal.

Rell looked at her. Then past her, at Edmund. Then at the place — she saw him look at it — where the Last Crown had sat humming on its throne of living stone not an hour before. There was a scar in the rock now, a pale seam like an old wound, and nothing else.

"Three centuries," Rell said quietly. "My order has spent three centuries and eleven thousand lives keeping the empire safe from that thing, and you broke it in an evening." He laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, more like a cough. "Do you know what I was told, when they gave me the extraction engine? I was told it was mercy. That a mind wiped clean was a mind at peace. I believed it. I have *made* myself believe it for twenty years." His hand rose halfway toward the dead engine and fell again. "I stood in that vault and I felt what it remembered. Every king. Every war. Everything it ever held. Do you understand? It wasn't a weapon. It was a *grave*, and we were grave-robbers, and I have been robbing graves my entire life and calling it peacekeeping."

One of his soldiers shifted. "Captain. Orders?"

Rell was silent a long moment. When he answered, his voice had flattened into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than anything else he'd said.

"We withdraw. All of us. Report the artifact destroyed by unknown agents. Report the valley clear." He turned his head, and in the failing lantern-light Sera saw that his eyes were wet, and that he seemed as surprised by it as she was. "There are refugees in these mountains, Sergeant. Four hundred souls in the high holds, by my own count, from villages my company burned looking for this thing. If I report them, the empire comes for them. If I don't—" he exhaled— "then somewhere in the imperial archives there will be a lie with my seal on it. Let it be the first honest lie I've ever told."

"Captain—"

"*Withdraw.*"

They went. Sera watched the lanterns shrink up the gallery stairs, watched the last one gutter and vanish, listened to boots fade into stone silence, and only when the final echo died did she let herself fall to her knees beside Edmund again and put her forehead against his cold, wet chest and stay there, shaking, while the Weeping Gate dripped its slow water onto both of them like a thing learning how to weep again.

* * *

Corvane found her at dawn.

She had dragged Edmund up to the first dry chamber above the flood line, built a fire from broken crate-wood and Choir stores, wrapped him in every blanket she could find, and sat watching his face change — because it *was* changing, hour by hour, the way a shoreline changes. The tension of a man holding himself together was draining out of him. The flickers of calculation behind his closed lids were smoothing away. Whoever had been lying there was becoming someone with less and less inside, and Sera kept vigil over that diminishment like a woman watching a tide go out, unable to tell whether she was witnessing a theft or a rescue.

So when soft footsteps sounded at the chamber entrance she was already on her feet with a knife drawn, and not even slightly surprised to see Master Corvane standing in the grey morning light with his fine coat dusted in limestone powder and his hands raised in their practiced, theatrical surrender.

"Now, now," he said. "If I wanted violence I'd have brought more than regret."

"I'll give you regret for free."

He smiled — but it was a thin thing, and it died young. He stepped into the chamber, looked at Edmund's sleeping form, looked at the fire, and for once in his gilded life seemed to have no performance ready. "Is he—"

"He's alive."

"And the Crown?"

"Gone. Shattered. Scattered through the bones of the mountain." She let the knife point steady on his heart. "You're too late, collector. Whatever you promised whoever paid you — whatever you thought you'd carry south in a locked case — it doesn't exist anymore. Nothing left to buy."

Corvane was quiet for a moment. Then he crossed the room — slowly, showing her his palms — and crouched near the fire, warming hands that did not need warming, and Sera realized with a small cold shock that the man looked *old* in the firelight. Old and tired, the lacquer cracked through at last.

"You misunderstand my trade," he said at length. "Or perhaps I do. I told myself I was a curator. That memories deserve better than to rot in dying heads. That preserving them was a kindness." He glanced at Edmund. "I had his childhood, you know. Bought it from the people who took it. Twenty-two years I've kept it in my vaults, waiting for a buyer — or waiting, if I'm honest, which is rare, for *him* to be worth the price of retrieving it." A pause. "And then he goes and destroys the single most valuable object in the world rather than sell it, or keep it, or use it. And pays for it with—" Corvane gestured vaguely at Edmund's sleeping face— "*that*. With himself. Do you know what that makes him, in my profession?"

"A fool."

"The most expensive man alive," Corvane said. "Everything he ever was, and no one to want it back but me. And now—" he spread his hands, and the gesture was strangely empty— "now I find I don't want it either. Or I can't. There's no market for a man who gave himself away. No price. You can't auction a sacrifice; it ruins the resale value of everything." He rose, brushed off his knees, and Sera noticed his hands were trembling and that he made no effort to hide it. "I'm leaving, Ash Choir girl. South, and then further south. There are other crowns — there are always other crowns, sleeping under other mountains, and some other fool will dig for them — but I find my appetite has... changed." He stopped at the entrance, silhouetted against the pale sky, and looked back once. "Tell him — no. Tell him nothing. He wouldn't know my name anyway, would he? He wouldn't know yours either." Something moved across his face that in a better man might have been grief. "That's the trade none of us read closely enough. We all assumed someone gets to keep the ledger."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the smuggler's road, and Sera stood with her knife still drawn for a long time afterward, feeling oddly bereaved, as though a door had closed on a whole wing of the world.

* * *

Edmund woke on the second day.

She was asleep sitting up when it happened — she heard the blankets shift and was instantly awake, knife out, heart slamming — and then she saw his eyes open and focused on her, and she understood, in one lurching drop of the stomach, that they were focused on her the way a stranger's eyes focus on a stranger.

"Where am I?" he asked. His voice was a rasp, unused. "Who are you?"

The question went through her like a blade finding bone. She had rehearsed this — some of it, in the long fireside hours — but rehearsal was nothing. Rehearsal was armor for a battle that turned out to be fought barehanded.

"You're safe," she said. "You're in the mountains. Thornfell."

"Thornfell." He said it like a foreign word. He looked at his own hands, turning them over, flexing the fingers as though checking whether they belonged to him. "I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a light. Under the ground. There was singing, or — or something like singing, and I was holding..." His brow creased, and Sera watched him reach for it and find nothing, watch the confusion bloom and then curdle into a low, animal fear. "Why can't I remember my name?"

And here it was, the moment she had dreaded and prepared for and dreaded anyway: the moment where she became, for this hollowed-out man, the sole keeper of everything. She took a breath that hurt all the way down.

"You gave it away," she said. "On purpose. To save four hundred people, and a valley, and everyone who will never know your face. It was the bravest thing I have ever seen anyone do, and I have seen people die for less." She moved closer, slowly, the way one approaches a wounded animal. "My name is Sera. You pulled me out of a burning village when we were children and neither of us knew it until a month ago. Your name was Edmund Winterbourne. You stole memories for coin in a city called Vael, and you hated yourself for it, and you were better than you believed. And everything else — everything else I will give back to you, piece by piece, as fast as you can hold it. Freely given. No coin. No price. That's the only inheritance anyone should ever have to receive."

He stared at her. Somewhere far below them, water dripped in the dark of the Weeping Gate, slow and patient, counting out a rhythm older than kingdoms.

"Winterbourne," he repeated, tasting it without recognizing it. "Did I—" A hesitation, and then, so quietly she nearly missed it: "Was there someone waiting for me? Before all this?"

Sera thought of Mother Ivenna. Of the Choir's hidden hold, burned. Of the village ash she still carried in a pouch at her belt. She thought about lying, and decided — for him, for this blank new man — that she was finished with lies given gently.

"Yes," she said. "There were people waiting for you. Some of them didn't make it. And one of them is sitting right here, and she's not going anywhere."

Something moved in his face — not memory, not quite, but the shadow memory casts, the way an empty window still holds the shape of its frame. He reached out, and his hand found hers, and gripped it, and outside the mountain went on being quiet — truly quiet, for the first time in three hundred years, the old song finally ended, the crownless dark holding nothing but stone and snow and the small human warmth of two survivors teaching each other the world again.

Above them, on the southern ridgeline, unseen, a traveler paused at the pass and looked back at the silent peak — and turned, and walked on toward Vael, carrying rumors of other crowns asleep beneath other mountains, waiting for someone foolish enough to listen.
