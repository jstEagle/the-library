## 20. A Man Without Yesterdays

Sera had watched men die in a hundred ways — by blade, by fire, by cold that crept into the lungs like a patient thief. She had watched her village burn. She had watched Mother Ivenna stand in the doorway of the hold and hold the line with nothing but her voice and the last of the Choir's old songs, buying seconds with her life like a woman counting out coins she knew would never be returned.

She had never watched a man give himself away piece by piece while still breathing.

Edmund had not moved in two days.

He lay on a pallet of pine boughs and sheepskins in the shepherd's hut above the valley, where Sera had dragged him after the mountain finished its long, shuddering exhalation. The Crown was gone. Shattered. Unbound into the stone and root and singing dark of Thornfell, scattered so thoroughly through the bones of the earth that no engine, no collector, no king or emperor would ever gather it again. The mountain had gone quiet afterward — truly quiet, for the first time in three hundred years, as though some vast instrument that had been humming beneath everything had finally been allowed to rest.

And Edmund Winterbourne lay with his eyes closed and his breath slow and shallow, and when he finally opened them, he looked at Sera the way a stranger looks at weather.

"Who are you?" he asked.

His voice was hoarse from disuse. His eyes were the same eyes she had first seen across the sights of her bow, the man she'd meant to kill, the man she'd spared because of a leather map case and a name — *Thornfell keeps* — and yet they held none of the wariness, none of the quick calculating flicker she had learned to read in him. They were simply open. Empty the way a cleared field is empty: not barren, but waiting to be planted.

Sera's throat tightened. She had rehearsed this. All through the long nights of his sleep she had rehearsed it, turning phrases over like stones in her palm, trying to find ones smooth enough not to cut.

"My name is Sera," she said. "Of the Ash Choir. Of Hollowmere, before it burned." She crouched beside the pallet, keeping her hands visible, the way one does with frightened animals and soldiers. "You're Edmund. Edmund Winterbourne."

He repeated it slowly, tasting each syllable. "Edmund Winterbourne." A frown. "It doesn't... it doesn't feel like anything."

"No," she said gently. "It wouldn't."

The fire cracked and settled. Outside the hut's small window, the peaks of Thornfell caught the late morning sun and burned white-gold, clean and silent, their ancient menace dissolved. Somewhere below, in the valley, refugees were beginning to come out of the deep shelters — she could hear the faint sounds of life returning, a dog barking, an axe against wood, children whose laughter carried up the slope on the thin cold air. The world was resuming. It seemed almost obscene, how easily the world resumed.

"What happened to me?" Edmund asked.

"You broke the Crown," Sera said. "You took all of it into yourself — every memory it ever held, every king who ever wore it, three centuries of Thornfell's whole buried history — and you poured it back into the mountain. You unmade it. And the price—" Her voice caught. She steadied it. "The price was everything you were carrying. Every memory you'd ever taken from anyone. And your own."

He looked at his hands then, turning them over slowly, as if expecting them to belong to someone else. Perhaps, Sera thought, they did. Perhaps the man who owned those hands had poured himself out like water into thirsty stone, and what remained was only the vessel — clean, new, and terribly light.

"It doesn't hurt," he said at last, and there was something in his voice — wonder, maybe, or grief learning its own shape. "I thought it would hurt. Losing things. But it's more like..." He searched. "Like waking from a dream you know was important, but can't even remember dreaming."

"Yes," Sera said softly. "I know something of that feeling."

Because she did. That was the thing she could never say aloud without breaking open: she understood the arithmetic of loss better than any living soul should. Hollowmere's ashes. Her mother's face, which she now had to reconstruct from fragments, from other people's descriptions, from a locket portrait that flattened a living woman into paint. Grief was its own kind of forgetting — slower, crueler, wearing away the sharp edges of the beloved until you began to doubt whether the person you mourned had ever truly been that person at all.

But Edmund had lost it all in a single act of will. He had chosen emptiness. He had stood in the Vault of Kings with the Grey Company at his back and Corvane's promise in one ear and Rell's mercy in the other, and he had chosen neither restoration nor erasure but sacrifice — had chosen, with full knowledge of what it would cost, to become no one rather than let anyone else decide what he should be.

She had loved him a little for that before he did it. Now she didn't have the word for what she felt.

"I keep reaching for things," he said. "Habits. I reached for my left boot first this morning, before the right. I don't know why. I don't know if I've always done that or if it just feels right." He laughed — a small, startled sound. "I know how to whistle. I found that out an hour ago. I don't know where I learned it."

"The body remembers," Sera said. "Even when the mind is swept clean. My grandmother used to say grief works the same way. The hands go on doing what the heart has forgotten."

He turned his head to look at her fully, and again she saw it — the openness, the absence of the flinch most men had when a woman with a scarred cheek and a soldier's posture leaned close. This man had no past to make him flinch.

"Will you tell me?" he asked. "About myself. About — all of it. You were there, weren't you? For part of it?"

"Some of it," Sera said. "Not all. Not the parts before we met."

"Then tell me the parts you know. And after that—" he hesitated, and she saw the first shadow cross the cleared field, the first intimation of how frightening a blank page could be to the man standing before it— "after that, tell me what you think I ought to know. Even if it isn't flattering. Especially if it isn't."

Sera sat down properly then, folding her legs beneath her, settling in the way one settles for a long watch. Because that's what this was, she realized. A watch. Someone had to keep vigil over this man's story until he could carry it himself, and she had appointed herself to it sometime in the last two sleepless nights, without ever quite deciding.

"All right," she said. "From the beginning. Or from where I begin knowing it."

* * *

"You were born in Thornfell," she told him. "That much Corvane gave us, and the Crown confirmed it. Your family was the royal line — the last of it. When the empire came, three hundred years ago, your ancestors didn't fight to the last the way the songs say the kings did. They did something braver. They erased themselves. Wiped their own memories, scattered their own bloodline into the lowlands like seed, so that no one — not emperor, not collector, not prophet — could ever find the heir and use him to wake the Crown. They ended their own kingdom to end the war."

Edmund listened with the absolute attention of a child hearing scripture for the first time. She watched his hands tighten on the sheepskin.

"And my family — my line — kept the secret," he said slowly. "The Ash Choir."

"Your family founded it. Or helped to. The Choir grew around the secret like bark grows around a wound." She paused. "There's irony in that, if you want it. The loyalists who guarded the Crown's resting place were descended from the kings who wanted it dead. We spent three centuries standing guard over a grave our own ancestors dug."

"And me? How did I end up—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the hollow where a history should be— "like this?"

"We don't know all of it. Corvane knew more than he told. But the shape of it is this: someone found the heir. A child of the old blood, living in the lowlands, and the memory-thieves' guild got to him before the empire did — or perhaps the guild was paid by people who meant well. Someone took your childhood from you. Sold it off in pieces, probably. Kept the rest in vials somewhere in Vael, in a collector's vault, labeled like wine." She said it flatly, refusing to soften it, because he had asked for the unflattering truth and because she believed — had come to believe, over these hard months — that kindness built on concealment was just another cage. "You grew up with nothing. And you learned to take memories from others, because taking was the only relationship with memory anyone had ever taught you."

He was silent a long moment. The fire popped.

"I stole from grieving women," he said. Not a question. He must have heard it in her tone, or perhaps some residue of conscience survived the erasure, some sediment of guilt too heavy to wash away.

"Yes. You did. You lifted a merchant's grief out of her head and sold it to Master Corvane for more than it was worth, and you told yourself it was mercy — that she was better off without it." Sera held his gaze. "I'm telling you this because you asked. And because I think a man should know what he was, if he's going to choose what he'll be."

To her surprise, Edmund nodded slowly. No horror crossed his face — or rather, horror crossed it and passed through, the way weather crosses an open sky, leaving no stain.

"That sounds like me," he said quietly. "I mean — I don't remember doing it. But I understand why I would have." He flexed his fingers. "When you've been robbed of the only thing that makes you yourself, I imagine theft starts to feel natural. Like breathing."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," he agreed. "It's an explanation. There's a difference. I think I used to confuse them." A pause. "Did I pay for it?"

"In the end," Sera said. "More than anyone should."

She told him the rest then, and it took hours, and she gave it to him the way one gives water to a man dying of thirst — slowly, in measured sips, so it wouldn't tear through him. She told him about the courier dying in his rented room in Vael, pressing the map case into his hands. About the flight through the smuggler roads. About the burned village — *my* village, she said, and watched him absorb that, watch him understand that the man sitting before him was partly responsible, however indirectly, for the ashes she had risen from — and her arrow aimed at his throat, and the map that stayed her hand. She told him about Mother Ivenna, about the bargain, about the hold burning, about the traitor whose name she still could not say without spitting. She told him about the Weeping Gate, the flooded galleries, the singing stone. About the vault. About Captain Rell and his engine of merciful annihilation. About Corvane's offer — *your whole self, restored, for the price of a map* — and how Edmund had refused it before the offer was even fully spoken.

"He offered you yourself," Sera said. "Everything they took. Your mother's face. Your first snowfall. Your own name, in your own mother's voice. And you said no."

"How could I say yes?" Edmund asked — genuinely asking, she realized, wanting to understand the man he had been. "After seeing what the Crown showed me. What memory does when it becomes power. If I'd taken it back from him, I'd only have been trading one thief for another. He'd have owned me worse than the guild ever did." He shook his head slowly. "I can't remember making the choice. But I think — I hope — it was the right one."

"It was," Sera said, and her voice broke slightly on it, and she let it break, because hiding it would have been a lie and she had sworn, sitting at this pallet through two nights, that she would give him nothing but truth. "It was the right choice, Edmund. It cost you everything, and it was right. The mountain is quiet. The Crown is dust and song. Rell walked away from his engine like a man walking out of a church he'd defiled. Whatever happens next, no one will ever wear that crown again — not an emperor, not a king, not a collector. Because of you."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Why are you telling me all this?" he asked finally. "You owe me nothing. From what you've described, I'm lucky you didn't put that arrow through my neck when you had the chance."

Sera smiled — a real smile, rare on her face these days, feeling strange on muscles that had forgotten the shape.

"Because everyone else who might have given you your story is gone, or bought, or an enemy," she said. "Ivenna is dead. Corvane fled. Rell is marching north with his tail between his legs. There's no one left, Edmund. No archive, no vault, no Choir elder. Just me. So I'm giving it to you — freely, the way no one ever gave you anything. Your story, as a gift. Not sold. Not stolen. Not traded for a map or a crown." She reached out and, carefully, touched his hand. "It's the one inheritance you get to keep. And you don't even have to carry it alone."

His fingers curled around hers. His grip was weak — he was still desperately frail, eating the broth she brought him like a convalescent, sleeping ten hours a night — but the grip was deliberate. Present.

"Sera," he said. Testing the name. Then, with the first glimmer of something that was not grief and not wonder but simply *him*, whatever *him* would turn out to be: "What happens now?"

* * *

What happened now, in the immediate sense, was soup. Sera ladled barley broth from the pot over the fire and taught him, patiently, how to eat it without spilling — his hands had recovered their competence faster than his mind, but there were gaps, strange and specific, places where the erasure had gone deeper. He could whittle. He could not write. He remembered how fire worked but not how fear felt. When she mentioned Vael, the city drew nothing from him at all; when she mentioned rain, his whole face softened with recognition.

"You're rebuilding," she observed on the third evening, watching him study the flames.

"Am I? Or am I just collecting?" He glanced at her, wry. "Old habit. Everything new I learn — I keep reaching to store it somewhere. Like I'm afraid of losing it again."

"Maybe that's not a flaw," Sera said. "Maybe that's just a man who knows what memory costs."

They fell into a rhythm over the following days, the two of them in the shepherd's hut, and the rhythm became a kind of domesticity that startled her whenever she noticed it. Mornings, she told him stories — pieces of himself, pieces of their road together, pieces of the world. Afternoons, they walked the mountainside in slow circuits, his strength returning day by day, and he relearned the names of things: *that's larch; that bird is a nutcracker; that peak is called the Widow's Tooth.* He greeted each fact like a gift, unwrapped it, thanked her for it. Even the painful ones. *Especially* the painful ones.

One evening, a week after he woke, he stopped halfway along their circuit and stood looking down at the valley, where the refugee camp's cookfires had begun to dot the dusk like fallen stars.

"They'll need help," he said. "Down there. Rebuilding. The empire won't come back soon — Rell's done, and everyone knows it — but winter's coming, and those people have nothing." He turned to her. "I don't know how to build a wall or plant a field. But I'm strong enough now to haul timber. And I'd like to be useful. Is that — does that sound like something I would have wanted? Before?"

Sera considered him. The sunset gilded the ruined side of his face — the scar from the flooded galleries, which he would never remember earning — and behind him the mountain rose, silent, serene, holding its shattered secret forever in its stony heart.

"I think," she said, "that the man I knew spent his whole life running from usefulness. Running toward coin, toward safety, toward anywhere the past couldn't find him." She stepped closer. "So yes. I think he would have wanted this. And I think the man you're becoming wants it more."

"Will you stay?" The question came out rougher than he likely intended. "Here. With — I mean—" He stopped, laughed at himself, started again with the honesty she had come to prize in him, this man with nothing left to hide behind. "I don't know who I am yet. I know who you are. You're the only fixed point I've got. So I'm asking, plainly, like a man with nothing to lose, which is exactly what I am: will you stay?"

Sera looked out at the valley, at the smoke rising from a hundred small fires, at the world remaking itself without permission from kings or empires. Somewhere south, she knew, rumors were already moving along the smuggler roads — whispers of other crowns sleeping under other mountains, other secrets waiting for their thieves and their guardians. The work of the Ash Choir was not finished. Perhaps it never would be. Perhaps guarding the world against buried power was a task that outlived every generation given to it, and someday she would take up the watch again, or pass it to hands younger than hers.

But not today. Today the mountain was quiet, the man beside her was alive, and the future was a blank page — which, she had learned, was not the same as an empty one.

"For now," she said, and took his arm as they turned back toward the hut, "I'm staying. And tomorrow, we start teaching you the valley roads. Every path, every stream, every place the snow drifts deep. You'll learn them the way I did — one mistake at a time."

Edmund laughed — really laughed, unguarded, a sound with no past attached to it, brand-new in the world. And far to the south, though neither of them could know it, in the city of Vael, that same laugh was already waiting in a glass vial on a collector's shelf, patient as a seed, ready to outlive them both.
