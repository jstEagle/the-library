## 1. The Quiet God's Debts

The rain had stopped an hour before sundown, and Ashgrave's night market rose out of the wet streets like something the city dreamed and then forgot to dismiss.

Lena Zhou knew every seam of it. The lantern-sellers who hung their glass globes from the eaves of the tannery row so the light came down amber and greasy. The fishwives who set up under the aqueduct arches because the stone kept the smell of brine cool through the night hours. The charm-row — his row — where a dozen licensed sellers laid out their wares on stained velvet: knot-charms against toothache, salt-bottles for bad dreams, iron nails bent into rings that were supposed to keep a husband faithful and mostly just kept him poor.

All of it legal. All of it blessed by the Edict of Marrow, stamped with the wax seals of the Office of Purity, inspected twice a season by men who liked to be called inquisitors but who dressed like clerks and smelled like ink.

Harmless charms. That was the whole trick of surviving in Ashgrave now. You sold people things that did nothing, and you let them believe, and belief was not yet a crime — though Kaelen Marrow's lawyers were working on it.

Lena arranged his own stall with the care of a man setting a table for ghosts. Dried lavender bundled with red thread. River-stones drilled and strung on leather cord. Little clay whistles shaped like sleeping birds, which he told children would keep nightmares away if they blew them before bed. None of it worked. He made sure of that. He checked each piece the way other men checked blades — turning it over, feeling for the hum, the warmth, the faint pressure behind the eyes that meant something real was living inside the object. If he ever found one, he'd smash it in the gutter and take the loss.

Five years since Veyra went silent. Five years since the god of oaths and quiet courage stopped answering her sworn, and the world learned what her power had been holding back.

He'd been good at this once. Not the charms — the other thing. The thing he didn't name, even in the privacy of his own skull, because names were how the inquisitors' dogs tracked you. He'd been battle-sworn. He'd carried the marks. And when the silence came, when the god's voice cut off mid-word like a door slammed in the middle of a sentence, Lena Zhou had done the one thing no Veyra-sworn was supposed to do.

He'd walked away.

* * *

The market filled slowly, the way water fills a lock. First the servants buying supper scraps, then the dockhands with coin still warm from the harbor tally-house, then the better sort — clerks, minor guildsmen, widows in good wool — all of them drifting down charm-row with the particular hunger of people who wanted very badly to believe in something small.

A woman bought three of the bird-whistles for her granddaughters. A dockhand with a crushed finger wrapped in dirty linen paid two coppers for a knot-charm and held it against his hand like it might knit bone. Lena took the money and said the words he always said — *wear it well, may your days be quiet* — and felt the familiar hollowness open under his ribs like a trapdoor.

May your days be quiet. It was half a prayer. The other half had belonged to someone who no longer spoke.

"Zhou." A voice from the next stall, low and dry. Old Maretta, who sold luck-strings and had sold them since before the Edict, back when luck-strings had actually been lucky. She nodded past his shoulder without looking. "Gray coat. Third time he's walked the row tonight."

Lena didn't turn. Turning was a tell. He weighed a river-stone in his palm as though considering its balance and let his eyes do the work sideways, in the polished face of a copper tray.

Gray coat. City watch, not inquisition — the gray of Ashgrave's municipal guard rather than the white-and-ash of Marrow's purity officers. Young. Walking too slow. Looking at stalls instead of crowds, which meant he wasn't hunting a thief.

Looking at *him*, or near enough.

"Probably collecting the stall-tax," Lena said.

"Stall-tax was collected at dusk." Maretta's hands never stopped braiding. "That's not a tax man. That's a man looking for a reason."

There was always a reason, in Ashgrave. That was the genius of the Edict. It didn't need to name you; it only needed you to flinch. Marrow had understood, early and coldly, that the surest way to kill magic was not to burn every witch but to make every ordinary person afraid of being mistaken for one. Fear did the culling for you. Neighbors informed on neighbors. Mothers on sons. The city policed itself down to the marrow — his marrow, everyone's.

The gray coat reached the stall two rows down. Lena watched him stop there, pick up a charm, put it down. Pick up another. Ask the seller something. Move on.

Coming this way.

"Evening," the guard said, arriving at Lena's table like weather. He was maybe twenty-five, with the soft jaw of a man who'd joined the watch for the bread ration and the pale, overcareful eyes of a man who'd discovered the job involved more than bread. His gaze moved across the wares. "Slow night."

"Rain earlier," Lena said. "Keeps the customers home."

"You're Zhou? The charm-seller?"

"I'm the charm-seller," Lena said, which was true, and not the question.

"Lena Zhou." The guard said the whole name carefully, tasting it. From his belt pouch he drew a folded paper, and Lena's stomach performed a slow, professional drop, five years of discipline keeping his face pleasant and slightly bored. "Got a list tonight. Names to check against licenses."

"Check away."

The guard unfolded the paper. Read. Lena used the time to catalog exits without moving his head: the alley behind the tannery, narrow, ending in a wall he could climb if his shoulder cooperated; the crowd thickening toward the fish-arches; Maretta, watching with the flat patience of a woman who had survived four regimes and intended to survive a fifth.

"Hold out your hands," the guard said.

And there it was.

"Hands?" Lena smiled. "You want to buy a charm or arrest me?"

"License inspection. New procedure. Hands, palms up, on the table."

New procedures arrived in Ashgrave the way boils arrived — suddenly, painfully, and always a symptom of something worse upstream. Somewhere in the white tower above the harbor, some clerk had decided that palm-inspection caught smugglers of banned talismans, because real magic left traces in the skin. Which was true. Which was exactly the problem.

Lena placed his hands on the velvet, palms up, and became a man showing a doctor a rash.

The oath-marks ran from the heel of each palm up across the wrist and faded somewhere beneath his sleeves — fine silvered scarring, old, deliberate, the ghost of a brand applied with ceremony by a priestess whose name he could no longer say aloud. To anyone who didn't know, they looked like burn scars from a forge accident. Plenty of smiths had palms like his. But they weren't forge scars, and both of them knew what a license inspector was really looking for, and the space between those two facts was where men like Lena lived or died.

The guard leaned close. His breath smelled of cheap beer. His fingers hovered over Lena's right palm without touching.

"These from the wars?" he asked.

"Smithing," Lena said. "Ten years at the anchor-works, before I took up charms. You know how it is. Hot metal finds skin."

"Mm." The guard's eyes came up. They stayed on Lena's face a moment too long, and something moved behind them — not recognition, not yet, but its older sibling, suspicion, tapping at the window. "Anchor-works. Which one?"

"The one on Weir Street."

"There's no anchor-works on Weir Street."

"There was," Lena said mildly. "Before the flood year. They pulled it down and built the rope-walk on the lot. You're young. You wouldn't remember."

It was a good lie. It was the kind of lie that had kept him alive through three inspections, two informants, and one long terrible winter when the inquisitors posted sketches of forty-one branded deserters on the customs house wall and Lena had walked past his own face every morning on the way to market, hat down, shoulders rounded, performing the posture of a man with nothing to hide.

The guard straightened. Wrote something on his paper — Lena couldn't see what, and not knowing would keep him awake until dawn, which was perhaps the point. Inspections weren't only for finding guilt. They were for teaching the whole row to sleep badly.

"You'll want to renew that license," the guard said. "Month's end. There's a new schedule fee."

"A fee."

"For sellers of, ah—" he glanced at the paper again, "—devotional goods. New category. Council's orders."

Devotional goods. Lena almost laughed. Five years ago, selling a prayer to Veyra would have gotten them both hanged. Now the council wanted a fee for the word *devotional*. The city had found a way to tax even the hole the god had left behind.

"Of course," Lena said. "Month's end."

The guard lingered. Looked at the bird-whistles. Looked at Lena's hands one last time, and this time Lena saw it land — the flicker, the small cold click behind the young man's eyes, the moment a vague suspicion sharpened into a shape. The guard opened his mouth.

Maretta dropped a tray of luck-strings.

The clatter was enormous, deliberate, theatrical. Every head on charm-row turned. Two fishwives began shouting about whose basket had been knocked, and by the time the small riot sorted itself out, the gray coat had been drawn three stalls away by a dockhand demanding to report a cutpurse, and Lena was gathering his unsold wares into their box with steady hands and a heartbeat going like a war drum.

"Month's end," Maretta said, not looking at him, rethreading her strings. "You should be gone by month's end, Zhou."

"I have a shop."

"You have a face that's on somebody's list. It's been on somebody's list for five years. Tonight somebody got close enough to read it." She tied off a braid. "I liked you, charm-seller. Don't make me watch them do it in the square."

* * *

He closed the stall early and walked home the long way, through streets that smelled of wet ash and lamp oil, past the customs house where the sketches had once hung. The wall there had been replastered twice since. He still knew where his own face had been. Fourth row, second from the left, between a woman who'd drowned trying to swim the harbor and a boy of nineteen who'd lasted eleven days before a neighbor sold him for a sack of flour.

His rooms were above a shuttered dyehouse on Tanner's Cut: one room, one cot, one stove, one window that looked out on a courtyard where rain collected in a broken cistern and bred mosquitoes all summer. He'd chosen it for the two doors — the main stair, and the roof hatch over the dye-works, reachable from his window by a ledge a cat would find insulting. A man with his history learned to think in doors.

He lit the stove. Put water on for tea he wouldn't drink. And then he did what he did every night, the ritual that was the last honest thing left in his life, though honesty had nothing to do with it anymore. Habit, maybe. Grief wearing the costume of habit.

From beneath the floorboard by the cot he took out the prayer.

It was written on paper he'd made himself, from mulberry bark, in ink he'd ground from oak gall — the old way, the way the scriptoria of the silent order had taught him, back when the order had scriptoria and the god had a voice. The words were in the First Tongue. He hadn't spoken it in five years. He wasn't sure he still could. But his hands remembered the letterforms the way a drowning man remembers swimming, and every night he wrote the same prayer, and every morning he burned it, because a prayer you keep is a prayer you're asking to be answered, and he had stopped asking.

*Veyra who binds and is bound. Veyra who keeps the word. Keep my word for me. I am too tired to carry it.*

He held the paper over the stove plate and watched the corners brown, curl, catch.

The flame took the words the way it took everything — completely, indifferently. No warmth spread through his marks. No pressure behind his eyes. No voice in the silence behind the world, where a god used to live. Just ash lifting off the plate and swirling toward the ceiling, and the tea going cold beside him, and the city outside settling into curfew with its shutters and its bells and its patrols, and Lena Zhou sitting alone in a room full of smoke, performing ordinariness so well that sometimes he fooled even himself.

He was washing the cup when he heard the storm come back.

It rolled in off the harbor fast and black, the kind of curfew storm that emptied the streets and brought the watch indoors and turned Ashgrave into a city of drumming roofs. Rain hit the window like thrown gravel. Lightning stood the courtyard in white for a heartbeat — the dead cistern, the wet walls, the ledge to the roof hatch — and in that same heartbeat, thunder swallowed everything, and under the thunder, faint, impossible, there came a sound from the base of his stairs.

Knocking.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes, if a child were fool enough or desperate enough to be out in a curfew storm, knocking on the door of a stranger in a city where strangers reported strangers for bread.

Lena stood very still, cup in hand, listening to the rain and the small fists and the five years of careful invisibility that all of it threatened to undo.

Then the knocking came again — and beneath it, muffled by the door, by the storm, by everything, he heard a voice. High. Young. Speaking words he did not understand and that his body understood completely, because along both forearms, in the dark, in the empty room, in the silence of a god five years dead or sleeping or caged —

his oath-marks began to burn.

* * *

He crossed the room before he decided to. That was the thing he'd remember afterward — that his feet went first, that five years of caution counted for nothing against whatever was saying those words on the other side of his door. He lifted the bar. He opened the door onto the storm.

A child stood on his landing. Small, seven years old at most, soaked to the skin, mud to the knees, shivering so hard the lantern-light seemed to shiver with him. No coat. No bundle. Nothing in his hands but the rain.

And the boy looked up at him, water streaming off his chin, and said it again — the words that burned, the words that were older than kingdoms, older than the Edict, older than the silence — and Lena Zhou, who had spent five years learning to be no one, felt the last of his hiding place crack open like ice in spring.

The boy's mouth kept moving. The storm roared. And the language coming out of that small, blue-lipped mouth was the First Tongue, and it was speaking to *him*.
