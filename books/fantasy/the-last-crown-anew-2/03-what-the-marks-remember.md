## 3. What the Marks Remember

The archives of Ashgrave smelled of dust and fear in equal measure.

Sefa had learned to tell the difference between the two within her first week hiding there. Dust was honest—it settled on everything equally, on the banned grammars and the permitted ledgers alike, indifferent to guilt. Fear had a texture all its own. It lived in the way she shelved a codex of pre-Edict liturgy spine-inward so no passing warden would read its title. It lived in the candle she burned down to a stub rather than risk a second trip to the storeroom for more. It lived, most of all, in the silence of the lower stacks, where the books that had survived Marrow's purges sat like orphans, catalogued by no one, mourned by no one, waiting for a reader who would never come because readers who touched such books ended up in the pyre-records.

Sefa touched them anyway. Every night. That was the whole of her disgrace, and she could not bring herself to repent it.

She was copying out a fragment of the Veyran canticles—transcribing by hand into a cipher of her own devising, because memory could be searched but paper could be seized—when the knock came at the archive's side door. Three strikes, then a pause, then two. Not the warden's pattern. Not anyone's pattern.

Her pen stopped mid-stroke. A drop of ink swelled at the nib and fell, blooming across the word *mercy* like a small black wound.

No one came to the side door after the second bell. No one came to the side door *ever*, except the rats and once, memorably, a drunk who had mistaken the archive for a tavern and wept against the wood until dawn. Sefa sat very still in her alcove, listening past the sound of her own pulse, and told herself the sensible things: that scholars were not hunted, only *corrected*; that her name had been struck from the rolls at the Collegium three years ago and struck names were forgotten names; that the Inquisition had larger fish than a woman who loved dead languages too much.

Then the knock came again. Three strikes, pause, two. Impatient now.

She blew out her candle and went to the door with a letter-knife gripped in her fist, which was the most weapon she had ever held in her life, and asked, barely above a whisper: "Who is it?"

"A man with a problem," said a voice, low and roughened, "that only a dead language can solve."

Sefa's heart performed a maneuver she had no name for. "I don't know what you mean."

"Veyra," the voice said. "I need someone who knows Veyra."

For three years she had rehearsed this moment. She had composed denials in the dark, elegant ones, scholarly ones, built on the principle that a lie with citations in it was harder to disbelieve. And now, with the actual moment upon her, every prepared sentence dissolved, and what remained was the naked truth of her, which was hunger.

"Open the door," she heard herself say.

* * *

He was smaller than his reputation. Everyone from the wars was, in Sefa's experience; the stories inflated men like bellows inflate fire, and what walked through her door was just a lean, gray-streaked man with the flat watchfulness of a stray animal and rain running off the hem of a charm-seller's cloak. She knew him by sight the way everyone in Ashgrave's night market knew him—the quiet man who sold luck-knots and fever-charms and never once, in five years, sold anything that worked.

"You're Lena Zhou," she said.

"I'm a man with a locked box." He set a wrapped bundle on her worktable, and his hands, she noticed, were shaking—not with cold. "You were at the Collegium. Before they struck you. You wrote the monograph on pre-Edict liturgical forms. The one they burned."

"They burned four hundred copies." She found she was smiling and made herself stop. "I kept the manuscript. That's why I'm here instead of there."

"I need you to listen to something and tell me what it is." He unwrapped the bundle, and inside was not a box at all but a child—a boy, perhaps seven years old, mud dried in pale rivers down his cheeks, asleep or pretending to sleep against Lena's shoulder with the boneless totality of true exhaustion. "And before you answer, understand that I'll deny ever coming here. If the wardens ask, you dreamed me."

"Everyone in this city dreams things," Sefa said. "It's practically civic policy." She leaned closer to the boy, and the smell of river-mud and wet wool rose off him, and beneath it something else—something faintly mineral, like the air inside a cave that has never known weather. "What's wrong with him?"

"He talks," Lena said. "In his sleep. Listen."

And the child, without waking, without stirring, spoke.

Sefa had spent her life among languages the way other people spent their lives among gardens. She knew the old imperial court-tongue, the trade-pidgin of the salt roads, the high formal registers of the Collegium and the low profanity of its kitchens. She had reconstructed half of proto-coastal from funeral inscriptions and been stripped of her rank for publishing it. She believed she had a scholar's ear, an ear that could find the family resemblance in any tongue, however distant.

The words the boy spoke had no family. They had no cousins, no grandparents, no kin anywhere in the great branching tree of human speech. They were older than the tree. Listening to them was like listening to bedrock speak—each syllable landed in her chest with a weight that had nothing to do with volume, and the single lamp flame bent toward the child as if leaning in to hear.

The oath-marks on Lena Zhou's forearms began to glow.

She hadn't seen them when he entered; the faded brands had looked like nothing, old scars, market-worn skin. Now they woke. Thin lines of pale gold traced themselves up from his wrists along the paths of old vows, and the light moved in time with the boy's speech—pulsing when the syllables pulsed, dimming in the pauses—and Sefa watched a grown man stand perfectly still while his own skin answered a sleeping child in a language he claimed not to know.

When the words stopped, the marks cooled. The boy sighed and burrowed deeper into Lena's collar. The lamp righted itself.

"Well?" Lena said. His voice was steady. His hands were not. "Is it what I think it is?"

Sefa realized she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She made herself let go. Made herself sit. Made herself do the one thing she had been trained for and ruined for and exiled for, which was to *think*.

"Say nothing else," she whispered. "Not here. Not with the wards—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "Ashgrave sits inside a suppression lattice. You know this. Every city under Marrow's edict does. Nine anchor-stones sunk beneath the streets, each one tuned to dampen working-craft within the walls. The lattice doesn't just suppress. It *listens*. It's built to detect resonance—to feel any unauthorized use of power the moment it begins, and report it to the wardens' stones." She looked at the sleeping boy, and felt the terror rise in her throat like floodwater, and made herself keep talking, because talking was the only muscle she trusted. "That thing he just spoke. It wasn't a use of power. It was older than the distinction between use and existence. But the lattice heard it anyway. I'd stake my life on it."

"You already have," Lena said grimly. "Look at your hands."

She looked. Her hands were trembling worse than his.

"That's not fear," she said slowly, wonder creeping up through the dread like green through ash. "Or—it is, but it's not only that. Do you know what happens to a tuning fork when another fork sounds nearby? Sympathetic vibration. The lattice isn't just listening to him, Zhou. It's *answering* him. Every stone in Ashgrave is ringing like a struck bell, quietly, below the threshold of perception, and my body is picking it up because I've spent twenty years handling texts written in that language and some part of me—" she laughed, a thin, unsteady sound, "—some part of me is tuned to it too. God help me. He's reshaping the wards just by dreaming."

Lena sat down heavily on her stool, the boy still in his arms. "Reshaping how?"

"I don't know yet. That's the honest answer, and I hate it." She was already pulling books toward her, the old reflexes asserting themselves over the fear, the hunger winning ground inch by inch. "But I can tell you what the language is, because I've spent three years staring at fragments of it and telling myself I was imagining the patterns. It has no inflectional decay. Every living language rots as it spreads—endings wear smooth, meanings drift. This doesn't. Which means either it was never spoken widely enough to rot, or—" she met his eyes, "—or it isn't a language that was invented at all. It's a language that was *given*. There are nine words in the Collegium's sealed corpus attributed to the First Tongue. Nine. Scraps. Prayers recorded secondhand by priests who admitted they didn't understand what they were writing down. What he just spoke used at least four of those nine roots. It's not descended from the First Tongue, Zhou. It *is* the First Tongue."

"The god's own language," Lena said. Flatly. Like a man reading his own death warrant aloud to check for errors.

"The language that commanded the god's power. Yes." Sefa stood and began to pace, because sitting still had become impossible, because her mind was a fire that had been banked for three years and was now roaring back to life and burning straight through the walls of her caution. "Do you understand what this means? The Edict says magic is outlawed because magic is *chaos*, because the war proved power corrupts—but the First Tongue predates the war, predates the kingdoms, predates every schism we've ever fought over. If the boy speaks it natively—if it's not learned but *inborn*—then it isn't a tool someone taught him to wield. It's more like... a name. Or a wound. Something the world has been carrying around inside itself, waiting."

"And the wards?"

"Are being rewritten around him even as we speak." She stopped pacing. The thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed in perfect clarity. "A suppression lattice is a sentence, Zhou. A long legal sentence written in stone: *no power shall move here.* But sentences can be edited. And if the First Tongue is the grammar underneath all grammar—if it's the language the lattice's own foundation-stones were first conjured in—then everything Marrow's engineers built is standing on ground that answers to that child's voice. He's not breaking the ban. He's *revising* it. Slowly. From underneath. One dream at a time."

The silence that followed was long and full of rain. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors of stone and paper, the city's curfew-bells rang the third hour.

"So the inquisitors will have felt it," Lena said at last.

"The Captain of Inquisition arrived in Ashgrave four days ago," Sefa said. "Doran Vess. I've seen the warden-stone reports—he's hunting what his orders call a 'linguistic anomaly.' I assumed it meant smuggled texts. Heretical grammars." She looked at the boy again—at the mud-dried face, the small fists curled against Lena's chest, the utter ordinariness of a sleeping child—and felt the full vertiginous horror of what had just walked through her door. "They're not looking for a book, are they."

"No."

"How long have you had him?"

"One night."

"One night, and the entire lattice is ringing." Sefa pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. She wanted, desperately, to calculate. To weigh. To do the cold arithmetic that had gotten her expelled from the Collegium in the first place—the arithmetic that always ran: *what would this knowledge be worth, what could it build, what could it prove.* And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and older, the other voice, the one that sounded like her mother: *they burned your book, girl. They will burn you faster.*

"There's a copy of the sealed corpus in the restricted vault," she heard herself say. "Third sublevel. Nine fragments of the First Tongue, with the Collegium's attempted transliterations. If I can compare them against what he speaks—if I can map his grammar onto the fragments—I might be able to determine what the words *do*. Whether they're prayers, or commands, or..." She lowered her hands. "Whether one of them is a key."

"To what?"

"To whatever the god left locked." She met his eyes and did not look away, though every instinct screamed at her to. "Zhou, I have to ask you plainly, because no one will ask it for me: do you understand what you're carrying? Because I have spent three years in this tomb loving dead things, and I am telling you that this child is not dead. Whatever sleeps in that language, your boy is *waking it up*. And there are men in this city whose entire profession is finding things that wake up and putting them on pyres."

Lena looked down at the child for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

"I know what he is," he said. "I just don't know yet what I am to him."

* * *

They worked until the fourth bell.

Sefa laid out her cipher-notes, her transcriptions, the nine fragments copied from memory in a hand so small it was nearly code itself, and beside them she set fresh paper and ink, and together—in the strained, halting way of two people building a bridge across a canyon—they began to translate. Lena would wake the boy gently, coax a word or two from him, a question answered, a name given; the child—Ilo, he was called, though whether that was a name or a fragment Sefa couldn't yet say—would speak in that bedrock voice, and Sefa would write, and the marks on Lena's arms would glow and fade like slow breathing, and outside, beneath the streets, nine ancient stones rang on in frequencies no ear could catch.

By the fourth bell, Sefa had confirmed three things, and each one was worse than the last.

First: the language was real, complete, and structurally alive—which meant it was not a relic. Relics decay. This was being *maintained*.

Second: the boy's speech followed rules of grammar so consistent that Sefa suspected the language did not permit error—that a mistake in the First Tongue was not possible in the way a false note is not possible on a single-stringed instrument.

Third—and this was the one she wrote down and then, after a long moment, scratched out, and then, after a longer moment, wrote again, because a scholar who censors her own findings is no scholar at all:

*The lattice's anchor-stones were originally consecrated in this language. The Edict's foundations are Veyran. We did not ban the god's power. We borrowed it, chained it, and called the chain a law.*

She stared at that sentence for a long time. Then she covered it with her palm, as if it might cry out.

"It's not just listening to him," she said softly. "Oh, Zhou. It's *obeying* him. Slowly. The way a sleeper turns toward a voice calling their name." She looked up. "We have days. Perhaps less. Before the rewriting becomes loud enough that even the wardens' stones can't pretend not to hear it. And when that happens, Doran Vess will come down into these stacks, and he will not be asking about grammars."

Lena stood, gathering the boy against his chest. At the door he paused, and for the first time since entering, he looked at her not as a stranger or a tool but as a person—as one marked thing looking at another.

"You could walk away," he said. "Pretend you dreamed me. You said it yourself—it's practically civic policy."

Sefa looked at her table: the scattered notes, the scratched-out and rewritten truth, the stub of a candle burned down in service of forbidden things. Three years of hiding. Three years of loving dead languages in a tomb, safe and small and slowly suffocating.

"I've been walking away since they burned my book," she said. "I find I've run out of corridor."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face. "Then pack what you can't lose. Because tomorrow night, I'm going to need a smuggler—and after that, I suspect, a miracle."

After the door closed behind them, Sefa sat alone in the dark among her books, one hand still pressed flat over the sentence she should never have written, listening to the stones beneath the city ring like bells, and understanding, at last, exactly what she had prayed for all these years—and how much it was going to cost her that her prayer had finally been answered.
