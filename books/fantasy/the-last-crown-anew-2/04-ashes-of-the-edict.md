## 4. Ashes of the Edict

The bells woke Ashgrave before the sun did.

Lena heard them from the cellar stairs, three quick strikes and one long toll—the signal every child in the city learned before they learned their letters. *Officials in the gate district. Close your shutters. Count your sins.* He stood in the dark with a lamp unlit in his hand and listened to the city below him groan awake, shutters slamming like a thousand small confessions.

Behind him, in the cellar's far corner, the boy stirred under a blanket that smelled of mildew and old turnips.

"Lena." Ilo said it in the First Tongue, because Ilo said everything in the First Tongue—*Lena* rendered in vowels that made the oath-marks along Lena's forearms prickle like ants beneath the skin. "What is the bell?"

"Nothing," Lena answered in Trade Common, keeping his voice low and flat. "A bell."

"You are afraid." The boy sat up, eyes catching what little light came through the grate at street level. Six years old, maybe seven. Mud still lived in the creases of his knuckles from the night he'd appeared on Lena's doorstep, though Lena had scrubbed him twice. "Your heart is loud."

"My heart is none of your business." It came out harsher than he intended, and he saw the flinch, small as a candle guttering, and hated himself for it. He crouched, put the unlit lamp down, and softened by force. "Listen to me. Today, whatever happens—you don't speak. Not a word. Not in your language, not in any language. You're a deaf-mute orphan from the river wards who doesn't understand a thing anyone says. Can you be that?"

Ilo considered this with a gravity no child should carry. Then he nodded once.

"And if someone speaks to you?"

"I hear water," Ilo said, which was not an answer, but when Lena frowned he added, in careful Trade Common he'd been hoarding like coins: "Water. Loud. Nothing else." His mouth twisted around the foreign sounds like they were stones he was learning to swallow. "I can do this."

"Good." Lena lit the lamp at last, and the boy's shadow jumped huge against the cellar wall. "Good."

Upstairs, the shop looked exactly as it always looked: jars of dried lavender and river-clay charms, bundles of knot-grass tied with red thread, little wax seals stamped with harmless nonsense sigils Sefa had designed for him—sigils that meant nothing, did nothing, were worth exactly what superstitious dockworkers paid for them. A shop of lies so small they circled back around to honesty. On the wall behind the counter, hidden under a hanging tapestry of the Twin Rivers, were the oath-marks' originals: the brand-scar on his shoulder blade where Veyra's sigil had been seared into him at nineteen, kneeling in a war-camp, swearing his silence and his service to a god who had, five years later, stopped answering anyone at all.

He pulled the tapestry straight. Checked the latch on the cellar door. Slid the rug over the trap.

Then he opened his shutters onto a street already filling with fear, and waited for the inquisition to come to him.

* * *

They came at midmorning, and they came wrong—not the usual sweep of city guards shaking down stalls for unpaid tariffs, but a column of grey-and-black moving through the market with the terrible unhurried discipline of men who had never once needed to run. Twelve soldiers. Two leashed hounds with pale, hairless hides that seemed to drink the light. And at the center, a tall man in a captain's coat cut clean as a knife wound, riding a horse too fine for Ashgrave's mud.

Lena knew the coat without knowing the man. Inquisitorial black, silver piping, the crowned fist of Kaelen Marrow stitched at the breast. Five years, and the usurper's people had never bothered with Ashgrave's night market except to burn a fortune-teller now and then for show. This was different. This was a hunt.

Captain Doran Vess dismounted in front of the fountain at the market's heart, and his voice carried the way voices carry when they've been trained in halls built for judgment.

"People of Ashgrave." He held up a scroll he did not unroll. "By edict of the Regent-Protector, the Crown seeks an anomaly—a child, or one wearing the shape of a child—who speaks a forbidden tongue. This creature is an instrument of sedition. It carries plague of the soul. Anyone harboring it commits treason against the peace of the realm, and treason, as ever, is punished by fire."

A murmur went through the market like wind through wheat. Lena stood at his stall with his hands folded and his face arranged into the dull, pleasant blankness of a charm-seller who understood perhaps half of what was being said.

Vess continued. "House searches will be conducted district by district, beginning here. You will open your doors. You will answer questions truthfully. And you will look at this—" he gestured, and a soldier held up a slate on which someone had chalked a rendering of a child's face, generic, almost gentle, "—and you will think carefully about whether mercy for a stranger is worth your neighbor's house burning beside yours."

*There it is,* Lena thought. *The old arithmetic.*

The searches began at the fountain and spiraled outward. Lena watched them work with the professional attention of a man who had once been on the other side of such columns—in another country, another life, sweeping villages for Marrow's enemies before Marrow had even taken his crown. They were good. Better than Ashgrave deserved. They worked in pairs, one questioning, one watching the questioner's face, because fear leaked and guilt leaked worse.

And they had the hounds.

Lena had seen ward-hounds once, years ago, at a distance. They were bred—grown, some said—to smell the residue of workings. Harmless charms wouldn't draw them; his stock was clay and lavender and honest fraud. But a child saturated in the First Tongue, a child whose sleep-talk cracked ward-stones across a city—

The trapdoor under the rug might as well have been painted gold.

He sold charms all morning with hands that did not shake, because they had been trained not to shake in worse places than this, and all morning he catalogued exits. The cellar's coal chute opened into the drainage channel behind the tannery—too narrow for the boy? No, the boy was small; narrow was the point. The channel ran south two hundred paces to the culvert under the weir road. If the search reached his shop while the market still crowded the square, there would be chaos enough to cover a muddy child running bent-double through the reek of the tannery flats.

If the search reached his shop while the hounds were fresh, there would be nothing to cover anything at all.

*Move him now*, said the part of him that had survived five years of invisibility. *Now, mid-search, while every eye is on the fountain. Move him and walk away and let the city sort itself out.*

But moving him meant the drainage channel, and the drainage channel flooded when the tide turned, and if a mute river-orphan was seen crawling out of a culvert two streets from an active inquisitorial sweep—

Lena sold a love-charm to a fishwife. His smile held. Inside, the arithmetic gnawed.

* * *

It was nearly noon when they took Old Maretta.

She kept the candle-shop two doors down, a widow with white braids and hands that shook from age rather than guilt, who bought Lena's charms wholesale and resold them to sailors' wives. She had known him six years and asked him nothing, which in Ashgrave was the deepest form of friendship.

Lena heard her before he saw her—an old woman's voice pitched high with indignation, not terror, not yet—and came to his doorway in time to see two soldiers marching her out of her own shop while a third carried armfuls of her candles into the street.

"—ridiculous, I make *candles*, I have made candles for forty years, ask anyone, ask Lena, he'll tell you—"

She had seen Lena. She reached toward him, absurdly, as though he could be a wall between herself and the column.

"Lena! Tell them. Tell the captain. Forty years, candles, my husband poured the molds before me—"

The soldier holding her arm glanced at Lena. So did Captain Vess, standing by the fountain with his scroll finally unrolled, making notes in a small precise hand. The whole market, in fact, had found a reason to look at the charm-seller whose name the old woman had offered up like a coin.

Here was the moment. Lena felt it arrive the way he'd once felt arrows arrive—a cold line of attention drawn straight through him. All he had to do was step forward. Say: *I've known her six years. She's a candle-maker. Whatever you found, you found wrong.* One sentence. Ordinary neighbors did it every day in ordinary cities, at real risk, because that was what belonging cost.

But the hounds were ten feet away, and one of them had lifted its hairless head, nostrils working, oriented—not at Maretta. At *him*. At the shop behind him, at the rug over the trapdoor, at the boy below listening to every word with those ears that heard more than sound.

One sentence from Lena put him at the center of twelve soldiers' attention. Put questions on him. Put *why does the hound stare at your shop, master charm-seller* on him.

Maretta was looking at him. Waiting. Trusting, God help her, trusting him the way she'd trusted him for six years, buying his lies by the dozen because she thought they were kindnesses.

"Tell them," she said again, softer now. Pleading.

Lena's mouth opened.

What came out was: "I—I hardly know her. She buys stock sometimes. Wholesale." His voice sounded like someone else's, thin and reasonable. "I couldn't speak to her character, captain. I'm sorry."

He watched the words land. Watched something go out of the old woman's face—not surprise, worse than surprise, a kind of sad recalculation, the look of a woman doing her own arithmetic and arriving at the same sum. The soldier tightened his grip. She didn't struggle after that. That was the worst part, how quickly she stopped struggling, as if his sentence had weighed more than the soldier's hand.

"They say she had letters," someone whispered near Lena, too low for the soldiers. "Hidden under her floor. Old letters. Writing nobody could read."

Writing nobody could read.

The world tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Lena kept his face stupid and pleasant and inside it a single thought screamed: *the First Tongue looks like nothing, looks like mad scribble, looks exactly like what frightened men find under a widow's floorboards.* Had she found pages? Bought them off a sailor? Curated them like she curated everything, laid them in lavender?

Or had the inquisition simply decided that any unreadable writing justified a pyre, and the truth of it didn't matter, because the anomaly needed to be flushed and fear was the solvent?

They stacked her own candles around her in the fountain square. Dry goods, forty years of her trade, a pyre assembled by efficient soldiers in under twenty minutes while the city watched from its windows. Vess read something formal—Lena heard the words *obstruction*, *forbidden script*, *example*—and struck flint to wick with the same unhurried care he'd shown all morning.

Old Maretta did not cry out. Lena would carry that longer than anything else, he knew even then. She stood in the ring of her own flames and pressed her lips together and looked, once, only once, across the square at the charm-seller who hadn't known her.

Lena looked back. He owed her that much and it wasn't nearly anything, and he gave it anyway, held her gaze while the smoke rose, because closing his eyes would have been a second betrayal and he could only afford the one.

The hounds whined at the fire and turned their heads away. Even they had more decency than the edict required.

When it was done the crowd dispersed in silence, that particular Ashgrave silence, doors opening just wide enough for people to slip back into the routine of survival. Vess consulted his notes. The column moved on, spiraling outward, unhurried, thorough. One of the soldiers paused at Lena's stall, bought a river-clay charm against damp with the flat courtesy of a man following procedure, and moved on.

Lena stood behind his counter until the square emptied. Then he walked, very slowly, to the rug, and lifted it, and went down into the dark.

Ilo was sitting exactly where he'd left him, blanket around his shoulders, eyes enormous.

"He burned," the boy said quietly, in the First Tongue. The words made the marks on Lena's arms ache like weather coming. "The old one. I heard it. I heard her heart stop through the floor."

"Yes."

"She looked at you."

"Yes."

"Why did you not save her?"

Lena crouched in the lamplight, and for a long moment he had no answer that wasn't ash. *Because I was a coward. Because I chose you. Because choosing you was also a kind of cowardice and I cannot tell the difference anymore.* He settled on the truth, stripped bare, because the boy deserved at least that.

"Because saving her would have lost you. And I chose. That's what I did today, Ilo. I chose, and an old woman burned for my choice, and there is no version of me that gets to feel clean about it." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "This is what hiding costs. I told you once I could keep you safe. I should have told you the price."

Ilo studied him for a long time, the way children study things adults hope they won't. Then he reached out and put his small hand flat against the brand-shaped scar high on Lena's forearm, where the oath-mark showed through the rolled sleeve, and said a word Lena didn't know—but felt, all the same, like a hand laid on grief.

"Don't," Lena whispered. "Don't use that word unless—"

"It means *seen*," the boy said. "In the first language. When no one sees you and someone does. That is what it says."

Lena closed his eyes. Above them, boots crossed the shop floor—two pairs, pausing, the creak of someone lifting the tapestry of the Twin Rivers. His whole body went to stone.

The boots lingered. Moved to the counter. Opened a jar—lavender, the lid scraping—and set it down again. A low voice, speaking to a partner outside: "Charm-seller. Clean. Next house."

The boots left.

Lena exhaled for what felt like the first time since noon. Below, Ilo's hand still rested on his scarred arm, warm as a coal.

"We leave tonight," Lena said. "Both of us. Before those hounds get one clear breath of this cellar. There's a smuggler on Rope Street—Ryn Okafor. If anyone can move us out of Ashgrave under that man's nose, it's her."

"What if her price is too high?"

Lena looked at the trapdoor above them, at the memory of smoke still clinging to the city's throat, and almost laughed.

"Kid," he said, "after today, I'd pay anything."

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, faint as a pulse under the floorboards, the First Tongue murmured—*Ilo practicing his silence, failing gently*—and somewhere across the city, Lena would have sworn he felt a ward-stone hum.
