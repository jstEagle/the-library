## 5. The Smuggler's Price

Ryn Okafor counted her coin twice, the way she always did when the night felt wrong.

The Saltcellar was half-empty at this hour—just the usual drunks, a pair of dockhands arguing over dice, and the smell of fried eel and spilled beer that clung to everything in Ashgrave's harbor district like a second skin. She sat in the corner booth with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, shuffling silver marks into neat stacks. Forty-one. Not enough for what she owed the tide-guild, not nearly enough, but honest arithmetic at least. Honest arithmetic was getting harder to find.

The door opened, and there he was.

She'd seen the charm-seller around the market for years—the quiet one with the careful hands and the face you forgot on purpose. He never haggled, never drank, never looked anyone in the eye longer than courtesy demanded. Ryn had catalogued him long ago as harmless and moved on. Harmless men were good customers.

But tonight he walked through the Saltcellar's door like a man carrying something breakable against his chest, and his eyes found hers across the room with the terrible directness of someone who had run out of other options.

*Damn it all,* Ryn thought, and slid her coin away. *He knows what I do.*

Which meant someone had told him. Which meant someone else knew too. Which meant this was already more expensive than it looked.

Lena Zhou crossed the room and stood at her table, rain dripping off his coat onto the floorboards. Up close, she could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, the way his hands wouldn't quite stay still.

"Ryn Okafor," he said. "I need passage out of Ashgrave. Tonight."

"Everyone needs passage out of Ashgrave." She kicked the chair opposite her an inch from the table. "Sit down before you make us both interesting."

He sat. He didn't order anything. Amateurs.

"Out of the city," he said. "Not through the harbor gates. Somewhere Vess's people aren't watching."

Doran Vess. The name landed in Ryn's stomach like a swallowed hook. Everyone in Ashgrave knew the inquisitor captain had come hunting something—a *linguistic anomaly*, the criers called it, which was inquisitor-speak for a person. And here was the market's most invisible man asking to vanish.

"You've got the wrong smuggler," she said. "I move silk and salted fish. I don't move people. People scream, people bleed, people get you hanged."

"I can pay."

"Everyone can pay. That's the problem with money—it makes people brave." She leaned back and studied him properly now, the way she'd study a hull for rot. "Here's my price just to keep listening: who is it? A mistress? A debtor? Somebody Vess wants?"

Lena Zhou was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Behind the bar, the eel-fryer scraped his grill and pretended very hard not to exist.

"A child," Lena said finally. "A boy. About eight years old."

Something cold walked up Ryn's spine.

"And Vess wants him because—"

"Because of what he can say."

Ryn let that sit. Then she laughed, short and ugly, because the alternative was letting him see that her hands had gone cold too. "You want me to smuggle the linguistic anomaly. Out from under the nose of the man whose hounds can smell a lie at forty paces. For money."

"For whatever your price is."

"My price," Ryn said, "is that I don't hang. And you—" she leaned forward, dropping her voice to almost nothing, "—you're going to tell me exactly why a charm-seller with no history and no friends comes to *me*. There are six smugglers working this harbor. Cheaper ones. Stupider ones. Why Okafor?"

And then he did the thing she would think about for years afterward.

He pulled back his sleeve.

* * *

Ryn had seen oath-marks before. Every soldier of a certain age had, in the years before the Silence. Brands of the sworn orders, inked and burned into the forearm in patterns that mapped the body like river deltas. Her brother Kofi used to trace his own in the firelight and tell her they were roads the god could walk.

Kofi had died with his sleeves cut open so the inquisitors could read his arms before the pyre took them.

Lena Zhou's marks were older than most, faded to a dull silver-gray, but Ryn knew the pattern the way you know your own name. The delta of Veyra. The silent god's own geometry, branching from wrist to elbow, each line a vow.

Her mouth went dry.

"Take it off," she heard herself say. Quiet. Too quiet. The fryer actually stopped scraping and left the room.

Lena didn't lower his sleeve. "You recognize it."

"I recognize it." Her voice came out flat as a blade laid on a table. "My brother wore that same river on his arm. Third Sworn Company, the Weeping Campaign. You know the one? Where Veyra's faithful held the ford at Serel for nine days so the retreat could get clear. Nine days. And when the god went silent mid-battle—" her voice cracked and she hated it, hated him for hearing it, "—the power just *stopped*. Mid-word. Mid-prayer. Three hundred sworn soldiers standing in a river holding swords that suddenly weighed what swords should weigh, waiting for a god who'd stopped listening. The Marrow cavalry came through at dusk."

Lena's face had gone gray. "I know the ford at Serel."

"Do you." It wasn't a question. "Were you there?"

"No." His voice was rough. "I was south. Holding a line nobody thanked us for, when the silence came down like a lid. Half my company threw themselves off the cliffs rather than live unsworn." He looked at his own arm like it belonged to a stranger. "I ran instead. I've been running five years."

"Good," Ryn said, and meant it viciously. "One of us should have."

The silence between them filled the whole booth. Outside, the storm was picking up again, rain hammering the shutters in gusts. Somewhere in the city a curfew bell rang, muffled and distant.

"So that's your game," Ryn said at last. "The silent god's leftover mercenary, hiding in a market stall selling love charms to teenagers, and now you're sitting in front of me asking me to ferry the exact thing Vess is burning this city down to find. Do you know what they did to the last Veyra-sworn they caught in Callowmere province? They made him kneel in the square and recite his old vows while they burned the words out of his mouth. Took four hours. They sold tickets."

"I know."

"You know nothing, charm-seller. You hid. I buried." She jabbed a finger at his covered forearm. "That mark killed my brother. Not the brand itself—the *faith*. The faith that told three hundred men to stand in a river and wait for a miracle that had already packed up and left. And now you want me to risk my neck, my ship, my crew, for another piece of that faith?"

"The child isn't faith," Lena said quietly. "He's eight years old. He's mute except for one language, and that language is making the wards around this city crack like ice on a pond. And if Vess gets him, Marrow doesn't just finish conquering the free cities. He finishes *everything* the god started, but pointed the other way."

Ryn stared at him.

"You've talked to Sefa," she said. "The archivist ghost. Of course you have." She dragged both hands down her face. "Gods below. A branded deserter, a disgraced scholar, and a child who speaks the First Tongue. That's not a cargo, Zhou. That's a funeral procession with extra steps."

"Then say no."

The words hung there. Say no. Walk away. Let the boy burn or worse, let the wards fail, let the world keep doing what it had been doing since the Silence—grinding down everything soft until only crowns and chains were left.

Ryn thought about Kofi tracing his marks in the firelight. She thought about the tide-guild debt, forty-one silver marks against two hundred owed. She thought about the look on the fryer's face when he'd fled the room, and how everyone in Ashgrave had learned the same survival trick: be somewhere else when the bad thing happens.

She'd been somewhere else her whole life.

"What's your real price?" Lena asked. "Not the coin. You didn't come to this table for coin—you came because you already decided, and you hate yourself for it. So name it. Whatever it is."

And that was the worst part, that he'd seen it. That he'd sat there with his dead god's river on his arm and read her like a manifest.

Ryn looked out the shutter at the rain. When she spoke, her voice was steady, which cost her more than screaming would have.

"When we get out—and if we get out—you take me to wherever this ends. The abbey, the cage, whatever's left of your silent god at the bottom of it. And you let me ask it one question." Her jaw tightened. "Why. One word. *Why.* Nine days at the ford, and nothing. My brother drowned in armor waiting for an answer. I want to stand where it went quiet and hear it say *why*."

"That's not a price," Lena said softly. "That's a wound."

"It's both. Take it or leave it."

Lena Zhou held out his hand across the table—an old soldier's gesture, palm open, the way sworn companies sealed contracts before contracts needed paper. Ryn looked at it for a long time. The oath-marks disappeared under his rolled cuff, but she could see them anyway, printed on the inside of her eyelids, the same rivers that had flowed down her brother's arm into the mud at Serel.

She shook it.

"One condition of mine," she said. "The boy stays below decks the whole passage. If Vess's hounds board us, I give the signal and you get him into the bilge hatch, and whatever happens topside, you don't come up. You swear it on those marks. All of them."

"I swear it."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "Sworn men always are. But it'll hold long enough."

* * *

They left the Saltcellar separately, ten minutes apart, Ryn first. The rain had settled into a steady gray curtain, the kind that made the whole city feel underwater. She moved through it the way she'd learned to move through everything—fast, low, forgettable—cutting down Tanner's Row toward the wet dock where the *Kindly Tide* sat low on her mooring lines, fat with contraband and ready to run.

Halfway there, a lantern flared in an alley mouth ahead, and Ryn stopped walking.

Three figures. Oilskin cloaks, the cut of them wrong for dock work, right for inquisition auxiliaries. One held the lantern high; the other two had the loose-kneed stance of men who'd been standing in the rain a long time, waiting for someone specific.

"Evening, captain," the lantern-holder said. Not a dock inspector's voice. A clerk's voice, polite and dry. "Ryn Okafor, master of the *Kindly Tide*. Routine questions about your next sailing."

"Next sailing's salted fish to Greywater," Ryn said. "Ask the harbormaster. He loves company."

"Our records show you haven't filed a destination bond yet." The clerk smiled. Rain dripped off his hood. "Curious, for a ship that sails tomorrow. Unless it's not going where the fish go."

Ryn kept her breathing even and her hands loose. Behind the clerk, one of the auxiliaries shifted his weight, and she saw the shape under his oilskin that meant crossbow.

"They're filing bonds in the morning like everyone," she said. "Or does the inquisition file paperwork for honest traders now? Because I've got a cargo list you're welcome to read, and a tide-guild stamp you're welcome to kiss."

The smile didn't waver. "We'll check again tomorrow evening. Before you sail." He tipped his hood an inch—not quite mockery, but close. "Safe harbors, Captain Okafor."

They walked past her into the rain. Ryn stood in the middle of Tanner's Row until they were gone, counting her heartbeat down from the red line, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: Vess already suspected her. The net wasn't coming—it was here, and it had been here since the charm-seller walked into the Saltcellar, maybe longer.

Second: tomorrow evening was a deadline, not a threat. Which meant tonight was the only night there was.

She turned and walked the rest of the way to the wet dock through the rain, past the *Kindly Tide*'s riding lamp, up the gangplank to where her bosun, a one-eyed woman named Petra, was mending netting under the awning.

"Change of plans," Ryn said. "We sail tonight. Midnight tide, no bond, no lights."

Petra's needle paused. "Cargo?"

Ryn looked out at the black water, at the drowned undercity gates beneath it, at the whole sleeping weight of Ashgrave that wanted very badly for her to stay ordinary and invisible and somewhere else.

"A passenger," she said. "Two, counting the small one. And Petra—if anyone asks what we're carrying—"

"We're carrying fish," Petra said.

"We're carrying fish." Ryn pulled her collar up against the rain. "Get the bilge hatch cleared. And send word to the charm-seller's shop: midnight, the weir gate, the old stairs. Tell him to bring the scholar too, and tell him—" she hesitated, hearing her own voice say the next part like it belonged to someone braver, "—tell him to bring the boy's ears warm. Cold water's hard on children."

Petra raised her one eyebrow. "Since when do you care about passengers' ears?"

"Since never," Ryn said, and went to find her charts. "That's what scares me."

Below the wet dock, far under the harbor's black skin, the drowned gates of the old city waited in the dark—and somewhere above them, in a cellar full of harmless charms, a child who spoke the god's own tongue was about to learn that midnight had teeth.
