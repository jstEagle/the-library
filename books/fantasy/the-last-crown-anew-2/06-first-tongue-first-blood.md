## 6. First Tongue, First Blood

The cellar had become a country of its own in the three days since Ilo arrived. Lena had built it the way he built everything now—quietly, with whatever lay at hand. A pallet of folded sacking. A candle stub he rationed like a miser. The boy's muddy clothes, washed and drying on a line strung between a nail and the iron hook where Lena once hung charms for curing fevers.

He told himself it was temporary. Three days, maybe four, until Sefa finished translating enough of the child's speech to tell them what he was saying—until Ryn named her price and they could be gone from Ashgrave before Vess's hounds caught a scent. Temporary was a word Lena had learned to live inside, the way other men lived inside houses.

He came down the ladder after midnight with bread and a cup of watered wine, and stopped on the last rung because the candle had gone out.

Not blown out. The flame stood upright in its own wax, frozen, a needle of light that did not flicker when his breath moved across it. And Ilo sat up on the pallet in the dark, eyes open but not seeing, lips moving around a word that had no shape Lena's mouth could have made—a word like a door opening underwater, like the first syllable of a name spoken before there were ears to hear it.

The cellar flooded with light.

It did not come from anywhere. It simply *was*, rising out of the stone floor and the boy's skin and the spaces between Lena's ribs, white-gold and total, and for one suspended heartbeat Lena was five years younger and standing in a war-camp at dawn while ten thousand sworn soldiers knelt in the mud because the god had passed over them like weather. He felt his oath-marks ignite along his forearms—not pain this time, not exactly. Recognition. The old brands waking like dogs hearing their master's step on a road long empty.

Ilo spoke the word again, softer, and this time Lena heard it with something deeper than his ears. It went through him the way a bell's note goes through water. The walls sweated condensation. The jar of fever-charms on the shelf rang faintly, all of them, in unison, though nothing had touched them.

Then it ended. The light drained away like tide off sand, the candle remembered how to burn, and Ilo slumped back onto the pallet, breathing slow and even, asleep again before the last glow left the stones. His face was peaceful. Whatever lived in him had used his mouth and gone back to its silence, and the boy knew nothing of it.

Lena stood on the ladder with the bread going cold in his hand and his heart slamming against his oath-marks, and he understood two things with terrible clarity.

The first: the child was not cursed. Curses leaked. Curses festered and spread rot at their edges, and Lena had seen enough cursed men in Veyra's wars to know the smell. This was not leakage. This was *broadcast*—a voice raised in a dark room, not knowing the room had listeners. Ilo wasn't speaking to anyone. He was speaking *through* everything, and everything answered.

The second: somewhere above them, across the sleeping city, every ward-stone and ward-warden and inquisitor's scrying glass had just heard a god clear its throat.

He set down the bread. His hands were steady, which surprised him; five years of performing ordinariness had taught his body to be calm in exactly the moments his mind was screaming. He crossed the cellar and crouched by the pallet, and Ilo's hand found his wrist in his sleep—the way children do, anchoring themselves to warmth—and rested there over the oath-marks as if holding them shut.

"Who taught you that word?" Lena whispered, in trade-tongue, then in the border dialect, then, feeling foolish, in the three words of the First Tongue Sefa had drilled into him that afternoon. *Veyra. Silence. Home.*

The boy slept on. But under Lena's wrist, faint as a pulse in a drowned man's throat, the marks burned warmer.

* * *

He did not sleep. He climbed to the shop and watched the street through the shutter-slats, and within the hour he saw what he'd known he would see.

Ashgrave's night watch moved differently when something was wrong. They didn't hurry—that was the tell. Hurrying drew eyes. Instead they thickened, the way blood thickens around a wound. Lanterns clustered at the corner of Tanner's Row where no lanterns needed to be. Two figures in grey-and-silver stood talking outside the chandler's, and though they wore no livery, they wore it in their posture: the stillness, the way their heads turned together like birds. Inquisition. Not Vess himself—yet—but his reach, stretching into the dark toward whatever had just sounded across the city like a struck bell.

Lena counted them the old way, the way he'd once counted enemy campfires. Four visible. Which meant eight, which meant twelve. And one of them would be a warden with a resonance-glass, and the glass would point, however fuzzily, however blurred by the city's layered wards, toward a charm-shop on a street too poor to matter.

They had until dawn. Maybe less.

He went down into the cellar and shook Sefa awake. She'd insisted on staying close since the second day, sleeping in the corner behind the wine racks with her codex pages wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a breastplate. She surfaced from sleep the way scholars do—all at once, eyes sharp before her body agreed to follow.

"What time is it?"

"The kind where we leave." He kept his voice low, beneath the boy's sleeping breath. "He spoke. In his sleep. One word."

Sefa went very still. "You're certain it was the First Tongue."

"Sefa. The candle froze. The cellar lit up like the inside of a star. My marks—" He pushed back his sleeve. In the dark she couldn't see them properly, but she leaned close anyway, and her breath caught.

"They're warm," she said. "Lena, they're never warm. You told me yourself they've been dead weight for five years. Scars don't—"

"This isn't a scar answering. It's an ear." He pulled the sleeve down. "Whatever he is, he's not leaking power. He's calling. Every ward-stone in Ashgrave heard him, and so did every glass Vess's people carry. We're done hiding in plain sight. Plain sight just started glowing."

Sefa sat back against the wine rack, and he watched her do the thing she always did—retreat into the problem, make it a puzzle, because puzzles were safer than fear. "One word," she murmured. "A single phoneme shouldn't carry past the room. Unless the speaker isn't generating the sound but *resonating* it—using the wards themselves as a medium. That's why the ban-wards are degrading around him. He's not breaking them. He's teaching them his accent." Her eyes came up, bright and frightened. "Do you understand what that means? The edict-wards were built to smother magic. If they can be made to carry it instead—"

"It means we leave tonight." He said it flatly, and she flinched, and he hated himself a little for the flinch and loved her a little for making it. "Sefa. Look at me. The council of your beloved archives can debate resonance theory when we're beyond Vess's reach. Here, now, tonight: the boy spoke one word in his sleep, and the inquisition felt it from across the city. What happens when he has a nightmare?"

That landed. He watched it land—watched her imagine it, the whole city lighting up, the boy screaming in the god's own voice while grey-coats kicked in doors down every street that pointed at them.

"I need Ryn," Lena said. "Her boat, her route, her price. Tonight."

"She'll bleed you."

"She'll try." He was already moving, gathering the go-bag he'd packed the night Ilo arrived and hadn't let himself believe in until now. Charms that were actually useful beneath the harmless ones. Coin. The knife he'd sworn never to draw again, which he took anyway, because vows were a luxury of men whose gods still spoke to them.

At the foot of the ladder he paused. Ilo had turned in his sleep, curled small, one fist tucked under his chin. Mud-stains still ghosted his knuckles, from roads Lena didn't know yet and would learn. The boy looked, in that moment, like nothing at all—like any orphan the war years had coughed up onto any doorstep.

Except the stones around him were warm. Except the candle bent slightly toward him, like a flower toward sun.

Lena crouched and, very carefully, laid his branded forearm against the boy's small shoulder. An oath-mark to a living word. The old soldiers' gesture, the one you made to a fallen comrade you couldn't carry home. *I see you. I will not leave you for the crows.*

The marks sang, faint and high, like a tuning fork struck under water.

"All right," Lena said quietly, to the boy, to the god, to whoever was listening. "You've got my attention. Just—hold on. Hold on till we're out of this city."

Above them, distantly, a whistle cut the night. Then another, answering, closer.

* * *

Ryn Okafor came to the back door at the third knock, dressed, armed, and furious, which Lena understood meant she'd already heard. Smugglers heard bells before they rang.

"You woke half the waterfront," she said, instead of hello. "Don't bother denying it. My people say the harbor wards hummed at moonset like a struck gong, and the watch is pulling boats out of the water for inspection. Boats, Zhou. Do you know what that costs me? Do you know what it costs *me* when the inquisition gets nervous about water?"

"One word," Lena said. "He said one word in his sleep."

Ryn stared at him. Rain had come back with the small hours, beading in her cropped hair, running down the scar that split her left eyebrow. Behind her, in the smuggler's den that smelled of tar and wet rope, her crew pretended very hard not to listen.

"And you waited—" she checked nothing, because she carried no clock but her anger, "—hours? To come to me? While the city fills up with grey-coats?"

"I needed to know what I was asking."

"You needed to know *before* the sky lit up, you absolute—" She stopped. Breathed. Ran a hand down her face, and when it came away, something in her expression had shifted from fury to the colder thing underneath it, the arithmetic. "How many exits do you think I have out of this city? Three. One's a toll-gate with inquisitors on it by now. One's a chimney flue that fits children and corpses. The third is the weir gate, under the river, through the drowned quarter. It floods on the turn of the tide, it's crawling with things the city forgot to kill, and it opens on the salt marsh where every honest patrol in Ashgrave watches the horizon."

"But it's open."

"It's open." She said it like a curse. "And you want me to take a branded deserter, a disgraced archivist, and a walking beacon-child through it, tonight, with the watch pulling every hull out of the harbor. You want me to bet my crew, my boat, and my neck on a boy who glows."

"He doesn't glow."

"I heard the harbor wards, Zhou. From the *waterfront*." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something that wasn't anger anymore, or wasn't only. "You know what I know about those marks on your arms? I know the same brand was on the men who burned my brother's village for harboring a 'heretic tongue.' I know Veyra-sworn soldiers held the line while the fires were lit, because the god's law outranked human law, and none of them—wept. So forgive me if the sight of you asking me for mercy makes me want to laugh until I cry."

Lena took it. He'd earned worse. "I'm not asking for mercy. I'm asking for passage. And I'm telling you the truth, which is worth more: that child is the only thing in Ashgrave the inquisition wants more than they want you, and if Vess takes him, Marrow gets a weapon that ends the free cities. Not weakens. *Ends.*"

"And why should I care about the free cities?"

"Because your brother cared. Because everyone who ever fought under that banner thought they were fighting for something, and Marrow stole it and turned it into engines. This is the chance to steal it back." He held her gaze. "Or don't. Name your price and I'll pay it, and hate myself, and we'll both be even."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere behind her, a crewman coughed, and someone else hissed him quiet.

Ryn looked at him for a long moment, and he saw her doing the thing he'd seen commanders do at the end of lost battles: weighing not whether they could win, but what losing would cost beyond themselves.

"My price," she said finally, "isn't coin. Coin I can get anywhere. My price is that when this is over—if any of us are over anything—you tell me everything. What the marks mean. What the boy says. Why your god went quiet and whether it's coming back. Everything, Zhou, on your life. I spent ten years hating a silence. I want to know what it was hiding."

"That's it?"

"That's it." She turned away, already shouting orders in the low clipped cant of the waterfront—*Wrenna, wake the boat. Doss, the weir gate keys, the ones we don't officially have.* Then, over her shoulder, without softness: "Get your scholar and your beacon. We leave from the tannery steps at the change of tide. If you're late, I sail without you, and I'll sleep fine."

* * *

The tannery steps stank of lime and old blood, and the tide was turning, and Lena came down them with Ilo on his back and Sefa clutching her oilcloth-wrapped codex and both of them moving fast through the rain because the whistles had started again—closer now, organized, a net being drawn through the streets grid by grid.

Halfway down, Sefa grabbed his arm. "Listen."

He listened. Under the rain, under the river's mutter, there was a new sound: a deep, grinding hum, like a millstone turning in the earth's bones. The ward-stones. All of them. The whole buried lattice of the magic-ban that ringed Ashgrave like a collar, singing at a pitch it had never sung in five years of enforced silence.

And threaded through the hum, thin and clear as a wire:

Ilo, awake now on Lena's back, was humming. Matching it. Harmonizing with the cage around the city the way a child hums along with a songbird, innocent, unconscious, learning the tune.

The nearest ward-stone—a squat pillar at the mouth of the alley, older than the edict, its runes worn smooth—cracked.

Not broke. *Cracked.* A single fissure, hairline-fine, running top to bottom with a sound like ice on a spring lake, and through the crack bled a thread of white-gold light that bent, impossibly, toward the boy on Lena's back like a plant toward the sun.

Sefa made a sound that was half gasp, half prayer. "It's resonating with him. Lena—it's not suppressing him anymore, it's *answering* him—"

"Later." He said it gently, which cost him. "Scholar-later. Right now, smuggler-now."

But he understood, walking down into the reek of the tannery with the cracked stone bleeding light behind them and the whistles closing like a hand around the city's throat. He understood what Sefa would take days to prove and what Vess already knew in his bones from across the darkened city: the boy wasn't hiding the way Lena hid. The boy couldn't hide. Every hour in Ashgrave was a hour the cage grew thinner, the light grew louder, the net drew tighter.

Ilo's small hands gripped Lena's shoulders. Against the back of his neck, the boy's breath was warm, and when he spoke—awake this time, deliberate, in the language that predated kingdoms—it was only one word, and Sefa translated it in a whisper that trembled:

"*Faster,*" she said. "He said *faster.*"

The river opened ahead of them, black and rain-hammered, and at the foot of the tannery steps Ryn Okafor stood beside a flat-bottomed skiff with her crew already aboard, and her face, when she saw the light still bleeding from the cracked ward-stone up the alley, went pale and set all at once.

"Board," she said. "Everyone. Now. Before that—" she jabbed a knife-hand at the glow, "—brings the whole grey army down on my boat."

Lena swung Ilo down into the skiff. The boy's eyes found his in the dark, wide and unafraid in a way that frightened Lena more than any whistle, and the oath-marks along his arms burned like banked coals as the skiff pushed off into the current, toward the drowned quarter, toward the weir gate, toward the open dark beyond the city that had been his grave and his hiding place for five years.

Behind them, upstream, torches began to gather on the banks like a constellation falling to earth.

"Hold on to something," Ryn said, and put the boat at the river.
