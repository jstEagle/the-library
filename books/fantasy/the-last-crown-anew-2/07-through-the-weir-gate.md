## 7. Through the Weir Gate

The rain hadn't stopped since the ward-stone cracked, and Ryn Okafor had learned long ago that when the sky wept over Ashgrave, the undercity drowned first.

She stood at the mouth of the Weir Gate with a storm-lantern in one hand and a knife in her teeth while she tied off the last knot on the skiff's mooring line. The gate itself was older than the city above it—a black arch of riverstone furred with moss, its keystone carved with a face that some dead mason had given a serene expression, as if drowning were something to be calm about. Beyond it, the flooded galleries of old Ashgrave stretched away into darkness: streets that had been streets once, before the river was turned and the low quarters were left to the water. Now they belonged to eels, drowned bells, and smugglers.

Ryn belonged to the third category. Barely.

"You said this would be quick," she said, not turning around. "Quick, you said. In and out before the tide turns."

Behind her, Lena Zhou came down the slick steps with the child on his back and a scholar's satchel banging against his hip. He looked like a man who had slept in a cellar for three days and dreamed of inquisitors, which, as far as Ryn knew, was exactly what he was.

"The tide turns in an hour," Lena said. "That's why we're going now."

"That's why we're going now," Ryn repeated, flat. "Wonderful. Nothing like a rising flood to make an escape feel festive."

Ilo peered over Lena's shoulder at the black water. The boy hadn't spoken—not in any tongue Ryn understood—since she'd met him two days ago, all sharp angles and mud and eyes too old for his face. But he watched everything. He watched her hands on the rope. He watched the lantern flame lean with the wind off the river. He watched the drowned archway the way other children watched festival fires, with that hungry, unblinking attention that made the hair on Ryn's arms stand up.

Sefa came last, hunched under an oilcloth, clutching a wrapped bundle against her chest the way a miser clutches coin. The codex. Ryn had seen what it cost them—the archive fire, the alarms, Sefa's hands still shaking from where she'd pried it out of a locked case while smoke ate the shelves around her. The woman had traded her whole hidden life for that book. She held it now like it might still be taken from her.

"Boat holds four," Ryn said. "Barely. Anyone brings luggage beyond that bundle, they swim."

"This is not luggage." Sefa's voice cracked on the second word. She cleared her throat. "It's the only complete record of the First Tongue's conjugations outside the usurper's vaults."

"It's paper, scholar. Paper floats. You don't."

"Then I'll hold my breath."

Ryn looked at her—this disgraced archivist with soot still ground into the creases of her knuckles—and saw something she recognized and hated seeing: a person who had already decided what they'd die holding onto. Ryn had buried enough of those. Her brother had been one. Branded by Veyra-sworn mercenaries in a war nobody won, drowned in a canal not half a mile from this gate, and Ryn had sworn on his body that she'd never again carry anyone whose faith weighed more than their sense.

And here she was, running four fugitives through a flooded city because a branded mercenary had paid her—badly—in favors and desperation.

"Get in," she said. "All of you. Before I come to my senses."

* * *

The undercity swallowed them by degrees.

First the sound of the rain faded, replaced by the drip and echo of the galleries. Then the lantern-light shrank to a small gold room around them, and the dark took everything else. Ryn poled them through streets where second-story balconies hung overhead like ribs, where a fountain stood submerged to its cherubs' chins, where shop signs in dead merchants' languages swung gently in the current, creaking their silent advertisements to no one.

"Keep your hands inside," she murmured. "There are things in here that bite, and most of them used to be people's pets."

Sefa made a small horrified noise. Ilo leaned out anyway, fascinated, trailing his fingers an inch above the water. Where his fingertips passed, Ryn noticed—though she told herself she hadn't—the ripples went strangely still, smoothing out behind him like a bed being made.

Lena sat at the prow with his coat hooded up, and Ryn could see the oath-marks crawling up the back of his neck even in the bad light: the interlocking rings of the silent god, inked into skin with something that had never quite healed right. Five years old, those marks. Five years since every Veyra-sworn blade in the world had gone quiet at once, mid-prayer, mid-battle, mid-scream. She'd seen the aftermath. She'd helped bury some of it.

"So," she said, mostly to break the silence, partly because she was cruel and tired. "You're going to tell me where we're actually going, or am I poling us into the sea on faith?"

"Callowmere," Lena said. "Through the salt marshes. There's a channel Ryn—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "There's a channel *you* know."

"I know it. That's not the same as liking it." Ryn planted the pole and shoved. "Callowmere's free, if you call a council of merchants freedom. They'll take the boy. Question is what they'll want in trade."

"They'll want what everyone wants," Sefa said quietly, from behind her bundle. "A weapon."

"He's a child," Lena said.

"He's a child who speaks the language that used to move armies," Sefa said. "People don't stop wanting things just because the things are terrible. Believe me. I've read what people wanted."

The lantern guttered. Somewhere far behind them—far above, back in the living city—a bell began to ring. Not the curfew bell. Not the tide bell. A fast, ugly, repeating pattern that Ryn knew from her worst years: the pursuit bell. Three-and-one, three-and-one. Hounds on a line.

Vess.

Her hands didn't shake. She'd trained that out of herself years ago, the way you train a scar out of a story. But her jaw tightened until her teeth ached.

"How did he find the cellar?" Lena asked, low.

"Doesn't matter how. Matters that he did." Ryn swung the skiff hard down a side channel, under a collapsed arcade where the water ran shallow and fast. "Inquisitor Captain Doran Vess doesn't bring hounds somewhere unless he intends to leave with what he came for. Or ashes. Usually both."

The bell rang on. And beneath it, fainter but closer, another sound began: splashing. Measured. Disciplined. Boots in flooded streets, wading in formation.

"They're in the undercity," Sefa whispered. "How are they in the undercity? The entrances are sealed—"

"Marrowners buy seals the way other men buy bread." Ryn poled faster, the skiff hissing through the black water. "There's a grate ahead, then the weir channel, then open marsh. If we can reach the weir before they reach us—"

The hounds found them before the grate did.

They came around a drowned colonnade in a wall of lantern-light—six men in oilskin, wading chest-deep with crossbows held high, and behind them the dogs, big river-hounds bred for this exact work, swimming with their heads low and their eyes flat. At their center, dry as a sermon, walked Doran Vess on a floating causeway of planks laid by his own soldiers, his grey coat buttoned to the throat, his face carrying the mild disappointment of a man who has caught children stealing jam.

"There," Vess said, without urgency. "The skiff. Take the scholar alive. The child unmarked. The mercenary—" his pale eyes found Ryn's across the water, and something flickered there, something almost like recognition, almost like grief, gone too fast to name, "—at the captain's discretion."

"Discretion," Ryn muttered. "That's new."

"Lena." Sefa's voice had gone thin. "Lena, the codex—if they take the boat—"

"Hold it," Lena said. "Whatever happens, hold it."

The first bolt hit the skiff's rail a hand's width from Ryn's thigh. The second clipped the lantern, and the flame died, and the world went black except for the distant grey glow of the weir channel and the white wake-lines of the swimming hounds.

Ryn did the arithmetic she'd done her whole life: four souls, one boat, six crossbows, no cover, no time. The arithmetic always came out the same. Somebody pays.

"Down!" she roared, and shoved the skiff sideways with the pole as a volley hissed overhead, and then the hounds hit them.

* * *

Later, Ryn would remember it in pieces, the way drowning people remember weather.

The skiff lurching as a hound's weight came over the gunwale. Her knife finding something warm. Sefa screaming—not for herself; Ryn realized that much even then; the scholar was screaming about the bundle, holding it above the water with both arms as the boat rolled. Lena putting his body between the child and a soldier's boarding-hook, taking the hook across the shoulder with a sound like tearing canvas.

And Ilo—

Ilo standing up in the capsizing wreck of the skiff, standing on the gunwale as it went under, a small soaked figure in the lantern-light of their enemies, and opening his mouth.

He spoke one word.

Ryn didn't know it. She would spend the rest of her life not knowing it, hearing it in dreams sometimes, a syllable shaped like a wave drawing back before it breaks. But the water knew it. The whole drowned city knew it.

The flood stopped.

Not drained—stopped. Every ripple in every gallery froze mid-curl, the way a crowd freezes when a king enters. The hounds went still in the water, treading, whining. The wading soldiers staggered as the current that had been pushing against their chests simply ceased to push. Even the drips from the ceiling hung suspended, a thousand small silver beads, falling nowhere.

And then the water moved the other way.

It gathered. It rose off the flooded streets in slow ropes and sheets, gathering itself toward the soldiers like a herd animal answering a whistle, and the men broke—one dropped his crossbow, one turned and fled through chest-deep water that suddenly, horribly, cooperated with his flight, hurling him backward down the gallery. Vess stood on his plank causeway as the water rose around it in a smooth black wall taller than a man, and Ryn saw the inquisitor captain look up at that wall, and she saw his face do something she had never seen an inquisitor's face do.

He stepped back.

"Row," Lena gasped, hauling the skiff upright by sheer stubbornness, hauling Sefa in by the collar, hauling Ilo in last, the boy limp and gray-faced as a landed fish. "Ryn. *Row.*"

She rowed. She rowed like the tide itself owed her money, through the grate, into the weir channel, into the roaring dark where the frozen flood finally remembered its business and crashed down behind them with a sound like the end of an age. The skiff shot out of the undercity into the open marsh, into rain and wind and the vast indifferent grey of the estuary, and behind them Ashgrave's skyline burned its lamps against the storm, and somewhere in that glittering mess a man named Doran Vess was standing dry in a drowned street, reconsidering his life.

* * *

They made the reed-flats before anyone spoke.

Ryn grounded the skiff on a shelf of mud among the salt-grass and sat there, chest heaving, pole across her knees, watching the boy. Ilo lay in the bilge with his head in Sefa's lap, unconscious, breathing in small careful sips. His lips were cracked. His fingers twitched, once, twice, like a sleeper working through a dream of heavy lifting.

"What did he say?" Ryn asked finally. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Back there. What was the word?"

"It's not a word the way you mean words." Sefa pushed wet hair off the boy's forehead with a tenderness that seemed to surprise even her. "It's more like... a name the water answers to. Or an instruction the water was always waiting to receive." She looked down at him, and Ryn saw the hunger in the scholar's face again, that terrible loving appetite, and saw her fight it down. "He shouldn't be able to do that. Not alone. Not untrained. The codex says the First Tongue required choirs—dozens of speakers in harmony—to shape anything that size."

"Well, he's a poor listener to the rules."

"Everything about him is a poor listener to the rules." Sefa's hand stilled on the boy's brow. "That's rather the point of him, I think."

Lena sat at the bow with his ruined shoulder cradled against the hull, saying nothing, watching the child with an expression Ryn couldn't file anywhere. It wasn't fear. It wasn't wonder. It was closer to recognition—the look of a man hearing a song he'd forgotten he knew.

Ryn looked away from both of them and out at the marsh, where the rain was easing and the first dirty light of dawn was finding the channels. Her brother's canal was thirty miles south of here. She hadn't been back. She'd told herself it was because of the warrants, the debts, the practical reasons, the good sensible reasons.

But sitting in a leaking skiff with a branded deserter, a book-thief, and a child who commanded floods, she finally admitted the truth she'd been poling away from for five years: she wasn't angry at the god who went silent. She was angry at the people who'd worn its marks and spent its power like coin, and she'd hated Lena Zhou on sight for a brand he clearly hadn't chosen, the way you hate a uniform after a war.

"You could have run," she said. She didn't look at him. "Back in the cellar. When Vess came. You could have handed the neighbor's kid a charm and walked out clean. Instead you've got a hole in your shoulder and a price on your head."

Lena was quiet a moment. "Could say the same about you. You got paid in favors and desperation. Your words."

"Yeah, well." Ryn picked up the pole. "Desperation's the only currency that's never devalued."

Ahead, the marsh opened toward the sea road and the long grey miles to Callowmere. Behind, Ashgrave smoked faintly against the storm-light, and Ryn found herself thinking, with a strange lack of dread, that she'd probably never see it again—that whatever she'd been running from for five years was in this boat now, and it was wet, and bleeding, and arguing about conjugations, and for the first time since her brother died it felt less like cargo than like crew.

"Don't make it weird," she said, to no one in particular, and shoved off into the channel.

It was Sefa who found it, an hour later, when the rain finally quit and the boy woke. She was checking his pulse and he caught her wrist—gently, the way you catch something you're afraid of breaking—and spoke. Two words this time, soft, deliberate, looking straight at her as if asking permission.

Sefa went white. Then she laughed, a broken, astonished laugh, and translated with shaking hands:

"He says—he says he's sorry about the boat."

Ryn snorted despite herself. Then the boy turned his head, found Ryn's eyes across the skiff, and said a third word. And this one she didn't need translated, because Sefa's face told her everything: her own name, *Ryn*, in a language older than kingdoms, spoken like a promise—or a debt.

She poled harder, and didn't look back at Ashgrave, and told herself the chill down her spine was just the rain.
