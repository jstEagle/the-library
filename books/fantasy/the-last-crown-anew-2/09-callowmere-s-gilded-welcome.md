## 9. Callowmere's Gilded Welcome

Callowmere announced itself before we saw it.

We crested the last ridge of the salt road at dusk, and there it was—spread across the delta like a spilled purse of gold. Lantern-light on the canals. The great sea-gates open, hung with banners of the Concord: seven silver fish on green, one for each free city that had refused to kneel to Kaelen Marrow. Bells ringing from the Harbor Temple, and not alarm bells either. Welcome bells.

Ryn, propped against the wagon's rail with her bandaged shoulder and a face gray as old ash, snorted. "They always ring the bells when they want something."

"They're ringing them for Ilo," Sefa said.

"They're ringing them because they've decided what they want, and they're about to tell us the price." Ryn spat over the side, delicately, the way sailors do, as if the road were a deck. "I've run cargo into this city for eleven years. Nobody rings bells for cargo."

Ilo sat between us on the wagon bed, wrapped in Sefa's spare cloak, watching the city come toward us with an expression I had learned to read the way I'd once read weather before a fight. He wasn't afraid, exactly. He was *listening*. He'd been listening for three days—since the codex, since Sefa had read aloud what Veyra had been and what the boy carried in his small chest. He listened the way a man listens at the door of a house where someone is speaking his name.

"Words," he said, in trade-speech. His second word of it ever, and he pronounced it carefully, like a coin he'd been given and wasn't sure was real.

"That's right," I said. "The city is full of words. We'll find some that are true."

I didn't believe it when I said it. Ilo looked at me anyway, the way he always did when I said things I didn't believe, as though he could taste the lie and was deciding, out of some private mercy, not to spit it back at me.

* * *

They met us at the sea-gates, which should have been my first warning. Councils don't come to gates. Gates come to councils.

There were six of them, robed in the Concord colors, flanked by a honor guard of pikemen whose armor was too polished for anyone who'd ever stood in a line. At their head was a broad, silver-haired woman with a councilor's chain of office and a smile that had been fitted by a craftsman. She introduced herself as Councilor Amara Voss—no relation to the inquisitor, she said, with a little laugh, as if the name were a joke we'd all agreed to find funny—and she looked at Ilo the way merchants look at a cargo manifest.

"The child who speaks the First Tongue," she said. "The whole city has heard. The whole *Concord* has heard."

"News travels fast," Sefa said.

"Truth travels faster, when it's useful." Councilor Vess knelt then—actually knelt, robes pooling on the wet stone, before a mud-streaked boy of perhaps eight years—and held out her hand, palm up, in the gesture of fealty. Ilo looked at the hand. He looked at me.

I gave him the smallest nod. *It's all right.* I didn't know if it was.

He touched her palm with one finger, the way he'd once touched the cracked ward-stone in my cellar, and something passed over the assembled guards—a shiver, a stillness, the particular hush of men who have just felt something old move in its sleep. Councilor Vess rose, and her smile had gone from fitted to genuine, which was worse.

"Sanctuary," she said. "Food, physicians for your wounded, rooms in the Lantern Quarter. The Concord's protection, sworn before witnesses." She spread her hands. "All we ask in return is that the child help us."

"There it is," Ryn said, from the wagon. "There's the price."

"Ask him," I said. "Not us. Him."

The councilor's smile flickered—the first honest thing on her face. "He's a child."

"He's a person," Sefa said, stepping forward, and I heard the edge in her voice that had been sharpening since the council of the road, since the codex, since the night she'd said *he is not a text, Lena* and I had agreed and meant it and still, some part of me, kept reading him. "And he's exhausted, and he's been hunted for a month, and his guardian is half-dead. Ask him when he's slept. Ask him in a week. If you can't wait a week for your miracle, then your miracle matters less than you say it does."

The pikemen shifted. The councilor studied Sefa for a long moment, and something passed behind her eyes—recalculation, the merchant weighing a difficult shipment.

"A week," she said. "The Lantern Quarter. And Master Zhou—" she turned to me, and now I saw she knew exactly who I was, oath-marks and all, and had known since the gates "—the Concord does not forget its debts, either. We have archives here. Records. Some of them mention the Veyra-sworn."

The bells rang on. Ilo's hand found mine, small and cold, and I understood that we had not escaped a cage in Ashgrave. We had been invited into a larger one, with better light.

* * *

The rooms in the Lantern Quarter were beautiful, and that was the second warning.

High ceilings, deep beds, a balcony over a canal where the water ran gold with reflected lamps. A physician came for Ryn and worked on her shoulder with skill and poppy and no questions. Servants brought food I couldn't have bought in Ashgrave with a year of charm-sales—white fish, honeyed bread, wine the color of garnets. Ilo ate until he fell asleep at the table, mid-bite, his cheek on the wood, and I carried him to the bed and he didn't wake even when I laid him down.

Sefa stood at the balcony, arms crossed, watching the canal.

"Count the windows," she said.

I looked. The building across the water. The one beside it. The rooftops above.

"I've counted six men who've been in the same positions since we arrived," she said. "They're not guards. Guards rotate. Those are watchers, and they're watching *in*, not out."

"I know."

"You knew at the gates."

"I knew at Ashgrave." I sat on the edge of the bed, near Ilo, near his slow breathing. "Every door out of that city had a price. This one just wrapped it in silk."

Sefa turned. In the lamplight her face was hollowed from the road, from the codex, from the week of carrying knowledge that sat in her like swallowed glass. "Lena. They're going to ask him to stand on a stage."

"I know."

"They're going to ask him to speak. In front of thousands. To prove the ban is a lie, to rally the cities, to be—" she stopped, and her hands, which were always precise, always a scholar's hands, curled into fists. "To be the proof. Not to *be*. To be the *proof*. There's a difference and nobody in this city can see it."

"I can see it."

"Then say it to them."

"With what?" I looked at her. "Sefa. Look at what we have. One wounded smuggler. One scholar with no rank and no country. One child who speaks a language that can crack ward-stones in his sleep. And across the sea, an army that burns people in market squares for less than what Ilo does standing still. If the Concord won't fight, we have nowhere left. If the Concord will fight but wants a price—" I touched the oath-marks under my sleeve, the old brands that ached when the boy spoke. "Then the price is a price. I've paid worse."

"Paid worse," she repeated softly. "You keep saying things like that. As if you're still spending *yourself*. As if it's still yours to spend." She crossed the room and stood over Ilo, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He trusts you. He learned the word for *no* last week and he's only ever used it once—to Ryn, about the fish. He's never used it with you. Do you understand what that means? He hasn't decided yet whether he's allowed to refuse you. And every day we stay here, every day the council smiles at him, he's learning the opposite lesson. He's learning that people who feed you get to ask for things."

The lamp guttered. Down in the canal, the water ran gold, and somewhere a drunk was singing, and the watchers across the water stood in their six positions and did not move.

"Then we teach him the word," I said.

* * *

Ryn was awake when I came to the physician's room, propped on pillows, gray but present, her eyes tracking me the way they tracked everything—doors, windows, the weight distribution of a room.

"Let me guess," she said. "Silk walls. Watchers on the far bank. A councilor with a smile like a signed contract."

"You've done this before."

"Been welcomed somewhere? Once. Didn't like the ending." She shifted, winced, refused the poppy the physician offered with a look that could curdle milk. "Lena. I need to say something and I need you to not make it a thing."

"Say it."

"Back at the gates. When the councilor knelt." Her jaw worked. "I've seen men kneel to that boy's voice before. In the wars. Veyra's officers, when the god spoke through the—through the vessels, the old ones, before the silence. Men knelt like that. And then they stood up and did what the kneeling was for." She looked at the ceiling. "My brother knelt once. He told me about it. Said it felt like being forgiven for something he hadn't done yet. Three years later he was dead at the Weir Gate, and the man who killed him was Veyra-sworn too, and both of them thought they were serving something holy." She turned her head and looked at me, and her eyes were the flat gray of open water before weather. "I'm not saying the boy is a weapon. I'm saying kneeling is. It makes the kneeler feel clean and it makes the kneeled-upon into an altar. And altars don't get asked. They get *used*."

"I know."

"You keep saying that." She almost smiled. "Say something else."

I sat down on the chair beside her bed. Through the wall, faintly, I could hear Ilo breathing in his sleep—or maybe I imagined it; maybe after a month I heard him everywhere, the way sailors hear the sea.

"The council wants a proclamation," I said. "A living proof. The child on a stage, speaking the First Tongue, and every free city rallying to a banner with a miracle on it."

"And?"

"And Marrow wants the same thing." I said it and heard it land in the room like a dropped blade. "Think about it. Marrow wants the boy because the boy's voice is power. The council wants the boy's voice because it's *proof*. But either way, everyone in this story has decided what his voice is *for*. Nobody has asked him what it is."

Ryn was quiet a long moment. Outside, the bells had stopped, and in the silence the canal water lapped at the pilings below, patient as time.

"You're going to refuse them," she said.

"I'm going to ask him first."

"That's not the same as refusing."

"No," I said. "But it's the only thing I've got that Marrow doesn't. And it's the only thing the council doesn't. One person in this whole war who gets asked." I stood, and my knees ached, and the oath-marks ached under my sleeve, and I was so tired of being tired. "Get well, Ryn. I think I'm going to need someone good at saying no to powerful people, and Sefa's too polite."

"I'm not polite," Ryn said. "I'm expensive."

* * *

They came for him on the sixth day, which told me they'd counted the week I'd negotiated and decided five was close enough.

Councilor Vess again, with two of her colleagues this time and no pikemen, which was somehow more menacing. They filled the parlor of our beautiful rooms, and the silk walls drank their voices, and Ilo stood at the window with his back to them, watching the canal, and I stood beside him, and Sefa stood at the door with her arms crossed like a gate of her own.

"The Concord votes in three days," Councilor Vess said. "Seven cities. Four are ready to stand against Marrow. Three are afraid, and fear is reasonable—he has engines, he has inquisitors, he has victories. What the three afraid cities need is not arguments. They've heard arguments. What they need is to *see*." She turned to Ilo, and her voice went soft and warm, the voice people use for children and for animals they intend to keep. "One gathering. The Concord square. You speak a few words—the true words, the old words. Let them hear what Marrow has outlawed. Let them hear it living, in a child, free under our protection. And three afraid cities become three brave ones, and the war turns, and no one has to die for it. No one, Master Zhou. Isn't that worth an afternoon?"

Ilo didn't turn around. But his hand found mine, and his fingers were cold.

"He doesn't want to," I said.

"He hasn't said so."

"He's eight years old and six strangers just walked into his home to ask him to perform miracles for a crowd." I kept my voice level. It took everything. "In Ashgrave, I watched a woman burn in a market square for selling charms weaker than what this council is asking him to do on a stage. You want to know what your *afternoon* costs? Ask the ash."

Councilor Vess's warmth cooled by one degree, no more. She was too skilled for anger. "The alternative, Master Zhou, is that the Concord debates, and delays, and Marrow's army lands before the vote, and we are all burned in our own squares. You speak of costs. We are weighing the cost of a child's afternoon against the cost of seven cities." She spread her hands, and the gesture was almost humble, and it was completely honest, and that was the worst part. "I will not pretend it isn't a cage. I will only tell you that it is a cage with the door open, and outside the door is a world on fire. Choose which cage."

"Or," said Sefa from the door, "he chooses neither."

The room turned to her.

"He's a person," Sefa said, and her voice shook and she let it shake, let them see it, because that was the kind of brave she was—the kind that cost. "Not a proof. Not a proclamation. Not a key, not a weapon, not a vessel, not a banner. He is a boy who remembers a god from the inside, and every single one of you—Marrow, the Concord, the temples, the armies—looks at him and sees a *thing that speaks*." She stepped into the room. "He is not a thing that speaks. He is someone who speaks. And the first word he ever learned in trade-speech was *no*, and I think it's time somebody in power heard it."

Silence. The canal lapped. Ilo turned from the window at last.

He looked at Councilor Vess. He looked at her colleagues. He looked at Sefa, and something in his face was the closest to a smile I had seen in a month.

Then he looked at me, and he said, in the First Tongue, one word.

The oath-marks on my arms lit like coals. The lamps in the parlor guttered. Councilor Vess's colleagues stepped back, and one of them made a sign against evil, an old gesture, a farmer's gesture, and I understood suddenly that these were not rulers of a brave new world. These were frightened people in nice robes, and they had been frightened for five years, and they wanted the fear to end and did not care much what it cost.

"He said no," I translated, though I didn't need to. Everyone had understood. Some words don't need translating.

Councilor Vess stood very still. Then she bowed—shallow, precise, a diplomat's bow—and said, "The council will want to hear of this," and left, with her colleagues, with her silk voice, with the door closing soft as a purse-string.

And in the quiet after, Ilo tugged my sleeve and pointed at the balcony, at the city beyond, at the bells and the banners and the watchers on the far bank who had not moved all week, and he said, in careful, halting trade-speech, his third word ever:

"More."

I looked at him. "More what?"

He frowned, searching, and then gave up on the trade-tongue and spoke the First Tongue again, softer, and Sefa—pale, shaking, triumphant—translated in a whisper:

"He says the no wasn't finished. He says there was more to it. He says—" she stopped, and her eyes went wide. "Lena. He says the council isn't the only ones listening. He says someone else has been listening to him since we crossed the salt road. Something that knows the words. Something that's been *answering*."

The lamps went out. All of them, at once, in every room of the beautiful house, and down in the canal the gold light on the water guttered and died, and in the dark I heard Ilo say, calm as a child naming a friend:

"*Veyra.*"
