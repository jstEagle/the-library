## 11. Words That Build, Words That Burn

The room they gave him in Callowmere had a window, and the window had a garden under it, and the garden had a tree that dropped small bitter plums in the autumn. Ilo knew all of this the way he knew most things now—by watching, by waiting, by learning which silences were safe to stand inside.

He was six years old, or nearly. He had stopped counting the way other children counted. He counted instead in words: how many he could hold without them spilling out of his mouth and breaking something.

Tonight there were three people in the room with him. The tall one with the marks on his arms who moved like a man walking on ice—Lena. The woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that went soft when she looked at him but sharp when she looked at doors—Sefa. And a candle, because Ilo did not like the dark anymore, though he would never have been able to say why, not in their language, not yet.

Sefa set a slate on the floor between them and drew a circle on it. Then she drew a line through it, and looked at him with that patient face she wore like armor.

"Again," Lena said quietly. "Show her again."

Ilo knelt and put his finger where hers had been. He could not draw what he meant—not in lines, not in their letters, which were cages with bars made of sound. So he did the only thing he could do. He opened his mouth and let one word out, carefully, the way you might let water out of a cupped hand: one drop at a time.

The word was *hesh*.

The candle flame leaned toward him, gentle as a cat. The slate's chalk dust rose in a slow spiral and hung there, glittering. Nothing cracked. Nothing burned. Sefa's breath caught, but her hands stayed flat on her knees, and that mattered—Ilo watched hands more than faces; hands told the truth first.

"That's not a command," Sefa whispered. "Lena. That's not imperative mood. That's—" She laughed, a strange broken sound. "That's a memory. He's showing us a memory."

Lena crouched down across the slate from Ilo. Up close, the oath-marks on his forearms looked like rivers seen from very high above, if rivers had been carved into skin by someone who loved them once and then left. Ilo liked looking at them. They hummed, faintly, the way a hearthstone hums after the fire goes out.

"Can you show me?" Lena asked. His voice did something careful around the question, the way people's voices did when they were afraid of the answer. "What happened? When everything went quiet?"

Ilo looked at him for a long time.

Here is what no one understood, what no one had ever understood, because everyone who might have understood it was either dead or silent: Ilo remembered the silence from the inside. Not the silence that came after—the silence itself, the moment of it, stretched out like a held breath that lasted five years. He had been there when Veyra stopped speaking. He had been *in* her mouth, or near enough; he was young then, younger than memory should allow, and the god had gathered up her last unspoken word and folded it into him the way you fold a letter you cannot send, and shut him inside the folding.

So when people said *the god went silent*, they meant it the way you mean a lamp going out. But Ilo had been the wick. He had felt the flame drawn back down into the oil.

He tried to say this. He got as far as reaching for the word, and the word was too big—it was not one word at all but a whole weather system of words, and if he let it out all at once it would take the roof off the house and the memory out of every mind on the street, and so he swallowed it, and it went down cold, and he shook his head.

"You can't," Lena said. Not unkindly. "It's too much."

"He's been carrying it alone." Sefa's voice had gone thin. "All this time. A sentence five years long, held in a child's chest." She pressed her ink-stained fingers to her mouth. "Gods forgive us. We've been calling it power."

* * *

They came back the next night. And the next. That was the thing about these two that Ilo learned first, before he learned anything else about them: they came back. People in his short and terrible life had mostly been things that happened *to* him—hands that grabbed, doors that locked, voices that argued over his head in languages of ownership. These two sat on the floor at his level and waited, night after night, as if he were a well that might give water if no one shouted down it.

So he began, drop by drop, to give them the word.

Not the whole word. Never the whole word. But pieces of it, the way you might break bread for birds—one fragment each night, spoken slow, with Sefa's chalk racing to catch it and Lena's marks burning faint gold along his arms whenever the syllables landed true.

The fragments were not commands. This was the second thing Ilo learned about himself, sitting in candlelight with two adults who treated his mouth like a country rather than a weapon: the First Tongue did not make things happen. It *remembered* things happening. Every word in it was a memory so complete that reality agreed to repeat it. When he spoke *hesh*, the flame did not obey him—it recalled being lit, and leaned toward the warmth of its own beginning. When he spoke the word that stilled floodwater, the water did not stop; it remembered stillness, some ancient pond before any river, and grieved its way back toward it.

"It's not magic," Sefa said one night, chalk dust in her hair, three pages deep into a diagram that looked like a storm seen from below. "Or it's the magic underneath magic. It's grammar as inheritance. Every spell anyone ever cast in the old kingdoms was a bad copy of a memory this language holds perfectly." She looked up at Lena, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "We outlawed the wrong thing. We outlawed the echo and called it the voice."

"And the voice," Lena said slowly, "is a six-year-old who wants to know if the plum tree will fruit again next year."

Ilo, hearing his name-shaped-silence in the sentence, nodded solemnly. It would. He had checked. The tree had told him, in the way trees tell things, which was not in words at all.

Lena laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, gone almost before it arrived, but Ilo caught it and kept it. He collected Lena's laughs the way other children collected stones. There were only four so far. He kept them somewhere behind his ribs, in the same place the god's unfinished word lived, because that place was warm and nothing else had ever been allowed in.

* * *

On the ninth night, the candle guttered low and Sefa had fallen asleep sitting up, her chalk still in her hand, and Ilo made a decision.

He reached for the fragment he had never shown anyone. Not the silence itself—he was not ready, might never be ready—but the edge of it. The last thing the god had said before she stopped saying things. Or almost the last. The part that had been folded into him still wet, still warm, still trying to finish.

He took Lena's hand. That was important. He had learned that the words went easier when they had somewhere to go, and Lena's marked skin was the only door he'd found that fit them.

And he spoke.

The room did not shake. That was the frightening part. The room simply *remembered*, all at once, something it had never witnessed: a hall of white stone, a crowd of kneeling soldiers, a woman made of quiet standing at the center with her hands open—and her voice, her actual voice, vast and tired and kind, saying words that meant, as near as human mouths could bend them:

*I am not leaving you. I am being taken from you, and I choose what they may keep.*

Then it was gone, and the candle was a puddle, and Sefa was awake and crying without any noise, and Lena was staring at his own forearm where the oath-marks had flared bright enough to throw shadows.

"What did she say," Lena whispered. "Tell me what she said."

Ilo did not have the human words. But he had nine nights of practice, and a slate, and Sefa's alphabet, which was a poor cage but a cage nonetheless. Slowly, letter by letter, with both of them leaning over his shoulder, he built the sentence in their language. Built it twice, because the first time his hand shook too hard.

Lena read it. Read it again. Then he did something Ilo had never seen an adult do: he sat down on the floor entirely, back against the bed, knees up, like a boy himself, and put his face in his branded arms.

"She chose," he said, muffled. "Five years I thought she abandoned us. Five years I—" His shoulders moved. "She was taken, and she spent her last word making sure we'd know it wasn't our fault."

Sefa knelt beside Ilo and pulled him gently against her side, and he let her, because her heartbeat was steady and her tears fell on his hair and they were warm.

"You've been holding her goodbye," Sefa murmured into his crown, "all by yourself. For five years."

Ilo considered this. Considered telling them the worst part—that the word was unfinished, that a folded letter half-delivered ached worse than one never sent, that some nights the unsaid ending climbed his throat like a animal trying to get out and he had to bite it back down because he did not know what it would do to the world if it finished itself.

He decided they already knew. He could see it in the way Lena lifted his head, eyes red-rimmed and terribly clear, and looked at Sefa over Ilo's hair, and said:

"The codex. The cage. If she was caged, and he's her last word—" His jaw tightened. "Then finishing the word means opening the cage. Doesn't it."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the plum tree scratched its branches against the window, and somewhere across the gilded city a council bell rang the hour, indifferent and beautiful.

"It does," she said finally. "And I think, Lena—I think someone else has worked that out too. Because this morning, when I left the archives, there was a man watching the school. Grey coat. Inquisitor's boots. And he wasn't watching the children."

She looked down at Ilo.

"He was counting the guards on our street."
