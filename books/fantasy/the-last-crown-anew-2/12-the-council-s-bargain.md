## 12. The Council's Bargain

The Hall of Nine Lanterns smelled of beeswax and old money, and Sefa hated it on sight.

She had spent the morning preparing Ilo for this—brushing the road dust from his hair with a borrowed comb, straightening a collar that had been mended twice by Lena's clumsy hands, teaching him three words of Ashgrave common that he could offer like coins to strangers. *Hello. Thank you. My name is Ilo.* He had practiced them in the mirror of their rented rooms, solemn as a scholar defending a thesis, and each time he finished he had looked up at her with those gray eyes that held too much weather for a child of seven or eight, waiting to be told whether he had done it right.

He had not spoken the First Tongue once all morning. She had asked him not to. That was the whole point.

Now he sat on a chair built for ambassadors, his feet not quite reaching the floor, while nine councilors of Callowmere arranged themselves behind a crescent table of pale riverwood and pretended they had not already decided what they intended to do with him. Sefa stood at his shoulder. Lena stood at the door, arms folded, positioned—she noted with something like admiration—exactly where he could reach both exits and the window without turning his head. Ryn was absent, still feverish from the wounds she'd taken on Callowmere's rooftops, and her absence sat in the room like an unpaid debt.

"Master Scholar," said Councilor Aurel Vane, who chaired the session and wore his authority the way other men wore perfume. "You will forgive us if we dispense with ceremony. The situation has... evolved."

Sefa bowed the precise degree required of an advisor of the third rank. No more. She had learned long ago that excess courtesy was a currency the powerful accepted gladly and never returned.

"Callowmere faces invasion," Vane continued. "Marrow's vanguard crossed the Weir two days ago. His engines are burning the salt farms along the coast. The other free cities are dithering, as they always do, waiting to see which way the wind carries their spines." He laced his fingers. "And we possess, sitting in this very hall, the single most persuasive argument against Kaelen Marrow's entire reign."

"He is a child," Sefa said.

"He is a *proof*," said Councilor Imre Doss, a broad woman with a soldier's close-cropped hair and a voice like a door slamming. "You've seen what Marrow tells the world—that magic died with the silent god, that his ban is merely housekeeping, that anyone who claims otherwise is a madman or a fraud. And here sits a boy who speaks the language that moved mountains. Let him speak before the assembled cities. Let every delegate hear the First Tongue with their own ears. Marrow's lie collapses in an afternoon."

"And then what?" Sefa asked.

Doss frowned. "Then the war turns."

"That isn't an answer to my question. After the speech. After the delegates have heard him and gasped and wept and voted their resolutions. What happens to the boy then? Does he go home?" She let the silence stretch. "Where is home, Councilor?"

Vane's smile thinned. "You're being dramatic."

"I'm being *precise*, which is worse." Sefa stepped forward, and felt Lena shift at the door—not moving, just attending. "I have read every surviving text on the First Tongue. Most of them burned in Marrow's purges; I know because I catalogued some of the ashes myself, before the inquisitors burned my career along with them. The tongue is not a performance. It is not a trumpet to be sounded when convenient and shelved between wars. Every account we have—and there are forty-one credible ones, I've checked them twice—describes it as *binding*. Words spoken in the First Tongue take root in the speaker. They reshape the world, yes, but they reshape the speaker first. You are proposing to hand a seven-year-old a blade that cuts inward and asking him to swing it for your politics."

"The alternative," said Councilor Pell, a soft-voiced man who had not yet spoken, "is the Temple of the Neutral Flame."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Sefa had known it was coming—had seen its shape in the councilors' faces since the moment she entered—but knowing did not blunt it.

"The temple is exactly what its name promises," Pell continued gently, as though offering her a kindness. "Neutral. Beyond the reach of any crown, including Marrow's. The boy would be fed, schooled, protected. Scholars of the Flame have studied anomalous children for three centuries without—"

"Without what?" Sefa's voice cracked out harder than she intended. She reined it in. "Without letting them outside? Without telling them what they are? I have read the Flame's protocols, Councilor. Their neutrality means no one can *rescue* them, either. A neutral temple is a locked room that belongs to no one, which means whatever happens inside belongs to everyone and therefore to no one. You would be washing your hands so thoroughly you'd forget you ever had them."

"You would prefer we give him to Marrow?" Doss snapped. "Because that is the other road, scholar. If we don't use him, we lose him. The man is marching here with conquest engines forged from a caged god's power, and you want to lecture us about the boy's feelings."

"I want you to look at him," Sefa said, "before you decide what he's for."

For a moment, no one spoke. Then, deliberately, she turned and knelt beside Ilo's chair.

He looked small in the ambassador's clothes. His hands were folded in his lap the way she had taught him, and his face was composed, and only someone who had spent weeks learning his silences would have seen the tension in his jaw, the way his thumb pressed white against his forefinger. He had understood. Of course he had understood. Children always understood the shape of a conversation about themselves, even through a language barrier—perhaps especially through one, because they were not distracted by the words.

*Ilo,* she signed—the crude hand-language they had built together on the road, part gesture, part drawing in the air. *These people. What do you see?*

He considered her gravely. Then he raised one small hand and pointed, slowly, around the crescent of councilors: at Vane, at Doss, at Pell. And for each one, he made the sign they had invented on the salt road, the one that meant *a person carrying something too heavy.*

Then he pointed at himself, and made the same sign.

Sefa's throat tightened. She rose and faced the council again.

"There," she said. "That's his testimony. He sees people drowning under burdens they shouldn't carry, and he thinks he's one of them. He is *seven years old*, Councilor Vane. He remembers the inside of a god's silence. He watched the woman who made him—made *of* her—be bound in iron beneath an abbey, and he has been trying for years to finish a word he doesn't fully understand, because finishing it might kill him, and we only learned that last week. And your proposal is to stand him on a stage in front of ten thousand frightened people and ask him to speak the most dangerous language in existence *on cue*, for a war effort, because it polls well against Marrow."

"It would save lives," Vane said quietly. "Thousands of them. Perhaps hundreds of thousands. Can you say the same for your scruples?"

It was the oldest argument in the world, and it had never once been wrong, which was precisely what made it so dangerous. Sefa took a breath.

"Yes," she said. "I can. Because I've read the histories you haven't, Councilor. Do you know how the god-cults of the Third Age began? Not with tyrants. With *rescues*. A village in danger, a holy child with a gift, a desperate council saying *just this once, just this far.* And the child speaks, and the village is saved, and next season the village asks again, and the season after that the village stops asking and starts commanding, and within a generation the child is an instrument with a maintenance schedule. Marrow didn't invent the enslavement of power. He inherited the method. It is the oldest method there is: take something sacred, tell yourself it's for a good cause, and use it until nothing sacred remains." She spread her hands. "You are not choosing between using the boy and losing the war. You are choosing which kind of world survives the war. If you win it by making him a proclamation, you have already lost everything worth winning."

"Pretty words," Doss said. "From a woman whose last institution stripped her rank for pretty words."

The blow landed where it was aimed. Sefa felt it in her chest, the old bruise—the archives, the purge, the colleagues who had looked away while her name was scraped from the rolls. But she found, examining herself in the silence that followed, that the bruise had changed. It hurt less than it used to. Perhaps because she finally understood what it had been for.

"My last institution," she said, "stripped me because I insisted that a banned text described real suffering and should be studied rather than burned. They were wrong, and five years later Marrow proved it by building engines from the very knowledge they suppressed. So yes, Councilor. I have been stripped before. I have some practice at standing in a room full of reasonable people explaining why their reasonable plan is a catastrophe, and being right too early, and paying for it." She lifted her chin. "Here is what I know, and I will say it plainly so there is no confusion afterward. If this council puts that child on a stage, I will stand beside him—and when it is done, I will stand in every archive and every market square in the free cities and tell the truth about what you did and what it cost. If you give him to the Neutral Flame, I will spend the rest of my life finding a way through their doors. And if you try to quiet me the way my last institution did—" she smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile, "—you'll find I write faster than you burn."

Vane studied her for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and administrative, which was how powerful men sounded when the decision had already been made and the discussion was merely furniture.

"Advisor Sefa of the third rank. Your advisory commission to this council is hereby dissolved. You will surrender your seal and your access credentials before nightfall. You may remain in Callowmere under guest-right, subject to the usual restrictions." He turned to the others. "The council will convene tomorrow to formalize the resolution regarding the child. Master Scholar—or rather, *former* Master Scholar—you will receive notice of our decision through ordinary channels."

"Through ordinary channels," Sefa repeated. "Meaning a courier. Meaning paper. Meaning none of you will have to look at him when you tell him."

No one answered. That was answer enough.

She took Ilo's hand. He rose from the ambassador's chair without a word—he never needed to be told twice, poor child, he had learned early in life how meetings about him tended to end—and they walked toward the doors together, past the guards, past the tall windows where Callowmere's gilded harbor glittered in the afternoon light like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up.

Lena fell into step beside them. He said nothing until they were down the first flight of stairs, into the cooler air of the council building's stone corridors. Then:

"You knew they'd strip you."

"I hoped they wouldn't." She shrugged. "But hope is a hypothesis, and I test my hypotheses."

"They took your rank. Your access. Everything you crawled back for after the purges." His voice was low, rough with something she almost didn't recognize in him—it took her a moment to place it as anger on her behalf, an emotion so foreign to her own experience of institutions that it arrived like a message in an unknown script. "Why? You could have negotiated. Bought time. Played it their way and worked from inside."

"Because inside is where they keep the cages," Sefa said. "I told you once—I didn't leave the archives because I was thrown out. I left because I stayed too long. I watched them burn a codex and I filed a protest instead of stealing it. I have regretted that filing for eleven years, Lena Zhou. I will not file another one."

They emerged onto the council steps. Below, the city spread out in its tiers of pale stone and hanging gardens, beautiful and oblivious, market bells ringing the third hour. Somewhere beyond the horizon, Marrow's engines were chewing through the salt farms.

Ilo tugged her sleeve. She looked down. He was watching her with that grave, weathered gaze, and he raised his small hands and made a sign she hadn't taught him—a new one, self-invented, the grammar slightly wrong in the way that broke her heart. He touched his chest, then hers, then pressed his palms together.

*Same,* the sign said. *We are the same.*

"No," she said softly, kneeling on the steps so their eyes were level, heedless of the guards, of the councilors who might be watching, of her rank dissolving somewhere in a clerk's ledger. "Not the same. You are going to be free of all of this someday. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and I intend to see it happen."

Behind them, a door opened. A page in council livery descended the steps, breathless, and held out a folded note with a shaking hand—not to Sefa, but to Lena.

"For the mercenary," the page said. "From the harbor quarter. The woman Okafor—her fever's turned. The healers say if you want to see her alive, you come now."

* * *

They came now.

Ryn Okafor lay in a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and salt, propped on pillows gone gray with sweat, her bandaged side rising and falling in shallow, rapid intervals. Her skin had the waxy pallor of a candle left too near a flame. But her eyes, when they found Lena in the doorway, were still sharp enough to cut.

"Took you long enough," she rasped. Then her gaze slid past him to Sefa, and narrowed. "You look different, scholar. Smaller. Did the council finally figure out what you are?"

"They revoked my rank," Sefa said.

A grin ghosted across Ryn's cracked lips. "Good. Now you're one of us." She coughed, wet and long, and when it passed she gripped Lena's wrist with surprising strength and pulled him close, close enough that Sefa could see the fever-bright desperation underneath the smuggler's armor of sarcasm.

"Listen," Ryn whispered. "My crew. The ones who sold the route to Marrow's agents—they weren't the only ones bought. There's a fleet movement in the harbor, ships flying grain colors, and grain ships don't anchor at the war quay. Marrow's not just marching on Callowmere." Her grip tightened. "He's coming for the abbey road. Someone told him where the child sleeps at night—and it wasn't my crew."

Sefa felt the floor tilt beneath her, the way it always did when a hypothesis collapsed. She thought of the council, convening tomorrow, formalizing its resolution. She thought of the Neutral Flame's locked rooms. She thought of Ilo upstairs in their rented rooms, practicing *hello* and *thank you* for a city that had already decided he was a proclamation.

"How long do we have?" Lena asked quietly.

Ryn's eyes closed. "If you're asking as a fugitive—tonight. If you're asking as a father—" she exhaled, and it might have been a laugh, "—you stopped having that luxury yesterday."
