## 13. Night of the Long Knives

Ryn Okafor had smuggled a hundred things through Callowmere's harbor in her life: salted eels, uncut garnets, plague-doctors fleeing quarantine, a deposed duke folded into a wine cask. She knew every bolt-hole and bribed gatekeeper from the Weeping Stair to the Gull's Teeth. So when she walked the Lantern District that evening and felt the city breathing wrong—too many shadows standing still, too many doorways holding their breath—she trusted the feeling the way other sailors trusted the weather glass.

Something had come into Callowmere wearing the dark like a uniform.

She stopped at a fried-eel cart near the Tidewalk, bought two skewers she didn't want, and used the transaction to study the alley across the way. A man leaned against a rain barrel with the particular stillness of a man who was not waiting for anyone. He wore dockworker's canvas, but his boots were wrong—oiled leather, military heel, the kind of boots that had never hauled a net. And when a lantern-seller passed, the man turned his face away with a smoothness that wasn't shyness.

It was training. Marrow-trained.

*Three days,* Ryn thought. *They gave the council three days of gilded hospitality before they came for him.*

Ilo. The boy who spoke the god's tongue. The boy the council wanted to parade on their balcony like a living banner while they argued about what he was worth. The boy Lena guarded like a dragon guards one egg—the only egg, the last one, the whole future folded up small and breathing.

Ryn paid for her eels and walked away without hurrying, because hurrying told watchers you'd seen them. Her mind was already laying out routes, counting rooftops, weighing doors. She'd taken coin to move this crew out of Ashgrave, and she'd told herself it was just another job, another cargo, a child instead of casks. She had told herself that right up until Sefa started reading the codex aloud by lamplight and Ilo fell asleep against Lena's shoulder like something finally unclenched.

Cargo didn't do that. Cargo didn't learn your name.

* * *

"You're certain?" Lena stood in the doorway of the townhouse the council had lent them—a fine house, too fine, all marble sills and windows made for looking out of rather than defending. Ryn had hated it from the first night. Fortresses you chose were homes; fortresses given to you were traps with cushions.

"Certain as I get," she said. "Dockside man with soldier's boots. Two more on the Tidewalk pretending to be lovers, which would be sweet if either of them had ever touched anyone. And a fourth I lost near the customs house—which means he's good, which means there'll be more."

Sefa looked up from the codex, ink on her fingers, exhaustion carved deep under her eyes. "Marrow's agents. Here. That fast?"

"His spymaster doesn't send letters, girl, he sends knives." Ryn dropped into a chair, joints aching from a day of hauling wounded-Ryn-shaped cargo up and down stairs. "Question isn't whether they're here. Question is how many, and where they'll come from."

"The council should be told," Sefa said.

Lena laughed once, short and bitter. "The council will tell us to trust their guard. Their guard is forty men guarding six gates and a treasury. They'll post two at our door and call it protection." He looked at Ryn. "You have a better idea."

It wasn't a question either. That was the thing about Lena Zhou she'd never expected, back in Ashgrave when he was just a charm-peddler with branded arms and dead eyes: underneath the invisibility he'd wrapped around himself like a wet cloak, there was still an officer. He read battlefields the way she read harbors.

"I have a worse idea and a faster one," Ryn said. "We don't wait for them to choose the ground. We move tonight, before their numbers gather. Out through the harbor district, onto a boat I keep warm for exactly this kind of night."

"North," Sefa said quietly. All three of them heard the word land. North, where the codex pointed. Where the Sunken Abbey waited under its drowned bells, and where the truth about Veyra—and about Ilo—was buried in salt and stone.

"The boy's asleep," Lena said.

"Then wake him gently," Ryn said, "because we've got maybe two hours before whoever's running those watchers stops watching and starts working."

* * *

They were wrong about the two hours. They had less than one.

Ryn knew it the moment she reached the roofline of the neighboring granary to check the approach roads and saw the signal: three lanterns lit in sequence along the Tidewalk, green-shuttered, spaced like notes of music. Old code. Harbor code. *Come now. Come quiet. Come together.*

Except it wasn't harbor code anymore, not tonight, not coming from that stretch of warehouses. Tonight it meant the trap had been set and the hunters were converging.

And she knew—I knew, she corrected herself, because lying to yourself is the first luxury a mercenary gives up—*knew* who had sold the route. There was only one person outside this crew who knew the layout of these safehouses, the timing of the watch changes, the servant's entrance off the kitchen court. Only one person she'd trusted with the shape of the job because old loyalty is a debt you keep paying long after the creditor's dead.

Brannick. Her quartermaster, once. Her brother-in-arms, once, back before Veyra went silent and the world forgot what they'd all been sworn to. He'd found her in Callowmere two weeks ago, older and harder, asking for work, and she'd turned him away gently because his eyes had gone the color of coins. He must have gone to someone else after. Someone with deeper pockets and a longer reach.

*You sold the boy's route for Marrow's silver, Brannick.* She gripped the granary roof-edge hard enough to sting. *Or maybe you sold it cheap, because that's all any of us are worth now.*

Below, the night began to move.

They came over the kitchen wall first—six of them, black-clad, moving with the economy of men who'd done this in a dozen cities. Not inquisitors in their gray-and-crimson finery. These were the ones before the inquisitors, the ones who left no paperwork: Marrow's quiet hands. Two more came up the alley from the street, and when Ryn swept the far roofline she caught a glint of steel at the granary's opposite corner—overwatch. Crossbow or arbalest, covering the courtyard.

The house had one way out that wasn't watched yet: up. Through the attic, onto the tiles, across the spine of the terrace row to the harbor stair. It was narrow, wet, and suicidal in the dark.

It was also the only road.

* * *

"We go up," Ryn said, sliding back through the attic hatch. "Now. Kitchen court's breached, six hostiles inside the wall, overwatch on the granary corner."

Lena already had Ilo in his arms, the boy blinking awake, small hands clutching the collar of his coat. Sefa had the codex strapped to her chest like a breastplate, which Ryn supposed, in a way, it was.

"The harbor stair," Lena said. "How far?"

"Four roofs. Then down ninety steps to the water and my boat." She met his eyes. "I'll take point. You take the boy. Scholar keeps between us and stays low. Nobody stops for anything."

"And you—" Lena started.

"Don't make speeches," Ryn snapped. "Speeches take time."

But he held her gaze a moment longer, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever say aloud. Five years ago she'd have spat at his oath-marks. She'd told him so, that first night on her boat, when she'd shown him the scar where a Veyra-sworn blade had opened her side during the sack of Miren's Ford—told him her brother had died under those same brands, fighting for a god who couldn't be bothered to notice. She'd taken his coin anyway, because coin spends the same no matter whose hand it comes from.

That was the creed she'd built her whole life on. Coin spends the same. People are cargo. Nothing is worth dying for because nothing lasts.

Then why did her chest feel like this, looking at a mute child rubbing sleep from his eyes?

"Move," she said, and kicked open the roof hatch.

* * *

The tiles were slick with sea-mist, the moon behind cloud, and Ryn ran the ridgeline by memory alone—here the gap to jump, here the chimney to duck, here the weak spot where a slate had come loose last spring and she'd been meaning to fix it. Behind her: Lena's measured footfalls, light for a big man, the boy a silent weight against his chest. Sefa's breath sawing, her scholar's legs doing a scholar's worst.

At the second roof, the crossbow spoke.

Ryn heard the string, not the shot—that flat wooden snap that every soldier's ear knows—and threw herself sideways off the ridge as the bolt cracked past where her head had been and buried itself in a chimney pot. She rolled, came up behind the parapet, and pulled both her knives.

"Keep moving!" she screamed back down the line. "Don't stop, don't turn, GO!"

The overwatch man was reloading on the granary corner, forty yards out, and he was good—he'd shifted position after firing, already hunting her new line. But Ryn Okafor had spent twenty years learning how killers moved, and she moved the way they didn't expect: forward. Up. Along the parapet, fast and low, closing the distance while he cranked the windlass.

She reached the gap between roofs as he raised the weapon. No time for elegance. She jumped.

Forty yards became fifteen in one long falling stride, tiles exploding under her boots, and then it was knife-work in the dark—his crossbow swinging toward her, her blade taking him through the forearm so the weapon spun away, her shoulder driving him back against the parapet, her second knife stopping a hand-span from his throat when she saw his eyes.

Young. Scared. Following orders in the dark for a man in a distant palace.

*Like you were,* whispered the part of her that kept accounts. *Like we all were.*

"Run," she told him, and shoved him hard toward the stairwell door, and he ran, because young scared men always do, and she turned back toward her people with blood on her sleeve and something strange burning in her chest that she didn't have time to name.

* * *

She found them at the third gap—the wide one, the bad one, four feet of empty air over a drop to the cobbles. Lena had Ilo on his back now, the boy's arms locked around his neck, and he was measuring the jump with a professional's eye while Sefa stood white-faced at the edge.

"I can't," Sefa said. "I can't—"

"You can." Ryn took her arm. "On my count. I'll throw you if I have to and apologize after."

"They're in the stairwell below—" Sefa's voice cracked. "I heard them. On the stairs. They're cutting off the harbor stair—"

Of course they were. The quiet hands hadn't survived a dozen cities by leaving exits open. Which meant the plan had just changed shape: not escape down, but hold and divert, and pray the diversion bought enough minutes for—

For what? For Lena to jump four feet with a child on his back? For Sefa to fly?

No. It meant somebody stayed on this roof and made themselves loud enough, dangerous enough, unforgettable enough that the men in the stairwell came up instead of waiting below.

Ryn did the arithmetic the way she'd done it a thousand times in a thousand bad spots. One fighter, wounded already, aging, carrying two knives and no future worth mentioning. Against that: a child who was the last living word of a silent god, a scholar carrying the only book that explained him, and a branded ex-mercenary who had somehow, without her permission, become the closest thing Ryn Okafor had to family since her brother stopped breathing at Miren's Ford.

The arithmetic was insulting. It wasn't even close.

"New plan," she said. "You two jump together—Lena takes the scholar, boy holds on. Land rolling, run for the stair, and when you hit the bottom you go left along the seawall, not right. Right is theirs now. Left there's a fish-cellar with a grate to the tideline; the grate's rusted, kick it, my boat's moored thirty yards past it, black hull, red lamp."

"Ryn." Lena's voice was very quiet. "What are you doing."

"My job." She grinned at him, and found the grin came easy, easier than it had in years. "Moving cargo. You're the cargo now, all three of you. Go."

"I'm not leaving you on this roof."

"You're not *leaving* me anywhere. I'm staying. There's a difference, and it matters." She put her hand flat on his chest, over the sternum, where the oath-marks burned beneath his shirt. "Listen to me, Zhou. I spent five years telling myself your god was a lie and your war was theft and everything I lost was spent on nothing. I believed it right up until I watched that boy fall asleep against you like the world was a safe place." Her throat tightened. "Some things are worth the bill. I just never had one before. Now go, before I start crying and ruin my reputation."

He held her eyes for three full seconds. Then he nodded, once, the soldier's nod, the one that says *I'll carry this*, and turned to Sefa. "Arms around my neck. Legs around my waist. Don't let go even if it hurts."

"It'll hurt," Sefa said faintly.

"Yes. Count of three."

Ryn turned her back on them and faced the stairwell door, drawing both knives, setting her feet on the wet tiles the way she'd learned in a hundred rooftop fights in ports whose names the wars had erased. Below her, through the door, boots were climbing. Many boots. Steady. Unhurried. Men who believed the roof was already theirs.

Behind her she heard the leap—Sefa's yelp, the hard landing, Lena's grunt, then running footsteps fading along the terrace spine toward the harbor stair. Toward the boat. Toward north, and the Abbey, and whatever waited in the drowned dark under its bells.

Toward the thing Ryn Okafor was about to die believing in.

* * *

The first man through the door got her boot in his teeth and the roof edge across his throat, and went over the side screaming. The second was better—he came low, sword out, and she traded cuts with him across four yards of slick tile, giving a wound, taking one, a hot opening along her ribs that soaked her shirt immediately. The third and fourth came together, and that was when it stopped being fencing and became butchery.

She fought the way the harbor had taught her: dirty, close, economical. She used chimneys and guy-ropes and a loose slate she pried up and swung like a shield. She took a slash across her thigh that buckled the leg and answered it by driving her knee into a throat. Somewhere in the middle of it a fifth man reached the roof and she stopped counting, stopped thinking, let the old animal take the wheel—the animal that had kept her alive through the sack of three cities and the drowning of a fleet and five years of telling herself survival was the only prayer worth saying.

*Ilo,* she thought, absurdly, in the middle of it. *He likes the eel carts. He points at things and Lena names them and the boy mouths the shapes of the words even though he can't say them.*

She killed the man trying to flank her and laughed, actually laughed, breathless and bloody, because it was such a strange thing to be fighting for—not coin, not contract, not the cold arithmetic of hire. A boy pointing at gulls. A god's unfinished word learning the shape of ordinary speech. A future, small and breathing, folded up and running north on a smuggler's route she'd drawn from memory.

*This,* she understood, parrying, bleeding, backing along the parapet as the survivors regrouped. *This is what Brannick sold for silver. This is what I almost never had.*

A crossbow bolt took her high in the back of the shoulder—from the granary again, a second shooter, someone who'd climbed while she danced—and the impact spun her half around and drove her to one knee. The roof tilted. The knives grew heavy as anchors. Through the fog rising in her skull she saw them coming, three of them, unhurried now, swords ready, and she thought with detached professional irritation: *sloppy, Okafor, sloppy, you taught better than this.*

She got one of them. She was almost sure she got one of them—felt the knife go home before the sword took her across the hip and the roof came up to meet her, tiles cold against her cheek, the taste of copper and salt.

Above her, boots. Voices, sharp and urgent:

"She's one of Okafor's—check her papers—the boy, where's the boy—"

"The stair. They went down the stair—"

"After them! Move!"

Boots receding. The harbor stair. Lena. Sefa. *Run, you beautiful stubborn man, run.*

Ryn lay on her back on the wet tiles and watched the clouds slide off the moon, and Callowmere spread out below her, gold-lit and oblivious, and she found that she was smiling. Blood filled her mouth and she smiled anyway. Twenty years of freight and contracts and *coin spends the same*, twenty years of keeping her heart in a strongbox welded shut, and it had taken a mute child and a broken soldier and a terrified scholar to pick the lock.

*Worth the bill,* she thought. *Finally, something worth the whole damn bill.*

Her fingers crawled across the tiles toward the dropped knife. The wound in her shoulder pulsed with her heartbeat, and her heartbeat, stubborn old thing, refused to slow. She'd seen men live through worse. She'd been one of them.

*Not dead yet,* she told the moon. *And if I'm not dead, then I've got a job. Boat's got to meet them past the fish-cellar. Black hull, red lamp. Somebody has to be at the helm.*

She got one elbow under herself. The roof swam. She hauled.

Down at the mouth of the harbor stair, torchlight bloomed—men pouring down after her people—and beyond it, far off across the black water, a single red lamp flickered awake at the end of a pier, and Ryn Okafor began, inch by agonizing inch, to crawl toward the edge of the roof, laughing and bleeding, because the cargo wasn't delivered yet, and Ryn Okafor had never once in her life failed to deliver.

*Hold on, little word-of-god,* she thought, dragging herself over the parapet toward the drainpipe and the long dark drop to the next terrace. *Your ferryman's coming. Just—hold on.*

Below her, at the harbor's edge, the first screams of the ambush rose from the fish-cellar stairs, and the red lamp went dark.
