## 14. The Caged God's Country

The north road forgot them the way it forgot everything.

Three days out of Callowmere, the salt marsh gave way to grey heather, and the heather to pine, and the pines stood so thick along the old pilgrim track that their shadow fell like a second dusk even at noon. Lena walked at the front of their small, battered procession and let the forest do what forests did best in Ashgrave's stories: swallow things whole. Behind him, Sefa led a mule whose saddlebags held one stolen codex wrapped in oilcloth, three changes of clothes, and every scrap of paper she'd been able to smuggle out of Callowmere's council archive before they fled. Behind her, on a litter dragged between two more mules, Ryn Okafor lay with a fevered cheek and a wound in her side that Sefa changed the dressing for twice a day, each time with the same tight silence, as though the wound were an argument she was losing.

And beside the litter, holding Ryn's rope-burned hand when the shaking got bad, walked Ilo.

The boy had not spoken since the rooftop. Not in the First Tongue, not in any tongue. He had watched Ryn take the blade meant for his throat; he had watched the blood pool between the chimney-pots; and whatever word had risen in him then—the word that could have cracked the attackers' bones like green kindling—he had swallowed it. Lena understood why. He also understood it was a kind of miracle the boy was still walking, still breathing, still here. A vessel who refused to pour was still a vessel. But he was learning, this child, that his voice was a thing people died over. That lesson would cost them all if it took root too deep.

"Water," Ilo said at dusk on the third day. Just that. His first ordinary word in days, hoarse and small, and Lena nearly wept over the canteen because of how ordinary it was.

They made camp under a rock shelf where an old wayshrine had once stood—Lena could tell by the socket in the stone where a votive lantern used to hang, by the shallow basin carved for offerings. The basin held rainwater and pine needles now. Someone had chiseled Veyra's sign out of the shrine's face decades ago, during the Edict, but they'd chiseled carelessly, and the curve of the crescent-moon still showed beneath the scars, like a face behind a veil.

Sefa saw him looking. "You've been quiet since the pass," she said. She settled beside him with the codex open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She never really stopped reading it; she just sometimes pretended to. "Quieter than a man carrying your secret has any right to be."

"I know where we're going."

"We all do." She kept her voice low. "The Sunken Abbey. It's in the last folios—*the cage stands where the river remembers its name*. The Aldwater forks around those hills. It fits."

"That's not what I mean." Lena turned the canteen in his hands. The metal was cold enough to bite. "I mean I know it. I've been there before. I swore my oath there."

Sefa went very still, the way scholars went still when a footnote suddenly became a knife. "You were Veyra-sworn at the Abbey itself?"

"The last company ever sworn there. Nine years ago. Before Marrow's engines, before the Edict, before—" He gestured at everything: the dark trees, the sick fire, the sleeping smuggler, the silent god. "Before all of it. There were four hundred of us kneeling in the flood-court. The water came up to our ankles and the cantor sang the First Tongue over our heads, and I remember thinking the words weren't being said *to* us. They were being said *through* us. Like we were the bell and someone else had hold of the rope." He laughed, short and joyless. "I thought that was holy. I was twenty-two years old and I thought being rung like a bell was holy."

"And then?"

"And then five years ago the god went quiet, and everyone called it abandonment, and I called it that too, because it was easier than the truth." He looked into the fire. "The truth is I broke first, Sefa. The night Marrow's army reached the Weir Gate, my captain ordered the company to hold the bridge so the city could evacuate. Four hundred sworn against ten thousand. And I ran. Not in the battle—in the *before* of it. I slipped out through the drainage culverts while the others were singing the evening office, and I never looked back until Ashgrave, and by then Veyra's voice had gone out of the world like a lamp pinched between wet fingers." He heard his own voice shake and hated it. "Everyone says the god abandoned us. Every mercenary in every gutter tavern says it. But I was there, and I know the order things happened in. The silence didn't come first. My running came first. Maybe not mine alone—maybe half the company ran, maybe faith is a bridge that only holds while everyone stands on it—but I can only answer for the feet that were mine."

Sefa was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her scholar's voice had gone soft. "You know what the codex says now. What we learned at the Abbey road marker. Veyra asked to be caged. Her power was being twisted into Marrow's engines—"

"I know what it says."

"—then the silence wasn't punishment for your desertion. It might have been mercy happening at the same moment you were doing the worst thing you'll ever do. Both things are true, Lena. You broke your oath, and the breaking may have saved nothing, and the god you broke it against was already gone—not from cowardice, not from spite. From love, if the codex is right. You can't carry a debt that was never issued."

"You sound like a sermon."

"I sound like someone who spent eleven years in archives learning that guilt makes terrible history." She closed the codex. "Sleep. Tomorrow we cross into the valley, and you'll want your legs under you."

He didn't sleep. He sat by the fire and watched Ilo, who had curled against Ryn's litter like a kitten against something warm, and he thought about bridges and bells and the weight of a word spoken through you rather than by you.

* * *

They smelled the Abbey before they saw it.

It was the water that carried it—a cold mineral breath rising out of the ground, iron and old stone and something faintly sweet underneath, like incense drowned a long time ago. The track climbed through the pines toward a saddle in the hills, and where the trees broke, the whole valley opened below them: a bowl of green ringed in slate ridges, and in its floor, the ruins.

From above, the Sunken Abbey looked exactly like what it was named for—a cathedral drowned in earth instead of sea. Its towers had fallen inward centuries before the Edict; its nave lay open to the sky like a ribcage picked clean; and through the middle of it, unhurried and silver-grey, ran the forked channel of the Aldwater, splitting around the ruin's heart and joining again beyond, as if the river had decided long ago that anything built there belonged to it.

Ryn woke as the mule lurched down the switchback trail. Her fever had broken in the night, which Sefa said was good and which Ryn herself treated as a personal insult. "If I have to be carried into a haunted ruin," she rasped, gripping the litter ropes, "at least point me at something worth stabbing."

"Hold still," Sefa said.

"I've been still for three days. I'm going to grow moss."

"You almost died."

"I *almost* die professionally. It's different." But her eyes found Lena's over the litter's edge, and there was no joke in them. She knew whose ground this was. She'd branded the knowledge into Lena's arm herself, years ago, in another life—the same oath-marks, the same god, the brother she'd lost to the wars those marks served. "You don't have to go down there, you know," she said quietly. "We can read the folios anywhere. Sefa can translate in a ditch. The Abbey doesn't get a vote."

"It gets the truth," Lena said. "And I'm done living next door to it."

The descent took the rest of the morning. By the time they reached the valley floor, rain had begun again—thin, needle-fine, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang. The air grew colder near the walls. Not weather-cold. Something else. Sefa felt it first and said so, frowning at the thermometer she'd rigged from a vial and a cork. "There's a ward-lattice under us. Dormant. It's like standing inside a held breath."

Inside, the Abbey was vast and ruined and strangely gentle. Moss upholstered the fallen columns. Ferns grew from the pulpit. Rain came through the shattered roof in long bright threads and struck the flooded floor, and the flood-court where four hundred knees had once knelt was still there, still ankle-deep in silver water, still shaped like an amphitheater around a central dais where the altar-stone had stood.

The altar-stone was gone.

In its place was a pit—a round shaft perhaps six feet across, rimmed in carved script, descending into darkness, and from it rose the cold sweet smell, stronger now, and a sound. Very faint. A rhythm like slow breathing, or like surf heard from very far away, or like a great heart beating under the floor of the world.

Sefa knelt at the rim, codex forgotten, and traced the carved script with fingers that trembled. "This is the cage. This is the binding. These are anchor-words—First Tongue, archaic register—and they run all the way down." She looked up, and Lena saw that she was afraid, truly afraid, in a way the inquisition had never managed to make her. "Lena. It's not empty down there."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I swore my oath on that dais," he said, "and I always wondered why the cantor made us swear facing *down*."

Ilo had gone to the edge of the flood-court and stood there, motionless, rain threading through his hair. He wasn't looking at the pit. He was looking at the far wall, where the chisel-scars of the Edict's iconoclasm scored the stone in long parallel gouges—and beneath the gouges, surviving in the protected hollow of a carved recess, an old fresco survived: Veyra, rendered life-size, robed and crowned in crescents, her hands extended downward over kneeling figures, palms open. Giving. Not commanding. Giving.

Ilo raised his hand and touched the fresco's painted palm.

The sound from the pit changed.

The slow surf-breath stuttered, quickened, and for one heartbeat the whole flood-court lit from beneath—silver light rising through the ankle-deep water, through the moss, through the seams of every stone, and Lena's oath-marks ignited like brands pulled fresh from coals. He dropped to one knee in the flood-water without deciding to. Across the court, Sefa cried out. Ryn hauled herself half-upright on the litter, hand pressed to her wound, staring.

And in the light, Lena felt it—not a voice, not words, but something older than both: a *recognition*, vast and patient and unbearably sad, passing over him like a searchlight. It found the marks on his arm. It found the shape of the oath he'd spoken on this exact stone nine years ago. It found the culvert he'd crawled out of, the years of charms sold small and harmless, the prayer burned unbelieved in an Ashgrave alley, and it held all of it up against him, gently, the way you hold a broken thing to see if it can be mended.

Then it withdrew. The light sank. The surf-breath resumed, slow, waiting.

Lena stayed on his knee in the cold water, shaking. Ilo crossed the flood-court to him—small bare feet slapping the shallows—and put one hand on Lena's branded arm, right over the burning marks, and the pain eased, and the boy looked up at him with eyes that were ancient and five years old at once.

"She's not angry," Ilo said. In the trade-tongue. Plainly, simply, a child reporting a fact about a neighbor. Then, after a moment, softer: "She waited."

Lena covered the boy's hand with his own. Around them the rain fell through the broken roof, silver threads stitching ruin to sky, and the pit breathed its long slow breath, and every lie he had told himself for five years—that he had been abandoned, that the silence was a wound dealt to him, that his running had cost nothing because nothing remained to be cost—lay in the flood-water around his knees, drowned at last.

He had left a bridge. The bridge had held anyway, long enough. And what remained of the one he'd sworn to was down in the dark, caged by her own request, waiting for a word only a child could finish.

"Tomorrow," he said aloud—to Sefa, to Ryn, to himself, to whatever listened beneath the stone. "At dawn, we finish it. Whatever it costs."

Behind them, far down the southern trail, thin and almost lost in the rain, came the sound none of them wanted: a horn. Once. Twice. Then a third time, closer—the three-note signal of an inquisitor company closing on a position, and riding under it, faint but unmistakable, the grind of heavy wheels that no pilgrim cart ever made.

Conquest engines were climbing the pass.
