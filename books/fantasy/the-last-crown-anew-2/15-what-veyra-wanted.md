## 15. What Veyra Wanted

The stairs went down farther than the Abbey had height.

Sefa counted them the way she counted everything—because counting kept the fear at a manageable distance—and lost her place somewhere past two hundred. The stone beneath her boots was older than Ashgrave's founding, older than the Edict, older than the kingdoms whose ruins she'd spent her career cataloguing before those same kingdoms burned her name from every register that mattered. Each step was cut with the same shallow spiral groove, and when she dragged her lantern low enough to see it, the groove caught the light like a vein of silver.

"It's script," she said, and heard her own voice crack with the wonder of it. "Lena. The whole staircase is a sentence."

Behind her, Lena Zhou shifted Ilo's sleeping weight on his shoulder. "Is it a nice one?"

She almost laughed. Five days on the road with a dying smuggler in the cart and a god-child who spoke in thunder, and the man still asked if ancient sacred architecture was *nice*. "It's a question. It's the same question repeated down the entire flight." She ran her fingers along the groove, careful not to touch the edges. Scholars who touched things carelessly ended up as cautionary footnotes in other scholars' books. "And it's written in the First Tongue."

Ilo stirred against Lena's shoulder. His eyes opened—not all the way, just a sliver of grey catching lamplight—and his lips moved around a word too soft for human ears.

The spiral grooves brightened.

Sefa snatched her hand back. The light traveled downward ahead of them, step by step, like something descending to open doors. Like a household hearing its master come home.

"Does he do that often?" she whispered.

"Since we entered the valley." Lena's voice was flat in the way she'd learned meant the opposite of calm. "He's been humming since the weir gate. Same three notes. Over and over."

*Same three notes.* Sefa filed it away with everything else—the ward-stone cracking in the cellar, the floodwaters parting, the way the boy's breath fogged in shapes no wind should make—and kept walking, because the alternative was standing on these stairs until her legs gave out.

At the bottom, the stairs opened into a hall that made her forget how to breathe.

It was round, vast, and drowned in light. No windows, no torches, and yet the whole chamber glowed from within, the way a lamp glows through cloth. Pillars rose in concentric rings, each carved floor-to-ceiling with the spiral script, and above them all, suspended in the center of the ceiling like a heart in a ribcage, hung a cage.

Not iron. Not stone. It was made of words—millions of them, interlocked, dense as chainmail, forming a sphere perhaps thirty feet across. And through the gaps between the words came a light so old and so patient that Sefa felt her knees soften.

Something was inside it. Something enormous. Something that had been waiting so long that waiting had become its shape.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're still *there*."

"What did you say?" Lena asked sharply.

"Nothing. I—" She steadied herself against a pillar. "I said nothing. Out loud."

The codex was burning hot in her satchel. She'd noticed it growing warmer for two days, ever since they crossed into Veyra's country, and now it nearly scorched through the oilcloth wrapping. Her hands shook as she unwrapped it there on the floor of the god's prison, kneeling in the dust of an age that predated dust.

The final pages were no longer blank.

They had been blank when Ryn bled on them in Callowmere. They had been blank when Sefa fell asleep over them in the cart, dreaming of her colleagues' faces as the inquisitors read her crimes aloud. Now ink crawled across them in real time, line by line, in a hand that changed mid-sentence—first formal, then hurried, then something raw and human that no academy would have accepted in a manuscript.

"Ilo," Lena said quietly. "Look at me. Don't look up."

But Ilo was already looking up. All five years of him were looking up, and his face wore an expression Sefa had never seen on a child: recognition. The look of someone finding the door to a room they'd been born remembering.

"He wants me to read," the boy said—in the First Tongue, but Sefa heard it double now, the way she'd begun to hear everything he said, the meaning arriving underneath the sound like current under ice. "He says the pages are his voice. He says he can't hold them much longer."

"Hold *what* longer?" Lena demanded.

"The truth," Sefa breathed. She bent over the codex, and began to translate aloud, because some things were not meant to be carried alone.

* * *

*They tell me I am a god,* the first page read, *and gods do not get to be tired.*

*Sefa's voice wavered. Lena crouched beside her, one arm still curled around Ilo, and listened.*

*I was young when the first city called me. Do you know what that means, for something like me? A thousand years is a season. A war is an afternoon. When Ashgrave's founders knelt in the mud and swore their oaths, I thought: here is a people who want what I want. Silence between battles. Stillness after grief. I gave them my name to hold, and they held it gently.*

*For six hundred years, we were gentle together.*

*Then came the man Marrow.*

*Not this Marrow. His grandfather—or his grandfather's grandfather; your bloodlines blur, and I confess I stopped keeping track of which conqueror descended from which. What matters is this: Kaelen Marrow's line found a way to take my power without asking. Oath-marks, they called their theft. They branded soldiers with my sigils and fed the marks with my name, and the soldiers became engines. Engines of conquest. Every city they took, they took with my stillness turned inside out—a silence that falls on a battlefield is peace, but a silence forced into a screaming throat is something else entirely.*

*I felt every throat.* 

*You cannot imagine it. I barely could, and I am the one who felt it. Sixty years of war, fought with my hands. Cities that prayed to me for quiet and received the quiet of graves. And the branded ones—your mercenaries, your oath-sworn, men and women who gave their lives to me honestly—I watched my own name burn them hollow. They thought I had abandoned them. I could not even scream loud enough to tell them otherwise, because screaming is exactly what I had been made unable to do.*

"So the silence," Sefa whispered. Her eyes were burning. "When Veyra went silent five years ago—"

*Keep reading,* said the page.

*I made a choice, and I need whoever finds this to understand it, because history will call it death and it was not death. It was surgery.*

*A god's power cannot simply be switched off. But it can be bound—if the binding is spoken in my own tongue, by my own will, sealed with my own consent. No cage built by enemies can hold a god; only a cage the god builds willingly has walls strong enough. So I went to the Sunken Abbey, where my oldest faithful once gathered, and I asked the last of them to speak the binding over me. I asked them to lock me away from the world, so that Marrow's engines would starve. Better that the free cities lose their god than live under a god made into a whip.*

*The last of them refused. He said the people needed me. He said surrendering my power was desertion.*

*He was wrong, and I loved him, and he ran anyway. I felt him go. I have felt him go for five years.*

Sefa stopped reading. Her gaze lifted despite everything, found Lena Zhou's face, and saw there the exact expression she imagined was on her own: the particular devastation of a man learning that his deepest wound had been self-inflicted.

Five years ago, Lena had walked away from the binding ceremony. She'd pieced it together on the road—his fragments, Ryn's contempt, the way he never said *my god died*, only *my god went silent*. He had been there. He had been the last of the faithful, and when Veyra asked to be caged, he had called it betrayal and fled, and the binding had happened anyway, without him, and he had spent five years believing his god had abandoned *him*.

"Lena," she started.

"Don't." His voice was very quiet. Ilo's small hand came up and pressed flat against his cheek, and Lena closed his eyes and let him.

"Keep reading," Ilo said, in both languages at once. "It gets worse."

* * *

*Here is the part I need you to understand most of all,* the codex continued, the handwriting growing tighter, more urgent, as though the writer were running out of the strength to hold the pen. *A willing cage requires a key. That is the price of consent—you must leave yourself a door, or the binding becomes just another theft, and I have had enough of theft.*

*The key cannot be a thing. Things can be stolen; Marrow would have stolen it within a decade, and my sacrifice would have bought nothing. The key must be a word—an unfinished word, held in living memory, that only completes when the world is ready for me to end or to return.*

*So I unmade my last utterance into flesh.*

*Do not recoil. Hear me. I poured everything unfinished in me—one single word, the word that means both release and ending, the word I have never finished saying in all my existence—into a vessel that could grow, and learn, and choose. Not a weapon. Weapons are finished things. A child. A child who would speak the First Tongue because it would be, quite literally, his native language. A child whose every syllable would echo in my prison walls and remind me why I did this. A child who would grow up in a world I emptied of magic, hating the silence, until someone found him and brought him home.*

*I am sorry. I am so sorry, little one. I could not ask permission. You did not exist yet. If you are reading this and you hate me, I will spend whatever eternity remains to me understanding that hatred, and I will deserve it.*

"But he doesn't hate you," Sefa said helplessly, looking at Ilo.

The boy wasn't listening. He had stepped out of Lena's reach and walked to the center of the hall, directly beneath the cage of words, and stood there with his arms slightly away from his body, the way a person stands in falling snow. The light through the word-gaps pooled around him. Where it touched his skin, faint spirals appeared—oath-marks, but soft ones, provisional, like a signature practiced in pencil before being committed to ink.

"I remember," Ilo said. "From inside. I remember the engines. I remember the throats." His voice stayed level, which was somehow worse. "That's why I talk all the time. In my head there's always... a lot of talking. Trying to finish. One word. It's very long. It's the longest word." He looked back at them. "I'm the beginning of it. Somebody has to say the rest."

"And when it's finished?" Lena's voice was gravel. "What happens to you?"

The codex answered before the boy could. Sefa read the next lines and felt the floor tilt beneath her scholarship, her principles, her careful neutral ground.

*I do not know if the vessel survives the word's completion. A word this size may need every syllable of him. I have tried to make it otherwise—to make myself small enough to pass through a child and leave the child intact—but I am what I am, and I have never once in my existence managed to be small.*

*If you love this boy, understand what bringing him here means. Understand that mercy has a cost, and I paid mine gladly, but the final payment is not mine to make. It is his. Or yours.*

*One more thing, and then my strength fails: Marrow knows. Not everything—he does not know about the consent, the willingness, the choice—but his scholars have always known I was bound rather than dead. Why do you think he outlawed magic? Not from piety. A silent god suits a tyrant beautifully, but a caged god is a treasure vault with the door showing. Marrow has spent five years searching for the key. He calls it a linguistic anomaly. He calls it a weapon. He will call it anything except a child, because the moment he calls it a child, he would have to admit what he intends to do with it.*

*Do not let him finish my sentence. Whatever else happens—whatever you decide about me, about mercy, about whether this cage should open at all—do not let my name become his again. I would rather end forever than be his engine one more day.*

*This is Veyra. This is what Veyra wanted.*

*Remember it.*

The ink stopped moving. The final page lay blank and warm under Sefa's fingers, and the great light overhead dimmed by a fraction—not dying, she thought. Resting. Conserving what remained of itself for whatever came next.

* * *

For a long time, none of them spoke.

Sefa sat on the cold floor with the codex in her lap and let the grief move through her in stages, the way she'd learned to let it after the archives expelled her, after her mentor denounced her, after every loss that had taught her that knowledge was never free—it cost, and the currency was usually yourself.

Because here was the thing she could not stop examining, turning over like a flawed coin: when she'd first deciphered the codex on the salt road, her first feeling—her honest, shameful first feeling—had been *at last*. At last, a text that answered everything. At last, a living specimen of the First Tongue, a phenomenon worth ten careers. She had wanted to study Ilo before she wanted to save him. On the road, watching him sleep, she had caught herself composing footnote structures. Cataloguing him. Making him, in the privacy of her own hungry mind, exactly the thing the council in Callowmere had wanted to make him publicly: an exhibit. A proof. An *it*.

And now the codex itself had done what no accusation could. It had shown her the face of the crime. Marrow's scholars had wanted a weapon; the council had wanted a proclamation; Sefa had wanted a thesis. Three different cages, and every one of them built from the same material: the refusal to see a child standing in front of you, tired and lonely and carrying the longest word in existence.

"You knew," she said finally. Not accusing. Asking.

"I suspected." Lena hadn't moved from where he knelt. His oath-marks were visible at his collar, dark and old, and for the first time since she'd known him they seemed less like brands and more like scars—wounds that had finally been explained. "Five years ago, she told us what she intended. Most of the order called it heresy. I called it..." He exhaled. "I called it abandonment. Because I couldn't bear it being anything nobler. If she chose to leave, then leaving was possible, and I had to live with having been left. It was easier to be angry." His jaw worked. "She was right. Gods. She was right, and I ran, and she locked herself in the dark alone."

"She wasn't alone," Ilo said.

Both of them looked at him.

"She had me." The boy shrugged, small and matter-of-fact, in the manner of children stating enormous truths. "Every time somebody spoke kindly, she said she could hear it through the walls. Every lullaby in every village. She said the cage was full of holes, and the holes were shaped like voices." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile Sefa had ever seen on anyone under four feet tall. "She says thank you. For reading it properly. She says most readers skip to the end."

"There is no end," Sefa murmured. "That's the point. The word isn't finished."

"No," Ilo agreed. "It isn't."

Above them, the cage of words pulsed once, slow as a sleeper's heartbeat.

Sefa got to her feet, joints aching, mind moving faster now than it had moved in years. Because the scholar in her—the real one, the one that had survived disgrace—was already assembling the shape of the problem, and the problem was terrible and clean:

Veyra had asked for this cage. Which meant opening it required consent twice over—hers, freely renewed, and the vessel's. Ilo could refuse. *Should* be able to refuse. And yet the codex's warning rang in her skull like a cracked bell: *a word this size may need every syllable of him.* The god had not been able to promise the boy's survival, only her regret.

And outside, somewhere down the drowned stair and across the valley, Doran Vess was coming with a company of inquisitors, and behind Vess stood the armies of Kaelen Marrow, and behind *them* stood the free cities begging to be saved, and every single one of them wanted the same unfinished word—for a crown, for a chain, for a salvation—and not one of them, not the tyrant nor the councils nor the desperate grateful crowds, had asked the child what *he* wanted.

Except, Sefa realized, one had.

She turned. "Lena. What did you mean to do when you brought him here?"

Lena looked up at her, then at Ilo, then at the light.

"I meant to ask him," he said. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it saves. I meant to ask."

Before Ilo could answer, the staircase behind them lit from top to bottom—all two hundred steps and more, the spiral sentence flaring white—and through the flare came a sound Sefa had hoped never to hear again in this life: the long, thin cry of inquisitor horns, echoing down through the Abbey's throat.

They had found the door. And they were coming down.
