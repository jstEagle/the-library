## 16. Vess at the Door

The Sunken Abbey did not look like a place where gods were kept.

It looked like grief that had been given architecture—arches drowned to the waist in black water, a bell tower leaning like a mourner who had stood too long at the graveside and forgotten how to leave. The tide came through the broken eastern wall twice a day, and the monks who had once chanted here were five hundred years of salt-bleached bones beneath the flagstones, or so Sefa said, her lantern held high as they picked their way down the flooded nave.

Lena carried Ilo on his back, the boy's arms looped loose around his neck, his breath warm against Lena's ear. Ryn lay on a litter of sailcloth and oar-shafts between Sefa and the smuggler's one surviving deckhand, a boy called Tam who hadn't spoken since Callowmere burned behind them. The wound in Ryn's side had been bound with charm-cloth and prayer, in that order, because Sefa insisted the order mattered.

"She's still with us," Sefa murmured, for the fourth time in an hour. "She's still with us."

"Say it again," Lena said. "I like the sound."

Ryn's eyes opened a sliver. "I heard that," she rasped. "And if I die, I want it noted I was conscious for the good parts."

"The good parts are ahead," Lena lied gently.

The codex's final pages had led them here, down the coast road and then off it entirely, along a pilgrim path that no longer appeared on any map Sefa owned—which was how she knew it was the right one. *The caged god sleeps where the sea remembers her name.* The Abbey's foundations went down further than any building should, into chambers the water had claimed and never surrendered. Somewhere below them, behind doors that had not opened in six years, Veyra waited in her cage of silence.

Ilo stirred against Lena's neck. He had been quiet since dawn—not the frightened quiet of the early weeks, but something older. Focused. As they descended toward the heart of the Abbey, he began to murmur under his breath, fragments of the First Tongue, and each syllable made the oath-marks on Lena's forearms warm like stones left too long in the sun.

"You feel it too," Lena said quietly.

Ilo pressed his palm flat against Lena's shoulder. *Yes,* in the wordless grammar of children.

They reached the inner sanctum near dusk—a circular chamber above the floodline, its dome pierced by a single oculus through which the last grey light fell in a column onto a floor of black stone. Inlaid in that stone, worn nearly smooth by centuries of kneeling feet, was the sigil of the silent god: a closed mouth, ringed by open hands.

Lena set Ilo down. The boy walked to the center of the light and stood there, small and solemn, and the marks on Lena's arms began to burn in earnest.

"It's real," Sefa whispered. She had the codex open across her knees, but she wasn't reading it; she was watching Ilo the way scholars watch eclipses. "The seal is here. Under us. Six years of silence, and it's been under our feet the whole time, under every city, under—" She stopped herself. Lena saw her jaw tighten, saw her drag her thoughts back from the precipice of *what does this mean for scholarship* to *what does this mean for the child*. Progress. Slow progress.

"We rest tonight," Lena said. "We move at low tide tomorrow, when the lower chambers drain. Sefa, you'll need light down there. Ryn—"

"I know my part." Ryn's voice was thin but steady. "I lie here and complain."

"That's the whole plan," Lena agreed. "You've always been the load-bearing element."

Her laugh turned into a cough. Tam brought her water without being asked. Outside, the sea worked at the broken wall, patient as a siege.

* * *

They came two hours past midnight.

Tam heard them first—or rather, heard the absence: the hounds' silence on the cliff road above, dogs that had been baying at the surf all evening going abruptly, unnaturally quiet. Then the lantern-light bloomed in the ruined cloister, dozens of lanterns, and boots on old stone, and the particular disciplined clatter of a company that did not need to be told to spread out.

"Inquisitors." Sefa was already moving, snuffing their own fire, gathering the codex. "How? We didn't—there was no signal, no trail—"

"They didn't follow us," Lena said. His stomach had gone cold and calm, the way it did before battles, the mercenary's gift he'd spent five years trying to bury. "They knew where we were going. They've known since Callowmere." Since the council. Since someone in that gilded hall had sold them the same way Ryn's crew had been sold. It didn't matter who. It only mattered that the door had only ever had one exit, and Doran Vess had found it.

He woke Ryn. She came up out of sleep with a knife already in her hand, which Lena thought showed admirable priorities for a dying woman.

"How many?" she asked.

"Forty. Maybe more."

"Then we fight clever."

"We don't fight at all." He looked at Ilo, awake now, standing barefoot in the dark with his hand finding Lena's sleeve without needing to see. "There's nothing to fight *for* here yet. The chamber below doesn't open until we're ready. If we die in this room, we die having done nothing."

"So what's the plan, mercenary?"

Lena looked at the column of moonlight falling through the oculus, and at the closed-mouth sigil beneath it, and felt the old oath stir in him like a sleeper turning over.

"I'm going to have a conversation."

* * *

Doran Vess entered the sanctum alone.

That was the first surprise. Behind him, visible through the shattered doorway, the inquisitors had formed their cordon—grey cloaks, iron masks, crossbows levelled—but Vess himself had come forward unarmed, his lantern held in one white-knuckled fist, his face bare of the mask that marked his rank.

Up close, the years had not been kind to him. Lena remembered Vess from before—the tall, immaculate captain of the Third Oath, Veyra-sworn, whose blessing-marks had gleamed silver at his throat during morning devotions. This man's cloak was patched. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that suggested it had happened quickly. And when the lantern light caught his collar, Lena saw the thing he could not hide, the thing he had perhaps stopped trying to hide:

A brand. Not an oath-mark. A brand—the scar of an oath cut out by force, a ragged star of burn tissue where Veyra's mark had once lived.

Deserters got brands. Traitors got brands. Men who swore to the silent god and then served the man who caged her got brands, because Kaelen Marrow trusted no faith he could not hold hostage.

"You remember me," Vess said. His voice was flat, careful. A voice walking on ice.

"I remember a better man," Lena said.

Something flickered across Vess's face—pain, fury, he couldn't tell which—and died. "Lena Zhou. The Charm-Seller of Ashgrave. Five years selling luck-knots and fever-washes while your brothers burned for the same oath you wore." He glanced at Ilo, standing half-hidden behind Lena's leg, and his expression did something complicated. "And this is the anomaly. The linguistic weapon Marrow wants alive."

"He's a child."

"He's the end of everything I built my survival on." Vess set the lantern down, slowly, deliberately, and stepped back from it—an officer's gesture, *I am not reaching for a weapon*, though they both knew forty crossbows made the gesture decorative. "Captain Zhou. I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to do my duty, and one of those things is going to hurt more than the other. Surrender the vessel. Surrender the scholar. Come with me, and Marrow will give you back your rank, your pardon, your name. He offered it specifically. He remembers you. He *respects* you." The last word curdled in his mouth.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take him, and you die among people who loved you, which is more than most of us get." Vess's eyes moved to Ryn's litter, to the blood-darkened bandages, to Tam's white face. "I have forty men and the tide is coming in. There is no version of tonight where you win."

"No," Lena agreed. "But there might be a version where you do something else."

Vess went very still.

Slowly, watching the captain's face, Lena pushed back his sleeves.

The oath-marks caught the lantern light. Six years of scrubbing and concealment-charms and long sleeves in summer heat, and still they showed: the interlocked rings at his wrists, the running script up both forearms, the closed mouth over his heart that matched the one inlaid in the floor beneath their feet. Silver-grey, alive, *waiting*. Every other sworn soldier's marks had gone dull and dead the day Veyra fell silent. Lena's had not. He had told himself for five years that it was a deformity, a scar that wouldn't heal, a god's mistake.

Standing in her abbey, he finally admitted what Sefa's codex had told him and he had refused to believe: the marks weren't dead because the god wasn't dead. They had stayed lit because some small, stubborn part of him had never stopped speaking to her—even when he'd run, even when he'd hidden, even when he'd burned his prayers.

"I know what you're doing," Vess said hoarsely. "Put them away."

"Look at them, Doran."

"*Put them away.*"

"Look at mine," Lena said quietly, "and then look at yours."

The silence stretched. Out in the nave, the cordon shifted, restless. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping time like a metronome.

Then, with the movement of a man dismantling himself piece by piece, Vess pulled open his collar.

The brand stared up at them both—raw even after six years, the oath-mark's shape inverted, hollowed, a wound shaped exactly like the thing it had replaced. Two versions of the same devotion, side by side in the lamplight: one that had endured, and one that had been torn out by the roots and paid for with a lifetime of service to the god's jailer.

"I was at the caging," Vess said. His voice had gone somewhere far away. "Did you know that? Third Oath, honor detail. They told us we were escorting her to a sanctuary. That she'd *asked* for it. And I believed them, because believing them meant I got to keep my rank, and keeping my rank meant my family ate." He laughed—a terrible sound with no humor in it. "When the marks went silent, Marrow gave us a choice. Burn them out and swear to him, or go into the fires with the rest of the faithful. I had three children, Zhou. Three children. You were a mercenary—you understood contracts. Faith ends when the payment stops."

"My marks never went dark."

"I know." Vess's eyes were wet and furious. "Do you think I haven't wondered why? Do you know what I told myself? That yours were defective. That some mercenary's cheap blood couldn't hold a real oath anyway. I told myself that for six years while I hunted your brothers through the free cities and burned their bodies so the marks wouldn't *sing*." His hand rose halfway toward his brand and dropped. "And then I felt the ward-stone crack in Ashgrave. Across the whole city, everyone sworn felt it, and every branded man flinched, and I stood in my quarters and I knew—I *knew*—that the god wasn't gone. She was waiting. And everything I burned was burning for nothing."

"Then put the lantern out," Lena said. "Let us pass."

"You know I can't." But Vess's hand had gone to his sword-belt, and it was shaking, and it did not draw.

"You can," Lena said. "Or you can stand there another six years, and another six after that, telling yourself the story gets less true every time you tell it." He took a breath. And then he did the thing he had practiced in whispers for three nights, the syllables Ilo had pressed into him syllable by syllable, patient as tide, the boy correcting him with small touches of his fingers:

He spoke the First Tongue.

It came out rough, accented, human—a soldier's pronunciation, wrong in a dozen ways Sefa would have catalogued for hours. It didn't matter. The words were not for precision. The words were the ones Ilo had taught him first, the oldest ones, the ones that predated kingdoms and crowns and cages:

*I see you. I remember you. Your name is not lost.*

The effect was immediate and total.

Every lantern in the sanctum flared. Beneath their feet, the black stone lit from within, the closed-mouth sigil blazing silver, and the oath-marks on Lena's arms answered—burning, singing, alive. Out in the nave, men cried out. Crossbows wavered. And Doran Vess staggered backward as though struck, his hand pressed flat over the brand on his chest, tears cutting clean lines down his dust-grey face.

Because the brand had answered too.

Not healed—nothing healed a brand. But beneath the scar tissue, deep where the root of the old oath had been cut away, something *moved*. Lena watched the man feel it, watched six years of self-loathing crack down the middle like river ice in spring, and understood that he had just done the cruelest and kindest thing of his life in the same breath.

"What did you say," Vess whispered. "What did you *say* to me."

"What you've needed someone to say since the day they took your marks." Lena held out his hand—not commanding, just offering. "Come with us, Doran. Help us open the cage. Whatever she asks when she wakes—whatever it costs—we'll pay it together, and maybe that's the only absolution either of us gets. Or stay. Stay and be Marrow's blade until the day he throws you away too. But choose it knowing what you know now."

For a long moment, Doran Vess stood in the flaring light with his hand over his ruined chest, and Lena watched a war happen inside him that no army could have settled.

Then the captain of the inquisition turned toward the doorway, drew himself up, and raised his voice in the parade-ground bark of twenty-five years of command:

"Company! Withdraw to the cliff road! The vessel is not here—the trail leads north to the salt flats! Move, move, *move!*"

The cordon broke. Boots thundered away through the cloister, lanterns swinging, orders snapping—and Vess stood alone in the doorway, holding the fiction together with his body, buying them minutes with his authority.

"Why," Lena breathed. "You know what this costs you."

Vess smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly, terribly, the man he might have been. "Marrow doesn't forgive failure, Zhou. You know that better than anyone. When they discover the lie—and they will, within the hour—there's only one traitor in this company to punish." He straightened his patched cloak with a soldier's precision. "My children are grown. My wife is ten years dead. I have been dead since the caging; tonight is simply the paperwork."

"Doran—"

"Don't." Vess lifted a hand. "Don't make it a debt. Just—" His voice caught. He mastered it. "Finish the word. All of it. Don't let them use her, and don't let her rule either. Let her *end* like she asked. Promise me that, and my whole rotten record balances."

"I promise."

Vess nodded once, satisfied, a man closing a ledger. Then he paused in the doorway, looking back at the sigil blazing in the floor, at Ilo's small face upturned in the light.

"She used to sing," he said. "During the march hymns. Did you know that? The god. Nobody wrote it down anywhere, but she used to hum the harmony line, very quiet, like she was embarrassed by it." His throat worked. "Tell her I'm sorry I forgot."

He turned and walked out into the dark.

They heard it twenty minutes later, distantly, from the direction of the cliff road: a single shout, the sharp order of an execution, and then silence. Sefa buried her face in the codex. Tam wept openly, without shame. Ryn stared at the ceiling and said, very quietly, "That fool. That magnificent, stupid, *brave* fool."

Ilo tugged Lena's sleeve and pointed down—at the floor, at the sigil, at the cage beneath the rising tide. His eyes were wide and wet and certain.

Below them, faint as a heartbeat, faint as a woman humming the harmony line so as not to be noticed, something in the deep water had begun—very softly—to sing back.

And far to the south, along the coast road, the torches of an entire army had turned north toward the Abbey, marching through the night with Kaelen Marrow at their head.
