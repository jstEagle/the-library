## 17. The March on the Free Cities

The fever dreams came in colors first.

Ryn Okafor had been stabbed, shot with arrows, half-drowned in the Weir Gate's black water, and once—memorably—set on fire by her own quartermaster during a cargo dispute that had gotten out of hand. She knew pain the way other sailors knew tides. But this was different. This was the knife Sefa had pulled out of her side on the abbey road going septic, and her body burning itself down to the keel trying to fight it.

In the fever, she was back on the *Kestrel's Debt*, her brother Tomas at the wheel, laughing at something stupid she'd said. The sun was out. The water was wrong, though—too still, too silver—and when she looked closer she saw it wasn't water at all. It was a field of oath-marks, thousands of them, branded into skin stretched over the whole horizon, and every one of them was hers.

She woke screaming the second night, and Sefa was there with a cup of willowbark and poppy that tasted like boiled earth.

"Drink," the scholar said. "You've been talking in your sleep."

"What'd I say?"

Sefa hesitated, and Ryn had known the woman long enough now to know what that hesitation meant. "Names," Sefa said. "Mostly names."

Ryn drank. Outside the shuttered window of the safehouse—some Callowmere merchant's abandoned counting-house, three streets off the harbor—she could hear the city breathing wrong. Not the usual breathing of gulls and cargo bells and drunk sailors arguing about tide tables. Something tighter. Something listening.

"How long," she said.

"Two days since they brought you in from the boat." Sefa set the cup down. "Ryn. There's news."

"There's always news."

"Not like this." Sefa's hands were doing the thing they did when she was frightened—straightening papers that weren't crooked, aligning the inkpot with the edge of the table, ordering a small universe because the large one had come apart. "Marrow crossed the Serein four days ago. Three legions. The vanguard is already through Thornmere pass. Callowmere's council has—" She stopped. Started again. "The council has recalled every soldier from the coast garrisons. They're fortifying the river line. They say they can hold for six weeks. Maybe eight."

"And then?"

"Then Marrow takes the last free city on the coast." Sefa looked at her hands. "And everything we carried out of the Sunken Abbey stays buried with it."

* * *

The third day, Ryn got out of bed.

It took her the better part of an hour to do it without waking anyone—the fever had eaten muscle she didn't have to spare, and her left side screamed like a rent crew every time she put weight on it—but she'd captained ships through storms with two fingers of rum and a prayer, and this was only a room. She dressed. She leaned on the wall. She made it to the top of the stairs and stood there, sweating, listening to the voices below.

Lena's voice, low and level, the way he talked when he was keeping himself together by main strength. And under it, small and clear, Ilo's voice—not words Ryn understood, never words she understood, but the sound of them. That strange ringing cadence that made the air feel thicker, like the moment before lightning.

She went down.

They were in the counting-house's old receiving room, where merchants had once haggled over spice futures. Lena sat across the table from the boy, and between them lay a slate covered in Sefa's careful transliterations—the First Tongue rendered into common script, which everyone involved knew was like rendering a storm into a ledger. Ilo was pointing at one of the marks. Saying it. Slowly. Patiently. The way you'd teach a child.

Teaching *Lena*. Like Lena was the child.

"You look terrible," Lena said, without turning around. He always heard her. Years of sharing watches aboard the *Debt*, before everything; some things didn't burn.

"I've seen worse," Ryn said. "Usually in mirrors after a bad season."

Ilo turned and beamed at her, and Ryn felt the thing she always felt when the boy looked at her—that lurch, that grief with teeth. Because Tomas had smiled like that. Because Tomas had died with a brand on his arm and Veyra's name in his mouth, back when the god still answered, back when answering meant conquest engines and burning coastlines and a war Ryn had spent fifteen years pretending hadn't touched her.

"Sit down before you fall down," Lena said.

"I'm standing fine."

"You're holding the doorframe."

Ryn let go of the doorframe. Sat down. It cost her more than she let show, and she saw by Lena's face that he let her pretend otherwise, which was its own kind of mercy.

"The council sent word again," Lena said quietly. "This morning."

"How many times is that?"

"Fifth since we arrived. Sixth, if you count the one addressed to 'the vessel and custodians.'" His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "They've stopped asking politely."

"What do they want now?"

Lena slid a letter across the table. Heavy paper. Council seal, broken twice already—once by Sefa, once, Ryn guessed, by Lena staring at it all night. She read it.

*To the bearers of the Word: the armies of the Usurper stand at the river. Our walls are strong but our will falters. The people have heard rumors—a god walks among us, a power that can break the engines of Ashgrave's master. Let the child speak from the wall. Let him speak against Marrow as he spoke against the flood at the weir gate. One word from him and ten thousand swords will remember their courage.*

*We do not ask this lightly. But the alternative is chains for us all.*

*One word.*

Ryn set the letter down. Her hands were steady. She noticed that, distantly, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a quiet room.

"One word," she said. "That's what they're calling it now."

"That's what they've always called it," Lena said. "Since Callowmere. Since the gilded welcome. They just stopped wrapping it in banquets."

* * *

Sefa came at dusk with the harbor reports, and Ryn watched her read the council's letter with an expression Ryn had learned to fear—the scholar's hunger, that bright terrible wanting, fighting against something older and wearier.

"They're not wrong," Sefa said at last. "That's what makes it obscene. If Ilo speaks from the wall—if the First Tongue carries the way it carried in the flood—half of Marrow's army might turn. Those soldiers are sworn men, Ryn. Sworn the way Lena was sworn. Their oaths are still in there, buried under Marrow's brands. We *saw* what happened to Vess." Her voice caught on the name. Everyone's voice caught on the name now. "One word woke him after ten years of hunting his own god. What would it do to ten thousand?"

"And if it doesn't work?" Ryn said.

"Then Ilo has shown Marrow exactly what he's hunting, and the legions come here instead of stopping at the river, and the boy dies in a cellar or a cage or on an altar somewhere while the city burns around him." Sefa pressed her palms flat against the table. "But it will work. That's the horror of it. I've run the precedents. It will work."

"So we're not arguing about whether it works." Ryn leaned back, and her side flared white-hot, and she kept her face still through sheer spite. "We're arguing about whether it should."

"Yes."

"Then let me make it simple." Ryn looked at both of them—Lena with his burned-out eyes and his marks gone quiet as dead coals, and Ilo, who was supposed to be asleep but had come down the stairs at some point and was standing in the doorway with his blanket around his shoulders like a small grey ghost.

"When my brother died," Ryn said, "he died carrying Veyra's word into a battle the god herself ordered. And everyone told him it was holy. The priests, the captains, the war-councils. *You carry the word, Tomas. You are the vessel. It's an honor.*" She heard her own voice go flat, the way it went when she was cutting ballast loose. "Nobody told him he was ammunition. Nobody used that word. But that's what a vessel is. It's a thing you load and aim and spend. And the free cities' council, with their seals and their heavy paper and their *we do not ask this lightly*—" she flicked the letter with one finger, "—they're loading him. Same brand, new flag. That's all."

"We know," Sefa whispered.

"Knowing isn't the same as refusing. Refusing costs." Ryn pushed herself upright, slow, until she was standing, until the room tilted and settled. "Here's the cost. We refuse, and Marrow crosses the river in six weeks, and Callowmere burns, and then Thornmere, and then every town that ever gave shelter to a smuggler or a scholar or a branded deserter. People who never chose anything die in our place, because we were too pure to spend the boy. And here's the other cost. We say yes, and Ilo stands on that wall and speaks, and it works, and we win—and then what? Then every council, every king, every frightened man with an army learns there's a living word that can turn battles. And they will never, ever stop coming for him. Not in his lifetime. Not in his children's lifetimes, if he lives to have any. We won't have freed the cities. We'll have crowned a weapon and called it mercy."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a bell rang the hour, and Ryn thought: *eight weeks*. Maybe less. Maybe Marrow's engineers had already built something new from the twisted scraps of Veyra's stolen power, something that made six weeks a generous number.

Ilo spoke from the doorway. Not the First Tongue—his common speech was coming slowly, word by word, like ice breaking up in spring. "Lena." A pause, working at it. "What is *ammunition*?"

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Lena closed his eyes. When he opened them, he crossed the floor and knelt in front of the boy, so their eyes were level, and Ryn saw him choose every word the way you'd step through a flooded hold.

"It's something spent," Lena said. "Something used up to make a bigger thing happen. A thing that gets thrown so something else can hit harder."

Ilo considered this with the grave attention he gave everything. Then: "Am I that?"

"No." Lena's voice cracked on it, and he steadied it. "No. And I will spend every breath I have left making sure no one treats you that way. Including me. Including the council. Including you, if someday you decide to throw yourself, and I have to be the one to catch you."

"But the city," Ilo said. Simple. Devastating. Children cut straight to the wound because nobody had taught them to go around it. "People die if I don't."

"People died if you didn't calm the floodwater too," Lena said. "At the weir gate. You spoke then because *you* chose it. No one put you on a wall. No one asked you to be a crown or a chain. You saw drowning people and you helped them." He took the boy's hands. "Whatever happens next—I need you to hear me. If you ever stand on that wall, it will be because you decided. Not because a letter asked. Not because we needed you. Because *you* looked at the river and chose."

Ilo looked at him for a long time. Then at Ryn. Then at Sefa, who had turned away and was pressing her knuckles to her mouth, and Ryn realized the scholar was crying, silent and furious about it, the way she cried over every page of that codex where knowledge and guilt shared the same ink.

"I don't know how to choose that big," Ilo said.

"Nobody does," Ryn said. All three of them looked at her. She shrugged, and paid for it, and kept talking anyway. "I've captained crews through choices bigger than any of us for twenty years. Secret is, you don't choose the big thing. You choose the next true thing. Then the next. The big thing takes care of itself, or it doesn't." She jerked her chin at the window, toward the river, toward the dark massing weight of Marrow's legions somewhere beyond the hills. "Next true thing right now is sleep. All of us. Tomorrow we decide whether we're a crew or a cage."

* * *

But sleep didn't come, and near midnight Ryn gave up on it and dragged herself to the window, and that was where Lena found her—two invalids watching a city hold its breath.

Fires on the southern horizon. Distant even from here, a smudge of orange against the stars, where the river garrisons were burning the bridgeheads ahead of the advance. Eight weeks, the council said. Ryn counted the fires and thought: fewer.

"Marrow sent a herald today," Lena said. "Did Sefa tell you? Proclamation nailed to the harbor gates. He offers the cities terms. Kneel, keep your councils, keep your gods—as long as there are no gods. He calls it *the Peace of Silence*." His jaw worked. "He'll win with words like those. Chains always dress as peace first."

"Lena." Ryn didn't look at him. "When you take the power—you've been thinking about it. Don't tell me you haven't. I've watched you stare at your own marks for a week. Ilo's been teaching you. Not just translating. *Teaching.*"

Lena was quiet long enough that the fires had time to shift on the horizon.

"If it comes to that," he said finally, "then yes."

"It'll kill you. Sefa's pages say so. The god's power spends whoever holds it—Veyra let herself be caged rather than be spent hollow, and she was *built* for it. You're a mercenary with old brands and a bad heart."

"I'm aware of the arithmetic."

"Then hear mine." Ryn turned from the window, and the fever was still in her, and maybe that's why the truth came out clean, without the armor she usually wore over it. "I hated your god for fifteen years. Your marks, your wars, your engines—my brother's ash. And then I met you, and you were just a tired man selling charms, and I thought: *there's what the god left behind. Nothing. A beggar.*" She laughed, low and rough. "But I was wrong. What the god left behind was a man who kneels in front of frightened children and tells them the truth. That's worth more than the god was. So listen to me, Lena Zhou. Whatever you decide to spend—don't spend yourself cheap on a wall for a council that wants a weapon. If it must be spent, spend it *free*. On your choice. For the boy. Not for them."

Below them, in the dark of the counting-house, a small voice began to murmur in its sleep. The First Tongue, soft as rain. And with it—faint, unmistakable—the groan of stone. Down in the cellar, Ryn knew, the old ward-stones were singing again, cracking a little more, the way they'd cracked in Ashgrave, the way they cracked wherever the boy dreamed aloud.

Lena closed his eyes. "He's dreaming about the river."

"Then we're out of time." Ryn pushed off from the wall, found her balance, held it. "Get Sefa. Get the maps. If the boy's going to choose, someone had better teach him what he's choosing between—and if the council comes for him tonight, they'll find a smuggler, a scholar, and a branded deserter standing in the doorway."

"And after that?" Lena asked. "After tomorrow?"

Ryn looked south, at the fires, at the future marching toward them with the Peace of Silence in its mouth.

"After tomorrow," she said, "we go to the wall. All of us. And we find out whether a word can be given without being spent—or whether everything I just said was fever-talk." She turned for the stairs, one hand pressed to her ruined side, her voice dropping to almost nothing. "Wake the others, Captain Zhou. Dawn's coming early, and it's bringing an army with it."
