## 18. The Word Unfinished

The word begins in his chest.

That is the first thing Ilo understands about it—that it is not a sound he makes but a sound that is making him. It has been waiting under his ribs since before he had ribs to wait beneath, coiled in the dark like a seed in frozen ground, and now the Sunken Abbey's heart has thawed it, and it wants out.

He stands in a circle of white stone at the center of everything. The Abbey around them is drowned architecture—pillars rising from black water, walls furred with pale moss that glows faintly, like the memory of light rather than light itself. Above, far above, a shattered dome opens to a sky gone the color of pewter. The water does not lap here. It holds its breath.

Lena stands at the circle's edge. Sefa behind him with the codex open across her forearms, its final pages bare. Ryn is not there—Ryn is upstairs in the ruined cloister with a fever and a bandaged shoulder, and Ilo carries the thought of her like a smooth stone in his pocket.

*Say it,* Lena had said an hour ago. Not *you must*. Never *you must*. Lena never says *must*, which Ilo has learned is its own kind of must, heavier than shouting. *Only if you want to. Only if you're sure.*

Ilo is not sure. But he is certain, which he has learned is different. Sure is a feeling. Certain is a floor you stand on when all the feelings have fallen away.

The codex says Veyra asked for the cage. The codex says Ilo is her unfinished word of release—the last syllable she could not speak, wrapped in a child's body and set adrift down five years of rain and roads until it washed up on the doorstep of one of her own broken soldiers. Sefa read it aloud in her careful scholar's voice, and then she had stopped reading aloud, and her voice had done something small and cracked, and Lena had put his hand flat on the stone table and said nothing for a long time.

Ilo knows what none of them will say plainly: that the word might need all of him to be said.

He knows it the way he knows the First Tongue—not as knowledge but as weather, as pressure, as the shape of a thing pressing against the inside of his skin. The god's power is not in the Abbey like water in a cup. It is in the Abbey like a scream in a sealed room. And he is the crack in the seal.

He looks at Lena.

Lena's oath-marks are burning. Ilo can see it even in this dim place—the brands along the man's forearms glowing a low ember-orange through his rolled sleeves, pulsing in time, Ilo realizes, with his own heartbeat. They have been doing that since they entered the Abbey. Since before. Since the night in the storm, maybe, when the boy spoke his first word on a stranger's doorstep and watched a grown man flinch as if struck.

*I'm going to say it now,* Ilo signs. His hands are clumsy; he learned Lena's sign-language only months ago and his fingers still trip over it. But Lena reads him better than anyone has ever read anyone, Ilo thinks. Lena reads him the way you read firelight—by what it shows, and by how it trembles.

Lena nods once. Slowly. His jaw is set the way it gets when he is afraid and has decided fear is a bill he will pay later.

Sefa closes the codex. "For what it's worth," she says quietly—and her voice echoes strangely off the drowned stone, coming back thinner—"the grammar requires consent. All of it does. Every line we've translated. The First Tongue doesn't compel. It *invites*." She looks at Ilo. "Whatever happens, it can't happen without your yes."

That should comfort him. It does not, entirely. Because he understands, in the way of the word inside him, that his yes was given long ago. Before Ashgrave. Before the rain. Before he had a name anyone used. The yes is the oldest thing in him. Everything else—Ilo, the boy, the garden he wants, the way Ryn calls him *sprout* like an insult that isn't one—all of that grew up around the yes like moss on a standing stone.

But he nods anyway. He wants it to be his yes too. Even if it already was.

He breathes in.

And speaks.

* * *

The first syllable is easy. It comes out of him like a sigh he has been holding for years, and the whole Abbey answers—every pillar ringing with a tone too deep to hear properly, felt instead in teeth and bones and the water itself, which shivers into rings spreading outward from the white circle.

The second syllable costs more.

It is not that it hurts. It is that it *takes*. As he shapes it, he feels something lift out of him—small things first. The smell of the bread Lena bought in Callowmere on their third morning. The exact blue of the scarf Sefa wore when they met her. The name of the street where his charm-shop memory lives, the one with the crooked door. Little coins paid out of a purse he did not know he carried.

He keeps speaking.

The third and fourth syllables come together, braided, and the power comes with them—a flood now, no longer trickling, the god's caged strength pouring up through him as though he were a channel cut for exactly this purpose, because he was, because that is what he is *for*, and the white circle blazes so bright that shadows of the pillars leap and wheel across the flooded dark like a flock of frightened birds.

Inside the flood, Ilo feels himself thinning.

It is hard to describe even to himself. It is like being a word written in water, and the water is being spoken through. He reaches for his own name and finds it slippery—*Ilo*, two syllables, warm, given to him by Lena on the salt road when the boy finally managed to sign it—but the name wobbles, blurs, threatens to resolve into something older and vaster. Something that remembers the world's first morning. Something that has been silent for five years and is so *grateful*, so relieved, so hungry—

He clings to the name. *Ilo. Ilo.* A garden. There is supposed to be a garden someday. Lena promised—or didn't promise, but talked about it, in his sideways way, about soil and seasons and a place with a crooked door where nobody would ever knock with bad intentions again. He holds the garden like a rope over a drop.

But the drop is very long, and the rope is very thin, and the word still needs more of him. Four syllables spoken. Seven remain. He can feel each unwritten one waiting like a stone step over emptiness, and he understands with a child's terrible clarity that each step will cost a piece of the boy who climbs it. By the last, there may be nothing left climbing. Only the word, finished at last, wearing whatever is left of him like a coat.

Veyra planned it this way. He feels that too, in the flood—her intention, patient and grieving. She had asked for the cage because her strength had become Marrow's engine, and she had shaped her release so it would need a vessel small enough to burn up completely. So no one could hold the power after. So liberation could not become lordship. She had built her own death into her own freedom, and she had built it into him.

She was merciful. That is the worst part. Even this is mercy, from her side of it: better one unfinished child consumed than a god unchained into another age of crowns.

Ilo keeps speaking anyway. Because the free cities are falling. Because Ryn bled on a rooftop. Because Vess died at a door. Because every person who ever lost anything to the silence deserves the end of it, and he is the only door.

The fifth syllable tears loose, and with it goes the memory of his mother's face—he did not know he still had it, and now it is gone, and grief and relief arrive together holding hands.

* * *

"—stop."

The word cuts through the blaze like a knife through silk. Not First Tongue. Plain, rough, human speech, cracked down the middle.

Lena.

Ilo surfaces from the flood far enough to see him, and what he sees frightens him more than the erasing does. Lena has stepped into the circle. Into the light. His oath-marks are not glowing now—they are *streaming*, threads of ember-light peeling off his arms and shoulders and jaw, drawn toward the boy like sparks toward a chimney. And his face—

His face is the face of a man watching a house burn with someone inside it.

"Stop," Lena says again, and crosses half the circle in three strides, and grabs Ilo by the shoulders, and the light screams—actually screams, a note of pure refusal shaking dust from the ceiling of the drowned world—but Lena does not let go.

"You listen to me." His voice is low and ferocious, the voice Ilo heard once through a cellar door when the inquisitors took the neighbor, the voice that has never once been raised at him. "You are not a door. You are not a word. You are a boy who likes honey on bread and hides Sefa's quills because you think her temper is funny. I know the difference. I've been both."

Ilo tries to answer. He tries to say *it has to be me, it was always going to be me*—but his mouth makes the next syllable instead, the sixth rising in his throat like water finding its level—

Lena covers the boy's mouth with his branded hand.

The contact detonates. Light erupts between palm and lips, and Lena makes a sound Ilo has never heard a man make—not pain exactly, or not only pain—and the flood *changes direction*. Ilo feels it happen: the current that was emptying him reverses, pouring instead into the man holding him, into the old marks, into the oath-brands that were meant to carry this weight five years ago and were abandoned at the moment of the god's silencing.

Through the roaring, dimly, Ilo hears Sefa shouting—something about the codex, something about *that's not in the grammar, that shouldn't be possible*—and then, sharper, her voice breaking into a different shape: "*Lena, it's taking you—let go, LET GO—*"

He doesn't let go.

Ilo knows why. He can see it in Lena's eyes, which are wet and furious and absolutely certain, the same look the man wore on the doorstep in the rain. Lena would rather burn than bury. Lena spent five years learning the cost of invisibility and has decided, here in the heart of the drowned Abbey, that he is done paying it in other people's coin.

The sixth syllable dies unspoken in Ilo's throat. Six remain.

Six remain, and the god's power is now moving through Lena Zhou instead, filling the channels of an old broken oath, and Ilo watches the marks climb the man's neck like dawn climbing a wall, and understands that they have only traded the price between them, not canceled it.

"No," Ilo manages—his own voice, rusty, human, *his*—"no, you said—you said only if I wanted—"

"I lied." Lena's smile is terrible and tender. "First time. Don't get used to it."

Sefa is crying and translating at the same time, flipping pages with shaking hands, reading fragments aloud: "*...the vessel may yield the burden... to any sworn hand that takes it freely... oh, gods, it's in the marginalia, it was always in the marginalia—*" Her voice cracks. "Lena, if you finish it, it won't stop with Marrow. It'll spend *you*. That's the design. That was always the design."

"I know," Lena says.

And Ilo, drowning in borrowed light, feels the shape of the choice settle over all three of them like the Abbey's broken dome: seven syllables left, a usurper's army marching on Callowmere, a god's power looking for a body to spend, and a father—who is not a father, who is a man who sells charms and once burned a prayer he didn't believe in—standing in the fire with his hand over a child's mouth, choosing.

Far above, muffled by stone and water and distance, a horn sounds.

Then another.

Then many, many more.

Sefa lifts her head toward the shattered dome, and her face goes gray. "Marrow," she whispers. "He found us. He's *here*."

The light in Lena's arms burns brighter, as if in answer.
