## 22. A City That Speaks

The bell over the schoolhouse door had been cast from a melted ward-stone, and Sefa still flinched at its sound.

A year, and her body hadn't learned. It heard that bright, broken chime and braced for boots on stairs, for the rattle of a cellar door, for the particular silence of a city holding its breath. Old habits lived in the tendons longer than in the mind. She stood at the front of the room with chalk dust on her fingers and waited for the fear to pass, and it did pass, and she hated that it passed—hated how quickly terror could be domesticated into nothing more than a small cold shiver before breakfast.

"Again," she said, and thirty voices said it with her.

*Veyra-sel.* The first word. The word Ilo had spoken in his sleep in a flooded cellar while ward-stones cracked like ice on a spring river. The word that had once made inquisitors weep and kings reach for their swords. Now it was a lesson for children who came to school with jam on their faces and their mothers' complaints about tuition still warm in their ears.

The word meant *remember*, though no translation quite held. Sefa had spent eleven months writing the grammar of a dead god's language and discovered, somewhere around the fourth month, that it refused to die. The First Tongue had no word for *obey*. That was the discovery that had unmade the usurper's whole theology, the one printed now on cheap pamphlets sold in every market from Ashgrave to Callowmere: Marrow's conquest engines had run on a corrupted dialect, a slave-language grafted onto the god's speech, and when Lena Zhou spoke the true tongue into his own branded body, the graft had simply—failed. Words that build cannot be forced to burn.

She wrote the glyph on the slate. *Veyra-sel.* Remember.

A boy in the second row—Tomas, gap-toothed, the son of a fishwife—raised his hand without waiting. "Teacher. If remembering is the first word, what's the last one?"

Sefa set down the chalk.

There were twenty-two words in the recovered lexicon. They had published nineteen. The last three—the ones Ilo had carried out of the Sunken Abbey, the ones that had let Veyra end rather than rule—Sefa kept locked in a drawer beneath her bed, not because they were dangerous but because they were sacred, and she had learned the difference the hard way, in archives, under candlelight, with her hands shaking over pages that men had killed for.

"The last word," she said, "isn't mine to teach yet."

Tomas frowned. "Whose is it?"

"Ask me again in ten years." She smiled, and the class laughed, and the bell rang, and the children poured out into the morning like water finding its level, and Sefa stood alone in the emptying room and let herself feel the thing she never said aloud in front of them:

That she had wanted, once, to be the only person alive who understood this language. That the hunger had sat in her chest like a coal, and she had fed it in secret for fifteen years among forbidden codices, and it had nearly cost her everything—nearly cost *him* everything—and now the language belonged to fishwives and dockhands and gap-toothed boys, and the coal was out, and what remained where it had burned was something she didn't have a better name for than gratitude.

She locked the classroom door. She walked down through the terraced streets toward the harbor, because today was a day for walking past old wounds and checking whether they'd healed.

* * *

Ashgrave's harbor had been Ryn Okafor's idea, which meant it had been argued about, bribed for, and built three times before anyone admitted the first two versions were sabotage by contractors Ryn had personally threatened.

It was beautiful anyway. Wide stone quays where the drowned undercity used to fester, cranes salvaged from Marrow's war-barges and repainted in the free cities' colors—no single color, all colors, because the covenant had no flag, only words. Fishing boats went out at dawn now without paying passage to anyone. The tide-gates ran on ward-light instead of chain gangs. And on the harbormaster's office, above the door, burned into the wood in letters a hand high: *Veyra-sel.*

Ryn Okafor stood on the quay with a ledger under one arm and a cane she refused to call a cane—"it's a walking stick, Sefa, there's a difference, the difference is dignity"—supervising the unloading of grain barges from Callowmere.

"You're late," Ryn said.

"I'm a teacher. We keep scholarly hours."

"You're late," Ryn repeated, with the flat satisfaction of a woman who had been right about things for so long that being right had become a personality. "Council session started an hour ago. You sent a student as your proxy. A *child*."

"Mira is fourteen and knows the lexicon better than half the councilors."

"That's not the defense you think it is." But Ryn's mouth twitched. She turned to watch the barges. Her limp had improved over the winter; the wound from the rooftop night had finally stopped aching in rain, or she had stopped mentioning it, which with Ryn amounted to the same thing. "They voted on the harbor charter?"

"Unanimous." Sefa handed her the copy. "Even Vellin signed. He cried a little. Don't tell him I told you."

"Everyone cries at these things now. It's the whole problem with freedom—no discipline." Ryn read the charter, and Sefa watched her read it, watched the woman who had once priced human lives in coin and found the ledger suddenly cheap. On the last page, where the signatories' names went, someone had added a line in different ink, older ink: *For Okafor, who bought this harbor with blood and was overcharged.*

Ryn stared at it for a long moment.

"I'm going to find whoever wrote that," she said, "and I'm going to make them repaint my office."

"Of course."

"And then I'm going to buy them a drink."

Sefa said nothing. There was a version of Ryn Okafor that existed only in these small silences—the one who had lain bleeding on a rooftop in Callowmere buying strangers time to run, the one who had recognized a dead brother's brand on a mercenary's arm and chosen, bitterly, at terrible cost, to believe in something anyway. That version did not do well with witnesses.

"The garden," Ryn said finally, gruffly, recovering. "You visiting the garden today? It's the anniversary."

"I know."

"He'll want you to bring the flowers. He always wants to be the one carrying them. Let him carry them, Sefa. It matters to him."

"It matters to me too."

"Yeah, well." Ryn tapped her walking stick against the quay stones. "You're both stubborn. Go on. I've got barges."

Sefa walked on along the waterfront, past the fish market, past the shrine—not a shrine, the council insisted, a *memorial*, though people left offerings anyway, because people would make shrines of anything, and perhaps that was the truest religion left in the world—up through the terraces toward the eastern hill where the gardens began.

* * *

Ilo was eleven now, or twelve; no one knew his true age, and he had decided it didn't matter, which Sefa considered the most sophisticated philosophical position anyone in Ashgrave had taken in a century.

He met her at the garden gate with dirt on his knees and a basket on his arm, and he was, unmistakably, simply a boy. Brown from the sun. Quick. Missing a tooth of his own now, the front one, lost to a fall from the pear tree, a loss he narrated to everyone with tremendous pride. His hair was cut short in the Callowmere style because he'd asked for it himself, because he wanted to look like other children, and the barber had wept quietly the whole time and charged him nothing, and Ilo had left flowers on the man's counter, which was the kind of thing Ilo did.

He had spoken his first ordinary sentence eight months after the Abbey. *The pears are ready.* Four words in the trade-tongue, delivered while standing on a kitchen chair, and Sefa had cried into a dish towel, and the household had pretended not to notice, and then given up pretending, because everyone cried in those days. Everyone cried at everything. Grief that has become gratitude expresses itself, Sefa had written in her journal, chiefly through weeping at fruit.

Now he spoke constantly, ceaselessly, the way children do—about beetles, about the neighbor's dog, about the unfairness of bedtime—and every common syllable was a miracle nobody remarked on anymore, which was exactly what he wanted, and exactly what he deserved.

"You're late," Ilo said.

"It's contagious, apparently."

"Ryn says you're always late. She says scholars think time is a suggestion." He fell into step beside her, matching his shorter stride to hers with the unconscious courtesy of a child raised among adults who loved him. "Did you bring them?"

"In my bag."

"Can I carry them?"

"You may."

He took the bundle of white flowers with both hands, carefully, the way he handled everything that mattered, and they walked together up the garden path between the bean rows and the bee boxes, to the far corner where the ground leveled off beneath the old pear tree, where the grass was always a little greener, where the soil had been turned twice a year for twelve months by every pair of hands in the city that owed him.

The grave was marked with no statue. No crown. No name in stone.

Just a wooden post, weathered silver, and carved into it in the First Tongue, in Lena Zhou's own handwriting copied from the last page of his journal: *Veyra-sel. Aneth-kai.*

Remember. Thank you.

Two words. The first word of the language and the simplest phrase in it, and Sefa had spent a year trying to write the definitive commentary on why that was enough, and had concluded, in the final draft, with a single line she crossed out and rewrote and finally left bare: *He gave away the only crown that doesn't corrupt, and it fit him perfectly.*

They knelt. Ilo arranged the flowers with a gardener's precision, stems crossed, heads outward, the way Lena had taught him one autumn afternoon in this same garden, back when teaching a child to arrange flowers had seemed like the smallest possible use of a man who had once held a god's fire in his body and let it spend him down to ash rather than let it touch the boy.

"Do you remember his voice?" Ilo asked.

"Yes."

"What did it sound like?"

Sefa considered. "Tired," she said. "And certain. Both at once. Like someone who had walked a very long way and knew exactly why."

Ilo nodded slowly, filing it away the way he filed everything. Then he bowed his head, and Sefa expected the usual silence, the child's version of prayer, which was mostly just squinting at the middle distance.

Instead, softly, in a voice still too young for gravitas but trying honestly for it, Ilo spoke.

*Veyra-sel,* he said. *Aneth-kai.*

Not the power-word. Not the vessel speaking. Just a boy saying thank you to a dead man in a language that belonged to everyone now, and the marks of it—that was the thing, that was the thing Sefa would carry home and write about tonight—the marks of it rose faintly on the backs of his small hands, ghost-pale, gone in a breath. Not power. Memory. The language itself, remembering him back.

Ilo looked up, startled, delighted. "Did you see?"

"I saw."

"It does that sometimes. When I mean it." He grinned, gap-toothed, sunlit, ordinary. "Ryn says it means the language likes me. Sefa—do you think he can hear? From wherever—"

"I think," Sefa said, and stopped, because she had prepared a scholar's answer and found she didn't want it. "I think a man who gave a whole city its voice can hear one boy in a garden."

Ilo considered this, accepted it, and moved on with the ruthless efficiency of childhood: "Come look at the bees. One hive swarmed yesterday and Ryn climbed the tree to get them and swore the entire time in three languages, and one of them wasn't even real, I checked with Mira—"

He took her hand and pulled her up, and they walked back down the path together, teacher and student, mourner and mourner, two people who had survived the same year and were learning, day by day, to call the survival something gentler than luck.

Behind them the pear tree moved in the wind, and the grave-post caught the light, and the two carved words held their silence the way good words do—not emptily, but patiently, the way a door waits for a hand.

* * *

Evening. Sefa sat at her desk with the window open, the harbor sounds rising up the hill—gulls, hammers, the new bell on the new ferry, someone singing badly and happily—and she wrote by lamplight in the journal she had kept since the cellar, since the rain, since the knock.

*One year ago I believed knowledge was safety. Then I believed knowledge was power. Now I believe it is neither. It is inheritance. You do not own it. You keep it the way you keep a garden—for the next hands.*

Below the entry, she pressed a white flower from the grave between the pages, and closed the book, and blew out the lamp.

Through the window, across the roofs of Ashgrave, the city glowed with ward-light that answered to no crown and no god—only to thirty thousand small agreements, renewed daily, in a language anyone could learn. Somewhere down in the terraces a mother was teaching her daughter the first word. Somewhere on the water Ryn was closing the harbor for the night. Somewhere in a garden a boy was asleep with dirt still under his fingernails, dreaming, probably, of pears.

The last three words stayed in the drawer beneath her bed. Sacred, not dangerous. Waiting—not for a king, not for a vessel, not for a weapon.

Just for the next hands.

And if some nights, walking home past the market square, Sefa paused at a certain corner stall that stood empty—swept clean, freshly painted, fresh flowers in a jar upon the counter, tended by neighbors who had never known its owner except as the charm-seller who vanished one stormy spring and returned, briefly, as something larger than a man—she paused only a moment. Touched the worn wood of the counter where ten thousand harmless charms had changed hands. Said nothing.

The city said it for her, everywhere, in everything, in every voice that chose to speak:

*Veyra-sel.*

Remember.

And it did.
