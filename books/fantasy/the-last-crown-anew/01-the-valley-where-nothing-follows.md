## 1. The Valley Where Nothing Follows

The trouble with killing a man in the Godless Valley, Emrys Fairweather had learned over ten long years, was that you had to do it the honest way.

No blessings. No borrowed strength. No whisper of divine fire running down your arm like a second pulse. Just muscle and steel and the cold arithmetic of a body that was getting older every winter. Out here, past the boundary stones where magic pooled and died like rain on oiled leather, every fight was fought at even odds — and Emrys had spent a decade learning to be grateful for that, or at least to pretend he was.

The bandit in front of him clearly hadn't gotten the same education.

"Last chance," the man said. He was young. They were always young. He held his knife low and wrong, weight on his back foot, eyes flicking to the courier's strongbox between them as if it might open itself and settle the matter. "Walk away, old man. Nobody needs to bleed today."

"Old," Emrys said. He rolled his shoulders, felt the ache of forty-two winters settle into place like an old dog finding its spot by the fire. "That's generous."

He heard the third one before he saw him — boots in snow behind the alder thicket, moving fast, no care for quiet because their plan assumed two against one from the front. Emrys let the young one commit first. The knife came up in a clumsy arc, and Emrys stepped inside it the way he'd been taught before this valley had a name worth saying aloud, caught the wrist, turned, put the boy into the snow with enough force to knock the wind out of him but not enough to break anything that mattered.

Then the second man came around the alders with a wood axe, and Emrys did what he always did when the odds turned ugly.

He reached for the blessing.

It wasn't a decision anymore. It was older than thought, older than the scar. Ten years of silence and still his soul turned toward that door out of habit, the way a man reaches for a lamp in a room where the oil ran dry long ago. *Vaelen,* he called, into the hollow place inside himself where a god used to live. *Old friend. Lend me your fire.*

And for the first time in ten years —

*something answered.*

Not a voice. Not words. It was smaller than that, fainter than memory: a warmth, deep beneath the scar tissue over his heart, like an ember breathing under ash. One heartbeat. Two. Emrys felt it rise through him the way spring rises through frozen ground, and he almost wept, there in the snow with an axe swinging at his skull, because after ten years of nothing, *nothing*, there was —

— and then it guttered. Died. Went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the axe came down.

Emrys got his forearm up instead of his head, which was the only reason he was alive to regret it. The axe bit through his vambrace and into the meat of his arm, and pain went through him white and total, and he stopped thinking about gods entirely. He headbutted the man — inelegant, effective — wrenched the axe free with a sound he preferred not to hear, and put its owner down hard enough that the snow around him went dark and stayed dark.

The young one was scrambling backward through the drifts, knife forgotten, face the color of the sky. Emrys let him go. He watched until the boy crested the ridge and vanished, and then he stood alone in the falling light with blood running warm down his fingers and the strongbox sitting fat and undisturbed between the trees.

His arm throbbed. His breath smoked. And underneath all of it, quieter than the wind but impossible to ignore, the scar over his heart itched.

It had been itching for three days now.

* * *

The courier, a nervous man named Aldous with ink-stained fingers and a merchant's talent for being elsewhere during violence, emerged from behind the boulder where Emrys had told him to stay once it was clear the bleeding had mostly stopped.

"Gods above," Aldous breathed, looking at the two men in the snow. "You killed them."

"They're breathing." Emrys wrapped his forearm with a strip torn from his own sleeve, knotting it with his teeth and his good hand. "They'll wake up cold and sorry. That's punishment enough."

"You didn't even draw your sword. I've seen sellswords work before, but that was—" Aldous shook his head. "How do you do it? Truly. Is it some trick of the valley? They say nothing supernatural can touch you here. That you're safe from witchcraft within these borders."

"The valley doesn't protect me." Emrys shouldered the strongbox; it weighed about as much as his regrets, which is to say considerably more than he'd have liked. "It just makes sure that whatever happens next is my own fault."

Aldous laughed uncertainly, the way men laugh when they can't tell if a joke has teeth. They walked back toward the ford road together, boots crunching, the sun going down behind the western peaks in a spill of copper and iron-grey. Emrys found himself listening — not to Aldous, who was describing the wedding of some merchant's daughter in Brackenfell with the enthusiasm of a man who had never been hit with an axe — but to the space inside his own chest.

Empty. As it had been for ten years. As it would be tomorrow, and the day after, until one day it wouldn't matter because there would be no *him* left to notice the difference.

Except.

Three days of itching. And then, in the moment he'd reached for Vaelen — warmth. A heartbeat of warmth, real as the blood on his sleeve. He told himself it was the wound. Told himself it was wishful thinking wearing the costume of a miracle. Told himself a great many things as the valley darkened around them, and none of them took, because Emrys Fairweather had been a liar for exactly ten years now and had gotten no better at it.

* * *

His cabin sat at the valley's edge, half a mile below the boundary stones, close enough that on certain nights — though he'd never admit this to anyone, least of all himself — he could stand at his door and feel the pressure of the world beyond, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. He'd built it himself in the first year, when his hands still remembered how to build things and his heart still remembered why. Stone walls, timber roof, a hearth that had never once gone cold since the night he lit it. Habit, again. Everything was habit now.

He fed the fire first. Then the wound — boiled water, clean linen, the sting of spirits that made his eyes water but not much else, because he'd been hurt worse and recently. Then the strongbox, opened with Aldous watching, payment counted out in honest coin: twenty marks silver for delivery of a deed and a dowry-chest to the Harrow farm, plus a bonus Aldous pressed on him with sweaty insistence that Emrys only accepted because refusing would have required conversation.

Then, finally, he sat in his chair by the fire with a cup of barley tea going lukewarm in his hands, and did what he did every night, and hated doing, and would do again tomorrow.

He waited.

There was no ritual to it anymore. In the early years there had been — kneeling, prayers in the First Tongue, the whole architecture of devotion built brick by desperate brick against the silence. He'd given that up around year four, when he'd caught himself praying to a god who could not answer and understood that he was no longer worshipping, only rehearsing. Now he simply sat. Faced the fire. Let the scar itch. Let the silence sit across from him like an old debt come to collect.

*Ten years,* he thought. *Ten years tonight.*

He knew the date the way he knew the date of his own birth. The tenth anniversary of the last time he'd heard Vaelen's voice — really heard it, not echo, not memory, but the living warmth of it, vast and gentle and terrible, filling the hollow places of the world. The night of the Binding. The night the gods' voices were sealed away and the burning of the world stopped, and everyone who survived called it mercy, and Emrys had known even then — even with the oath-scar still fresh and screaming on his chest — that mercy was the wrong word for it.

*Tourniquet,* the priests had said afterward, when they'd needed a word that sounded kinder than *amputation.* A tourniquet buys time. A tourniquet is not a cure. But nobody had ever come back to take it off.

He looked down at his chest. The shirt was loose enough at the collar to show the top edge of it: the oath-scar, a seal-shaped mark burned into the skin over his heart, fine lines radiating outward like frost on a windowpane. Every sworn member of the Binding companies bore one. Most men he'd served with wore theirs like medals. Emrys kept his covered, the way you keep a receipt for something you'd rather not have bought.

It itched now. It had been itching for three days, and it had never done that before — not in ten years of aches and weather-pains and the thousand small complaints of scarred skin. Scars didn't itch after they healed. Everyone knew that.

Scars also didn't warm up. Didn't breathe. Didn't feel, for one impossible heartbeat in the snow that afternoon, like a door opening somewhere very far away.

"Coincidence," he said aloud, to the fire.

The fire crackled. The wind worried the eaves. Somewhere outside, a fox screamed down in the hollow, high and strange, and Emrys's hand went to the sword leaning against his chair before his mind caught up and told him it was only a fox.

He sat back. Drank his tea. Told himself the same thing he'd told himself for ten years, the litany that kept the days moving: *the god is silent, the god chose silence, you helped Him choose it, and a man who helps build a prison has no business waiting at the gate.*

But his thumb moved, without permission, to the edge of the scar. Pressed. The itch flared, sweet and awful, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

*Something is coming,* the itch said, in the language scars speak. *Something is coming, and it knows your name.*

Emrys finished his tea, banked the fire, and lay down in his bed with his sword in reach and his boots on, which was how he'd slept for ten years and how he expected to sleep until the valley finally let him stop.

Outside, the snow began again — soft, patient, filling in tracks, erasing the day. And far off, at the very edge of hearing, at the very edge of the valley where nothing was supposed to be able to follow, something small was moving through the storm.

Barefoot. Unafraid. Coming toward his door.
