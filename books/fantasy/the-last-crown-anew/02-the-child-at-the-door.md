## 2. The Child at the Door

The storm came down off the Spine like something hunting.

Emrys had watched it build all afternoon — a bruise-colored wall crawling along the peaks, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, dragging its skirt of white across the valley floor. By dusk the wind was screaming against the shutters, and by full dark it had found every gap in his cabin's bones and filled the rooms with a low, keening whistle, as though the house itself were mourning.

He sat by the fire with a whetstone and a knife that didn't need sharpening. That was the truth of his evenings now: small rituals performed without purpose, motion without meaning, a man keeping his hands busy so they wouldn't start remembering things. The blade slid against the stone with a sound like breathing. Outside, snow struck the window in fistfuls.

Ten years ago tonight — or close enough to it; he'd stopped marking the exact day after the fifth anniversary, because counting had begun to feel like prayer and he'd sworn off prayer — ten years ago, the voice had gone out of the world. Not just *his* god's voice. All of them. Every blessing, every whisper of grace, every warm presence that had once pressed against the backs of mortal minds like a hand on a shoulder. Gone between one heartbeat and the next, all across creation, and no one had ever explained why.

In the Godless Valley, most people shrugged and got on with it. Magic had never reached here anyway; that was why he lived here. The valley was a dead spot in the world's flesh, a place where blessings failed and curses slid off and gods, if they still existed anywhere, did not bother to look. Emrys had come for exactly that reason. He'd wanted a place where nothing could follow him — not inquisitors, not old comrades, not the thing he'd done.

He hadn't counted on the silence following him anyway. It lived inside him now. It lived behind his sternum, in the scar.

He set down the knife and pushed back his sleeve. In the firelight, the oath-scar stood pale on his forearm: a long spiral wound around the limb twice, starting at the wrist and ending just below the elbow's crook, fine as wire and old as grief. The mark of the Binding Oath. Every member of his company had worn one by the end of the Godswar. Most were dead now. The rest had scattered like ash on wind.

It itched tonight. It always itched before storms, or so he told himself. He scratched it absently and went back to pretending he wasn't listening for a voice that would never come again.

* * *

A knock — three small raps, polite as a bird pecking glass — came sometime past midnight.

At first he thought it was the storm. Branches hit the cabin often enough in high wind. But branches don't knock *twice*, and when the sound came again, deliberate and patient, Emrys rose from his chair with the knife already in his hand and moved to the door the way he'd learned to move in a war: weight low, breath shallow, mind running ahead to what might be waiting.

He opened the door six inches, chain fastened, knife angled down along his thigh.

Snow blew in over the threshold. Beyond it, the night was a white roaring chaos — and standing in the middle of it, small and still as a fence post, was a child.

She looked eight, perhaps nine. Thin as a winter branch, wrapped in what had once been a fine cloak but was now ragged at the hem and crusted with ice. Her hair was dark and plastered flat to her skull, her face pinched blue with cold — and she was barefoot. Barefoot in snow deep enough to bury a man to the knee, and she stood upon it as lightly as if it were a hearth rug, her toes pink and unmarked.

Her eyes found him through the gap in the door. They were gray, the gray of slate under water, and utterly calm.

"You should be frozen," Emrys said, because it was the only thought that made it out of his mouth intact.

The girl tilted her head. Then she spoke — and the language that came out of her was one he knew, though no living tongue should know it, though he'd heard it last from the lips of a dying god.

"*Vael en torum,*" she said. "*Sul veth kai.*"

Let me in. I am cold.

The First Tongue. The speech of the elder days, the language the gods had spoken among themselves before men learned to beg in lesser words. Scholars called it extinct. Priests called it holy. Emrys had heard it exactly once in his life, spoken whole and unbroken, on the last day before the silence — and hearing it again now, out of a half-frozen child standing barefoot in a blizzard, was like hearing a dead man call your name.

His hands started shaking. He hid them behind the door.

"I don't know that word," he lied, in Common.

The girl looked at him with an expression far too old for her face — not anger, not pleading, something closer to patience, the way you wait for a horse to stop spooking. She said it again, slower, and this time the syllables seemed to sink through the wind and the wood and the wall of his own denial straight into the marrow of him. *Sul veth kai.* I am cold. And beneath the words, faint as a pulse under bandages, something else. Something he had spent ten years learning never to listen for.

"No," Emrys said. "Whatever you are — whatever sent you — no."

But his hand was already lifting the chain.

Later he would tell himself it was the cold, that no child could survive that storm, that turning her away was murder regardless of what she carried in her. All true, probably. None of it the reason. The reason was simpler and more shameful: she had said two words in a dead god's language, and some buried, starving part of him had lurched toward the sound like a drowning man toward light.

He pulled the door wide. Snow roared in; the girl walked through it as though wading through a curtain, unhurried, and stood dripping on his floorboards, looking around the single room with frank curiosity. Fire. Bed. Table. Knife, which she regarded without fear. Her gaze snagged on the whetstone, then moved on.

"Sit," Emrys said gruffly, and shut the storm outside.

* * *

He worked in silence, the way he did everything now. Stripped off her ruined cloak, wrapped her in his own blanket, set her by the fire where the heat could get at her properly. He put broth on to heat and bread on to warm and did not ask questions, because questions required answers and answers required trust and he trusted nothing that arrived speaking the First Tongue.

The girl ate like someone who had forgotten food existed and had just been reminded. She held the bowl in both hands and drank in slow, reverent swallows, and between mouthfuls she studied him — openly, relentlessly, the way children study things they've decided belong to them.

"What's your name?" he asked finally.

"Wren," she said.

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." A pause. "For now."

That sat strangely in the air. Emrys let it lie.

"Where did you come from?"

She pointed east, through the walls, through the storm, through a hundred leagues of mountain and forest. East was the capital. East was Aldermarch, the throne, the bells, everything he had spent a decade walking away from.

"Alone?" he asked.

Wren considered the question with grave seriousness, the way she seemed to consider everything. "Not alone," she said at last. "There was a voice. But it stopped." She touched her own chest, just below the collarbone — the same place, Emrys noticed with a lurch, where his own scar began its spiral. "When I crossed into the valley, it went quiet. Like falling asleep mid-word."

His blood turned to ice water. "What valley do you think you're in?"

"The quiet one." She said it simply, without drama. "The place where nothing follows. The voice told me about it. It said, *find the man who keeps the scar; even there, he will hear you.*" She looked up at him, slate-gray eyes steady. "You have the scar."

Emrys stood very still. The fire popped. Wind clawed at the shutters. And the itch in his arm had become something worse — a heat, a low smoldering burn, as though the scar remembered who had carved it and was trying to say his name.

"That voice," he managed. "What did it tell you to find me *for?*"

Wren set down her empty bowl. She reached across the space between them — slowly, deliberately, the way you reach toward a wounded animal — and laid two small fingers on the spiral scar.

Fire.

Not pain, exactly. Or not only pain. It was the feeling of a limb waking after years asleep, pins and needles turned to agony turned to something beyond both, and in the middle of it — one heartbeat, a single thunderous heartbeat —

*a voice.*

*— emrys —*

It said his name the way spring says it to frozen rivers. It said his name the way it used to say it every morning of his soldiering life, warm and vast and sorrowful and *glad*, and Emrys Fairweather, who had not wept since the Binding, who had not prayed since the silence, felt his knees nearly buckle under the weight of ten years of longing arriving all at once.

Then it was gone. Snuffed like a candle in wind. The scar cooled. The room was only a room, and the fire was only a fire, and the girl was looking up at him with an expression of terrible gentleness.

"There you are," she whispered. "It said you'd hear."

Emrys stepped back until his shoulders hit the wall. His heart slammed. His mouth tasted of copper and old incense. Terror and want warred in his chest with such violence he could hardly breathe — terror because nothing that spoke the First Tongue came bearing good news, and want because for one impossible instant the world had not been silent, and he had not been alone, and he would sell his soul — *had sold his soul, once, already* — to feel that again.

"What are you?" he rasped.

Wren drew her fingers back into the blanket and tucked her feet beneath her, small and cold and impossibly calm.

"I'm the last one," she said. "The last vessel. There were others, but they were taken. Worn." Something flickered behind the gray eyes — the first crack in that unnatural serenity, a flash of a child's genuine fear. "The king wears them. He wears them like armor. And when he's finished wearing them—" She stopped. Swallowed. Looked into the fire. "I ran."

Outside, the storm howled. Inside, Emrys stood with his back against the wall and the ghost of his god's voice ringing in his skull, and understood with sickening clarity that his decade of quiet had just ended — that the valley had kept nothing out after all, that the past had simply waited at the edge of the snow until he opened his door.

"They'll come for me," Wren said softly. It wasn't a question. "Men in gray cloaks. They ask questions politely. Serel Vane asks them most politely of all."

Emrys closed his eyes. Against the blackness of his lids he saw the road east, the capital, the throne, everything he had crawled away from — and he heard, fading, that single heartbeat of warmth, *emrys*, glad to find him after ten years of silence.

God help him. He had missed it.

"They'll come tomorrow," he said at last, hoarse. "Or the day after. No sooner — the ford floods in this weather, even for inquisitors." He crossed the room, took down his bow from the pegs, and began, with hands that had not yet stopped shaking, to string it.

Wren watched him work, head tipped, slate eyes reflecting firelight.

"You're going to help me," she said. Not quite a question either.

Emrys tested the string, nocked nothing, breathed once. "Get some sleep, girl," he said. "You'll need it. We leave at first light."

And far away in the roaring dark, at the flooded ford, the first lanterns of the crown's riders kindled one by one in the snow — patient, polite, and already moving.
