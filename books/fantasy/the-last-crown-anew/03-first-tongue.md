## 3. First Tongue

The fire had a voice.

Wren lay on the floor beside it with the blanket pulled up to her chin and listened to it talk. It said *pop* and *hiss* and sometimes a long crackling word that meant nothing in any language she knew, and that was good, because words she didn't know couldn't ask her questions. The man — Emrys, he had said, like it was a stone he was handing her instead of a name — had put the blanket over her an hour ago without saying anything at all, which was also good. Blankets were easier than names.

The cabin smelled of woodsmoke and old iron and, underneath everything, something green. She hadn't smelled anything green since the city. In the capital there were only smoke and tallow and the river's rot and the sweet-rot under the palace where the Wearing happened, and then there had been the road, which smelled of nothing, because she had run through the nothing part of the world for so long that her nose forgot how noses worked.

Here there was green. Here the voice in her chest had gone quiet as soon as she crossed the line of stones, and quiet, for once, did not feel like dying.

She watched Emrys across the room. He sat at his table with his back half-turned to her, doing something with a whetstone and a knife, *shhhk, shhhk*, slow and even, like the man himself was being sharpened. He had not asked her a single question since she touched his arm and made him make the sound — the deep, startled sound, like a bell struck underwater — and she was grateful, and she was also afraid of it, because people who did not ask questions were either very kind or saving them up.

She decided he was kind. She decided it the way you decide a ladder is safe before climbing: quickly, and with your whole weight.

Her feet still hurt. They had been hurting since the mountains, since the snow started, since she'd left her shoes somewhere on the far side of a frozen river because they filled with water and water-filled shoes are worse than no shoes, everyone knows this. Her toes were pink and wrong-shaped now, and when she wiggled them they complained in a small hot way. But the man had put them near the fire too, propped on a stool like two little guests of honor, and the complaining had softened into a dull throb, and the throb was almost friendly. Pain you understood was almost friendly.

*You fled,* the quietness said inside her. Not a voice anymore. More like the shape a voice left behind, the way a footprint stays in mud after the foot has gone. *You fled, and you must remember why.*

"I do," she whispered, too low for the man to hear over his knife.

He heard anyway. His hands stopped. *Shhhk* — silence.

"Eat first," he said, without turning around. "Questions after food. That's the order of things here."

She hadn't asked any questions out loud. But maybe grown men could hear whispering through floors the way she could hear bells through walls. She filed this away with all the other things she knew and could not explain: how many stairs were in the palace (four hundred and eleven), what color the inquisitor's cloak would be (grey, greyer than weather), the word for the thing that had been done to the king (*Wearing*, capital letter, spoken only in rooms with no corners). Knowing things was like breathing. She didn't remember learning to breathe either.

Emrys set a bowl in front of her: barley and salt pork and a carrot cut into coins, floating gold in the grey. Steam climbed off it in ribbons. She ate the way she had eaten on the road, which was to say she held the bowl against her chest with both arms and guarded it while she ate, eyes up, chewing fast, ready to run with it if she had to.

The man watched her do this and something moved across his face, quick as a fish under ice.

"No one's taking it," he said.

"You don't know that." Her mouth was full. Manners were for people who weren't starving. "Nobody knows that."

He huffed — not quite a laugh, more like a laugh's ghost — and went back to his chair. "Fair enough."

She ate all of it, even the gristly bit, even the fat she would have hidden under her plate in another life. Then she licked the bowl, because the bowl deserved respect too, and when she looked up the man was staring at the fire with an expression she recognized from mirrors she wasn't supposed to look in. It was the face of someone counting things they'd lost.

"The voice went away," she said, because the silence between them had gotten heavy and heavy silences attracted questions like honey attracted flies.

"What voice?"

"The one that walked me here." She hugged the empty bowl. "It talked in my chest. Not in my ears. In my ears I got the other sounds — the dogs, the horns, the men saying *vessel* and *heretic* and *little queen*, which is a lie, I'm nobody's queen, queens get crowns and crowns are bad." She said that last part with great certainty, because it was true down to her bones, truer than her own name some days. "But in my chest it said *north*, and I went north, and it said *cold*, and I was cold, and then it said *the valley, past the standing stones, where nothing follows*." She looked at him. "Nothing followed me here. Did you know that? First time in my whole life nothing followed me."

Emrys had gone very still. Still in a different way than before — before he'd been still like a man resting; now he was still like a door that has just heard its name knocked on. The scarred arm — the one she had touched, the one that had rung like a struck bell under her fingers — lay on the table, and she noticed he had turned it slightly toward the fire, away from her, the way you turn a wound away from someone who might poke it.

"It talks in your chest," he repeated. Carefully. Like carrying water across ice.

"Carried. Past tense." She shrugged, and the shrug hurt her shoulders, and she winced, and pretended she hadn't. "It got quieter every day of the walking. By the stones it was just... the shape of talking. Like when a bell stops ringing but your ears still hold it." She tapped her sternum with one finger. "In here. Empty-ish. But a good empty. A safe empty. Like a house after everyone's gone to sleep."

Something crossed his face again, that fish-under-ice movement, faster this time, and darker.

"And before?" he asked. "In the capital. What did it say there?"

And here was the problem, the big one, the one she had been circling all evening the way she circled the fire's light. Because she remembered the capital in pieces that didn't fit together, like a dropped plate glued by a child. She remembered the palace kitchen and a woman with flour on her arms who used to sing. She remembered running. She remembered a corridor with no corners — she was sure of it, absolutely sure, though she'd never seen such a thing anywhere else — and a door, and a sound behind the door like a hundred people breathing through one mouth. She remembered the word *Wearing* the way you remember a burn.

"I ran," she said. "From the Wearing."

His whetstone stopped mid-stroke. *Shhhk* cut off like a snapped string.

"They wanted to put it on me," she went on, watching his hands now instead of his face, because his face was doing too much and his hands were honest. "The crown. Or — not the crown. Under the crown. There's a thing under the crown, and it wears people like..." She searched for the shape of it. "Like gloves. And it wore out the last ones, and it needed a new one, and the voice said *run* before I even knew what from, and I ran, and the men in grey came after, and the voice said *north, north, where magic can't reach, where nothing follows.*" She frowned at the fire. "I don't know how I know that valleys can be holes in magic. I've never been anywhere. But I knew it the way I know four hundred and eleven stairs."

Silence. The fire popped its little word. Outside, the storm dragged its white sleeves across the roof.

"Four hundred and eleven," Emrys said.

"Stairs. In the palace. Down from the king's hall to the river gate." She counted them every time she'd gone down, back when going down those stairs was her job, back when she had a job, back when she was a girl who carried messages and stole bread and slept in the flour-woman's loft. "I counted them forty times. Forty-one. So I know it's right even though knowing-it is impossible."

He looked at her for a long moment. In the firelight his eyes were the brown of winter oak, and very tired, and — this surprised her — afraid. Not of her, exactly. She'd seen plenty of people afraid of her, the grey-cloaked men especially, their fear coming off them like heat off a stove. This was a different flavor. This was a man afraid of a door he himself had locked.

"You should sleep," he said finally.

"I'm not tired."

"You're nine years old and you crossed the Godless Valley barefoot in a snowstorm. You're tired down to your teeth."

"How do you know I'm nine?"

That stopped him again. She was getting good at stopping him; it took very little. "Aren't you?"

"I don't know. Nobody ever told me. The flour-woman guessed eight, the baker guessed ten, and the voice doesn't say numbers about me, only about stairs." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I think I'm nine. Nine feels correct. Like four hundred and eleven feels correct."

He made the ghost-laugh again, but it came out bent in the middle, and he rubbed his hand over his face, and for a moment he looked less like a wall and more like a man, which she liked better, though she suspected he wouldn't.

"There's more blankets in the chest," he said. "And if you hear anything in the night — wind, wolves, me getting up — it's all just the house doing house-things. Nothing follows you here. You said so yourself."

"Nothing followed me *in*," she corrected. "I don't know yet about staying."

He gave her a long look, and she gave him back the same look, and something passed between them that neither of them would have been able to name if you'd asked. It was not trust. Trust takes longer. It was more like two animals discovering they're the same kind of animal — the same scars, the same habit of sleeping facing the door — and deciding, provisionally, not to bite.

* * *

Sleep came for her eventually, the way it always did, sudden and total, like falling into deep water.

She dreamed of the corridor with no corners.

In the dream she was small — smaller than nine, small as a thought — and the corridor stretched ahead of her, pale and smooth as the inside of a shell, and the breathing-sound came through the door at the end, in-out, in-out, patient as tide. And the voice in her chest was loud again, louder than it had ever been on the road, and it was not saying *north* or *cold*. It was saying:

*When the time comes, you will have to choose what it costs.*

"That's not fair," dream-Wren said. "I'm nine. Nine-year-olds don't pay for things."

*Everyone pays,* said the voice, gentle and vast and terribly sad. *The only question is whether you choose the price or it chooses you.*

"But what does it cost?"

And the voice began to answer, and the answer was so enormous that her ears — her chest-ears, her heart-ears — folded shut against it like hands over a flame, and the dream tore along its seams, and she woke.

The cabin was dark except for banked embers. Snow ticked against the window. And Emrys was awake too — she knew it without looking, knew it the way she knew the stairs — sitting up in his chair by the cold hearth across the room, his scarred arm cradled against his chest like something injured, staring into the middle distance where dreams go to hide.

"You have it too," she said quietly. Into the dark. "The empty-ish feeling."

For a long time she thought he wouldn't answer. The storm breathed against the walls. Somewhere under the floor, the house settled its old bones.

"Ten years," he said at last, rough as gravel. "Give or take."

"That's a lot of empty."

"Yes." He shifted. The ember-light caught the edge of his jaw, the lines around his eyes, the scar she'd touched. "Go back to sleep, Wren."

She rolled over. Pulled the blanket to her chin. Watched the embers breathe.

"It's Wren," she said, mostly to herself, testing it. "That's not really my name either. It's the bird-name. The voice gave it to me on the second day of running, said *be small and brown and everywhere and no one's*, and I was, and it worked." A pause. "But I had a name before. Before the corridor. I can almost hear it. It starts soft."

Emrys didn't ask what it was. That was the kindest thing anyone had done for her since the flour-woman, and she told him so by pretending not to notice, and he told her the same thing back by letting her pretend, and in this way the two of them built the first small room of whatever they were making, wordlessly, in the dark, like children stacking stones.

* * *

Morning came white and ringing. The storm had spent itself in the night; the world outside the window was blinding, blue-shadowed, silent, the snow lying waist-deep and glittering like a country made of salt.

Wren woke to the smell of frying pork and the sound of Emrys cursing softly at his woodpile, and both of these things felt so ordinary that for three full seconds she forgot everything — the corridor, the grey cloaks, the breathing behind the door — and simply existed as a girl waking up warm. Then memory arrived, all of it at once, and her stomach clenched, and the empty place in her chest stirred with that footprint-shape of a voice.

*Not yet,* she told it. Or the space where it lived. *Let me eat first. Questions after food. That's the order of things here.*

She smiled at her own joke, a small private smile, and went to help with breakfast, and that was when they both saw them.

Tracks.

They came up the valley from the south, following the frozen streambed — a line of dark prints pressed neat and deliberate into the fresh snow, too regular to be wolf, too many to be one man. They ended fifty yards from the cabin, in a wide churned circle, as though whoever made them had stood there a long time, looking at the little smoking chimney, before turning back the way they came.

Except — Wren squinted against the glare, her heart beginning its hard rabbit drum — except the tracks didn't turn back. They walked into the circle and simply stopped. As though the walker had stepped off the world.

Beside her, Emrys had gone utterly still, the pork forgotten, the spatula dripping fat into the fire with a hiss.

"Wren," he said, and his voice was the flat calm she already knew meant danger, "when the voice told you nothing would follow you into the valley—" He swallowed. "What exactly did it say about leaving?"
