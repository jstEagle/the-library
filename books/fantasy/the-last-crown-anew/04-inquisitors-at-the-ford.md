## 4. Inquisitors at the Ford

The mule came first.

Emrys heard it before he saw it — the wet slap of hooves in the shallows of the ford, a sound that carried strange and far across the frozen water. He was splitting kindling behind the cabin when the noise reached him, and he stopped mid-swing with the axe raised, because nothing crossed the ford in winter unless it had business worth freezing for. The valley's edge kept its own counsel. Traders went around by the high road, three days east, where the passes were plowed and the inns were warm. The ford was for desperate things: wolves, smugglers, and men who did not want to be counted at any gate.

He set down the axe and walked to the front of the house, unhurried, though his heart had begun a slow, deliberate drumming that he recognized from thirty years of soldiery. The drumming said: *something is coming, and it is not good.*

The child was inside. He thought of her the way he'd think of an open wound if he heard footsteps on a battlefield — instantly, entirely, without deciding to. Three days now since the snowstorm had delivered her to his door, barefoot and unblinking and speaking a language older than the kingdom. Three days of feeding her barley porridge and watching her eat with the terrible efficiency of something that had starved a long time and did not trust the food to last. She slept curled against the hearth like a coal banked for morning. When she slept, the cabin felt less haunted. When she woke, it felt more true.

Six riders came up out of the treeline along the near bank, moving at a walk that was somehow worse than a gallop. They wore grey — not the bright livery of the crown guard but the ash-colored traveling cloaks of the Office of Inquiry, cut plain as mourning. Their horses were good ones. Their tack was oiled and silent. No jingling, no creak of leather, none of the small honest noises soldiers make. These were men trained to arrive without announcing themselves, which meant they were men who expected their arrival itself to do half the work.

Emrys counted them the way he counted everything, out of habit too deep to kill: six riders, five wearing the blank grey hoods of sworn inquisitors, one ahead on a white mare whose saddle bore no insignia at all. The rider in front sat his horse with the loose elegance of someone who had never once worried about falling off. Even across the distance Emrys could see that his cloak was better wool than the others', and that beneath it he wore no armor whatsoever.

A man who wears no armor is either a fool or so dangerous he considers armor an insult. Emrys had met perhaps four of the second kind in his life. He looked at the rider's hands — long, clean, folded loosely on the pommel — and revised his estimate upward.

They halted at the edge of the ford. The leader lifted his head, and even at forty yards Emrys felt the attention settle on him like a hand on the back of the neck.

"Goodman!" the man called, and his voice carried across the ice with unnatural clarity, the way sound behaves over frozen water. "Might we trouble you? The ford looks treacherous, and my men are strangers to your country."

*Your country.* Not *this country.* Small words, chosen carefully. Emrys set them aside to examine later.

"Ford's solid enough," he called back. "Stay to the stones."

The six crossed single file, hooves finding the flat rocks with a precision that told Emrys these men had ridden together a long time. Water sheeted thin as glass around each step. When they reached the near bank and reformed, the leader dismounted and left his mare standing without tying her, which was either arrogance or a very quiet command she would not move from.

Up close, the man was younger than his bearing suggested — forty, perhaps, with a face built for courtesy. Soft jaw going firm at the corners. Eyes a pale hazel, almost colorless, the eyes of riverwater over sand. He smiled the way a door opens onto warmth, and Emrys understood immediately that everything past the threshold of that smile would be cold.

"Serel Vane," the man said, extending a gloved hand. "First Inquisitor to His Majesty's Office of Inquiry. Forgive the intrusion, Goodman...?"

Emrys took the hand. The glove was kid leather, and beneath it the grip was dry and exact. "Fairweather," he said. "Emrys Fairweather."

"Master Fairweather." Vane's smile widened by a degree, no more. "You have the look of a soldier."

"You have the look of a tax collector," Emrys said. "We're both wrong about each other, I expect."

Something flickered behind the pale eyes — appreciation, maybe, or appetite. "Ha. Yes. I imagine we are." Vane turned slightly, presenting the landscape as though he owned it and was being gracious about sharing. "We're searching for a runaway. A child. Eight or nine years old. Dark-haired." He produced a square of paper from his cloak, and Emrys made himself look at it.

It was a drawing of Wren. Not a likeness — a portrait. Every line of her face rendered with a care that had nothing to do with law and everything to do with obsession. Whoever had drawn it had studied her for hours. Her eyes in the picture were wide and dark and afraid, and the artist had gotten the fear exactly right, which meant the artist had seen it up close, had perhaps been the cause of it.

"She is believed to be ill," Vane said. "Fevered. Confused. She wandered from her minders during transit through the northern provinces, poor thing, barefoot in the snow. We've followed her trail to the valley's edge." He paused, delicately. "Which is odd, Master Fairweather, as I'm sure you know. Nothing crosses into the Godless Valley that can be spared from it."

"The valley takes all kinds," Emrys said. "Hermits. Debtors. Fools who hate the cold road east."

"And children?"

"Children mostly stay where they're fed."

"Mm." Vane folded the portrait away with two fingers, gently, as though putting something back to sleep. "May we impose on your hospitality? My men have ridden since before dawn, and there's a storm building in the west — you'll feel it in your knees if nowhere else. A fire, an hour's rest, and we'll trouble you no further."

There it was. Emrys had known it was coming since the mule-slap of hooves on ice; hospitality was the oldest key in the world and it fit every lock. Refuse a crown inquisitor shelter in winter and you might as well paint *GUILTY* on your roof in pitch. Admit them and you brought six wolves into the house where the fawn slept ten feet from the hearth.

His oath-scar itched. It had been itching for three days, ever since the girl's fingers touched it and burned, ever since he heard — for one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat — a voice like a mountain remembering it could speak. The scar ran from his collarbone down under his shirt, a seam of old silvered tissue, and it itched now the way it always itched when something was about to go badly wrong. Ten years of silence, and in three days the scar had woken like a dog hearing footsteps it knew.

"I've little to offer," he said. "Barley, black bread, watered ale."

"That sounds like a feast," Vane said warmly, and gestured his men forward, and just like that Emrys was walking six inquisitors toward his own front door, composing in his head the layout of the cabin and every object in it that could hang him.

* * *

The girl was gone.

He saw it in the first second through the door — the hearth swept, the blanket folded into a shape too neat for a child, the porridge bowl washed and turned mouth-down on the shelf, the small boots by the door removed and hidden. Wren had heard the riders before he had. Children who run from something learn to listen the way rabbits learn, in the marrow. She had cleaned every trace of herself in the time it took the inquisitors to cross the ford, and now, presumably, she was somewhere in the treeline behind the cabin, barefoot or booted, he didn't know, watching through the pines.

Relief hit him so hard he nearly swayed. Then shame arrived right behind it, because relief meant he'd already decided. Sometime in the last three days — feeding her, listening to her strange syllables that weren't nonsense, watching her sleep — he had decided. He hadn't signed anything. He hadn't spoken any words. But a choice had been made in him the way frost forms in a wall, quietly, everywhere at once, until the stone cracks.

Vane stood in the middle of the room, turning slowly, reading it the way he read everything. Emrys watched him read: the single chair, the single mug, the bed built for one broad man. A hermit's house. A hermit's life, ten years in the making.

"Spartan," Vane observed.

"Cheaper than a wife."

One of the hooded inquisitors laughed — a single bark, quickly strangled. Vane did not laugh. He drifted toward the hearth and held his palms to the coals, and Emrys noticed what he should have noticed at the ford: the other five had spread out without instruction. One by the window. One by the back door. Two flanking the room's center like bookends. Not searching. *Positioning.* This was choreography, and Vane was the choreographer, and the dance had been planned before they ever crossed the ice.

"You live alone, Master Fairweather?" Vane asked the fire.

"Aye."

"No family? No household?"

"My household died in the Godswar. Most households did."

"True enough." Vane turned. The firelight moved on his colorless eyes and made them briefly golden, briefly almost kind. "Then you'll forgive a stranger's curiosity, but I must ask. Have you seen anyone pass this way? Anyone at all. Travelers, pilgrims—" the pause was surgical, "—a child?"

Here it was, then. The moment. Emrys had spent ten years keeping faith with silence — his god's silence, and his own. He had not lied since the war. Not to magistrates, not to priests, not to himself. Truth had become the last discipline left to a man whose god no longer answered, the final coin in a purse otherwise empty, and he had guarded it the way misers guard gold: not because it bought much anymore, but because it was *his.*

And now he opened his mouth and heard himself say:

"There was a peddler, day before yesterday. Went north along the ridge trail with a heavy pack. And before that, nothing since the thaw-melt froze. Nobody comes here, First Inquisitor. That's rather the point of here."

The lie came out smooth as riverstone. Smooth as if it had been waiting in him all along, worn round by ten years of current, needing only to be picked up. Emrys felt the scar flare hot against his collarbone — not pain, exactly. More like attention. Like something old and buried turning over in its sleep to see what he'd done.

Vane regarded him for a long moment. The courtesy did not leave his face; that was the horror of it. Courtesy was simply the shape his face made when it was working.

"North along the ridge," Vane repeated. "In this cold."

"He had a big coat."

"A big coat." Vane smiled again, and this time the smile reached his eyes, and Emrys wished devoutly that it hadn't, because the eyes were patient the way a fisherman is patient, the way a trap is patient. "You know, Master Fairweather, I've interviewed four hundred souls on this road. Farmers, ferrymen, priests. Do you know what they all have in common?"

"Lying to me," Emrys guessed.

"They all tell the truth eventually." Vane let that sit in the warm air of the little room. "Not because I threaten them. Because the truth wants out. It's the most restless thing in creation. Lies need tending — feeding, propping up, remembered details kept alive like houseplants. The truth just sits there, breathing on its own, waiting for you to get tired." He clasped his hands behind his back. "I'm very patient, Master Fairweather. It's the whole of my talent, really. Everything else is decoration."

"Must make dinner parties exhausting."

This time Vane did laugh, a real one, brief and startlingly human. "Oh, I like you. I shouldn't — it complicates things — but I do." He nodded toward the table. "Now. About that ale."

They drank. Vane talked — easily, pleasantly — of roads and weather and the price of iron, and all the while his men moved through the cabin's small space with the silent thoroughness of beetles under bark. Emrys watched the one by the window tilt his head, once, toward the treeline behind the house, and Vane's gaze followed the gesture without turning his face toward it at all. Some communication passed between them that needed no words, and Emrys understood that the net was already cast. The question of the next hour was not whether they would find her trail. It was how long the finding would take, and what would be standing between them and her when it happened.

He was an old mercenary. He knew arithmetic like this. Six against one was not arithmetic; it was weather.

But the scar was burning now, steadily, and underneath the burn he could almost feel it again — that vast attention, that mountain-voice, silent for ten years and yet somehow, impossibly, *present*, the way a dead friend is present in the habits they left behind. And threaded through the burning was the memory of the girl's voice saying, in the First Tongue, words he had not understood and had understood completely: *they put it to sleep, and I am where it dreams.*

She was eight years old. She had crossed a kingdom barefoot. Whatever hunted her had drawn her portrait from memory, lovingly, like a lover or a torturer, and sent six grey riders into winter after her.

The law was coming across the ice, courteous and patient and certain of its righteousness. Emrys looked at Serel Vane warming his elegant hands at the fire and understood, with the calm of a man watching the last plank fall away behind him, that the law was not justice. It was only the thing that wore justice, the way something else, far away in a capital city, wore a king.

He'd broken one oath already in his life, and it had cost the world its gods. Perhaps breaking another, smaller, truer thing would balance the ledger. Perhaps nothing would. But the girl was in the trees, and the wolves were in his house, and Emrys Fairweather found that his body had already voted, his weight shifting imperceptibly to keep himself between Vane and the back door.

*Already chosen,* he thought. *God help me. Already chosen.*

* * *

An hour later, Vane rose, thanked him with genuine-seeming warmth, and pressed a coin into Emrys's palm for the ale — a full silver, twice the ale's worth, done deliberately, so that Emrys would remember taking it. "If the peddler returns," Vane said at the door, settling his fine cloak, "or anyone else — a child, a woman, anyone unusual — send word to the garrison at Brackenfell. There's a reward. There's always a reward." He paused on the threshold, and the wind came in around him smelling of snow. "You're a truthful man, Master Fairweather. I can always tell. It's in the shoulders."

Then they were remounting, wheeling, crossing the ford in that same silent precision — but not heading back the way they came. They turned south, toward the treeline, spreading out along the bank in a line Emrys recognized with a cold drop in his gut. A sweep formation. Driving the woods toward the water, where tracks would show in the mud and snow like writing on a page.

He waited until the pines swallowed them. Then he moved.

Behind the cabin, under the floorstone where he kept nothing worth finding, he pulled out the oilcloth bundle he'd sworn never to open again: sword, mail shirt gone stiff with disuse, a knife, a bow, twenty arrows fletched in a pattern no army used anymore. His hands shook, and he let them. Shaking was honest.

He found Wren a quarter mile into the pines, wedged in the hollow of a lightning-split fir, watching the smoke of the cabin with eyes that had seen too much. Beside her stood Tam Ostler's mule — old Bramble, gray-muzzled, patient, saddled with the farm sacks Tam would grumble about for a month. The girl had stolen a mule. Or persuaded one. With her, Emrys suspected, the distinction was academic.

"You lied to the wolf-man," she said. Her common tongue was getting better daily, though the accent of the First Tongue still curved every word like a bow. "I heard it. From the trees." She looked at him with that unnerving directness. "Lying burns you. Your chest. Why does lying burn you?"

Because his whole body was a signed document, sealed in blood and divine assent, and every falsehood scrawled across it stung like salt. Because the oath had been the last true thing he owned and he had just spent it.

"Never mind why," he said, swinging up the pack. "Listen. South road, hard ride, two days to Brackenfell. Find the cooper's yard by the tannery — ask for a woman named Ilsa Vey. Tell her Emrys sent you, tell her the word is 'Kestrel's Ford.' She'll grumble. Let her. She'll keep you safe."

Wren didn't move. "You're not coming."

"I'm coming behind you. Slowly. Somebody has to stay here and be found innocent." He knelt, gripped her thin shoulders, and made himself meet those dark old eyes. "Wren. Listen to me. If I'm not in Brackenfell by the third dusk, you take Ilsa and you keep running east and you don't stop for anything that calls itself holy. Promise me."

"You promised something once," she said softly. "To a voice that doesn't answer anymore. Did you keep that promise?"

The question went through him like a spear finding the gap in armor. He had no answer, and she seemed to know it, and after a moment she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against his — a gesture from some rite older than kingdoms, a blessing or a farewell or both.

"Third dusk," she said. Then she clicked her tongue, and Bramble turned south through the pines, and the girl who dreamed for a sleeping god rode away into the falling snow.

Emrys watched until the trees took her. Then he walked back to his cabin, banked the fire so it would look lived-in, took down the axe from beside the door, and sat facing the ford with the blade across his knees.

Out on the ice, faint and getting fainter, he could hear the inquisitors beating the southern woods. Coming closer. Patient as traps.

He began, quietly, to count.
