## 5. What the Oath Cost

Emrys did not sleep that night.

He sat in the chair by the hearth with his boots off and his sword across his knees — an old habit, the kind of habit that outlives its reasons — and watched the girl sleep in his bed. Wren had curled into the smallest possible shape, a knot of bones and dark hair, one hand tucked beneath her cheek as though even in dreams she was holding something back. She had not spoken since the touch. Neither had he.

The scar on his forearm had stopped burning an hour ago. Now it only itched, the way it always itched, the way it had itched for ten years — a low, maddening whisper of skin that no salve or scratching ever satisfied. He had learned to ignore it the way a man learns to ignore a bad tooth or a bad marriage: by pretending it was simply part of him.

But tonight he could not pretend. Tonight the itch felt like a question.

He pulled back his sleeve and looked at it by firelight. The oath-scar ran from the crook of his elbow to his wrist, a seam of pale tissue too smooth to be natural, too deliberate to be accidental. If you turned it in the right light you could almost make out letters in it — not words, exactly, but the *shape* of words, the ghost of a sentence written in a language that predated ink. The First Tongue. The language the girl had spoken at his door as if it were her mother's milk.

*You are the receipt,* his old company commander used to say, tapping her own matching scar. *Every oath leaves a mark on the one who swears it. That's how you know it's real.*

Maren. Gods below, he hadn't let himself think that name in years.

The fire popped. Wren stirred but did not wake. And Emrys Fairweather, who had spent a decade telling himself a story about silence and abandonment, finally let himself remember how the story actually went.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with weather.

The year the sky burned, Emrys was twenty-three and already three years sworn to Vaelis, the Quiet-Handed, god of thresholds and small mercies. It was not a glamorous patronage. Vaelis kept no thunderbolts, demanded no pyres. His were the blessings of doorways held open, of wounds closed before fever set in, of the exact right word spoken at the exact wrong moment. When Emrys fought with Vaelis's blessing on him, he did not become a storm. He became a hinge — everything turning around him, nothing breaking that could be saved.

His company — the Ninth Lance of Aldermarch, forty-two soldiers and a chaplain who drank — had been mustered to the Godswar like everyone else. Not because they were holy. Because the war left no one uninvited.

People who had not lived through it thought the Godswar was gods fighting each other. It wasn't. It was worse. It was gods *dying*, and their deaths pouring out into the world like arterial blood. Every divine death was a flood of raw power with nowhere to go — power that soaked into the land and the people and the animals and made them burn. Whole provinces caught fire without flame. Rivers boiled in their beds. In the east, they said, a village of six hundred woke one morning all speaking in unison, all speaking with the voice of a drowned god, and did not stop until they starved.

And still the gods fought on, because the thing hunting them gave them no other choice.

They called it many names in those years. The Hollow One. The Unfed. The first oath-breaker. Whatever it had been originally — man, god, something older than either — it had learned to devour divinity, and every god it consumed made it stronger and hungrier, and every god that fell in battle against it fed it anyway. The war was a feast laid out for it by its own victims. The pantheon was losing, and losing meant the world lost with them.

Emrys remembered the council at Karst Hold because it was the last time he saw the sky behave normally. Forty companies. Eleven living gods, present as voices, as pressures, as light behind the eyes of chosen vessels. And at the center of it all, Maren Cole, captain of the Ninth Lance, standing on a table because she was five foot two and wanted to be heard.

She'd said: *There's a way to starve it. There's a way to end this. But none of you are going to like it.*

* * *

The Binding Oath was not invented at Karst Hold. It was found — dug out of the foundations of the oldest temple anyone knew, inscribed on stone so ancient the scholars argued over whether men had written it or whether it had simply always been there, waiting. A tourniquet, the archivists called it. A litany designed to cinch shut the wound between the divine and the mortal world. Not to kill the gods. Not to banish them. To *silence* them — to close the channels through which their power bled into creation, so that when they died, their deaths would pour out into a locked room instead of onto the world.

A tourniquet. Temporary. Meant to be loosed when the bleeding stopped.

The catch was simple and terrible: the Binding required mortals to swear it. Gods could not bind themselves; the lock needed a key turned from the outside. It required living throats, willing ones, sworn in full knowledge of what they gave up. And what they gave up was everything — every blessing, every miracle, every whispered comfort. A world where prayer hit a ceiling. A world where magic guttered and died.

*A world where my god cannot speak to me,* Emrys had thought, standing in the rain at Karst Hold, listening to Maren lay it out. *That's the price. That's the whole price. Just that.*

Just that.

Forty-two soldiers of the Ninth Lance swore. So did thirty-one other companies — near two thousand throats in all, gathered in the ruined temple below Karst Hold, kneeling on wet stone while eleven gods stood witness in whatever manner gods stand. Emrys remembered the cold. He remembered Maren's hand gripping the back of his neck like a father's. He remembered that he was not afraid, which later seemed to him the most obscene detail of all.

And he remembered Vaelis.

The Quiet-Handed had never been loud, but that night he filled Emrys's skull like water fills a cup — gently, completely. Not words. Vaelis rarely bothered with words. What came through was a feeling, vast and patient and unbearably sad, and beneath the sadness something Emrys took years to name correctly:

*Consent.*

The god was not being bound. The god was *choosing* it. Choosing silence, choosing exile from his own faithful, choosing the locked room — because the alternative was the Hollow One drinking the world dry through the holes the dying gods left behind. Vaelis consented the way a surgeon consents to amputation. The way a mother consents to drown rather than let the wolves have her screaming.

Emrys had felt all of that in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and then he spoke the litany with two thousand others, and the scar seared itself down his arm like a brand of white iron, and the world went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. There is a difference, and he had ten years to learn it. Silence is absence. Quiet is presence, held still. Even bound, even voiceless, Vaelis was *there* — Emrys could feel him the way you feel someone sleeping in the next room. For the first months after the Binding, that was enough. That was more than enough. The war ended. The fires went out. The Hollow One, cut off from the flood of dying divinity, starved and dwindled and was forgotten by everyone who had not stood at Karst Hold.

A tourniquet. Temporary. Loosed when the bleeding stops.

Except the bleeding never quite stopped, and no one ever came to loose it.

* * *

The fire had burned down to coals. Emrys realized his jaw ached and unlocked it.

Here was the story he had told himself for ten years, the story he told bounty marks and barkeeps and the occasional priest drunk enough to ask about the scar: *My god went silent. I don't know why. I waited. He never came back.* A story with him at the center of it, wronged and bewildered, a faithful man abandoned by heaven.

And here was the truth, which he had known all along and carried like a swallowed stone: *I helped bind him. I spoke the words. I felt his consent and I spoke the words anyway, and then, when theBinding should have ended, I walked away to the one place in the world where I would never have to think about it again.*

The Godless Valley. Clever name, that. Comforting name. As if the valley had simply been passed over, a blank spot on the map where miracles forgot to happen. The truth was uglier: the valley sat atop one of the Binding's anchor-stones. It was quiet there because the quiet had been nailed down there, hammered in deep by two thousand oaths, and Emrys had moved to the exact center of his own crime the way a guilty dog returns to the scene of the mess.

He hadn't been abandoned. He had been complicit. Worse — he had been *comfortable*. Ten years of taking mercenary work in a place where his blessing couldn't reach and his conscience didn't have to follow. Ten years of telling himself the silence was heaven's failure rather than his own.

Because here was the part that curdled, the part he had never once said aloud: the Binding was supposed to be temporary. Everyone at Karst Hold knew it. The litany itself said so — *until the wound is closed, until the hunger passes, until the gods consent to wake* — and the gods had consented, all eleven, their assent woven into the oath like thread through cloth. Which meant the Binding could not simply *persist*. Someone had to be holding it. Feeding it. Turning the key from the outside, decade after decade, keeping the tourniquet cinched long past any surgical purpose.

Two thousand swore the oath. Most were dead now — the war's aftermath, ordinary wars, ordinary years. The oath did not die with them; it pooled in the survivors, thinned but enduring. Emrys had assumed the Binding simply endured on its own, a machine left running.

But machines left running need fuel. And the girl asleep in his bed had crossed half a kingdom to reach him, guided by a voice that went quiet at the valley's edge, fleeing something in the capital called *the Wearing*. A king who had not slept in three years. Inquisitors hunting a heretical vessel with the polite efficiency of men following orders from very high up.

Emrys looked at the coals and felt the shape of a thought assemble itself, slow and horrible, the way ice forms on still water.

If someone was maintaining the Binding — if someone had taken the tourniquet meant to save the world and turned it into a leash — then the silence wasn't a scar across the world. It was a theft. Everything the gods had poured out when they died, everything the Binding had locked away for safekeeping, was still in the locked room. And whoever held the key had been drinking from it for ten years.

The Hollow One had starved. But starvation teaches patience.

Wren made a small sound in her sleep and turned her face toward the fire. In the ember-light, Emrys saw that her hands, folded under her cheek, bore faint marks — old ones, healed over, arranged in patterns too regular for accident. Litany-marks. The same script that ghosted beneath his own scar, written small on a child's skin.

Someone had been preparing her. Someone had been writing the counter-litany into her the way you'd write a letter you couldn't risk sending any other way.

The itching in his arm flared once, sharply, and faded — and this time, in the fading, Emrys could have sworn he felt something on the far side of it. Not a voice. Not yet. More like a hand pressed flat against a door from the other side, waiting to see if he would finally turn the latch.

"Gods below," he murmured to the empty room, and then corrected himself, because that was the whole problem, wasn't it. "Vaelis. What did we do?"

The coals settled. The girl slept. And at the valley's edge, far off through the snow, Emrys heard — or imagined he heard, though he no longer trusted the difference — the first bell of a rider coming up the frozen road.

Inquisitors traveled with bells. He'd forgotten that. He'd tried to forget a great many things.

He rose, quietly, and began to dress.
