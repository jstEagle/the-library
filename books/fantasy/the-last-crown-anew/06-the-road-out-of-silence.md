## 6. The Road Out of Silence

Wren had never seen the edge of the world before. She had not known it would look like a line of birch trees.

That was what the voice had called it, back when the voice still spoke to her — *the edge* — and she had imagined something grander than this: a pale stand of trees at the bottom of a hill, their trunks white as candle-wax, their leaves gone gold and shivering in the wind that came down off the valley's high shoulders. No wall of fire. No gate of stone. Just trees, and beyond them a country she did not know, and behind her the whole gray length of the Godless Valley where nothing had followed her for eleven days because nothing could.

She stood at Emrys's shoulder with the mule's lead rope wrapped twice around her fist and looked at the birches and felt her heart go strange in her chest, like a bird that had forgotten which way was up.

"You'll want to breathe," Emrys said. He said very little else these days. Since the night the inquisitors came to the ford and he lied to them — she had felt the lie leave him like a tooth pulled, she had heard it in the way his voice went flat — he had been quieter even than before, and he was already the quietest man she had ever met. "Once we cross, everything you left behind catches up. The magic. The things that hunt you. All of it, all at once. So breathe first."

"Is it going to hurt?" Wren asked.

He considered the question honestly, which she liked about him. Most grown people answered children's questions with other questions, or with smiles that meant *no*, or with lies dressed up as kindness. Emrys just looked at her with his scarred face and his gray eyes and thought about it before he spoke.

"I don't know," he said. "You're the only one who'd know. Has anything like you crossed before?"

"Not that I remember."

"That's two of us who don't know, then." He adjusted the pack on his shoulder — the pack with the bread and the dried apples and the little iron knife he'd given her, hilt wrapped in leather so it wouldn't bite her palm. "We cross together. If it goes badly, I carry you back over the line and we think again. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

But it was a lie, hers this time, and she wondered if he felt it the way she had felt his. If it went badly for him, she would not let him carry her back. She had come too far to be carried backward by anyone.

The voice had told her once, in the early days when it still had strength enough to be more than a warmth behind her ribs: *I gave you what I could carry out of the burning, little wren. It is heavy. It will always be heavy. But heaviness is only weight, and weight is only love pressed small.* She hadn't understood then. She was beginning to.

They walked down through the birches together, and the mule — whose name was Buttercup, though Emrys refused to acknowledge this — followed with the patient resentment of her kind.

The moment Wren's bare foot touched the shadow the last birch threw across the road, the world *opened*.

There was no better word for it. The valley had been like living inside a held breath, inside a cupped hand, inside the silence after a bell is struck and before the sound begins. She hadn't known how loud the silence was until it ended. Color poured into the grass. The wind arrived carrying ten thousand small voices — the trees talking to each other in creaks and leaf-whispers, the stones remembering the heat of the summer sun, the road itself humming faintly with every foot that had ever walked it. And beneath all of it, vast and slow and deep as a sea under sea-ice, the presence she had carried since the night she fled the capital rose up in her chest and *stretched*.

It didn't speak. Not yet. It was too busy being everywhere, the way a man shut in a cellar for years doesn't say anything when the door opens; he just stands there filling his lungs.

Wren stopped walking. Her eyes were full of tears she hadn't decided to cry. "Oh," she said. "Oh, it's *loud*."

Emrys had gone rigid beside her. His hand was pressed flat against his own chest, over the scar she knew lived there — the oath-scar, the receipt, though she didn't yet know what it was a receipt *for* — and his face was doing something complicated, grief and hunger fighting over the same ground. For one moment she thought he might fall to his knees on the road like a man struck by lightning that loved him.

Then the moment passed, and the presence settled back into her bones, and the world was simply the world again — except bigger, except awake, except *full*.

"It's been quiet in there," Wren said, touching her own sternum. "In the valley. Like sleeping in a room with no windows."

"I know." His voice was rough. "I built part of that room."

She didn't understand that yet either. There were so many things about Emrys Fairweather she didn't understand, and he kept them the way he kept everything, close and silent and worn smooth from handling.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen oak, and Wren learned what the crossing had cost the world around her, because the world came looking.

Not the inquisitors — not yet. Something smaller. They heard the cart before they saw it, a wet squeal of an ungreased axle coming up the forest track, and then a light bobbing between the trees, and then a voice calling out, thin and cracked: "Hello? Hello, the fire? Please — please, I can see your fire—"

Emrys was on his feet with his hand on his knife before the words finished. Ten years in the valley had taught him that travelers at night were either desperate or bait. But the voice that came stumbling into their little ring of firelight belonged to neither. It belonged to a woman, sixty if she was a day, leading a swaybacked horse that dragged a cart, and in the cart —

"In the cart," the woman gasped, "my husband, please, he took sick three days past, fever, and I thought — they say there's a healer over the mountains but that's four days and he hasn't got four hours—" She saw Emrys's face, the scars, the soldier's stance, and her hope visibly curdled into fear. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry to trouble soldiers. I'll go—"

"We're not soldiers," Emrys said. "Not anymore." But he looked at the cart, and Wren watched him look, and she knew what he saw: a man lying among sacks, gray-lipped, breath rattling, sweat freezing on his brow in the autumn cold. A man with hours.

"There's nothing I can do," Emrys said quietly. "I'm no healer. The nearest one is four days—"

And Wren was already climbing into the cart.

She didn't decide to. That was the terrible, wonderful truth of it, the thing she would turn over in her mind for years afterward: she didn't decide, any more than water decides to run downhill. The presence in her chest leaned toward the dying man the way a plant leans toward light, and her body simply followed.

The man's face was a ruin of fever. His eyes rolled under closed lids. Up close she could hear the fight going on inside him, the small stubborn animal of his life losing ground to the dark, and the presence rose in her throat like a song she'd always known and never sung.

"Wren." Emrys's hand closed on her shoulder. "Wren, stop."

"He's dying," she said. Her own voice sounded far away to her, layered, as if someone much older were speaking underneath it. "I can feel it. I can *fix* it."

"You don't know what it costs. You crossed the line today. Whatever's in you is awake and hungry and it doesn't know its own strength—"

"*Please*," the wife said, and fell to her knees in the leaves, and Wren looked at Emrys, and Emrys looked at Wren, and something passed between them that neither of them had words for yet.

"If it hurts you," he said finally, "you stop. You promise me. You stop and you let go, whatever happens to him."

"I promise."

It was the second lie she told that day. She was getting good at them. She hated that.

She put both hands on the farmer's burning forehead, and she let go of the thing she'd been holding shut since the valley's edge, and the power came through her like a river through a broken dam.

It didn't feel like power. That was the strangest part. It felt like *remembering*. She remembered the man whole: remembered the shape of his lungs before the sickness filled them, remembered the rhythm his heart was meant to keep, remembered the name his mother had called him across a kitchen forty years ago — *Tam, Tam, supper's on* — and she held all that remembering against the ruin of him the way you'd hold a pattern against torn cloth, and she *sewed*.

The fever broke like a wave hitting rocks. The rattle in his chest smoothed into breath. Color climbed back up his neck and into his face, and his eyes opened, clear and brown and astonished, and he said, in a voice gone rusty, "Marta? Love, why are we stopped in the—"

And Wren took her hands away and looked down at them.

They were old hands.

Not tired-old, not chapped-from-cold old. *Old*. The skin loose and papery over tendons standing up like cords, liver-spotted, the knuckles swollen, a fine tremor running through the fingers that she could not make stop no matter how she commanded them. She turned them over. Veins stood out on the backs like blue rivers on a map of somewhere far away. These were the hands of a grandmother. These were the hands of a woman who had lived seventy years and buried a husband and knitted a hundred winters away, and they were attached to her eight-year-old arms, and they were shaking, and they did not stop shaking.

"Oh," Wren said. Very quietly. Looking at them.

Then the bill came due in earnest, and it took her knees.

The world tilted. Firelight smeared sideways. Somewhere above her Emrys was shouting her name and the farmer's wife was shouting her husband's and the sounds tangled together into one long frightened rope. Wren felt herself lifted — Emrys's arms, careful as a man handling a bird with a broken wing — and laid down by the fire, and her old, old hands were gathered into his scarred ones, and he was saying her name over and over like a word in the First Tongue, like a prayer, like a curse.

"I'm all right," she whispered. Her mouth felt full of ash and marigolds. "I fixed him. Look. I fixed him."

"You *fool*," Emrys said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle, and she understood, lying there with the sky wheeling gently overhead, that he wasn't angry at her. He was angry at the arithmetic. At a world where saving a man cost a child thirty years. "You promised me you'd stop."

"It didn't hurt until it was done." Which was true, and also the most dangerous kind of true, the kind that makes bargains feel free right up until the collector arrives. "Emrys. Emrys, my hands."

"I see your hands." His thumb moved over her knuckles, gentle, and she watched his face as he catalogued what he saw there — the spots, the swelling, the age — and she watched the horror arrive in him and settle in like a tenant. "How much?" he asked. "Wren. How much did that take?"

She tried to count. The presence stirred sleepily in her chest, sated, warm, utterly without remorse, and offered her a number the way a merchant offers change: here, this, thirty-odd years, spent well, no refunds.

"I don't know," she lied, the third lie of the day, because some mercies are also lies. "Some."

* * *

The farmer's wife — Marta — wouldn't leave. She fed the fire, and put blankets over Wren, and pressed a heel of cheese and a jar of honey into Emrys's hands while tears ran openly down her weathered face, and when Wren woke near dawn, stiff and strange in her borrowed body, Marta was sitting watch beside her like a hen over one chick.

"You saved his life," Marta said, when she saw Wren's eyes open. "Whatever it cost you — and I saw, girl, I saw your hands — whatever it cost, I'll pray for you every day of mine to pay it back. Both of us will."

"Prayers are worth more now than they used to be," Wren said, and didn't know where the words came from, and Marta laughed and cried at once.

They parted on the road at first light, the cart rolling east toward whatever village the couple had been fleeing toward, Tam upright on the bench and breathing easy and calling thanks back over his shoulder until the trees swallowed the sound. Wren sat wrapped in blankets in front of the dead fire and flexed her fingers. The tremor was still there. Fainter, but there, a resident now rather than a visitor.

Emrys crouched across from her. He had barely slept. In the gray light his face looked carved out of the same material as the valley's cliffs, and just as hard to read.

"How many times?" he asked.

"What?"

"How many times can you do that. Before there's nothing left of you to spend." His voice was level, but she was learning to read the level places, the way you learn to read a river by its calm stretches. Underneath, current. "And don't tell me you don't know. You knew last night. I watched you know."

Wren looked at her hands. Old hands. Hands that had done a grandmother's lifetime of work in the space of a heartbeat, and would do more, whenever the need came, because that was what she was for — the vessel, the last vessel, the little clay cup that something enormous had poured itself into when the world was burning and the gods were being silenced one by one.

"One more," she said. "Maybe two, if they're small." The number rose in her, true and cold. "After that, the cup's empty. Or the cup breaks. I'm not sure which."

Emrys closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had changed in them — something had hardened and softened at the same time, the way steel does, the way a decision does.

"Then we don't spend another drop unless there's no other way," he said. "Not for me. Not for strangers. Not for anything short of a life that cannot otherwise be saved. Do you hear me, Wren? We get you to the capital with everything you have left intact. Every year of you. Every hour."

"And then what?" she asked. Because she had carried the answer since the night she fled the Wearing, since the voice went quiet in her chest as she crossed into the valley, and she needed to hear him say he understood. "You know what I'm for, Emrys. You've known since the door. What do you think it costs to unmake what was made in the capital? To pull the crown off the thing that wears the king?"

The wind moved through the birches behind them, and far away — too far to be real, surely, surely only the wind — Wren heard bells. Ash bells, the voice named them, from a city she had never seen and was walking toward. Ringing for a king who never rested.

Emrys looked east down the long road, and his jaw worked once, twice, and when he answered, his voice was so quiet she had to lean forward to catch it.

"I think," he said, "it costs exactly what it cost last night. And I think we're going to spend you anyway, because the alternative is letting the world stay on its knees. And I think that before we get there, you and I are going to find a third option if I have to dig it out of the earth with my bare hands." He stood, and shouldered the pack, and held his hand down to her — scarred palm, steady grip. "Come on, little wren. Brackenfell by dark. And Wren —"

"Yes?"

"Next time you lie to me, I'll know. I always know. It's the one blessing the silence left me."

She took his hand and grinned up at him with her old woman's fingers curled around his, and behind them, far to the west, at the edge of the valley they had left forever, something that had waited eleven days for magic to return to the world finally caught their scent — and began to follow.
