## 7. The Hollow King's Men

Brackenfell smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and, beneath it all, the faint copper tang of fear.

Emrys had known the town in better years — a sprawl of timber-framed market stalls along the Alder Road, where farmers from three valleys traded grain for salt and the alewives sang louder than the church bells. He'd spent coin here after campaigns, drunk bad wine with good men, and once, memorably, been thrown out of the Drowned Hart for arm-wrestling a tanner twice his size. That had been eleven years ago. Before the silence. Before everything went quiet and stayed that way.

Now he walked its streets with a child's hand tucked inside his own and a hood pulled low over his face, and every bell tower he passed made his teeth ache.

Wren had not spoken since they crossed the boundary out of the valley two days ago. She walked when he walked, stopped when he stopped, ate what he put in front of her without comment or complaint. The voice that had guided her across half of Aldermarch had gone mute at the valley's edge, and whatever it had left behind in her was a girl of eight or nine with old woman's hands and eyes that catalogued exits.

"Market first," Emrys murmured. "Food, then information. In that order."

She didn't answer. She never did, anymore, unless the question was simple enough for her limited Common — *hungry*, *cold*, *hurt* — or important enough to crack through whatever wall she'd built between herself and the world. He missed the First Tongue, absurdly. Missed hearing words pour out of her like water over stones, even if he hadn't understood them. Silence, he was learning, was a language too, and it said things he didn't want to hear.

The market square opened before them like a wound.

It wasn't empty — that would have been simpler. It was full, but wrong-full, the way a room is wrong-full when everyone in it has agreed not to look at one another. Farmers sold their goods in low voices. Buyers counted coins without haggling. Two town watchmen stood at opposite corners of the square with the rigid stillness of men who had been told to stand there and were afraid to sit down.

And on every post, every shutter, every stretch of available wall, the posters hung.

They were printed on good paper — crown paper, heavy stock, expensive — which told Emrys more than the words did. Someone in the capital wanted this found badly. The ink was still sharp; these were fresh, days old at most, pasted up in layers so thick some posts wore coats of them like bark.

He steered Wren toward a stall selling dried apples, positioning himself so that his broad back blocked her view of the nearest board. It didn't matter. He could see it over his own shoulder anyway, burned there now behind his eyes: a woodcut rendering of a small face, wide-eyed, dark-haired, unmistakable despite the crude artistry. Beneath it, in letters tall enough to read from horseback:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN — THE VESSEL IS TO BE SURRENDERED UNHARMED. REWARD: TWO HUNDRED MARKS AND THE KING'S GRATITUDE. ANY WHO CONCEAL OR AID HER COMMIT TREASON AGAINST THE REALM AND THE REST OF THE KING.**

*The rest of the king.* Emrys turned the phrase over as he paid for a sack of apples and a heel of hard cheese. It was an odd construction. Official language, but odd official language — the kind of phrasing that crept into edicts when the man signing them no longer entirely understood what the words meant.

"The king's rest," a woman near the fruit stall was saying to her neighbor, voice pitched under the general murmur like something smuggled. "My cousin came through from Aldermarch City last month. Says the bells ring every hour now. Every hour, day and night. *For the king's rest.*"

"He sleeps, then?"

"That's just it." The woman leaned closer. Her hands kept sorting apples through the whole conversation, automatic, a body doing work while the mind did something else. "He doesn't sleep. Nobody's seen him sleep in three years. The bells ring *for* a rest that never comes, and the edicts keep coming down from the palace, and each one's stranger than the last. Last month it was a tax on dreams — I swear on my mother, a *tax*, every household pays a copper per sleeper per night, and if you can't pay they come and count your beds. Month before that, he ordered every well in the city drained and refilled with water carried up from the river by hand, because—" she dropped her voice further, and Emrys had to slow his browsing to catch it— "because water that sits gathers memories, and memories feed things."

Her neighbor laughed, but it was the thin, defensive laugh of someone who wanted very much to be reassured and suspected they wouldn't be. "That's madness. That's a mad king talking."

"Or a king who knows something we don't," the apple-seller said. "My cousin says the palace priests have all been dismissed. Replaced with laymen nobody's ever heard of. And the Ash Bell ceremony's coming — you know that, the anniversary of the Silence. First time His Majesty's presided in person since it started. They're saying—" and here her voice went so low Emrys nearly lost it entirely— "they're saying he hasn't left the throne room in a year. Eats there. Sleeps there, if he sleeps at all. They say the chair's grown into him."

Emrys bought his apples, nodded to no one, and walked away with Wren's cold fingers wrapped around two fingers of his scarred hand, and the phrase ringing in his skull like a cracked bell.

*Grown into him.*

* * *

They took a room above a chandler's shop on the far side of town, paying double for a windowless garret and triple for the chandler's discretion. The man took the coins without counting them, which was its own kind of message: he knew what discretion cost and had priced it accordingly.

Emrys sat on the narrow bed with Wren asleep against the wall beside him — she slept curled tight as a fist, always facing the door — and forced himself to think the thoughts he'd been refusing to think since the ford.

Serel Vane. The courteous inquisitor with the gentle voice and the eyes like a ledger. He had stood in Emrys's doorway with snow melting on his immaculate shoulders and asked, politely, whether Emrys had seen a feral child, and Emrys had lied to his face for the first time in ten years. Lied easily. That was the part that still unsettled him — not the lying itself, but how little it had cost. How ready his mouth had been, after a decade of oath-true silence, to bend.

But Vane had known. Emrys was almost certain of it. There had been a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — when Vane's gaze had drifted past Emrys's shoulder toward the interior of the cabin, toward the mule track behind the woodshed, and something in his expression had shifted by a degree too small for most men to measure. Vane had let them go. Or thought he had. Or was letting them run so he could follow them somewhere useful.

Inquisitors didn't work alone. Inquisitors didn't ride out to the Godless Valley on rumor and whim. Which meant Vane answered to someone, and there was only one someone whose word required a hundred posters on good paper and bells ringing hourly for a rest that never came.

The enemy wasn't a man hunting a heretic. The enemy was the throne itself, wearing a kingdom like a coat.

Emrys looked down at Wren. In sleep her face lost its wariness and became simply what it was: a child's face, sharp-chinned, smudged with road dirt, one hand curled around the hem of his sleeve in a grip she probably didn't know she had. The white streaks at her temples caught what little light leaked under the door. Two workings, Odji had guessed. Maybe three. He had watched her pay for healing a dying farmer with years carved out of her own flesh, and the arithmetic of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Two hundred marks. Half a village could live a year on two hundred marks. Every farmer between here and the capital had her face pinned to their kitchen doors, and every one of them was honest until they were hungry, and winter was coming, and hunger made liars of saints.

He needed to know how much rope they had. And there was exactly one person in Brackenfell who might tell him the truth, assuming she didn't arrest him first.

* * *

He found the garrison house just off the drill square as the light was failing, and found Ilsa Vey exactly where he'd known she'd be: on the back step, away from the noise, cleaning a crossbow with the methodical violence of a woman who would rather be shooting it.

Ten years hadn't softened her. If anything, the army had finished the job the war started — she was broader through the shoulders now, grey coming in at the temples in two hard wings, her hands scarred in the pattern of a lifetime of mechanisms and blades. She looked up when his boots hit the bottom step, and Emrys watched recognition move across her face in stages: surprise, calculation, and then a long, weary resignation that was so familiar it hurt.

"Well," she said. "The Godless Valley finally spat you out."

"Ilsa."

"Don't 'Ilsa' me. Do you have any idea—" She set the crossbow down with exaggerated care, the way she did when she was angriest, and stood. Up close he could see new lines around her eyes, deep ones. "Do you have any idea what your face is worth right now? Because it's not just the girl. There's a bounty sheet going through the officer channels with a sketch of *you* on it. *Emrys Fairweather, former Oathsworn, considered dangerous, approach only with company.* You've got a price too, old friend. Yours is higher than hers, if you can believe that."

"They think I'm protecting her."

"They think you're the only man alive who could." She glanced past him at the empty yard, then jerked her chin toward the door. "Inside. Not the common room. My quarters. And if anyone stops us, you're a drover I owe money to, and you're boring, and you talk about drainage ditches."

Her quarters were spare as a cell: cot, chest, washstand, a single candle, and a wall-mounted rack holding her dress uniform, brushed and pressed and worn soft at the collar from being put on and taken off and put on again without ever quite being worn. Emrys looked at it and understood something about Ilsa Vey that she would have gutted him for saying aloud.

"You heard about the ford," he said.

"Everyone heard about the ford. Serel Vane rode back to the capital with an empty prisoner wagon and a smile on his face, and nobody smiles on that wagon, Emrys. Then three days later the posters go up everywhere at once, which means the order came from the top, which means—" she spread her hands— "Vane isn't running this hunt. He's reporting on it."

"To whom?"

Ilsa looked at him the way soldiers look at recruits who ask why the sky is blue. "To the king. Who else? But listen — it's not that simple, and simple is the thing that'll kill you. Vane doesn't report through the Marshal's office. Not through the Chancery. Not through any channel I can find, and I've looked, because a captain likes to know who signs her orders. His writ comes direct from the throne. Signed by the king's own hand. Only—" she paused, and for the first time since the step, hesitation crossed her face, "only the signatures have changed. Three years ago the royal seal was firm, deep-stamped, even pressure. Now it varies. Some documents it's barely there, like a dying man pressed it. Some it's stamped so hard the wax cracks through. Same seal, same hand supposedly, but—" she shook her head. "I've handled a thousand sealed orders. A hand changes when a man ages. This isn't aging. It's like two different men are taking turns."

Emrys sat down slowly on the edge of her cot. The candle flame bent in a draft and straightened.

"There's more," Ilsa said. "There's always more, lately, and none of it makes sense, and I'm telling you because you're the only man from the old company I trust further than I can throw, and also because you're a fool who's going to get himself killed and I'd rather it not be stupidly. The regiments are being rotated. Quietly. Units with long service in the capital are being posted to the coast, replaced with fresh companies from provinces that've never seen the city. The palace guard doubled in a month. And last week — last week, Emrys — we got standing orders regarding the Ash Bell ceremony. Crowd control. Standard. Except the standard order says disperse unrest. These orders say *prevent departure*. Anyone trying to leave the ceremony square during the rite is to be detained. Not moved along. Detained."

"For a religious festival?"

"For a festival where the king will appear in person for the first time in a year." Ilsa lowered herself onto her chest lid, forearms on knees, and stared at the floor. "I've served the crown twenty-two years. My father served it thirty. I have given it everything I had and most of what I didn't, and I am telling you, Emrys, as one soldier to another: something is wrong inside those walls, and the wrongness is wearing our colors."

Outside, distantly, a bell began to ring. Brackenfell had adopted the capital's custom, then — hourly, *for the king's rest*. The sound came through the shuttered window flat and joyless, a duty performed rather than a prayer offered, and Emrys watched Ilsa's jaw tighten with every stroke.

"How many men does Vane have?" he asked quietly.

"Officially, six inquisitors and a mounted escort. Unofficially?" She looked up. "Whatever the throne gives him. And here's the part you really won't like. He's already here. Rode in yesterday evening ahead of his escort — alone, plain-clothes, which Vane never does unless he's hunting something specific. My sergeant saw him in the market square this morning. Standing at the notice boards. Reading the posters. Watching who read them longer than a glance."

The bell finished its count. The silence afterward felt heavier than the ringing.

"Ilsa." Emrys chose the words the way a sapper chooses footing. "The girl. The vessel. She's not what they say she is."

"I don't care what they say she is."

"You should hear it anyway. Because if Vane catches her, it won't end with her. Whatever the crown is doing — whatever's sitting on that throne signing orders with a dying hand — it needs her, and it needs her *found*, and the reason is bigger than heresy." He held her stare. "I've heard the god's voice again, Ilsa. Through her. Once. One heartbeat. And I'm telling you the silence is ending whether the crown likes it or not, and everything Vane is doing, everything those bells are for, it's all about making sure it ends *their* way instead of any other."

For a long moment Ilsa Vey said nothing. The candle guttered; outside, the drill square grew dark; somewhere below, soldiers laughed at some joke Emrys would never hear again with the same ease.

Then she stood, crossed to the wall, and took down her dress uniform — and instead of putting it on, folded it, deliberately, into the bottom of her chest. On top of it she placed her commission papers, weighted with a whetstone, and left them there like a stone laid over a grave.

"I need to check the stable rotation," she said, in a voice that meant something else entirely. "If a cart happened to leave the north gate tomorrow morning — my cart, the one with the false bottom under the fodder, don't ask why I have a cart with a false bottom, it's a long story involving cards and a lieutenant who owed me — well. A cart can carry a lot of things. Fodder. Crossbows. A fool and a child." She turned, and her eyes were dry and hard and frightened underneath, the way brave people's eyes are frightened. "But you should know what you're asking me to become, Emrys Fairweather, before you ask it. Because the moment that cart rolls out the gate, I'm not Captain Vey anymore. I'm a deserter with a price on her head, and the state I gave my life to will be hunting me with the same paper it's using for you."

"I'm not asking," Emrys said. "I'd never ask. What you swore to the crown—"

"I swore an oath to protect the realm and its people." Something flickered across her face — the same expression he'd worn for ten years looking at his own scar, he realized. The look of a person measuring what their word actually purchased. "Twenty-two years, and I finally have to ask myself which one the crown is. Because lately it isn't both."

She blew out the candle.

"North gate," she whispered in the sudden dark. "Dawn. Don't be late, don't be followed, and for the love of every silent god — don't let the girl be seen."

* * *

Emrys moved through the darkening streets with his collar up and his heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't felt since the Godswar, that old battle-drum of purpose returning to a chest that had forgotten it.

He was three streets from the chandler's when he saw the figure.

Across the lane, half-sheltered beneath a cooper's awning, a man stood reading a poster by the light of a hanging lamp — a slight man in a plain traveler's cloak, unhurried, gloved hands clasped behind his back. He did not look up. He did not need to. Even after ten years, even in shadow, Emrys knew the particular stillness of those shoulders, the courteous tilt of that head, as though the whole street were a document he was reading with patient interest.

Serel Vane turned a page of nothing, smiled faintly at the poster bearing Wren's stolen face, and walked away down the lane toward the garrison quarter —

— toward Ilsa's door.

Emrys broke into a run.
