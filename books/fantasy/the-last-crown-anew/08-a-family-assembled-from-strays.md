## 8. A Family Assembled From Strays

Ilsa Vey had served the crown of Aldermarch for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one lesson worth carrying: orders are easy. It's the moments after the orders that cost you.

She sat in her quarters at the Brackenfell garrison with a report form spread before her on the scarred oak desk, and the quill might as well have been made of lead. *Sighting confirmed: Fairweather, Emrys, former Oathbound, traveling with an unidentified juvenile female matching circulated description.* Eleven words. Eleven words and she'd be done — promoted, probably, or at least noticed by people whose notice usually ended careers rather than beginning them.

The girl's face stared up at her from the poster pinned beneath the lamp: *Wren*, age indeterminate, last seen fleeing the capital during the Wearing purges. Reward sufficient to buy a farm. Or a coffin, depending on who collected it.

Ilsa thought about Emrys Fairweather's face when she'd cornered him behind the tannery that morning. Nineteen years since they'd marched together, and he hadn't changed much — same grey stubble, same eyes like river stones, same way of standing as if he expected the ground to betray him and had already forgiven it. But there had been something new in him too. Something she'd only ever seen in soldiers guarding things they loved.

"Tell me it isn't true," she'd said, crossbow half-raised, hating herself for the half.

And Emrys — who had once been the most honest man in three companies — had looked her dead in the eye and lied to her, badly, and then stopped lying, and said: "They want to wear her, Ilsa. The way they're wearing the king."

He'd told her everything after that. The valley. The voice in the child. The hollow king and the thing that sat inside his skin like a hermit crab in a borrowed shell. And through all of it, Wren had stood behind him in the alley shadow, small and barefoot even in the mud of early spring, watching Ilsa with eyes far too old for the face they lived in.

Now Ilsa dipped the quill in ink and wrote one word on the report: *Nothing.*

Then she crossed it out. Then she burned the whole form in the brazier and watched the ash curl, and felt lighter than she had in years, which was its own kind of obscenity.

*Desertion,* she thought. *Nineteen years, and this is how it ends.*

She packed her cart before dawn.

* * *

The garrison stables smelled of straw and horse sweat and the particular despair of animals owned by the military, and Ilsa moved through it with the ease of long habit, loading her supplies into the little two-wheeled cart she'd bought off a widow three winters back for the price of a good knife and a promise never asked twice. Dried meat. Hard cheese. A sack of oats. Her father's old fowling crossbow, because the army-issue ones were accurate but ugly, and if she was going to hang for treason she'd do it with a weapon that had some personality.

"Where exactly are we going?"

Emrys stepped out from between the stalls like a man materializing out of a bad conscience, the girl a step behind him, wrapped in a cloak three sizes too large so that only her face showed — a pale, watchful moon in a sea of brown wool.

"Gods' teeth," Ilsa swore, hand to chest. "Announce yourself, you grave-robbing bastard. I nearly put a bolt through your reputation."

"You don't have my reputation."

"No, mine's worse." She cinched the last strap and turned to face him fully. "We're going nowhere in particular. That's the beauty of a cart — it goes wherever nobody's looking. There's an old drover's track north of here that skirts the king's road for forty miles. Inquisitors don't walk drover's tracks. Inquisitors think mud is beneath their station, which is the one thing they've ever been right about."

Emrys studied her the way he'd once studied terrain before a fight — patient, thorough, looking for the trap. "You burned the report."

"I burned a great many reports. You'd be amazed how careless a woman gets after nineteen years." She swung up onto the cart bench and gathered the reins, then paused. "Fairweather. Look at me."

He looked.

"I'm not doing this for you. You were always insufferable and now you're insufferable with a guilty conscience, which is a combination I wouldn't wish on a plague doctor. And I'm not doing it for her, particularly, though she seems less cursed than advertised." Ilsa jerked her chin toward the girl, who was regarding the cart's mule — a sway-backed creature named Turnip — with an expression of profound scientific interest. "I'm doing it because I read those edicts coming down from the capital. Curfew bells. Confession quotas. A tax on *unregistered grief*, Fairweather. Someone is wearing our king like a glove, and every order that comes out of that fist smells wrong. I didn't swear my oath to a glove."

From within the oversized cloak, Wren spoke for the first time. Her voice was small and rough, like a door unused for seasons.

"The bells are lies."

Ilsa turned. "What's that, sprout?"

"In the city." The girl's hands emerged from the cloak, small fingers working against each other. "Before I ran. The bells ring for the king's rest, but he doesn't rest. I heard him. Through the floors. He screams in the hours between the bells, and everyone pretends they can't hear it, because pretending is safer than hearing." She looked up, and her eyes caught the stable lantern light and held it. "You hear it too. That's why you're really leaving."

The stable went very quiet. Even Turnip seemed to hold his breath.

Ilsa cleared her throat. "Well," she said. "Aren't you a cheery little thing. Get in the cart."

* * *

The drover's track proved everything Ilsa promised — muddy, winding, mercifully empty. They traveled north through country just waking from winter: black fields going soft at the edges, streams running loud with meltwater, copses of alder and birch budding green haze against the grey sky. Ilsa drove. Emrys walked alongside with a crossbow across his knees that he never quite pointed anywhere but managed to make look threatening anyway. And Wren rode in the cart bed among the sacks, emerging gradually from her cloak the way a snail emerges from its shell — an inch at a time, testing the air.

By the second day, Ilsa had catalogued the girl's strangenesses the way she'd once catalogued enemy positions. Wren ate like a starving thing but only when invited, as if hunger itself required permission. She flinched from nothing — not thunder, not the crossbow's snap, not Emrys's rare flashes of temper — but went rigid as a plucked string whenever anyone mentioned the capital. She knew things she shouldn't know. On the third morning she told Ilsa her left knee ached before Ilsa had mentioned it, and told her, moreover, that the ache came from a wound taken at the ford at Marrow Creek eleven years ago, which was true, and which Ilsa had never told another living soul.

"How did she do that?" Ilsa demanded that evening, low, while Wren was occupied trying to teach Turnip to whistle.

"She does that," Emrys said, with the weary tone of a man describing weather.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He fed the fire a branch and watched it catch. "There's a god in her somewhere. What's left of one. It leaks. Sometimes it leaks out as knowledge. Mostly it just leaks out as—" He gestured vaguely at Wren, who was now whistling at the mule herself, tunelessly, with total commitment.

"*That*," Ilsa said, "is not divine leakage. That's a child annoying livestock."

"Give it time."

But it was Ilsa who gave it more than time, though she'd have denied it under torture. It was Ilsa who started calling her *sprout*, first mockingly and then without thinking. It was Ilsa who taught her to load a bolt — "smaller hands than yours have done it, and most of them were smarter" — and discovered the girl had an archer's stillness, that uncanny patience some people are simply born with, the ability to become furniture until the moment matters. It was Ilsa who, on the fifth night, caught herself braiding Wren's tangled hair by firelight while telling the story of the Battle of the Broken Bridge, heavily edited for audience, and realized with a lurch that she couldn't remember the last time she'd touched another human being gently.

Nineteen years of soldiering will do that. Nineteen years of walls and watches and men who mistake proximity for intimacy. Somewhere in there, Ilsa Vey had become a person who kept a cart full of supplies for an escape she'd never planned, because some part of her had been waiting, all this time, for a reason to use it.

She wasn't sentimental about it. Sentimentality was for people with spare time. But sitting there in the firelight with her fingers full of dark hair and her voice full of old war stories, she thought: *Oh. This is what they meant. All those songs about comradeship. This is the actual thing, and it feels like nothing special at all, and that's how it gets you.*

* * *

It rained on the sixth night, a cold, needling rain that drove them under the leaning slab of a rock overhang above the track. The fire fought for its life in the damp, and supper was the sad arithmetic of hard cheese and the last of the bread, and tempers were short.

Or rather, Emrys's temper was short. Ilsa's was merely present, as always, like a knife she carried in her mouth instead of her boot.

"—can't keep following the high ground," Emrys was saying, jabbing a stick at the mud map he'd drawn for the fourth time. "Vane knows we'll trend north. He thinks in terrain. We should cut west, through the wetlands—"

"The wetlands," Ilsa said, "are where carts go to die. This is a good mule, Fairweather. I will not drown him for your tactical vanity."

"It's not vanity, it's—"

"He's right that Vane will expect north."

Both of them turned. Wren sat with her knees drawn up, bread held in both hands, and repeated it in her flat, certain way: "North. He thinks north. He thinks like a hunter, and hunters follow the shape of the thing they're hunting, and your shapes are both very obvious."

"Is that so," Ilsa said slowly.

Wren nodded, chewing. Then, with the terrifying non sequitur logic of the young, she added: "Turnip should go left at the stream tomorrow. Left is luckier."

"Left is *luckier*," Emrys echoed.

"I know things," Wren said, with dignity. "I don't know why I know things. But I know them." She took another bite of bread and then ruined the gravitas entirely by offering the crust to Turnip, who accepted it with the world-weariness of an animal who has been bribed before.

And then — Ilsa would replay this moment for the rest of her life, turning it over like a coin she couldn't quite believe was real — Wren laughed.

It wasn't much. A snort, really, escaping sideways as the mule's lips tickled her palm hunting for more bread. A sharp little hiccup of sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter were a language she was still learning and embarrassed to speak aloud. But it cracked across the rainy night like sun through cloud, and Ilsa saw Emrys go absolutely still beside the fire, staring at the girl as if she'd performed a miracle — which, given everything, perhaps she had.

"What?" Wren said, defensive, the laugh already buried.

"Nothing," Emrys said. His voice was rough. He looked away and poked the fire harder than any fire deserved. "Nothing at all."

But later, when the girl finally slept — curled against Turnip's warm flank under a blanket, one hand still loosely holding a crust she'd refused to surrender — Emrys sat beside Ilsa at the fire's edge and said quietly:

"Three weeks I've known her. That's the first time. Three weeks, and tonight's the first time she laughed."

Ilsa turned her crossbow bolt over in her fingers, considering. "Kids are like that. Takes a while to learn whether a place is safe enough to waste breath on joy."

"And you decided this place is safe? After six days?" His mouth twisted. "You're deserting the crown to sleep in the rain with a fugitive and a haunted mule, and you've decided it's *safe*?"

Ilsa looked at the sleeping child. At the mule. At the man she'd once followed across half a continent and had lately followed again, for worse reasons and better ones. The rain hissed on the fire, and somewhere far off a nightjar called across the dark, and she felt the strange, unarmored weight of being exactly where she intended to be.

"I decided," she said, "that if the crown wants me to turn in a barefoot kid for wearing a god like a stolen coat, then the crown and I have fundamentally different definitions of *heresy*. Everything else follows." She knocked the ember off her bolt and slid it home in the quiver. "Besides. Somebody has to teach her to shoot properly. You'd have her throwing rocks like a peasant."

"She's eight, Ilsa."

"She's whatever she is. Age is complicated when there's divinity involved. But she has the stillness, Fairweather. I've seen ten thousand soldiers, and maybe thirty had the stillness. If we're marching her to the capital to unmake a king — and we are, don't pretend we're not; I can smell a doomed mission the way I smell rain — then she'll want a crossbow that obeys her, and I'm the only one of us qualified to teach obedience."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had that gravel-edge quality it got around anything resembling feeling. "Why are you really here, Vey? Truly. No jokes."

Ilsa fed the fire and watched the flames climb.

"When I was a girl," she said at last, "my mother told me the gods went quiet because we failed them. That silence was a punishment, and one day they'd come back angry, and we'd deserve it. I believed that for thirty years. Carried it like a stone." She glanced at the sleeping girl, at the white-blond hair catching firelight. "And now I find out the silence wasn't punishment at all. It was a theft. Somebody stole the sky and hung a curtain in its place and called the curtain *law*. Well." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "I've spent my whole life enforcing law. Be a nice change to rob a thief."

Emrys looked at her for a long time. "The temple," he said finally. "Two days north. There's a place we need to see — where I took my oath. Where it all started. If anyone's ever written down what actually happened during the Binding, it'll be carved there."

"Then we'd best get some sleep," Ilsa said, settling back against the cart wheel. "Robbery at dawn."

Above the overhang, the rain began to slacken, and through the thinning cloud a single star showed itself — pale, distant, and for the first time in ten years, Ilsa could almost believe it was watching back.

She slept better than she had in nineteen years, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was the finest gift the night had to give.
