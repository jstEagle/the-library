## 10. Serel Vane's Offer

The trap was not a trap at all, Serel Vane reflected. A trap required cunning. This had required only patience and a map.

He stood in the doorway of the shepherd's hut with his hat in his hands — the courtesy of it mattered, he had always believed that courtesy mattered — and watched Emrys Fairweather's face do the thing faces did when they realized the walls had been theirs all along. Behind Vane, six of his men fanned out through the thin rain, unhurried, their boots quiet on the wet bracken. There would be no violence here unless someone insisted on it. He very much hoped no one would insist.

"Captain Vey," he said warmly, to the woman whose crossbow was leveled at his chest from beside the cold hearth. "Still aiming for the throat, I see. You always were efficient."

"And you still talk too much," Ilsa Vey said.

"Guilty." He smiled, and let the smile reach his eyes, because sincerity could not be counterfeited — only misunderstood — and he was not a man who misunderstood himself. "May I come in? The rain is disagreeable, and what I have to say will take some little time."

Emrys hadn't moved from where he stood between Vane and the inner room. The girl was behind that door; Vane knew it the way one knew the location of one's own hand. Ten years since the Godswar's last fires, ten years of silence, and still Emrys Fairweather stood like a wall between the crown and whatever it wanted. It was almost admirable. Almost.

"You may not," Emrys said.

Vane sighed and stepped inside anyway, slowly, hands visible, setting his hat on a peg by the door as if he had been invited and merely anticipated the invitation. The hut smelled of peat smoke and old wool and, faintly, of something else — something sweet and wrong underneath, like lilies left too long in water. He breathed it in with something close to reverence.

"There," he said softly. "Do you smell it? No — of course you don't. You've lived with her for weeks now; the nose makes peace with miracles." He turned. "She is awake, I imagine. She has been awake since we entered the valley, hasn't she? Since the boundary. Children sleep poorly when they can feel the leash again."

Emrys's jaw tightened. "You talk as though you know what she is."

"I know *precisely* what she is." Vane let the words settle, let them do their work. He took the single stool by the hearth — Ilsa's crossbow tracked him the whole way down — and folded his hands over one knee like a man settling into a sermon. "That is rather the point of this visit, Captain Fairweather. Or may I say Emrys? We are, after all, nearly colleagues."

"We're nothing."

"Oh, but we are. We are two men who were present at the same wedding and never introduced." He glanced toward the inner door, then back, and his voice gentled into something almost tender. "A vessel. Do you understand the word? Not a host — hosts are taken unwillingly. A vessel is *made*. Shaped. Fired in a kiln until it holds its shape and can carry what must be carried." He tapped his own sternum, twice, lightly. "I helped fire this one."

* * *

The silence that followed had texture. Vane had learned, across a career of difficult rooms, that silence was not an absence but a material, and that a skilled speaker could carve it like wood.

Ilsa spoke first, because soldiers always did. "You're lying."

"I have never once lied in the performance of my office," Vane said, and meant it, and enjoyed meaning it. "It is why the Inquisition trusts me with children. Ask anyone. Serel Vane does not lie; Serel Vane merely tells you truths you were not prepared to hold." He unfolded his hands. "You want the history. Very well. You of all people have earned the history, Emrys, though I confess I don't understand why you haven't already assembled it yourself. A mercenary swears a Binding Oath at the end of the Godswar. The gods go silent the same season. And for ten years the man asks no questions? You simply... accepted the receipt?"

Emrys said nothing. His hand had drifted, unconsciously, to his forearm — to the scar beneath the sleeve. Vane watched the gesture with genuine interest.

"The Binding Oath," Vane continued, "was real. I want that understood. Whatever you think of what came after, the tourniquet was necessary. The world was burning. Gods do not moderate themselves, Emrys — they love the way avalanches love. Your god consented, yes. They all consented, more or less. Some screamed going under; yours went quietly, which I have always thought says something about the quality of your company." A thin smile. "But a tourniquet must be tied by someone who stays awake. Someone who watches the wound while the patient sleeps. The gods could not watch themselves. So certain servants of the crown — myself included, though I was younger then, and merely taking notes — remained behind to keep the knot true."

"Keepers," Ilsa said flatly.

"Curators, we preferred. But yes." He studied his own knuckles. "And here is the truth that has cost three people their tongues and one man his head: the Binding did not have to be permanent. It was designed — beautifully, mercifully designed — to loosen. To let the voices return drop by drop, generation by generation, the way a fever breaks slowly rather than all at once. The world would have woken gently. Instead—" He gestured outward, at the rain, at the gray light, at the whole godless country beyond the walls. "Instead someone pulled the knot tight and threw away the litany. Do you wonder why the crown grew so strong so fast, Emrys? Why Aldermarch's borders have never once been threatened in ten years? Where do you imagine the power of nine sleeping gods went?"

Emrys's face had gone the color of the ash outside the window. "No."

"Yes." Vane said it without triumph. That was the thing Ilsa would remember later, if she survived to tell it: there was no gloating in the man at all. Only a terrible, patient certainty, like frost spreading across glass. "Not stolen, even. Nothing so vulgar. It was *invested*. Held in trust by the throne, for the good of a realm that could not be trusted to survive its own miracles. I was there when the decision was made. I signed my name. And I will tell you freely, since you seem determined to believe the worst of me: it was the right decision. It *is* the right decision. Look around you. Ten years without a god, and how many burning villages? How many prophets raving in market squares, how many wars fought over whose whisper was holiest?" He shook his head slowly. "We traded madness for quiet. I have never once lost sleep over it."

* * *

From behind the inner door, very softly, came the sound of a child breathing. Vane tilted his head toward it the way a musician tilts toward a note slightly flat.

"She hears us," he said. "Of course she does. The First Tongue isn't a language, precisely — it's closer to listening. She has been listening to this entire conversation." He raised his voice fractionally, kindly. "Wren. I know your name because I chose the naming convention for your batch. You were the eleventh attempt. Did they tell you that? Eleven vessels before you cracked or burned or simply stopped. You should be proud. You are the finest thing the crown ever made."

The door did not open. But the breathing changed, quickened, and Vane nodded as though he'd received an answer.

"Do not speak to her," Emrys said, and his voice had dropped into a register Vane recognized from old battlefields — the flat tone of a man measuring distances.

"I intend to do nothing of the kind. I intend to offer her sanctuary. Which brings me—" Vane leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time his manner shifted, became almost confiding, almost weary, a tired clerk at the end of a long ledger. "—to the reason I rode into the Godless Valley in the rain instead of sending men to do it badly. Emrys Fairweather. Oath-sworn of a silent god. Do you know what the penalty is, under current law, for oath-breaking? For *harboring* a vessel? For striking a crown officer?" He counted on his fingers, unhurried. "Hanging, hanging, and hanging. Three ropes. And Captain Vey here adds desertion to her account, which under wartime statute means the rope plus forfeiture — her family's name struck from every record in the realm. Erased. As far as Aldermarch will ever remember, Ilsa Vey was never born."

"They're not deserters yet," Ilsa said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

"Tomorrow the paperwork completes itself. That is how paperwork works; it is why we invented it." He turned back to Emrys, and now his voice went soft and sincere, and the horror of it — Ilsa saw it, saw Emrys see it — was that the man meant every word. "Here is my offer. Surrender the child. Unconditionally, tonight, into my custody alone — not the men outside, me. And in exchange: full pardon. All charges dissolved. The desertion stricken. Vey restored to her regiment with honors owed. You, Emrys, free to walk home to this valley and live out your years in the quiet you have so obviously earned. No inquisitor bothers you again. My word on it, and my word has never once been broken."

"And the girl?" Emrys asked quietly. "What happens to Wren?"

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not cruelty, nothing so simple. Recognition, perhaps. Pity, even.

"The Wearing," he said. "It is not a punishment, whatever the tavern songs have decided. It is a *surgery*. The power in that child is a splinter of divinity lodged in mortal flesh, and it is killing her — you have seen it, don't insult us both. Her hair, her hands, the way she tires. Every miracle she performs, she pays for. Left alone, she will spend herself entirely within the year, and die screaming in a language no one living speaks, and the splinter will pass to whoever stands nearest, and we will begin again with attempt twelve." He spread his hands. "The Wearing removes the splinter. Yes, it hollows the subject. Yes, the subjects do not afterward resemble people. But the alternative is a child dying in agony and a divine ember loose in the world to set another fire. I am offering to save her from that, Emrys. I am offering to save *everyone* from that. I have done it eleven times, and I have hated it eleven times, and I have slept soundly after each one, because the arithmetic is clean and I refuse to pretend otherwise."

"You call that arithmetic," Emrys said. "I call it butchery with a ledger."

"And I call your sentiment a luxury purchased by other people's suffering." Vane rose, unhurried, retrieving his hat from its peg. Rain drummed on the sod roof. Outside, his men waited with the patience of furniture. "Consider it tonight. Consider what your oath actually requires — not what you wish it required. You swore to guard against the return of unbound divinity. That child is unbound divinity wearing small shoes. By the letter of your own scar, Emrys, I am the lawful party in this room, and you are the heretic."

* * *

At the door he paused, and looked back, and for one moment the courtesy fell away entirely and something older looked out — something tired and vast and terribly reasonable, the way a glacier is reasonable.

"One kindness, before I go, because I believe you deserve it. The voice your god gave you, ten years ago, at the binding — you've missed it. Of course you have. Ten years of silence, and a man begins to believe he was abandoned, that he was unworthy, that the silence is a verdict." Vane settled his hat against the drizzle. "It wasn't a verdict. It was surgery. Your god is not gone, Emrys. Your god is *in storage*, along with everything else, and the key is turning in the lock as we speak, and when the king finishes the final working — and he is very close, closer than you can imagine — there will be no need for vessels at all. No need for children in kilns. No need for any of it. The crown will hold all of it, safely, forever, and no one will ever burn again." He smiled, small and genuine and ghastly. "Help me finish it. Or stand aside. Those are the only two merciful options, and I am offering you both."

He stepped out into the rain. Then paused once more, without turning.

"For what it is worth — and I know it is worth nothing — the eleventh vessel asked about you. Weeks ago, before she ran. Asked whether the oath-man in the valley was happy." A beat. "I told her no one in the valley is happy. I hope I wasn't wrong to spare her the details."

Then he was gone, and his men folded after him into the gray like water closing over stones, and the hut was left to the rain and the peat smoke and the three people inside it, none of whom moved for a long time.

It was Wren who opened the door at last. She stood in the frame with her too-old eyes and her bare feet, and she looked at Emrys with an expression no eight-year-old should have had cause to wear.

"He lied about one thing," she said, in her careful, accented Common, and then, slipping without seeming to notice into the First Tongue, a word like a bell rung underwater: *"Eleven didn't crack. Eleven got away. I'm supposed to find them."*

Outside, far off toward the ford, a horn sounded once — the signal Vane's men used to seal a road — and Ilsa began, quietly, to load her cart in the dark.
