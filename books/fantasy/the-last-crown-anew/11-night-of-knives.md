## 11. Night of Knives

Wren woke because the fire had gone quiet.

Not out — quiet. The way a held breath is quiet. She lay in the crook of Ilsa's arm beneath the cart's canvas, listening to the night around the camp, and the night was wrong. The crickets had stopped. The wind had stopped moving through the pines the way water stops moving when something big stands upstream of it.

She counted the sounds she knew. Emrys breathing slow and even on his bedroll near the ashes — not asleep, though; his kind of awake made almost no sound at all, just a stillness deeper than sleeping. Ilsa's snore, soft and broken like a song sung by someone who'd forgotten most of the words. The mule shifting its weight. The river below them, running dark over stones toward the bridge they'd crossed at dusk, the old one with the leaning rail and the moss-slick planks that Ilsa had called *a death trap with opinions*.

And underneath all of it, so faint it might have been her own blood in her ears: a whisper in the First Tongue.

*They are coming.*

Wren sat up very slowly. The voice had been getting braver since they'd left the valley behind, since the day it poured through her into the dying farmer and left her hands looking like her gran's hands, knuckled and thin-skinned and spotted. Some nights it only murmured. Some nights it sang, low and wordless, and those were the nights Wren slept best, wrapped in a sound that felt like being carried.

Tonight it did not sing.

*Ilsa,* she whispered, and touched the woman's arm.

Ilsa came awake the way a door comes off its hinges — all at once, hand already finding the crossbow under the canvas, eyes open and flat and clear. She didn't ask what. She'd learned that about Wren in the weeks since Brackenfell: the girl knew things before there was anything to know.

"How many?" Ilsa breathed.

Wren didn't know how she knew this either, but she knew it the way she knew her own name. "Seven. Maybe eight. They're at the tree line."

Ilsa was already moving, rolling out of the cart without a single board creaking, crossing to Emrys in three silent strides. One touch to his shoulder and he was up, no groan, no stretch, just a man who had spent ten years waking ready for bad news.

"Tree line," Wren said before he could ask. "The voice says—"

"I know." His face in the banked coals' light was all shadow and scar. He looked older tonight. He looked like the stories Ilsa told when she thought Wren was asleep, the ones about the Godswar, the ones that always ended with Ilsa going quiet and drinking more than usual.

Emrys looked at the darkness beyond the camp for a long moment, and then he said the thing Wren would remember for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

"They'll come at the bridge," he said. "It's the only approach we can't watch. Vane will have read the ground same as us." A pause. "Ilsa. The bridge."

Ilsa didn't argue. Wren watched her check the crossbow, check the quillens of bolts, check the little knife at her hip, and every check was calm, and none of the calm reached her eyes. "How long do you need?"

"To get her gone? An hour, if the fog holds."

"Then hold the fog over your own head and let me hold the bridge." Ilsa grinned, and it was the grin she wore when things were worst, the one that showed all her teeth like a dog deciding to be friendly right up until it wasn't. She crouched to Wren's level, which she almost never did, and took Wren's chin in one calloused hand. "You listen to me, sprout. Whatever you hear from the bridge — screaming, breaking, me singing rude songs about Serel Vane's mother — you run. You don't look back, you don't get clever, you get gone. That's an order. I'm still a captain until somebody takes the rank off me."

"You're deserting," Wren said. "You can't order me if you're a deserter."

Ilsa laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and kissed the top of Wren's head, smelling of leather and woodsmoke and the onion soup they'd had for supper. "Then consider it a favor between friends. Best favor you'll ever be given. Don't waste it."

* * *

They went the other way, downriver, through the ferns and the wet black loam, Wren riding the mule with Emrys leading, and every step away from the bridge felt like tearing something. Behind them, faint through the trees, came the first sounds: a challenge shouted in a soldier's clipped cadence, Ilsa's voice answering with words that were definitely rude and possibly true.

Then the crossbow. Once. Twice.

Then the world caught fire behind them — not flame but light, cold white light rising off the bridge like steam off a kettle, consecrated fire, the kind the inquisitors carried in vials blessed by a god who no longer answered anyone. Wren twisted in the saddle to look and Emrys's hand closed hard on her ankle.

"Eyes forward."

"The voice is saying—"

"I know what it's saying." And she saw, even in the dark, even at a distance, that his hands were shaking on the mule's lead.

They went maybe half a mile, far enough that the light behind them dimmed to a glow through the pines, far enough that Emrys stopped at a stream fork and stood listening, and Wren listened too, with the part of her that wasn't a girl at all anymore but a doorway, a vessel, a throat for something vast and patient.

The whisper said: *The captain falls.*

"No," Wren said aloud.

"Wren—"

"She's not fallen. She's holding. She's holding and they're too many and the fire—" The whisper came again, gentler now, the way you'd speak to something small and frightened, and that gentleness was worse than any warning. *Little vessel. They will not stop. They have never once stopped. The man in the crown sends them and sends them and they come like winter comes.*

"We keep moving," Emrys said, and his voice was iron, was oath-iron, was the voice of a man who had made one promise ten years ago and paid for it ever since. "That's the whole plan. We move."

But Wren was looking back down the trail, at the white light pulsing through the trees, and she could hear it now clearly, hear everything: the ring of steel on the bridge rails, Ilsa shouting — actually singing, gods, she really was singing something obscene about Vane's mother — and under it the wet heavy sounds of men dying badly, and under *that*, thin as wire:

Ilsa's heartbeat stuttering.

Wren didn't decide anything. That was the horror of it, what she understood later and understood completely: there was no decision, no brave choice, no moment where a child weighed her life against her friend's. There was only the fear, huge and total, and the voice inside the fear opening like a door.

She ran.

Emrys shouted her name. The mule shied. The forest blurred, ferns whipping her legs, and she burst from the tree line onto the near end of the bridge and the sight of it burned itself into her forever.

Ilsa stood at the far end with her back against a broken rail, one arm hanging wrong, blood sheeting down her side, three dead men around her boots and four more coming up the road behind them with shields and that terrible cold light in clay pots. Her crossbow was empty. Her knife was out. When she saw Wren her face did something terrible — not fear for herself, fear *for Wren*, pure and blazing.

"RUN, YOU LITTLE IDIOT!"

One of the shield-men raised a clay pot and threw it, and the consecrated fire bloomed across the bridge boards, and Wren felt it reach for her the way the valley's edge had once pushed magic away — except this fire didn't push, it *unmade*, it was made for unmaking vessels like her, she could feel its intention like a hand reaching into her chest—

And the door inside her opened all the way.

The First Tongue came out of her mouth, but it wasn't her speaking. It was her standing aside while something spoke *through* her, something older than the fire and the crown and the men who served both, and the voice was not loud. That was the strangest part. It was quiet. It was the quietest thing in the world, and the whole night bent around it.

"*Be not.*"

The fire went out. Not smothered — *answered*. The flames simply agreed, the way everything agreed, and went dark.

Three men stood on the bridge with their shields raised, and the whisper passed over them, and Wren felt it pass through her own body on its way, felt it take something from her with each syllable, warm years pouring out of her like water from a tipped cup —

— and the three men came apart.

Not killed. *Unmade.* Their shields sagged into rust-bloomed flakes. Their armor sighed into red dust that drifted on the river wind. The men themselves — Wren would see this every time she closed her eyes for the rest of her life — the men themselves grew suddenly, terribly old, decades and decades in a single breath, skin slackening, hair whitening, knees buckling, and then they were kneeling, and then they were nothing but clothes and dust and, briefly, three heartbeats slowing like drums at the end of a procession.

Their last expressions were not pain. That was somehow the worst part. They looked *relieved*, like soldiers finally allowed to sleep.

The remaining four broke. Wren heard them crashing away through the dark, screaming about the godless valley and the witch-child and the wrath of the silenced one, and their terror meant nothing to her, because the taking was still happening, it hadn't stopped when they fled, the cup was still tipping and she couldn't find the bottom of it—

"*Enough.*" Emrys. His arms around her from behind, his scarred palm pressed flat against her sternum, over the place where the voice lived. "*Enough. She's paid enough. Close the door.*"

And the voice — the god, the vast patient thing that had chosen her without asking — hesitated. For one heartbeat Wren felt it weigh him, weigh the oath-scar under his hand, weigh some private accounting between the two of them that she would never be shown.

Then, gently, like a parent setting down a sleeping child: it stopped.

* * *

The fire was small when she next saw it. Everything was far away and soft-edged, the way the world looks through water. Someone had laid her by the flames and someone else — Ilsa, hoarse, gray-faced, arm bound tight against her chest with a splint and strips of shirt — had put a blanket over her and then, thinking she was asleep, had begun to cry, silently, the tears cutting clean lines through the soot on her face.

Wren wanted to tell her not to cry. Her mouth wouldn't work right.

Emrys crouched over her, and his face was the worst thing she'd seen all night, worse than the dissolving men, because it was the face of a man doing arithmetic he already knew the answer to.

Her hands lay in her lap where she could see them. They were old women's hands again, thinner than before, the veins standing up like roots under paper. She turned them over. On the backs, where a girl her age should have had smooth unmarked skin, there were fine lines now, silver-white, branching like frost on a window. They climbed her wrists and vanished under her sleeves.

She reached up — slowly, with effort, everything was effort now — and touched her own hair. It came away in her fingers. Not much. Just a handful of strands from her temple, gone white as snowmelt, drifting down like ash.

"Oh, sprout," Ilsa whispered.

"It's alright," Wren said. Her voice sounded strange to her, older, like someone else borrowing it. She tried to smile and mostly managed it. "There were three of them. Only three. So it's — it's fair, isn't it? Three for three."

Neither of them answered, and their silence told her everything the hedge-healer Odji would later confirm with his grave, ink-stained fingers counting on her wrist: the price was not three coins set aside. The price was the whole purse, opened, and whatever the god chose to pour out.

Emrys took her old-woman hands in his scarred ones, careful as a man handling eggshell, and bowed his head over them, and Wren realized with a distant, floating surprise that he was praying — to her, or to the thing inside her, or to whatever remained of the god who had gone silent rather than make his people do this.

"The capital," he said at last, quietly, to Ilsa, over Wren's head. "We go tomorrow. No more delays. If there's workings left in her, I won't spend another one on bridges and roads."

"And if the rite spends the rest?" Ilsa asked. Her voice cracked on *rest*.

Emrys was silent a long time. The fire crackled. Somewhere across the river, the surviving inquisitors were carrying their dust and their shame back to Serel Vane, and Vane would smile his courteous smile and write a report, and the man in the crown would read it with borrowed eyes.

"Then she chooses," Emrys said finally. "No one chooses for her. Not me. Not the god. Her."

Wren lay back against the cart wheel and looked up past the flames at the stars, and the voice stirred in her once more, faint and tender, and said the thing she would carry like a coal in her chest all the long miles to Aldermarch:

*Little vessel. There is less of you than there was. But what remains is enough — if you spend it where it matters.*

She closed her eyes. Her temples itched where the white hair grew.

Tomorrow, the capital.
