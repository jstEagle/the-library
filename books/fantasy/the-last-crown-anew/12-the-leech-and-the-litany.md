## 12. The Leech and the Litany

The hedge-healer's house smelled of boiled willow bark and old smoke, a low crooked thing built into the hillside above the mill town of Duncarrow, its walls hung with drying bundles of herbs that clicked softly in the draft like small bones. Emrys sat on a stool too short for him and watched Odji work, and he did not pray, because there was no one left to pray to, and because the man's hands were doing the only kind of holy work the world still permitted.

Ilsa lay on a plank table padded with sacking, her armor cut away from her side, her skin gray as tallow. The bolt had gone in under her ribs — Vane's men used consecrated steel, of course they did, the crown spared no expense for murder — and the wound wept a slow dark blood that would not clot properly, as if her body had simply lost interest in the argument.

"Hold this." Odji pressed a bowl of something greenish and steaming against the wound's edge. His hands were brown and precise, a leech-cutter's hands, a bonesetter's hands, hands that had closed a thousand wounds on people the crown had already written off. He was perhaps sixty, perhaps eighty; his face gave nothing away except tiredness worn down to the grain. "You'll sing if I tell you to sing."

"I don't sing," Emrys said.

"Then you'll hum. Pain is easier to carry with noise under it. Everyone knows that. Only soldiers pretend otherwise." He worked as he talked, threading a curved needle through flesh with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once been interrupted by a superior officer and did not intend to start now.

Emrys held the bowl and watched the thread pull shut, and thought about how strange it was that after everything — the bridge, the knives, the white spreading through Wren's hair like frost climbing a window — it was here, in a room that smelled of pond water and feverfew, that his hands finally began to shake.

He had not shaken on the bridge. He had not shaken when Ilsa went down holding the span alone while he carried Wren's collapsed body up the far bank, both of them deadweight, one of them worse than dead. He had not shaken when he laid Wren by the fire and could not tell for three long breaths whether she was breathing or merely remembering how.

But watching a needle draw Ilsa's torn side together, stitch by patient stitch, some wall inside him quietly gave way, and the shaking started low in his back and climbed, and he was grateful that Odji was too busy to look up.

* * *

Wren slept in the loft above, wrapped in every blanket the house owned. She had not woken since the bridge. Her breathing was slow and even, which Odji said was good, which Emrys chose to believe, though he had learned over ten years in the Godless Valley that belief and truth were neighbors who rarely spoke.

Her temples were white. Not streaked — *white*, clean and total, two wings of snow where chestnut hair had been, as though someone had taken a brush dipped in winter and touched her there. The rest of her hair held its color still. For now.

Emrys climbed the ladder eleven times in the first night just to watch her breathe. He told himself he was checking. He knew better. A man does not check eleven times. A man counts.

He counted her breaths. Sixteen to the minute, then fourteen as she sank deeper. He counted the freckles across the bridge of her nose, the ones she'd earned crossing the valley in the sun, ordinary child-freckles, proof that whatever lived behind her eyes was at least partly made of the same clay as everyone else. He counted the years between them — thirty-odd, a gulf, an ocean — and then he counted the years she might have left, and stopped counting, because that number refused to hold still.

Below, Odji's mortar scraped in slow circles. The sound went on for hours. It was, Emrys thought, the sound of the world trying to put itself right with a spoon.

* * *

Near dawn, Ilsa woke.

She woke badly — the way soldiers wake, all at once, hand snapping toward a weapon that wasn't there — and Emrys caught her wrist before she tore the stitches loose.

"Easy. You're at Odji's. Above Duncarrow. We crossed the ford at night, remember the reeds—"

"I remember the bolt." Her voice was gravel and rust. She looked down at herself, at the neat ladder of stitches along her ribs, and grimaced. "Ugly work."

"He saved your life."

"I said what I said." But her hand found Emrys's forearm and gripped it, brief and hard, soldier's thanks, and then she turned her head on the sacking pillow. "The girl?"

"Asleep. Alive."

"The men I didn't kill?"

"Gone back to Vane, or dead in the river. Either way, word travels. We have days, not weeks."

Ilsa absorbed this with the flat economy of a woman who had spent twenty years absorbing bad news before breakfast. Then her eyes narrowed. "And her hair. Emrys. Her *hair*. I saw it happen. She unmade those men and it took—" She stopped, jaw working. "It took something out of her. I've seen channeling burn a mage hollow in a heartbeat. This was different. This was *priced*."

"I know."

"You don't know. You weren't standing close enough to see her veins light up like lantern-glass. I was." Ilsa pushed herself up on one elbow, winced, kept going anyway, because she was Ilsa. "Get the healer up here. Now."

"He needs sleep—"

"*Now,* Fairweather, or so help me I'll walk down these stairs bleeding and make it his problem."

So Emrys woke Odji, who came up the ladder grumbling with a clay lamp and the expression of a man whose fees were about to be paid in questions rather than coin.

* * *

Odji examined Wren while she slept, gently, thoroughly: eyelids rolled back, pulse taken at throat and wrist, palms turned over, the white at her temples brushed aside with two fingers as tender as if he were handling ash. Emrys stood at the foot of the loft bed and waited, and the waiting was its own kind of wound.

Finally the healer sat back on his heels. He looked at Wren a long moment. Then he looked at Emrys, and something in his face changed — the professional mask slipping, and underneath it an older, wearier honesty.

"Sit down," Odji said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Everyone would rather stand. Sit anyway." Emrys sat. Odji set the lamp between them so their faces were half-lit, conspiratorial, like men dividing stolen goods. "How much do you know about vessels?"

"Enough to know I hate the word."

"Mm. Wrong answer, but an honest one, so we'll build on it." He folded his hands. "There are two kinds of power in a body. The kind you're born carrying — blood-magic, witch-light, the small coin most folk spend without knowing. That regrows. Spend it, rest, it comes back, like sweat or seed. Then there's the other kind. Borrowed power. Power that passes *through* you from somewhere else." He tapped the floorboards, twice, meaningfully — meaning *below*, meaning the deep places, meaning perhaps the place where gods go when they are silenced. "A vessel is a pipe. And a pipe, friend, is not a well. What flows through a pipe does not refill it. It wears it."

"She healed a farmer on the road," Emrys said quietly. "Her hands aged. I saw it. I thought — I hoped it was a fluke. An accident of the working."

"It was a receipt." Odji's voice was gentle and merciless. "Every time the god's voice moves through her, it takes payment. Not strength — strength comes back. Years. Meat and marrow and the quiet chemistry of being young. The body pays in the only currency it cannot counterfeit." He nodded toward the sleeping girl. "The healing cost her perhaps two years. What she did on that bridge — unmaking armed men, three of them, that is not a candle she lit, that is a bonfire — cost her more. Look at the white. Look at her fingernails. See the lunulae? Faded. In an old woman I'd call that normal. In a child of nine, it is a ledger with entries on it."

Emrys heard himself ask the question the way a man hears himself step off a cliff: from very far away, already falling.

"How many times can she do it? How many workings left in her?"

Odji did not answer immediately. He picked up the lamp and held it closer to Wren's sleeping face, studying her the way a moneylender studies a debtor, and Emrys hated him for it and understood him completely, because love means counting someone else's remaining days, and someone has to be cruel enough to count.

"I've seen four vessels in my life," Odji said at last. "Two died of the spending. One went mad — the body lived but the person paid out entirely, like a rope run through a pulley until nothing's left on the near side. The fourth was my daughter." The lamp flame shivered. "She channeled six times over nine years. Small workings, mostly. Healings. One storm turned. At the end she was thirty-one years old with the heart of a woman of sixty. She knew the price going in, every time. She always said it was worth it." He set the lamp down. "Your girl has done two great workings that I know of, and one of them was enormous. Going by the signs — the hair, the nails, the depth of this sleep, the way her pulse stutters when I press here—" he touched a spot below Wren's collarbone, lightly, and even asleep the girl frowned, "-I would say she carries perhaps three more workings in her. Three, if they're small. Fewer, if they're not. After that, there is no *after*. There is only a body that keeps breathing out of habit, like a house where everyone has moved away but left the door open."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, water dripped in the herb-room, patient as a clock.

"Three," Emrys said.

"At most. Possibly fewer. And understand me — it is not only the great workings. Every whisper of that voice through her costs something small. A month here, a season there. Even now, sleeping, she may be paying rent on the power that shelters her." Odji rose stiffly, knees cracking. "I can ease her sleeps. Feed the body what it needs to rebuild what can be rebuilt. But I cannot mint years, mercenary. No one can. The gods could, once." His mouth twisted. "That was rather the point of them, I'm told."

* * *

Emrys went down the ladder and out into the grey morning without deciding to.

Duncarrow was waking below — smoke rising from chimneys, a dog complaining, the mill wheel turning with its endless wet groan. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary lives, spent by ordinary people who would never have to count anyone's remaining days, who got their decades the way rain falls, freely and without ledger.

He stood in the yard until the cold found the gaps in his coat, and he tried to think like a tactician, because thinking like a man was unbearable.

Three workings. They needed one to reach the king. Perhaps two, if the road fought them — and the road would fight them; Vane did not lose, he recalculated. Which meant the arithmetic of survival allowed for exactly zero mistakes and zero mercy, and Emrys had never once in his life seen a plan survive contact with either.

Unless. Unless the Binding could be broken *without* her speaking the counter-litany at full voice. Unless there was another way to carry the god's voice to the crown — his oath-scar, the temple inscriptions, the litany he'd sworn and could perhaps unswear—

But he remembered the temple walls. The counter-litany required a living vessel's tongue. First Tongue was not learned; it was *inhabited*. He could break his oath a hundred times and it would mean nothing without her voice to fill the shape of it.

Which meant the plan, any plan, ended with a child spending herself like a purse emptied onto a table, and everyone around her pretending not to watch the coins fall.

"You look like a man doing sums he doesn't want the answer to."

Ilsa stood in the doorway, upright by sheer fury, a blanket around her shoulders, her color bad but her spine straight. She lowered herself onto the bench beside the door with the care of a woman negotiating with her own stitches.

"You should be lying down."

"I should be a lot of things." She squinted at him. "He told you, then. About the price."

"He told me she has three workings left. Maybe fewer."

Ilsa nodded slowly, and did not flinch, and Emrys loved her a little for that — for refusing to soften it, because softening it would be lying, and they had agreed somewhere on the road, wordlessly, that they were done lying to each other.

"So," she said. "We get her to the capital using none of them. We find another way. Or—" her jaw tightened— "we decide, before she does, what's worth spending. Because mark me, Emrys, that child will spend herself to the last copper if we let her. She thinks she was *sent*. Sent children don't haggle."

"I know."

"And you'll want to take her place. Put your scarred hand on the crown instead, let your old oath pay the bill." She looked at him sideways. "Can you?"

"I don't know. Odji says the litany needs a vessel's tongue. Mine's been silent ten years."

"Ten years ago you swore an oath that sealed a god." Ilsa's voice dropped. "Maybe oaths aren't only spent by the young. Maybe yours has weight left in it too. Worth asking. Worth *begging* to know."

From the loft above, faintly, came a sound that stopped them both: Wren, turning in her sleep, murmuring. Not words. Not quite. A low syllable rolling over and over, in a language older than the valley, older than the crown — the sound, Emrys thought with a cold that had nothing to do with morning, of a voice rehearsing.

Below the hill, on the road from the ford, a rider appeared through the mist at a hard gallop, crown colors streaming, and the mill town's dogs began to bark.
