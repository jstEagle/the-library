## 13. The Capital of Ash Bells

The bells found them before the walls did.

They began at the crest of the pilgrim road, a mile out, where the capital first showed itself as a smudge of grey against a bruised morning sky — and they did not stop. Ilsa Vey had served in garrison towns where bells rang for fires, for festivals, for the changing of the watch. She had never heard bells like these. They rang every hour on the hour, deep and slow and mournful, and between each toll there was a silence so complete it felt less like absence than like listening.

"For the king's rest," said the pilgrim walking beside her, an old woman with a bundle of dried herbs strapped to her back and eyes that never quite met anyone's. She said it the way people recite something they've been made to memorize. "We ring for the king's rest."

Ilsa glanced sideways. "He's ill, then?"

The old woman's hands went still on her bundle. For a moment — just a moment — her gaze flicked up, and what lived in it was not grief or pity but fear, worn smooth as a river stone from years of polishing. Then she looked down again.

"He rests," she said. "The bells say so."

Ahead of them, Emrys shifted the hood of his pilgrim's cloak and did not look back. Wren walked between him and Odji's mule-cart, small hands tucked into sleeves too big for her, her white-shot hair hidden beneath a scarf Ilsa had knotted herself that morning. The girl had not spoken since dawn. She didn't need to. Ilsa had learned to read her silences the way one reads weather: this one was the flat grey kind, the kind that meant the voice in her head was getting louder the closer they came to whatever had made it go quiet in the first place.

The road thickened with travelers as they crested the last hill. Pilgrims, mostly — the crown had declared a season of devotion, according to the talk in Brackenfell, though nobody could say devotion to what. There was no god left to pray to. That was rather the point of the last decade. And yet here they came, hundreds of them, trudging toward Aldermarch's capital with candle-stubs and prayer-knots and the particular hunched patience of people who had decided that if heaven would not answer, they would simply stand outside its door until it did.

"They're not pilgrims," Wren said suddenly. Her voice was thin. "Not most of them. They're being *called*."

Emrys's stride hitched — barely, but Ilsa caught it. Ten years she'd known him, and he'd taught her to catch things like that. "Called by what?"

Wren's jaw worked. Under the scarf, her face had gone the color of tallow. "I don't have the words. It's like — like when you whistle for a dog you've already got on a leash. They think they chose to come." Her fists tightened in her sleeves. "The bells are the leash."

Nobody answered that. What was there to answer? Ilsa adjusted the crossbow slung beneath her cart-seat cushions — wrapped in wool, oiled, illegal as sin within city walls — and told herself the cold crawling up her spine was just the morning.

* * *

The gates of the capital were called the Ash Gates, and now Ilsa knew why.

They stood three men high and twice that wide, carved with the likenesses of the Twelve who had once spoken to the world — gods whose names were still legal, whose worship was still technically permitted, whose voices had nonetheless gone silent as grave-dust ten years ago. Someone had climbed those carvings with buckets of ash and lime. The faces of the Twelve were white now, featureless, their mouths smeared blank. It should have looked crude. Instead it looked like a warning written in the only language everyone understood.

A queue of carts and foot traffic stretched half a mile. Crown soldiers worked the gate in pairs, checking papers, waving through anyone with a pilgrim's token — a lead medallion sold at every roadside shrine for a copper, blessed by no one, honored by everyone. Ilsa had bought six. They hung around her neck now under her shirt, along with Emrys's and Wren's, and the metal was cold enough that she felt each one like a fingertip pressing her breastbone.

"Papers mean nothing today," Emrys murmured without moving his lips. He stood at the cart's side, playing the role of a hired guard with a slouch he wore like armor. "Tokens and faces. Keep your face stupid."

"I have a gift for stupid faces," Ilsa said. "Years of command."

That earned her the ghost of a smile, there and gone. Then they reached the gate itself, and there was no more room for jokes.

The soldiers at the arch were young. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that sat wrongest. Young men and women in crown black, faces scrubbed and solemn, checking tokens with a reverence that had nothing to do with procedure. One of them held each medallion to his lips before handing it back. Kissing lead stamped with the sigil of a dead faith. Ilsa watched him do it three times in a row, mechanically, tenderly, and understood that she was not looking at piety. She was looking at training.

"State your purpose in the capital," said a soldier at their cart. His voice had a rehearsed cadence, call and response, sermon-shaped.

"Devotion," Ilsa said, pitching her voice into the flat regional drone she hadn't used since her army days. "My brother's family. We come for the Rest."

"The king rests," the soldier said.

"The king rests," she repeated.

Something flickered behind his eyes — the same polished-smooth fear the herb woman had carried — and he waved them through, and the shadow of the Ash Gate swallowed them whole.

Inside, the city hit her like a memory with the furniture rearranged.

Ilsa Vey had been born forty miles from these walls. She'd trained in the capital barracks at seventeen, drunk in the Low Quarter at nineteen, marched in the King's Day parade at twenty-two with her chin up and her boots shining and her heart full of a loyalty so simple it embarrassed her now. She knew this city the way you know a childhood house: by the creak of its stairs, the smell of its corners. And everything smelled wrong. Not foul — Aldermarch's capital had never been sweet — but *muted*. No market shouting. No dogs fighting over scraps. No music from the taverns, which were open but quiet, patrons drinking in silence like mourners at a wake that refused to end.

And everywhere, everywhere, the bells.

They tolled the hour and the city stopped. Ilsa watched it happen from the mouth of Copperlane Street: a fishwife setting down her baskets mid-stride, a lamplighter freezing with his pole half-raised, two children standing stock-still over a game of knucklebones. Everyone bowed their heads. Everyone. For the length of the bell — twelve slow strokes — the entire street held its breath, and then exhaled all together and went back to living, quick and furtive, like animals grazing between a predator's passes.

"It's not devotion," Ilsa said quietly. They had turned the cart down a side lane toward the lodging Odji's contact had described — a smuggler's safehouse above a chandler's shop, if the man's directions held. "It's a curfew you can hear."

"Curfews end," Emrys said.

* * *

She saw her old regiment on the second day.

They'd spent the first day establishing the safehouse — a low room above the chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and secrets, run by a gap-toothed woman named Maren who asked no questions because questions cost money and answers cost more. The second day was reconnaissance. Emrys wanted the palace approaches mapped, the bell schedule timed, the ceremony routes learned. Ilsa wanted eyes on the garrison. Old habit. If trouble came, it would come wearing uniform, and she needed to know which uniform.

So it was almost funny, in the way a knife in the ribs is almost funny, when she found it on Palace Hill Road at midmorning: the Third Banner of the Aldermarch Regulars, drawn up in parade formation outside the Hall of Records, and her own old company — Fourth File, the Ironwolds — holding the left flank.

She knew them by their marching order before she knew them by their faces. Captain Dossel had always kept his tall men on the left. She counted the files out of pure reflex, the way she'd once counted them before every engagement, and her chest did something complicated and painful when the numbers came up short. War had thinned them. Or something else had.

Their uniforms were wrong, too. Crown black where the Regulars had worn the king's blue-and-silver since before Ilsa's grandmother was born. Black cloaks. Black facings. And stitched at every shoulder, a new device she didn't recognize: a closed eye inside a ring of ash-grey thread. She stared at it, and some animal part of her brain that had survived two campaigns supplied the translation without being asked. *An eye that does not open. A king who does not wake.*

Then Sergeant Bren Oakes turned his head, and Ilsa stopped breathing.

Bren. Who had carried her three miles on his back after Harrow Ford with an arrow through her thigh and a joke in his teeth the whole way. Bren, whose wedding she'd attended, whose daughter she'd held when she was two days old. Bren, who had wept at the funeral of every soldier he'd ever lost, and there had been many.

His face was clean-shaven, well-fed, and utterly empty. He scanned the crowd with the same smooth, trained blankness the gate soldiers had worn, and when his gaze passed over Ilsa — passed *through* her — she saw nothing behind it. No recognition. No suspicion. Nothing. A man she had trusted with her life, looking at her the way a shutter looks at a window.

She walked past him on legs that felt borrowed. She did not let herself shake until the corner.

"You knew them," Emrys said. It wasn't a question. He'd materialized beside her near a fruit stall, Wren perched on the cart bench watching the street with those too-old eyes.

"My company." Ilsa kept her voice level by main force. "Fourth File. The man at the front — Bren Oakes. I bled next to that man for nine years. He carried me off a field once. He—" Her throat closed. She swallowed past it. "He's gone, isn't he. Whatever's in the palace, it's in them too."

"Not necessarily." Emrys bought an apple he had no intention of eating, using the transaction to angle them both into the stall's shadow. "The king is worn directly. The soldiers — maybe it's simpler. Fear makes excellent armor for a usurper. You don't need magic to hollow a man if you can make him afraid enough to hollow himself." He turned the apple over in his scarred fingers. "But the ones closest to the throne. The ones who report straight up the chain. Those may be something else."

"Vane," Ilsa said.

"Vane reports directly to the throne." Emrys's jaw tightened. "And Vane is not empty. Believe me. I've seen what fills him instead."

Down the street, the bells began to toll. Eleven strokes, though the sun had barely cleared the rooftops — and Ilsa realized with a cold lurch that the count was wrong, that the schedule had changed since yesterday, that the bells were not marking time at all but *consuming* it, ringing more often, tighter, like a fist slowly closing. The whole street bowed. Across the square, so did Fourth File, every soldier dropping his head in perfect unison, Bren Oakes among them, and the sight of it — her proud, loud, profane company bowing to a bell — hurt worse than any wound she'd taken under the crown's colors.

When the last stroke faded, Wren spoke from the cart, so softly Ilsa almost missed it.

"It's counting down," the girl said. "The bells. It's getting ready to do something, and the bells are counting down to it."

Emrys and Ilsa exchanged a look over her head.

"How long?" Emrys asked.

Wren's white-streaked hair slipped loose from her scarf as she shook her head. "Before the next Ash Bell ceremony. The big one. When everyone comes to the palace." Her small hands gripped the cart rail. "I can't tell how far. But it's close. It's *soon*."

* * *

That night, in the safehouse above the chandler's shop, Ilsa sat by the window with her crossbow across her knees and watched the city glow.

No lights burned late in the capital anymore — she'd noticed that the first evening. Households banked their fires at dusk and slept early, obediently, the whole city folding itself into darkness like hands closing. Only the palace stayed lit, up on its hill, every window blazing gold against the night sky. A king who never rested, keeping vigil over a city forced to sleep.

Maren had brought word from her network while they ate: the Ash Bell ceremony would be held in four days. Attendance was mandatory for guild heads and district elders; voluntary for everyone else, which in this city meant universal. The king himself would appear on the balcony. The bells would ring one hundred strokes.

"One hundred," Ilsa repeated now, to the dark window. Behind her, Emrys sharpened his sword in slow, rhythmic strokes, and Wren slept curled in the corner like a fox, exhausted from a day of the city pressing against whatever door the god's voice knocked on inside her.

"One hundred strokes," Emrys echoed. "For a man who never wakes."

"Loyalty," Ilsa said. The word came out bitter as gall. "I gave this crown twenty years. I thought loyalty meant something — that it ran downhill as well as up. That the crown protected what served it." She turned the crossbow over in her lap, checking the string by touch. "You know what I keep seeing? Bren kissing that lead medal. Bowing to the bell. Empty as a drum. And I keep thinking — that's what it costs. Not death. Death is honest. It costs *this*. Serving something that eats you and calls it devotion."

Emrys set aside the whetstone. "Then we take it back."

"We?" She looked at him.

"You deserted for us, Ilsa. You crossed a country with us, bled at a bridge for us." His voice was quiet, and underneath the quiet was steel — the same steel she'd followed into bad ground a dozen times and would follow again. "Whatever happens in four days, you're not a soldier of that crown anymore. You're something better. You're one of the few people left in this city who remembers what loyalty was supposed to mean." He nodded at the sleeping child in the corner. "She doesn't need soldiers. She needs family with weapons."

Isla looked at Wren — at the white streaks in her hair, the shadows under her eyes, the terrible small weight of everything she carried — and felt the last knot of her old life come undone, quietly, painlessly, like a bandage finally pulled free.

"Family with weapons," she said. "Gods help us. That's the company motto now?"

"It'll do."

She almost smiled. Then, below them, in the sleeping street, a single bell began to ring.

Not the hour-bell. Not the palace. Something smaller, closer — the chapel two lanes over, whose bell had hung silent since they arrived. It tolled once, twice, a third time, and stopped.

And in the silence after, from every quarter of the city, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of doors opening — dozens of them, scores, all at the same moment — followed by footsteps in the streets below. Many feet. Moving in unison. Toward the palace hill.

Ilsa was at the window before she knew she'd moved. In the moonless dark, she could just make them out: figures in the streets, walking calmly, unhurried, converging from every direction like iron filings drawn to a lodestone. Ordinary citizens in their nightclothes. Shopkeepers. Fishwives. Children.

And at the head of the column passing below the chandler's shop, walking with the smooth, empty stride she knew too well — Sergeant Bren Oakes, fourth file, left flank, carrying a lantern he did not seem to see.

"Emrys," she breathed. "Wake the girl. It's starting tonight."
