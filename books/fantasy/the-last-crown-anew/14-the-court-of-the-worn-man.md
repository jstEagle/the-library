## 14. The Court of the Worn Man

The bells rang as they entered the city, and Emrys counted them the way a soldier counts arrows left in a quiver.

One for the hour. One for the king's rest. A third, softer, that seemed to have no purpose at all except to remind everyone within earshot that Aldermarch's sovereign slept soundly, slept always, slept so deeply and so long that three years of his reign had passed without a single witnessed waking.

"He doesn't rest," Ilsa had said, two nights before, in the smuggler's tunnel beneath a tannery that smelled of rot and lye. "That's the joke nobody laughs at. The bells ring *for* him, but he's never asleep. My old sergeant used to stand night watch outside the royal apartments before all this. She said you could hear him pacing. Every night. Pacing like a man counting the floorboards."

"And now?" Emrys had asked.

Ilsa had looked at her hands for a long moment. "Now there's no watch to stand. The Ash Guard took over the inner palace. Nobody's seen the king eat, drink, or bleed in three years. He just... appears. On balconies. In processions. At ceremonies." Her jaw had worked. "He appeared at my commissioning, back when I still believed in things. Shook every officer's hand. His grip was warm. That's what I remember most — how warm it was, like something freshly killed."

Emrys hadn't slept after that. He'd sat sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening while Wren slept curled against Odji's spare cloak, her white-streaked hair fanned out on the stone, her small chest rising and falling with the patience of someone who had already decided how she would die and was merely waiting for the date to arrive.

He'd promised himself, ten years ago in this same city, that he would never come back. Promises, he was learning, were the one currency Aldermarch devalued fastest.

* * *

They went in as pilgrims, because pilgrims were the one class of traveler the Ash Bells did not question. There were thousands of them — gaunt, quiet people in undyed wool who had walked for weeks to stand in the capital's great plaza and be near the king's rest. They carried candles they never lit. They spoke in whispers as though the whole city were a sickroom. And they filed past the palace gates each evening at dusk, in a procession that stretched for half a mile, to bow toward the shuttered windows of the royal apartments.

"Look at their faces," Ilsa murmured. She wore a pilgrim's hood low over her cropped gray hair, and her crossbow was broken down and hidden in a cart of dried fish, which she had complained about for two days straight on the grounds that it was an indignity no honest weapon should suffer. "They're not worshipping. They're *apologizing*. Like they've all done something wrong and only the king can forgive them."

"They have done something wrong," Emrys said. "They're alive. Whatever wears him hasn't finished being hungry."

Wren walked between them with her hand in Emrys's, and she did not look at the palace with fear. She looked at it the way Emrys had once looked at a wound that needed cleaning — knowing it would hurt, knowing it must be done anyway. She had not spoken since the undercity. She had eaten what she was given, slept when she was told, and watched everything with those too-old eyes, and Emrys had stopped trying to pretend to himself that he didn't know what she was saving herself for.

Three workings, Odji had said. The bridge had spent one.

The pilgrim disguise got them through the gates and into the outer court, where the real problem waited: the Ash Guard, checking papers. Not searching for weapons. Searching for names.

"Ilsa Vey," Emrys had said quietly, the night before. "Captain's credentials. Are they still valid?"

"They're valid," she'd said. "That's the rotten part. I never resigned. You can't resign from the Aldermarchi army — you can only desert, and desertion gets you a trial, and a trial requires a functioning court, and the courts have been closed since the king stopped sleeping. So officially, Captain Ilsa Vey is still in service. On extended assignment." She'd smiled without any humor in it at all. "Extended assignment. Gods. Ten years chasing bounties in a valley where nothing follows, and I'm the one who ended up on paper forever."

So it was Ilsa who stepped forward at the inner gate, Ilsa whose captain's seal opened doors that pilgrim's wool could not, Ilsa who announced — in a voice trained by twenty years of parade ground — that Captain Vey, returned from extended assignment, requested audience with the Lord Protector's office regarding matters of internal security.

Not the king. Never ask directly for the king. Asking for the king was either madness or treason, depending on the mood of the guard.

What they had not planned for was how quickly the answer came.

* * *

They were escorted. That was the first wrong thing. Audiences took weeks; petitions rotted in ledgers; that was how courts worked, how they had always worked. But within a quarter hour of Ilsa presenting her seal, a functionary in ash-gray silk appeared, bowed, and said that His Majesty would receive Captain Vey and her companions *this afternoon*, and would they please follow.

Emrys exchanged a glance with Ilsa. *It knows,* the glance said. *It's expecting us.*

"It wants to see the scar," Wren whispered, so quietly that only he heard it. Her hand tightened around his fingers. "Emrys. It wants to look at your oath."

The palace interior was beautiful the way a preserved corpse is beautiful. Every hall gleamed. Every tapestry hung straight. Servants moved through the corridors in perfect silence, and none of them — Emrys noted this with a coldness that surprised him — none of them looked up. Not at the guards, not at the visitors, not at anything. Their eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance, and they stepped aside with the fluid obedience of water finding its level.

"They're not drugged," Ilsa breathed. "I know drugged men. This is worse. They're *agreeing*."

The throne room doors were taller than the gates of most towns, black oak banded in iron, carved with the history of Aldermarch's kings going back nine hundred years. The last three carvings were newer, and wrong. The sculptors had run out of kings to depict and had begun instead on abstract work — spirals, mostly, descending spirals, like water going down a drain.

The doors opened without anyone touching them.

The throne room was vast and dim, lit by a hundred candles that burned without flickering, and the air inside was warm — that same terrible warmth Ilsa remembered from a handshake, the warmth of something freshly killed. Incense hung in it, thick and sweet, and beneath the incense was another smell, older and wetter, that Emrys refused to name.

The throne itself stood on a dais of white stone, and upon it sat the king of Aldermarch.

Emrys had expected a monster. Some slavering puppet-thing, some obvious horror. What he saw was worse: he saw a king. A tall man in robes of state, crowned, seated with perfect posture, hands resting on the arms of the throne with the ease of long possession. His face was dignified. His beard was trimmed. Everything about him was correct.

Except the eyes. The eyes were like shuttered windows in a house where someone had died — closed, yes, but closed *over* something, and behind the shutters, if you looked too long, you could feel movement.

And the crown. Emrys's stomach turned slowly as he understood what he was seeing. The crown had not been placed on the king's head. It had been *grown* there. The iron filigree sank into the scalp at the temples, and the skin had healed over it in smooth, shiny seams, and where the metal met flesh there was no boundary at all anymore, just a gradual transition from king to crown, crown to king, as though the head had been dipped in metal and pulled out partway.

"He's still in there," Wren whispered. She was trembling now, her whole small body, though her voice held. "Underneath. He's been awake the whole time. Three years. He's been awake inside his own head, watching, the entire—"

"Hush," Emrys said, and even his whisper came out ragged.

They crossed the floor. Their footsteps echoed, but the echoes came back wrong — delayed, doubled, as though the room were repeating their sounds back to itself, learning them. Fifty feet from the dais, a guard — or something shaped like a guard — gestured them to stop.

And the king raised his head.

The motion was slow and graceful and entirely wrong, like a marionette operated by someone who had studied human movement from a great distance and gotten almost everything right except the parts that matter. The shuttered eyes opened. There was nothing behind them. No pupil, no iris — just a pale, depthless gray, like frost on a pane of glass.

"Captain Vey."

The voice was the king's voice — deep, resonant, a voice built for commandments and coronations. But the cadence was off. Pauses fell in the wrong places. Words ran together and then separated too far, as though the speaker were translating into this language from some other tongue in real time.

"You have been away a long time, Captain. Extended assignment. How fruitful it has been." A pause. "You have brought guests. How generous. We do love guests."

Ilsa's face had gone the color of tallow. But she was Ilsa Vey, and twenty years of holding a line against panic surfaced in her spine, and she bowed — precisely, correctly, the bow of a subordinate to a sovereign — and said, "Your Majesty. I've returned with intelligence concerning a heretical vessel reported in the Godless Valley."

"We know," the king said pleasantly. "We have known for some time. Do introduce your companions, Captain. Manners cost nothing."

"This is—"

"No." The shuttered eyes slid off Ilsa and fixed on Emrys, and the warmth of the room seemed to gather itself around him, attentive, interested, hungry. "No, Captain. Let him speak for himself. Or rather—" the mouth curved, a smile assembled from the outside in, "—let us speak *of* him. Of the mercenary. The oath-man. The last sworn sword of the Quiet God."

Every candle in the room leaned slightly, as one, toward Emrys. Not the flames — the flames stayed upright. The candles themselves tilted, wax and wick bending a degree or two, the way flowers turn toward sun.

Emrys felt the scar itch. Then burn. Then sing.

"How—" His voice cracked on the word and he steadied it. "How do you know what I am?"

The thing wearing the king laughed. The laugh used the king's throat beautifully, warmly, the laugh of a beloved uncle at a festival table, and that beauty was the most obscene thing Emrys had ever heard.

"Know you? Little oath-man. We *made* room for you." The king leaned forward on the throne, and the crown's iron seams glistened. "When the Binding was woven, when your god consented to silence, when you and your little company of heroes pressed your hands to the stone and swore the world quiet — do you think we raged? We wept with gratitude. Every vow is a door. Every door has two sides. Your god locked itself behind one. And you—" the shuttered eyes brightened, if brightness could be called that, "—you kept the key against your skin all these years and called it a scar."

"The Binding was a tourniquet," Emrys said. His hand had found Wren's shoulder without his deciding it, drawing her half behind him. "Meant to be undone."

"Everything is meant to be undone. That is what makes undoing so delicious." The king rose from the throne — unfolded, really, joint by joint, tall beyond the tallness of the body he wore — and descended one step of the dais. The guards did not move. The candles bent further. "We have worn three kings of this line, oath-man. Before that, we wore a high priestess, and before her, a warlord, and before *that*—" the smile again, wider than the face wanted, "—we wore the first man who ever broke a promise to heaven. Do you know what we learned, across all those centuries of borrowed mouths?"

"That hunger doesn't teach," Emrys said. "It only eats."

"Close. So close. We learned that gods are *rude*." Another step down. Twenty feet now. Wren's grip on the back of his coat had gone crushing. "They speak and speak and speak — commandments, covenants, demands, dawn prayers, dusk prayers — a thousand years of noise, oath-man, until a man cannot hear his own heart over the choir. We simply asked the world a question your god never dared ask: what if the voice went quiet? What if mankind, uncommanded, might finally grow *up*? And your kind — your god's own faithful — answered for us. Sealed the heavens with their own hands. We have lived in the lovely quiet ever since, feeding gently, ruling kindly, asking nothing but obedience."

"The edicts," Ilsa said hoarsely. "The purges. The villages burned in the king's name—"

"Pruning," the king said mildly. "A garden must be pruned. But come, come — we are being poor hosts. You came all this way. Through snow and ford and fire and knife. Carrying our little vessel across half the world to us." The shuttered eyes dropped to Wren, and Emrys felt her flinch against his back like something struck. "Did you truly believe you were bringing her here to save the king? Child. Sweet vessel. We let you walk here. Every mile. Every inquisitor we recalled, every checkpoint we softened, every patrol conveniently blind — you traveled inside our patience the whole way, the way food travels inside a throat."

The floor seemed to tilt. Emrys reached for the truth of it and found it waiting, horrible and neat: the open gates. The instant audience. Vane's pursuit that had always pressed close but never quite caught them. Ten years of silence, and then a child arrives speaking the First Tongue, and within weeks every road leads here.

"You want the rite spoken," he said slowly. "Here. In this room. You *need* it spoken."

"We need the Binding broken, yes. From the inside, by a vessel, with a broken oath to season it." The king spread his hands, gracious, magnanimous, monstrous. "Your god locked a door and swallowed the key. We have starved in the dark ever since, nibbling at kings. Break the lock, oath-man. Unbind the voice. Let the god pour back into the world — and we will be standing in the doorway when it does. All that power, returning like a tide. And we will drink the tide."

"And Wren?"

"What of her? Vessels are made to be poured out." A shrug rippled down the king's borrowed frame, elegant and obscene. "She was always going to spend herself. The only question was whether her little life bought a locked door or an open one. We have merely arranged the more... efficient purchase."

Something in Emrys went very still and very cold, the way he went before a fight he expected to lose. Ten years of habit. Ten years of surviving without faith. And underneath all of it, deeper than the oath, older than the scar — the plain stubborn refusal of a man who has carried other people's costs his whole life and is tired, *tired*, of watching children pay them.

"You've made one mistake," Emrys said.

The king paused on the last step of the dais, head tilting with birdlike curiosity. "Oh?"

"You think the god swallowed the key." Emrys lifted his hand, palm out, and dragged back his sleeve, and let the oath-scar show — raw and burning in the candlelight, the mark that had itched for ten silent years. "It didn't. It gave the key to me. And unlike you—" his hand closed, fist tight against his heartbeat, "—I don't break promises. I *keep* them. Even the ones I hate."

For the first time in three hundred years, the thing wearing the crown of Aldermarch hesitated.

Then the shuttered eyes narrowed, and the sweet warmth drained out of the air, and every bell in the capital — every bell, all at once, unstruck — began to toll.

"Guards," said the king's beautiful dead voice. "Keep them *here*. Keep them *whole*. The vessel especially." The mouth curled one final time. "We will return for the breaking at the Ash Bell ceremony. Do try to survive until then, oath-man. It would spoil everything if you died of hope."

The great doors boomed shut behind them, and Emrys Fairweather stood in the dark of the throne room, holding a child and a broken kingdom between his two scarred hands, and understood at last exactly what kind of enemy he had sworn against:

One that had read his oath before he ever spoke it — and had been waiting, patient as famine, for him to finish the sentence.
