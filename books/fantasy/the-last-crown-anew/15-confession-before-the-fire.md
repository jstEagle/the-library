## 15. Confession Before the Fire

The undercity of Ash Bells did not appear on any map Ilsa had ever been issued, and she had carried maps for twenty years.

They reached it through a wine cellar behind a shuttered chandler's shop, down a stair that had been worn into a smooth trough by generations of feet that officially never existed. The smugglers who owned the passage — a broad woman named Ket with silver rings on every finger and her silent brother Doss, who communicated mostly by tapping the wall twice — asked no questions about the white-streaked child or the crossbow-scarred soldier. They asked only whether they were being followed, and when Emrys said no, Ket had shrugged and said, "Then you're either honest or careful. Both get the same room."

The room was a vaulted cellar of old foundation stone, older than Aldermarch itself, half-flooded with candlelight. Barrels of smuggled brandy lined one wall. A fire burned in a hearth that must have vented somewhere clever, because the smoke simply *left*, the way everything in the undercity left without trace. There were low cots, a table scarred with knife-marks and initials, and a ceiling so low Emrys could have touched it standing on his toes.

It was the first room in three weeks where he did not feel the need to sleep facing the door.

He slept facing the door anyway. Habit outlived its reasons. That, he was beginning to understand, was most of what a man was made of.

* * *

Wren slept like the dead for two days.

Odji's warning rode Emrys's shoulders the whole while: *three workings left in her*. He counted them the way a miser counts coins in the dark, over and over, hoping the total might change. Three workings between Wren and nothing. And ahead of them lay a throne room, a hollowed king, and a rite that Odji himself had called "a working beyond workings." The arithmetic did not forgive. It did not even negotiate.

On the second evening, Wren woke, ate an entire loaf of dark bread with honey, declared the undercity "smelled like a cave that ate a church," and then sat very still by the fire, watching the flames with eyes too old for her face. The white at her temples had spread. It framed her like early frost on a windowpane, delicate and terrible.

Ilsa cleaned her crossbow for the fourth time that hour. She always cleaned it when she couldn't say what she meant. Emrys had known her fifteen years; he knew the grammar of her hands.

"Say it," he told her quietly, in trade-tongue, not the First Tongue Wren sometimes slipped into. "Whatever you're oiling the stock about."

Ilsa set the bow down. She looked at him across the fire, and her face was the face she'd worn the day she'd told him their captain was dead and the war was lost and they'd all be fools to go home. Honest to the point of cruelty, because kindness without honesty was just a slower knife.

"You're going to tell her," Ilsa said. Not a question.

Emrys looked at Wren. The girl had gone still in the way she went still when she heard things other people couldn't — head tilted, listening to some frequency beneath the world. But now her eyes came back from wherever they went and settled on him, patient, waiting. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard. The First Tongue had words for truth the way rivers had water.

"Yes," Emrys said.

"Tonight?"

"Tonight."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "Good. Because if you don't, I will, and I'll enjoy it less than you think." She picked up the crossbow again. "And Emrys. Whatever it is — I've marched with you ten years and you've never once lied to me. Don't start with the person who matters most."

* * *

He waited until the smugglers' footsteps above had faded, until the bells of the city rang their hourly lie — *for the king's rest, for the king's rest* — and their echo died in the stone.

Then Emrys Fairweather knelt.

Not dramatically. His knees simply decided, and he let them. He knelt on the cold cellar floor before the fire, before the child, and took a breath that hurt going in like swallowing broken glass.

"I'm going to tell you what I did," he said. "All of it. And after, if you want me to carry you back to the valley and put you on a boat south and never see you again — I'll do that. I won't argue. I deserve whatever you decide."

Wren folded her small hands in her lap. "Tell me," she said, in the First Tongue, and the old words moved through the cellar like wind through wheat.

So he told her.

"I was twenty-two years old when the Godswar ended," he began. "You've heard the songs. The gods burning the sky over Corvale. The sea boiling off the Amber Coast. Three nations drowned in a single season because two gods quarreled over a river crossing, of all the stupid, human reasons." He watched the fire as he spoke, because looking at Wren while he said it would have stopped his mouth entirely. "What the songs don't tell you is how it ended. Not with victory. Not with a hero. With a bargain."

"The Binding Oath," Wren said softly.

"The Binding Oath." The words tasted like iron and old blood, the way they always had. "There were nine hundred of us in the last company — soldiers from every army, priests who'd abandoned their temples, people who just wanted the burning to stop. We gathered at a temple in the mountains. Your temple, though I didn't know whose voice I carried until you touched my scar. We swore an oath. Every god still living consented to it. Their voices would be bound — sealed away from the world — not destroyed, but *silenced*. Taken out of the mouths of kings and prophets and madmen. No more miracles wielded as weapons. No more gods reaching through mortal hands to strangle each other. The world would be godless, and the world would live."

He turned his arm over. In the firelight the oath-scar stood pale against his skin, a line of script in the First Tongue circling his forearm like a shackle.

"This is the receipt. Each of us who swore carries the oath written into the flesh. It's why magic can't follow me in the valley — the scar is a closed door. It's why my war-blessing has been failing for years. The Binding wasn't just sealing the gods away from the world." His voice caught. He pushed through it. "It was sealing them away from *us*. From every mouth that had ever held a piece of them. I gave up the voice of my god willingly. We all did. Nine hundred people stood in that temple and each of us became a lock."

"And the gods?" Wren asked. Her voice was very quiet. "Did they suffer?"

Emrys closed his eyes.

"That's the part I've never said aloud. Not to anyone." He opened his eyes and looked at her, because she deserved this much: his face, unhidden, saying the worst of it. "Your god — the Quiet God, the one whose voice you carry — came to me before the swearing. Spoke to me. Told me it consented. Told me the Binding was *its idea*, or near enough. That the gods had gone mad with grief and power, and the only mercy left was to take the power away from themselves." He shook his head. "But here is what I understood only later, what I spent ten years refusing to understand. Consent isn't the same as wanting. Your god walked into that silence the way a wounded animal walks into a trap it knows it needs. And I was the hand that set the trap. I held the door. I *swore first*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, the bells rang again, off schedule, frantic — *for the king's rest* — and fell silent mid-peal, as if embarrassed.

"I hid," Emrys went on. "When the Binding was done and the world didn't end — just went quiet — the others scattered. Some killed themselves within the year. Couldn't bear the silence where their god had been. Some founded orders, tried to keep the memory alive. Me? I found the one place in creation where I wouldn't have to feel it. The Godless Valley. Where the scar means nothing, where the silence is just weather. I built a cabin and I took bounty work and I told myself I was keeping my head down. Ten years, Wren. Ten years of telling myself I was surviving instead of hiding." He laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "A man can survive on habit the way a body survives on water alone. Long enough to forget he's starving."

"Why are you telling me this now?" Wren asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking, the way children ask, cutting past every layer of performance to the live nerve underneath.

"Because you're going to die for this."

The words came out flat and final, and Ilsa's crossbow-cleaning hands went still across the room.

"Odji says you have maybe three workings left. The unbinding will take nearly all of what remains. And I led you here. I'm leading you to it. So before you stand in that throne room and give whatever's left of your childhood to fix what my oath broke — you deserve to know that the man beside you built the cage you're trying to open. You deserve to know I'm not your rescuer. I'm the reason there's anything to rescue you *from*." His voice broke on the last word and he let it break. "I am sorry. I have been sorry for ten years in a language with no word for it. And I will spend whatever I have left making it weigh something."

Silence filled the cellar like water filling a hull.

Ilsa had stopped pretending to clean the crossbow. She sat motionless, watching the two of them across the fire, and her eyes were wet, though her jaw was set like a woman determined to be a wall and nothing else.

Wren unfolded herself from her chair. Bare feet silent on stone — she was barefoot again, always barefoot, the girl who had crossed a kingdom's worth of winter with nothing on her feet and fear nowhere in her — she crossed the space between them and stood before Emrys where he knelt.

Then she did something he did not expect. She did not touch his face, or speak comfort. She took his scarred arm in both her small hands, turned it gently palm-up, and pressed her lips to the oath-script like a knight kissing a ring.

The scar did not burn. For the first time since the night at his door, it *warmed* — a low, banked heat, like a coal remembered rather than felt, like a hearth in a house someone still loved enough to keep.

"He never blamed you," Wren said.

Emrys's breath left him in pieces. "You can't know that."

"I can." She looked up at him, and her eyes held that depth again — the frequency beneath the world, the voice that had guided her across a country while it faded with every mile toward silence. "I've been carrying his voice since before I could talk. Do you know what he says about you? When I ask?" Her thumb traced one character of the oath-script, slow, deliberate. "He says: *the soldier who swore first.* That's all. That's what he calls you. Not the jailer. Not the traitor. The soldier who swore first — because someone had to, and because he needed it to be someone who would understand what it cost."

"Wren—"

"He chose you *because* you would understand cost." Her voice firmed, and beneath the child's register Emrys heard — unmistakably, for one heartbeat — something vast and patient and grieved, moving under the words like a whale under ice. "*You thought he abandoned you. He thought he was sparing you. A god who commands is a god who owns. He wanted, just once, to owe a mortal instead. To be in debt to a man who kept faith without being forced. You weren't his prisoner, Emrys. You were his proof.*"

Tears — he hadn't wept in ten years, hadn't managed it, hadn't earned it — came now without permission. They came the way the tide comes, inevitable and indifferent to dignity, and Emrys Fairweather, mercenary, oath-swearer, coward, knelt on a smuggler's floor and wept like a boy while an eight-year-old held his arm in both hands.

"Proof of what?" he managed.

"That faith could survive the silence." Wren smiled, and it was a child's smile again, crooked and warm. "That a promise doesn't need a god behind it to be real. You kept your oath for ten years with no voice, no blessing, no reward. Nothing but the promise itself." She squeezed his arm. "That's what he wanted to know was possible. That's why the valley never freed you from it. You thought the scar was a chain. It was a question. And you answered it every single day."

Across the fire, Ilsa had given up entirely on being a wall. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way soldiers do, furious at her own eyes.

"So what now?" Ilsa asked, voice rough. "We know what he did. We know what she'll pay. We know Vane knows we're coming — he let us walk into this city too easily, and I don't like how easy it was." She stood, restless, a woman needing a mission the way other people needed air. "Tomorrow we find the smuggler routes into the palace district. Tonight—" she hesitated, then softened, which for Ilsa Vey was practically a religious experience, "—tonight we rest. Actually rest. All of us."

Wren yawned enormously, suddenly, jarringly eight years old again. "Is there more bread?"

"There's a whole barrel of things better than bread," Ilsa said, "and you're having bread anyway, because you're a growing menace."

* * *

Later, when Wren had eaten her fill and fallen asleep against Ilsa's shoulder mid-sentence, and the fire had burned down to a red patience, Emrys sat alone with the last of the brandy he didn't drink and the confession he finally didn't carry.

It was strange, the lightness. He had expected absolution to feel like forgiveness. Instead it felt like putting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing — not joy, exactly, but the sudden shocking absence of weight, and the discovery that his shoulders had reshaped themselves around it, and would take time to learn straightness again.

Ten years of silence. Ten years of believing himself abandoned. And the truth had been waiting in the mouth of a barefoot child the whole time: *you were his proof.*

He turned his arm in the dying light. The oath-script caught the embers' glow, and for the first time he read it not as a shackle but as it had perhaps always been — a covenant, a promise given flesh, a question asked in the oldest language there was:

*Will you keep faith when there is nothing in it for you?*

Every day in that valley, he had answered yes.

Ket appeared at the top of the stair, silent as smoke, rings glinting. She descended two steps and stopped, reading the room the way her kind read rooms — quickly, completely.

"You'll want to see this," she said quietly, and held out a scrap of paper. "My boy pulled it off a courier an hour ago. Palace district courier. Sealed with the king's own signet."

Emrys unfolded it. The writing inside was elegant, unhurried, and signed with a name he knew.

*I know you have the vessel. I know you are below the Chandler's Walk — you were seen at the ford, and I do not forget faces. Bring the child to the Ash Bell ceremony tomorrow at dusk. Come openly, come armed if you must, and no one else needs to die. Refuse, and I will burn the undercity street by street beginning at midnight. I helped make the Wearing, Fairweather. Do not imagine I cannot unmake a neighborhood.*

It was signed: *Serel Vane.*

Emrys read it twice. Then he looked up at the sleeping child, the fire, the friend holding vigil at his side, and felt the strange new lightness settle into something harder and calmer than he'd felt in a decade.

"He knows where we are," Emrys said. "He wants us at the ceremony tomorrow."

Isla was already reaching for her boots. "Then it seems," she said grimly, "we've been invited to our own ambush. I hate when they plan the party for us."

Above them, faint through the stone, the bells began to ring — and this time, they did not stop.
