## 16. The Unbinding Rite

Wren counted her remaining years the way other children counted sweets.

She did it in the dark of the smugglers' undercity, curled in a nest of wool blankets that smelled of lamp oil and river water, while the others argued in low voices across the room. Odji had said three workings. Maybe four if the god was merciful, and gods were never merciful — she knew this now, had learned it in the burning of the bridge at Night of Knives, when the voice had poured through her and taken its payment in a single hot swallow. Three strands left on the loom of her, maybe four.

The counter-litany would take all of them.

She lay with her hands palm-up against her stomach and felt for the silver strand inside herself, the one thin thread of light that remained after the temple, after the bridge, after the farmer on the road whose rotting leg she had healed without asking permission from anyone, least of all him. The strand was warm. It hummed when she listened hard enough, like a wasp asleep in a wall. *Little vessel,* it seemed to say. *Little door.*

"I'm not a door," she whispered to it. "Doors don't get to choose."

But that wasn't true either, was it? Doors chose which way they opened.

Across the room, Emrys stood with his back to the candle and his scarred hand pressed flat against a map of the capital, and Ilsa Vey sat sharpening a knife she had already sharpened twice, because Ilsa sharpened things when she was frightened, and Wren had learned to read the big woman like weather.

"The Ash Bell ceremony," Emrys was saying. "Every bell in the city rings on the hour. During the ringing, the whole court stands in the throne room. The king — the thing wearing the king — has to be there. It's the one hour it can't hide."

"It's also the one hour the hall will be full of guards," Ilsa said. "Full of consecrated fire, full of Vane."

"Yes."

"So we're not sneaking in. We're walking into a mouth."

"We're walking into a mouth," Emrys agreed, and his eyes went to Wren, the way they always went to Wren now, careful and heavy, like a man carrying something breakable he couldn't put down.

Wren sat up among the blankets. "I'll do it."

They both looked at her. They always did that too — looked at her as though she might say something different if they waited long enough, as though the child in her might outvote the vessel. But the child and the vessel had been having their arguments for months now, alone in the dark where no one could hear, and they had finally reached the same conclusion.

"Wren—" Emrys started.

"You need someone who can touch the crown and speak the litany at the same time." She kept her voice steady. She had practiced being steady; steadiness was a kind of armor, and armor was what you made when you couldn't afford tears. "It can't be Ilsa. She doesn't speak First Tongue. It can't be you. Your oath is broken — the words would tear you apart coming through your scar. Odji said so. So it's me."

"That's not a reason," Emrys said quietly. "That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic is a reason."

"Wren." He crossed the room and crouched in front of her, and up close his face was all its old scars and new worries, the lines around his eyes carved deeper than ten years of silence should have managed. "Odji said it would take nearly all of you. Do you understand what nearly-all means?"

"I'm eight," Wren said. "Almost nine. I understand dying better than most people. I watched my whole village learn it."

* * *

She hadn't told them everything about the night she ran. There were pieces she kept in the locked room inside herself, behind a door she had built the way Emrys must have built his silence: brick by brick, on purpose, because some things could not be carried in the open.

But tonight, sitting in the lamplight with the counter-litany written out before her in Emrys's careful hand — he had transcribed it from the temple walls, every glyph copied true, though he could not read them — she found the lock turning on its own.

"In the capital," she said, "before I ran, there was a room under the palace. They called it the Wearing Room. I don't remember arriving there. I remember waking up there. There were other children. At first there were eleven of us."

Ilsa's knife stopped moving.

"The men in gray masks fed us to the crown one at a time. Not all at once. They wanted it slow. They said the king's body would reject anything rushed." Wren turned her hands over and studied her palms, the ordinary child-palms with their small creases, hiding the old woman's hands that lived inside them, waiting. "I heard the others go quiet, one by one. And then the voice came — the real voice, the god's voice, the last piece of it the Binding hadn't swallowed — and it hid inside me. Inside the smallest one. Because the smallest one was beneath their notice."

"Wren," Ilsa said, very softly.

"They thought they'd caught all of it. That's the funny part. They hunted the god's voice for years, and it was sleeping in an eight-year-old girl the entire time, right under their bells." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a child's smile. "The voice told me to run. It told me about the valley. It told me about the man at the edge of the valley with the scar on his arm who had helped make the Binding and hated himself for it. It said: *he will understand cost.*"

Emrys had gone very still. "You never said it sent you to me."

"You never asked." She reached out and took his scarred hand, the one that had burned when she first touched it, and pressed it between both of hers. "It didn't send me to be saved, Emrys. It sent me to finish something. I'm the last vessel because I'm supposed to be the last. That's what vessels are *for*."

"No." His voice cracked on it, and she saw the terrible thing underneath his gruffness, the thing she had known since the snowstorm at his door: he loved her. He loved her the way you love a thing you have already failed once. "No, Wren. A god doesn't get to spend a child like coin—"

"It's not spending." She squeezed his hand harder than a child should be able to squeeze. "It's *asking*. And I'm saying yes. Nobody made me say yes. That's the whole point. That's why it has to be me — because I choose it. The Binding was done *to* everyone. The Wearing is done *to* the king. Everything wrong in the world is things done to people without their leave." She lifted her chin. "So this will be a thing done by one. By me. Freely."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and earth, a bell began to ring — the evening bell, for the king's rest, though the king never rested. Wren felt the sound come down through the floor and into her bones, and inside her, the silver strand stirred and hummed.

*Tomorrow,* it whispered. *At the last bell.*

*Yes,* she answered. *At the last bell.*

* * *

She could not sleep, so she got up and walked the undercity.

The smuggler tunnels were older than the city, older than the kingdom, maybe — passages cut by hands that had needed hiding places before Aldermarch had a name. Candles burned in niches. People nodded to her as she passed, these strangers who had become, in two days, the strangest family yet: a one-eyed fence named Corvo who kept sweets in his pockets for her; twin sisters who spoke only to each other; an old woman mending nets in a tunnel that had never seen water, humming songs in a minor key.

Wren walked until she found Odji.

The hedge-healer sat alone in a side chamber surrounded by his jars and bundles, grinding something that smelled of bitter green. He didn't look up when she entered. "Can't sleep either?"

"No."

He gestured at a stool. She climbed onto it and watched his mortar turn. For a while neither spoke. Odji had a way of waiting that made waiting feel like company instead of emptiness.

"Tell me true," she said at last. "Not kindly. True."

His pestle stopped. "You know what's true."

"Say it anyway."

He set the mortar down and looked at her, and his face, which was usually all business and bandages, grew old and gentle. "Three workings left, I told him. Maybe four. This rite is not a working, little one. It's a working and a half, held open for as long as you can hold it, while the thing in the crown fights you the whole way. If you speak it fast, you die quick. If you speak it slow — if you fight for every word — you live longer and hurt more, and die anyway." He rubbed his eyes. "There is one chance in twenty that you set the litany down and walk away with a few grey hairs and a debt. Nineteen chances in twenty otherwise."

"Nineteen in twenty," Wren repeated. She turned the numbers over like stones. "Then I have one chance in twenty. Better odds than my village had."

"That's not comfort, girl."

"I know. It's better than comfort. It's honest." She slid off the stool and stood in front of him. "Odji. Will you do something for me?"

"If I can."

"If I fall — if the rite takes me all the way — don't let them bury me in the city. Take me home. To the valley. Plant me by Emrys's cabin, facing east, so I can see the sun come up over the boundary where the magic starts." Her voice wobbled, just once, and she steadied it. "And tell them it wasn't their fault. Tell them I chose."

The old healer bowed his head. When he raised it again his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them. "I'll plant you myself, little vessel. And I'll curse that god every day of my life for asking."

"He isn't asking," Wren said. "I am."

* * *

Dawn came down through the smugglers' grate like honey poured through iron.

They dressed her for it. Ilsa braided her hair — her white-streaked hair, the streaks wider now, spreading like frost across brown fields — and wound the braid tight against her skull so nothing could grab it in a crush of bodies. Emrys gave her his knife, the short one, the one he'd carried since the war, and showed her again how to hold it low and hidden, though they both knew steel would mean nothing where she was going. Corvo pressed a sweet into her pocket. "For after," he said gruffly, and wouldn't meet her eyes.

They gathered around the map one final time. Ilsa traced the route with a blunt finger: the pilgrims' gate, the colonnade, the servants' stair, the antechamber behind the throne. "Smugglers hold the undercroft doors. My old regiment holds the colonnade — half of them still owe me, and the other half owe me money. Once the bells start, we have one hour. One ring to reach the antechamber, one ring for Wren to cross the hall, and however many it takes after that for the rest of us to keep her alive."

"And if the trap is sprung early?" Emrys asked.

"Then it's sprung early." Ilsa met his eyes. "Vane expects us. We knew that going in. Courteous men always expect you; that's what makes them courteous."

Wren listened to all of it and let it wash past her like rain off slate. Plans were for people who expected to need them. She had a simpler inventory: one litany, memorized in a tongue older than kingdoms. One strand of silver. One yes.

When the preparations were done, Emrys knelt in front of her, and for a moment the hardened mercenary was gone and there was only a tired man with a scarred arm and ten years of guilt, looking at the last good thing he had ever been given.

"When this is over," he said, "when the king wakes and the usurper burns — you and I are going home to the valley. You and me and Ilsa. We're going to fix the roof, because the roof leaks. And in spring I'm going to teach you to fish, properly, not the way I fished, badly. And you're going to complain about it, and Ilsa is going to laugh at us both, and none of it will matter, any of it, except that we're together." His voice roughened. "That's the future, Wren. Hold onto it. Come back to it. Promise me you'll try."

Wren looked at him — at the man who had lied to inquisitors for her, who had broken a ten-year silence for her, who had unbound his own oath rather than see her spent — and she gave him the truth, because he had earned truth and nothing less.

"I promise I'll try," she said. "But Emrys — if trying isn't enough, don't you dare follow me. Don't you dare trade yourself for me. Swear it."

His jaw worked. Every line of him resisted. Then, because he was Emrys Fairweather, who kept his promises even when they cost him everything, he said: "Sworn."

"Good." She kissed his scarred cheek, where the skin was old and rough as bark. "Now we keep our oaths. Both of us. All the way to the end of the day."

* * *

They went up into the city as the morning bells began.

Aldermarch rang for its king every hour — *for the king's rest*, the criers said, though the king never rested — and today the bells rang louder than Wren had ever heard them, great brazen throats bellowing from every tower, shaking soot from the eaves and pigeons from the steeples. The streets flowed toward the cathedral square, toward the palace, a river of the obedient and the afraid, and Wren and Emrys and Ilsa moved inside that river wearing pilgrim gray, three drops of water in a flood.

Wren kept her head down and her hand in Emrys's and felt the silver strand singing louder with every step, waking, stretching, tasting the air of the city that had hollowed its king and hunted its god. It was not afraid. It had waited ten years in the Binding-dark and months in a child's chest, and it was not afraid, and so she borrowed its fearlessness the way she had once borrowed its healing, and walked on.

At the pilgrims' gate the guards barely glanced at them. In the colonnade, Ilsa peeled away toward her old regiment with a nod that carried twenty years of soldier's history in it. In the shadow of the antechamber stair, Emrys squeezed her hand once — *one ring, remember, one ring* — and let go.

Wren climbed the servants' stair alone, small feet silent on worn stone, the stolen knife cold against her ribs, the litany rising in her throat like a tide coming home.

Above her, through the ceiling, she heard the throne room filling: the shuffling of courtiers, the clink of armor, the dry whisper of robes. And beneath it all, another sound, one only she could hear — a vast and hungry attention turning slowly, like a lighthouse beam, searching the crowd it knew was coming.

The first bell of the ceremony began to swing.

Wren stepped out of the stairwell, into the light, and pushed open the door to the throne room.
