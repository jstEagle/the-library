## 17. Bells for the King's Rest

The bells began at dawn.

They came through the floor of the pilgrims' hospice like a sickness, one toll and then another and then another, each strike of the Ash Bell rolling down through stone and marrow until Emrys felt it in the fillings of his teeth. *For the king's rest,* the criers had called through the streets all week. *Rest for our worn father. Rest, rest, rest.* Three years of hourly bells, and the city had learned to move between them like water around stones — merchants timing their bargains to the intervals, children playing a game called *silence-catch* where whoever moved during the toll was out. Obedience worn so smooth it looked like love.

Emrys stood at the narrow window of the hospice attic, dressed in a pilgrim's undyed wool, watching the processional road fill with people moving toward the Grand Basilica. Tens of thousands of them. They carried candles they were not permitted to light, unlit flames being this kingdom's favorite prayer, and their faces in the gray morning light had the particular blankness of people who have stopped asking why.

Behind him, Wren slept on a pallet of straw, her white-shot hair spread across the pillow like frost creeping over green grass.

She had barely spoken since Odji's warning. She didn't need to. He'd seen her that last evening in the undercity, sitting cross-legged across from him, studying his oath-scar with the grave attention of a merchant appraising goods she already meant to buy.

"Three workings left," she had said, in that voice too old for her body. "And I only need one."

"You need none," he had said. "We find another way."

"There is no other way." She had reached out and laid two fingers on the scar, gentle as snowfall. "You broke your oath to free me from mine, remember? Don't you dare reforge one now just because it's heavier."

He had no answer for that. He still had no answer. He had spent the whole night rehearsing arguments and found them all made of ash.

Now he watched the crowds flow toward the basilica and did the arithmetic every soldier learns before a bad assault: how many guards at which points, how long the bells would ring before the ceremony proper began, how far it was from the western pilgrim's gate to the throne dais. Two hundred paces, give or take. Two hundred paces through consecrated ground, past the Court Guard, past whatever Vane had waiting, with a child who could barely carry her own pack.

Ilsa came up the ladder without a sound, which for a woman of her size remained a small ongoing miracle. She wore the gray of a discharged quartermaster, her crossbow broken down and hidden in a beggar's cart below, and she stood beside him looking out at the crowd with the expression of someone reading a battlefield.

"Smugglers are in place," she said quietly. "Twelve of Odji's cousins along the western colonnade, plus the ferryman's boys at the canal gate. If we can get Wren to the ambulatory door, they'll hold the way open behind us."

"And if Vane—"

"He knows." Ilsa said it flatly, without drama. "Look at the guard rotations. Doubled on the west colonnade, tripled at the ambulatory, but the pilgrim's gate is nearly bare. That's not carelessness, Emrys. That's an invitation written in soldiers."

Emrys had seen it too, and hated it, and walked into it anyway, because there was nowhere else to walk. "He wants us in the room."

"He wants *you* in the room. The usurper wants you. You heard what the thing said through its puppet mouth." She turned to look at him, and in the half-light her scarred face was all hard planes and old grief. "It knew your name before you gave it. Whatever bargain it means to offer, it's been cooking since before you crossed the valley line."

Below them, faintly, they heard Wren stirring. When they went down, they found her already awake, kneeling by the pallet, braiding her hair with hands that shook only slightly. Her knuckles were wrong somehow — not swollen, not bruised, just *old*, the skin finer than a child's should be, mapped with pale threads that glimmered when the light caught them.

She caught him looking and lifted her chin, daring him to say something about it.

"Bells started," she said.

"Aye."

"Then it's today."

"It's today," he said, "and you stay behind me the whole way, and when I say run, you run, and when I say stop—"

"When you say stop, I stop." She finished the braid and tied it off. Then, softer, in the First Tongue: three words he had learned by now to translate even without the god's voice to help him. *Together, or nothing.*

"Together or nothing," he agreed.

* * *

The Grand Basilica of Aldermarch had been built by men who wanted to frighten God Himself. Its vaulted ceiling rose higher than any mason had a right to attempt, ribbed in black granite and gold leaf, and the pillars along the nave were carved with the likenesses of dead kings, forty generations of them ascending toward the altar like a staircase of stone ancestors. In the old days — Emrys remembered this from his single visit as a young soldier, before the Godswar, before everything — the kings' eyes had been painted bright, and pilgrims had sworn the statues blinked.

Now the statues' eyes had been chiseled flat. Smooth. Blind. Someone had gone through the whole cathedral with hammer and chisel and erased every gaze in the building. It was the most honest architecture Emrys had ever seen.

They entered with the pilgrim tide through the western gate, exactly where the invitation lay open, and Emrys understood within ten steps why the trap was shaped this way. There was no need to stop them at the door. The crowd itself was the cage. Ten thousand bodies pressed shoulder to shoulder, all moving in the same direction, all chanting the same low litany — *rest, father, rest* — and any blade drawn here would be seen by everyone, and everyone would see the heretics who disturbed the king's rest.

Vane hadn't posted guards at the gate because Vane didn't need them. He had posted the entire congregation.

They moved with the flow, Ilsa slightly ahead, Wren between them, the smugglers drifting alongside in ones and twos according to plan. The air inside was thick with tallow smoke and incense and the sour smell of too many people too afraid to sweat openly. Above the altar, beneath a canopy of black silk, stood the empty throne — empty, Emrys noted, though the ceremony could begin at any moment — flanked by braziers already burning with the pale, smokeless flame of consecrated fire. Godfire. The real thing, or what passed for it now, hoarded by the crown for ceremonies and executions both.

His scar began to itch.

Not the ordinary itch of healed flesh, but the deep itch, the itch that had woken him some nights for ten years — the feeling of a word trying to be remembered. He pressed his palm against it beneath the pilgrim's robe and kept walking.

They were fifty paces from the ambulatory door when the bells stopped.

Mid-toll. The great bell simply choked off its note, and the silence that fell was so total, so sudden, that ten thousand people flinched at once, a shudder running through the crowd like wind through wheat. Into that silence, a voice spoke from everywhere at once — from the blind statues, from the rafters, from the throats of the trumpeters who had not yet raised their instruments:

*"Let the court assemble."*

The doors at the far end of the nave opened, and the Court Guard came through in column, halberds gleaming, parting the crowd with mechanical courtesy. And behind them, walking alone down the center aisle with his white gloves folded behind his back, came Serel Vane.

He was smiling.

That was the detail Emrys would carry afterward, more than anything else — that Vane smiled as he walked, a small, courteous, almost apologetic smile, the smile of a man arriving slightly early to a dinner party. His eyes swept the congregation once, unhurried, and Emrys felt those eyes pass over him the way a hand passes over grain, sorting, weighing, finding.

Finding.

Vane stopped twenty paces away, in full view of ten thousand witnesses, and inclined his head with perfect civility.

"Master Fairweather." His voice carried effortlessly, pitched for the whole nave to hear. "And the good sister Vey, whose desertion I regret to report has been formally noted. And—" his gaze dropped to Wren, and something in it softened into genuine, terrible tenderness, "—the little vessel herself. You honor us. We feared you would miss the ceremony."

Around them the crowd had gone rigid, heads turning, breath held. Somewhere a candle dropped and rolled.

"We came to pray," Emrys said.

"I know." Vane nodded warmly. "So have I. Every day for eleven years. You see, Master Fairweather, I expected you — I *hoped* for you — and I prepared accordingly." He raised one white-gloved hand, and the Court Guard moved as one, sealing the aisles fore and aft. "But please. Do not make me do this in front of the faithful. The throne room, if you would. The king is so looking forward to meeting your friend properly."

Every instinct Emrys owned screamed ambush. But the alternative was a massacre of civilians, and they both knew it, and Vane's smile said he knew that Emrys knew it.

Wren's small hand found Emrys's and gripped it. Her pulse was steady as a drumbeat. *Together or nothing.*

They walked.

* * *

The throne room swallowed them.

Beyond the great doors, the congregation's murmur cut off like a snuffed lamp, and the space opened vast and cold and echoing — the dais rising ahead under its black canopy, the braziers of godfire burning in ranks along both walls, dozens of them, throwing light that cast no warmth. Consecrated fire, wall to wall. A corridor of it. Anyone carrying the least spark of divinity would have to walk that gauntlet, and the fire would know.

Which was, Emrys realized coldly, precisely the point. Vane hadn't built a trap for smugglers and deserters. He'd built it for *her*.

At the foot of the dais, the king sat slumped in state — hollowed, vacant, the crown fused to his skull in a lattice of grown metal and bone, his chest rising and falling with the slow rhythm of a man three years asleep. The thing behind his eyes was not home, but something used the house. As they approached, the royal head lifted, and the mouth opened, and the voice that came out was layered like a chorus sung through a cracked bell.

*"Fairweather. You brought her after all. Sentiment is such a useful leash."*

"You knew we'd come," Emrys said. "Then you know why."

"I know what you intend, yes." The hollow king's lips curved. "*Counter-litany. How touching. Do you imagine the Binding holds her back, little thief? The Binding holds ME. She is merely the key you keep sharpening.*" The shuttered eyes fixed on Wren with an appetite that had nothing human in it. "*Give her to me, and I will be gentle. Refuse, and I will take her in front of your friends, slowly, and then I will still take her.*"

"Now," Wren whispered.

She stepped forward — and Vane moved.

The inquisitor had been standing aside with the patience of a stagehand, and now he simply raised his gloved hand, and every brazier along the walls roared up at once. Flames leapt eight feet high, white-hot, screaming silently, and the temperature of the room climbed in a heartbeat until Emrys's lungs scalded with every breath. A wall of godfire, continuous, cutting Wren off from the dais — cutting everything off from everything.

"The rite requires her touch upon the crown," Vane called over the roar, courteous to the last, "and the fire will permit no vessel to approach. I designed it myself. Forgive me, child. I truly did try to save the world once. I recommend surrender; it is less painful than heroism."

Emrys drew steel.

It was a stupid gesture and he knew it — sword against a wall of holy flame — but the sound of it leaving the sheath steadied something in his chest. Behind him he heard Ilsa's crossbow crank, heard the great doors boom as Odji's smugglers hit the outside of them, heard the first screams of the fight beginning in the nave beyond.

"Hold the doors!" Ilsa roared, to the smugglers, to God, to anyone listening. "Nobody comes through — *nobody!*"

Then she looked back at Emrys, one heartbeat, gray eyes bright as knife-points.

"Get her to the chair, Emrys. I'll mind the house."

And Emrys Fairweather — oath-breaker, mercenary, complicit ghost of a silenced god — charged the consecrated fire.
