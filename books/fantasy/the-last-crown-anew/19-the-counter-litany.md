## 19. The Counter-Litany

The throne room was burning and Wren was running through the middle of it.

She had never seen so much fire in one place that wasn't meant to warm anyone. It climbed the pillars in ribbons of blue-white light, consecrated flame that hissed where it touched stone and screamed — actually screamed, thin voices caught inside it — where it touched flesh. Somewhere behind her, near the great doors, Ilsa's crossbow spoke its flat iron word again and again, and men shouted, and Emrys —

Emrys was a shape moving through the fire ahead of her, sword drawn, coat smoking at the hem, carving a road toward the dais. He did not look back. That was how she knew he trusted her to keep running.

The crown sat on the head of the thing wearing the king.

Up close, past the last ring of braziers, Wren could see what the posters and rumors had only hinted at. The king of Aldermarch sat on his throne like a coat hung on a hook. His chest rose and fell because something made it rise and fall. His hands lay open on the armrests, palms up, as if he had been waiting ten years for someone to take them. And the crown — the Ash Crown, iron worked with bells no bigger than fingernails — had grown into him. Filaments of black metal threaded through his scalp like roots through soil, vanishing into skin gone gray as old candle wax.

His eyes were open. They were the worst part. Not dead eyes. *Shuttered* eyes — windows in an empty house where someone had nailed the boards from the outside.

*He is still in there,* the voice said.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did when it wanted to be gentle. The god-voice, the borrowed tide that lived in her chest and paid its rent in her bones. It sounded tired tonight. It sounded old.

*I know,* Wren answered, in the First Tongue, which no longer felt like a language but like breathing. *I know he's in there. That's why I'm here.*

She reached the foot of the dais and stopped, because the usurper stood up.

It wore the king's body the way a bad tailor wears a stolen suit — almost right, never quite. The shoulders were too square, the smile too symmetrical, and when it moved there was a half-second delay, like sound lagging behind lightning. It looked down at her with the king's shuttered eyes and smiled with the king's mouth, and beneath both, faint as a stain under whitewash, something else looked out.

"Little vessel," it said. Its voice was many voices braided together — a man's voice, a woman's, a chorus worn smooth by centuries of use. "You crossed my valley. You survived Vane. You burned three of my knives to ash and shadow." It descended one step. "I have eaten gods older than your alphabet. Do you truly think you can carry one?"

Wren's legs were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice weather — distantly, irrelevantly. Her heart was going like a rabbit's. She thought about Odji's hedge-healing hut and the smell of boiled willow bark, and the healer's long fingers counting years off her life like coins on a table: *three workings left, child, maybe four if you're lucky, and luck is not a currency I'd bank on.*

This was working number three.

"I'm not carrying him," she said aloud, in the common tongue, because some things needed to be said in words everyone could hear. "I'm giving him back."

Then she put her hand on the crown.

* * *

The metal was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Cold like deep water. Cold like the space between stars. The filaments woke beneath her palm — she felt them taste her, thread-thin tongues of iron probing for the door they knew was there, the door the Wearing had cut into her when she was small enough not to remember being cut.

She let them in.

That was the rite, in the end. Not a battle. A door held open on purpose.

She began the counter-litany.

The words rose in her without effort, the way tears rise — the First Tongue pouring up through her chest and out of her mouth in a voice that was hers and also very much not hers, a child's soprano doubled with something vast and patient and grieving. She heard herself speak the first line and understood it fully for the first time: *What was bound to stop the bleeding, let it be unbound when the bleeding stops.*

A tourniquet. The temple inscriptions had said it plainly, once Emrys stopped flinching long enough to listen. A tourniquet, tied in desperation at the end of the Godswar, meant to hold until the world stopped burning — and then stolen, tightened, turned from mercy into lock by the man who now wore kings like coats.

Her hair went white at the roots.

She felt it happen. Felt it as a coolness sweeping back across her scalp like frost climbing a windowpane, strand by strand surrendering its color. The pain started lower — in her knees, her knuckles, the small bones of her feet — a deep grinding ache, the ache of decades arriving all at once, settling into her joints like silt into a riverbed. Her veins lit beneath her skin, pale gold threads glowing from wrist to elbow, throat to temple, the god's voice made visible as it poured through her.

*Let the bleeding be ended. Let the seal be loosened. Let the silenced speak.*

One line for each bell on the crown. There were nine hundred and ninety-nine bells. She had counted them in her dreams.

The usurper grabbed her wrist.

Its grip should have shattered bone. Instead it recoiled — she saw why a heartbeat later: where its skin touched her glowing veins, it *smoked*. The litany was already loose in the room, already rewriting the rules, and the thing that fed on silence could not hold a voice without being scalded by it.

"You don't understand what you're unbinding," it snarled, and the chorus-voice cracked, and beneath the crack she heard the other thing, the true thing underneath: hunger. Enormous, bottomless, ancient hunger. "Your god consented to be quiet. It chose the seal. Break it and every power it carried comes back to a world that spent ten years learning to live without it — wars, witch-fires, the Godswar again, everything we—"

"*We?*" Wren laughed. It hurt to laugh; her ribs ached like an old woman's. "There's no *we*. You ate yours. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it took me eight years and two thousand miles to see it."

The Binding had been consent. She knew that now, knew it in her marrow where the god-voice lived. Her god had lain down under the knife willingly, to save the world, trusting someone to untie the knot afterward. The usurper's god had been devoured screaming. One was sleep. The other was murder.

And the murderer had spent ten centuries starving, wearing priest after priest, king after king, trying to fill a belly shaped like a god with meat shaped like men.

*Third line,* the god-voice murmured. *Hold the door, little one.*

She held the door.

* * *

Across the burning hall, Emrys Fairweather reached the base of the dais and found the usurper standing between him and the throne, and the usurper made him an offer.

She couldn't hear all of it. The litany was loud in her ears now, loud in her blood, each bell on the crown tolling softly as its corresponding line left her lips, and the whole room rang with a sound like the inside of a seashell. But she saw it. She saw the usurper lean close to Emrys, saw it cup one gray hand around his scarred forearm — the oath-scar, the receipt — and she saw Emrys's face do something terrible and tender, the face of a man being shown the exact thing he had mourned for ten years.

The offer. She didn't need the words. *Kneel, and hear your god again.* The bargain every hollowed servant had ever accepted. The reason the court was full of shuttered eyes.

Emrys drew breath. Wren braced for it — for the kneeling, for the collapse of everything, because she was nine years old and her hands were eighty and the litany was only at the two-hundredth bell and she could not hold the door alone forever—

Emrys broke his oath instead.

She felt it happen before she understood it. Every oath has a sound in the First Tongue, a single syllable like a struck nail, and his lived coiled around his scarred arm, silent since the valley. Now he seized it — she *watched* him seize it, jaw clenched, sweat cutting lines through the soot on his face — and snapped it in half.

The sound was enormous. Not loud. *Deep*, a note below hearing that made the flames bow sideways and the braziers gutter and every window in the hall spiderweb with frost. The scar on his arm split along its old seam and instead of blood there was light, thin and gold and furious, the same gold that ran in Wren's veins.

And Emrys opened his mouth and added his broken oath to her litany.

Not the words. He didn't know the words. He gave it something better — he gave it the *shape* of ten years of silence, the itch of the scar, the habit-survival, the shame, the guilt reframed, the whole weight of a man who had sealed a god away believing it was mercy and then doubted it every single day. All of it, offered up, folded into the tide of her voice like a stone dropped into a river — not blocking it, *feeding* it.

The counter-litany leapt forward.

Bells tolled in ranks now, hundreds of them, the little iron bells on the crown ringing themselves, and the filaments binding crown to skull began to glow and smoke and *retract*, root by root, withdrawing from the king's gray scalp like snakes pulling back from a dying fire.

"No," the usurper said, and for the first time its chorus-voice was only one voice, small and shrill and terrified. "No — I saved — I *saved* the world, I ended the burning, I—"

"You ended nothing," Wren said, and her voice came out doubled, tripled, the god speaking through her now as much as she through it, and it was not angry, it was *sad*, and the sadness was worse than any thunder. "You just kept taking. Give it back."

She wrenched her hand free of the loosening crown, planted both palms flat against the king's sunken chest — against the heart that had beaten ten years on borrowed rhythm — and spoke the final line.

*What was bound in love, let love unbind. What was kept in trust, let trust return. Let the silenced speak.*

The world broke open like a bell.

* * *

There was no explosion. Wren would remember that later, telling it to Ilsa over and over until Ilsa stopped wincing. There was no explosion, no shattering, no pillar of fire. There was a *tide*.

The god's voice returned to the world the way the sea returns to a tidal pool cut off from the ocean — not violently, but absolutely, irresistibly, filling every low place, every empty channel, every dry creek bed carved by ten years of silence. Wren felt it leave her and go everywhere at once. Out through the walls. Down through the foundations, into the earth, into the roots of things. Over the city of ash bells, where every bell in every tower began to ring on its own, not the hourly dirge for the king's rest but something wilder, gladder, a carillon rung by hands unseen. Out past the walls, past the fields, out to the Godless Valley itself — she knew it with certainty that bypassed thought — where magic seeped back into the one place it had been barred from, gentle as snowmelt.

And into the king.

The filaments withdrew the last inch. The crown lifted from his head — lifted *by itself*, rising, and then it came apart, nine hundred and ninety-nine bells scattering like a burst seedpod, raining ringing across the marble, and every bell that touched the floor went dark and ordinary and dead.

The king of Aldermarch collapsed forward into Emrys's arms, and he was *weeping*, great ugly sobs of a man surfacing from drowning, and he was old — so much older than the posters, thirty years added in one exhalation — and he was awake.

The usurper had nowhere left to stand.

Wren watched it come apart. Without the crown, without the worn king, without the silence to feed on, the thing unraveled like rope ends — the borrowed body sloughing away into gray ash, the chorus-voices thinning to whispers, and at the last, in the half-second before it finished coming apart, she saw its face clearly for the first time. Not a monster's face. A man's face. Very old. Very hungry. Very tired.

Looking, at the end, almost relieved.

Then even that was ash, and the ash was wind, and the wind went out the broken windows toward the mountains, and the throne room was only a room again — burning at the edges, littered with the fallen, full of smoke and ringing echoes and the sound of Ilsa Vey laughing and crying at once somewhere near the doors, her crossbow still clutched in both hands like she didn't trust the world to stay saved.

Wren tried to say something. To Emrys, maybe. To the god. To anyone.

Her knees went first. Then the gold light guttering under her skin flickered, steadied, flickered. She felt Emrys catch her before she finished falling — his scarred arm around her back, the split scar still shining faintly, and through her fading senses she felt the god settle into him for a moment, brief as a bird on a branch, and she heard it say — not command, not demand, just *say*, in a voice like the tide going out:

*Thank you. Both of you. Rest now. We will talk about what comes next — when you wake.*

*When,* Wren noted, through the gray rushing in. Not *if.*

That was the last thing she knew for a long time: the word *when*, and Emrys's heartbeat against her cheek, steady as a drum, and far above, through smoke and broken glass, the first honest sunrise the capital had seen in ten years.

Somewhere beyond it, in a valley where nothing used to follow, spring was already deciding to come early.
