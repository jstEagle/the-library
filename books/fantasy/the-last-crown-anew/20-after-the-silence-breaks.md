## 20. After the Silence Breaks

The silence, when it came, was worse than the screaming.

Ilsa Vey had spent twenty years learning to read the sound of a battlefield — the way it breathed between volleys, the way it held its breath before a rout or a rally. She knew every species of quiet: the quiet of men deciding whether to run, the quiet of the dying, the quiet after a wall comes down and no one yet knows if anyone is left beneath it.

This was none of those. This was the quiet of a room remembering how to be a room.

She lay where she'd fallen behind the overturned ceremonial brazier, one arm cradling her rebandaged side, crossbow spent, three of Odji's smuggler allies dead around her in a crescent she had drawn with her own hands. The great doors of the throne hall hung off their hinges, smoking at the edges. Consecrated fire still crawled along the banners of Aldermarch, eating the crowned stag stitch by stitch, and through the haze Ilsa could see the dais — could see Emrys Fairweather on his knees before the throne with his scarred hand pressed flat against the fused crown, and Wren beside him, small and blazing, her hair gone white as frost from temple to crown, her veins lit from within like a lantern made of skin.

Then the light went out of her all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and she folded.

And the thing that wore the king began to scream again — but this time there was nothing underneath it. No power. No hunger fed on thirty years of stolen divinity. Just sound, raw and animal, coming out of a mouth that had forgotten it belonged to a man.

Ilsa dragged herself up by the brazier's edge and watched it die.

That was the only word for what she saw, though *die* was too kind and too cruel by half. The shape that had been King Aldous of Aldermarch — the shape that had stood at ceremonies for three years without blinking, that had issued edicts written in a hand no scribe recognized, that had ordered children taken from their mothers because their blood sang wrong — that shape came apart like ash under a boot heel. Not the man. The man stayed. But the thing wrapped around him, the second skin of it, the *armor* of it, peeled away in ribbons of grey light that unraveled as they rose, thread by thread, until the vaulted ceiling drank them and gave back nothing.

What remained was small.

Ilsa hadn't expected that. She had braced herself for a monster's corpse, something vast enough to justify three years of dread and a city full of bells. Instead there was an old man collapsed sideways across his own throne, crown clattering loose down the steps, and he was — gods help her — *thin*. Thin the way men get when someone else has been eating their meals. His robes pooled around him like shed skin. His hands, when they came up to his face, were spotted and shaking and unmistakably human.

He was weeping. Great, graceless, hiccuping sobs, the kind Ilsa hadn't heard since her brother was four years old and had lost his dog in a snowstorm.

"Emrys." Her voice came out ruined, smoke-scraped. She tried again. "Fairweather. The girl."

Emrys didn't answer. He was already moving, catching Wren before her head struck the marble, gathering her up against his chest with a tenderness Ilsa had never once seen on the face of the hard-bitten mercenary who'd walked into Brackenfell looking like a man carrying his own funeral. He pressed two fingers beneath her jaw and went very still.

"Is she—" Ilsa started.

"She's breathing." His voice cracked clean down the middle. "She's breathing. It's shallow, but she's breathing."

*It's shallow.* Ilsa let her forehead rest against cold stone and did not pray, because she had stopped praying three years ago when the edicts turned strange and the regiment she loved started marching for a king whose eyes were shuttered windows. But she thought, very clearly and very stubbornly, in the direction of whatever god had just come flooding back into the world: *You owe her. Whatever you are, whatever you were, you owe that child everything, and I will collect.*

* * *

Getting out of the hall took longer than getting in.

The fire was dying now — consecrated flame guttering out as its purpose burned through it, leaving the banners blackened and the air thick with the smell of char and old incense. Ilsa made the circuit of the doors twice, checking the smugglers. Two were beyond anything; she closed their eyes and said their names aloud, because Odji's people believed names should be the last thing spoken over you by someone who wasn't family, and today, apparently, she was family. The third — a boy called Tam with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a donkey — was breathing. Barely. She bound his ribs with strips torn from a fallen inquisitor's cloak and left him propped where the medics would find him.

The inquisitors themselves were scattered across the steps of the dais like dropped puppets. Vane's strike team. Some were dead. Some were worse than dead — three of them simply *weren't*, not entirely, their outlines smudged the way chalk smudges when a palm drags across it. Ilsa looked at them for exactly as long as it took to understand, then looked away, because the understanding was Wren's doing and thinking about it too long put a cold hand around her heart.

Serel Vane himself sat against the base of a pillar, alive, one arm hanging wrong, watching her with those courteous grey eyes that had never once blinked at the right moments.

"You should have surrendered the child," he said. There was no rancor in it. That was the horror of Serel Vane — there never was. He spoke as a man discussing crop rotation. "Thirty years of order. Thirty years of a world that did not burn. And you've traded it for — what? A god's whisper? A king who will wake to find his reign a cage?"

"A king," Ilsa said, hauling Tam's weight onto her shoulder, "who gets to be *in* his own life. You can keep your order, Vane. I've seen what it costs. It's billed to other people."

She walked past him. He didn't try to stop her. Later — much later, in trial chambers lit by honest daylight — she would learn that he had watched the whole unbinding with tears running silently down his face, because he had genuinely, sincerely believed he had saved the world, and the world had just told him otherwise. But that was later. Now he was just a broken man against a pillar, and she had a child to carry.

Because that was the thing. Emrys had Wren in his arms, but Emrys was swaying on his feet, ten years of guilt and one night of war finally collecting their bill, and when Ilsa reached the dais he looked up at her with an expression she'd last seen on soldiers who'd held a line too long — the look of a man who cannot remember how standing works.

"Give her to me," Ilsa said.

"I can carry—"

"You've carried enough." She gentled it the best way she knew, which was not very gently at all. "You're listing, Fairweather. Hand her over before you both go down and I have to explain to a waking king why his rescuers are unconscious on his carpet."

He handed her over. And Wren was so *light*.

That was the detail that broke something open in Ilsa's chest, right there on the smoking steps of a liberated throne: how little the girl weighed. Eight, nine years old, and light as a bundle of dry kindling, her white-streaked hair plastered to her forehead, her small hands curled loosely at her chest as if even unconscious she was holding on to something. Her breath moved her ribs in tiny, uncertain increments. A silver strand glinted among the white at her temples — just one, catching the torchlight, the last bright thread of whatever color childhood had left in her.

Ilsa shifted her higher against her shoulder, felt that fragile breath fog warm against her neck, and made a decision so fierce it barely qualified as a decision at all.

*Not total,* she told the world, the god, the ashes, anyone listening. *Whatever this cost, it is not total. She breathes. I'm holding her. Therefore it is not total.*

* * *

They came out into a capital that was ringing.

Of course it was. The Ash Bells rang hourly for the king's rest — that had been the law for three years, a city trained like a dog to flinch on the hour. But this was not the hour. Every bell in Aldermarch was ringing at once, and not the flat disciplined tolling of ceremony either. Someone in the bell towers — pilgrims, probably, some of Odji's people among them, or maybe just bell-keepers seized by the moment — was ringing them wild, overlapping, joyous, a chaos of bronze that rolled down the streets and echoed off the ash-grey walls and sounded, for the first time in three years, like celebration instead of mourning.

The streets were full.

Isla had expected empty streets, curfew, fear. Instead the thoroughfares below the palace steps were packed shoulder to shoulder, and the crowd was making sounds she had no category for — half cheering, half keening, people on their knees in the gutters, people embracing strangers, a fishwife standing on an overturned cart screaming the news to a widening ring of listeners: *"The king! The king woke up! They say the king is awake and weeping!"*

And threaded through all of it — magic. Coming back unevenly, like water finding its old channels after a long drought. Ilsa felt it pass over her and through her without settling; whatever talent she'd been born without was still absent, thank the absent gods. But she saw it land elsewhere. A lamplighter stood frozen in the street as every lamp on his pole ignited at once without flint or oil, and he began to laugh, high and slightly unhinged. An old woman pressed her hands to a dead window-box and green things surged up between her fingers, blooming out of season, and she wept into the flowers. Somewhere a man screamed. Somewhere else a whole knot of pilgrims dropped their bundles and fled the square as if chased, and Ilsa understood — some people had built whole lives on the silence. Some people had *profited* from it. For them the bells weren't freedom. They were the sound of everything they'd arranged coming due.

A child ran past them laughing with a ball of light cupped in both hands like a captured firefly, and Ilsa pulled Wren's body closer, angling away, unreasonable terror spiking through her exhaustion — *not her, don't touch her, she's paid enough* — before reason caught up. The light-child was a stranger. The debt was settled. Or nearly settled.

Nearly.

"She needs a healer," Emrys rasped beside her. He'd found his feet somewhere on the stairs, and now he walked like a man wading upstream, one hand hovering near Wren's back without quite touching. "Not a hedge-healer. A real one. Odji—"

"Odji's meeting us at the smugglers' gate, and Odji already told us what a real healer would say." Ilsa kept her eyes forward, on the crowd, on the route. Left at the cistern, down the cloth-market stairs, third arch on the right. She'd memorized it in the temple while binding her own wounds, because planning retreats was the one prayer she'd never given up. "Every working takes years. We count what's left. So we count, and we don't waste a drop of it, and meanwhile she *breathes*, Fairweather, so walk faster and save your sermons."

He shut his mouth. Good. Twenty years ago she'd have saluted the man. Ten years ago she'd have bought him a drink and asked about the war. Tonight he was just a stricken father-shaped problem walking beside her, and she found, to her mild surprise, that she'd have cut down anyone in that jubilant crowd who slowed him.

The crowd itself parted for them without being asked. She saw it happen — people registering the white-streaked hair, the small unconscious form, the smoke-stink and blood, and understanding instantly, wordlessly, spreading open like water around a prow. Someone pressed a bunch of late roses into Ilsa's free hand and was gone before she could speak. An old soldier — she recognized the regimental tattoo, Third Foot, her own old company — fell into step beside them for half a block, saying nothing, just walking escort until the alley mouth, then touching two fingers to his brow and peeling away.

By the time they reached the smugglers' gate, Ilsa's arms ached and her side had soaked its fresh bandaging and she would not have traded a single step of the distance for anything in the restored world.

* * *

Odji met them in the undercity with lamps and curses and, miracle of miracles, a proper bed — a real feather mattress dragged from who-knew-where into a dry cellar that smelled of tallow and cardamom. The hedge-healer worked over Wren for an hour while Ilsa sat against the wall with Tam's head in her lap, keeping pressure on the boy's bandages with one hand and refusing, with her whole stubborn heart, to fall asleep.

Wren did not wake. But her breathing deepened. Color crept back into her lips. At some point she turned toward Ilsa's warmth without surfacing, the way children do, seeking the nearest solid thing in the dark, and Ilsa sat absolutely still for the better part of an hour so as not to disturb it, her legs going numb, not caring in the slightest.

Near midnight, Emrys came and sat down beside her. He smelled of smoke and consecrated ash. In his scarred hand he held two cups of something hot, and he passed her one without asking, and they drank in silence for a while — barley tea, terrible, blessed.

"The king's awake," Emrys said eventually. "Really awake. Old. Older than he should be — the thing took its toll in flesh too. He asked for paper and wrote his first edict in his own hand." A pause. "It's an apology. Three pages. To the whole kingdom. Starting with the families of the children the inquisitors took."

"And the council?"

"Being argued into existence as we speak. Half the nobles want to flee the city — the magic's got them frightened. The other half want to kneel to the god, and the god, as far as anyone can tell, hasn't asked anybody to kneel." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "First deity in history to return and immediately request fewer temples."

Ilsa snorted into her tea. Then, quieter: "Vane?"

"Alive. Under guard. The king insists he answers to trial, not to the sword." Emrys stared into his cup. "I think the king remembers what Vane was before. What any of us were before."

Ilsa looked across the cellar at the bed. At the small figure in it, white-threaded hair stark against the pillow, chest rising and falling with slow, steady, *stubborn* breaths.

"She'll wake," Emrys said. It wasn't certainty. It was a man laying his whole remaining faith on a table and daring the universe to take it.

"She'd better," Ilsa said. "I promised her I'd teach her to shoot. She laughed at my crossbow stance. Nobody laughs at my stance and escapes the lesson."

Emrys smiled — a real one, cracked and exhausted and new, like spring ice. And then his gaze went distant, unfocused, the way it had gone in the temple when the inscriptions spoke to him, and Ilsa realized with a small chill that he was listening to something she couldn't hear.

"What," she said. "*What is it.*"

"He's here," Emrys whispered. "The god. He's — Ilsa, he's asking me a question."
