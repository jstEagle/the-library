## 21. What the God Asked For

The god came at dusk, and it did not come as thunder.

Emrys had expected — had braced himself for — the old way of things: the sky splitting open, the voice that pressed a man's knees into the dirt whether he willed it or no. That was how the gods of Aldermarch had always arrived, in his grandfather's stories and in his own memories both. When the divine returned to the world, he assumed it would return the way a flood returns, indifferent to whatever stood in its path.

Instead, on the third evening after the Ash Bells fell silent forever, something knocked at the door of Odji's lean-to where Wren lay sleeping.

Not the door of Emrys's chest, where the god had always lived before the Binding. Not the scar. An actual knock — two knuckles against warped wood, polite as a neighbor asking to borrow salt.

He was sitting beside Wren's cot when it came, as he had sat every waking hour since Ilsa carried her out of that smoking hall. The girl looked like a candle burned down to its stub. Her hair was white from temple to crown, not the white of age but the white of ash, and her hands lay open on the blanket like two small fallen birds. Odji said she breathed. Odji said her heart beat steady as a millwheel. Odji said, in that maddening hedge-healer way, *the body keeps what the spirit spends elsewhere*, and then went off to tend Ilsa's bandages and leave Emrys alone with the worst kind of waiting.

So when the knock came, he nearly drew steel on it.

He crossed the little room in three strides and opened the door onto the last light.

There was no one there. Of course there was no one there. There was a figure — or the memory of one, or the suggestion of one, the way heat suggests water on a summer road. It stood at the edge of Odji's herb garden where the shadows pooled under the elder tree, and it was made of evening itself: the gold going out of the light, the first star trembling into being above its shoulder, the long blue hush that falls over fields when the work is done. If Emrys squinted his mind rather than his eyes, it resolved into something almost like a shape — tall, robed in dusk, with a face he knew the way you know your own handwriting without ever having seen it written down.

*You,* Emrys said. He didn't say it aloud. There hadn't been words between them for ten years, but the channel was still there, worn smooth as an old footpath.

*I,* said the god, and its voice was not what he remembered either. The old voice had been a bell, a commandment, a weight. This voice was low and roughened, like someone who had spent a long time shouting and then a longer time being unable to speak at all. *You kept my scar well.*

"You kept your distance well," Emrys answered, and hated how much of the boy he'd once been leaked into the words. "Ten years of silence while I carried your mark and your war-blessing and your name."

*The silence was mine to keep. You sealed it shut with your own mouth.* The dusk-figure tilted its head, and there was something in the gesture that might have been gentleness, or might have been the particular care of someone approaching a wound. *I have come to ask you something, oath-keeper. But first I would look at her.*

Emrys stood aside. It felt absurd, holding a door for a god, but some courtesies ran deeper than sense.

The presence moved through the doorway the way twilight moves through a window — all at once and without disturbing anything. It gathered by Wren's cot, and the lamplight bent toward it, and for a long moment neither god nor man spoke.

Then the god said, very quietly, in the First Tongue, a word Emrys had never learned and understood anyway:

*Little bell.*

"She rang herself hollow for you," Emrys said. His voice came out gravel. "Three workings left, Odji told us. She used them all in that hall, and more besides — she used the ones she didn't have. She paid until there was nothing left to pay with." He stepped forward, and found his fists were shaking, and let them shake. "So if you've come to collect something, if you've come to tell me this was your plan all along, if you've come to *demand*—"

*I have not come to demand.*

"—because I will tell you now, as I told the thing that wore the king: I am done being bound. I broke my oath on the throne room floor and I would break it again every morning for the rest of my life. Whatever covenant we had, it ended when I shattered the litany. You got your voice back through a broken promise, do you understand? Through the one act of faithlessness in forty years of faithful service. So don't stand there wearing the sunset and ask me for—"

*Emrys.*

Just his name. Just that. And it undid him more thoroughly than any thunder could have, because it was said the way Ilsa said it across a campfire, the way Wren had said it that night in the undercity with her small hand on his scarred palm. A name between equals.

He sat down heavily on Odji's stool and put his face in his hands.

*You misunderstand what happened in that hall,* the god said, after a while. *Or perhaps you understand it perfectly and cannot bear the understanding. Either way, hear me. The Binding Oath did not fail. It worked exactly as designed — as a tourniquet, a temporary stopping of the bleeding. What failed was everything built upon it. The first oath-breaker found the tourniquet and turned it into a leash. Your company gave the world ten years to breathe. The crown used those ten years to build itself a cage around heaven.* A pause, long as a held breath. *I consented to be silenced because I saw what the Godswar was making of my worshippers. I asked only that the binding be undone when the burning stopped. No one came to undo it. Until a child walked out of the capital barefoot in the snow, and a tired mercenary broke the only vow he had left.*

"That wasn't faith," Emrys said into his hands. "That was just — I couldn't watch her die for a lie."

*It was the truest act of faith I have witnessed in three thousand years,* the god said, *and I include in that count every prayer ever offered me by kneeling crowds. Faith is not keeping promises. Faith is knowing which promises must be broken, and being willing to carry the cost of the breaking. You carried it. She carried it. Now I have come to ask what comes next — and I find I do not know. That is why I ask.*

Emrys lifted his head.

The god stood in the middle of the small room, dusk-colored and patient, and it looked — he searched for the word and could hardly believe it — it looked *uncertain*. Three thousand years of divinity, ten years of silence, an eternity of being the answer to every question, and here it stood in a hedge-healer's sickroom asking a mercenary what it ought to do with itself.

"What do you mean, what comes next?" Emrys said slowly. "You're free. The world has its magic back — unevenly, Odji says, like frost melting off a field, but back. People are praying again. Half of them are terrified and half of them are singing. You can take up where you left off. Temples. Tithes. Commandments."

*And in thirty years, another king builds another cage. In a hundred, another war burns in my name. In three hundred, some other desperate company swears some other binding, and the whole wheel turns again.* The dusk-figure's edges stirred, like wind moving through wheat. *I was made to be obeyed, Emrys Fairweather. It is the shape of my nature, as fang is the shape of the wolf's. Every covenant I have ever sworn became a chain in someone's hand eventually — mine or theirs, it made no difference. I do not wish to be a chain again. I find that I do not know how to be anything else. So I ask the man who broke the chain: what now?*

Outside, somewhere down the valley, a nightjar called. Wren breathed. The lamplight steadied.

Emrys thought about oaths. He had carried one so long it had grown into his arm like a splinter working its way toward the heart, and he knew their anatomy better than most men knew their trade. An oath was a bridge built between two wills. The old covenants had been bridges of rope and iron — one side anchored in heaven, one in earth, each side able to haul the other across by force. That was why they all ended the same way. Any structure built to compel will eventually be seized by whoever holds the taller tower.

But there were other kinds of bridges.

"When Ilsa deserted," he said, "she didn't swear me anything. Didn't need to. She looked at Wren asleep in the cart and she said, *someone has to teach that girl to swear properly, and it won't be a god.*" He almost smiled. "When the smugglers held the doors in that hall — Dree and the others, the ones who died — nobody commanded them. They chose. And when Wren walked into that throne room, knowing the price, she didn't do it because you ordered her or because I begged her or because some litany compelled her. She said she was sent to finish it, but she *chose* it, god. That was hers. All of it was hers."

*Yes,* the god said softly.

"So here is what comes next." Emrys stood, and the shaking in his fists had gone quiet, and he heard himself speaking in the cadence of the old rites — the cadence of the Binding Oath itself — but changed, turned inside out, rebuilt. "No more bindings. No more vows that bind either party, no more commands carved in stone, no more tourniquets waiting to become leashes. Only this: promises, freely made and freely kept, between equals. You whisper; we choose whether to listen. We ask; you choose whether to answer. When either side breaks a promise, they say so plainly and pay the cost openly, and no one seizes anyone else's power to punish it. A covenant not of iron but of *keeping*. Kept day by day. Kept because it's worth keeping. Renewed every dawn or abandoned every dawn, whichever the truth requires." He paused. "It's a weaker thing than what you had. It'll fail more often. Men are bad at keeping promises, and I suspect gods are worse."

*We are worse,* the god agreed gravely. *We have had longer to practice our failures.*

"Then it will have to be enough."

The dusk-figure regarded him for a long moment. Outside the window, the last light drained from the sky, and the stars came out one by one, and Emrys realized with a strange lurch that the god was drawing its substance inward — pulling the evening in around itself, condensing, choosing a smaller shape.

*I accept,* it said.

And then it knelt.

A god. Kneeling, in the First Tongue, in a hedge-healer's sickroom, before a mercenary with an empty scabbard and a scarred forearm. The gesture lasted only a heartbeat, and it was not worship — Emrys understood that with total clarity. It was the gesture of one equal acknowledging another's terms. It was the thing no god had ever done in the whole history of Aldermarch, and it cracked something open in Emrys's chest that ten years of silence had scarred shut.

*Rise,* he said, uselessly, hoarsely. *Get up. Don't ever—*

*One more matter.* The god rose, and its form had settled now into something nearly human-sized, nearly solid, wearing the shape of a traveler in a hooded cloak with nothing but starlight where a face should be. *The little bell. Come and see.*

They stood together over the cot. The god extended one hand — a hand now, fingers and all, translucent as smoked glass — and held it above Wren's sleeping face without touching her.

*Her debt was great. Greater than Odji knows. She spent not only her allotted workings but reached into the store of years she should have lived and had not yet earned — she borrowed against a future that did not yet exist. That is why she does not wake. Part of her is still out there, in the ledger, walking home.*

Emrys's throat closed. "Can you—"

*I can do one thing,* the god said. *Only one, and only because of the covenant you have just named. Under the old law, I could simply forgive — wave my hand and erase the account, and she would owe her rescue to my power, and my power would be a chain again before she was strong enough to notice. Under the new law, forgiveness must be *given*, by someone who has standing to give it. The debt was incurred in my service. By the oldest accounting, therefore, I hold claim to repayment — and I may release that claim, freely, as one equal releases another.* The starlight where its face should be bent downward, toward the sleeping girl. *I release it. Let the account read: paid in full, by Wren, who was sent, and who finished.*

The hand descended the last inch and touched — barely touched — Wren's brow.

Light moved under her skin. Not the fierce luminous veins of the throne room, not the terrible white fire of the counter-litany, but something slow and silver, like moonlight finding a stream. It traveled from her brow to her fingertips and back, tracing paths beneath her ash-white hair, and where it passed, Emrys watched — he would swear this to his dying day — watched a single strand of that white hair darken at the root. Just one. One thread of brown-black curling up from her scalp among all the silver, like the first green shoot through scorched ground.

*Her debt is paid down to this,* the god murmured. *One silver strand. She will carry it all her life — the color, and the memory, and the years she lent to a stranger's freedom. She will wake older than eight. Perhaps older than she will ever comfortably be. But she will wake, and live, and grow, and choose — every year of it hers, unowed.* The hand withdrew. *I could not make it less. Forgive me.*

"It's not mine to forgive," Emrys managed. "Ask her. When she wakes. Ask *her*."

*Yes,* the god said, and there was wonder in it, fresh and unguarded as a child's. *I will have to learn to ask. What a strange and excellent discipline.* It moved toward the door, and paused on the threshold, and the evening gathered to it again like a dog coming home. *One last thing, Emrys Fairweather. The scar on your arm. Would you have it healed? I can unmake it — the mark of the old covenant, the receipt of the broken vow. One whisper, and your arm is smooth as a boy's.*

Emrys looked down at his forearm, at the pale branching lines he had carried since he was nineteen years old and on fire with certainty. Ten years he had scratched it in the night. Ten years he had hidden it under sleeves and lies.

"No," he said. "Leave it. Itches like sin, but it's honest. It's the truest thing about me — I made a promise, and I broke it, and both halves cost, and I'm still here." He met the starlight face. "Besides. New covenant, right? Promises kept between equals. Somebody should remember what the old one looked like, so we don't build it twice."

The god laughed.

It was not a sound Emrys had expected — warm and cracked and startlingly human, the laugh of someone remembering how. Then the dusk poured away through the doorway and was gone, and the nightjar called again, and the room was only a room, smelling of herbs and lamp oil and healing.

* * *

Wren woke at dawn.

Emrys knew it before he opened his eyes — knew it the way you know rain coming, a change in the pressure of the world. He had slept in the chair beside her cot, upright, arms crossed, the way soldiers sleep, and he came awake to the sound of a small voice saying his name with the particular patience of someone who has said it more than once.

"Emrys. Emrys. Your beard has grey in it. When did that happen?"

His eyes flew open.

She was propped on one elbow, watching him, and his heart stopped and restarted all in the same terrible instant. She looked — she looked *older*. Not aged the way the wounded look old, hollowed and gray. Older the way a portrait looks older than its subject: her face had kept its bones but lost its roundness, the last baby-fat melted from her cheeks, leaving something fine-boned and grave and unmistakably, irrevocably a person rather than a child. Eight years old still, by the count of her years. Twenty, by the count of her eyes. Her white hair spilled over the pillow like frost, except for that single dark strand near her left ear, which she had already found and was twisting around one finger.

"I dreamed about the bells," she said. "All of them falling silent at once, like birds landing. And I dreamed someone let go of my hand right at the end, and I wasn't scared, because the letting-go was the point." She frowned at him, sharp-eyed. "You talked to it. Last night. I heard you even asleep. You talked to it for hours."

"Not hours."

"You talk in complete sentences when you're arguing with a god. Ilsa talks in fragments. You argue in *paragraphs*." She sat up fully, winced, pushed through it. "What did you tell it?"

Emrys rubbed his face. His hands weren't shaking anymore, and that was its own strange grief — ten years of tremor ending overnight, the body finally believing what the mind had decided. "I told it no more bindings. Promises instead. Whispering instead of commanding. Keeping instead of compelling." He hesitated. "It agreed. It knelt, Wren. I'm telling you, a god knelt in this room, and I think the world changed more in that moment than it did when the bells fell."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, twisting the silver-dark strand. Then: "Good. Because I woke up owing somebody something, I could feel it, like a stone in my shoe — and sometime in the night the stone went away, and I want to know who took it out and what they want for it."

"Nothing. It was released. Freely." He leaned forward. "Under the new rules. Which means—" he tapped the cot frame between them, "—if you ever meet it, you're allowed to say thank you, or say nothing, or tell it you didn't ask for the favor. All of it counts. All of it's yours."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Outside, the valley was waking — Odji's chickens, the creak of the well-chain, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of Ilsa's laugh from the cook-fire, rough and alive, and beneath *that*, something Emrys had not heard in the valley in ten years: a bird singing a note too pure, too sustained, a note with a blessing folded invisibly inside it, the way the world sang before the Godswar.

Magic, creeping back across the boundary like spring thaw. Reaching even here. Even to the place where nothing could follow — which, it seemed, was no longer quite true of anywhere.

"The god," Wren said at last, carefully, testing each word like ice on a pond. "Does it have a name? A real one, not the titles?"

"It didn't offer one."

"Then I'll give it one," she said, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old with twenty-year-old eyes, "when I decide what it's earned. That's how covenants work now, isn't it? Everybody gives something." She swung her legs off the cot, stood — swayed — gripped his offered arm with fingers thin as a wren's bones and strong as iron, and looked up at him. Up, still; he was relieved to note she'd have to look up at him for years yet. "First promise I'm making under the new rules: I'm planting something today. Something that grows. Over the old stone, maybe, or wherever you buried the pieces of it. And you're going to help, and you're going to complain the whole time, because that's how you love people, and I've figured that out."

Emrys Fairweather, oath-breaker, god-friend, opened his mouth to protest that he did not complain the whole time.

What came out instead was a laugh — cracked and startled and human — and beyond the window the blessed bird sang on, and far to the north, in a capital sweeping up its ashes, a council chamber waited for its first meeting, and an old king waited to testify, and a trial waited for Serel Vane, who had begun, they said, to pray.

But that was spring's business. This morning there was only the smell of Ilsa's porridge, the weight of a small hand on his arm, and the first promise of the new covenant, already kept.
