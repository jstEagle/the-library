## 22. The Last Crown Anew

Spring came to the Godless Valley the way forgiveness came to Wren — slowly, and all at once.

She woke on the first warm morning to the sound of water. The snowmelt had begun in earnest overnight, and the little stream behind Emrys's cabin — *their* cabin now, though no one had said so aloud; it was simply understood in the way that bread was understood, and firewood, and the third chair Ilsa had dragged home from a neighbor's barn — ran loud and bright over its stones. Wren lay in her loft bed with the blanket pulled to her chin and listened, and for the first time she could remember, the listening asked nothing of her.

The voice was still there. It would always be there now, faint as a hearth-fire heard through a wall. But it did not command. It did not *need*. It only murmured, sometimes, like an old friend humming while they worked, and when Wren hummed back — which she did more often than she admitted, in the First Tongue, under her breath, words that meant *dawn* and *enough* and *stay* — the murmur warmed.

One silver strand. That was what remained of her debt. Odji had checked in the winter, turning her hands over with those clever dark fingers, frowning at the single white thread that grew from her crown where the rest had gone brown again, or nearly brown, or brown enough. "You'll carry it to your grave," Odji had said, not unkindly. "But you'll carry it a long time."

Wren had decided, privately, that she liked it. It was proof. When she forgot — and some mornings she still woke reaching for hands that were eighty years old, expecting knuckles like river stones — she could touch the strand and remember that everything had been real, and everything had cost, and she had paid, and she was here anyway.

Downstairs, Ilsa was arguing with the stove.

"You are a *treacherous* piece of iron," Ilsa informed it, "and I fought a war for people who deserved it less." She had her sleeves rolled up, one arm buried to the elbow in the firebox, her gray-streaked braid threatening to catch a spark. The crossbow leaned against the wall by the door, as it always did, but it had gathered a fine soft skin of dust these past months, and neither woman had wiped it off.

"It's the damper," Wren said, swinging down from the loft. "Emrys fixed it before he left. You have to pull it toward you, not push."

"I know how a damper works, sprout."

"You pushed it."

Ilsa glared at her over her shoulder. The glare had lost all its teeth months ago; it was mostly theater now, a ritual they both performed because rituals were how families told each other *you're mine* without having to say anything so embarrassing. "Where's the big oaf?"

"Chapel road. He left at first light. He said—" Wren paused, savoring it, because this was the best part, better than the damper, better than the snowmelt. "He said to tell you the council riders would be through by noon, and if you wanted to be decent for once in your life, today was the day."

"*Decent.*" Ilsa extracted her arm from the stove and examined the soot on it with the expression of a general reviewing a catastrophic battlefield. "I'll have him know I own a shirt without blood on it. Somewhere. Probably."

They found it together, eventually — the shirt, folded at the bottom of Ilsa's chest beneath three cloaks and a spare quiver, smelling of cedar and old campaigns. And by noon, when the council riders came up the valley track with their banners furled against the mud-splash of early spring, Ilsa Vey stood in the dooryard in her clean shirt with her boots polished and her arms crossed, looking exactly like a woman who had never deserted anything in her life except perhaps good sense.

There were four riders. Wren knew two of them by name now: Magistrate Corrin Hale, round-faced and serious, who had traveled out from the capital three times already to sit at their table and drink their tea and ask careful questions; and a young herald whose name Wren had forgotten on purpose because he chewed too loudly.

Hale dismounted stiffly, bowed — actually bowed, to Ilsa, which made her shift her weight uncomfortably — and produced a document sealed in green wax.

"The council's formal notice," Hale said. "Read it aloud, if you please, Captain. It's meant for the whole household."

Ilsa broke the seal. Her eyes moved down the page, and Wren watched her face do something complicated — the mouth wanting to be stern, the eyes wanting to be wet, the whole arrangement settling at last into a crooked half-smile that Wren had learned to read as *I am deeply moved and will deny it under torture.*

"'By unanimous consent of the Council of Aldermarch,'" Ilsa read, "'the office of king is dissolved. The crown of Aldermarch — reforged, purified, and renamed the Circlet of Service — shall henceforth be worn by no sovereign, but borne by rotation among the council's elected members for terms of one year, that power may never again settle into any single skull.'" She snorted at that. "*Skull.* Someone on that council has a sense of humor. "'Further: Captain Ilsa Vey, late of the Third Regiment, is hereby restored to honor, her desertion struck from record, her service commended above all others in the matter of the Unbinding. Signed—'" Ilsa stopped. Her jaw worked. "Signed by all nine of them. Even the millwright. Even *Fennick*, the old goat, he used to steal my rations at Brackenfell garrison twenty years ago."

"He remembers," Hale said mildly. "He insisted his signature go beside yours."

Ilsa looked at the page a long moment. Then she folded it very carefully along its existing creases, tucked it inside her shirt, over her heart, and said, gruffly, "Well. About time somebody noticed," and went inside to put the kettle on, and if her eyes were shining when she came back out with the cups, nobody was rude enough to mention it.

The second item was smaller, and heavier. Hale drew out a second paper, thinner, official in a different way.

"The trial concluded last week," he said. "Serel Vane. You'll want to know how it ended."

The dooryard went quiet. Even the stream seemed to lower its voice.

"They gave him the same law he served," Hale continued. "That was the council's decision, and the former king's — the man insists on being called Aldous now, plain Aldous of the Marches — that was *his* insistence above all. No execution. No dungeon. Vane is to spend the rest of his life in the capital's records hall, under supervision, restoring every document the Wearing corrupted. Every forged edict. Every erased name. There were thousands. There may be tens of thousands. He began last Monday, and by all accounts he works from dawn until the candles fail, and he does not complain."

Wren thought about Serel Vane's face as she had last seen it — across the trial yard, pale and precise, watching the council with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already knew the sum of. He had not begged. He had not justified himself beyond the truth, which was somehow worse: *I believed I was saving the world,* he had said, in that courteous, chilling voice, *and I would choose it again, and that is why I am guilty.* The yard had shuddered. Aldous — thin, white-haired, weeping freely and without shame — had leaned forward in his plain chair and answered him in a voice like dry leaves:

*"So did I, once. Every morning, for three years. That is exactly why you must live long enough to learn what it cost."*

"I don't hate him anymore," Wren said. She was surprised to hear herself say it, but it was true, and true things had a way of walking out of her mouth these days whether she invited them or not. "It's like hating a locked door. He was locked. Somebody locked him a long time ago and threw away the key, and then he went around locking everybody else."

Hale studied her — the small girl with the single silver strand and the old, old eyes — and bowed again, deeper this time. "The council could use such wisdom," he said. "The seat for the valley remains open. Any season you wish to fill it."

"We'll think about it," said Ilsa, from the doorway, in the tone that meant *we will not think about it for at least five years.*

Hale smiled, saluted, and rode out. The hoofbeats faded down the valley track, and the spring took the silence back and filled it with birdsong, and Wren stood in the dooryard of her home and felt the day open before her like a book with all its pages blank and waiting.

* * *

Emrys came back at dusk, and he was not alone.

Wren saw them from the ridge where she'd gone to meet him — two figures on the chapel road instead of one, the second smaller, wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate. Emrys walked with his hand resting light on the stranger's shoulder, guiding rather than steering, and even at a distance Wren could see the particular set of his shoulders that meant *this matters.*

By the time they reached the gate she was running, boots throwing up mud, and Emrys caught her the way he always did, one arm, lifting her clean off the ground, his beard scratchy against her cheek, smelling of road dust and pine and the faint ozone sweetness that clung to him ever since the Silence broke — the smell of a man the god spoke to often and gently.

"Wren," he said, setting her down, "there's someone I want you to meet properly. You've met, in a manner of speaking. Every day since the bells. But you've never met *like this.*"

The stranger pushed back her hood.

She was ordinary. That was the first thing, and the strangest thing. A woman of middle years, weathered, with laugh lines and work-calloused hands and hair going gray at the temples in a pattern almost exactly like Wren's own silver strand. She might have been a farmer's wife, a ferry-keeper, a grandmother on a market day. Only her eyes were otherwise — not glowing, nothing so crude — but *present*, the way a window is present when someone stands behind it, looking out at you with complete attention and perfect kindness.

"Hello, Wren," the god said, in a voice like the stream behind the cabin, like snowmelt, like the space between bells. "You carried me a long way. I've come to help you put me down."

Wren's throat closed. She had spoken with the voice — *through* the voice, *as* the voice — in the throne room, in the burning, in the moment when her body was failing and the litany poured out of her like her own blood. But hearing it addressed to her, *to her*, small and warm and unhurried, was different. It was the difference between holding a bird and being sung to by one.

"You're staying?" she managed.

"For supper, if I'm welcome." The god glanced at the cabin, at Ilsa framed in the doorway with a wooden spoon held like a scepter. "Your captain makes a stew I have missed for ten years. I could smell it from the ridge. That is a joke," the god added, gravely. "I do not smell things. But Emrys tells me jokes are how this family says affection, and I am practicing."

Ilsa's spoon lowered. Her face performed its complicated maneuver — stern, wet, crooked smile — and she pointed the spoon at the gate. "Well, get in, then. And wipe your feet. Divinity or no divinity, I just swept."

They ate stew around the table that had grown, over the winter, from three places to four to five, because Odji came down from the hills some weeks and Hale came up from the capital others, and Ilsa kept saying *we need a bigger table* and never once saying *maybe fewer people*, which was how Wren knew the table would keep growing forever. The god ate nothing but held a bowl anyway, warming her hands on it, saying it was the participation that mattered. Emrys sat across from her and they talked the way old comrades talk — easily, with silences that didn't itch — and twice Wren caught them laughing about something that had happened during the Godswar, something terrible turned tender by thirty years of distance, and she thought: *this is what he lost. This is what he got back. Not commands. Company.*

Later, when the dishes were done and Ilsa had fallen asleep in her chair mid-sentence about the council's absurd new flag design, the god rose and stood at the window, looking north toward the deep valley.

"Come walk with me a moment, both of you," she said. "There's a thing I should return."

* * *

The oath-stone lay where they had buried it, at the edge of the dooryard, beneath a cairn of river stones that Wren had built herself, stone by stone, in the first week after they came home. Emrys's Binding Oath — the scar itself — had faded to a pale seam on his forearm, a line like an old healed break. But the stone on which the original covenant had been cut, the one he had pried from the ruined temple floor and carried home because, he said, *a receipt shouldn't be left lying around for fools to find* — that stone still bore the old words, the tourniquet-litany, the promise made in desperation and kept too long.

The night air smelled of thawed earth and new grass. Above them the stars were thick, and somewhere far off an owl asked its one question and got no answer and didn't seem to mind.

"She wants to plant something," Emrys said quietly. "Over the stone. She asked my leave, but it's your ground as much as mine now, sprout. More, maybe. You paid more."

Wren looked at the god, standing patient in the starlight with her work-calloused hands folded, waiting — actually waiting, asking permission, a power that had commanded armies once and now stood in a dooryard awaiting the yes or no of an eight-year-old girl. Something in Wren's chest went wide and warm.

"What kind of tree?" she asked.

"That," said the god, "is the whole question, isn't it? What grows from a buried vow?" She knelt, and pressed her palm flat to the cairn, and lifted the stones away with a gesture gentle as turning a page. The oath-stone lay dark in the earth, its carved letters faintly luminous, the old binding words still legible: *WE SWEAR THE SILENCE. WE ACCEPT THE COST. LET IT BE TEMPORARY.*

Temporary. It had taken ten years and a child with white-threaded hair to make the word true.

"An apple tree," Wren said. Decision arriving whole, the way true things did. "Apples mean somebody's planning to stay. Apples mean next year and the year after. You can't plant apples for a war."

For a moment the god was silent, and Wren felt the silence the way she felt sunlight — falling on her, weighing nothing, giving everything.

"An apple tree," the god repeated softly. "Ten thousand years of covenants written in blood and fear, and the vessel chooses fruit." She laughed — a sound like the stream, like the space between bells — and reached into the fold of her cloak and brought out a sapling that had not been there before: small, bare-rooted, its buds tight and green and utterly ordinary. "Then let it be the most ordinary miracle in the world. Let it need watering. Let it need *us*."

They dug together. Emrys dug, mostly, because his spade-work was still the best in the valley, but Wren laid the roots in with her own hands, spreading them over the buried stone the way you'd tuck a blanket over a sleeping thing, and the god packed the earth firm, and Ilsa — woken by the noise, grumbling, wrapped in a blanket and refusing to admit she'd been crying about the flag — held the lantern and offered unsolicited advice about drainage that everyone ignored.

When it was done, the sapling stood thin and hopeful against the stars, and the oath-stone slept beneath its roots, and Wren pressed her palms flat against the cool soil and felt, beneath the earth, beneath the stone, beneath ten years of silence and a war and a wearing and a breaking — the faintest pulse. Not a command. Not a need. Just a murmur, like an old friend humming while they worked.

*Grow well,* she hummed back, in the First Tongue. *We'll be here.*

* * *

Summer found the valley the way spring had — slowly, and all at once.

The apple tree took. Of course it took; the god had chosen the rootstock personally, and also, Wren suspected, had cheated slightly, though she'd promised not to, and when Wren accused her of it the god assumed an expression of profound innocence that fooled absolutely no one, least of all Ilsa, who said, "If that tree fruits by autumn I'm converting," and the god said, "To what?" and Ilsa said, "To whatever you're selling," and Emrys laughed so hard he had to sit down on the chopping block.

The council seat stayed open. Hale sent letters. Wren answered them herself now, her letters slow and careful and signed with a small drawing of a bird, and in the autumn she planned to travel to the capital for the first Rotation Day, when Aldous of the Marches would pass the Circlet of Service to the millwright Fennick, and the bells would ring — not hourly, not for anyone's rest, but once, for joy, because the city had voted on it, because bells belonged to everyone now.

And on a bright morning in high summer, Wren came out to the dooryard with the watering can and found the tree in bloom — out of season, impossibly, defiantly, a single branch of white blossom opened early just because it could — and she stood there a long time, touching her one silver strand, smiling at the small ordinary miracle that needed her.

Behind her, in the cabin, Ilsa was arguing with the stove again, and Emrys was laughing, and the god was humming, and the valley — the valley where nothing followed, the valley where nothing needed to — held them all, and let them stay.
