## 1. Ink Owed

The candle tells me when to stop.

Not the bell — the bell is the Order's instrument, and the Order's instruments lie the way scripture lies, which is to say beautifully and for reasons of their own. The candle is honest. When it gutters at three fingers' height, that's my allotment spent. Brother Edran says a scribe should work until the words are finished, not until the wax runs out, but Brother Edran has never owed anyone anything, or if he has, he paid it so long ago he's forgotten the taste of it in his mouth.

I have not forgotten. I keep accounts.

Tonight it's the Lamentations of Saint Vessil, forty lines of it, copied into a psalter for some minor noble family who will display it on a chain between two silver clasps and never open it again. The vellum is good — calfskin, smooth as a frozen pond — and my nib is new, cut this morning from a goose quill I bribed out of the kitchens with half my bread ration. The ink is Order ink, black as sin and twice as expensive, and every drop of it is logged against my account like everything else in my life. Ink, vellum, candles, bread, the roof over my head, the robe on my back: all of it entered in a ledger in the Bursar's office under a number that is supposed to be my name.

Entry 4471-Dunmore. Debtor.

I write *Blessed are the quiet* in letters so fine they could thread a needle, and I think about how many of these I'd have to write to buy back the years of my life that the Order says belong to them. The arithmetic is not kind. It's never kind. Arithmetic never is, if you're the one being subtracted from.

Let me give you the numbers, since numbers are what I'm good at. My father took the indenture when I was six — a loan against his labor, then against mine when his lungs went bad in the tannery fumes. The principal was eleven crowns. Eleven. You could lose eleven crowns in a gutter and not miss them. But the interest compounds at one part in twenty each season, and the Order's scribes are very precise about compounding, and by the time I was old enough to hold a pen the debt had grown teeth. It eats faster than I can feed it. I copy scripture twelve hours a day and the ledger grows anyway, patient as moss, because the interest is charged in labor-hours valued at a rate the Order sets and revises whenever it suits them, and somehow it always suits them to revise it upward.

Eleven crowns became four hundred hours. Four hundred hours became nine years. Nine years became — well. Last season's reconciliation, which I am not supposed to have seen but did see, because I reconcile the Bursar's own correspondence and he leaves things where a clever person can find them, put the remaining balance at seven years, four months, and eleven days of my life. I know it down to the days. Most people in my position would make themselves stop counting. I made myself count better instead.

There's a version of me, I sometimes imagine, who never learned to read. She sweeps floors somewhere, or mends nets, and she owes nobody anything because nobody ever trusted her with anything worth owing. She's free in all the ways I'm not and poor in all the ways I am, and some nights — the nights when the candle burns low and my wrist aches and the psalter blurs — I envy her with a purity that frightens me.

But she can't do what I do tonight.

* * *

After compline, when the corridors go dark and the brothers are snoring in their cells like a roomful of leaky bellows, I take out the other work.

It lives inside a hollowed psalter, which I find fitting. The Order taught me precision; the smugglers taught me what precision is worth. Twice a month, on no fixed schedule — schedules get people hanged — a folded scrap of paper appears wedged in the loose stone behind the cistern in the laundry yard, and if the mark on it is right, I leave a map in its place. Routes through the Thornfell passes, drawn small enough to hide under a thumbnail: which trails wash out in spring melt, which shrines have eyes, which stretches of the high road the garrisons patrol and when, where the customs men drink themselves stupid on shrine-wine and can be walked past blindfolded. I've never met the people who collect them. I don't want to. I know what happened to the last temple servant in Thornfell who got curious about his correspondents, and it involved a ravine and was described to me by someone who thought it was funny.

The coin comes wrapped in oilcloth with the paper. Not much. Enough. Every copper goes straight into the ledger against my account, hand-delivered to the Bursar's clerk with a face like a shut door, because the one thing the Order respects is payment. Pay them regularly and they'll overlook a great deal. That's not corruption, exactly. It's more honest than corruption. It's arithmetic.

Here is what nobody tells you about selling maps of smuggler routes to smugglers while living in a temple: it requires exactly the same skills as copying scripture. A steady hand. An eye for detail. The discipline to make the hundredth thing look as careful as the first. Brother Cassian says my lettering has "a stillness" in it, which I choose to take as a compliment, though with Cassian you're never sure whether you've been praised or diagnosed. He's my mentor — the word is too warm; he's the brother assigned to correct my work and occasionally my posture — a narrow man going grey at the temples with hands that smell permanently of lamp oil and a way of looking at you that makes you feel your pockets have been turned out even when they haven't been.

He caught me once. Early on. I'd left a practice sheet in my cell with contour lines on it instead of capitals, and he picked it up, turned it over twice, and said, "Interesting geography."

My heart stopped so completely I heard the silence in it.

"Practice," I said. "For illumination. Mountains are hard."

"Mountains are hard," he agreed, and set the sheet down, and walked away, and never mentioned it again, which was either mercy, blindness, or something I didn't yet have the wit to be afraid of. With Cassian it's usually the third one.

* * *

Tonight the candle is at two fingers and I'm finishing a route I'm quietly proud of — the Salt Cinder track above the treeline, the one that saves three days off the pilgrim road if you don't mind a drop on your left that would kill a goat — when I hear the evening bells start, and something in my chest goes cold before my head knows why.

The bells are wrong.

Evening bells come in nines. Always nines — Father Abbot's preference, some theology about the nine wounds of Vessil that nobody believes and everybody repeats. I've listened to those bells my whole life the way a sailor listens to weather. Tonight they ring seven times, stop, hesitate like a man forgetting a name, and begin again.

Someone is ringing them badly. Someone who doesn't know them.

I blow out the candle — instinct older than thought — and sit in the dark listening. The scriptorium below me is empty except for the drying racks and the smell of ink. Through the wall I hear footsteps in the cloister, uneven ones, the drag-and-catch rhythm of a person walking on a leg that has stopped agreeing to the job. They pass my door. They stop past my door. Then there's a sound like a sack of turnips being lowered respectfully to the floor, and after that, nothing.

I should stay in my cell. Every sensible voice I own — and I own several, they argue at night like a synod — says stay in the cell, bar the door, let whatever is dying out there die somewhere it isn't your problem. In the morning there will be brothers and procedures and the whole thick blanket of the Order to sort it out.

But the footsteps stopped *past* my door. And the stairwell outside the scriptorium's back entrance is the one nobody uses after compline except me, because I found years ago that its third step doesn't creak, and a person who knows which step doesn't creak is a person with secrets, and a person with secrets learns to recognize company.

I take the hollowed psalter off the shelf and slide it inside my robe, because I am, whatever else is true, a professional.

The stairwell smells of wet stone and, underneath it now, blood — that hot iron smell that the cold can't quite cover. He's slumped against the wall at the turn of the stairs, and even in the dark I can tell he's dressed wrong for a temple: riding leathers, no colors, boots worn through at the outer edge the way boots wear when a man has walked a long way in mountains rather than roads. Younger than I expected. That's the detail my mind grabs, stupidly, uselessly: *he's younger than me.*

His eyes find me in the doorway light I'm trying not to cast. He's been holding something pressed to his side, and when he moves his hand away it's dark and glistening, and I decide very firmly not to think about what I'm seeing.

"You're the scribe." His voice is a ruin, but there's command left in it, the way there's fire left in a banked stove. "Dunmore. The mapmaker."

"I don't know what you—"

"Don't." One syllable, and it costs him. "Don't waste my time lying. I've read your routes for two years. Nobody draws a pass like that who hasn't walked it in their head a thousand times." He fumbles at his belt, at a tube of oilcloth strapped there, and holds it out, and his hand is shaking so badly the tube rattles against the stone. "Take it."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it." Something that might have been a laugh turns into a cough, and the cough goes on too long, and when it stops there's blood on his lips and he talks faster, spending himself like a man paying out rope. "Undervault. You'll see it on the map. Everyone who sees it starts wanting things. That's how it works — that's how it *gets* you—" His breath tears. "Listen. The Order will come asking. The crown's people will come asking. Worse things will come asking. Don't let them read your name aloud. Whatever happens, however they ask — don't let them read your name."

"Whose name? Mine? Why would my name—"

But he's not answering me anymore. He's looking at me with an expression I will spend a long time failing to describe — not fear, worse than fear, the look of a man watching a debt come due on someone who hasn't been told about it — and his mouth works once, twice, shaping a word that never gets air behind it. Then his head tips back against the stone, and the shaking stops, and the stairwell is very quiet except for the bells upstairs finally finding their ninth stroke, late and ragged, like an apology.

I stand there longer than I should have. Long enough to notice that my hands are perfectly steady, which seems obscene. Long enough to understand that I have maybe a quarter hour before someone finds him, and that whoever comes looking for this man will follow the same logic I just did, and that the first place they'll look is the last place he stopped.

The tube is still in his outstretched hand.

I could leave it. God knows — whichever god bothers with the Quiet Flame — I could simply walk up the stairs, bar my door, and in the morning be Entry 4471 again, seven years and four months and ten days from freedom, minus one night's sleep. The sensible voices are all saying it at once now, a whole synod of them.

But I spent nine years learning to read what people write, and I know what it means when a dying man spends his last breath on a warning instead of a prayer. It means the warning was worth more to him than the prayer. And I know what it means when he presses a sealed map into the hands of a stranger whose only crime is drawing good passes: it means he believed the map was safer with me than anywhere else in the world, including wherever he came from.

That's not a gift. That's a transfer of debt.

I take the tube. It's lighter than it should be, the way dangerous things always are. Inside the oilcloth, when I check it with numb fingers in the dark, wax seals a rolled paper, and the wax bears a stamp I don't recognize — a circle broken by a vertical line, pressed deep, deliberate, like a signature.

Behind me, up in the dark of the cloister, a door opens. Footsteps. The measured, unhurried steps of someone who belongs here, walking toward a stairwell where a stranger is bleeding out on stones that belong to the Order of the Quiet Flame.

I fold the map into my sleeve, step over the dead courier — *sorry*, I think, absurdly, *I never even learned your name*, and then remember his warning and am glad I didn't ask for it — and climb the stairs to my cell with the candle of my life burning down to the wick, carrying, along with seven years and four months and ten days of debt, one more thing I owe to a man who can no longer collect.

Outside my window, far off in the black shoulder of the Thornfells, lightning flickers inside a cloud that has no thunder. Or perhaps it isn't lightning. The mountain keeps its own weather, my mother used to say, and keeps its own counsel too.

I bar the door. I do not sleep.
