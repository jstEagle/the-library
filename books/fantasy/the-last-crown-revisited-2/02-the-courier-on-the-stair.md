## 2. The Courier on the Stair

Evening bells in the temple of the Quiet Flame sound like a debt collector knocking twice.

I mean that literally, if you've never heard them. The Order rings them in pairs — *dun-dun*, pause, *dun-dun* — because Brother Aldous back in the third century decided that silence was holy and therefore bells announcing the hours of silence ought to be as brief and graceless as possible. Four hundred years later we all flinch at dusk and call it devotion.

I was copying the Litany of Ashes for the eleventh time that month when they rang. My candle had burned down to a thumb's width, which meant I had perhaps twenty minutes before the scriptorium master made his rounds, and I had exactly one more column of scripture to fake my way through and three columns of something else entirely to finish hiding under my pallet.

The something else was a map.

Not a holy map. The Order doesn't hold with maps generally — the Quiet Flame teaches that the faithful should be like ash, drifting where the wind of doctrine blows them, and a map is a document for people who have decided to blow themselves somewhere specific. Which is why the smugglers of Thornfell pay so handsomely for a scribe with steady hands and a flexible conscience. I'd been drawing their routes through the mountains for two years now: passes, waystations, which toll-houses took coin and which took things you didn't want to give. Each sheet bought down a sliver of my family's debt. At my current rate — I did the arithmetic every night the way other people pray — I would be free at forty-one.

Assuming nothing went wrong.

Something went wrong at the second peal of the evening bells.

* * *

The back stairwell of the scriptorium is a spiral of stone that smells of tallow and old dust, and it exists mostly so that novices can carry ink downstairs without crossing the abbot in the main corridor. I knew every step of it the way you know your own teeth. Which is why, when I heard the thud from below — a wet, heavy sound, like a sack of grain dropped by someone who no longer cared about the grain — I knew immediately that it was not a novice.

Novices drop things with apologies. This was dropped without one.

I should have gone back up. Let me say that plainly, because everything afterward grew out of that stairwell like a root out of bad soil, and I've had years to consider the version of me who simply climbed the twelve steps to his desk, finished his column of litany, and slept the sleep of a man whose worst problem is arithmetic. That Theo existed for about four seconds. Then curiosity — the same flaw that got me indentured, that got me moonlighting for criminals, that would eventually get me into considerably deeper water than a stairwell — put its hand on my shoulder and walked me down.

He was crumpled at the turning of the stair, half on the fourth step, half against the wall, and he'd bled enough to make the stone look polished. A courier's cloak, travel-stained gray, the kind sold cheap in Thornfell town to anyone who wanted to look unremarkable. He'd looked unremarkable his whole life, I imagine, right up until whatever caught him caught him.

His eyes found mine before I could decide whether to run.

"You're the scribe," he said. His voice was a whisper dragged over gravel. "Dunmore."

"I'm nobody," I said, which was both a lie and, in the eyes of the Order, an aspiration.

"Good." Something that might have been a laugh cost him a mouthful of blood. "Keep being nobody. It's worth — " A breath, ragged. "It's worth more than you think."

I crouched. I want to be honest about why: it wasn't courage. It was the cold professional assessment of a man who has spent two years around smugglers learning to price risk. A dying man in the stairwell was a catastrophe waiting for whoever found him. But a dying man who knew my name was a catastrophe already addressed and sealed, and the only question was whether I read the letter or burned it unread.

"Bleeding like this, you need a physiker," I said, uselessly, the kind of thing people say when their mind is doing anything but listening.

"The physiker's in the Order's pocket." His hand came up. It took visible effort, like a man hauling a rope from a well, and what was wrapped in it was a scroll — a tube of oilskin, wax-sealed, the seal pressed with a symbol I didn't recognize then. A flame inside a circle, but wrong somehow, the flame drawn as though it were falling rather than rising. "Take it."

"Take it where?"

"Don't take it anywhere." His fingers closed around my wrist, and for a dying man his grip was insultingly strong. "Take it *under*. You'll know when. Don't open it here. Don't let anyone — " another breath, thinner, "— don't let them read your name aloud."

"What does that — whose name? Mine?"

But his eyes had gone past me, up the stairwell, toward the scriptorium above, and whatever he saw there wasn't stone. "They're close," he said. "They follow debts. They always follow debts." And then, with the last of his voice, fast, like a man paying out rope before the anchor drops: "You signed. When you were small. You won't remember, but you *signed*, Dunmore, and they keep excellent — "

The bells finished. *Dun-dun.* Silence after, the holy kind, and his hand went slack around my wrist, and the only sound left in the stairwell was my own heart doing something undignified.

* * *

Here is what I did not do: I did not scream for help. I did not fetch Brother Anselm, who kept the keys to the infirmary and a face like a locked door. I sat on the stairs beside a dead stranger while the tallow smell mixed with the copper smell, and I thought, with a clarity that frightened me more than the blood did:

*They keep excellent records.*

I am a scribe. Records are my trade. I know better than most people alive what a written word can do to you — how a ledger entry can chain a family for a generation, how a signature can reach across thirty years and close around your throat like a cold hand. The Order's whole power rests on the principle that what is written cannot be unwritten. And this man, bleeding out on consecrated-adjacent stone, had told me that somewhere there was a record with my name on it. Signed when I was small.

I have almost no memories from when I was small. I told myself for years that this was ordinary — everyone's childhood goes foggy — but there are gaps in mine with edges. Clean edges. Like pages cut from a book.

The scroll sat between us on the step. Oilskin, wax-sealed, heavier than paper has any right to feel. I didn't touch it. Touching it made it mine. Not touching it made me the man who sat next to a corpse and did nothing, which had its own problems, but problems of conscience rather than consequence, and I have always been better at surviving those.

Then footsteps above. Measured ones. The scriptorium master's rounds came early, or someone else was walking them.

There is no version of this where I make a good decision, so I made a fast one. I picked up the scroll, tucked it into the front of my robe against my chest where it lay against my skin like a second heartbeat, and stepped over the dead man onto the lower flight, moving away from the footsteps, down into the dark of the undercroft where the Order stored its surplus candles and its surplus secrets.

Behind me, above me, I heard the footsteps stop.

I heard a voice say, quietly, in the tone of a man confirming an inventory item: "Here. One courier, expired." A pause. "Search the scriptorium. Find out who was working late."

* * *

The undercroft is not a good hiding place. It is a good delaying place, which at that moment was the entire difference between living and not. I folded myself behind the racks of spare candlestock and listened to boots cross the floor above, and I did the thing I do best, which is think too much, too precisely, about my own situation.

Facts. One: a courier had come looking for me specifically, in a temple he should never have known housed me, and had died on my stairs. Two: whoever killed him — and someone had; men don't open their own bellies climbing stairs — either knew the message reached me or assumed it hadn't. Three: the searchers upstairs were organized, unhurried, and spoke about a human being the way I speak about miscounted vellum. Four: *you signed.*

Five: my mother died eleven years ago of a lung-rot that the physikers said should have taken her in a season and instead lingered three years, fading and rallying, fading and rallying, like a candle someone kept relighting. She rallied the winter I turned nine. She rallied impossibly, against every prognosis, long enough to see me placed with the Order — placed, I was always told, by a benefactor who preferred to remain unnamed.

I had never thought hard about the benefactor. Children accept miracles the way they accept weather.

Now, crouched among candles that smelled of nothing because the Order renders them from memory-tallow — no, that's not true, they render them from ordinary fat, I only wish the truth were that interesting — now I did the arithmetic again. Not the debt arithmetic. The other kind. The kind where you line up facts you've spent years not lining up, and you watch them click together, and you understand that the sum total of your life is not the number you'd been carrying.

My name. *Don't let them read your name aloud.*

Why would a name need protecting from being spoken? Names are free. I write names all day — saints' names, donors' names, the names of the dead in the remembrance rolls. Ink costs less than bread.

Unless a name isn't just a name. Unless somewhere under the mountain — because where else, in Thornfell, does anything end — unless somewhere there is a contract with *Theo Dunmore* written on it in a child's uncertain hand, and speaking the name aloud is how such contracts get called in.

Boots again, closer. Descending.

I moved. I'll spare you the details of the next hour except to say that the Order's undercroft connects to the laundry, the laundry connects to the well-house, and the well-house has a window sized for exactly one underfed scribe with a guilty conscience and a scroll against his ribs. I have used that window twice in my life. The first time was for coin. This time I didn't yet know what it was for, and that frightened me worse.

* * *

I went back for my pallet-roll before I went over the wall, because even fleeing for my life, I am still the sort of person who packs. Under the pallet: three finished route-maps awaiting delivery to Maren Voss's people in Thornfell town, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing I actually couldn't leave — my own working copy of every route I'd ever sold, annotated, corrected, cross-referenced. Two years of my mind in ink. If I was going to run, I was going to run informed.

And underneath that, in a box I hadn't opened since I filed it away at fourteen, my mother's letters. All six of them. I took the box. I told myself it was sentiment. Sentiment is lighter than regret, and both weigh less than questions unanswered, and I was carrying all three up over the garden wall in the dark.

From the top of the wall I looked back once at the scriptorium windows, gold-lit, serene, the abbot's tower black against the first stars. Somewhere inside, men were searching my desk. By morning, Brother Cassian would learn that his most reliable copyist had vanished on the same night a stranger bled out below the novices' dormitory, and Cassian — old, sharp-eyed Cassian, who had taught me letters and, I sometimes suspected, larceny in equal measure — would draw whichever conclusion kept me safest. Or the one that endangered me most. With Cassian it was never possible to tell until afterward.

The scroll shifted against my chest as I dropped into the lane outside. Warm now, from my skin, as if it were something alive. As if it had been waiting a long time in the cold and had opinions about being found.

*You signed,* the courier had said. *When you were small.*

Tomorrow, I told myself, I would find a safe dark place and break the seal and learn what nine-year-old me had put his name to. Tomorrow I would decide whether to carry this thing under the mountain or bury it in a river. Tomorrow, tomorrow.

But tonight, walking the smuggler roads I knew better than the psalms, with the temple bells silent behind me and the mass of Thornfell black against the stars ahead, I noticed something that made my hands go cold on the wall-stone.

The seal's symbol — the falling flame in the circle — I had seen it before. Not on documents. Not on any map I'd ever drawn or sold.

In my dreams. For as long as I could remember, in the corner of every dream I'd ever bothered to recall on waking, drawn small and patient in the margins, the way a child doodles a thing they've been told to forget.

The mountain had been sending me correspondence for twenty years.

I just hadn't opened it until now.
