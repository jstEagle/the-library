## 4. Debt Collectors

The dead man's blood was still tacky on my hands when the enforcers arrived.

I want to say I handled it well. That I washed, composed myself, and greeted them with the serene blankness of a temple scribe who has never once thought about anything beyond his margins. But the truth is that I spent the hour between evening bells and their arrival standing in the scriptorium's back stairwell with a rag and a bucket, scrubbing at stone that had drunk deeper than any rag could reach, while my mind performed a series of increasingly inventive catastrophes.

*They'll find the body. They'll find the map. They'll ask why a courier died on temple stairs and you'll say something clever and they'll hang you from the bell tower by your own ink-stained fingers.*

The stairwell was cold. The candle I'd brought guttered in drafts that shouldn't have existed in a building this old, and every time the flame dipped I saw the courier's face again—grey as wet ash, lips moving around words that wouldn't come. *Don't let them read your name aloud.* He'd pressed the wax-sealed tube into my hands with the last strength he had, and then he'd looked at me with an expression I've been trying to forget ever since: not fear. Recognition. As if he knew exactly whose stairs he was dying on.

I told myself it meant nothing. Dying men see things. Dying men grab whatever is nearest.

I told myself a lot of things that night.

* * *

Brother Cassian found me on the third pass of the rag over the same clean spot.

He didn't announce himself—he never did; thirty years in the Order had worn his footsteps down to a whisper—but I felt him there anyway, the way you feel a change in weather. When I turned, he was standing at the top of the stairs holding a candle of his own, and the light caught the deep lines of his face and turned his eyes into two shadowed pools.

"You've missed compline," he said.

"I was—" The lie assembled itself out of habit, half-formed, transparent. "Copying. The Vespers marginalia needed—"

"Theo." He said my name the way he always did, gently, like setting down something fragile. "There is blood under your fingernails."

I looked at my hands. He was right. The rag hadn't gotten everything; there was a rust-brown crescent beneath the nail of my left index finger, and no amount of scrubbing would take it before morning. Before *them*.

"Someone died here," I said. It came out flat, which surprised me. "A courier. On these stairs. He gave me something and then he died, and I don't know what it was, Cassian. I don't know what to do."

For a long moment Brother Cassian said nothing. Then he descended the stairs—not quickly, his knees had been complaining for a decade—and crouched beside me with a soft grunt, taking the rag from my hand. He examined the stone. He examined my fingernails. He did all of it with the unhurried attention of a man who had once, I suspected, cleaned up considerably worse than blood.

"Did anyone see?" he asked.

"No. Evening bells. Everyone was at service."

"And the thing he gave you?"

"I have it hidden."

"Good." He folded the rag neatly, corner to corner, a scribe's habit. "Then hear me, child, because we will not have long. There are men in the guest wing who arrived at dusk wearing pilgrim grey and carrying swords under their cloaks. They asked Prior Anselm about a missing courier of the Thornfell road. The prior, being a prudent man, told them nothing and sent for me, because the Order's charter says questions about roads belong to the archivist's office, and I am the archivist." His voice stayed level, but something underneath it had gone very quiet and very sharp. "They will come to the scriptorium tomorrow. Possibly tonight. When they do, you will be copying scripture. You will know nothing. You will have seen nothing. And above all—" he took my chin in his ink-stained fingers, gentle as handling vellum, "—you will not offer information they have not asked for. Do you understand? Guilt speaks first. Innocence waits."

"Guilt speaks first," I repeated. It sounded like something worth embroidering on a cushion.

"Theo." He held my gaze until mine dropped. "Whatever you are carrying—I don't want to know what it is. But I know what you are. You are a boy who has been selling maps of smuggler roads to buy down his family's debt, and you have never once been caught, because you are careful and clever and slightly too proud of both. Tonight none of that will save you. What will save you is being boring. Can you be boring?"

"I can be devastatingly dull," I said. "I once put a visiting bishop to sleep mid-blessing."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the memory of one. Then it was gone, and what remained was the face of a man preparing to spend something he could not get back.

"Then go wash," he said. "And Theo—if they separate us, if they take you somewhere and ask you questions alone, remember: you owe the Order eleven years. Eleven years is a long time to survive on lies, but it is longer to survive on truth told badly."

He rose, joints cracking, and paused at the top of the stairs.

"One more thing. The courier. Did he say anything?"

My throat tightened. *Don't let them read your name aloud.*

"He said—" I hesitated, and the hesitation cost me, though I wouldn't learn how until much later. "He said the roads were watched."

Cassian nodded slowly, and I saw—just for a moment—the look of a man doing arithmetic with terrible numbers.

"Yes," he said. "They usually are."

* * *

They came at dawn, which I learned later is doctrine. Enforcers of the Order of the Quiet Flame arrive with the morning light because guilt, like mold, grows best in the dark, and dawn catches it fresh.

There were three of them. Two wore the flame-grey of the Order's disciplinary arm, faces like closed doors, hands resting near the short iron rods at their belts—punishment rods, not weapons, technically, a distinction that mattered only to lawyers and corpses. The third was different. Older, leaner, dressed in plain black with no insignia at all, which in the Order marked a man either as unimportant or as so important he needed no marking. He had the kind of face that made you want to check whether you'd said something wrong recently.

Prior Anselm received them in the scriptorium itself, which was either an insult or a message, depending on who you believed. Forty desks in ordered rows, forty brothers bent over their work, quills scratching in unison—a room built to prove that nothing here had ever happened, nothing here would ever happen, nothing here was anything but devotion rendered in perfect copies.

I sat at my desk in the fourth row and copied the Lamentations of Saint Verren for the eleventh time and tried to be furniture.

"This is Brother Cassian," the prior was saying, "our archivist. All matters of roads and correspondence pass through his office."

"We're grateful for his time." The man in black had a voice like dry paper turning. "Inquisitor Hale, on business of the Archive Division. We seek a courier in the employ of the Thornfell post—a man called Odric Vale. He failed to arrive at Highmoor station four nights past. His route passes within a league of this temple. Perhaps his papers came through?"

"They did not," Cassian said smoothly. "Our records show no courier traffic since the autumn rains closed the high passes."

Hale smiled. It was a small smile, economical, the kind that saved its energy for better uses. "The rains ended a month ago, Brother Archivist."

"Then our records show no courier traffic since the rains ended," Cassian said, without missing a beat. "We are a contemplative order, Inquisitor. Roads interest us chiefly as things to pray about."

One of the flame-grey men snorted. Hale ignored him. He walked the length of the scriptorium slowly, and I kept my eyes on my page and copied *the waters rise against me, O Lord, the deep calls to deep* and thought about how much I suddenly hated that verse.

At my desk, he stopped.

I felt him stop the way you feel a draft. My quill continued moving through sheer animal stubbornness, laying down letters I would later find nearly illegible from the pressure.

"You copy beautifully," Hale said.

"Thank you, brother." I pitched my voice low and humble. Boring. Devastatingly dull.

"For an indenture. How many years remain on your debt?"

"Eleven." The number came out too fast. Guilt speaks first. Damn Cassian and his correct proverbs.

"Eleven," Hale echoed. "Long time. And yet your hand shows none of the resentment such terms usually breed. Remarkable." He leaned in slightly, and I caught his scent—ink and something colder underneath, like a cellar. "What is your name, scribe?"

And here is where I nearly hanged myself, in the way that only a clever person can. Because I heard *name* and thought *danger*, thought of the courier's dying words, thought *don't let them read your name aloud*, and so instead of saying simply what any boring scribe would say, I paused. A fraction of a second. A hesitation with a shape to it.

Hale saw it. I watched him file it away behind those dry-paper eyes, some small slip of parchment added to some vast drawer.

"Theo Dunmore," I said finally. "Of the Dunmore debt, bound to the Order."

"Dunmore." He tasted it. "A common enough name."

"Common as rain," I agreed.

"Mm." He straightened. "Well, Brother Dunmore of the common name. If a courier should happen to stagger onto your stairs bleeding, you will of course report it immediately. The Order values obedience above all virtues. Even honesty." He smiled again, that small dry smile. "Especially honesty."

He moved on. I resumed copying. Somewhere in the row ahead of me, Brother Aldous's quill scratched steadily on, oblivious, and I envied him with my whole heart.

* * *

They did not leave at once. They lodged in the guest wing, ostensibly to rest before continuing their search along the Thornfell road, actually—any fool could see—to wait. For what, I didn't know yet. I spent the day in a fog of dread, producing the worst calligraphy of my career, and when the afternoon bells rang I sought out Cassian in the archive, that low vaulted room where the Order stored its ledgers of debt and sin in equal measure.

He was already waiting. He had a way of seeming to have been wherever you needed him, hours in advance.

"Well?" I asked.

"You hesitated."

"I—yes."

"He noticed."

"Cassian—"

"Theo." He set down the ledger he was pretending to consult. "Listen to me now, because I am going to tell you something I have told no one, and then I am going to ask something of you that I have no right to ask. Are you listening?"

"I'm always listening. It's my worst quality."

That ghost of a smile again, briefer than before. Then it faded entirely, and what he said next he said quietly, in the voice of a man reading from a document he wished didn't exist.

"When you were nine years old, your mother lay dying of the lung-rot in the tannery quarter. Your father had drowned the year before. Your family's debt to the Order stood at a sum no tanner's widow could ever pay, and the prior of that time had petitioned to have you and your sister taken into direct servitude—sold, effectively, to settle the account." He watched me. "Do you remember any of this?"

"I remember mother coughing," I said slowly. "I remember... candles. Someone coming to the house. I don't remember—" Something stirred, deep and old, like a fish turning in dark water. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the visitor who came to your house carried the seal of the Archive Division. Because whatever passed between your mother and that visitor, the debt was halved within the week, and your servitude was transferred to this temple, into *my* care, specifically requested, by name, by a division that does not make requests." He closed the ledger. "I have spent nineteen years keeping certain documents out of certain hands, Theo. I have done it because your mother asked me to, with her last breath, in this very building. And I have done it knowing that someday someone like Inquisitor Hale would come asking questions, and that on that day I would have to choose how much of myself to spend protecting you."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above us, footsteps crossed the floor of the guest wing—Hale's men, pacing, patient.

"What are you asking?" I said.

"I am asking you to leave tonight. Before they finish their tea and their patience. Take the road south—you know the roads better than any living soul, God forgive me—and do not stop walking until the mountain is behind you." He reached into his robe and produced a purse, heavy, and pressed it into my hands before I could refuse it. "This is not charity. This is an accounting. And accounts, Theo, must one day be settled."

"That sounds like a price."

"It is a price." He turned back to his ledger, and would not look at me. "You'll understand when you're older. Or you won't, which is also a kind of mercy. Now go. South road, before second watch. And Theo—" still not looking at me, "—whatever the courier gave you, burn it. Whatever it costs you to burn it, it will cost less than keeping it."

I stood there with a purse full of my mentor's savings and a question with teeth in it, and I said the only honest thing I'd said all day:

"I can't burn it, Cassian. I think it's why he died. I think throwing it away just makes it nobody's problem except everyone's."

Cassian was quiet for a long moment. Then, without looking up from his ledger:

"Then you'd best be gone before they read your name aloud."

* * *

I packed in twenty minutes: the map-tube against my spine, wrapped in oilcloth; three days' bread; the purse; a spare quill, because apparently even fleeing for my life, I am still a scribe. At the dormitory door I paused, looking back at the scriptorium's lit windows, at the rows of desks where I had spent nine years learning to disappear into beautiful handwriting.

Eleven years of debt. Nineteen years of Cassian's protection. One night's head start.

It didn't feel like escape. It felt like a bill arriving early.

I went out through the crypt door, the one only I and the rats used, and slipped into the dark below the walls—and behind me, faint through the stone, I heard the guest wing door open, and boots on stairs, and voices asking, politely, at each cell in turn, for the brothers to state their names.

They'd started at the bottom of the list.

Alphabetically.

Dunmore would not take them long.
