## 5. The Knight Without Yesterday

The first thing I learned about the day was that it was not the day I remembered.

I knew this because the day I remembered had been autumn, and the light on my face was high summer. I knew this because I remembered falling asleep in a barracks at Cael Rowan with my boots on and a letter from my sister unopened under my pillow, and I woke in a ditch full of standing water with my boots off and no pillow at all. And I knew this because when I sat up—slowly, the way you do when your body is trying to tell you something your head hasn't caught up to yet—the world tilted sideways, and somewhere behind my eyes three days simply weren't.

Not foggy. Not blurred. *Absent.* The way a tooth is absent after the puller's done his work. You can run your tongue over the gap all morning and it stays a gap.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to yourself is either madness or survival and I've never been able to afford the distinction. "Let's take inventory."

Inventory: one sword, lying in the ditch beside me like it had been placed there by someone considerate, which was already suspicious. One knife, still in my boot where it belonged. One set of clothes, soaked, mud-stained, and—this was the part that made me stop breathing for a moment—stiff in places with blood that was not mine. I checked. Thoroughly. Whatever else the last three days had taken from me, they'd left my skin unbroken, which meant the blood belonged to somebody who'd had a considerably worse time of it than I had.

And one sword. I said that already, but it deserves saying twice, because when I picked it up and turned it to the light, I saw what was notched into the edge near the guard, and I have been a soldier since I was fourteen years old, and I know every kind of damage a blade can take.

Steel nicks on stone. Steel nicks on bone. Steel nicks on other steel.

This was none of those. The notches were shallow crescents, five of them, spaced like fingers, and the metal around each one had a faint rainbow sheen, the way iron does when it's been touched by heat that shouldn't exist. I've seen dragon-scale exactly once in my life, mounted above the throne room in Vael Serrin, behind glass thick enough to stop a siege bolt, and even through the glass it had looked at me the way this notch was looking at me now: patiently, the way old things look at young ones.

There is no dragon. Everyone knows there is no dragon. There hasn't been a dragon in two hundred years; there's only the fire in the capital beacon that everyone pretends will burn forever, the way you pretend a candle guttering in a draft will last the night.

So either I had fought something wearing dragon-scale, or I had fought a ghost, or I had gone mad in a ditch, and of the three options I found the third most comforting and least plausible.

* * *

I walked to Thornfell because Thornfell was the only thing on the horizon, and because walking felt better than thinking, and because every other direction made something in my chest clench like a fist.

That last part took me an hour to admit. I'd start down the road toward the lowlands—the sensible road, the road toward garrisons and magistrates and people who could tell me what day it was—and my body would simply decline. Not pain, exactly. More like the feeling of walking against a river current, except the river was inside me. Turn south, and the current pushed back. Turn north, toward the mountain with its broken crown of cloud, and everything went smooth as oiled hinges.

Compulsion. That's the honest word for it, and I hate the honest word, so let's also use the dishonest ones: instinct, intuition, a hunch, a professional assessment of terrain. Any of those sound better than *the mountain wants me and I don't get a vote.*

A knight who can't trust her own feet is worse than no knight at all. A knight who follows them anyway is a dog on a leash, and I have been called many things in my career but never anyone's dog.

I stopped at the crossroads outside the village of Lower Marrow—yes, that's really its name, and yes, the people who live there are tired of the joke—and I stood very still in the middle of the road and said, out loud, to whatever was pulling me north:

"No."

The current pushed.

"I am Ser Wren Halloway of the Order of the Broken Lance, and I do not follow feelings. I follow orders, evidence, and occasionally bad ideas of my own choosing, but never—" the current pushed harder, a slow cold pressure behind the sternum, like a hand laid flat and patient— "*never* instructions from geography."

An old woman leading a goat gave me a wide berth. The goat seemed less judgmental.

Here is what I could reconstruct, sitting on the milestone afterward like a beggar, going through my own skull the way you go through pockets after a theft:

Three days missing. Before that, Cael Rowan, autumn, my sister's letter. I remember the letter because I remember deciding not to read it yet, which tells you it was probably about Father, and I wasn't ready, and I told myself I'd read it after patrol. Patrol. I remember mounting up. I remember the rain starting. And then—

Nothing. A gap with edges as clean as if a page had been cut out of a ledger with a razor.

Except. Except there were fragments, and fragments are worse than nothing, because nothing you can dismiss but a fragment keeps whispering. A smell of wet stone and hot metal. A voice reciting something in a cadence like a legal document being read at a funeral. And a feeling—not an image, just a feeling—of kneeling. Of kneeling on stone in front of something enormous and warm, and being asked a question, and the question being so heavy that even the memory of being asked it made my hands shake.

I didn't kneel. That's the one thing I'm sure of. I have knelt exactly twice in my adult life—once to receive my spurs, once at my mother's grave—and both times it was a choice, freely made, and I have built an entire self on the foundation of *it was a choice.*

Whatever I'd knelt to in those three days, I hadn't chosen it. I could feel that the way you feel a splinter.

* * *

Thornfell town smelled of tanneries and wet wool and, underneath it, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit going off in a closed room. I kept my sword wrapped in my cloak—I'm disgraced, not stupid; a woman in knight's kit with a notched blade gets questions, and questions get answers, and some answers get you hanged—and I bought bread and information with the eleven coppers in my pocket.

The bread was terrible. The information was worse.

"You're the fourth one asking after the mountain this week," said the innkeep at the Drowned Bell, a woman built like a barrel with opinions, wiping a counter that didn't need it. "First there was a courier fellow, all in grey, kept to himself. Then two men in temple grey, different grey, the Order kind. Then a scholar type with ink on his fingers looking at maps like they owed him money."

"What happened to the courier?"

"Gone." She shrugged. "Up the mountain, I'd guess. They always go up the mountain."

"And the others?"

"The scribe ran out of here two nights back like the hounds of heaven were after him. The Order men went after him." She leaned in, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman who'd been saving this. "And yesterday the crown's people came through. Inquisitor Ashgrave. Black gloves. You know what black gloves means?"

"It means he doesn't want to leave prints," I said, and she laughed like I'd said something clever instead of something true.

Black gloves. An inquisitor in a border town nobody cared about, chasing a runaway scribe. And before him, an Order detail. And before *them*, a courier heading up a mountain that had recently developed a strong opinion about where I should be standing.

I am not a clever woman by the standards of clever women, but I survived nine years of military politics, which requires a different kind of cleverness: the kind that notices when several unrelated dogs are all pointing at the same bush.

Something was in that mountain. Something worth an inquisitor's black gloves. And whatever it was, it had reached into a ditch forty miles away, put its hand flat on my chest, and said *come here*—and the fact that I couldn't remember agreeing to anything made it worse, not better, because things you agree to while unconscious tend to be the expensive kind of agreement.

I finished my bread. I paid for a room I didn't sleep in. And at midnight I sat on the bed with my sword across my knees and did the thing I'd been avoiding since the ditch: I held the blade up close to the lamp and looked properly at the notches, all five of them, and let myself think the thought underneath the thought.

Dragon-scale takes a notch out of steel only if the steel strikes it hard enough to matter. Hard enough to matter means a real fight, a committed stroke, the kind of blow you give something when you mean to kill it. Five notches. Five strokes. Somebody—or something—had swung this sword in earnest five times at a creature with dragon-scale, and the creature had walked away, and so had I, and neither of us had left a mark on the other except these little rainbows in the steel, like scars on a witness.

And beneath the notches, when I tilted the blade just so, there was something else. Scratched into the fuller, small enough that you'd need the lamp angle and a certain stubbornness to find it: a line of characters in no alphabet I knew, thin as spider silk, and around them the faintest warmth, like the memory of a fever.

I put the sword down. My hands were not steady, and I resented them for it.

"All right," I told the dark, the mountain, the current in my chest, whoever was listening. "You want me up there? Fine. But understand the terms. I walk because I choose to walk. I am not summoned. I am not leashed. And the moment this stops making sense—which, to be fair, gives us a generous margin—I turn around and walk out again, and whatever holds my leash can explain itself to the pointy end."

The current eased. Just slightly. Like something amused.

I hated that most of all.

* * *

I left before dawn, taking the shepherd track north because the main road felt too much like obedience. The mountain grew as I walked, the way mountains do, slowly and then all at once, until it filled half the sky and the other half was just its shadow. The sweet-wrong smell got stronger. Twice I passed shrines at the roadside—little stone alcoves, older than any faith currently collecting tithes—and twice I found fresh offerings: bread, a lock of hair, a child's shoe. Once, a jar sealed with wax, and I didn't look too closely at what moved inside it.

At midmorning I passed a milestone, and something about it snagged my eye, and I stopped, and stood there like a fool while my heart tried to climb out through my ribs.

Someone had scratched words into the stone. Old words, weathered nearly smooth, but legible to anyone who'd spent her life reading boundary markers and muster rolls and the occasional treasonous pamphlet:

*ALL ROADS UPWARD ARE PAID.*

Below it, newer, in a different hand, almost playful: *except the ones you walk for free.*

I stood there a long time. The current in my chest pulled north, patient as tide. Behind me lay the lowlands, my sister's unread letter, my disgrace, my name—which, I realized with a lurch I couldn't immediately account for, felt suddenly less solid than it had three days ago, less like a fact and more like a coat I was fairly sure was mine.

Three days ago I'd been a knight with a past. Now I was a knight with a hole in it, holding a sword that had bitten a dead god's hide, walking toward a mountain because my body had been conscripted, following a trail of offerings and scratched warnings and one vanished courier, and the only thing I owned outright—my pride, my stubborn, useless, load-bearing pride—insisted this was all my idea.

It was nearly noon when I heard footsteps on the track behind me. Light ones. Someone moving fast and badly, the way people move when they're more scared than tired.

I stepped off the path into the rocks, drew my knife, and waited to see who else the mountain had decided to invite.

They came around the bend at a stumbling half-run—a slight figure in travel-stained temple grey, ink-stained fingers clutching a leather tube like it was the last breath of someone they loved, glancing over their shoulder at a road that was, as far as I could tell, empty.

Empty, that is, until they saw me step out of the rocks with a knife, at which point their face performed a small, complete tragedy.

"Don't," they said, backing up, voice cracking. "Please. I don't have anything worth taking."

"Neither do I," I said, and heard how it sounded, and sighed. "That came out wrong. I'm not robbing you. I want to know why you're running, and where you got that map case, and whether you've seen anything on this mountain with scales."

The scribe—for that's clearly what they were, terror and ink stains notwithstanding—went very still. Their eyes dropped to my cloak, where the wrapped hilt of my sword showed its unmistakable curve, then rose to my face, and something in their expression shifted from fear to a strange, dawning recognition, as if they'd seen me before.

Seen me before. In the last three days. In the place where my memories should be.

"Well," I said, and the word came out flatter than I intended. "That's an interesting face you're making."

"You're the knight," they whispered. "From the shrine. You were—" They stopped themselves, jaw tightening, and I watched them decide, visibly, whether to tell me the truth, and watched them lose. "You don't remember."

"No," I said. "I don't. So you're going to walk down this track with me, slowly, and you're going to tell me everything I've forgotten, and if any of it involves me kneeling, I warn you now—I'll want details."

Behind us, far up the mountain, where the cloud broke for just a moment around a summit that hadn't been visible from the valley, something vast exhaled. Not wind. Not thunder. A long, low sound like a bell the size of a cathedral, rung once, in a language older than bells.

The scribe flinched. I didn't. And that frightened me more than anything else that day—because some buried, borrowed piece of me had heard that sound before, and had answered it, and had *kneeled*.
