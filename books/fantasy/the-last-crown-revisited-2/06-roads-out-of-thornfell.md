## 6. Roads Out of Thornfell

*Theo*

There are three ways out of a temple, and only one of them is the door.

I had spent six years learning the other two. The first was the crypt passage beneath the reliquary, which the brothers believed sealed since the founding and which I had unsealed at nineteen with a borrowed chisel and a great deal of guilt. The second was the laundry chute, which I mention only to establish that I have never been above indignity when my life depended on it. The door itself was watched tonight—two shapes in grey cowls flanking it like bookends on an empty shelf—so the crypt it was.

I went down the stair past the ossuary shelves, past Brother Aldous's resting place and the forty-seven others who had died owing the Order money, and put my shoulder to the stone that pretended to be a wall. It gave with a sound like a held breath released. Cold air came through, smelling of pine and night rain and, underneath, faintly, of ash.

Behind me, the scriptorium bell rang the ninth hour. Somewhere in that building sat my desk, my unfinished psalter, and eleven years, four months, and some days of debt recorded in a ledger whose ink I knew better than my own handwriting. Eleven years. I had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones.

Eleven years ago, I would have said, walking out. But the courier's blood was still under my fingernails no matter how I scrubbed, and his voice was still in my ear—*don't let them read your name aloud*—and the map was against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from my body as if it were alive and I were incubating something.

The tunnel ran forty paces and opened behind the tannery wall, where the drainage ditch carried the temple's shame down toward the river. I crouched in the ditch for a full minute, listening. Rain on leaves. A dog somewhere complaining about philosophy. No footsteps.

Then I ran.

Not toward the town gate—that would be watched by now; Ashgrave's people worked fast and Cassian's warning had been explicit—but up. North, along the pilgrims' path toward Thornfell itself, where the mountain stood against the stars like a door someone had forgotten to close. My own maps told me the smugglers' ways: the goat track behind the slate quarry, the ledge over Gallow's Drop that looked like suicide and was merely unpleasant, the fire-road that the charcoal burners used and the excise men ignored because bribing them was cheaper than fighting them.

I had drawn all of these. Sold them, too, which is the part I try not to think about—the routes I'd sold had carried things I preferred not to name, and the coin they'd earned had bought years off my family's debt, and both facts lived in me like tenants who refused to speak to each other.

The rain thickened as I climbed. By midnight I was soaked through, my scribe's hands—ink-stained, soft, built for holding quills not cliffs—were bleeding in two places, and I had made perhaps four miles. Four miles closer to a mountain that a dead man had warned me about with his last breath. If you'd asked me why, I couldn't have given you an answer that would survive daylight. The map wanted to go home. That was the truest thing I could say about it. And I was beginning to suspect that whatever part of me had signed something once, long ago, wanted to go home with it.

I stopped in the lee of a boulder to wring out my sleeve and that was when I heard, very distinctly, the sound of someone trying very hard not to breathe.

* * *

*Wren*

Three days ago I had been somebody. I was fairly sure of this because I had a sword with somebody's crest half worn off the scabbard, boots broken in exactly to my feet, and a body that knew seventeen ways to kill a man, none of which I remembered learning. Three days ago—or possibly four; the days had gone soft at the edges—I had woken in a ditch outside Thornfell with mud in my teeth and a hole in my head where my recent past should have been.

The hole had a shape. That was the worst part. It wasn't blankness, it was a wound, and wounds have direction. Mine pointed at the mountain.

So I walked. I resented every step of it. I am a practical woman, or I had been, whoever I'd been, and practical women do not follow compulsions like geese following a gourd on a string. I tried going south once, properly south, a full day's march toward the lowlands and towns and people who might tell me my own name. By evening I was walking north again with no memory of deciding to turn around, and my left hand had gone numb in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

Fine, I told the mountain. Fine. But we do this my way.

My way involved ambushes.

The road below me—the old pilgrim path, mostly abandoned since the Order started charging tolls for grace—was empty for an hour, and then it wasn't. A single figure, small, hooded, moving with the particular careful exhaustion of someone who has walked all night on legs that weren't built for it. They stopped by a boulder maybe thirty yards below my position and began wringing out their sleeve, and I settled myself in the dark like a debt coming due.

I dropped the last eight feet instead of taking the switchback, landed soft, and had my sword at their throat before they finished turning around.

"Evening," I said.

They screamed. I will grant them this: they screamed quietly, which showed either discipline or experience, and then they did something I did not expect. They threw a fistful of wet wool cloak at my face.

* * *

*Theo*

I want it noted for posterity that I fought well. Not *well* well—not knight-well—but I had grown up in the smuggling quarter of Lowmarket before the debt brought us to the temple, and there are certain lessons a gutter childhood teaches that no cloister can unteach. So when the stranger in the dark said "evening" with a sword at my throat, I did not freeze. I threw my sodden cloak into her face, dove left off the path, rolled, and came up holding the only weapon I owned: a letter knife with a blade the length of my hand, sharp enough to open correspondence or, apparently, knights.

She cleared the cloak off her face faster than seemed fair.

"Really," she said, and her voice was dry as chalk dust even shouting over the rain. "A letter knife."

"It's sharpened."

"I can see that." She circled left; I circled right, which meant we were circling together, which felt stupid for both of us. "You're the scribe."

The word hit me harder than any blow. "How—"

"Ink on your fingers. Quill calluses. You fight like someone who learned before they learned to be afraid of it." Her eyes flicked to my chest, to the shape under my shirt where the oilcloth bundle rode. "And you're carrying something that matters enough to run all night with."

"Give me one reason not to run right now."

"You're exhausted, it's pitch black, the path drops two hundred feet to your left, and I've been waiting here for an hour." She lowered her sword an inch. An inch. "Also, I don't actually want to hurt you. I want information, and dead men give terrible interviews."

"Scribes aren't men," I said, because if I was going to die on a mountainside I was going to be pedantic about it.

"Noted." She sheathed the sword. Just like that, which either meant she trusted me or she was confident enough not to need it, and I genuinely could not tell which frightened me more. "Wren. Formerly of somewhere. Currently of nowhere, in case the mud and the ditch-sleep didn't announce that."

"Theo." I didn't lower the knife. Habit. "Scribe, formerly of the Order of the Quiet Flame. Currently of—" I gestured at the rain, the dark, the general catastrophe. "This."

"Theo," she repeated, and something crossed her face—recognition, almost, except it slid off again, like water on slate. She frowned at the mountain as if it had said something rude. Then at me. Then, briefly, at her own hand, which had gone white-knuckled on the scabbard without her seeming to notice. "Say your name again."

"No," I said. "A dying man told me not to let anyone read it aloud, and I'm inclined to take advice from dying men, it's the only kind they give."

That startled a laugh out of her—one short bark, surprised out of her own mouth. She looked, for a moment, much younger and much more lost than the sword allowed. "Fair," she said. "Fair enough."

We stood there in the rain, two idiots on a mountainside, each deciding whether the other was a threat. I counted her weapons: sword, a knife at her belt, probably another at her boot, and the way she stood suggested she considered her own body a weapon too. She was, I realized, doing exactly the same count of me, and finding considerably less. The letter knife suddenly felt symbolic.

"Which way are you headed?" she asked finally.

I said nothing. She pointed up the path, toward the pass, toward the mountain.

"That way," she said. "Same as me. Same as I've been walking for three days without knowing why. So before we do anything else—" and here her voice changed, flattened out, became the voice of someone asking a question they already dreaded the answer to—"let me ask you this. When you look at Thornfell, does it look back?"

* * *

*Theo*

I should have lied. A good liar would have lied. But I was tired, and bleeding, and there was something in her face—not kindness exactly, but the specific honesty of someone who has also spent three days being dragged by the scruff of the soul—and the truth came out of me before I'd voted on it.

"Yes," I said. "It looks back."

She exhaled, long and slow, like she'd been holding that breath for days. "Right. Good. Terrible, but good. At least I'm not mad alone."

"What's pulling you? Do you know?" I kept the knife up but I turned my body toward her instead of away from her, which in the grammar of the last ten minutes amounted to a treaty.

"Something ate my memories." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "Three days' worth, maybe more, and the edges of older ones. There's a hole in my head and the hole points at the mountain, and when I walk toward it the hole gets quieter. Like scratching an itch." Her jaw tightened. "I don't like being an itch."

"And you think the mountain scratched it."

"I think the mountain *made* it. And I want to know what it took, because what it took—" she hesitated, and for the first time the soldier's composure cracked and I saw the person underneath, staring into her own skull and not liking the tenant count, "—what it took included why my sword has dragon-scale notched into it. Nobody notches a blade on dragon-scale by accident. That's not a memory you misplace. That's a memory someone *removed*."

Dragon-scale. The word went through me like a fishhook. On the map, in the corner where the cartographer had signed their work in symbols instead of letters, there was a mark I had spent two nights failing to identify—a coiled shape, winged, rendered in lines so old the wax had nearly swallowed them. I hadn't known what it was.

I thought I might know now.

"There's a place called the Undervault," I said slowly. "On the map I'm carrying. Beneath Thornfell. I don't know what it is. But the courier who gave me the map died saying I shouldn't let anyone read my name aloud, and the Order wants the map, and there are crown inquisitors asking questions in town, and smugglers who'll want it back within the week." I laughed, and it came out ragged. "I have a truly impressive number of enemies for someone who owns one knife."

"A letter knife," Wren said.

"*Sharpened.*"

She studied me for a long moment. Rain dripped off her nose. Somewhere below us, the dog had found its answer and gone quiet.

"Here's my thinking," she said. "You know things. Maps, routes, whatever's written on that thing you're hiding. I know how to keep us both breathing, which you clearly don't, no offense."

"None taken. It's accurate."

"So we walk together. You watch our route, I watch everything else. We don't share names, real ones, until we decide we trust each other—which may be never, and that's fine." She stuck out a gauntleted hand, then seemed to remember it was raining and we were both miserable and shook it anyway, briskly, like closing a deal. "Alliance of necessity. Worst partnership either of us has ever formed, and I include whatever contract got my sword notched."

I took the hand. Hers was iron despite the cold. "Partnership of necessity," I agreed. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we get wherever we're going—you get your memories back, I find out what my name has to do with all this—we settle accounts honestly. No lies between us after tonight. I've spent six years lying by the hour and I'm bad at it and tired of it."

Something moved behind her eyes. "Honesty," she said. "In this century. Bold."

"Wren." I sheathed the letter knife at last. "Why were you ambushing travelers on a road nobody travels? What were you looking for?"

She glanced up at the mountain, black against the faintly graying east, and when she answered, her voice was quiet enough that the rain nearly took it.

"Honestly? Anyone heading the same direction. Because whatever's calling me—" her hand flexed once at her side, "—it's been calling someone else too. I could feel it. Two hooks in the same fish, going the same way." She shrugged, awkward, armor clinking. "Misery loves company. Also company makes better bait than misery alone."

Above us, thunder rolled down out of the peaks—except it wasn't thunder, I realized, my hand finding the map through my wet shirt. Thunder doesn't repeat itself in a rhythm. This was a sound like vast wings, or like a forge breathing, or like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the stone.

Both of us froze. Both of us looked up. Neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was: *it knows we're coming.*

"Well," Wren said at last, drawing her sword and pointing it up the trail, cheerful in the way of the truly doomed. "After you, partner."
