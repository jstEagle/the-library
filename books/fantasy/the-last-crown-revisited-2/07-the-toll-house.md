## 7. The Toll House

The waystation sat where the map said it would, which was the first suspicious thing about it. In my experience, nothing on a smuggler's map is ever where it says. That's rather the point of a smuggler.

It was a squat stone building hunched against the mountainside like an old man sheltering from his own regrets, with one lit window and a sign that had been repainted so many times the letters were just suggestions. *TOLL HOUSE*, it might once have said. Or possibly *TOAD HOUSE*. I was prepared for either.

"We could go around," Theo said, though they didn't sound like they believed it.

"Go around what? It's a house."

"It's a toll house. On a road that isn't officially a road." They hugged the map case to their chest the way other people hug children or religious icons. "In my experience—"

"Your experience being scribing?"

"My experience being *maps*, actually, thank you. Roads like this don't have keepers. Keepers mean someone's enforcing something. Someone enforcing something means someone's getting paid. And nobody out here pays in coin."

I looked at them. Candlelight and stubbornness, mostly. "You know, for someone who spent years selling routes to criminals, you have a remarkably healthy sense of dread."

"I have a remarkably well-*fed* sense of dread. There's a difference. Dread that eats regularly survives the winter."

I wanted to argue. My pride wanted to argue very badly; my pride had been carrying me since the ditch outside Thornfell, three days of memory missing from my head like teeth knocked out of a smile. But my knees had opinions too, and they'd been voting for rest since the second switchback. And the light in the window was warm, and the wind coming off the high pass had teeth of its own.

We went in.

* * *

The keeper was a small woman, old in the way furniture gets old—worn smooth at all the handles. She sat behind a desk that must have been older than the building, carved with names. Hundreds of names, thousands, some scratched out and written over until the wood had gone soft as cloth. She didn't look up when we entered. She was writing another name into the grain with a tool that made no sound at all, which should have been my second warning, right after the map being honest.

"Two travelers," she said, when we'd stood there long enough for her to finish her line. "One scribe. One knight." Her eyes came up. They were pale as river ice and about as comforting. "The sword gives the second one away. So do the boots."

"The boots?" I said.

"Knights are issued their boots. Everyone else buys their own." She set down her silent tool. "Toll is four coppers each for the night. Bed, stew, water for the horses—"

"We don't have horses," Theo said.

"—or, if coin is short," she went on, without pausing, as though Theo hadn't spoken, "the mountain accepts alternative payment."

"Coin isn't short," I said quickly, and reached for my purse, because everything in me—the part that remembered being a knight, whatever else had been eaten—said *alternative payment* was a door you did not open.

She watched me count out coppers with an expression of mild disappointment, like a hostess whose guests have declined the wine. Then she took the coins, bit one out of habit or theater, and said, "That covers the bed and the stew. Not the firewood. Not the morning meal. And not the answer to the question your scribe friend has been not-asking since he walked in."

Theo flinched. "I haven't—"

"You've been looking at my ledger wall since the door opened. You want to know what I take, when there's no coin." She folded her hands. "Memories."

The word sat in the room like a dropped knife.

"Small ones, mostly," she said, conversational now, almost kind. "A face you can't quite place anymore. The taste of a meal. A name. The mountain collects them through me—I'm only the counter window, don't look so grim—and in exchange the passes stay open, the weather stays civil, and things that live under the stone stay under the stone. Everyone pays eventually. Most people prefer to pay small."

"That's—" Theo started, and stopped, and tried again. "That's not possible. Memories aren't *goods*. They're not—they can't be transferred, that's not how minds work, there are treatises—"

"Oh, there are treatises," the keeper agreed. "Written by people who never came up the mountain. Bring me four coppers more and you can keep believing them. Bring me a memory instead, and you'll know better by morning, and you'll wish you didn't."

I should have taken Theo and walked back out into the dark. I want that on the record. Whatever court eventually weighs my life—and given recent events, I no longer assume such a court doesn't exist—I want it noted that Wren of the notched sword knew this was wrong and nearly left.

Nearly.

But the firewood mattered. I'm not being glib. Cold kills more honestly than any toll-keeper, and Theo had gone the color of tallow, and there was something else too, something I didn't examine until much later: a pull behind my breastbone, faint but real, like a hook set in meat, drawing my attention toward the back of the house. Toward the mountain itself. I'd been feeling it for days. I'd assumed it was conscience.

"Which memories," I heard myself ask.

The keeper smiled. It did not improve her face. "Yours to choose. Mine to keep. Small ones travel best—a name, a color, a Sunday. Try to give me something large and it'll hurt us both."

"And if we refuse entirely?"

"Then the pass refuses you, and the pass is less polite than I am." She turned her ice-pale eyes on Theo. "You especially shouldn't refuse, scribe. The mountain already knows your handwriting."

* * *

Theo didn't sleep. I know because I didn't either, and the loft above the common room was small enough that their not-sleeping was audible—not tossing, exactly, but the particular silence of a person lying very still and thinking very loudly.

The fire crackled below. The keeper had banked it herself, humming something tuneless. Twice during the evening she had gone to her ledger wall and run her fingers over the names the way a woman strokes a cat, and both times the fire had burned a little brighter after.

"They're real payments," Theo whispered, somewhere past midnight. "Wren. The names on the wall. They're not decorative. I copied scripture for six years—I know what ritual bookkeeping looks like, and that's not it. That's an *inventory*."

"Go to sleep."

"How can you be calm about this?"

"Because if I stop being calm I'll start being afraid, and fear makes poor decisions, and I've apparently already made several this week." I rolled onto my back and stared at the rafters. Somewhere in the missing stretch of my memory there were probably answers to all of this—why my sword carried a dragon-scale notch, why the mountain pulled at me like a tide pulling at a rowboat—but asking the dark for them felt like begging a stranger.

"The name she takes tomorrow," Theo said. "If it comes to that. How does anyone choose? Every memory is load-bearing. Take one brick out and you don't know which wall falls."

"That's why you pay coin."

"That's why *you* pay coin. I counted. You have eleven coppers left. That's tonight's fire and tomorrow's breakfast and then you're paying her way whether you like it or not." A pause. Their voice went smaller. "And I have three."

I lay with that. Eleven coppers and a knight who couldn't remember three days, escorting a fugitive scribe with three coppers and a bloodstained map, toward a court beneath a mountain that ate memories for toll. Somewhere, whoever had trained me—if anyone had—was presumably hanging their head.

"Then we find work," I said finally. "There's always work on smuggling roads. Carrying. Guarding. Things knights are embarrassingly qualified for."

"*Former* knights."

"Do you know something about my discharge that I don't?"

They were quiet a moment. "No. Sorry. I forget sometimes that it's not—forgetting isn't an abstraction for you. It's just holes."

"Holes with edges," I agreed. "You get used to running your tongue along them."

I hadn't meant it to come out that raw. Theo, to their credit, didn't apologize again, which would have made it worse. They just said, softly, "Get some sleep, Wren," and for the first time since the ditch, the compulsion in my chest eased enough to let me.

* * *

Morning came gray through the shutters, and with it the bill.

The keeper had laid out bread and hard cheese and a pot of tea that tasted faintly of pine, and while we ate she sat behind her desk sharpening that soundless tool against a strop, watching us with the patience of someone who had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. The arithmetic was simple and she let us do it ourselves, which I decided was a form of mercy, though it may simply have been efficiency.

Eleven coppers minus last night's fire and breakfast left seven. Seven bought passage over the pass for one person. Perhaps.

"One of us goes hungry and cold and the other walks free," I said, doing sums on my fingers like a child. "Or we split the difference and freeze together halfway up."

"There is," the keeper said mildly, "the third option."

"No," Theo said immediately.

"You haven't heard it."

"I heard it yesterday. The third option is a memory, and I've been awake all night reading every treaty and temple ruling I have memorized on the trade of intangibles, and the fact that none of them forbid it frightens me more than if one did."

The keeper shrugged. "The mountain doesn't need your permission to be true."

"Wren." Theo turned to me, and their face did the thing it did when they were frightened and covering it with lecture-mode: chin up, eyes too bright. "We pay coin. We go up half-provisioned. We'll move faster anyway. Light travelers cover ground."

"We'll move fast and then stop moving permanently on an exposed shelf above the scree field, because neither of us slept properly and there's no supper waiting up there."

"Theo Dunmore does not sell pieces of their mind to a woman with a knife that makes no sound."

"And I do?" I asked.

That landed. I watched it land. Theo's mouth opened, closed, opened again. "You don't have—you can't remember most of your life, Wren. What's left is what you *have*. If she takes—"

"A name," I said.

"What?"

"You said it yourself. Every memory is load-bearing. So we pick the least load-bearing thing in the whole structure." I turned to the keeper. "A name. Some name I barely use. Not mine, not anyone's whose face I could point to. Is that acceptable merchandise?"

The keeper's pale eyes brightened with what I genuinely believe was pleasure. Merchants are merchants everywhere; the currency changes, the delight doesn't. "Acceptable. Provided it's freely given—that matters, more than you'd think. And provided it's truly small. The mountain checks."

"How?" Theo demanded. "How does a mountain *check*?"

"Same way your Order knows when a novice lies about copying his penance lines," she said, and Theo went white, and I filed that away—the keeper knew what Theo was, or had been—before standing and unbuckling my sword.

"Wren," Theo said, in a completely different voice.

"I'm not fighting her. I'm making sure I stand straight. Habit." It wasn't entirely a habit. Something about the back room, the doorway hung with cured hides that stirred without draft, made every muscle I owned want to be arranged for violence. The hook behind my breastbone had tightened all night, and now it pulled forward, and I hated that some part of me wanted to follow it.

The keeper gestured to the chair before her desk. It was an ordinary chair. I sat in it like it was an executioner's.

"Now," she said, folding her hands. "Give me the name."

"I don't—how do I choose which—"

"You don't choose. You offer. You hold the intention—*a name I will not miss*—and speak it aloud, and the mountain sorts the truth from the packaging." She leaned in slightly. "Think of it as paying with a coin pouch. The mountain takes what fits its hand."

"That's not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be accurate."

I closed my eyes. Behind them: the ditch, the road, Theo's terrified competent face, the notched sword, the missing days like a knocked-out tooth. Somewhere deeper, shapes I couldn't name—a banner? a bell? a hand gripping my shoulder?—all of it load-bearing, all of it mine, even the parts I couldn't reach.

*A name I will not miss.*

There was one. Of course there was one. Everyone has them: the name of a boy from training years, a cousin twice removed, a sergeant whose face had worn away years ago leaving only the sound. I found it the way you find a pebble in your boot—by touch, familiar and unimportant. *Ser Aldous Vane.* No. Not him; his face still worked. Deeper. Smaller. There.

I spoke it.

I won't write it here. That's not coyness; I literally cannot. I opened my mouth intending to describe what happened, and discovered the word was gone—not muffled, not faded, *gone*, the way a stair is gone in the dark when you expected one more step. The shape of the absence was all that remained: I knew I had known a name, known it was small, known I had chosen it. Around the hole, everything was intact. My tongue ran along the edge and found it smooth already, which was somehow worse than if it bled.

Beside the desk, the keeper's silent tool whispered across the wood, adding a name to a wall of names. The fire leapt. The hides in the doorway stirred. And far under us—under the floor, under the foundations, under the roots of Thornfell itself—something vast turned over in its sleep, the way a body turns toward warmth.

"There," the keeper said, gently, horribly. "That wasn't so bad."

I stood. My legs held. My sword arm held. Everything held except one smooth-edged hollow where a pebble used to be.

"You felt it leave," Theo said. They had come to the doorway without my noticing, and their face was the worst thing in the room—not pity, worse: understanding, dawning and appalled, the look of a scholar watching a theory become flesh. "You felt it leave, and the mountain *moved*. Wren, the mountain *felt* you pay. This isn't theft, it isn't a trick—it's feeding. It's been fed on. For how long? How many names are on those walls? How many—"

"Scribe," the keeper said, not unkindly. "Your friend paid for two breakfasts' worth of peace. Don't spend it all at once."

But Theo wasn't listening to her. They were staring at the ledger wall—at the thousands of names, scratched-out and rewritten, soft as cloth from handling—and I watched them arrive at the same arithmetic I had, the size of it, the *years* of it. All those small payments. All those smooth-edged holes walking around the valleys wearing people's smiles.

"We need to leave," Theo said quietly. "Now. Today. And Wren—we need to talk about what this place actually is, because I don't think it's a waystation. I think it's a *mouth*."

The keeper laughed, low and unhurried, and began banking the fire. "Everyone figures that out eventually," she said. "Usually on the way back down."

We were saddling ourselves—strapping on packs, Theo checking and rechecking the map case—when the door banged open and the cold came in with three men and a crossbow.

The tall one in front wore a wolf-pelt collar and a smile like a scar, and he looked at Theo the way a creditor looks at a debtor.

"Well," Maren Voss said. "Fancy meeting the little mapmaker so far from the scriptorium."
