## 8. Smugglers' Justice

I had a theory, in the hours before dawn, that we'd outrun them.

It was not a good theory. It was the kind of theory you build out of exhaustion and wishful thinking, like believing the candle will last until morning if you just don't look at it too often. Wren and I had put six miles between ourselves and the toll house, climbing through pine shadow along a track so narrow it seemed less built than tolerated by the mountain, and I let myself believe that distance was the same thing as safety.

The mountain disabused me of this shortly after first light.

We heard the horses before we saw them—no, that's wrong. We heard *the absence* of birdsong first, the way the trees went quiet in a moving line, and then the horses, four of them, coming up the switchback below at an unhurried walk. Unhurried was the bad sign. Men who are chasing you hurry. Men who already know where you're going don't bother.

"Off the trail," Wren said, and there was no argument in her voice, which meant she'd done the same arithmetic I had.

We got maybe thirty feet into the trees, crouched behind a granite shoulder furred with moss, before the riders reached our position and stopped. Not searched. Stopped. One of them dismounted, walked to a flat stone beside the trail, and set something down on it with the care of a woman arranging a place setting.

Then she looked directly at our boulder.

"Scribe," said Maren Voss. "You're behind the rock or you're under my boot. Your choice, but make it quick, because I've been riding since the bells and I'm in no mood for theater."

* * *

Here is what you should know about Maren Voss, since I sold her people's secrets and therefore owe her at least a fair description.

She was perhaps fifty, built like a doorframe, with grey shot through her black hair in a way that suggested weather rather than age. She wore a smuggler's coat with forty pockets and used every one of them. Her eyes were the pale brown of river water over stone, and they did the thing that frightened me most about her: they never widened, never narrowed, never gave anything away. Other people's faces were ledgers to her, and hers was a locked strongbox.

For three years I had drawn maps for her network—the goat paths through Thornfell's southern shoulders, the passes that flooded in thaw, the watch rotations of the Order's boundary shrines. I told myself it was honest work, or honest-adjacent. The coin bought down my family's debt one ledger line at a time. The Order would have called it treason. The crown would have called it smuggling. Maren called it *logistics*, and paid promptly, and never once asked me how I felt about any of it, which I had mistaken for respect.

Now she stood in a clearing with three armed riders and my name in her mouth, and I understood that I had confused being useful with being safe.

"The courier," I said, because there was no point pretending otherwise. "He came to me dying. He gave me the map."

"He stole it from us first." She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Kestrel took the master copy out of the cache at Hollow Bridge and went to ground. We assumed he was selling it to the Order. Turns out he was selling it to *you*, or to whatever you are now."

"I'm still what I was. A scribe."

"A scribe the Order wants to question. A scribe whose mentor signed a false entry in the gate log two nights ago." She tilted her head. "Yes, we have friends inside the temple. You knew that when you started working for us. Did you think the friendship only flowed downhill?"

Behind me, Wren shifted her weight. I didn't look at her. Looking at her would tell Maren where to aim.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"The map. And then—" Maren spread her hands, and even that gesture was economical, nothing wasted, "—then we discuss whether you leave these mountains as a debtor, a corpse, or a client. I'd prefer the third. You're the best cartographer I've ever bought, and I say that with genuine professional grief."

"And the second option?"

"The second option is that Brann here ties you up, and we deliver you to the Order's archive division at Cindermark, and collect whatever they're paying for couriers' accomplices these days." She glanced at the big man mounted at her left. He smiled the way stones smile. "The Order pays better than you do, scribe. That's not cruelty. That's bookkeeping."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because later—much later—I would need to remember it accurately.

What happened was that I thought about Brother Cassian.

Not his face. His hands. The way he'd held his pen when he covered for me in the gate log, steady, deliberate, the penmanship of a man writing a lie with the same care he'd give a psalm. He had spent years quietly erasing my footprints from the Order's records, and I had known it the way you know a roof is load-bearing: only when you imagine it gone. If Maren delivered me to Cindermark, the interrogators wouldn't stop at me. They'd pull my thread until the whole garment unraveled, and Cassian would be standing in the middle of it holding the scissors.

So it wasn't courage that made me speak next. It was debt. I seem to have been made almost entirely of debt; it's the family material.

"You can't sell me the way you think," I said. "Because the map isn't what you think it is."

Maren waited. She was good at waiting. It was probably half her business model.

"It doesn't lead where Kestrel told you it leads. It leads to the Undervault."

The word landed in the clearing like a dropped anvil. Even the horses reacted—one of them tossed its head and sidestepped, and its rider muttered something apotropaic under his breath. Smugglers are superstitious the way sailors are superstitious: not because they believe, exactly, but because the mountains and the sea both eat people who aren't.

"That's a story for drunk firesides," Maren said, but her voice had changed key. Half a step flatter.

"It's a market," I said. "A court. They trade in memory and time and names. The courier died telling me not to let anyone read my name aloud, and I have spent two days wondering why *my* name specifically, and I haven't liked a single answer I've come up with." I stood up from behind the boulder, slowly, hands visible, because I wanted her to see that I wasn't reaching for a weapon. "You want the map back. Fine. But if you take it to the Order, or sell it to some lordling, you'll be handing a candle to a man standing in a powder store. The Undervault isn't treasure. It's a creditor. And creditors always find their debtors."

Silence. Somewhere above us a jay screamed once and thought better of it.

"You've been reading your own maps too long," Maren said finally. "Mountains don't keep courts."

"This one does. Ask the toll-keeper at the waystation what he eats." I watched that land too. Word travels fast on smuggler roads; the toll house was theirs, or adjacent to theirs, and someone would have reported the memory-toll taken from a knight with a notched sword. "Ask him what he charged the woman standing behind me."

Wren stepped out beside me. I hadn't asked her to. Later I would learn that this was simply what she did—insert herself into the space between a threat and its target, with all the grace of a door slamming.

"She paid a name," Wren said. "Hers, or near enough. And I've lost days I can't account for, and my sword has dragon-scale notched into it, and I don't remember why. So either the scribe is lying, in which case you should kill us both quickly, or she's right, in which case you should be more afraid than you look." She paused. "You look fairly afraid."

"I don't," said Maren.

"No," Wren agreed. "That's why I said it."

* * *

Here is where I must confess to a piece of improvisation that I am not proud of, though I have stopped apologizing for it.

While Maren's attention was on Wren—and it was, fully; Wren has a talent for being the loudest object in any room without raising her voice—I moved. Not toward the horses. Toward the flat stone where Maren's rider had set down whatever he'd carried up the switchback.

It was a small chest, iron-banded, unlocked, because Maren Voss did not lock things she expected to retrieve personally. Inside lay papers. Copies. My copies—or rather, the copies her people had commissioned from me over three years, recovered from Kestrel's cache, every route I'd ever sold them rendered in my own careful hand.

And beneath them, folded twice, a page in a hand I didn't know. A report. I read three lines before I understood what I was holding:

*...the Order's archive division has posted a standing bounty for the courier's effects. The inquisitor Ashgrave has taken personal interest. Recommend we divest. The scribe is a liability the moment the crown connects the routes to the temple debt-ledgers...*

Someone in Maren's network had already written me off. Possibly Maren herself. Possibly everyone.

"Scribe." Maren's voice had gone very quiet. "Step away from my correspondence."

"You were going to sell me to Cindermark," I said. "Before I'd even finished talking. This letter's dated four days ago."

"Business planning is not betrayal."

"No," I agreed. "It's bookkeeping. You taught me the phrase." I held the letter up between two fingers. "Here's my counteroffer. I keep the map. You get your copies back—all of them, everything I ever drew, free of charge, a severance gift. In exchange, you forget my face, my name, and the road I take out of this clearing. And when the crown's men come asking—and they will come, because Ashgrave has taken *personal interest*—you tell them you never found me."

"And why," said Maren, in the tone of a woman who already knew the answer and wanted to hear if I knew it too, "would I take that trade? I could take the letter back, take the map, and take you, in roughly that order."

"Because the letter says *divest*. Someone smart in your organization already ran the numbers and decided I cost more than I pay. All I'm doing is letting you follow your own advice." I made myself smile. It felt like lifting something heavy. "Besides. You said it yourself. I'm the best cartographer you ever bought. Do you really want to find out what happens to your routes when the Undervault's creditors start collecting along them?"

I don't know if that argument won. I suspect arguments rarely win with Maren Voss; what wins is arithmetic, and I had given her new numbers. A dead or delivered scribe was worth a bounty. A loose scribe who might, improbably, actually reach the Undervault and burn it down—that was worth something too, though what, neither of us could price yet.

Wren's hand had found her sword hilt somewhere in the last minute. I noticed it the way you notice weather changing.

"Maren," I said. "Your move."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then at Wren. Then, briefly and with what I swear was genuine feeling, at the sky, as if asking it why her profession attracted people like this.

"The copies," she said. "All of them. And the letter stays with me."

"The letter stays with me."

"The letter—"

"—is insurance, Maren. You have three armed men and I have a knight with a notched sword and nothing left to lose, and you have a network full of people who read reports recommending they divest. How loyal do you think your riders feel right now?" I nodded at the youngest of them, a wiry man whose eyes had been flicking between his leader and the treeline since Wren drew breath. "Riders talk. Riders desert. Riders remember which side of a bargain had the better odds."

Another silence. Longer. Then Maren Voss laughed—a short, hard sound with no joy in it whatsoever, the laugh of a woman acknowledging a well-played hand she resented being dealt into.

"You know what your problem is, scribe? You think like a smuggler and you write like a priest. It's an unsettling combination." She jerked her chin at her riders. "Give them the chest. The empty one. Papers stay with the lady knight."

Brann dismounted and dumped the copies at Wren's feet with the enthusiasm of a man feeding documents to a fire he wished he could light. Wren gathered them without taking her eyes off the riders.

"The map," Maren said. "Show me you still have it. I want to see it burn or I want to see it pocketed—I don't care which—but I want to know it exists and I want to know where it sleeps tonight."

I hesitated. Every instinct I owned, honed by years of copying scripture under a censorious abbot, said *never show the original.* But instincts are for people with alternatives.

I drew it out—wax-sealed, bloodstained at one corner where Kestrel's blood had soaked through—and held it up long enough for her to see the Thornfell ranges inked across it, the routes beneath the routes, the small strange symbol at the heart of the mountain that I had not yet shown anyone.

Maren went still. Genuinely still, for the first time, in a way that made the hair on my arms rise.

"I've seen that mark before," she said. "On old coins. Pre-dynasty. My grandmother kept one in a charm bag and swore it opened doors." Her river-stone eyes fixed on me. "Where did a dead courier get a map to a door that predates the kingdom?"

"That," I said, tucking it away, "is an excellent question, and I intend to survive long enough to answer it."

"Survival's not in my power to grant." She swung back onto her horse, and just like that she was a businesswoman again, closing a ledger. "But I'll tell you what I can give you, scribe, gratis, because you amused me and because I'm a sentimental woman pretending not to be. Ashgrave isn't following the roads. He's following the debts. Crown inquisitors have access to every tax roll, every indenture, every lien in three provinces. He's not hunting a map." She nudged her horse forward, past me, close enough that I smelled leather and woodsmoke. "He's hunting whoever owes the most. Think about why that might be you."

They rode. The birds came back, cautiously, like creditors after a bankruptcy.

* * *

We stood in the clearing until the hoofbeats faded, and then a while longer, because neither of us quite trusted the woods to be finished with us.

"Well," said Wren eventually. "You lied to at least three separate people in the space of a quarter hour, threatened a smuggler queen with her own paperwork, and walked away with everything you came in with. Remind me never to play cards with you."

"I didn't lie."

"You absolutely lied. 'I intend to survive long enough'—you have no plan past the next ridge."

"I have a direction. Plans are just directions with anxiety attached." I crouched and began repacking the copies into my satchel, mostly to give my hands something to do while they shook. "She was right about one thing, though. Ashgrave follows debts. Which means he's not looking for the map at all. He's looking for the person the map points to."

"And that's you."

"I'm starting to suspect it's been me for a very long time." I tied the satchel closed. Under my fingers, through the canvas, I could feel the map's edge, and beneath the map, faint as a heartbeat, that symbol. "There's an abandoned shrine half a day east. Old Order waystation, pre-schism. Nobody goes there. We can rest, and—" I stopped.

"And?"

"And I want to see the walls. Shrines like that have murals. Foundation cycles, dedication scenes, the usual pieties." I heard my own voice go carefully flat, the way it did when I was copying a passage I didn't want to feel. "The courier's map uses iconography I've seen somewhere before. I keep circling it and landing nowhere. Maybe the shrine will tell me where *somewhere* is."

Wren studied me with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively. "You're afraid of a wall painting."

"I'm afraid of what happens when I stop being able to pretend I don't recognize things." I shouldered the satchel and started east, because walking was easier than explaining. "Come on. Daylight's burning, and according to the best cartographer she ever bought, we've got a mountain to owe."

Behind us, far below, the valley lay green and innocent in the morning sun, and somewhere in it, I knew, a man in an inquisitor's coat was reading tax rolls, running his finger down columns of names, looking for mine.

He would find it. Some part of me had already begun grieving for the finding.

But that was a problem for the bottom of the mountain. First: the shrine. First: the walls.

First: learning what my own handwriting had been trying to tell me all along.
