## 10. Brother Cassian's Letter

*From the private papers of Brother Cassian Vael, recovered from the scriptorium of the Order of the Quiet Flame, Thornfell Priory. Catalogued by the Archive Division under seal, never read aloud.*

---

**Letter the first — undated, though the ink suggests early spring, some eleven years past**

To no one. To myself. To the drawer.

I have taken to writing letters I do not send. The Abbot says a scribe who cannot stop writing should at least vary his subjects, and so: tonight my subject is a child.

Theo Dunmore came to us eight years ago, indentured against their family's debt, small enough then that they had to stand on a stool to reach the copying desk. They are not small now. What they are now is trouble wearing a novice's robe, and I say that with the particular affection one reserves for trouble one has decided to keep.

Today I caught them doing sums in the margin of a psalm. Not devotional sums. Debt sums. Their family's ledger, worked out to the copper, with a little column of projections — how many years at current wages, how many if they sold maps on the side, how many if the maps were *good*. The last column was short. Alarmingly short. The boy — the child, I'll use no gender for a soul still deciding what it wants to be — the child had calculated their way out of servitude using my own mountain.

I confiscated the page. I did not punish them. I am recording here, in this letter to no one, that I looked at those margins for a long while after they left, and what I felt was not disappointment.

It was pride. Which is its own kind of sin, in an order devoted to quiet.

The Quiet Flame teaches that ambition is a draft through a window: it makes the candle gutter. I have watched this candle for eight years and I will tell you what the Order's doctrine misses. A flame that never gutters is a flame that has never been asked for anything. Theo has been asked for everything since the day they were born, and they are still burning.

There is one more thing, and it is the reason this letter goes in the drawer rather than the fire. When Theo was brought to us, there was a paper in the bundle — intake documents, the mother's death certificate, the debt instrument. And beneath those, folded twice, a scrap I have never shown anyone. A child's signature on something older than the debt. I did not understand it then. I have spent eleven years not understanding it very carefully, which is a discipline of its own.

Tomorrow I will burn it. That is what I am telling myself tonight, anyway, in this letter to no one.

---

* * *

**Letter the ninth — midwinter, six years past**

To the drawer, keeper of my cowardices:

The Archive Division came today.

You must understand what that means, because even I forget between visits. The Order of the Quiet Flame keeps scripture, yes, but the crown pays our heating bills, and the crown's Archive Division does not care about psalms. They care about *records*. Contracts. Anything bearing a seal, a signature, or a name spoken into a legal register. Twice a year they descend on us like well-mannered crows and go through our rolls looking for — I have never been told exactly what. Instruments of the old courts, is the official phrase. Bargains made beneath Thornfell, is what everyone actually means, in the way everyone means things here without saying them, because this whole valley has learned to talk around the mountain the way you talk around a sick relative.

Brother Edmun, bless his literal heart, asked one of them directly what they were looking for. The archivist — gray robes, gray eyes, a face like a closed ledger — smiled and said, "Anything that was signed twice."

Signed twice. Once by the mortal party, once by whatever stands behind the mountain's masks. I have thought about that phrase all evening. Every contract in our archive was signed once, by us, in good faith, witnessed by God or by nothing. But somewhere under the stone there are papers with two signatures. And the crown wants them.

Why? What does a kingdom want with an old bargain? Renewal, whispers say. Leaseholds. There is a story every child in Thornfell knows: the dragon gave its fire to save the valley, and the fire is held below, and the beacon in the capital burns on sufferance. I always took it for a morality tale. Standing across from that gray-faced archivist today, watching him run his finger down our contract rolls like a man counting teeth, I found myself wondering whether it was instead a *terms of service*.

Here is the part I confess only to you, drawer. When he reached the D's — Dunmore — my heart did something unmonastic. Theo Dunmore appears in our rolls forty times: intake, indenture, wage postings, the debt schedule. Ordinary papers, signed once, by priors long dead. Nothing there. Nothing *should* be there.

But that folded scrap from the intake bundle — the one I told you eleven years ago I would burn — sits in the false bottom of my reliquary chest, and it is signed twice. Once in a child's careful hand. *T. Dunmore.* And once in something that is not ink at all, or is ink the way a bruise is a color.

He did not find it. He could not have found it; it is not catalogued; it exists nowhere except in my keeping. And yet when he finished with the D's he paused, and looked up at me across the reading table, and said, "You have a novice named Dunmore."

"They copy psalms," I said. "They are nine years behind on their calligraphy practice. You are welcome to inspect their penmanship."

Something flickered behind the gray. "No need," he said, and moved on to the E's.

I have been a monk for thirty years. I know what a lie costs. That one cost me nothing but breath, and I would pay it again before Vespers tomorrow. This is what I have become: a man who guards a child's secret from his own conscience by refusing to consult it.

The scrap stays where it is. I am done promising to burn it. Some fires you keep lit precisely so they will never catch.

* * *

**Letter the fourteenth — high summer, three years past**

Drawer:

Theo sold a map today. Or rather, Theo sold a *copy* of a route they reconstructed from pilgrim itineraries and the complaints of merchants, which amounts to the same thing, and which is either larceny or scholarship depending on who profits. Maren Voss's people paid in silver. Theo tithed a tenth of it to the poor box, which I pretend not to notice, and which is the most transparent act of moral bookkeeping I have ever witnessed. You cannot launder guilt through alms, child. I know. I have tried. The drawer knows. Everyone who keeps secrets eventually tries.

But here is why I write tonight, and my hand is not entirely steady, so forgive the slant of the lines.

Voss's courier came back for a second map, and with him came talk. Smuggler talk travels faster than smugglers. They speak of the Undervault openly now — not as legend but as *institution*, a court beneath the mountain that buys and sells in memory and time, whose toll-houses dot the passes like tumors. And they speak of a particular class of document. Soul-contracts, they call them, though the word they use is coarser. Old ones. Ones signed in blood-ink by children too young to know what a soul weighs.

One such contract, they say, was signed by a child of Thornfell. A debtor's child. In exchange for a mother's breath.

I sat in the refectory and listened to a man with a knife scar describe, secondhand and wrong in every detail except the shape of it, the worst thing that ever happened in this priory's keeping. And across the hall Theo Dunmore ate their supper and corrected a younger novice's Latin declensions and laughed at something, and I thought: they don't know. Eleven years old at signing, and they don't remember, or they've buried it so deep that even the mountain can't dig it up without an invitation.

Should I tell them?

I have drafted the telling four times in my head. Each version begins with *When you were nine* and each version ends with the same silence, because what follows that sentence? *You sold your soul* — except it wasn't theirs to sell, that's the horror of it, the masked thing took the dragon's fire on the child's promise and let the child believe — no. Even now I flinch from the shape of it. A nine-year-old watching their mother drown in her own lungs will sign anything put in front of them. That is not a bargain. That is a harvest.

And if I tell them, they will go looking. Theo's whole nature is a key turned toward locked doors. Tell them there is a contract under the mountain and they will be under the mountain inside a month, with a forged pass and a stolen lantern and that terrible competent calm they get when the odds are bad.

Whereas if I say nothing, they stay here. Copying psalms. Doing arithmetic in the margins. Paying down a debt that was always, underneath, a fiction stacked on a theft — because I have re-read their family's debt instrument a dozen times now, and the interest structure is wrong, deliberately wrong, compounded in a way no honest lender compounds. The mountain holds their family's paper too. Everything in this valley funnels downward. We are all tributaries.

So: I say nothing. I guard the scrap. I falsify the wage postings so the debt runs slower than it should — yes, I admit it here, where no one will ever look, I have been shaving years off Theo's indenture for a decade and calling it clerical error — and I light two candles at Vespers instead of one, and I wait for the Archive Division's crows, and I lie to them with a straight back.

Love, in my order, is mostly concealment. We hide the flame so it won't go out. I used to think that was metaphor.

---

* * *

**Letter the twenty-second — autumn, this year**

Drawer, old friend:

News travels badly up a mountain and worse into a priory, but even here we heard that the beacon in the capital guttered at the equinox. Half the valley saw it: the great flame on the citadel tower shrinking to a coal, the wards along the border roads going dark like lamps at curfew. The crown announced a maintenance issue. The crown announces many things.

Theo came to me at Compline, pale as vellum, and told me about the courier.

A stranger died on the back stair during evening bells — that much I already knew; I helped carry the body, though Theo doesn't know I was the second pair of hands in the dark. What I didn't know was the map. Wax-sealed, bloodstained, routes beneath Thornfell, a symbol Theo swears matches fragments of a memory they shouldn't have. They showed it to me by candlelight with their hands shaking and asked me — asked *me*, the man who has been lying to them since before their voice broke — whether I knew anything about bargains made beneath the mountain.

Reader — drawer — I looked my whole life's work in the face and said, "Only the stories."

Then I said, "Burn it."

They said, "You first," which is Theo all over, and we stood there in the mutual knowledge that neither of us burns anything, and something passed between us that I will not cheapen by naming. Call it the recognition of two liars who lie for love. Call it absolution, provisionally granted. Call it a father watching his child walk toward the thing he built his whole life to keep them from, and knowing that every road away from it is also a road toward it, because the door moves, the debts pull, and this child is the largest unpaid balance in the valley's ledger whether they know it or not.

They will run. Soon. Tonight, perhaps. I saw them checking the strap of their satchel at Matins, the way they check it before every illicit errand, and I have decided — I have finally, after thirty years of quiet, *decided* —

Let them run. I will hold the door behind them. If the crows come asking, I will misfile, misdirect, and misremember until the Archive Division's gray archivist wishes he had never learned the alphabet. And if it comes to it, I will give them the scrap — the true one, the double-signed one — because Theo deserves to hear it from a friend's mouth before they hear it from an enemy's, and because a secret kept too long stops being protection and becomes theft. I have been stealing their history from them for eleven years, one kind omission at a time.

Forgive me, child. Forgive the omissions. I told myself they were walls, but walls and prisons share masonry, and I built mine around you out of love, which is the most common mortar and the least excusable.

Go well. Go quietly. Do not let them read your name aloud — the dead courier was right about that, righter than he knew, and I curse myself nightly for never learning who sent him.

If you ever read these letters, know that every silence was a sentence in a longer letter, and the longer letter said this:

You were nine. You were afraid. You saved her. Any court that calls that a crime has never watched someone die, and any god that calls it damnation is smaller than the child who signed.

Now. The bells for Lauds are ringing, and my hand has outrun my caution, as hands do at this hour. One more matter and then I sleep: the Archive Division arrived at dusk, unannounced, off-schedule, and in greater number than ever before. Their leader is called Ashgrave. He carries a warrant with the privy seal and he has asked, specifically, by name, for the records pertaining to T. Dunmore, and when I told him the name does not appear in our active rolls he smiled the way winter smiles and said,

"Then you won't mind showing me your *inactive* ones, Brother Cassian. All of them. Starting with anything you may have removed from an intake bundle some eleven years ago."

He knows. God preserve us, he knows, or he guesses with the precision of knowledge, and the chest with the false bottom is ten feet from where I sit, and the candles are burning low, and I have just enough time to decide whether to burn the scrap at last or carry it out through the crypt and —

---

*The letters end here. The remainder of Brother Cassian's papers consist of ash, a scorched corner of parchment bearing half a child's signature —* T. Dunmo *— and, pressed flat between the pages of a psalter, a single gray feather that no bird of the Thornfell valley has ever been known to shed.*

*Brother Cassian Vael himself was not found. The Archive Division recorded his departure as "resignation, effective immediately." The priory bell rang once, at an hour when no one rang it, and the mountain, some witnesses said, seemed briefly to listen.*
