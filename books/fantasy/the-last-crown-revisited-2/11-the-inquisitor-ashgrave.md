## 11. The Inquisitor Ashgrave

There is a particular smell to a smuggler's confession, and it is not fear. Fear smells like vinegar and wet wool. No — a smuggler's confession smells like the inside of a strongbox: old coin, tallow, and the faint metallic tang of a man doing arithmetic with his own life.

I had been smelling it for three days.

The cells beneath the Thornfell garrison were cut into the mountain's root, which meant they were cold in a way that made honesty feel like warmth. I did not need irons. I needed a chair, a table, a lamp, and time. Time was the instrument. Everything else was furniture.

The man across from me was called Doss — I knew this because he'd told me his name twice, and liars always give you their name too readily, as though it were payment. He was one of Maren Voss's people, taken on the Salt Road with forty pounds of untaxed tin and a face that kept rehearsing innocence and forgetting its lines.

"Third time," I said pleasantly. "Tell me about the scribe."

"I told you. Never met any scribe."

"You told me that the first time, when it was fresh and therefore true. The second time you told me, and I quote, 'some ink-fingered clerk buys maps off us now and then.' That was after your friend Wexler mentioned the clerk. Now you're back to never having met one." I turned the lamp a quarter-inch toward him. Not into his eyes — I am not a butcher — just enough that the light sat on the table between us like a third participant. "You understand my difficulty. Your story has a plot hole where a person should be."

Doss wet his lips. "It's nothing. Some temple rat copies our routes for pocket coin. Buys candles with it, maybe. It's not crown business."

"Everything is crown business. That is the entire point of a crown." I opened the folder before me — a thin folder, deliberately, because thick folders intimidate and thin ones suggest that I already know everything worth knowing and am merely confirming details out of courtesy. "Your route through the Thornfell passes. Who drew the original?"

"Voss keeps the masters."

"And Voss learned them from—"

"Traders. Old traders. Dead ones."

"Mm." I let the silence do a lap of the cell. Silence is underrated; most interrogators talk too much because talking feels like work, and men who mistake activity for progress die at their desks surrounded by paperwork and no answers. "Doss. I'm going to tell you something, and you may repeat it to your cellmates tonight, because I find that a well-informed prisoner makes better decisions than an ignorant one."

He leaned in despite himself. They always lean in. Curiosity is the hook that doesn't rust.

"The beacon in the capital is going out."

He blinked. "The dragon-fire?"

"The dragon-fire," I agreed. "It has burned for three hundred years without refueling, because what feeds it does not eat wood or oil. And it is guttering. You will have noticed the wards along the valley road flickering at dusk. You will have noticed that the blight reached the barley two seasons early. You may even have noticed, if you are the sort of man who notices things, which I suspect you are not, that the king's physicians have started wearing iron again." I closed the folder. "Now. A temple scribe has been copying smuggler routes under Thornfell. Routes that lead down. Tell me why a servant of the Quiet Flame would care about what's underneath the world."

"I don't know," Doss said, and this time it was almost true, which was progress of a kind.

"No," I said. "But you know who might."

* * *

I want to be clear about my mandate, because history will misrecord it — history always does; it prefers its villains legible — and I would rather leave an accurate account somewhere, even in a cell transcript no one will read.

I am not a cruel man. I want to say that plainly, once, here at the beginning, so that later — when the fire fails, when the wards come down, when the things that the dragon-flame has kept out of the valley for three centuries come down with it — no one can say Ashgrave wanted this. Cruelty is inefficient. I have watched cruel interrogators and they get worse information than I do, because pain teaches a subject only one lesson: how to make the pain stop, by any means, including lying. I offer warmth instead. A blanket. Watered wine. The sense — carefully calibrated, never quite promised — that cooperation leads somewhere soft.

It is remarkable how much a man will give you for the mere suggestion of kindness. It costs me nothing. Kindness is the cheapest currency in the kingdom, and I am rich in it.

My mandate comes sealed, and I have read it perhaps forty times, which is thirty-nine more than anyone else alive. It says, in the Chancellor's own hand: *Recover the contract. Renew the lease. By whatever means.* Six words of instruction wrapped in a page of justification, and the justification is the part that keeps me awake — not with guilt, I want to be clear, but with arithmetic.

Because here is what the crown knows, and what the smugglers only guess, and what the temple has spent generations pretending not to remember: the dragon-fire is not a miracle. It is a payment. Three hundred years ago the last dragon gave its soul — willingly, the murals say, though I have learned to distrust the word *willingly* in any document drafted by the beneficiary — and that soul was placed in keeping beneath Thornfell, held as collateral against a debt the kingdom owes and has never once tried to repay. The flame burns because the collateral sits in the vault. The flame gutters because someone, somewhere, has begun to call the loan.

And the crown's answer, ratified under seal, is not to repay the debt.

The crown's answer is to renew the lease.

I have sat with that phrase longer than I sat with any confession. *Renew the lease.* As though a soul were a pasture, as though the dragon were a tenant who had simply failed to renew in writing, as though three hundred years of light and warding and warm hearths constituted rent paid in full rather than interest accrued on a principal we have never touched. The lawyers who drafted the original papers were very good. That is the horror of it — not that the court beneath the mountain is monstrous, but that our own chancery matched it clause for clause. We did not fall into this bargain. We negotiated it. We have minutes.

So when the Chancellor writes *by whatever means*, I do not read it as license. I read it as despair, dressed in wax and ribbon. The fire is failing. The alternatives to recovering that contract are plagues and dark roads and the end of the kingdom as a going concern. I am the man sent to prevent an apocalypse with a folder and a lamp, and I am expected to be tidy about it.

Very well. I will be tidy.

* * *

Wexler broke on the second evening, which surprised no one, least of all Wexler.

He was younger than Doss, with the permanent squint of a man who has spent his life judging distances on bad roads, and he had the smuggler's fatal flaw: he believed that everyone, given enough pressure, becomes a smuggler. That loyalty is just a price nobody's met yet. He'd held out for nearly two days out of genuine feeling for Voss — I'll grant him that; there was real affection there, the kind that can't be bought, only starved — and then I mentioned, gently, over the watered wine, that Voss herself had been seen meeting with a crown rider outside Highmoor.

She hadn't. But he didn't know she hadn't, and the idea did the work of a crowbar.

"There's a map," he said, staring at the table. "Not ours. Ours are copies. This one's older — came off a dying courier on the temple stairs, Voss thinks. Scribe has it. The one who draws for us. Theo Dunmore."

"Dunmore," I repeated, and wrote the name down slowly, because writing slowly suggests significance, and also because the name *was* significant, though not for any reason Wexler could guess. "A temple scribe holding a courier's map. And where does this courier's map lead?"

"Down." Wexler's voice had gone flat, the flatness of a man past the point of choosing what he says. "Under Thornfell. To a place the old routes mark but none of us go. Voss calls it the Undervault. There's a symbol on the master chart, been there since her grandmother's day, and her grandmother said her grandmother wouldn't draw it straight — always left a gap in it, like she was afraid finishing the line would finish something else."

"What symbol?"

He drew it for me in the spilled wine with one finger. A circle, broken. A ring with a gap in it, like a chain waiting for a link.

I looked at it for a long moment.

I had seen that symbol before. Once. In the sealed annex of the royal archive, on the outer margin of the original lease, drawn small and careful by a chancery clerk three centuries dead — the counterseal of the court below, the mark the Undervault presses into every contract it holds. The clerks called it *the open ring*. Debt uncollected. A promise still running.

And now a temple scribe was carrying a map to it, and the fire above the capital was guttering like a candle in a doorway, and somewhere in the arithmetic of my mandate those facts were adding up to a sum I did not yet know but could feel approaching, the way you feel weather in an old scar.

"Where is Voss now?" I asked.

"Gone to ground. Or gone after the scribe." Wexler laughed, short and ugly. "Or both. She wants that map back, inquisitor. Bad. She paid good coin for routes off that scribe for years, and now the scribe's run off with something bigger, and Voss doesn't forgive a debt."

"No," I said. "Few people do. It's the great failure of our species." I stood, gathered my folder, and paused at the door with the lamp, because a good exit line is worth ten hours of interrogation. "Wexler. One more thing. The courier who died on the temple stairs. Before he died — did he say anything? Anything at all?"

Wexler frowned, dredging. "Voss heard it secondhand. Said the courier grabbed hold of the scribe and told them—" he squinted at the middle distance, retrieving it—" *don't let them read your name aloud.*"

The cell was quiet. Somewhere above us, the garrison changed watch, boots on stone, the ordinary heartbeat of an ordinary fortress.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, which unsettled him more than any threat could have.

* * *

That night I wrote my report to the Chancellor, and because reports travel slowly and truth travels slower, I wrote two.

The first was the official one: *Smuggler network confirms a document of antiquity in the possession of one Theo Dunmore, indentured scribe of the Order of the Quiet Flame, now fled the temple. Document believed to concern passages beneath Thornfell terminating at a location matching the counterseal on the original instrument. Pursuit authorized. Subject considered asset, not enemy — recovery, not punishment.*

The second went into my private book, the one I keep against the day someone asks me what I knew and when. In it I wrote:

*Dunmore. The name is on the original rolls. I checked before leaving the capital — I check everything, it is the whole of my talent — and the surname appears exactly once in the lease documents, in the signature line of the tendering party. One soul, whole and willing, tendered by T. Dunmore. Age nine.*

*I had assumed the line extinct, or the record garbled. Chancery records are garbled the way rivers are wet — it is their nature. But a scribe of that name has spent years drawing maps toward the Undervault without knowing why, and a dying courier pressed the way into their hands, and the last words spoken over them were a warning about their own name.*

*Either this is coincidence, and I have built a career on the assumption that coincidence is merely pattern arriving early.*

*Or the signer has come back to the signing.*

I put down the pen and sat a while in the lamplight, listening to the mountain breathe around me. Below my feet, the cells. Below the cells, older stone. Below the stone, a door that moved, according to the smugglers' oldest charts — a door that walked the mountain looking for debtors, which meant it was looking for everyone, which meant it was patient, which was the most frightening thing about it. Institutions understand patience. I recognized a colleague when I saw one.

The Chancellor wants the contract recovered so the crown can sign again — renew the lease, keep the fire, keep the power that flows from being the kingdom that holds a dragon's soul in escrow. She does not care about the signer. Signers are furniture to her.

But I had read the original instrument, every clause, including the one the Chancellor's briefers had skipped because it was filed under theology rather than law. It said the contract bound the signer. Not the crown. The signer. The debt ran through T. Dunmore's name like a vein through marble, and while it stood unpaid, the crown's claim was secure — and if the signer ever dissolved it, forgave it, refused it —

Well. Then the fire went out for good, and the crown's mandate died on the page, and everything I had been sent to preserve would end in a single sentence spoken aloud in the dark.

Which meant, working the problem backward the way I work every problem, that the most dangerous person in the kingdom was not a smuggler queen with a knife collection, nor a disgraced knight whose file had crossed my desk two days ago with the notation *deserted — possibly compromised by the Vault*, and certainly not the Order of the Quiet Flame with its candles and its careful blindness.

The most dangerous person in the kingdom was a tired scribe with ink-stained fingers, walking toward a door that knew their name.

I closed my private book. I called for my horse to be saddled before dawn, and for Wexler to be fed — actually fed, not gruel; gratitude extracted under warmth lasts longer than gratitude extracted under fear — and I stood at the arrow-slit window watching the east go grey over Thornfell.

Somewhere down in that massif, behind stone that hadn't been stone yesterday, a lantern of gold fire was guttering. Above the capital, its twin was doing the same. Between them, unwinding through the dark like a fuse, was a paper trail that ended in a child's signature.

I am not a cruel man. But I am a thorough one, and the crown pays me to be thorough on its behalf, and by whatever means is a wide country indeed.

"Inquisitor," the sergeant said at the door. "Your horse."

"Yes," I said. "Let's go meet the debtor."
