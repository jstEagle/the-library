## 12. Salt and Cinder Pass

The pass was called Salt and Cinder because, according to Wren, everything in this country was named after whatever had last killed someone there.

"Cheerful," I said.

"I find it efficient." She walked ahead of me on the switchback trail, boots finding purchase on rock that looked like it had been chewed and spat out by something large and bored. "You want a mountain named *Hope's Rest*, you'll get pilgrims. You get pilgrims, you get toll-keepers. You get toll-keepers—" she gestured vaguely at the whole grey expanse of the world, "—you get what happened to us three days ago."

She had a point. The Toll House was still fresh in both our minds, though for very different reasons. For Wren it was a transaction: one memory, a name of some childhood acquaintance, paid out as easily as a coin from a purse. For me it had been a revelation about the architecture of the universe — namely, that the universe kept books, and the books were hungry.

I'd spent my whole life around ledgers. I knew how they worked. What unsettled me was how well this one did.

The wind came up the pass in long flat shoves, carrying salt dust off the flats below and cinder grit from some ancient burn higher up. It got into everything. My eyes, my teeth, the seams of my pack, the margins of my thoughts. By midday I'd stopped trying to talk much, which suited us both, since talking meant deciding what we were to each other, and neither of us had found a word for that yet.

*Allies* was too generous. *Hostages of circumstance* was closer but too many syllables for a mountainside.

We climbed.

* * *

By late afternoon the trail narrowed to something the smuggler maps called *the Ledge*, drawn in Brother Vessel's cramped hand with a note beside it: *do not look down, do not stop, do not trust the third handhold.* I had always assumed the note was decorative. Smugglers were a superstitious lot who wrote warnings like sailors painted eyes on prows — ritual more than instruction.

I revised this opinion when Wren tested the third handhold, put her full weight on it, and the entire shelf of stone detached from the cliff face like a page torn from a book.

She didn't scream. That's the thing I remember most clearly, later, when I made myself remember it at all. She didn't scream; she made a sound worse than screaming — a short, furious exhale, the noise of someone deeply annoyed at physics — and then she dropped.

I lunged. I don't know what I thought I was going to do. I'm a scribe. My hands are trained for quills and fine brushwork, not for catching falling knights. But I got her wrist, or part of it — fingers scrabbling, catching cloth, catching skin — and the sudden weight of her nearly took me over the edge too, and for one long horrible moment we were both just meat and momentum arguing with gravity, and gravity was winning on points.

Then her free hand found a crack in the rock. Then her boots found the wall. And then she hauled herself up with the casual obscene strength of people who train for things, and rolled onto the ledge beside me, breathing hard, one sleeve torn to the shoulder and blood welling up along her forearm where the rock had taken its toll in the old-fashioned currency.

"You're bleeding," I said, brilliantly.

"Noted." She sat up, wincing. "Third handhold."

"What?"

"The map," she said. "Your map. There was a note. I saw you mouthing it as I stepped past. Something about a handhold." Her jaw tightened. "You might have said it aloud."

"You might have asked before walking ahead of me for six hours."

Fair warning: Wren could have broken my arm with that sentence alone if she'd wanted to. Instead she laughed — one bark of it, surprised out of her — and then pressed her good hand against her arm and hissed through her teeth.

"Let me see it," I said, and this time it wasn't a question, and she let me.

It wasn't deep. Rock wounds never are; they're just ugly and honest. I cleaned it with water we couldn't afford to spare, tore a strip off the hem of my robe — the Order would have opinions about that, if the Order ever saw me again, which felt increasingly theoretical — and bound it while she watched me with an expression I couldn't read.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Bandaged people? No. Copied medical texts for the infirmary, yes. There's a difference between knowing where a bandage goes and knowing why."

"It held. That's the why that matters."

We sat there on the ledge with the drop yawning below us and the light going long and coppery, and the silence settled in, and I could feel it wanting to become a conversation. Some silences are empty. This one was full — crowded with everything we'd been not saying since the road out of Thornfell.

Wren broke it first, which surprised me. She struck me as a person who could out-stubborn a glacier.

"When I woke up in that ditch," she said, "three days missing, sword notched with dragon-scale, I assumed I'd been drinking. Or fighting. Or drinking and fighting, which is the traditional combination." She turned her arm over, looking at the bandage rather than at me. "But that's not it."

"No?"

"No." A pause. Wind. Somewhere below us, loose stone clattered down into the dark like small applause. "Theo. Have you ever lost time? Not forgotten — forgotten is soft. Lost. Like a room in your house that's been bricked over, and you know the shape of it from the outside."

My mouth went dry. "Why are you asking me?"

"Because when I look at you sometimes," she said, "you flinch. Like someone's knocked on that brick."

I should have lied. I was, by then, a practiced liar — I'd lied to enforcers of the Quiet Flame with Brother Cassian's calm settling over my shoulders like a borrowed cloak. But sitting on a ledge with my heart still banging from almost watching her die, lying felt like a luxury item, priced out of reach.

"My mother died when I was nine," I said. "I remember the year of it the way you remember a bad storm. Shapes. Sounds. I remember being sad without remembering her face." I picked at the salt grit on the stone between us. "Brother Cassian told me grief does that. Takes the sharp edges first."

"And you believed him?"

"He had no reason to lie to me."

"Everyone has reasons," Wren said, not unkindly. "Some people just keep them in better vaults."

* * *

We couldn't make the far side of the pass before dark — the maps were explicit about that, another of Vessel's marginalia: *the pass eats the unwary after sundown, and it doesn't spit them back out polite* — so we wedged ourselves into a hollow beneath an overhang, built a fire small enough to hide from anything watching, and ate the last honest food: dried lamb, hard bread, a wedge of salt-packed cheese that tasted mostly of its own packing.

Wren sat across the fire with her knees up and her sword across her lap, doing that thing she did every night: drawing her thumb along the blade, stopping at each notch. Counting them. There were seven. I'd watched her count them every night since the road, and every night she reached the same total and looked, briefly, like a woman reading a letter in a language she used to speak.

"Tell me," I said. "About the ditch. About the three days. If you want."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't. Then she laid the sword flat across her knees, so the firelight caught all seven notches at once.

"This is dragon-scale," she said, tapping the deepest notch. "Or was. Whatever's left of it now. And here's what I know, and what I know is almost nothing." She held up fingers. "One: I was a knight. That's not memory, that's habit — my body remembers forms my head doesn't. Two: I served someone. A banner, an oath, a name — gone. Three:" — the third finger, and here her voice changed, went flat and careful, the way people speak when they've rehearsed a thing precisely so it won't hurt — "three: I have been inside the Undervault."

The fire popped. Neither of us moved.

"How do you know," I said, "if you don't remember?"

"Because of what's *left*." She touched her temple, just above the ear. "When I try to reach for those three days, I don't hit nothing. Nothing would be restful. I hit a — texture. Like running your tongue over a tooth that's been pulled. The shape of absence. And I know — the way you know the difference between a door that's locked and a door that's been walled up — I know it wasn't taken. It was *paid*. I walked in owing something, and I walked out having settled the account, and the account was me."

I thought of the toll-keeper's smiling patience. Of Wren handing over that name — *a boy I grew up with, I think, I can't even miss him properly* — with the ease of long practice.

"That's why you didn't hesitate at the Toll House," I said.

"I hesitated plenty. I just didn't argue." She fed a stick to the fire. "Arguing with the mountain is how you end up paying interest. I learned that. Not how I learned it — but that I learned it. Do you understand the distinction? It's the worst thing about it. I have conclusions without memories. I know the Undervault the way a scar knows the knife."

"Why go back?" The question came out harder than I intended. "If your own instincts say stay away — why follow that pull down the mountain? Why ambush me on the road?"

"Compulsion," she said. "Or debt. Or —" she stopped, and I watched her decide to be honest, watched her jaw set the way it did before she stepped onto bad handholds. "Or recognition. When I first saw you, on the road, sword at your throat — I expected fear. Everyone fears the sword. You looked at me like you were *checking*. Like you had a list, and I might be on it."

I said nothing. The firelight moved on her face.

"And that frightened me more than any knight I've ever crossed blades with," she said quietly, "because it meant you knew something about me that I didn't. So I followed you. Call it compulsion. Call it the oldest instinct there is: when you've lost something, follow the person holding the list."

"Wren—"

"Don't apologize. Don't explain. I'm not asking you to open your bricks tonight." She picked the sword up again, slid it home in its scabbard with a click that sounded final. "I'm telling you this so you know what I am. A woman with holes in her, walking toward the thing that made them, because the alternative is standing still and letting the holes define her." A pause. "And so you know that whatever you're carrying — whatever made you flinch — I won't ask you to pay it before you're ready. I've seen what forced payments cost."

I should have told her then. About the map. About the symbol that matched the fragments behind my own bricked-over years. About the courier's last words — *don't let them read your name aloud* — and how often I turned them over at night like a coin I couldn't spend.

Instead I said, "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

She looked at me for a long moment. She knew. That was the thing — she absolutely knew I was withholding, and she let me, and somehow that mercy was heavier than any demand would have been.

"All right," she said, and lay back against her pack, and within minutes her breathing went slow and even. Knights, I supposed, slept when they could. It was probably in the training.

* * *

I kept the watch badly.

The stars over the pass were brutal and close, and the wind talked to itself in the cinder fields below, and I sat there turning the courier's map case over in my hands without opening it. I'd opened it a hundred times. I knew every line of it. What I couldn't stop doing was the other thing — reaching back toward those bricked-over rooms in myself, pressing my palm flat against the wall, feeling for the knock.

Nine years old. A year shaped like a storm. My mother's breath going shallow in a room that smelled of tallow and fever, and —

and a visitor. There had been a visitor. I had always known there had been a visitor; it was the one shard too sharp to bury. Tall, wrong, patient. A voice like paper folding. And me — small, terrified, desperate enough to listen — and a bargain struck in words I hadn't understood because I was nine and children sign things they don't read.

*Don't let them read your name aloud.*

Behind me, Wren stirred. Not waking — shifting in sleep, murmuring something. A fragment. "...not the gate... the *ledger*..." Then she subsided, and the wind took the words, and I sat with my heart hammering and the map case cold in my hands.

In the morning, I decided. In the morning I'd tell her everything — the symbol, the fragments, all of it. She'd given me her holes; the least I owed her was mine.

That was the plan, right up until dawn came grey over the pass, and with it, far below on the trail we'd climbed yesterday, came figures. Six of them. Moving fast. Moving with the unhurried competence of people who knew these roads better than their own prayers.

Through the thin morning air, faint but unmistakable, drifted the sound of Maren Voss laughing.

She'd found my maps' next mistake — or her own. Either way, she was climbing.

And she was two hours behind us, gaining.
