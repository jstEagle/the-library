## 13. The Village That Forgot Its Name

*Theo*

The village announced itself by smell before anything else—woodsmoke and baking bread, the ordinary perfume of ordinary life—and I nearly wept with relief. Three days in the high passes will make a person sentimental about bread.

"Smoke means people," Wren said, stating the obvious with the flat authority of someone who had once been paid to state obvious things for a living. "People mean questions."

"People mean food," I said. "I'm prepared to answer an alarming number of questions for stew."

We came down off the scree into a valley so green it looked painted, terraced fields climbing both slopes, a river running silver through the middle of everything, and at the center a village of maybe two hundred souls with slate roofs and whitewashed walls and laundry strung between windows like festival bunting. A church bell—no, not a church bell; I corrected myself as we got closer—a *toll* bell hung in a small stone tower at the village square, and beneath it stood a low building with no windows that I would later learn was called, simply, the House.

Everything about the place was lovely. That was the first wrong thing. Mountain villages this remote are usually held together by suspicion and weatherproofing; they squint at strangers and count their chickens twice. This one glowed. As we walked down the lane, people looked up from their work and *smiled*—not the wary half-smile you give travelers, but full, warm, unhurried smiles, the kind people give family.

"Good morning," said a man mending a fence, though the light said late afternoon.

"Good afternoon," Wren replied carefully.

"Good morning," he agreed pleasantly, and went back to his fence.

"He's wrong about the time," I murmured.

"He's wrong about more than that," Wren said. Her hand had drifted to her sword hilt, which for Wren was roughly equivalent to another person screaming. "Look at their eyes."

I looked. And I stopped looking quite so quickly, because what I saw made my stomach do something complicated. The villagers' eyes were fine—clear, healthy, kind. That was the problem. There was nothing behind the kindness. No flicker of assessment, no calculation, none of the quick animal arithmetic every person runs on strangers: *threat or friend, useful or dangerous, do I know you.* They looked at us the way you look at clouds. Pleasantly. Without consequence.

"They've paid," I said quietly. "All of them. This is a toll village."

"You can tell that from a smile?"

"I can tell that from a fence." I nodded at the man mending it. He'd been mending the same three slats since we'd come into view, in the same order, over and over. Pick up slat. Nail slat. Put down hammer. Pick up slat. "He's not repairing the fence. He's performing the memory of repairing it. Someone taught him the motions and the meaning wore off."

Wren watched for a moment. Her jaw tightened. "Like me," she said.

"No," I said, before I could stop myself, and then, because lying to her had become harder than telling the truth, "not like you. You're missing pieces. They're missing the *wants*. Look—he doesn't care about the fence. He doesn't care about anything. Caring is a memory too, maybe. Or it needs memories to grow on."

She was quiet for a long moment. "The toll house on the pass took one name from me and I've been grieving it for days like a lost tooth. This whole valley—" She shook her head. "How many years did this take? How many visits?"

"Generations, probably. One small payment at a time. A birthday here. A grudge there. Nobody pays enough all at once to notice they're being emptied." I heard my own voice go dry and bitter and didn't correct it. "That's how the mountain feeds, apparently. Not by robbing you. By *accommodating* you."

A woman emerged from the nearest house carrying a tray of small honey cakes and beamed at us with such uncomplicated delight that I felt my guard physically lower itself, which frightened me more than any knife could have.

"Travelers!" she cried. "You must eat! You must rest! It's been so long since we had travelers!"

"How long?" Wren asked.

The woman's smile didn't waver, but something behind it went smooth and blank, like water closing over a dropped stone. "Oh," she said, "it's been so long since we had travelers. Please, eat. Honey and flour from our own bees, our own field. You'll want for nothing here."

We ate the cakes. I'm not proud of it, but we'd been eating hardtack and self-pity for three days, and the cakes were genuinely excellent. The woman—Marta, she said, or she said a word that might have been Marta—watched us eat with the same placid pleasure, hands folded, occasionally repeating, softly, as if to herself: "Our own bees. Our own field."

It was while I was licking honey off my thumb that the old woman found us.

* * *

*Wren*

The old woman came out of the last house on the square, moving slowly, leaning on a stick of black mountain-ash, and everything about her was different from the others. She didn't smile. She looked at Theo the way you look at a door you thought you'd bricked up years ago and found standing open.

She stopped three paces away. Up close I could see she was very old—not village-old, but old the way bedrock is old, old in a way that should have required more food than this valley seemed able to remember providing. Her eyes were clear. Clearer than anyone's. That was the horror of it: everyone else had given pieces away, and she had kept *everything*, and you could see what keeping everything had cost, because her eyes were full to the brim and shaking.

"Tomas," she said.

Theo blinked. "No, ma'am. My name is—"

"Tomas." She said it again, gently, the way you'd repeat yourself to someone who was being slow, or deaf, or in denial. "You've grown. They said you wouldn't come back, but I told them. I told them the debt always comes home." She reached out and touched Theo's cheek with fingers like dry leaves. "You sold well, little one. Everyone said so. Such a fair price. Such a *willing* seller."

Theo had gone very still. I've seen soldiers take wounds with less stillness than that.

"Ma'am," I said, stepping slightly forward, "you've mistaken him for someone else."

"Her," the old woman said, without looking at me. "Her. Tomas was a girl when she left. Or near enough. Children are all the same until the mountain sorts them." Her hand stayed on Theo's cheek. "You have her chin. You have her way of standing, like the ground owes you money."

"Theo," Theo said hoarsely. "My name is Theo."

Something moved across the old woman's face—not confusion exactly. Recognition of a different kind. "That's not a name," she said. "That's a *sound*. Names have weight. Names have debts attached." She finally turned her head toward me, and I did not enjoy being seen by her. "And you. Knight. You smell of the vault. You've been in the dark under the mountain and come out with holes in you."

I didn't answer. Answering felt like paying.

"She's ill," said Marta the cake-woman, appearing at my elbow with terrifying gentleness. "Grandmother Sef remembers things that aren't true. We love her anyway." She smiled her bottomless smile. "Would you like more cakes?"

"Grandmother Sef," Theo repeated. Their voice had gone strange—careful, the voice of a scribe handling a page that might crumble. "Sef. Is that your name, grandmother? Do you remember it?"

"Oh, I remember everything," the old woman said, and smiled for the first time, and it was the worst thing I had seen in weeks, worse than the toll-keeper's scales, worse than the notched sword I couldn't explain. Because her smile was *proud*. "That's my payment, child. I'm the only one in this valley who kept it all. Everyone else paid in pieces. I paid in *witness*. I remember every bargain ever struck under this roof, every name surrendered, every year handed over the counter. I hold it all so the village doesn't have to." Her grip on Theo's cheek tightened, gentle and terrible. "Someone has to remember what they were. Otherwise how would they know what they gave up? Otherwise it isn't a price at all. It's just... weather."

"And what did you get," Theo asked, "for holding all that?"

"To stay," she said simply. "To keep my Tomas's face in my head until she came home. However long. However *changed*." Her eyes searched Theo's face with an hunger that made me step back. "You came through the pass. You're going down, aren't you. To the court. To settle."

"We're passing through," I said.

"Nobody passes through," Sef said. "The road only goes two ways now. Down to pay, or away to owe." She released Theo's cheek at last and stepped back, leaning on her ash stick. "Go and sleep in the guesthouse. Eat. Rest. They'll be kind to you—it's the kindest place in the world, because kindness is easy when you can't remember why you'd be cruel." She turned away, then paused. "But don't stay longer than one night. Kindness is easy to live in. That's the trap of it. Ask anyone here." She gestured at the smiling, working, empty-eyed village with a tenderness that broke something in my chest. "Ask me. I'm the only one who could answer."

* * *

*Theo*

They gave us the guesthouse: a clean room, a jug of cider, bread still warm. Through the window I watched the village perform its evening—the same smiles, the same unhurried motions, children laughing at nothing with total commitment. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune that circled back on itself every eight bars, endlessly content.

Wren sat sharpening her sword by candlelight, which I'd learned was how she prayed.

"One night," she said. "Then we move."

"Yes."

"The old woman knew you."

"The old woman knew a girl named Tomas who sold something, a long time ago, and never came back." I turned the cider jug in my hands. "Which is a coincidence I don't believe in anymore. Nothing about me has been a coincidence since the courier died on those stairs."

"You think she recognized you. Actually you."

"I think the mountain has good records." I laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow. "Gods, Wren. Did you see them? The *contentment*? That man with the fence will die happy. He'll die happy and he won't know his own mother's face, and he'll die *happy*, and some part of me stood in that square today and thought—" I stopped.

"Thought what?"

"Thought it looked *restful*." I said it fast, before I could lose my nerve. "There's a version of me that would trade a great deal to stop carrying all this. The ledgers, the debt, the years owed, the map burning a hole in my coat. Every night I lie down doing arithmetic and every morning I wake up already behind. And that village has solved it. They've just... put the arithmetic down. Forever." I looked at my hands, ink-stained, calloused, mine. "What frightens me isn't them, Wren. It's how much I understood them."

Wren set down the whetstone. For a moment she was quiet, and then she said, in the tone of someone reporting terrain, "When the toll-keeper took the name from me, I told you I barely missed it. That was true. That's what scared me—that it was *true*. I could feel the shape where it had been, smooth and painless, like a healed wound. If he'd offered to take ten more, right then, I honestly don't know..." She shook her head. "So yes. I understand them too. That's why we leave at dawn."

"Dawn," I agreed.

But sleep didn't come easily, and when it came, it brought the dream it had been bringing for days: a masked figure bending over a child's bed, a scroll unrolling, a small hand—my hand, smaller, nine years old—reaching for a quill. In the dream the old woman's voice followed me down into the dark: *the debt always comes home.*

I woke before dawn to the sound of the toll bell ringing in the square—not the slow domestic rhythm of the day before, but fast, urgent, wrong. Wren was already up, sword in hand, at the window.

"Theo," she said. "You need to see this."

Below, in the grey light, the villagers had gathered in the square—all of them, every soul, standing in perfect silence, faces turned up toward the tower. And descending the tower steps, unhurried, its feet making no sound on the stone, came a figure in a mask of smoke and bone.

The masks had found us. Or rather—they had come home to collect, in the one village that had already paid everything, and I understood with cold certainty that they hadn't come for the villagers at all.

Every blank, smiling face in the square turned, in perfect unison, toward our window.
