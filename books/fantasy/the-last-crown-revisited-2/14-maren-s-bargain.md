## 14. Maren's Bargain

The inquisitor had a tent the color of old teeth, and he received me sitting down, which told me everything I needed to know about him.

Men who make you stand while they sit want you to feel small before they've even learned your name. Men who offer you a chair want something. Ashgrave poured tea himself—poured it badly, over the rim of the cup, onto his own gloved hand without flinching—and slid it across the camp table between us like we were two merchants haggling over wool.

"Captain Voss," he said. "Or is it *former* Captain Voss? The guild rolls are unclear."

"The guild rolls are wrong," I said, and drank the tea. It was terrible. Oversteeped, bitter as a widow's prayer. I'd drunk worse in worse tents for worse men. "I left the guild. They kept the name."

"They keep more than names, from what my people tell me." He folded his hands on the table. Grey gloves, grey coat, grey eyes with a shine to them like coins at the bottom of a well—you could see them if you leaned far enough in, and you'd regret what you saw. "They say you moved contraband through Thornfell for eleven years without losing a single shipment. They say you knew every goat path, every smuggler stair, every cave with a back door and every toll-keeper willing to look elsewhere. They say you were the best route-writer the mountains ever produced."

"And then?"

"Then a temple scribe sold your routes to the Order for pocket change, and you lost everything."

There it was. He'd done his reading. I let the silence stretch long enough that he'd know I wasn't rattled, and not so long that he'd think I was stalling. A negotiation has a rhythm to it, same as a knife fight. You don't lead with the blade.

"Dunmore didn't sell to the Order," I said. "Dunmore sold to *me*. Copying smuggler routes is how a scribe pays down an indenture. I paid fair. What Dunmore did with the coin after that—" I shrugged. "Not my ledger."

"But the routes came to the Order regardless. And now there's a map missing, and a courier dead on a temple stair, and a scribe gone into the night with something the crown wants very badly." Ashgrave turned his cup slowly on its saucer, one quarter-turn at a time, like a man winding a lock. "You've been following them. My outriders found your cold camps three days behind theirs, moving faster than three days behind should allow."

"I'm good at what I do."

"You are. Which is why I'm offering you amnesty instead of a cell." He said it lightly, but I watched his hands when he said it. Still. Too still. A man's hands will tell on him before his face does; faces can be trained, hands mostly can't. His hands said: *this offer matters to me.* That made me curious. Curiosity, in my trade, is the most expensive thing you can carry, because everyone wants to sell you something to satisfy it. "Full pardon. Restoration of your guild standing, if you want it—which you don't, but the paperwork would be real. And five hundred crowns."

Five hundred crowns. I'd buried two hundred under a cairn above Salt Pass, and another hundred sewn into the lining of a coat I no longer owned. Five hundred crowns was enough to buy a boat and never see a mountain again.

"What do you want for it?"

"Dunmore's route." He reached beneath the table and produced a leather folio, thumbed soft at the corners, and opened it facing me. Maps. Copies—the ink was too even, the hand too careful, but I knew the original work underneath it the way you know your own signature forged by someone competent. Theo Dunmore's maps. Every one I'd bought, copied into the Order's archive, and now copied again into whatever this was. "The courier carried a document out of the Order's restricted archive. Dunmore has it. My mandate is to recover it. I need to know where they're going."

"And you think I know because—"

"Because you wrote half those roads yourself, and because you're the only person alive angry enough at that scribe to have spent the last week hunting them across frozen ground for free."

He wasn't wrong, which annoyed me. Anger is a fuel, and I'd been burning it since the night I'd learned my best routes were in an Order archive and my name was attached to them. Eleven years of work, catalogued by a temple clerk. If the Order ever decided smugglers were worth crushing instead of merely taxing, every road I'd ever cut would become a noose with my neck in it.

So yes. I'd followed. Not out of love for the crown.

"All right," I said. "Show me what you have, and I'll tell you where they're going."

Ashgrave turned the folio fully toward me, and I bent over Theo Dunmore's maps like a priest over scripture, and for a long while neither of us spoke.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about maps: they lie. All of them, always, in small ways—a stream drawn a mile east of truth, a pass marked wider than it is, a village placed where a village used to be before the plague took it. Cartographers call it artistic license. Smugglers call it insurance. A route-map that anyone could read is a route-map that gets you hanged, so every good mapmaker builds in errors, and every good reader knows which errors are lies and which are just laziness.

Theo Dunmore was not lazy. I'd known that from the first map I'd bought off them—cramped, precise, obsessive little script, elevations noted in a margin code, water sources marked with a symbol I'd never seen anywhere else. Buying Dunmore's work had felt like buying a knife already sharpened. So when I read these copies, I read them the way you read a lover's letters after the betrayal: looking for what they weren't saying.

"There," I said, after the better part of an hour.

Ashgrave looked up. "There where?"

"The Salt and Cinder approach. Here—" I put my finger on the pass where the map showed the trail bending north around a rockfall, "—and here." I moved my finger to a second copy, an earlier draft, dated four months prior. Same pass. The trail bent south.

"That's a drafting error," Ashgrave said. "Scribes correct their work."

"Scribes correct *spelling*," I said. "This isn't correction. Look at the third copy." There were seven drafts in the folio, spanning nearly two years. I laid them side by side along the table, weighting the corners with the teapot, an apple, my knife, and Ashgrave's own inkwell, which he surrendered without comment. Seven versions of the same mountain country. And through all seven, one feature stayed fixed—every peak, every river, every ruin exactly where it belonged—except one.

A blank space on the western face of the massif. Unmarked in every draft. But the *blank space moved.*

In the earliest map, it sat in a fold of cliff above the tree line, roughly where an old shrine stood—I'd sheltered in that shrine myself twice, and there was nothing above it but scree and sky. Two drafts later, the blank had drifted a half-mile north. In the latest, it sat above the high valley where the shepherd settlements thinned out, near ground I'd always avoided because the goats wouldn't graze there and goats know things.

"You see it?" I said. "Everything else is stone-steady across two years of drafts. This—" I tapped each blank in turn, "—walks."

Ashgrave had gone very quiet. Quiet men frighten me less than loud ones, but quiet *interested* men are the worst of both worlds. "It's a deliberate blind spot," he said finally. "Dunmore hid something."

"Dunmore hides things *in* places, not around them. You don't draw a hole that moves. You can't. Unless—" I stopped, because the thought arrived all at once, ugly and complete, the way bad news does. "Unless the thing itself moves. Unless Dunmore wasn't mapping a place. Dunmore was mapping a *door*."

"A door to where?"

"To wherever the courier's document says." I straightened up and looked at him properly for the first time. "What is it you're recovering, inquisitor? Because I've moved relics, fugitives, tax-dodging nobles, and once an entire altar nobody asked questions about, and I've never seen the crown pay five hundred crowns and a pardon for a piece of paper. Whatever's written on that document, it's worth more than paper. And whatever door it opens—" I tapped the walking blank space one last time, "—it doesn't stay put. Which means your soldiers, with their survey charts and their fixed positions and their lovely supply lines, are going to arrive at a cliff that was a door yesterday and isn't today."

Something moved behind the coin-shine of his eyes. Not doubt. Recalculation. I'd seen that look on buyers realizing the goods were rarer than advertised, and it never meant anything good for the seller.

"Then I'll need someone who understands how it moves," Ashgrave said.

And there it was. The hook under the bait. I sat back down slowly, because my legs had opinions about standing, and took stock.

Here's what I had: a pardon I didn't trust, five hundred crowns I did, a grudge against a scribe I'd liked more than was professional, and a dawning, sickening suspicion that Theo Dunmore hadn't sold me out at all—that Dunmore had sold me *routes*, honestly rendered, and that the walking hole in those routes was something Dunmore had been circling for years the way a moth circles a lamp, and that I'd been paid in good coin to help a moth find its flame. Here's what I also had: a company of eight smugglers who'd followed me out of loyalty or habit or the absence of better options, and whose names were now in an Order archive because of routes I'd bought, and whose lives would hang off whatever I did next.

Self-interest is a clean compass. It's why I trust it. But a compass only tells you which way north is; it doesn't tell you whether north is somewhere you want to go.

"I'll take your deal," I heard myself say. "Amnesty for my people first—in writing, tonight, with the guild seal. Then I take you to where the door was last, and I get you inside its reach. After that, inquisitor, we renegotiate."

"Agreed," said Ashgrave, too quickly, and wrote the pardon right there in his terrible oversteeped-tea handwriting, and I watched his hands while he did it, and his hands were steady, and that frightened me more than anything else in the tent.

* * *

I walked back to my camp through the soldier-lines in the dark, past fires and pickets and men who watched me the way dogs watch a fox that's been granted passage. Brannic had our fire built low, the way I taught him, and the others were pretending to sleep, which meant they were listening, which meant I gave them the news plainly: deal struck, pardon coming, we move at dawn, we're done running from the crown and starting to run *for* it, and any man who wants out can take his share and walk, no hard feelings.

Nobody walked. That's either loyalty or foolishness, and with my crew it's usually both.

I sat up alone after, feeding the fire splinters of pine, and took out the one map I'd kept for myself—the original I'd bought from Dunmore two years ago, the first one, the one that started everything. I'd told Ashgrave I'd surrendered all my copies. I'd surrendered all my copies. This one wasn't a copy. A distinction a lawyer would call fraud and I'd call survival.

By firelight I found it again: the blank space, walking. On this, the oldest version, it sat above the ruined shrine. Above the shrine where, on my second visit, I'd woken in the night certain someone had spoken my full name in a voice like dry paper, and found nothing but wind, and told myself nothing but wind, and never quite believed it.

Goats won't graze there. Doors walk. Debts, I'd heard said once by a toll-keeper with too many fingers, debts are the only roads the mountain respects.

I fed the map to the fire and watched nine-year-old ink curl and blacken, and I told myself the same clean lie I always tell: *this is business.* Get the pardon. Get the crowns. Keep my people breathing. Whatever debt Theo Dunmore is carrying into that mountain, it's not mine, and whatever walks inside those walls, it's not my problem.

Then why, I asked the fire, and the fire being a fire, it didn't answer—why did the blank space on that oldest map sit exactly where a bleeding courier must have come out of the mountain the night he died?

Why does the door open where the debts are thickest?

And why, Maren Voss, eleven years a route-writer, best in the mountains, are you already planning how to lose Ashgrave's soldiers in the passes you know and he doesn't?

Dawn came up grey over Thornfell. I broke camp.
