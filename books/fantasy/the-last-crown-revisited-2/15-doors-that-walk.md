## 15. Doors That Walk

The map had been lying to us for three days, and I finally caught it at it.

We'd made camp in the hollow of a rockfall above Salt and Cinder Pass—Wren's choice, because she said the stone would muffle our fire, and my choice too, because I was tired of arguing with her about anything. The fire was small enough to cup in two hands. Wren sat across from it sharpening her notched sword with the patience of someone who had nothing better to do with her hands and didn't trust them idle. I had the map spread on a flat rock between us, weighted at the corners with pebbles, and I was staring at it the way you stare at a ledger column that won't balance.

"You've been doing that for an hour," Wren said without looking up. "The squinting. Your face is going to set like that."

"The map moved."

"Maps don't move."

"This one does." I turned it toward her. "Look at the entrance mark. Here." Three nights ago, when we'd sheltered in the smuggler waystation below the pass, the door symbol—the little coiled thing like a knot pretending to be a mountain—had sat at the western end of a valley called Hollowmere. Last night, at the toll house, it had been half a day north. Tonight it sat above the tree line near a place marked only with a shepherd's crook and the word *Highmoor*, written so faintly I'd assumed it was water damage.

Wren leaned over and looked. Then she looked at me, then back at the map, and I watched her do the arithmetic every soldier does: is this person mad, or is this real, and which is worse.

"It's in a different place," she said flatly.

"It's always in a different place. It moves."

"Then how are we supposed to walk through a door that walks?"

"That," I said, "is the interesting question."

She gave me the look she reserved for my interesting questions, which was the look of someone being asked to admire a trap she was already standing in. But she put down the sword and came around the fire to crouch beside the map, and that was its own kind of answer. She could have gone anywhere these last days. She kept choosing the map.

"All right, scribe. Impress me."

So I did what I do best, which is talk about documents.

"When I first copied this, I thought it was just a smuggler chart. Routes, caches, sightlines—the usual commerce of not being seen. And most of it is. But the Undervault section isn't drawn like terrain. It's drawn like *bookkeeping*." I tapped the faint annotations crowding the door symbol, tiny figures in a hand older than any scriptorium style I knew. "These aren't distances. They're sums. Look—every route leading to the door passes through a place where something was traded. Here—" a bend in a river where a bridge had once been sold, according to local record; here, a mine sealed after the owner vanished mid-audit; here, the toll house itself, which took Wren's memory of a name as cheerfully as any toll-keeper takes copper.

"You think the door follows debts," Wren said slowly.

"I think the door *is* a debt collection route." I sat back. "Think about what the courier said. Don't let them read your name aloud. Why? Because names are currency down there. Everything is. Memory, time, names—and the court beneath Thornfell doesn't travel to collect. It doesn't have to. It builds its entrances wherever the collateral already is. A bargain made anywhere in these mountains opens a potential door. The entrance settles wherever the unpaid balance is heaviest. It's not hiding from us. It's *waiting for payment*."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind worked at the scree, patient as a creditor.

"So all those villages," Wren said. "The toll houses. The people who smile too much."

"Every one of them is a branch office."

"And the entrance keeps moving because—"

"Because debts get paid, and new ones get made. The door drifts toward whatever's currently owed." I traced the path the symbol had taken over the nights I'd been watching it: west, then north, then up. Always up, and always toward Highmoor. "Which means we don't need to find the door. We need to find the debt. Whoever in these mountains owes the most—that's where it will be standing."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful evenness she used when she was deciding whether to say something true. "And if the biggest debtor turns out to be someone who can't survive losing what they owe?"

"Then we'll be standing at the door watching someone pay it, and hating ourselves, and going through anyway. That's been the plan since the stairwell."

"That's not a plan. That's a confession."

"Wren." I looked at her across the small fire, and I tried to say it plainly, because she'd earned plainness. "Everything about this place runs on exchange. You paid a name at the toll house. The Order pays in years of my life. The crown, if Ashgrave gets his way, will pay in other people. I can't stop the accounting. Nobody can—it's older than the kingdom. But I can read it faster than they can, and I can choose which line items I'm willing to be part of. That's all any of us gets."

She held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then she nodded once, the smallest possible concession, and went back to her sword.

"One more thing," I said. "If I'm right—if the door sits where the debt sits—then whoever owns the largest balance also knows where it is. They may not know they know. But they'll be *drawn* to it. Like you were."

Her whetstone stopped moving. "That's not funny, Theo."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

I rolled the map closed before she could look at me again, and I told myself the shiver down my spine was just the mountain air. But I kept thinking about the courier's blood on my hands, and the word *Highmoor* written so faintly it might have been an apology, and the fact that the map had never once drifted toward the temple where I owed eleven more years of my life.

Maybe the Order's ledgers didn't count. Maybe ink wasn't the same as blood.

Or maybe—said a small cold voice I'd been ignoring since the shrine murals—the map wasn't drifting toward the biggest debt in the mountains at all. Maybe it was drifting toward a specific one. An old one. One signed by a child with a sash, whose mother needed breath, and whose account had been quietly accruing interest for fifteen years without anyone coming to collect.

The fire burned low. Above the pass, the stars came out hard and pitiless, and somewhere beyond them, under the stone, I imagined a door opening politely in a cliff face that hadn't existed yesterday, waiting for a customer it had been promised long ago.

"We leave at first light," I said. "For Highmoor."

Wren sheathed her sword. "And if the shepherd up there asks us why we've come?"

"Then we tell him the truth," I said, and hated how easily it came. "We're collectors. Everyone in these mountains is. The only question left is what we're willing to pay."

* * *

First light found us on the high track, and the mountain found ways to test us both.

The path out of the pass climbed through pine and then through nothing, just grey stone and lichen and a wind with opinions. Wren walked point because she couldn't bear not to, and I walked behind watching the map, and the door symbol stayed exactly where it was for the first time since I'd opened the courier's seal—which frightened me more than the movement had. A drifting door is chaos. A door that has stopped moving has arrived somewhere, and is waiting.

Near midday we passed the first sign that Highmoor was inhabited: a cairn, old and tumbledown, with a wooden charm nailed to it—a shepherd's crook carved from rowan wood, worn smooth by weather and by hands. Someone had tied a strip of cloth to it, faded past color. Offerings, or warnings. On mountains like this, the distinction is mostly administrative.

"There's a story in that," Wren said, nodding at the charm.

"There's a story in everything up here. That's the problem. This whole mountain is a library where every book is a contract."

"You know," she said, falling into step beside me, "when I met you, you were a temple scribe copying scripture by candlelight. Now you're lecturing me about metaphysical bookkeeping on a mountainside. What happened to you?"

"A courier bled on me, and I liked it."

That got a short laugh out of her, startled and rough, and I hoarded it the way I hoarded everything these days—against the certainty that none of it would last.

We crested the shoulder of the mountain in late afternoon, and there it was: Highmoor, a handful of dry-stone shelters clustered around a spring, terraced pasture stitched to the slope like repairs to an old coat. Smoke rose from one chimney, thin and straight in the still air. And beyond the last wall, at the head of the valley where the pasture ended and the cliffs began—

A cliff face that I would have sworn on every ledger I'd ever balanced had no door in it.

But the map said otherwise. The symbol sat there, motionless now, patient as a hook, directly beneath a shelf of overhanging rock. As I watched, the light shifted, and for one heartbeat I saw it: a seam in the stone, tall and narrow, too regular to be geology, veined faintly with gold like a crack filled with cooling candle wax. Then the angle changed and it was just rock again.

"There," I breathed. "You see it?"

"I see rock."

"Wait for the light."

We waited. The sun slid. The seam flashed gold again—there and gone, there and gone, a door blinking at us across the pasture like someone signaling with a lamp.

Wren's hand had gone to her sword. "It knows we're here."

"It doesn't know anything. It's a mechanism." Even as I said it, I didn't believe myself, and neither did she. Doors that walk don't stay mechanical for long. Give a door enough centuries of collecting memories and it develops preferences. Tastes. Patience.

Smoke curled from the single chimney. Where there was smoke, there was a hearth; where there was a hearth, someone to answer questions—or to lie to us beautifully, as everyone in these mountains seemed equipped to do. But I was done approaching doors blind. If the Undervault's entrance parked itself outside this shepherd's wall, then the shepherd was either its keeper, its victim, or its best customer, and in my experience the three categories overlapped more than any of them would admit.

"Food first," Wren said practically. "Questions after. Nobody talks well hungry, and nobody lies well hungry either."

"You're a philosopher."

"I'm hungry."

We ate hard bread and harder cheese in the shadow of the cairn, watching the seam flash and fade, flash and fade, keeping its slow golden time like something breathing in its sleep. And I sat there with crumbs on my fingers and the map heavy against my ribs, and I thought: *somewhere in that house is a person carrying the largest debt in these mountains, and they don't even know the mountain has been billing them.*

I thought about my mother's breath, purchased with a soul I hadn't known I was spending.

I thought: *how much does a man have to owe before the door comes to him?*

The sun dropped behind the western ridge. The seam in the cliff glowed once more—longer this time, brighter, almost eager—and went dark.

Tomorrow, I would knock.
