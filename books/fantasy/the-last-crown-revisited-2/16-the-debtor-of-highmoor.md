## 16. The Debtor of Highmoor

The trouble with following a debt across half a mountain is that debts don't leave footprints.

Theo's map did its moving-door trick again that morning—the inked entrance crawling a thumb-width eastward while we ate cold porridge and pretended not to watch it—and by the time the sun cleared the ridge, the mark had settled on a fold of high pasture called Highmoor, if the shepherd's hut we could see smoking in the distance was anything to go by.

"The person who owes the most," Theo said, for maybe the tenth time since we'd left the village that forgot its name. I'd started keeping count to keep myself sane. "It has to be someone here. The door follows unpaid debts like a dog follows meat."

"You've said."

"I'm saying it again because it keeps being true and I find that upsetting."

I couldn't argue with that. Most of what Theo said lately had the quality of things I found upsetting but couldn't argue with. We'd been walking together long enough now—two weeks, three? I'd lost track somewhere around the toll house where I'd paid away a name I couldn't even miss properly—that I'd developed a feel for Theo's silences too. There was the silence of a scribe double-checking his work. And there was the silence of a man looking at something he recognized.

He'd gone quiet at the village. Quieter still when we climbed out of it.

So we climbed toward the smoke.

* * *

The hut sat alone above the treeline like something the mountain had forgotten to swallow. Stone walls, turf roof, a fold for sheep below it, and an old man mending a wall with hands that moved slow and sure, the way hands move when they've done a thing ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more because there's nothing else.

He saw us coming from a long way off. Shepherds always do. He didn't stop working, just kept fitting stones while we crossed the last field, and when we were close enough he spoke without looking up.

"Travelers don't come to Highmoor. Nothing up here worth traveling for."

"We're not looking for something worth having," Theo said. "We're looking for something owed."

That got him. He set down his stone and looked at us properly—first me, then Theo, then back to me, and something in his face did a strange stuttering thing, like a man hearing a song he used to know.

"You're her," he said. To me. "You're the one who came before. Aren't you?"

"I've never been here," I said.

"No," he agreed mildly. "But you're the one who came before." He stood, joints cracking like green wood on a fire, and brushed off his knees. "Come in, then. If you're asking about debts, you'll want to sit down for it. Debts are best discussed sitting."

* * *

Inside was one room, one hearth, one table worn smooth as river stone, and wool everywhere—in baskets, in skeins, hanging from rafters like pale rain caught mid-fall. There were two chairs. Only two. He gave them both to us and sat himself on the bed, which told me something about how often he had visitors, and how long it had been since he'd expected any.

His name was Edric. He offered it freely, which after the village felt like watching someone juggle knives barefoot—technically fine, deeply unsettling.

"And you're looking for the door," he said, once we'd given our names—I gave mine as Wren, just Wren; Theo gave theirs with a hesitation I noticed and filed away—and warmed our hands at his fire. "Everyone who comes asking about the mountain's business is looking for the door."

"How do you know about the door?" I asked.

"Because I walked through it." He said it the way you'd say *because I broke my leg once*. Fact. Old fact. Scar tissue over it. "Forty-one years back this spring."

Theo leaned forward. "What did you trade?"

Edric looked into the fire for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd made himself memorize so he wouldn't have to remember it naturally.

"My wife was dying. Fever, in the bad winter. The healer was snowed in at Thornfell and wouldn't come, or couldn't. And a visitor came to my fire anyway. Masked. Polite. Offered me a bargain: her life through the winter, until the thaw brought medicine. In exchange—" he spread his hands, "—forty years."

"Forty years of what?" I asked, though I already knew. God help me, I knew. I'd paid at the toll house. I knew what the currency was.

"Memory. Forty years' worth, taken from wherever they liked. They took—" he counted on his fingers, unhurried, "—my mother's face. My wedding day. Every year of my childhood except the smell of bread, which they left, for reasons known only to themselves. The names of everyone I went to school with. How to swim. I used to swim, I'm told. The color of my daughter's eyes."

The word landed in the room like a dropped stone. Theo heard it too. I watched their whole body go still.

"Your daughter," Theo said carefully.

"Died the year after. Childbed fever. Which is the joke of it, isn't it?" He wasn't smiling, but his mouth had the shape of a smile that had died there years ago. "I bought one winter for the woman I loved and paid with the girl I made. I can't remember her face. Her name is written on a board under the loft bed and every morning I read it like scripture, and every night it's gone again, because they take it fresh each time, do you see? It's not forty years they took. It's forty years, forever. A well that refills."

Nobody said anything. Outside, a sheep complained about something. The fire ticked.

"So why," I said finally, because someone had to, "are you still living next door to it?"

"Where else would I go?" He seemed genuinely puzzled. "My wife is buried on the south slope. My sheep are here. And—" he hesitated, and this was the first time he hesitated at all, "—the door comes back. Every few years. Opens somewhere near, and I feel it, like a tooth that wants wiggling. I think part of what I paid was the knowing of what else I paid. And the part that knows wants it back."

"That's not how bargains work," Theo said quietly. "You can't reclaim payment by proximity."

"No," Edric agreed. "But wanting isn't a bargain, is it. Wanting's free." He looked at us both, and his eyes were old and clear and terribly tired. "You want the door. I know where it opens. I'll take you."

"What do you want for it?" Theo asked. Professional habit, I thought. Everything has a price; check the price first.

"Nothing you'd call a price." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I want you to promise me something. When you go through—and you'll go through, folk like you always go through—you find out what I paid. All of it. Not the list they give you, the real weight of it. What was in those forty years. And you come back, or send word, or shout it down the mountain if you must, and you tell me. Every piece."

"Why?" I asked. "If it's gone, what good does knowing do?"

He considered that with the patience of a man who spent all day alone with his thoughts and had gotten very good at examining them.

"When my wife was dying, she said to me: *I don't mind going, love. I mind being forgotten.* And I promised her she wouldn't be. Then I went and sold the remembering of her, bit by bit, to save her, which is either the most faithful thing or the most faithless thing a man ever did, and I've had forty years without enough of myself to decide which." His voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse than shaking. "Knowing what I paid won't give it back. But a debt you can't name is just a hole. Name it, and it's a wound, and wounds can close. Holes just stay holes. Do you understand?"

I understood more than he knew. I had holes. Whole counties of holes, three days deep and spreading. Somewhere in the Undervault, presumably, sat my missing yesterdays in a ledger, priced and shelved, and some traitorous part of me wanted exactly what Edric wanted—not restoration, not yet, just the *naming*. Just to know the shape of what was taken.

"Yeah," I said. "I understand."

"We promise," Theo said, and there was something in their voice I hadn't heard before—a rawness, like skin rubbed thin. "On whatever I have left to swear on. We'll tell you what you paid."

Edric studied them for a moment. Whatever he saw, it satisfied him, or at least it settled him. He rose, stiff and creaking, and pulled a cloak from a peg, and a crook from beside the door, and said, "Well then. Door's open till sundown, if it opens at all today. Best not keep it waiting. Doors that walk hate waiting."

* * *

He led us up behind the hut, along a path only he seemed able to see, threading between rocks that looked identical to every other rock, and talked as he walked. Not about the door. About sheep. About the weather, and the state of the wall he'd been mending, and whether we'd eaten, and did we want bread for the road—he pressed a cloth bundle on Theo before Theo finished saying no. It was the manner of a man filling silence with small things because the big thing was coming and he wanted no part of the quiet before it.

I fell into step beside him. Up close he smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke, and his hands, swinging loose at his sides, were cracked and scarred and steady.

"Shepherd all your life?" I asked.

"All my life," he said, and then stopped, and laughed—one dry note, surprised out of him. "Ha. Listen to that. *All my life.* Forty-one years ago I might have meant something different by that sentence than I do now, and I'll never know what the difference was." He resumed walking. "Aye. Shepherd. Whatever else I was, the sheep don't care, and neither does the weather, and between the two of them I get by."

"Wren," Theo said from behind us, low. A warning tone. *Don't poke the wound.*

But Edric waved a hand without turning. "Let her ask. Questions are free, I said. It's answers that cost." He glanced sideways at me, and his eyes crinkled. "You're the one who came before. I said that when you arrived. You didn't ask what I meant."

"I figured you were confused," I said. "Happens, at your age."

"At my age everything happens," he agreed placidly. "But I wasn't confused. There was a knight here once. Years back—ten, twelve? Came up the same path with the same look on her face, like she was walking toward something she'd already decided to lose to. Sword with a notch in it. Dragon-scale, the smith in Thornfell said, though he was a liar about most things." He nodded at my hip, where the notched blade rode. "She asked me about the door too. Went in. Never came out that I saw." A pause. "Or came out different. Or came out as you, and doesn't know it. The mountain does that, I think. Recycles."

My hand had found the sword hilt without my permission. The notch was cool under my thumb, familiar as my own teeth. "That's not possible."

"Neither is a door that walks," Edric said, "and yet."

Ahead of us, the cliff face changed.

I don't know how else to say it. One moment it was rock like any other rock, gray and indifferent. The next, the light hit it differently—or it decided to be hit differently—and there was a seam in it, tall as two men, thin as a knife blade, breathing open by degrees with a sound like a held sigh let slowly out. The air around it smelled of cold iron and old paper.

Theo made a sound beside me. Small. I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent two weeks learning their sounds.

"What?" I murmured.

"Nothing." Too fast. "It's just—I've seen this door before. In the map. In—" they stopped, jaw tight, and I let it go, because I'd learned that particular silence meant *not yet*, and I was trying, gods help me, to be the kind of person who respected *not yet*.

Edric stopped ten paces from the seam. Wouldn't come closer. I watched him plant his crook and grip it with both hands, and I understood: the door pulled at him. Forty years of payment sitting on the other side, and the man standing at the edge of it like a starving man outside a bakery window, holding himself back by main strength.

"Go on, then," he said. His voice was rougher now. "It's open for you. Won't be for me—it knows its customers." He swallowed. "Sundown. Remember. Tell me what I paid."

Theo stepped forward and did something I didn't expect. They took the old man's hand—both their hands around his one, cracked knuckles and all—and held it.

"I swear it," Theo said. "By the flame I copy and the ink I owe and every name I've ever signed. We'll tell you what you paid, Edric of Highmoor, all of it, every piece. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* those."

Something went out of the old man's shoulders. Not relief exactly. More like the setting-down of a weight he'd carried so long he'd built his posture around it.

"That's a fine oath," he said gruffly. "Wasteful. Should've sworn on something smaller." But he squeezed Theo's hand back, brief and hard, and then pushed us gently toward the seam, the way you'd shoo sheep through a gate. "In with you. And listen—if you meet anything in there wearing my mother's face, you tell it Edric's still sorry."

Then the seam was before us, and the dark beyond it was breathing, and I went through because going through was the only direction left that made any sense.

Behind me, faint and fading, I heard Edric of Highmoor begin to whistle—an old tune, wordless, the kind of tune a man whistles when the words are gone but the music stayed.

And ahead, in corridors of black glass, something turned toward us in the dark and began, softly, to read our names aloud.
