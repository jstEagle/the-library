## 17. Descent

The cliff face that wasn't there yesterday was there this morning, and I want it noted for the record that I did not scream. I made a sound of scholarly interest. It may have been pitched slightly above the range of scholarly interest.

"There," said the shepherd, whose name we still didn't know because he'd sold it along with forty years of himself, and who had therefore asked us to call him Tam on account of a hat he apparently owned once. He pointed with the crook of his staff at a seam in the rock that had no business existing. "That's where she went in. My wife. After I paid."

"Your wife went into the Undervault?" Wren asked. Her hand was already on her sword hilt, which was her way of saying *I am listening carefully and also prepared to hit something.*

"No." Tam's face did something complicated—the face of a man trying to remember grief he'd traded away and finding only the shape of its absence. "She died. The bargain kept her alive one winter, like I asked. And when she went—" he tapped his chest, twice, over the heart, "—something went down there instead. Into that door. To be held against what I owe." He looked at me. His eyes were pale as weathered bone, eyes that had been emptied and refilled with someone else's sky. "You promised you'd tell me what I paid. You haven't yet."

"I will," I said. "After."

"After what?"

"After I know whether knowing it helps or just breaks you sooner."

He considered this with the unhurried patience of a man who had, by his own accounting, nothing left to lose but the morning. Then he shrugged, which I was learning was how mountain people said *fair enough*, and turned back to the cliff.

The seam was thin as a knife cut in butter, running diagonally across stone the color of old iron. Yesterday, Wren and I had camped below this exact shelf of rock—I remembered the ledge, remembered complaining about it—and there had been nothing there but lichen and my own bad attitude about altitude. Today the seam breathed. That is not a word I use lightly; I spent nine years copying scripture and four years drawing smuggler maps, and I have a professional respect for the difference between things that breathe and things that don't. The seam breathed. In, out. Slow. Like something enormous sleeping behind very thin glass.

"It follows debts," I said, mostly to myself. "Doors that walk. The entrance only opens where someone's made a bargain, and it goes to whoever owes the most." I touched the map through my coat—Maren Voss's people had wanted it badly enough to kill for it, and I still carried it like a coal wrapped in wool. "Tam owed forty years. So the door came to him."

"And you?" Wren asked, watching me sidelong. She'd gotten good at watching me sidelong. It was one of perhaps three expressions she had available since the toll house, but she'd refined them. This one meant: *I know you're leaving something out.*

"I don't know what I owe," I said, which was true, and also the most dishonest sentence I'd spoken all week.

* * *

We went up to the seam single file, Tam first because he knew the ledges, then Wren because she didn't trust anyone behind her back, then me because I trusted no one anywhere and figured the rear position gave me the best odds of running.

At arm's length, the seam resolved into detail. There were characters cut into its edges—thin, angular script, deeper than any chisel work, and they moved. Not flickered. Moved, the way water moves under ice: the same shapes, always rearranging themselves. I'd spent half my life reading dead languages by candlelight and I recognized maybe a third of them, and every third I recognized was a term from contract law. *Consideration. Tender. Perpetuity.* Words that belonged in ledgers, carved into the skin of a mountain.

"It's writing itself," I whispered. "The door. It's—it's drafting itself. Constantly."

"What does it say right now?" Wren asked.

I leaned close enough to feel warmth coming off the stone. "Right now it says, um. 'The undersigned acknowledges—'" I stopped. My mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"The undersigned acknowledges what?"

"That the debt remains outstanding," I finished quietly. "And that interest accrues."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere below us a hawk screamed, indifferent, alive, part of a world that operated on the sane principle that doors stay where you put them.

"You've seen this before," she said. Not a question. Wren had lost three days of memory and possibly more, and she still read me better than Brother Cassian ever had, and Cassian had taught me letters before I could properly hold a pen.

"I've seen the symbol," I said. "On the map. In—in fragments I can't explain." I tapped my temple. "There are holes in me too, knight. Mine just came pre-installed."

Something passed over her face—not sympathy exactly. Recognition. Two broken clocks comparing pendulums.

Then Tam put his hand flat against the seam and pushed, and the mountain opened like an eye.

* * *

There was no dramatic rush of air, no groan of ancient hinges. The rock simply agreed to stop being rock, folding inward along lines I couldn't follow, and we were looking into a corridor.

Black glass. That was my first coherent thought, and my second, and honestly my thought for a good minute afterward, because the corridor walls were black glass and they were polished to a depth that made no sense—looking into them was like looking down a well while lying on your back. My reflection stared up at me from the vertical surfaces, multiplied and dimmed, and my reflections' faces were all slightly wrong. Older. Younger. One of them, far down in the depths, was wearing a child's sash, and when I blinked it wasn't there anymore.

"Lovely," Wren said. "Inviting. Really sells the hospitality."

"Stay close," Tam said. "The paths remember where you've been. Don't give them more than they need."

I wanted to ask what that meant. The whispers started, and I forgot the question entirely.

They came from everywhere—from the walls, from underfoot, from the air itself, which in the Undervault had texture, a faint grain like the tooth of paper. Voices. Dozens. Hundreds. Dead voices, was the only way I could describe it, though I couldn't have told you how I knew they were dead; they simply had the particular flatness of things recited rather than spoken, the cadence of scripture copied so many times the copier stopped hearing meaning. And they were reciting contracts.

"*...the undersigned grants unto the court one summer of her seventh year, tendered freely, in exchange for...*"

"*...one name, to be held in perpetuity, redeemable upon...*"

"*...three days of his mother's laughter, witnessed and sealed...*"

"*...one soul, whole and willing—*"

I stopped walking.

"—*tendered by—*"

"*Theo.*" Wren's hand closed around my wrist. Hard. Grounding. "Keep moving."

The whisper slid past and dissolved into the next voice, the next bargain, the endless ledger of the place. But the shape of it hung in my ears. *One soul, whole and willing.* I knew that phrasing. I'd known it since the night the courier died on the temple stairs with blood on his teeth and my name in his warning. I'd known it since the map, since the symbol that matched the burned edges of memories I didn't have.

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake, distantly, the way you watch someone else's hands, and I thought: *you are a scribe. Scribes observe. Scribes record. Record this.*

*Seventeenth day of descent,* I wrote in my head, in the neat bookhand Brother Cassian had beaten into me. *Entered the mountain. The mountain entered me. Will reconcile accounts later, if later exists.*

* * *

The corridor descended in long switchbacks, and the black glass gave way—no, that's wrong. It never gave way. It changed. The walls thickened, darkened, became something like obsidian shot through with veins of faint gold, and the gold moved too, slow as honey, and I realized after a hundred paces that the veins were text. Everything here was text. The floor under our boots was a page. The ceiling overhead was a page. We were walking through the middle of a document so vast it had become architecture, and the dead voices weren't haunting the corridors—they were the corridors. Contracts, read aloud forever by their own terms, holding the mountain up the way beams hold a roof.

"This is what they're built from," I heard myself say. My scribe's voice, the lecture voice, the one that surfaced when terror needed somewhere to go. "Bargains. The whole structure. Every wall is an agreement somebody made and couldn't take back."

"Cheerful building material," Wren muttered.

Ahead of us, Tam walked steadily, staff clicking on glass, and I noticed that the whispers changed around him. They slowed. They softened. Some of them, as we passed, simply stopped mid-clause and stayed silent until we'd gone by, and I understood with a lurch that they were making room for him. Forty years of a man had been poured into these walls. Somewhere in the gold and black around us were his wedding, his harvests, his wife's laugh, the names of his children—all of it load-bearing now.

I touched the wall as we walked. Just briefly. Fingertips on warm glass.

For half a heartbeat I felt something answer. A pressure against my fingers from the other side, gentle, questioning, like a sleeper turning toward a familiar hand. And beneath my palm, faint as breath on a window, a whisper that was not reciting anything:

*"...and the winter passed, and she lived, and it was worth it, it was worth it, it was worth..."*

I pulled my hand back and wiped it on my coat and did not look at Tam, because if I looked at Tam I would have to decide whether to tell him, and I had promised myself I wouldn't lie to him again before the telling that mattered.

Wren saw anyway. Wren always saw. She didn't say anything either. That was becoming its own language between us—the fluent silence of two people carrying more than they could set down.

* * *

The corridor narrowed. That was when the awe curdled.

It happened gradually, the way cold does—you don't notice the temperature dropping, you notice your fingers stopping. The walls drew closer. The ceiling lowered until Wren had to duck, until I could touch both sides at once, and the whispers got denser to compensate, contracts overlapping contracts, voices braided into a drone that pressed on the skull like deep water. My reflections in the glass crowded in with me. All of them. Every version. They walked when I walked, but a half-step late, all of them, and their mouths kept moving after mine stopped.

"Talk," Wren said suddenly. Her voice came out flattened by the space, swallowed almost before it left her.

"What?"

"Talk. Out loud. About anything." Her jaw was tight. "The whispers get louder when you go quiet. I've noticed it since the toll house. Silence is what they listen for." She met my eyes, and in the gold-veined dark hers were the color of nothing at all. "Inquisitors do the same thing. Leave you alone in a cell with your own head until you start confessing to fill it. This place is just—" she gestured at everything, "—a very large cell."

So I talked. I told her about the scriptorium, about the smell of hot wax and ink, about Brother Cassian's terrible singing at dawn offices and the way the novices bet on which psalm he'd butcher worst. I told her about drawing smuggler maps by moonlight, the illicit thrill of it, cartography as sin. I described the ledger in the bursar's office—the family debt, eleven years remaining when I last counted, ten and some months now—and I heard my own voice go strange on the numbers, because numbers had always been the honest part of my life and honesty felt different down here, where everything spoken seemed to be entered into evidence somewhere.

The walls drank it in. I could feel them listening. Every word I said about ledgers and debts made the gold veins pulse a little brighter, a little nearer, and I understood that I was feeding the corridor, that attention itself was currency here, that the mountain had been paid in advance and was simply waiting, patiently, for the balance to come due.

"Here." Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Rough palm, sword-callused, certain. "Better than talking. Hold on to something real."

Her grip anchored me the way a bookmark anchors a page. The whispers receded—not gone, never gone, but held at the edge of hearing, muttering their eternal inventory of everything anyone had ever given away. I squeezed her hand once, which in our developing language meant *thank you*, and she squeezed back, which meant *don't mention it*, and we kept descending.

* * *

The corridor ended at a threshold without a door.

Beyond it the space opened—vast, vaulted, lit by no source I could name, a gloom that glowed rather than darkened, the light of deep water or deep thought. Pillars rose out of sight. Between them, far off, I could see movement: figures, or the suggestion of figures, tall and slow, going about whatever business a place like this conducted. And rising from the open dark ahead, threaded through everything, the whispers gathered into something almost like music. Almost like a choir. Thousands of contracts, recited in unison, in round, in canon, the greatest legal instrument ever assembled and the loneliest song ever sung.

Tam stopped at the threshold. His staff trembled against the glass.

"She's in there," he said. "Whatever's left of what I paid. I can feel it. Like standing outside a house where someone's burning your letters."

"We'll come back out," Wren said. "Both of us. With answers."

He nodded once and stepped aside to let us pass, and I caught his sleeve.

"When we come back," I said, "I'll tell you everything you paid. Word for word if I have to. I'm a scribe. Recording things is the one thing I've never failed at."

He smiled—a small, worn thing, but real. "Then mind your grammar in there, boy. I hear they're particular."

We crossed the threshold together.

Behind us, I felt the corridor begin, very softly, to write down our names.
