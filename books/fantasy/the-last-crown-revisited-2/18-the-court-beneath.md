## 18. The Court Beneath

### Theo

The corridors of black glass had been whispering contracts at us for what felt like a day and a half, though time down here was a rumor at best. My feet ached in a way that suggested days. Wren's expression suggested she'd have paid good coin to stop hearing the whispers, and she'd already proven she'd pay worse things than coin.

Then the corridor simply ended, and we stepped out into light.

I want to describe it properly, because I've spent my life describing things badly on purpose—copying scripture in a hand that wasn't mine, drawing maps that lied about where they led. But the outer court of the Undervault resists description the way water resists being held.

It was vast. That much is safe. A cavern so large the ceiling was a guess rather than an observation, hung with lanterns that burned without flame—pale gold lights that drifted like slow thoughts. The floor was polished obsidian, and it reflected everything upward, so we seemed to walk beneath a second court, an inverted one, full of inverted figures.

And there were figures. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe, gathered in clusters around what I can only call stalls—not market stalls exactly, though there was commerce happening, but something between a merchant's booth and an altar.

"Tell me you're seeing this," Wren murmured beside me.

"I'm seeing it."

"The masks."

"I'm seeing the masks."

They were all masked. Every figure in the court wore one: shapes of smoke and bone fused together, some smooth as river stones, others jagged as broken crockery. The masks had no eye holes that I could see, and yet they saw. You could feel it—the attention of them, sliding over your skin like cold water.

A figure near us was conducting business with another figure whose mask was a single unbroken oval of white bone. The first held out its hands, palms up, and something glimmered there—a memory, I realized. An actual memory, visible, like a soap bubble with a scene inside it. I caught a glimpse: a kitchen, sunlight, someone laughing off-frame.

"That's a person's *afternoon*," I whispered. "Someone's whole afternoon, being sold."

"You sound scandalized," Wren said. "You sold maps to smugglers for six years."

"Maps are paper. Paper doesn't remember being had." I watched the bubble change hands. The buyer pressed it to their mask and it sank through, absorbed, gone. "That man will wake up tomorrow not knowing why he loves the smell of bread."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Maybe he didn't love it anyway."

"He did. That's the point. That's always the point. They take the things you love because those are the only things worth anything."

We kept walking. It was the only thing to do; standing still felt like volunteering.

The court organized itself around a raised dais at the far end—or I assumed it was the far end; the space had a way of suggesting distance without confirming it. On the dais stood a throne of black glass, and on the throne sat a figure larger than the rest, its mask a crown of antler-bone veined with smoke. It did not move. It did not need to. Everything in the court moved around it the way weather moves around a mountain.

Between us and the dais lay the marketplace proper, and here is what I learned about the Undervault's economy in the space of perhaps two hundred steps:

There was a stall where time was traded. Not metaphorically—a queue of figures waited to deposit years, which were decanted into glass vessels like pale wine. Another bought names. I watched a woman—I say woman; the body was human, the posture was human, the despair was human—hand over her name and receive a slip of parchment in return. She read it, nodded, and walked away lighter. When she passed us, I tried to catch her eye and found nothing behind it looking back. Not emptiness, exactly. More like a room after the furniture's been sold: the shape of a life still marked on the walls.

"They trade in everything people are made of," I said.

"Mm." Wren's hand rested on her sword hilt, which was either comfort or habit or a very optimistic plan. "Which means somewhere in this room, there's a price list with 'knight' on it."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. Joking would be funnier."

A bell rang somewhere—or rather, a sound occurred that my mind insisted on filing under *bell*, low and resonant, like a struck tooth. The nearest cluster of masks turned toward us.

Not all of them. Just the nearest dozen. And they turned *together*, smoothly, the way a flock turns, and I understood with a lurch that they hadn't heard us. They'd *sensed* us. Or—

Or sensed me.

Because the turning stopped when their blank faces found me, and then they did something worse than attack, worse than barter, worse than anything I'd braced for.

They inclined their heads. All twelve of them. A small, courteous, deferential bow, like shopkeepers greeting a customer who once spent lavishly.

"Oh no," I said.

"What?" Wren's sword cleared three inches of scabbard. "What's 'oh no'?"

"They know me."

"That's impossible. You've never been down here."

"I know that's impossible." My voice came out thin. "But try telling them that."

More masks turned. Word moved through the court the way wind moves through wheat—a ripple of smoke-faced heads bending toward me from every direction, each bow a little deeper than the last. Somewhere a voice like dry paper said, softly, *the young master returns*. Somewhere else, another voice, wetter, older: *so long, so long, the account stands open*.

Account. Open. Standing.

My legs felt like borrowed equipment. "Wren. I need you to keep walking and I need you to not ask questions until we're past the dais."

"I'm asking one question now."

"Fine."

"When you were nine years old—" she began, and I don't know how she knew, how she could possibly have known, except that she'd spent two weeks watching me flinch at symbols on a map, and disgraced knights are apparently trained to read people the way scribes read ink— "*what did you buy?*"

"I didn't buy anything." The words came out before I could decide whether they were true. "I think—I think I *sold* something. Or agreed to sell it. I don't remember. That's the whole horror of it, Wren, I don't *remember*, and they clearly do."

The crowd was parting for us now. That was the worst part, actually. No one stopped us. No toll, no challenge, no demand. The masks bowed us through like honored guests, and every courtesy felt like an itemized line on a bill I couldn't read.

At the base of the dais steps, a single figure detached itself from the throng. Its mask was different from the others—finer, a filigree of white bone over drifting smoke, almost beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful if you don't think about what it's for.

It spoke. The voice was neither male nor female, neither old nor young, and it used my name.

"Welcome back, Theo Dunmore."

Behind the mask, I felt, distinctly, the sensation of being smiled at.

"It has been," the figure said, "such a very long time since your last transaction. We have kept everything exactly as you left it."

### Wren

Here's what nobody tells you about disgrace: it teaches you to stand in rooms where everyone knows the worst thing about you. I'd stood in the king's hall while they read out my crimes. I'd stood in the rain outside the walls while children threw things. So I know what it looks like when a room decides what you are.

This room had decided what Theo was. And Theo didn't know yet.

I did, mostly. Two weeks of watching someone flinch at a symbol on old vellum, go gray at the word *toll*, walk faster whenever the mountain leaned close—you learn things. I'd guessed before the shrine murals confirmed it, and the murals had confirmed it, and Theo had gone quiet in the way of people carrying something too heavy to set down.

But knowing a person carries a weight isn't the same as watching a hundred masked strangers bow to them like a returning lord.

I put myself half a step in front of them. Old habit. The kind of habit that got me knighted and then un-knighted, so maybe it wasn't a good habit, but it was mine.

"We're just passing through," I told the fine-masked figure. Loudly. Courts understand loudness even when they pretend not to.

"Of course you are." The voice was pleasant. Pleasant voices are the ones to watch. "All travelers pass through. Very few pass *out*, but the court is generous with hope. Hope costs nothing here—it's the only thing that does."

Theo made a small sound beside me. I glanced over. They'd gone the color of tallow candle.

"Theo." I kept my voice low. "Breathe. Count the exits. You're the map person—count the exits."

Something flickered behind their eyes. The scribe-mind engaging, the sharp thing underneath the weariness that I'd first seen across a drawn blade on a dark road. I watched them look—not at the walls but at the *flows*, the ways figures moved, where the crowds thinned, which arches had traffic and which had shadow.

"Three," they breathed. "East gallery, the tunnel behind the time-stall, and—there's a stair under the dais. Everyone's pretending not to see it."

"Everyone pretending not to see it sounds like a trap."

"Everything here sounds like a trap. At least that one's an honest shape."

The fine-masked figure waited through all of this with the patience of furniture. When Theo finished, it inclined its head again—that same courteous, awful bow.

"You wish to descend. Naturally. The vault calls what belongs to it." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Though I wonder, young master, whether you recall *why* the vault considers anything yours. Shall I refresh the account? The court keeps excellent records. We could read your history aloud. It is short. Children write short histories."

"Don't," Theo said.

"One soul," the figure recited, sing-song, like a nursery rhyme, like a *tally*, "whole and willing, tendered by hand of the signer, against the mother's breath, interest accruing—"

"*Don't.*"

The word cracked out of Theo like a whip, and the whole court went still. Every mask turned. The lanterns dimmed, or seemed to. And in that silence I heard something I will hear until I die: the sound of a person hearing their own crime spoken back to them in a stranger's voice, and understanding, finally and completely, that it was true.

I'd refused an order once. Kneeling in the dragon's presence—I don't remember it, but the Archivist showed me the fragment, me with my sword drawn and my hands shaking—and whatever I'd refused, I'd refused it *knowing*. Knowing the cost, knowing the shame, choosing anyway. That's the difference between guilt and tragedy, and I'd built what was left of myself on that difference.

Theo hadn't chosen. Not really. A nine-year-old with a dying mother doesn't choose; a nine-year-old signs what's put in front of them because the alternative is watching someone drown and doing nothing.

But the court didn't deal in nuance. The court dealt in *accounts*.

"Walk," I said quietly. "Past the dais. Down the stair. Whatever it says, whatever it reads out—we move."

Theo moved. I'll give them that. White-knuckled, breathing wrong, but moving, and I fell in beside them, and the crowd of masks parted again, bowing, bowing, dozens of them now, and the whispers followed us like a tide:

*The signer returns.*

*The account stands open.*

*So long, so long, so long.*

At the top of the hidden stair, the fine-masked figure appeared ahead of us—simply *appeared*, no walking involved, which I filed under problems for later—and made one final bow, deeper than all the others.

"The vault has waited a long time for you, Theo Dunmore," it said. "Do give the warden our regards. And do hurry." The smoke behind the bone filigree curled, and I swear on what's left of my honor that it shaped itself, briefly, into a smile. "Your mother's breath was a lovely purchase. We should so hate for it to have been wasted."

Theo made it three steps down the stair before their legs gave out, and I caught them, and we sat together on black glass stairs in the dark under a court of smiling monsters while they shook.

"I was nine," they whispered. Not to me. To the dark, to themselves, to some child I'd never met. "I was nine, and I just wanted her to breathe."

"I know," I said.

And the terrible thing—the thing I couldn't say out loud—was that I was starting to suspect I knew more than that. That somewhere in the holes the mountain had chewed through my head, there was a kneeling girl with a drawn sword who had once stood in this same court and been offered a bargain of her own.

The stair descended into darkness, and the darkness, faintly, was warm.

It smelled, impossibly, of smoke and gold.

Of fire that remembered us both.
