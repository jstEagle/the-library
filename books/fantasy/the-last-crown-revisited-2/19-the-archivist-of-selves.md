## 19. The Archivist of Selves

The masks came for me at the third bell of the court's long, lightless afternoon.

I say afternoon because it felt like one—because my body still kept its own hours, stubborn as a mule in a hailstorm—but there was no sun down there. There was no sky. There was only the black glass of the Undervault's corridors, and the whispers that ran along them like water along a gutter, reciting contracts in voices that had been dead so long they'd forgotten they were dead.

I was sitting with my back against a pillar of smoked crystal, sharpening a knife I didn't need to sharpen, when I felt the change in the air. Down there you learned to feel it: a pressure behind your ears, like the moment before a storm breaks over high country. The whispers hushed. Even the dead voices wanted to listen.

She stood at the mouth of the corridor. Not a mask of smoke and bone like the others—I'd seen those drifting through the outer court, faceless as eels, trading in other people's yesterdays. This one wore a face. It was a good face, if you liked them carved: smooth cheeks, calm eyes the color of tarnished silver, a smile that had been measured by someone who understood smiles the way a carpenter understands joints. Her robes were grey as ledger paper, and they moved wrong—not flowing, but *settling*, like ash after a fire.

"Wren of the Broken Oath," she said.

"That's not my name."

"It is the name this court holds for you." She inclined her head, gracious as a hostess offering wine. "Names are our stock in trade. We keep them carefully. Yours has been on our shelves for some years now."

My knife was in my hand before I'd decided anything about it. Old habit. The body remembers what the mind loses—that's the joke of my life, isn't it? My hands knew drill formations I couldn't name. My feet knew the measure of a courtyard I'd never seen. And somewhere in my skull there were holes, three days wide and more, chewed out of me like a moth got into the wool.

"Then take it back," I said. "You've clearly got room."

The archivist smiled her measured smile. "Oh, we don't hold your name, knight. We hold what was paid *against* it. May I sit?"

She didn't wait for an answer. She settled onto the opposite bench the way dust settles—no decision involved—and folded her hands, and I noticed then that her fingers were stained to the second knuckle with ink the color of dried blood. Archivists' ink. Contract ink.

"You have been inside before," she said. "You know this. You feel the gaps."

"I know I'm missing three days," I said. "Maybe more. Maybe the missing parts are the only interesting ones."

"They are," she said simply. "That is why they were taken."

* * *

Here's what I'll tell you about temptation, since apparently I'm qualified now: it never looks like a devil. It looks like a door left open in winter when you're cold. It doesn't argue. It doesn't threaten. It just stands there being possible, and lets you do the rest yourself.

"You came down this mountain chasing something you cannot name," the archivist said. "A compulsion. A pull. You resented it every step—I could show you each moment of that resentment, if you like. They are catalogued. Everything is catalogued here." She touched the bench between us, and the black glass beneath her fingers lit faintly, like a coal remembering fire. "But consider, Wren of the Broken Oath: you did not come down here for Theo Dunmore. You did not come for the smuggler's map, or the Order's hunters, or the crown's men at your heels. You came because something in you was *owed*, and debts pull harder than fear."

Theo's name in her mouth made my jaw tighten. Two weeks. That's all it had been. Two weeks of bad roads and worse cooking and a scribe who lied badly and fought dirtier than any soldier I'd ever trained beside, and who looked at me sometimes like I was a puzzle they were afraid to finish solving. Two weeks wasn't loyalty. Two weeks was circumstance.

So why did I hate hearing their name in a stranger's mouth?

"What do you want," I said.

"To make you an offer. The court makes few; you should feel honored." She drew from her sleeve a slim case of dark wood, and opened it, and inside lay a single sheet of vellum so fine it seemed to glow against the black. "Your memories were taken as toll. Toll is payment, and payment creates record, and record can be—consulted. Restored. For a price."

"What price?"

"A small one. A future service. Unspecified as yet." Her silver eyes didn't blink. Often enough. "The court deals in obligations, knight. An unspecified obligation from a woman of your... capabilities... is worth more than gold."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I've heard enough. Nobody offers something for nothing, and nobody offers an unspecified favor to a stranger unless the favor is going to cost more than the thing bought." I sheathed the knife, slow, so she'd watch me do it. "Ask Maren Voss how her bargains have gone. Ask the shepherd up at Highmoor what forty years of his own head cost him. You people don't sell memories back. You sell hooks."

For the first time, something moved behind the archivist's face. Not a flinch. An adjustment—the smile recalibrating, a degree warmer, a degree sadder. Which was worse.

"You are right to be cautious," she said. "Caution is the residue of betrayal, and you have been betrayed, though you no longer remember by whom or how. Very well. No bargain. But the court permits—one viewing. A courtesy extended to all debtors. Consider it a sample from a merchant who respects your intelligence." She lifted the vellum from its case and held it flat between her ink-stained hands. "One fragment. Chosen at random from your account. Look, and judge for yourself whether what was taken from you was worth keeping."

And here is the thing I am most ashamed of, writing it plain: I looked.

I told myself it was reconnaissance. Intelligence gathering. Know your enemy's inventory. I told myself a lot of things in the half-second before I bent toward that glowing page, and none of them were true. I looked because I was hungry. Three days missing—or more, the archivist had let that *more* sit in the air like smoke—and a person can starve on their own past just as surely as on bread.

The fragment rose off the vellum like heat-shimmer off summer stone.

* * *

Fire.

Not wildfire—something older, vaster, a fire with a *mind* in it, gold at the heart and white at the edges, filling a cavern so large the ceiling was a rumor. And in front of the fire, on a plinth of black basalt, a lantern. Dimming. Guttering. A soul in a cage of glass and gold, burning low the way a candle burns at the end of a long night.

And me.

I was kneeling. Kneeling on stone worn smooth by centuries of other knees, and my sword was drawn—not raised, not lowered, but held across my body in the guard position, the one they teach you means *I will not move from here*. Dragon-scale notched the blade near the crossguard. I could see it even in the shimmer: a pale chip in the steel, like a bite taken out of the world.

Someone was shouting an order. I couldn't hear the words—the memory was soundless except for the fire's breathing, slow and enormous, like the tide—but I could see the shouting. A figure in white and gold, an officer's sash, arm thrown out in the gesture every soldier knows: *forward*. Do it. Strike. Finish it.

And kneeling-Wren—me, my own face, younger, unbroken-nosed, eyes hard as nailheads—shook her head.

No.

The officer stepped forward. I raised the sword higher. Not at him. At nothing. At everything. The flat refusal of a woman who had decided, once, in firelight, that some orders end where her spine begins.

And then the fragment turned, the way dreams turn, and showed me what knelt beyond me in the firelight: other knights, heads bowed, and among them a child—a small figure in a scribe's grey, clutching a scroll too big for their arms, watching me refuse with an expression I couldn't read because the memory chose that moment to fold shut like a book slammed closed.

The shimmer collapsed. The vellum went blank. The corridor's whispers resumed their dead recitation, and I was standing—when had I stood?—with my hand braced against the pillar and my heart going like a siege drum.

"Well?" the archivist said softly.

* * *

I made myself breathe. In through the nose, four counts. Out through the teeth. Drill-yard breathing, the kind that keeps your hands steady while your world rearranges itself.

"That was real," I said.

"It is catalogued as authentic," the archivist agreed. "Fragment seven hundred and twelve of your account. There are nine hundred and sixty-three others."

Nine hundred and sixty-three. I did the arithmetic the way you do it when you're trying not to feel anything: fragments of a life, shelved in a mountain, indexed by creatures who thought of a human soul as a filing problem. Somewhere in those shelves was the reason I'd refused an order in dragon-fire. Somewhere was the crime that broke my oath. Somewhere was whatever the court had eaten since—three days, or thirty, I genuinely no longer knew which—and somewhere, maybe, was the answer to why a notched sword pulled me down a mountain like a fish on a line.

All I had to do was sign.

"The price again," I said. "Unspecified future service."

"The court prefers flexibility."

"The court prefers *ownership*." I pushed off the pillar and faced her square. She hadn't risen. She didn't need to; the whole corridor leaned her way, the whispers bending toward us like grass toward wind. "Here's what I know about unspecified debts. I've spent two weeks watching a scribe drown in one. Their family signed a paper when they were a child, and the paper grew teeth, and now every year of their life belongs to somebody else's ledger. You're offering me the same deal with better lighting."

"And yet," the archivist said, "you want them back."

It wasn't a question. That was the obscenity of it. She stated it the way you'd state the weather, and she was right, and she knew she was right, and the knowing sat between us on the bench like a third party negotiating on her behalf.

I wanted them back. God help me, I wanted them the way a drowning woman wants air. Nine hundred and sixty-three fragments, and one of them had shown me a self I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting—kneeling in fire, refusing, *whole*. Whoever that woman was, she had a spine I'd been borrowing ever since without knowing where I'd gotten it. I wanted the rest of her. I wanted to know the officer's name. I wanted to know why the child with the scroll had watched me refuse, and what the child had done next, and whether the child and I had—

Theo. The child in the mural at the shrine had worn a sash like Theo's. Theo had gone white as bone-paste looking at it. And now a memory showed me a child with a scroll in dragon-firelight, and I would have bet my other kidney it was the same child.

Our threads were tangled before we ever met. That's what the mountain was telling me. That's what the archivist was selling, wrapped in vellum.

"One viewing," I said. "No signature. No obligation. And if you try to put so much as a comma of contract language in front of me again, I'll introduce your face to the business end of a principle I apparently died on once already."

The archivist studied me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, papery sound, like pages turning.

"Oh, you are *exactly* as advertised," she said. She slid the blank vellum back into its case. "No signature, then. But understand what you're refusing, knight. Memories are not property. They are *load-bearing*. Remove enough of them and the whole structure comes down—and someone else moves into the space. The court has seen it happen. The village in the valley below us—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the surface, toward the smiling people who repeated themselves, "—is not an accident. It is an *advertisement*."

"Is that a threat?"

"It is a forecast." She rose at last, and her robes settled around her like ash finding the floor. "Keep your fragments where they lie, Wren of the Broken Oath. But know this: the vault lies deeper, and the warden of the vault asks every visitor the same question, and the question is *what will you pay?* When it is asked of you—and it will be asked—you may find that 'nothing' is a harder word to say down there than it is up here."

She turned to go. I should have let her. Instead:

"The child," I said. "In the firelight. With the scroll. Who were they?"

The archivist paused. Her silver eyes found mine over her shoulder, and for one unguarded instant there was something in them that wasn't merchant-calm—something almost like pity, which from a creature like her was more frightening than any threat.

"That fragment is not yours," she said. "It is Theo Dunmore's. You saw it because accounts touch when debts entwine." She smoothed her sleeve. "Which suggests, knight, that your refusal in the firelight and their signature on that scroll are two halves of a single transaction. The court does not believe in coincidence. Neither, I think, do you."

She walked into the whispering dark, and the dark took her the way water takes ink.

* * *

I found Theo an hour later—they'd holed up in a side gallery with the map spread across their knees, scratching notes by the light of a stub of candle they'd stolen off a dead man's altar, because of course they had—and I stood in the entrance watching them work, and I didn't say any of it. Not the archivist. Not the offer. Not the fragment.

Because here's what I'd realized, standing alone in that corridor with my heart hammering: the memory hadn't ended where it ended. It had ended where the court had *cut* it. Right at the moment the child with the scroll came into view. Someone had edited my past, and they'd cut it precisely there, and either that was chance or it was design, and I no longer believed this mountain believed in chance.

If I told Theo—if I described the child, the scroll, the firelight—I might be handing them a wound they weren't ready for. Or I might be handing the court exactly the reaction it wanted, because everything down here was a lever, and I was starting to suspect that Theo and I were both handles.

So I said nothing. I sat down across from them, and asked what they'd found, and listened to them talk about doors that walk and debts that pull, and all the while the fragment burned behind my eyes: me, kneeling, refusing, and a child watching from the firelight with a scroll in their arms like a sentence they hadn't read yet.

Nine hundred and sixty-three fragments. One shown. And the one thing I knew for certain, the thing I'd have carved on my own headstone if I ever got one, was this:

Whatever I'd refused to do in that cavern, I'd do it again. Whoever I'd been, she was worth becoming twice.

And whoever the child was—the one whose name the courier had died warning Theo about, the one whose past was stitched through my own like a thread through cloth—I was going to find them. Even if finding them meant signing after all.

Even if it meant paying the warden's question.

The whispers in the walls changed key just then. I felt it before I understood it—Theo felt it too, their pen stopping mid-word, their head coming up like a deer scenting wolf. Down the corridor, deep beneath us, something vast had begun to move. Footsteps, or the idea of footsteps. And threaded through the stone, faint but unmistakable, came a smell neither of us had smelled in weeks and both of us knew instantly:

Smoke. Real smoke. Torch-smoke.

Someone else was in the mountain. Someone with soldiers.
