## 20. Reading the Contract

The public rolls of the Undervault were kept in a hall that looked, absurdly, like a tax office.

That was the thing nobody warned you about the court beneath the mountain. You expected grandeur—obsidian thrones, rivers of fire, the kind of architecture that makes you feel briefly religious. Instead, after Wren and I were escorted through three antechambers by a masked functionary whose face was made of folded smoke, we arrived at a long room lined with shelves, lit by lanterns that burned without flame, and staffed by clerks who moved with the unhurried resentment of every clerk I have ever known.

I had spent nine years in a scriptorium. I recognized my people immediately.

"They'll have an index," I whispered to Wren. "There's always an index."

"You say that like it's comforting."

"It's the most comforting sentence in any language. An index means rules. Rules mean the place can be understood, and things that can be understood can be survived." I paused. "Also it means I won't have to grovel for directions, which is good, because I don't think these ones blink."

The nearest clerk did not blink. It was roughly human-shaped, robed in grey, its mask a smooth oval of bone with no features except two slots where eyes might go, and behind those slots there was only a soft dark, like looking down a well. It held out a slate without being asked.

*State your business.*

I had rehearsed this. We had rehearsed this, on the road, in the pass, in the shrine with the murals I still couldn't think about without my hands going cold. The story was simple: we were debtors come to consult our own records. That was apparently legal here—the court prized transparency the way a spider prizes silk, everything visible so long as you never touched it—and the shepherd's information had gotten us through the outer gates. Debtor or creditor, you could look. You just couldn't take.

"Debt consultation," I said. "Personal records. Family contracts."

The clerk's well-eyes regarded me a moment longer than procedure required. Then the bone face tilted, very slightly, in a way I chose to interpret as bureaucratic indifference rather than recognition.

*Third gallery. Chronological arrangement. Do not remove documents from the hall. Do not copy documents within the hall. Reading aloud is permitted. Weeping is not prohibited but is discouraged.*

"Weeping is *discouraged*," Wren repeated as we walked away. "What does that even mean?"

"It means they've had enough criers that someone made a policy." I kept my voice light because if I didn't, it would shake. "Every institution has one sin it gets tired of. In the temple it was falling asleep during evening bells. Here, apparently, it's grief."

"Cheerful place."

"The most cheerful place I've ever been," I said, and meant it about as much as I'd ever meant anything.

* * *

The third gallery smelled of cold stone and old ink, which is a smell I love, which should have been my first warning. Anywhere that smells like home is somewhere you've already lost something.

The rolls went back centuries. That was the horror of it—not malice, not cruelty, just *volume*. Shelf after shelf after shelf of contracts, each one a bargain somebody had made in desperation, each one written in the same careful hand. I know that hand. I've spent half my life learning to write like it: the Order teaches a script designed to be beautiful, uniform, and impossible to alter without detection. Whoever ran the Undervault's archive had reached the same conclusions, possibly centuries earlier, possibly in the same school. I tried not to think about what that implied about the Order's founding. I failed.

Wren stationed herself at the end of the aisle, arms crossed, watching the entrances. She'd barely spoken since the murals. Neither had I, really. There are conversations that happen anyway, in the language of how someone hands you bread, or stands between you and a door. Ours said: *I saw what you saw. I'm not leaving.*

I found the index—it was, thank every merciful power, alphabetical—and ran my finger down under D.

Dunmore.

My finger stopped before my mind agreed to it. For a moment I just stood there, looking at the word in the clerk's immaculate hand, feeling the specific vertigo of seeing your own name in a place it should never be. Names are small things. Ink and breath. But put your name in the wrong ledger and it becomes a hook, and something on the other end of the line starts pulling.

*Dunmore. T. — see: Bargain of Breath, year 1147 After Cinder. Vault classification: SOUL-COLLATERAL. Gallery Three, Case 881.*

Soul-collateral.

Case 881.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I want to report some moment of wisdom, some instinct that whispered *turn back, Theo, there are doors that open only once.* But the truth is I pulled the case off the shelf the way I'd pulled ledgers off Brother Cassian's shelves a thousand times—automatically, reverently, with the little thrill of access that every scribe feels when the archive opens to them. Curiosity is a muscle. Mine has always been overdeveloped. It never once occurred to me not to look.

The case was a folder of black glass, thin as a hymnal, warm to the touch. Warmer than it should have been. Like a bird.

I opened it.

* * *

The contract was one page. I had expected—something more. A scroll, seals, ribbons, the theatrical apparatus of a big bargain. Instead: a single sheet of pale vellum, dense with that same careful script, formatted exactly like the indenture papers I signed when the Order took me at twelve. Standard terms. Standard clauses. The Undervault, whatever else it was, had excellent templates.

I read the header. *Contract of Tender and Exchange, executed beneath Thornfell, in the Court of Quiet Accounts, this ninth day of Frostmoon, 1147 After Cinder.*

Frostmoon. My mother died in Frostmoon. I knew that the way you know a scar is there—vaguely, constantly, without looking.

Then the body of the document, and I will set it down here as exactly as memory allows, because I have read it a hundred times since and it never changes, and I think part of me believes that if I write it out plainly enough, once, it will turn into something else:

*One soul, whole and willing, tendered by T. Dunmore, age nine, resident of Thornfell valley, in exchange for the continued breath of Miren Dunmore, mother of the undersigned, such breath to persist no less than five years from date of execution.*

Age nine.

*Tendered by T. Dunmore, age nine.*

I read it again. The words didn't rearrange themselves. Words never do; that's the cruelty of them. They just sit there being true while you stand in the aisle of a dead archive under a mountain, holding your whole life up to the light and finding the watermark.

*A soul, whole and willing.*

Not my soul. That was the first coherent thought I managed, and it came with a strange, hysterical relief—I hadn't sold myself, I wasn't hollow, I was still *me*, all of me, minus whatever pieces childhood grinds off everyone. Small mercies. The contract didn't specify whose soul, not in the clause I was reading. Just *one soul, whole and willing.* Which meant—

Which meant a nine-year-old had stood in front of something wearing a mask and promised it a soul, any soul, a soul to be named later, the way you promise a debt you don't yet understand, and the court had accepted. Had *drafted around it*. Had filed it. Case 881. Soul-collateral.

And then, further down, past clauses about enforcement and default that I skimmed with a scribe's eye and a dying man's attention, the signature block.

I knew what it would be before I turned the page. Some part of me had known since the courier died on the stairs saying *don't let them read your name aloud,* since the map bore a symbol I'd been drawing in my sleep since I was small, since the mural in the shrine showed a child in a sash I could still feel tied around my waist. Knowledge arrives in stages. It had been arriving for weeks, patient as water. This was just the last inch of flood.

The signature read: *Theo Dunmore.*

It was my handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. *Mine.* The same too-tall ascenders. The same way I cross my t's low, impatient, like I'm already halfway to the next word. The same little hook on the final e that Brother Cassian spent two years trying to train out of me and finally gave up on, telling me, with that dry smile he thought I didn't notice him using, that some faults are load-bearing.

Nine-year-olds' handwriting changes. Everyone knows that. Your hand at nine is a stranger's hand; you grow into your letters the way you grow into your bones. Except mine hadn't changed. Twenty years, two lives, an indenture and a flight into the mountains, and the signature on case 881 matched my hand stroke for stroke, as though I'd signed it yesterday.

As though I'd signed it *last night.*

The folder was warm in my hands. I realized distantly that my legs had stopped agreeing to hold me, and that I was sitting on the floor of the third gallery with my back against the shelf, which was the sort of thing discouraged clerks probably noted in their own ledgers.

"Theo?" Wren's voice, from far away. Boots on stone. "Theo, what is it? What did you find?"

I couldn't answer. My mouth wouldn't assemble words. So I held the folder up instead, and she crossed the aisle and took it, and I watched her face do what mine had done: the quick scan, the stop, the re-read, the blood leaving.

"'Tendered by,'" she read aloud, quietly, the way you'd read scripture over a grave. "Theo, this says—"

"I can read it," I said. My voice came out level. That surprised me. Somewhere underneath the shaking there was apparently a scribe after all, and scribes recite. "Thank you."

She crouched next to me. She didn't touch the contract again. She touched my shoulder instead, one broad warm hand, and left it there, and waited. Of everything that happened under the mountain, I think I remember that hand best.

"I was nine," I said finally. Because somebody had to say it, and it wasn't going to be her. "My mother was dying. Fever, the winter sickness—I remember that much, I remember the smell of the sickroom, wax and rosemary and—" My voice caught on rosemary, of all things, and I pushed through it. "And someone came. I don't— I remember a visitor. I remember masks. I remember being told there was a bargain available, if I was brave, if I loved her enough. And I said yes. I signed. And she lived five more years, almost to the day, and then she died anyway, and everyone told me it was the sickness coming back, and I believed them, because why would anyone believe—"

"Why would a child believe they'd done that," Wren said.

"Why would a child believe they'd *done that.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and cracked. "But that's not even the worst of it, Wren. Look at the terms. One soul, whole and willing. It doesn't say *my* soul. I couldn't have given mine—I was nine, what would I even—no. Someone offered me a bargain and let me believe I understood it, and I promised a soul I didn't have to give. Which means either the contract was void from the start—which it isn't, look where we're standing—or—"

"Or someone gave you the soul to offer," Wren said. "Someone handed a dying woman's child a loaded promise and let them pull the trigger."

"A dragon's soul," I said. "The last one. The one that's been keeping the beacon lit and the wards standing and the valley alive for twenty years, and it's sitting in a vault under this mountain because *I signed for it.*" The words tasted like ash and copper. "Every bad thing that's followed—the tolls, the memories eaten, that village that smiled at us, the knight who can't remember her own—"

"Don't," Wren said, sharp.

"—it all runs downstream from case 881. From my name. From my *hand.*" I looked at the signature until my eyes blurred. "I've spent my whole life paying down other people's debts. Copying scripture by the yard, mapping smuggler routes in the margins of psalms, counting years off a ledger that never got shorter. And the entire time, the biggest debt in the kingdom had my signature on it, and I didn't even remember signing."

"Theo—"

"And my mother." That was the one I'd been circling, the one that hurt below the waterline where you can't patch it. "She lived. Five years. Every birthday, every stupid argument about my handwriting, every night she sat up with me when I had bad dreams about masks—that was bought. With a dragon. With *that.*" I pressed my palm flat against the vellum, as if I could hold the page still, as if it were the page that was shaking. "Was it worth it? Is anything worth—how do you do that arithmetic? How do you weigh five years of my mother against twenty years of everyone else's—"

"You don't," Wren said. "You were nine."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." Her hand tightened on my shoulder. "Listen to me. I don't remember most of my life anymore. Whatever that vault ate, it's gone, and I've had to decide who I am with the holes showing. So I'm going to tell you what I've learned, and it's the only thing I know for certain: you are not the worst thing you did when you were small and scared and someone bigger handed you the knife. If you were, none of us would survive ourselves."

I wanted to believe her. God, I wanted to. But belief is expensive in the Undervault, and I was suddenly, catastrophically poor.

Because there was one more thing. One line near the bottom, in smaller script, the kind of clause nobody reads until it's too late—the clause I, professional reader of fine print, had read anyway, because I can't not:

*In the event of default, tender, or dissolution, the undersigned acknowledges full recall of all circumstances pertaining to execution of this contract.*

Recall. Full recall. The contract had taken the memory of itself when I signed—had folded those days up and locked them inside its own terms, the way the vault took Wren's memories, payment held in escrow. And now that I had read it, now that my name and my hand had met their reflection across twenty years—

The folder was getting warmer. Noticeably warmer. The black glass cover had begun to glow faintly at the edges, gold seeping in like dawn under a door, and from somewhere inside the vellum—from inside the *words*—came a sound I had heard once before, in a fever dream when I was small, and had spent my life failing to forget.

A heartbeat. Slow. Vast. Patient as stone.

"What's happening?" Wren was on her feet, hand on her sword. "Theo. What's *happening?*"

"Full recall," I said. The gold light was climbing the aisle walls now, and the whispers in the corridors beyond had gone silent, every dead voice in the mountain holding its breath. "The contract remembers itself. And apparently—"

The gallery lights went out.

"—apparently so do I."
