## 22. The Guttering Flame

I have served the crown for thirty-one years, and in all that time I have learned exactly one universal truth: institutions never panic early enough.

They panic late, and loudly, and at great expense, and they always—*always*—blame the messenger who told them the truth on schedule.

I was that messenger. I had been that messenger for six weeks now, standing in the same briefing chamber with the same men, pointing at the same chart, watching the same line descend. The line was drawn in red ink because I had learned long ago that ministers do not read black ink. Black is for ledgers. Red is for emergencies. And this—

"This is a clerical error," said Lord Chancellor Vessik, for the sixth time. "Inquisitor Ashgrave. It must be."

"Chancellor." I did not sigh. Sighing is for men who have given up, and I have never once given up on anything except my knees, which surrendered to the capital's damp some years ago without negotiation. "The beacon flame has diminished by forty percent since midsummer. The wards along the northern marches flicker at dusk. Three granaries in the river provinces report blight that our own agronomists cannot explain, and the agronomists are very good at explaining things—it is how they avoid honest work."

"The dragon-fire has burned for three hundred years."

"Yes."

"It does not simply *dim*."

"It does not simply dim," I agreed. "It dims according to a schedule. Someone set the terms of that schedule a very long time ago, and someone else failed to read them. I have been reading them for six months, Chancellor. Would you like to know what they say?"

Vessik looked at me the way powerful men look at inconvenient truths: as though sufficient staring could convert them into flattery. Behind him, the younger secretaries pretended to write. One of them was actually writing. I would want his name later; diligent men are useful.

"I am asking you," Vessik said, "to consider the political consequences of announcing that the kingdom's founding miracle is failing."

"I am not asking anyone to announce anything. I am asking permission to go underground and fix it."

"And you believe you can *fix* it."

I let the silence stretch just long enough to be felt. This is a technique I recommend to any young man entering crown service: never fill a pause when the other man is frightened. Frightened men fill their own silences, and they confess while doing it.

"I believe the fire is collateral," I said. "Collateral can be seized, renewed, or repossessed. All three require paperwork. I am, whatever my other failings, extremely good at paperwork."

* * *

The beacon stood atop the Spire of Founding, in the oldest quarter of the capital, and I climbed its two hundred and eleven steps myself because reports lie and stairs do not.

The flame room at the top was circular, windowless, and warm—or it had been warm, once. Now the warmth came only from the brazier-sized heart of gold fire suspended in an iron cradle at the room's center, and even that heat had the quality of a memory of heat rather than the thing itself. The keepers of the flame—a hereditary order of nine elderly men and women whose entire sacred duty consisted of not touching anything—stood along the walls like furniture.

The eldest keeper, a woman named Odile with hands like crumpled parchment, watched me approach with the resignation of someone who had been waiting decades for this visit.

"Inquisitor."

"Mistress Keeper." I inclined my head. "Show me."

She didn't pretend not to understand. That was another thing I liked about her; the religious orders in this kingdom wasted so much time on theater, but the flame-keepers had no budget for it. She lifted the iron cradle's outer ring, and the fire guttered.

That is the word. *Guttered.* A candle gutters when it is dying, and what sat in the cradle was dying, though it had perhaps a season left in it rather than an evening. The gold light pulsed in a rhythm I recognized from my research—the rhythm of breath. Slow, labored, patient breath. Something down there in the dark under Thornfell was breathing shallowly because something up here was burning low, and the correspondence between the two was exact, measurable, and documented in triplicate by the founders of this kingdom, who at least had the decency to keep good records.

"When did you last feed it?" I asked.

Odile's mouth thinned. "Feed it."

"The accounts reference offerings. Annual. Contractual."

"The accounts are old."

"The accounts are *binding*, Mistress Keeper. That is rather their defining feature." I stepped closer to the cradle. The flame leaned away from me—not fear, precisely. More like the way an old dog leans away from a stranger's hand: not expecting cruelty, but no longer expecting kindness either. "When did the offerings stop?"

She was quiet for a moment. "My grandmother's time. The court stopped sending the tithe. The crown said the debt was symbolic. Paid in ceremony." Her eyes came up to mine, sharp despite her years. "You've read the contract, haven't you. You know there's nothing symbolic about it."

"I know that everything is real to the party holding the paper." I straightened. "How long?"

"A season. Perhaps less if the winter is hard. When it goes out—" she hesitated, and for the first time the old woman looked genuinely afraid, not of me but of her own knowledge. "It doesn't go out, Inquisitor. That's what nobody remembers. It doesn't go out. It goes *home*. And what comes back in its place is cold."

* * *

I requisitioned sixty soldiers, four mule-teams, and a cartographer with a nervous disposition, and rode for Thornfell through country that was already telling on itself.

The wards along the high road were supposed to burn blue at every milestone. They flickered like bad lamps. In the village of Marrow's Cross, half the fields lay black with blight, and the farmers had taken to leaving out bowls of milk and honey at their field-edges—an old practice, older than the crown, from the days when people remembered that the land's fertility was rented, not owned. The milk went untouched. Even the old bargains were lapsing.

"You feel it too, then," my sergeant said that night, at the fire. His name was Brannoc, a broad, unimaginative man whom I trusted precisely because he lacked the imagination required for treachery. "The cold. Coming down off the mountain."

"I feel paperwork coming due, Sergeant. The cold is merely its shadow."

He grunted. Brannoc's philosophy held that all problems were either solvable or fatal, and that speculation between those categories was a waste of good sleep. He rolled into his blanket. Around us, sixty soldiers slept badly, dreaming of cold, and above us the stars seemed slightly less fixed than they ought to be.

I stayed awake with my ledger.

Here is what the crown knew, officially: that a soul-contract existed, sealed beneath Thornfell, securing the last dragon's soul as surety against the kingdom's continued fortune. Here is what the crown knew unofficially, from archives so restricted that I was one of four living men with access: that the contract was renewable, that renewal required the original signer or their blood, and that the original signer's name had been struck from every record by persons unknown approximately twenty-five years ago.

Persons unknown. I had spent four months chasing persons unknown, and they had led me, by a chain of confiscated letters and a dead courier, to a temple scribe with ink-stained fingers and a talent for lying that was improving under pressure. Theo Dunmore. Indentured to the Order of the Quiet Flame. Debtor. Mapmaker. Child of the contract.

I did not yet know which of these identities was load-bearing. But I intended to find out before the mountain finished deciding for me.

* * *

Thornfell announced itself three days later as a bruise on the horizon, purple-black against a sky that couldn't decide whether to snow.

The Order of the Quiet Flame received me in their chapter house with the particular hostility of an institution that knows it is guilty of something but hasn't yet determined what. Their abbot, a soft-spoken man named Ellery with the eyes of a man counting exits, offered tea, scripture, and stonewalling in equal measure.

"Inquisitor Ashgrave. We are honored."

"You are not, Abbot, but courtesy costs you nothing and me less. I'm here regarding a missing courier and a scribe in your employ."

"Scribe Dunmore has taken leave."

"Has he." I unfolded the document I had prepared for exactly this moment—a writ of inquiry bearing six seals, any three of which would have sufficed. "Then his cell will be available for examination."

What I found in the cell taught me three things. First, that Theo Dunmore had fled in the night with a competence that suggested rehearsal. Second, that Brother Cassian—Dunmore's mentor, an aging archivist—had covered the flight with forged duty rosters, and had done it well enough that only someone looking for forgery would find it. Third, and most interesting: that Cassian himself had vanished two days after Dunmore, leaving behind his office, his rank, and a locked drawer I opened with the patience of thirty-one years' practice.

Inside the drawer were letters. Copies, in Cassian's careful hand, of correspondence between himself and someone in the Order's archive division—correspondence spanning nineteen years, concerning a child's contract, a sealed record, and a promise extracted from a dying woman that her name would never be read aloud in connection with it.

Cassian had known. For nineteen years, the mild-mannered archivist had known what his student was, and had built a wall of misfiled documents around the boy—no, around them; the letters were scrupulous on the point—and had guarded it with nothing but ink and patience.

I sat in that small stone office for a long while, holding another man's devotion in my hands.

People assume an inquisitor's work is cruelty. It is not. Cruelty is common and requires no training. An inquisitor's work is *reading*—reading people until their motives lie open as ledgers—and Cassian's motive was legible in every line. Love, expressed as concealment. The most expensive kind. And somewhere along the road ahead of him, the letters stopped mid-sentence, and I did not need the archive division to tell me what that meant.

I locked the drawer again. Some evidence serves justice better unread—but I took the copies, because sentiment is a luxury and the crown pays me to be unsentimental.

"Dunmore went east," I told Brannoc outside. "Toward the mountain. Find me the smuggler roads."

* * *

We found the toll house first, or what remained of the story of it.

A waystation carved into the mountain's lower shoulder, abandoned now, its keeper gone—but the walls still bore the marks of the trade. Scratched tallies. A ledger nailed to the post, its pages filled with entries in a hand that grew shakier as it descended: names crossed out, memories itemized like inventory. *One summer afternoon, aged seven. The taste of oranges. My father's voice singing.*

And near the bottom, in fresher ink, two entries side by side. One marked with a small drawn bird. One unsigned.

"They paid in memories," Brannoc said quietly. He was not an imaginative man, but he was not a stupid one either, and the ledger offended something fundamental in him. "Actual memories. Like coin."

"Like collateral," I corrected. "Everything is collateral, Sergeant. The mountain merely keeps better books than we do."

I photographed the page with a sketcher's crystal, sealed the originals as evidence, and stood a long moment looking up the mountain's flank, where the rock face seemed—there was no other word for it—*expectant*. Somewhere above us, a debtor was climbing toward a door that walked. Somewhere deeper, a soul breathed in an iron cradle of its own. And somewhere behind me, the capital's beacon guttered a little lower with every sunrise, keeping its terrible appointment.

Sixty soldiers. Four mule-teams. One nervous cartographer. And a mandate signed in six seals: recover the asset by any means necessary.

The mountain would try to charge us, of course. Mountains always do. But the mountain had never dealt with the crown's exchequer, and I intended to introduce them.

"Move them out," I said. "Double-time. We're expected."

Brannoc frowned. "Expected, sir? By who?"

"By everyone," I said, and started walking.
