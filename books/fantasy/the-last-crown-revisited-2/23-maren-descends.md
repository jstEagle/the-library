## 23. Maren Descends

There are two kinds of bad decisions in this world, and I've made both.

The first kind is the kind you make fast, with a knife at someone else's throat and your own conscience conveniently asleep. The second kind is the kind you make slowly, over a cup of tea, telling yourself all the while that it's fine, that it's business, that the scribe knew what they were doing when they drew maps for people like me.

I made the second kind. I sold Theo Dunmore's likely route to Inquisitor Ashgrave for amnesty — amnesty, mind you, which is just a word meaning *we'll hang you last* — and I sat there afterward drinking his terrible soldier's tea and feeling nothing at all, which should have been my first warning.

The feeling arrived three days later, at about two in the morning, wearing the face of every person I'd ever shorted.

It started small. I was going over the copies of Theo's maps we'd seized from their temple quarters — Ashgrave had let me look at them because I was useful, and usefulness is a leash you tie yourself — and I noticed something wrong. I'd stared at these routes for years. Sold them, traded them, once bled on one. And the entrance to the Undervault, the little door-mark Theo had drawn at the end of every route, wasn't in the same place twice.

I told myself it was sloppy drafting. Theo Dunmore was not sloppy. I told myself the ink had shifted. Ink doesn't shift. I told myself a lot of things that night, and by dawn I'd run out of lies, and what was left underneath was this: I had handed a living person to a man whose job description included the phrase *recover the soul-contract*, and the only thing between that man and whatever he wanted down there was a scribe and a knight who'd already lost pieces of herself to the mountain.

So here I am, three days later, standing in the treeline above Salt and Cinder Pass, watching thirty crown soldiers and one very tidy inquisitor march into a hole in a cliff face that — I checked the maps again, twice — was not there yesterday.

"Right," I said to nobody, because my crew was gone, scattered or dead or smart enough not to follow me. "Right."

And I followed them down.

* * *

Let me tell you something about the mountain that no map will ever show you, because maps are drawings of places and the Undervault doesn't believe in places. It believes in debts. The door opens where somebody owes something. That's why it moves. That's why Theo's maps disagreed with each other. Every route they drew ended at a different debt, and the debt walks.

The soldiers didn't know this. The soldiers had charts. Lovely charts, drawn up by some clerk in the capital who'd never been north of a wine cellar, showing corridors in confident straight lines with numbers beside them. Ashgrave had studied those charts the way he did everything — thoroughly, coldly, like a man reading the terms of a loan he intended to renegotiate.

I watched him do it at the pass camp, night before last. He stood over the fire with the chart pinned to a supply crate, and he said to his sergeant, "The corridor widens here. We'll advance in column." And the sergeant said "Yes, sir," because sergeants always say yes, sir, right up until the mountain eats the column.

Here's what I know that the clerk didn't: passages under Thornfell aren't dug. They're *grown*, or maybe remembered, and they don't stay where you leave them. A corridor that's wide at dusk is a throat by midnight. There are junctions that open only if you ask them nicely in the old trade-tongue — which sounds like nonsense, but nonsense has power under a mountain, ask anyone who's paid a toll. There are drops that look like floors. There are floors that look like drops, which is worse, because the mountain likes a joke as much as anyone who's never had to laugh.

My mother taught me none of this. My mother taught me arithmetic and how to hold a grudge. Everything else I learned from a smuggler named Odo Pell who died in a passage exactly like the ones I'm about to describe, and whose dying words were, "Maren, love, never trust a hallway that wants something." Odo, wherever you are: I'm trusting several hallways tonight. Pour one out for me.

* * *

Getting past the picket at the cliff-mouth was embarrassingly easy. Soldiers post sentries facing out. They're trained to watch for enemies coming from outside, because the idea that the danger might be behind them, inside their own tidy operation, offends their sense of order. I came in behind them, through the scree field, along the goat track Odo showed me when I was nineteen and stupid in a more energetic way than I am now.

Inside, the dark was total. Not cave-dark — cave-dark is honest, it's just absence. This was the other kind, the kind with texture, the kind that feels like it's considering you. I got out my shutter-lantern, cracked it to a thread of light, and looked at the walls.

Black glass. Polished deep as a well. And in the glass, faint as breath on a window, whispers. Not words, exactly. Recitations. I pressed my ear close and caught fragments — voices reciting terms, clauses, names. Some of the names went on a long time, like lists. Some of the names stopped mid-word, and I found I didn't want to know why.

"All right," I whispered back, because talking to haunted architecture is exactly the kind of thing Odo would have done and I've never claimed to be better than him. "You know why I'm here. Same reason as everyone. Something to fix."

The whisper nearest me paused. Then it moved on. I chose to take that as encouragement. Under a mountain, you take your encouragement where you can get it.

* * *

I caught up with the column an hour later, or rather I caught up with what the column had become.

They'd lost the chart. Of course they'd lost the chart — the corridor they'd advanced down had narrowed around the middle ranks like a fist closing, and when it opened again the men at front and the men at rear were in different passages entirely. I could hear Ashgrave's voice somewhere ahead, still level, still tidy, ordering lantern counts. Lanterns came back wrong. Counts came back wronger. Somewhere below us, faintly, a man was screaming in a way that suggested he'd found one of the floors that were actually drops, and then he stopped screaming, and the mountain swallowed the sound so completely it was like it had never happened.

I stayed high. There are ledges in these passages — old ones, from when the mountain was younger or kinder or simply shaped differently — and smugglers have used them for generations to move cargo above the crown's head. I moved along the ledge line like a spider along a rafter, and I looked down at Ashgrave's operation, and I planned.

Not heroically. Let's be clear about that. I'm not a hero. Heroes are people who haven't priced things properly. What I am is a woman who made a sale she couldn't live with, and there is exactly one way to unmake a sale, and it isn't remorse. Remorse is just interest accruing. You have to pay principal.

The plan, such as it was: slow them down. Corrupt the column's sense of direction, drop their supplies into a chasm, put darkness between Ashgrave and whoever he was chasing. Give Theo Dunmore and the knight time. Time was the whole currency down here — time was what the mountain ate, time was what the crown was spending soldiers to buy, and if I could steal a few hours of it for the two people I'd sold, then maybe the ledger between me and my own reflection would balance enough to look at in the morning.

First job: the supplies.

They'd cached their reserve crates at a junction — rope, hardtack, lamp-oil, and, gods help us, a chest of contract-paper, blank vellum stamped with the crown seal, because Ashgrave intended to write his claim down in triplicate even at the bottom of the world. I waited until the rearguard shuffled past below me, counting under his breath the way frightened men do, and then I went down the ledge like water going downhill.

Three crates. I took the oil first, cracked a cask, and poured it slow along the passage floor toward the junction's inner mouth, away from the soldiers, into the dark where the floor sloped down to nothing. Then I set a slow-match — a proper one, smuggler's work, an hour of quiet before any flame — and I took the rope. All of it. You can fight a man with a sword. You cannot fight a chasm with no rope.

As for the contract-paper, I stood there holding a stack of blank crown vellum and thought about what paper means under this mountain. Paper is promises. Promises are fuel. I fed the whole chest to the slow-match's path too, page by page, like feeding bread to a duck that might eat you later.

Then I climbed, and I was twenty feet up my ledge when the oil caught.

The flame ran down the slope the way I'd laid it, neat as a signature, and dropped into the inner dark, and for one moment the whole junction lit up orange, and I saw — I *saw*, in that flash — the far wall of the lower passage. It was covered in doors. Hundreds of them, small and plain, like the doors on houses in villages where everyone smiles too much. And every single one was shut.

I don't know what I expected the Undervault to look like. Gold, maybe. Teeth. But rows and rows of ordinary locked doors was somehow the worst thing I'd seen all year, and I once watched a man try to buy back his own childhood with a sack of teeth.

The mountain, I decided, was going to burn clean or not at all, and I intended to be holding the match either way.

* * *

The sabotage went well for about four hours, which under Thornfell constitutes a triumph.

I cut their guide-ropes at two junctions. I rolled a stone onto the passage that Ashgrave's scouts kept choosing — the mountain helped with that one, I swear it leaned — and I left false marks, smuggler chalk-signs, pointing the column down a throat I knew dead-ended in a whisper-gallery where the reciting voices would stand the hairs on a man's neck for a week.

And then, near what I guessed was dawn, if dawn meant anything down here, I heard them ahead of me. Not soldiers. Voices. Two of them. One low and careful, one rough-edged and stubborn as a mule in a doorway.

I knew both voices. I'd sold one of them to the man behind me.

Theo and Wren were close. Close enough that I could hear the scrape of Wren's notched sword against black glass, close enough to hear Theo say something I couldn't make out, and hear Wren answer with a short laugh that sounded like a woman deciding to trust something against her better judgment.

And behind them, maybe half a mile back through the walking corridors, came the sound of thirty boots — fewer now, call it twenty, the mountain had taken its tax — and one pair of tidy steps, and a level voice saying, "The trail bends here. They're close."

I stood on my ledge with my hand on my knife and did the arithmetic I'm actually good at.

If I went forward, I'd be warning the people I wronged that their enemy was coming — but I'd also be leading Ashgrave straight to them, because I'm the only creature in this mountain who knows these passages, and my footfalls are worth ten of his soldiers'.

If I went back, I could slow the column again. Buy minutes. Maybe an hour. But I'd be doing it blind, without knowing whether the hour mattered, and Ashgrave had started leaving men at junctions to guard his flanks, and I am good but I am not immortal, despite what my crew used to say after drinks.

Or there was the third thing. The thing I'd been not-thinking about since the treeline. The thing Odo would have done, and hated himself for, and done anyway.

Go down between them. Stand in the gap. Be the door.

Because here's the truth I finally let myself say, there on the ledge, in the dark, with the whispering contracts murmuring in the glass around me like a market full of quiet creditors: Maren Voss sold Theo Dunmore to save her own neck, and Maren Voss can't unring that bell, but Maren Voss can stand in the corridor between the inquisitor and the scribe, and she can make herself *expensive*. Every minute I cost him, I buy them. Every trap I spring on his flank is a coin paid back into an account I'd overdrawn past all decency.

Redemption isn't a feeling. I told you that. It's a payment plan, and I was done making minimum payments.

Below me and ahead, Theo's voice drifted up again — and this time I caught the words, and they stopped my blood. Theo said:

"—the vault's close. Can't you feel it? Like a held breath."

And Wren said, "Then let's go spend it."

And behind us all, distant but gaining, the level voice of Inquisitor Ashgrave said to his sergeant: "Lanterns forward. We take them at the threshold."

I moved. Down the ledge, into the throat between the two parties, knife out, heart going like a smuggler's hammer on a locked strongbox. Somewhere in the black glass around me, a whisper recited a clause I couldn't hear, in a voice that sounded almost amused.

Fine. Let the mountain watch. Let it write me down.

I was coming to settle my account in person.

* * *

The corridor opened ahead of me into light — real light, gold and dim and trembling, spilling up out of a chamber deep below like lamplight through the floor of the world. The soul-vault. It had to be. And standing at the mouth of the approach passage, blocking it with her body the way I was about to block it with mine, was a soldier — no. Not a soldier. Smaller, quicker, moving wrong for a soldier.

Maren Voss knows her own silhouette when she sees one.

It was one of mine. Denna. One of the crew who hadn't scattered — who'd taken Ashgrave's amnesty instead, same as I nearly did, and was standing guard at the vault approach with a crossbow and a face like a woman trying not to think.

She heard me before she saw me. She always did. She turned, and the crossbow came up, and recognition crossed her face like weather.

"Maren," she said. "He said you might come. He said to stop you."

"I know," I said. And I stepped out of the dark with my hands empty and my knife still sheathed, because some sales you unmake with steel and some you unmake by standing still, and I needed to know which kind this was. "Denna. Love. How much did he promise you?"

Behind her, below, the gold light pulsed like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm. Behind me, the boots were coming. Between them, me.

"How long do we have?" Denna asked — and the fact that she asked *how long* and not *what do I do* told me everything about which way her heart had already tipped.

"Not long enough," I said, drawing my knife at last. "But it'll do."
