## 24. The Soul Vault

**Theo**

The corridor of black glass ended the way all important things end: without ceremony.

One moment we were walking through whispers—dead voices reciting contracts in a language that made my teeth ache—and the next, the walls simply opened, like a book deciding it was finished with us, and there was light.

Not much of it. That was the thing. After days in the dark, I expected the vault to blaze. Instead it glowed, faint and gold, the way a hearth glows at three in the morning when the fire has been left untended and everyone in the house is pretending not to notice.

A lantern hung in the center of an empty chamber, suspended from nothing, turning slowly. Inside it burned something small and dim and unmistakably alive.

"That's it?" Wren said behind me. Her voice came out flat, which I'd learned was what she did instead of reverent. "That's the last dragon?"

"It's smaller than I imagined," I admitted.

"Everything's smaller than you imagine. You're a scribe. Your imagination works in margins."

I might have argued, but my eyes had found the floor, and the floor had found my attention and refused to give it back. The chamber was paved in the same black glass as the corridors, but here the glass was not smooth. It was carved—thousands upon thousands of lines, spiraling out from beneath the lantern in patterns too regular to be natural and too organic to be architecture. Contracts. The whole floor was contracts, written so fine they blurred together into something like feathers.

Like scales.

"Oh," I said quietly.

"What?"

"I don't think this is a room." I crouched, touched the glass. It was warm. Not lantern-warm. *Body*-warm. "Wren. I think we've been walking inside something for the last hour."

She went very still, the way she did before violence or revelation, and honestly the two looked similar on her. "Inside what?"

"The dragon."

The word dropped into the silence and kept falling. Because of course. The soul sold generations ago—but a soul doesn't leave a body neatly, does it? A body without a soul doesn't die. It just *waits*. And the court beneath the mountain, with its masked figures and its toll-keepers and its appetite for memory—it hadn't built itself a vault inside a dead god's bones by accident. It had moved in. Squatted. Turned the corpse of the last dragon into a courthouse and let the hallways fill up with other people's debts.

The whispers in the corridor took on a new meaning. Dead voices reciting contracts. The dragon's own bones, listening to its soul be held hostage one hallway away.

Wren sat down abruptly on the warm glass, sword across her knees. "Right," she said. "I'm going to need a moment, and I'm going to have it sitting down, because if the floor is a dragon then I decline to be standing on it when it notices me."

"I think it noticed us a while ago."

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what nobody tells you about sacred things: they're tired.

I'd grown up on temple stories—the Order's version, all trumpets and glory, the dragon that gave its fire for the valley, hooray, sing the hymn, put it on a banner. What the stories never mentioned was that giving everything doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like holding a door open for four hundred years while your arms shake and no one says thank you.

I knew this because I could feel it. Standing in that chamber, closer than I'd ever been to the lantern, I could feel the tiredness coming off it like heat off a forge. Old tiredness. The kind that stops being an emotion and becomes a landscape.

Theo stood near it, hands clasped behind their back like a scholar at a lecture, but I saw their shoulders. I'd spent two weeks learning to read those shoulders. They were braced for grief.

"You knew," I said. "Before we came down. Some part of you knew what we'd find."

"I knew there was a symbol on the map that matched something I half-remembered from childhood." Theo didn't turn around. "That's not the same as knowing."

"It's close enough that you should have told me."

"Yes," Theo said. "It probably is." A pause. "I'm not good at telling people things. I had a childhood that taught me secrets keep you safe."

"And how's that working out?"

They laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, which from Theo was practically a confession. "Ask me again when we're above ground. If we're above ground."

The lantern turned. Slowly. And I swear on whatever gods are still taking appointments that it turned *toward* Theo, the way a sunflower turns toward the sun, except there was nothing living about the motion. It was more like a compass needle. Like something finding north after centuries of spinning wrong.

Then the floor spoke.

Not the whole floor. One point of it, directly beneath the lantern, where the carved lines converged into a shape almost like a face if you squinted and had recently suffered a head wound—which, in fairness, described my last two weeks fairly well. The lines brightened, gold bleeding up through black glass, and a voice came out of the stone. It sounded like a large bell being rung underwater. It sounded like someone who had been talking to themselves for a very long time.

"*A signer comes.*"

Theo went white. "I'm not—I haven't signed anything down here."

"*Everyone signs,*" the voice said, with the patience of geology. "*But you. You signed early. You signed once, long ago, and the ink has been waiting for you ever since.*"

I put my hand on my sword. It was a stupid gesture—we were standing on the thing I was threatening—but some habits are load-bearing. "Who are you?" I demanded. "What are you?"

The light shifted. For a moment—just a moment—the pattern beneath us resolved, and I saw it whole: wings folded through the foundations of the mountain, a spine arched along passages we had walked, a skull somewhere far below us wearing the court like a crown it couldn't take off.

"*I am what remains,*" the voice said. "*The will that stayed behind when the soul was taken. A warden, they called me. A jailer. I prefer custodian—it sounds less like I agreed to any of this.*"

"You guard your own soul," Theo said softly. "You've been guarding your own soul."

"*Someone had to. The court certainly wasn't going to do it well. They hold it as collateral, yes, but collateral degrades when neglected. So I tend the flame. I keep the lantern lit. And I wait.*"

"For what?"

"*For a signer to come claim it. Or free it. Those are the only two doors left, little scribe, and I have watched every door for four hundred years.*" The light dimmed, guttered, steadied. "*You cannot imagine what that waiting is like. You cannot imagine what I would give to simply stop.*"

And there it was. The sacred thing, stripped of its banner and its hymn: an old creature in the dark, keeping a lamp lit over its own stolen heart, asking strangers—not even asking, just *stating*, because hope was clearly a muscle it had lost the use of—that it wanted to stop.

I thought about the toll-house. About the name I'd paid, the small one, the one I barely missed. I thought about the village that forgot itself, smiling their too-wide smiles. Everyone in this mountain was trading pieces of themselves to get free of something, and here was the oldest debtor of all, who had traded everything and gotten nothing.

"Theo," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Tell me you're thinking what I'm thinking."

"I'm thinking about six different things and none of them are legal," Theo said.

"Good. Pick one."

* * *

**Theo**

The warden—*the dragon*, my mind kept correcting, uselessly, the way it corrects nothing else—regarded us with the particular stillness of something that has learned not to expect anything.

I should explain what I felt, standing there. People assume awe looks like kneeling. Mine has always looked more like arithmetic. I stood in the belly of the last dragon and my mind did what it always does: it counted. Four hundred years of tending. Four hundred years of the court drawing interest on a soul that was never theirs. Every ward in the kingdom, every beacon, every harvest blessed by dragon-fire—all of it leased, all of it mortgaged against a contract signed by a child who hadn't understood the words.

Signed by me.

I hadn't told Wren yet. There was a version of this conversation where I did, right now, immediately, because she deserved it and because carrying it alone in this place felt like bringing a lit candle into a powder store. But there was another version, the coward's version, where I waited until after—until we knew whether any of this mattered.

The warden's light pulsed, slow as breathing. "*You are calculating,*" it observed.

"Habit."

"*Your kind always calculates. It is why your contracts are so dangerous. You write them faster than you understand them.*" A pause, and something moved through the light that I can only call amusement, dry as old paper. "*Though I will say—you calculate differently than the others who have come. The crown's men calculate profit. The court's people calculate risk. You are calculating cost. Who pays. That is rarer.*"

"Is that supposed to flatter me?"

"*It is supposed to inform you. You will need accurate information about yourself shortly. Decisions are coming.*"

The lantern turned again, that compass-needle motion, and I felt it settle on me like a hand. Warm. Enormously, unbearably gentle. Somewhere under my ribs, something answered it—an echo, a resonance, like recognizing like. I didn't understand it yet. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my recent life: I did not yet understand that the reason the lantern leaned toward me was the same reason a debt leans toward its debtor. The contract in the rolls upstairs had my name in it, and names travel.

But I felt *something*. And so, judging by her expression, did Wren.

"It's dying," she said. Not a question. She'd gone to stand near the lantern, close enough that its gold light caught the notches in her blade—the ones shaped like dragon-scale, though neither of us had said that part aloud yet either. This mountain was full of things we weren't saying aloud. We'd need a bigger mountain soon. "The flame. It's going out."

"*Fires do not die,*" the warden said. "*They are extinguished, or they are fed. This one is being neither. It is being withheld. There is a difference, and the difference is cruelty.*"

"How do we feed it?" I asked.

The light flickered. If a voice made of bell-sound and stone could hesitate, this one hesitated. "*That question is not mine to answer fully. But I will tell you what I know, since you asked plainly and plainness has become rare down here.*" The carved lines beneath us brightened, tracing new patterns—diagrams, I realized, contractual diagrams, the grammar of obligation rendered visible. "*The soul is held by contract. The contract is held by the court. The court is held by its creditors, and its greatest creditor, these four hundred years, has been the signature at the bottom of one particular page.*"

My mouth went dry. "Which page?"

"*You know which page.*"

"Wren," I said. My voice came out strange, thin, like someone else's. "There's something I need to tell you. And I need to tell it now, because—" I gestured helplessly at the warm floor, the patient light, the whole impossible architecture of a god's grief. "Because I'm done letting secrets keep me safe. Look how well that's worked."

Wren turned from the lantern. She looked at me the way she looked at locked doors: assessing whether to pick them or break them down.

"Start talking," she said.

So I started. I told her about the map, and the courier, and the symbol I recognized. I told her about the rolls upstairs, the public records of the Undervault, the contract I had found with my own name at the bottom in a child's careful hand. *One soul, whole and willing, tendered by T. Dunmore, age nine, in exchange for the mother's breath.* I told her I didn't remember signing it. I told her I remembered my mother coughing blood into a cloth, and a visitor in a mask, and being told—being *promised*—that everything would be all right if I just wrote my name where the nice man pointed.

I told her all of it in maybe two hundred words, which is the advantage of having spent years compressing scripture into margins.

When I finished, the chamber was silent except for the lantern's slow turning and, distantly, the whispers in the halls, still reciting, always reciting.

Wren didn't move for a long time. Then she crossed the warm glass floor—walking on a dragon, I noted distantly, she got over it faster than I would have—and stopped in front of me.

"Nine years old," she said.

"Nine."

"And they let you sign."

"They *made* sure I signed. There's a difference, and I think the difference is the whole point of this place."

She nodded slowly. Then she did the last thing I expected, which was becoming a habit with her: she reached out and gripped my shoulder, hard, knight-hard, the grip of someone who has carried wounded friends off battlefields and intends to continue the practice.

"Okay," she said. "So we steal your soul back."

"It's not—it's not exactly *mine*—"

"It's in your name. In this place, that's the same thing." Her jaw set. "We came down here for answers. We've got one. Now we get the rest. Starting with—" she turned to the light, chin up, addressing a four-hundred-year-old remnant of divinity the way she'd address a stubborn gate guard, "—you. Warden. Custodian. Whatever you want to be called. You said there are two doors. Claiming and freeing. Explain both, and don't skip the parts about what they cost."

The light regarded her. Something rippled through the floor-lines that might, in a smaller creature, have been a smile.

"*You ask like someone who has already decided to pay,*" the warden said.

"I ask like someone who's been billed before," Wren said. "I like to see the itemized list."

"*Very well.*" The gold light gathered, drew itself up, and I felt the whole chamber lean in—the mountain leaning in, the dead god's bones attending. "*Then hear the terms, both of you. And know that once spoken, they cannot be unspoken, and once heard, you will not be able to pretend you never heard them. That is the first law of this place, and the last mercy I can offer you: knowledge, freely given, before anything is asked.*"

The lantern flared.

And far above us—though we could not know it yet, though the mountain kept its own counsel—boots were already on the stairs. Soldiers, descending. An inquisitor with a mandate and a cold, patient smile, following the debts down.

The warden began to speak.

* * *

**Wren**

Later—much later, on the other side of everything—I would try to remember exactly what the warden said in those first moments, and I never quite could. Some things burn themselves out of memory just by being witnessed.

But I remember this: the light rising. Theo's face, gold-lit and terrified and more awake than I'd ever seen it. And underneath my fear, steady as a heartbeat, one thought repeating like a drumbeat:

*Whatever it costs, we're not leaving without it.*

The warden drew breath it didn't have, and asked its question.
