## 25. The Warden's Question

Here is a thing they don't tell you about standing in front of a god: it's mostly waiting.

The vault of the Undervault was a sphere of black glass no bigger than a chapel, and in its center hung the lantern—a cage of gold wire around a flame the color of late afternoon, dimming so slowly you'd miss it if you blinked at the wrong decade. The warden stood between us and it, and the warden had just finished unfolding itself out of the shape of a man into something older, vaster, and considerably less comfortable to look at.

I had my hand on my sword. Habit. Also because when a ten-foot silhouette made of smoke and old regret shifts its weight, a girl likes to know where her sword is.

"Theo," I said, without moving my lips much. "Any time you want to tell me what we're doing here."

"I'm working on it," Theo whispered back.

They'd been whispering that for two days now, ever since the shepherd's door had opened in a cliff face that hadn't existed the day before, and every corridor since had been black glass and dead voices reciting contracts in languages that made my teeth ache. Theo had grown quieter with each one. Not frightened-quiet. Recognizing-quiet. Like a woman walking through her own childhood home after thirty years and finding all the furniture exactly where she left it, and hating that she knows where the furniture goes.

The warden looked at me. It didn't have eyes anymore—it had places where light went and didn't come back out—but I felt looked at the way you feel weather coming in your knees.

"Knight," it said. Its voice was like a fire settling. "You were asked once before. Long ago. You did not answer well."

"That tracks," I said.

Theo made a small sound that might have been *Wren, please* or might have been a prayer. Same thing, some days.

"You stand in the last vault of the Undervault," the warden said. "Before you is the soul of the last dragon, held in trust, held as collateral, held—depending on who is asking—as property, as hostage, or as a promise nobody has kept. You have come a long way down. So I ask you what I am bound to ask: what would you pay?"

And there it was. The question.

I'd like to say I thought about my answer carefully. What actually happened is that my mouth started moving while my brain was still catching up, which is how I've made most of the important decisions of my life, including knighthood, disgrace, and following a compulsion into a mountain to chase a scribe I'd ambushed on a road two weeks ago.

"The memories," I said. "The ones this place ate. You've got them—I saw them, in the rolls, in the Archivist's little theater. Three days I can't remember, plus whatever else got skimmed off the top over the years. Give me those back, and—" I gestured vaguely at the lantern, at Theo, at the whole situation. "Whatever it costs. Take them as payment. They're mine, they're worth something, and frankly half of them are terrible anyway. That's my offer."

The silence that followed had texture. It was the kind of silence a room makes when it's deciding whether to laugh at you or pity you.

Theo's head turned toward me very slowly. "*Wren.*"

"What?"

"You can't—those are your memories. Your actual self. That's not currency."

"It's exactly currency. Everything down here runs on memory. We watched the toll-keeper eat a woman's name like a sweetmeat." I kept my eyes on the warden, because looking at Theo right now would have been a mistake, and I was already making enough of those. "It's the only coin I've got that's worth anything. So I'm spending it."

"No," the warden said.

I blinked. Of everything I'd braced for—haggling, horror, a price so steep it would make the toll-house look charitable—*no* wasn't on the list.

"No?" I said.

"No." The great dark shape settled, folding downward like a campfire going low. "You misunderstand what is happening here, knight. You believe you are bargaining. You offer a thing you own in exchange for a thing you want. That is trade. Trade happens in the market halls above. Trade happens in the Archivist's gallery, where selves are bought and sold and the price is always theft wearing a polite coat." A tendril of smoke gestured—not at me, but at the lantern, the guttering gold heart of it. "That is not what this is. This is payment. And payment must be given."

"Those seem like the same thing."

"They are opposites," the warden said, and there was something in its voice now that I didn't like at all, because it sounded tired. Gods aren't supposed to sound tired. Tired is for people. "A bargain says: I hold this thing, and I will release it to you if the scales balance. Both parties keep their hands closed until the exchange. But a gift—" the word seemed to cost it effort, like lifting a stone too heavy for the arm— "a gift opens the hand first. Payment, true payment, is given without knowing what comes back. Without holding anything in reserve. You offered me your memories as a purchase price, knight. You wanted them returned to you *and* counted toward the debt. You cannot spend a coin and keep it."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because the horrible thing was that it was exactly what I'd meant. I'd meant it as leverage. Here are my memories back, and also charge them against the account, net zero, everyone walks away whole. Clever Wren. Careful Wren. Two weeks in this mountain and I still hadn't learned the first lesson of the place: nothing here respects a closed fist.

"So what do you want?" I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "You asked what I'd pay. Fine. What does paying look like, if it isn't a trade?"

The warden regarded me for a long moment. Behind it, the lantern flickered, and for just an instant I thought I saw wings in the flame—the suggestion of them, vast and folded, the way you see shapes in smoke—and then it guttered back down to ordinary dying gold.

"Look at the knight's sword," the warden said—but it wasn't talking to me anymore. It was talking to Theo, and its voice had gone soft in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up. "Look at the notch in the blade. Dragon-scale. Do you know how a blade comes to be notched with dragon-scale, scribe? Only one way. It must strike the living animal and be struck in turn. There has been no living dragon in these mountains for three hundred years."

"There was a scale on it," Theo said quietly. "When we met. I noticed."

"You noticed," the warden agreed. "And did you wonder why a disgraced knight carries such a weapon? Why her body walked her to my door across forty miles of bad road, resentful every step, refusing at every crossroads, arriving anyway?" The dark shape leaned closer, and I resisted—genuinely, physically resisted—the urge to step back. "Because she has stood in this room before. Because she was given a choice once, at the edge of this flame, and she chose. And the court took the choosing away from her afterward, filed it in the rolls, sold pieces of it to pay debts she never signed. But a choice, once truly made, leaves a mark deeper than memory. Ask her what her hands remember."

My hands, I discovered, remembered plenty. They'd gone cold. The sword suddenly weighed what it had weighed the day I'd earned it, seventeen years old and certain the world ran on honor.

"I don't remember any of that," I said.

"No," the warden said. "But you are standing here. Memory is what happened to you. Choice is what you did about it. One can be eaten." It turned that eyeless attention back on me. "The other only surrendered."

"* * *

Theo sat down on the black glass floor at some point. I don't think either of us decided it; I think the floor just became the sensible option. They pulled their knees up and stared at the lantern like it was a page of scripture in a language they almost read, and the gold light moved on their face, and I thought—not for the first time—that I'd known this person fourteen days and they'd rearranged something in me that usually takes years and a bad winter.

The warden let us sit with it. That was its style, I was learning. It had the patience of something that had been waiting in a room underground for longer than my family line had existed. Waiting was apparently most of its job description.

"Tell me what happens," Theo said finally, "if someone pays properly. If someone gives. What does giving look like, from your side of it?"

"From my side," the warden said, "it looks like the only thing that has ever opened this lantern."

"And how many times has that happened?"

The pause stretched long enough that I started counting my heartbeats to fill it. I got to eleven.

"Once," the warden said. "Three hundred years ago. The dragon itself."

The lantern flame bent sideways, though there was no wind, and for a moment the whole vault smelled of high altitude and thunder.

"It gave its soul willingly," the warden continued, "to save a valley full of people who would forget it within four generations. It did not sell. It did not trade. It did not negotiate terms. It opened its hand, and the court caught what fell, and has been holding it ever since—because a court cannot give, only hold. That is the tragedy of my office, scribe. I guard the one treasure in existence that was created by generosity, using methods designed for greed. And so it dims. Generosity cannot be stored. It can only be spent again."

Theo's voice came out small. "And if someone spent it again. Gave something. Truly gave."

"Then the ledger changes. Debts written against a willing gift become gifts themselves, and gifts cancel nothing—" the warden's smoke curled, restless— "they *overflow*. But understand what you are asking. The court will test it. The court exists to convert giving into taking; it has had three hundred years of practice. Whatever is offered freely will be weighed, measured, and presented back as a price. The masks do not accept grace. They invoice it."

"Wonderful," I muttered. "So even the nice answer gets audited."

"Everything is audited here, knight. Including you." The warden shifted, and the lightless places where its eyes had been found me again. "Which brings us back to my question, unanswered. What would you pay?"

And I sat there in the gold-and-black dark, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the Archivist's gallery, since I'd seen myself kneeling in front of a dragon with my sword drawn and no idea whose order I was refusing.

Here's what I knew about those missing memories: they were mine. Whoever I had been—the knight who faced a dragon, the fool who came down here once already, whoever I'd been before and during and after—they were sitting in the court's filing system like coins in a strongbox, and every hour I spent wanting them back was an hour the court owned a piece of me. Get them restored, and I'd be whole. Complete. The full inventory of myself, returned.

And I understood, suddenly, with the clarity that only arrives at the bottom of a hole, that wholeness was exactly the trap.

If I bought myself back—if I traded anything, bartered anything, negotiated the return of Wren-the-whole—I'd be signing the same contract everyone else in this mountain had signed. I'd walk out of here complete and owned. The woman who remembered everything would be a woman the court had assembled, itemized, receipted. Whereas the woman standing here—the one with holes in her and a sword she didn't fully understand and fourteen days of Theo Dunmore, which was somehow more load-bearing than twenty years of anything else—

She was built, not issued. And maybe that was the whole point.

"Nothing," I said.

Theo looked up. The warden went very still, the way fire goes still.

"I don't know what I'd pay," I said, and it came out steadier than anything I'd said all day. "You asked, and I've been turning it over, and here's my honest answer: I don't have anything I'm willing to give *yet*, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because pretending is how this place eats people. I offered you my memories five minutes ago and you told me that was shopping, not paying, and you were right. So. Nothing. Right now. Ask me again later."

The silence rang.

Then the warden did something I hadn't seen it do. It laughed—one short, rough exhalation, like a struck flint.

"Honesty," it said. "'I have nothing yet.' Do you know how many souls have stood in this vault and claimed poverty, knight? Thousands. But they claimed it as strategy. You claim it as truth, and truth is the raw material of giving. You cannot give what you have not first admitted you hold." The dark shape inclined itself—an enormous, slow bow that made the lantern flame shiver. "Your answer is declined, and accepted. Both. That is more than anyone has managed in a century."

"Glad to be of service," I said, because my mouth does that when I'm terrified.

"Wren." Theo's voice. Quiet. I turned, and they were looking at me with the lantern-light behind them, and their expression was doing something complicated that I filed away to think about at a time when the world wasn't ending. "What you said. About being willing later. Later than what?"

"Than whatever's coming," I said. "Something's coming. I've been a soldier long enough to smell it. This room is too quiet and you're too close to the thing everybody upstairs wants, and somewhere above us there's an inquisitor who didn't get to be inquisitor by staying home."

As if the mountain had been listening—which, for all I know, it had—a sound came down through the black glass walls. Faint. Rhythmic. Boots. Many boots, marching in step, somewhere in the corridors above.

The warden rose to its full height, and the vault grew colder, and the lantern flame pulled itself upright like a cat hearing a dog.

"They come with iron and paper," it said. "Iron for you. Paper for the soul." Its voice dropped to that tired register again. "Scribe. Knight. The question I asked you is not finished. It never is. When they break down the door—and they will break down the door—remember what you learned here tonight. The difference between a price and a gift. Because one of you is going to be asked to pay very soon indeed, and the court will be watching to see whether you open your hand or close it."

The boot-steps grew louder.

I put my hand back on my sword. Habit. Prayer. Same thing, some days.

"Well," I said to Theo, "you heard the big smoking fellow. Company."
