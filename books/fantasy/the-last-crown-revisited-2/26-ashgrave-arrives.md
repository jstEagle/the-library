## 26. Ashgrave Arrives

### Ashgrave

I have interrogated forty-one smugglers in my career, and I will tell you what I have learned: everyone lies about the same three things—money, mothers, and the direction they were headed. Maren Voss lied beautifully about all three, which is how I knew her route was true.

The descent took two days. My soldiers complained about it in the way soldiers do, which is to say constantly and at a volume calculated to reach heaven. I let them. Complaint is a pressure valve; a man who grumbles is a man who has not yet despaired, and despair is the only thing I have ever found truly contagious underground.

The shepherd's door was where Voss said it would be, in a cliff face that my own surveyors swore had not existed last spring. I made a note of their names. Incompetence I can work with; being lied to by stone is something else entirely, and I do not care for it.

Inside, the mountain did not behave like a mountain. It behaved like a ledger. The corridors were black glass, and as we marched, whispers ran along the walls reciting figures—names, sums, years of memory traded like coin—and I confess that for the first time since I took this commission, I felt something adjacent to awe. I put it away. Awe is a luxury for people whose mandates are not expiring.

Because that is the truth of it, the truth I carried down into the dark with thirty soldiers and a writ sealed in red wax: the beacon in the capital guttered again last night. The wards along the northern wall flickered like a candle in a draft. The crown's lease on the dragon-fire is coming due, and crowns, unlike men, do not die gracefully when their credit fails. They burn whatever is nearest.

We heard them before we saw them—the scribe and the knight, down in the vault itself, voices echoing off glass. And beneath their voices, another sound. Older. Like a fire trying to remember how to be a fire.

"Form up," I said. "Torches high."

And then I went down the last stair to collect what belongs to the crown.

### Maren

Here's a thing nobody tells you about sabotage: it's easy until you're doing it, and then it's mostly just running, falling, and lying to yourself about how much noise you're making.

I'd come down behind Ashgrave's column like a tick on a mule, through the smuggler cuts his soldiers couldn't find because his soldiers read maps and I *remember* them—which is not the same skill, whatever the guild says. I had a plan. It was a good plan. It involved wedging an iron bar into the mechanism of a stair-gate so that when Ashgrave sent for reinforcements, the reinforcements would arrive approximately never.

The plan failed at step one, because the gate didn't have a mechanism. It had a *toll*. A little alcove of black glass with a voice in it, sweet as syrup, saying: *Passage for passage. What will you give?*

"I'll give you nothing," I whispered, "you glassy horror, I'm sabotaging you."

*Then you may not pass,* said the voice, pleasantly.

So I gave it something small—a memory of my mother's second husband's face, which I'd been meaning to lose anyway—and squeezed through, and got the bar wedged into the hinge, and felt genuinely pleased with myself for maybe nine seconds.

That's when I heard the boots. Not Ashgrave's—he was already below—but the reinforcements he'd sent word for before descending. A dozen of them, coming up the corridor toward the gate I'd just jammed, and one of them carrying a signal-lamp, and all of them moving fast.

They'd reach the gate, find it stuck, force it or cut it, and be down in the vault inside a quarter hour. Ashgrave plus twelve more against a scribe, a knight with half a memory, and whatever ancient tired thing guarded the lantern. That arithmetic had only one answer, and I didn't like it.

I could have gone deeper. Slipped past, down some side-cut, out of the mountain, alive, free, amnesty intact. Every instinct I've sharpened over twenty years of smuggling said *go*, said it clearly, said it in my own voice.

Instead I stepped out around the corner, held up my hands, and said, "Looking for me?"

They were very surprised. Soldiers usually are, when the criminal cooperates. It breaks their whole rhythm.

"Inquisitor Ashgrave wants you," said the sergeant.

"He knows where I am. I'm standing here." I let my wrists come together like a woman surrendering, which I was, technically. "But listen—I'm worth more to him talking than hanging, and I'll talk better if you tell him I bought him time. Say it exactly like that. *Bought him time.* He'll understand."

He wouldn't, of course. But Ashgrave would, eventually, when he worked out why his reinforcements were late, and by then—

By then, hopefully, it would matter.

They bound my hands with cord, not kindly, and marched me down toward the vault, and I went willingly, loudly, complaining about the state of the stairs, buying every second I could with pure theatrical nuisance. Somewhere below, a lantern of gold fire was guttering, and a scribe who'd once sold me maps was standing next to it, and I found—to my considerable irritation—that I cared what happened to them.

Twenty years of self-interest, undone by one kid with ink-stained fingers. If I live through this, I'm never telling anyone.

### Theo

I heard the soldiers before Wren did, which surprised me, because Wren hears everything—she once woke at a moth landing on her boot three rooms away. But she was standing close to the lantern, closer than anyone should stand to something that holy, and the warden was speaking to her in that voice like wind through a long hall, and neither of them was listening for boots.

"Theo," I said. "Boots."

Wren turned. Her hand found her sword the way other people find a light switch—without looking, without thinking. "How many?"

"A lot. Coming down the main stair."

"And the gate?"

"Jamming it was Maren's job."

Wren looked at me, and I looked at Wren, and we both arrived at the same conclusion at the same moment, which is either love or logistics, and honestly at this point I've stopped keeping track.

"Maren," we said together.

The vault was smaller than I'd expected, after everything. A chamber of black glass shaped like the inside of a bell, and at its center, the lantern: a cage of iron and light no bigger than a bread oven, holding a fire the color of late afternoon. Dim. Guttering. But alive, in the way that a sleeping animal is alive—you could feel it breathing if you stood still long enough. The dragon's soul. The last one. Sold by a nine-year-old child with ink on her fingers, held as collateral against a kingdom for twenty years, and now, apparently, about to be seized by the crown at spear-point.

The warden stood between us and the stair. I still don't know what the warden was—not man, not ghost, not smoke exactly, though there was smoke in the shape of it, and bone, and eyes like banked coals. It had asked us each what we would pay, and it had refused Wren's memories, and it had told me, in front of witnesses, the thing that had been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone ever since:

*Payment must be given, not bartered.*

I hadn't understood it yet. I want to say I did—that some great understanding bloomed in me at the warden's words—but the truth is I was still turning the phrase over like a coin I couldn't read, when the soldiers came down the stair.

There were eight of them in the vanguard, then six more behind, torches high, and behind them, unhurried as a man entering his own parlor, came Inquisitor Ashgrave.

He was smaller than I'd imagined. I don't know why I'd imagined him large. He had grey in his beard and kind lines around his eyes, the kind of face you'd trust with your purse or your secrets, and that frightened me more than any number of cruel faces would have. Cruelty announces itself. Kindness used as a tool does not.

"Well," he said, surveying the vault, the lantern, the warden, us. "There it is."

"That's it?" Wren said. "You came all this way to say *there it is*?"

"I'm a man of few ceremonial instincts, Dame—" he paused, delicately, "—whatever your name currently is. Forgive me; records are unclear." He gestured, and two soldiers moved to flank the lantern while four more spread toward us. "By mandate of the Crown of Thornfell, under writ sealed this season: the soul held within this vessel is declared crown property, held in trust these twenty years against the kingdom's welfare, and now reclaimed for renewal of the beacon-lease. You are detained as accessories to theft of crown assets."

"It's not theirs," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "It was never theirs. It was given—freely, once—to save this valley. The crown doesn't hold it. The contract holds it, and the contract isn't the crown's either."

Ashgrave turned to look at me properly for the first time, and I watched him do the thing inquisitors do, which is weigh you. I felt the scales tip.

"Theo Dunmore," he said. "Scribe, third order. Debtor to the Order of the Quiet Flame. And signer." He smiled gently. "Yes. We know what you signed. The court was good enough to show us the rolls. You've been very helpful, coming down here. You saved my archivists a great deal of research."

Something cold went through me. "You can't renew the lease. Only the signer can dissolve it, and—"

"—and dissolution requires payment in full, or forgiveness entire. Yes, the warden explained it to us as well." He glanced at the warden with something almost like respect. "Which is why I don't intend to dissolve anything. Renewal requires only the signature of the current holder of the debt. Which is you." He produced, from his coat, a document. Black paper. Ink that seemed to move slightly when I wasn't looking directly at it. "Sign, and your family's debts are cleared, your Order's claims voided, your legal troubles concluded. Sign, and the fire burns another twenty years, the wards hold, the crops come in. Sign, and everyone goes home."

Behind him, the warden said nothing. But I felt its attention settle on me like a hand on the shoulder, patient as stone.

Everyone goes home. That's the trap of it. That's always the trap. Nine-year-olds sign things because someone promises everyone goes home.

"They'll starve anyway," I said. "A leased fire is a dying fire. You know that. Twenty more years of guttering, and then someone else's problem."

"Twenty years is a career, Master Dunmore. Perhaps several. Men in my position plan in twenty-year increments and leave the rest to providence." He held out the pen. It was black, and it was warm, and I swear it leaned toward my hand like a plant toward sun. "Or refuse, and be arrested, and the crown will simply wait. Contracts outlast prisoners. It's rather their whole nature."

And that's when the ceiling of the corridor above us rang like a struck bell, and shouting started, and I thought—for one bright, stupid second—that it was rescue.

It wasn't. It was Maren.

### Maren

Let the record show—if there's a record, and in this place there's always a record—that my sabotage did not fail so much as *succeed insufficiently.* There's a difference. Ask any smuggler.

The jammed gate bought eleven minutes. Eleven minutes during which Ashgrave's reinforcements hammered at a door that would not open, during which the sergeant escorting me kept glancing upward with increasing anxiety, during which I did the most annoying, obstructive, uncooperative version of prisonerhood I could manage without getting hit: stumbling on the stairs, asking for water, needing to relieve myself, questioning the chain of command. I am very good at being a burden. It's a professional skill.

Eleven minutes. Long enough for the warden to move between the soldiers and the lantern. Long enough for Wren to get her back to a wall and her sword out and her weight settled, ready. Long enough for Theo to look at that black document in Ashgrave's hand and take one full step back from it instead of forward.

Maybe that's all it needed to buy. Maybe that was the whole point, though I didn't know it yet.

Then the gate gave—they cut the hinges, because soldiers solve everything with axes—and the shouting started, and my eleven minutes were spent.

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do. More boots on the stairs. Wren engaging the vanguard—gods, she fought beautifully even half-erased, all economy and fury, dropping the first man with the pommel and the second with the flat, trying *not* to kill them, which costs you time you don't have. Theo pressed back against the lantern-cage, and the fire inside it flared at their proximity, gold light spilling between the bars like something leaning close to a window.

And Ashgrave, calm as Sunday, turned to his sergeant and said, "Take the scribe. The knight is secondary."

Four soldiers peeled off toward Theo. Wren was pinned by three of her own. The warden stood unmoving at the center of it all, watching, weighing, doing whatever ancient patient things wardens do instead of helping.

So I did the arithmetic again. One smuggler, hands bound, positioned—by luck or by design, I've decided to call it design—directly between the soldiers and the scribe.

I headbutted the sergeant.

I want that noted too. Bound hands, and I still got the headbutt in. He went sideways into his corporal, and the pair of them went into the third man, and for about four seconds there was a lovely tangle of armor and profanity blocking the stair, and I shouted, at the top of a voice honed by twenty years of market squares:

"Scribe! *Move!*"

Theo moved. I saw it—saw them duck under a spear, saw Wren break free of her pin and carve a path, saw the two of them collide and hold onto each other like the world was ending, which, give it a minute.

Then a soldier's gauntlet closed on my collar, and the floor came up, and a boot found my ribs, and the tangle resolved itself at my expense, which is the traditional resolution.

"Voss," Ashgrave said, crouching beside me where I lay wheezing on black glass. He sounded almost fond. "Your amnesty was contingent on cooperation."

"My amnesty was contingent on you being able to enforce it," I said, and spat blood, which on black glass looks surprisingly festive. "Check the gate upstairs. Check what's jammed in it. Then do your sums."

For the first time, the inquisitor's calm flickered. Just at the edges. Just enough.

"Bind her properly," he said, rising. "Gag her if she talks. And find the scribe."

Above us, the lantern flared—brighter than it had burned in twenty years, bright enough to throw our shadows huge and wavering across the glass walls, bright enough that every soldier flinched—and somewhere in the heart of that gold light, I heard Theo Dunmore start to speak.

Not to Ashgrave. Not to the soldiers.

To the fire.

### Theo

Maren bought us seconds. I counted them, later, and I think there were perhaps forty of them—forty seconds purchased with her ribs and her freedom and twenty years of hard-won self-interest thrown away like it cost her nothing. I owe her more than I can write here. This account will try anyway.

Forty seconds. Enough to reach the cage. Enough to press my palms against the warm iron bars and feel the fire lean into my touch like a cat that has waited a very long time by a door.

Enough to understand, finally, what the warden meant.

*Payment must be given, not bartered.* Ashgrave offered barter—my signature for my freedom, a trade, a contract, more of the same poison in a different bottle. The court dealt in barter. The whole mountain was built of barter, memory for passage, years for breath, soul for mother. Everything priced. Everything owed.

But the dragon had given its soul freely, once, to save the valley. No price. No ledger. That was the original act the whole rotten edifice had been built to imitate and could never reproduce.

And the child who sold it—nine years old, terrified, ink on her fingers—hadn't been evil. She'd been *bargained with*, by experts, holding the only currency she had. I had spent the whole descent hating that child. Standing at the cage, feeling the fire breathe, I found I couldn't anymore.

"Soldiers!" Ashgrave's voice cracked across the vault. "Take them!"

Wren's back met mine. Sword out, blood on her cheek, breathing hard. "Whatever you're going to do," she said, "do it now."

"I know," I said. "Keep them off me."

"For how long?"

"As long as it takes."

She laughed—actually laughed, short and fierce, in the middle of everything. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

The fire rose against the bars. The warden watched. Ashgrave advanced with his black document and his kind, terrible face, and above us Maren was laughing at something through her gag, and the mountain held its breath.

I opened my mouth to speak the words I'd been carrying since the cell, since the rolls, since the mural of a child with a familiar sash—

And the first word I spoke was not a name.

It was a forgiveness.
