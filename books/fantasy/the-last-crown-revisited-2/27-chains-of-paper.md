## 27. Chains of Paper

The cell was made of sentences.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that when I pressed my palm against the wall, I felt the grain of it — and the grain was words. *Let the prisoner be held. Let the prisoner be held. Let the prisoner be held,* stacked in columns so fine they looked like stone until you got close enough to read them, and then you wished you hadn't, because reading them made them truer.

Somewhere behind that wall, a scribe had sat down with a pen and written me into a box. As a scribe myself, I had to admire the craftsmanship. As the box's contents, I had some notes.

They'd taken everything at the vault door: the map, Wren's sword, my belt knife, the stub of pencil I kept in my sleeve out of habit older than sense. They'd taken Maren too — dragged her past me with blood on her mouth and a look on her face that said *don't*, though whether she meant *don't help me* or *don't do anything stupid* was unclear, and with Maren it was probably both.

They had not taken my hands. That was the first thing I checked, sitting in the dark with my back against a wall of my own eventual biography. Hands intact. Mind — well. We'd see.

"Ink," I said aloud, to no one. "Paper."

Nothing answered, which I'd expected. But it felt important to establish early that I still thought in terms of materials. A prisoner who stops thinking like a scribe is just meat in a sentence.

* * *

Time passed. I know because the wall told me so.

That sounds mad. Let me explain. The cell didn't have windows or torches or any of the traditional furnishings of misery. What it had was clauses, and the clauses updated. Every so often — I want to say hourly; the mountain doesn't believe in hours — fresh script would bleed up through the older layers like bruises surfacing, and the new script was always bookkeeping.

*Prisoner has slept. Prisoner has dreamed; dreams unrecorded per Section Twelve. Prisoner has not eaten. Prisoner remains bound by prior agreements.*

That last one appeared more often than the others. It liked appearing. I began to suspect the cell enjoyed it.

"Prior agreements," I said. "Which ones? Be specific. Any contract worth its salt specifies its terms."

The wall obliged. Of course it did. Contracts always want to be read; that's their whole pathology.

Script surfaced directly across from my face, close enough that I could have kissed it if I'd been the kissing-a-wall type:

*Agreement the First: tendered by T. Dunmore, age nine years, one soul whole and willing, against the breath of the mother. Status: active. Signatory: present.*

I closed my eyes.

Nine-year-old me had signed that with a hand that barely knew how to hold a pen. I knew this now the way you know a scar's history — not because anyone told me but because the memory had come back in the shrine, all at once, the masked visitor crouched to my height, the smell of hot wax, my mother's rattling breath upstairs, and the visitor's voice like a ledger being balanced: *You needn't give your own soul, little scribe. Only promise one. Souls are fungible. Everything is fungible, if the paperwork is right.*

And nine-year-old me — clever, desperate, raised on scripture and ledgers — had understood exactly enough to be dangerous and exactly not enough to be safe.

"I know," I told the wall. "You don't have to keep telling me."

But it did keep telling me, because that's what contracts are for. Not enforcement, really. Reminding. The Undervault ran on reminding people what they owed until the owing became the shape of their lives. I'd spent twenty years buying down my family's debt one smuggler map at a time, and I'd thought that was freedom work. It wasn't. It was payment work. There's a difference, and the wall was teaching it to me in fine print.

*Prisoner is reminded,* the script added, almost gently, *that refusal to acknowledge an agreement does not dissolve it.*

"Thank you," I said. "I'll be sure to file that under things I already knew."

* * *

Ashgrave came at what I decided to call morning, because he arrived with light — a globe of pale flame floating ahead of him, and behind it two soldiers whose armor was the color of wet slate. The Inquisitor himself wore grey wool, unadorned except for the crown's seal at his throat, which was a dragon coiled around nothing. I'd never noticed before that the dragon on the royal seal had nothing in its coils. Now it seemed less like decoration and more like an admission.

He looked at me the way I looked at a badly copied page: cataloguing the errors, deciding how many were salvageable.

"Theo Dunmore," he said.

"Technically," I said. "Though I'm given to understand the court has opinions about whether that name holds."

"Names hold." He gestured, and a stool unfolded itself out of the floor — more script braiding itself into something you could sit on, which struck me as showing off. He sat. "Names are the only thing in this mountain that reliably holds. That's rather the point of everything."

"You've been down here long?"

"Long enough." He folded his hands. He had the patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, which is the most frightening kind of patience there is. "Do you know why the beacon failed?"

I did, actually. I knew more than he wanted me to know. The dragon-fire in the capital's great beacon wasn't fire at all — it was interest. The kingdom had been drawing on a soul for three hundred years, warmth and wards and harvest-luck, all of it skimmed off the collateral the court held. And collateral, when the debtor defaults, gets called.

"Crops," I said instead. "Wards thinning. Cold coming earlier each year. You're here because your lease is running out."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, quickly filed away. "You read the murals."

"I read everything. Occupational hazard."

"Then you understand the situation better than most of my colleagues above ground, who believe the fire fails because of weather." He leaned forward slightly. "Here is what happens next, Master Dunmore. The crown presents its claim to the court. The soul reverts to sovereign holding. The beacon is fed. Winter ends. Order continues. This is not villainy; it is maintenance. Empires are mostly maintenance."

"And the part where you arrest everyone who helped me find the place? Is that maintenance too?"

"Housekeeping," he said, without a flicker.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. There was something almost restful about a man that honest about being awful.

* * *

He let me laugh. He even waited it out, which was worse than interrupting would have been. Then he produced, from inside his coat, a document.

I knew it was trouble before he unrolled it, because it was beautiful. Heavy vellum, ink gone brown-gold with age, the crown's seal stamped twice — once in wax, once, horribly, in something darker. The script on it was old court-hand, the kind they teach novices to copy before they let them touch real contracts, and every line of it hummed faintly, the way the walls hummed. This paper was kin to my cell. It had been written *in* the Undervault, or by someone with the court's permission, and either way it was alive in the way legal documents are only ever alive at their worst moments.

"Do you know what this is?" Ashgrave asked.

"A renewal of lease," I said. "Three hundred years, same terms. Drawn the last time the fire guttered, presumably, and never used because the fire recovered on its own."

His eyebrow rose a fraction. "Your mentor would be proud."

"My mentor is dead." The words came out flat, and I watched them land. Cassian's letters had stopped mid-sentence weeks ago, and I had spent every night since composing versions of what happened, and none of the versions were survivable to think about, so I'd stopped thinking about them, and here was Ashgrave handing them back to me like change from a purchase.

"I'm sorry," he said, and sounded it, which somehow made it worse. "Cassian Vael was a good archivist. Too good. He spent thirty years misfiling things to protect a child he should have reported." He smoothed the document on his knee. "This is why I'm here personally, Master Dunmore, and not simply sending soldiers. Because the renewal requires a signature."

"Yours."

"Mine is insufficient. Read the preamble."

I took the scroll. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Scribes' hands go steady at the worst moments; we're trained to it the way soldiers are trained to stand in lines.

*Be it renewed upon the signatories named herein: the Sovereign's instrument, and the Debtor of Record, whose consent confirms the original tender remains valid, willing, and binding.*

The Debtor of Record. Me. Nine years old, T. Dunmore, one soul whole and willing. The crown couldn't renew the lease without the original signer confirming that the soul had been *willingly* tendered — because willingness was the load-bearing word. The whole architecture of the Undervault stood on it. Take away the willing, and every contract built atop it wobbled.

They needed me to say yes. Again. Twenty years later, in my own adult hand, with full knowledge of what I was agreeing to this time.

"There it is," I said quietly.

"There it is," Ashgrave agreed. "Sign, and you walk free. All of you — the knight, the smuggler woman, even the shepherd, though he's not our concern. The Order's debts against your family will be marked paid in full. Your mother's grave stays a grave and not a liability. You could be in Thornfell by spring with nothing owed to anyone above ground or below."

I looked at the signature line. It had my name already printed on it, in advance, in careful court-hand — *T. Dunmore, Debtor of Record* — waiting above a blank space shaped exactly like a hand.

"They wrote my name before they asked me."

"That's how contracts work here. The asking is a formality." He said it without shame. "The formality matters, though. Willingness must be witnessed. So I am witnessing: Theo Dunmore. Will you renew?"

* * *

I set the scroll on my knees and didn't sign it.

Not yet. I want to be honest about that, because I've had weeks now to remember this moment and I'd rather record it accurately than heroically. I looked at that blank space for a long time, and I wanted to fill it.

Consider what signing bought. Freedom — real freedom, the kind with bread in it. Wren out of whatever cell they had her in. Maren out of chains she'd earned honestly, in her way, by coming back down for us. My family's ledger closed after twenty years of me bleeding maps into the dark to buy down pennies of it. One signature, and every debt I'd ever carried turned to ash.

One signature, and the dragon burned for another three hundred years in a lantern while a kingdom warmed its hands.

"It isn't even your soul they'd be leasing," Ashgrave said, watching me. He was good at watching; I'll grant him that. "It's the beast's. It gave freely once, by every account. You'd merely be confirming the gift. Arguably you'd be honoring it. Keeping faith with a dead creature's generosity."

"That's a very elegant argument."

"Thank you. I composed it on the walk down."

"And underneath it," I said, "is a kingdom that has spent three hundred years spending something it never owned, and now that the account's run dry, it needs a nine-year-old's signature laundered through an adult's hand to keep spending it. You don't want me to honor a gift. You want me to countersign a theft."

"I want winter to end," Ashgrave said, and for the first time his voice had weight in it, actual human weight. "There are four hundred thousand people above us, Master Dunmore. Farmers. Children. Soldiers' widows. The beacon warms the valley and wards the passes and I have seen what comes down out of the high country when the wards fail. You've read the murals. Have you read the border reports?" He shook his head. "You're a scribe. You love precision. So be precise about what your principles cost, and whom."

And there it was. The trap wasn't cruelty; cruelty I knew how to refuse. The trap was arithmetic. Four hundred thousand warm children weighed against one dead dragon's remainder, and any honest scale tips, and I was raised on honest scales.

I looked at the signature line for a long moment. Then at my own hands, which had copied ten thousand pages of scripture about mercy without once understanding it.

"You know what I did when I was nine?" I said. "A masked thing came to our house and offered me a bargain I didn't understand, and I signed it, because my mother was dying and I would have signed anything, and nobody — *nobody* — asked me whether I understood. They just needed a hand. A small one. A cheap one." I rolled the scroll closed. "And now you're here to ask me to do it again, except this time I do understand, which means this time it wouldn't be a tragedy. It would just be a choice."

"Yes," Ashgrave said.

"So my answer is no."

He didn't move. Neither did the soldiers. The light-globe burned steadily between us, and somewhere far above us I imagined the beacon guttering, four hundred thousand hearths a fraction colder, and I made myself hold the weight of it instead of putting it down.

"Master Dunmore—"

"No," I said again, steadier. "Find another way to heat your valley. Steal from yourselves for once. Tax something that consented to be taxed. But you can't have my yes. It's the only thing I own outright, and I'm keeping it."

For a long moment the Inquisitor of the Crown of Thornfell looked at me the way a man looks at a bridge that has just declined to be a bridge.

Then he took back the scroll, unhurried, and stood, and the stool unraveled into clauses again beneath him.

"Refusal is noted," he said. "Witnessed and noted. The court will weigh it."

"I'd hope so. It cost me."

"What costs," he agreed, "is usually what counts." He turned to go, then paused, and something in his posture shifted — not softness, exactly. More like a man setting down an official mask for one second to show the tired face under it. "The knight escaped during the transfer. An hour ago. She took three guards down doing it, and she's loose in the lower galleries, heading — the wardens think — toward the cells."

My heart did something complicated.

"If she's caught," Ashgrave went on, "she'll be bound same as you, and I'll have no reason left to negotiate gently. If she reaches you—" he glanced at the humming walls, "—well. Contracts require witnesses, Master Dunmore. Remember that. It cuts both ways."

And he walked out with his light, leaving me alone in a room made of my own signature, listening to boots fade down a corridor of black glass, counting seconds, hoping — for the first time since a dying courier bled out on a stairwell and put all of this in my hands — hoping that somebody was stubborn enough, and loved enough, and lost enough to come get me.

The wall wrote, softly:

*Prisoner awaits events.*
