## 28. Wren Alone

I ran.

There's no more dignified way to put it. Somewhere behind me, the vault of black glass was filling with soldiers and shouting and the particular sound a person makes when they stop being brave, and I ran the other way, boots skidding on corridors that hadn't existed an hour ago and might not exist in another hour either, because doors that walk don't care about your plans.

In my defense: everyone else was running too. Maren Voss had bought us—bought *me*—about forty seconds with a smuggler's knife and a smuggler's grin, and forty seconds is exactly enough time to survive or to be stupid, and I chose survival like a sensible woman with two weeks' acquaintance and no debts worth dying for.

That's what I told myself, anyway, as I took the left passage instead of the right. The left one sloped down toward darkness that smelled faintly of rain and old iron. The right one sloped back toward the vault, where Theo Dunmore was standing very still while men in crown colors closed around them like ink closing over a written word.

Left. I went left. I want that noted for the record, since apparently there's always a record.

* * *

The corridor let me out into a gallery of pillars so tall their tops vanished into a dark that wasn't empty—it was *listening*. The whispers were everywhere down here, reciting contracts in dead voices, and now that I'd been in this place long enough for my skin to know it, I could almost pick out individual clauses floating past me like moths.

*...tendered by T. Dunmore, age nine...*

*...one soul, whole and willing...*

*...in exchange for the mother's breath...*

I stopped walking. Put my hand against a pillar to steady myself. It was warm under my palm, which pillars have no business being.

"Shut up," I told them.

They didn't. They never do. But something in the cadence of my voice made the nearest whispers falter, just slightly—the way a room goes quiet when someone says a name it recognizes—and I stood there in the dark with my heart going like a siege drum, understanding at last why the mountain had been pulling at me for weeks like a hook through the ribs.

It knew me. I'd been inside before. And whatever I'd paid on my last visit, it wasn't finished taking.

* * *

Here's what I remembered having: nothing useful. That's been the shape of my life since I woke in a ditch outside Thornfell with a notched sword and three days missing. Before that, I had fragments—a courtyard, a dragon's eye the size of a shield, myself kneeling with my blade drawn and refusing some order I couldn't name. The Archivist of Selves had shown me that much, dangled it like bait, offered to sell me back the rest at a price I couldn't pay without becoming someone else entirely.

So I'd walked out of that office with my fragments and my pride and a scribe who looked at me like I was a map they'd drawn themselves.

Two weeks. That's all it's been. Two weeks of ambush and stalemate and grudging truce; two weeks of Theo talking to maps the way other people talk to lovers, patient and exacting; two weeks of watching them lie badly to enforcers and then, an hour later, lie beautifully; two weeks of salt-and-cinder wind and honest conversation forced out of both of us by near-death, which is the only kind of conversation I've ever trusted.

And somewhere in those two weeks—I couldn't tell you the day, and that bothers me more than anything—the person I was fleeing from stopped being a stranger I'd happened to ally with and started being...

Well.

Started being the reason I was standing in a listening dark arguing with myself instead of running for the exit like a sensible woman.

"Go back," I said aloud, to hear how it sounded.

It sounded like suicide. The court had soldiers in it now, Ashgrave's soldiers, and Ashgrave himself, a man who looked at living people the way archivists look at documents—assessing condition, noting damage, calculating shelf life. Going back meant chains. Possibly worse than chains, because this was the Undervault, and here even your chains came with terms and conditions.

"Don't go back," said a dead voice from the ceiling, helpfully.

"Nobody asked you."

*...the debtor forgives, or the debtor pays,* whispered another, drifting past like smoke through a keyhole, and I didn't understand it then but I wrote it down anyway, in the ledger I keep behind my eyes where the mountain can't reach without permission. Some habits survive memory loss. Paranoia, mostly. Paranoia keeps beautifully.

* * *

I did the arithmetic while I walked, because arithmetic is what I do when I'm frightened. It's the knight's version of prayer.

Option one: keep moving. There were smuggler ways out of the Undervault—I'd seen Maren navigate passages the soldiers couldn't map, and I'd watched her long enough to steal maybe a third of her tricks. Odds of reaching daylight alone: fair. Odds of staying free after: poor, because Ashgrave would have my description, my sword, and eventually my location, and the crown does not forget disgraced knights it has finally found a use for.

But alive. Alive for months, maybe years. Long enough to drink cheap wine in a border town and watch sunsets and feel the specific pleasure of having survived something enormous.

Option two: go back.

Going back meant Theo. Theo, who had sold a dragon's soul at nine years old to buy their mother one more breath, and had spent every year since paying down debts that weren't even theirs in any way that mattered. Theo, whose face when they'd read their own contract in the public rolls had been the worst thing I've ever seen—not grief, grief I know how to fight, but *recognition*, the look of someone finding their own handwriting on a confession they don't remember writing.

Theo, who had turned to me afterward and said, in a voice scraped down to the grain, "You'd have signed it too. If it was your mother."

And I'd said, "Yes," because it was true, and because lying to them had stopped being possible somewhere around the toll house, when I'd paid a memory for passage and they'd watched me do it with horror and hadn't once treated me like a fool for it.

That was the moment, if I'm honest. Not the ambush, not the pass, not the fall we barely survived. A toll house, and a small gray memory I gave away—the name of a dog, I think, some animal from a childhood I can't otherwise verify—and Theo looking at me like what I'd lost mattered, like *I* mattered, not as an asset or a sword arm but as a person who'd just become slightly less herself and deserved to be mourned for it.

Nobody had mourned pieces of me before. People mourn whole people. Pieces, they just step around.

* * *

I found a side chamber off the gallery—an alcove, really, walled in the same black glass, with a bench that may or may not have been furniture before the mountain got hungry. I sat down because my legs had opinions and I was tired of overruling them.

The whispers were quieter in there. Or maybe I'd stopped hearing them. Hard to say which.

Here is the thing I figured out on that bench, sitting in the dark with my notched sword across my knees, and it arrived with the unglamorous certainty of a debt coming due:

The memories I wanted back—the ones the Archivist had dangled, the ones I'd been chasing down this mountain like a woman chasing her own shadow—those memories weren't the point anymore. The courtyard, the dragon, the order I refused. I wanted them because they were *mine*, because a self with holes in it feels like a house with the windows broken, cold in places cold shouldn't be. But wanting them had stopped being the engine of me somewhere on the road, and I hadn't noticed until now, sitting still, doing the sums.

Because when I imagined getting them back—really imagined it, the Archivist's hands opening the little drawers of me, restoring everything—I realized the restoration would include the weeks I'd lost. Three days in a ditch. Whatever compulsion had dragged me toward Thornfell. And it would *not* include the two weeks after, because those weren't eaten; those were earned.

The memories I wanted back included Theo now. That was the discovery. That was the ugly, inconvenient, heart-stopping truth of it: somewhere between a toll house and a contract scroll, Theo Dunmore had gotten into the part of me I'd assumed was sealed, the part the mountain had already billed, and now if I fled and lived, I wouldn't just be a woman with holes in her history. I'd be a woman who'd chosen the holes over the one person who'd ever looked at them without flinching.

Love, as it turns out, isn't a feeling you catch. I've had the catching kind—knew a boy in the capital once, before, whenever *before* was, and the memory of him is soft-edged and possibly invented. No. Love is a decision you make in a corridor with soldiers between you and the person, and you make it knowing the price, and you make it anyway, and then it stops being a decision and starts being a fact about you, load-bearing, structural, the beam you build the rest of the house around.

I decided on the bench in the dark. It took perhaps four seconds. The other fifty-six I spent being afraid, which I mention because courage gets romanticized, and in my experience courage is ninety percent terror with the direction reversed.

* * *

Getting back was the problem.

Maren's trick—one of the thirds I'd stolen—was that the Undervault's passages respond to debts. Doors that walk follow unpaid obligations. So I stood in the gallery, held my notched sword out flat on both palms like an offering, and said to the listening dark:

"I owe someone. Take me to them."

Nothing. The whispers kept reciting. My ears burned with the stupidity of it.

Then I thought about what the mountain actually trades in, and I amended: "I owe *Theo Dunmore*." And when that got nothing: "I owe the person who signed the contract. The signer. Take me to the signer, and I'll pay the interest."

The wall opened.

Not dramatically—no grinding stone, no theatrical reveal. One moment it was black glass, and the next it was a doorway, and beyond it a corridor sloping down toward orange light and shouting, and I understood that the mountain considered me a customer in good standing and was happy to facilitate repeat business.

"Charming establishment," I muttered, and drew my sword, and went.

* * *

The corridor delivered me to the edge of chaos.

The outer court had become a battlefield conducted in the language of bureaucracy. Ashgrave's soldiers had formed ranks around the vault entrance, and masked figures of smoke and bone drifted among them like weather, and somewhere in the middle of it all I could hear Ashgrave's voice, dry and precise, reading legal language aloud as though it were ammunition—which, here, it was.

And there, at the center, hands bound in ribboned paper that glowed faintly with their own signature: Theo.

They were on their knees. They weren't struggling. They were watching the soldiers with that terrible calm of theirs, the scribe's calm, the look of someone cataloguing everything for later, and when one of the masks drifted close and inclined its bone-white face, Theo inclined their head back, polite as a clerk, and I felt something in my chest go tight and furious and absolutely clear.

Forty seconds, I'd given myself. That was the plan forming as I moved—forty seconds of chaos, same currency Maren had paid, except she'd paid hers with blood and I intended to pay mine with noise.

There was a brazier near the court's edge. Court of smoke and bone, and they still need fire for light; even nightmares keep housekeeping standards. I crossed the distance in the blind spot between two pillars, kicked the brazier over into the paper-ribbon bindings stacked on a trestle—because of course a contractual prison keeps its materials organized; archivists are archivists everywhere—and the ribbons caught with a sound like a scream played backwards.

Every mask in the court turned toward the fire.

Every soldier turned toward the masks turning.

And I came out of the blind spot moving, low and fast, the way I'd been trained by someone whose face I couldn't remember but whose lessons lived in my body like scripture lives in a scribe's hand, and I reached Theo, and I cut the burning ribbons off their wrists in two strokes, and their head came up and their eyes found mine and went wide.

"Wren," they said. Just the name. Like checking it was real.

"Told you," I said, hauling them up, "I'm bad at leaving things unfinished."

"You didn't tell me that."

"Well." I put my back to theirs, sword up, the first soldiers already wheeling toward us through the smoke. "Consider it told."

Somewhere above, the whole court shuddered, and a whisper slid down through the listening dark, and this time it wasn't reciting old contracts. It was reciting a new one, fresh ink, wet and waiting:

*...the knight returns for the signer... witnessed, witnessed, entered into the record...*

Good, I thought, parrying the first blade, then the second. Let them write it down.

Let the whole mountain know exactly who I came back for.
