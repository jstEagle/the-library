## 31. Trial Before the Masks

**Theo**

They came for me at what I judged—by nothing, because there was no sun down there, no bells, no candle to count down to its own death—to be morning.

Two wardens of smoke and bone, their faces smooth as river stones, and behind them the Archivist of Selves carrying a ledger so large it needed a second archivist just to hold the other end. I thought about making a joke. I had been thinking about making jokes for what felt like two days in that cell of contractual language, because the alternative was thinking about what Ashgrave had offered me, and what I'd said in reply, and how my voice had sounded when I said *no* — steady, almost bored, as if refusing an inquisitor of the crown were something I did every week between copying scripture and forging smuggler charts.

"Prisoner," said the lead warden, in a voice like a page turning. "You are summoned to weighment."

"Weighment," I repeated. "That sounds festive."

The warden did not laugh. Nothing down here laughed. The corridors of black glass swallowed sound the way a miser swallows coins, and our footsteps came back to us thin and wrong, like someone else's footsteps, someone lighter, someone who hadn't spent nine years paying off a family debt one copied psalm at a time.

I let myself think about Wren instead. That was my ration, my single indulgence per hour: thirty seconds of Wren, then back to survival. In the melee — Ashgrave's soldiers flooding the vault like water finding every crack, Maren's sabotage going up in sparks and shouts, her being dragged down by three men while she laughed at them, actually *laughed*, the magnificent idiot — in all of that, Wren had been beside me, and then she wasn't. I didn't see her taken. I didn't see her fall. I held onto that absence of evidence the way a drowning scribe holds onto a floating ledger: not buoyant, exactly, but better than nothing.

She'd gotten out. She had to have gotten out. Because if she hadn't, then the last thing I ever said to her was "Go, I'll be fine," and I would like the record — any record, even theirs, especially theirs — to show that I am capable of saying something better than that before I die.

* * *

**Ashgrave**

I have interrogated forty-one traitors, heretics, debtors, smugglers, and one remarkably unrepentant nun, and I will tell you what none of them understand: everyone breaks the same way. Not quickly. Not dramatically. They break the way a rope breaks, fiber by fiber, each strand insisting to the last that it is still whole.

Dunmore would not break. I found this irritating.

Not because resistance offended me — resistance is simply data — but because Dunmore's resistance had a specific texture I recognized from the archives. It was the resistance of someone who had already lost everything once and done the arithmetic on grief. You cannot threaten a person like that with loss. You can only offer them something, and hope they want it more than they fear you.

I did not want Dunmore's cooperation out of cruelty. I want that written somewhere, though I know better than to expect fairness from history; history is kept by whoever survives, and I intend to survive. I wanted the crown renewed. I wanted the beacon burning and the wards holding and the granaries full and the border lords fat and quiet. A kingdom is a machine, and the dragon-fire was its boiler, and I had been dispatched — by men who signed nothing, risked nothing, and would remember nothing of this — to go beneath the world and collect the fuel.

The soul-contract was the mechanism. T. Dunmore, age nine, tendered one soul, whole and willing. The crown's claim rested on that paper the way a roof rests on a wall. And now the signer sat in my custody, and the mountain itself had convened to settle accounts, and I understood — I am not a stupid man, whatever the smuggler woman screamed at me while they carried her off — that everything, crown and court and contract alike, would turn on what happened in that courtroom of smoke.

They dressed me in my grey coat for the occasion. Grey, the color of ash, which is what fire leaves behind. My mother chose the color when I took my oaths, and she said, "So they never mistake you for the thing you burn." I have worn it forty years. Some mornings I still smell smoke where there is none.

The wardens came for the prisoner. I followed. Behind us, the mountain hummed like a struck bell that refuses to finish ringing, and far above us — beyond miles of stone, beyond the pass, beyond the salt flats — I knew without needing a report that the beacon in the capital was guttering. I had seen the flame the day I left. It had looked at me. Fire does not look at people. This fire did.

It had looked at me the way a debtor looks at his creditor's agent.

I did not care for the symmetry. I went anyway.

* * *

**Theo**

The courtroom was not a room. That was my first thought, and my second, and eventually my only thought, because the mind snags on impossibility like a sleeve on a nail.

It was a hollow in the mountain the size of a cathedral, or perhaps the mountain was the cathedral and we were inside one of its thoughts. The floor was black glass, and the glass moved — slowly, tectonically — and under it, far under it, shapes drifted: contracts, actual contracts, thousands of them, scrolls and ledgers and scraps of vellum swimming in the dark like fish in a well. Every bargain ever signed beneath Thornfell, filed alive. Somewhere down there was a scroll with my signature on it, small and crooked, age nine, ink still wet after twenty years because some inks do not dry until the debt does.

The masks stood in tiers around the hollow. Smoke given posture, bone given faces. There were hundreds. They did not breathe. They turned, all together, all at once, when the wardens brought me in, and the motion made a sound like a book closing.

And there, on a dais of fused glass that might once have been a throne, stood the Archivist of Selves, holding the great ledger open across both arms, and beside him — smaller, older, wearing patience like a coat that no longer fit — was the warden from the vault. The dragon's remnant will, tired and ancient and watching me with eyes that had been waiting twenty years for this appointment and resented having to keep it.

"The trial of account," it said. Its voice was not loud. It arrived inside your chest rather than your ears, the way thunder arrives through the floorboards. "The court convenes. All debts stand open. All signatures stand binding." A pause, the length of a held breath nobody took. "Present the claimants."

Ashgrave stepped forward from the tier where he'd been standing — when had he arrived? He moved like a man who had always already been in every room — and produced, from a case lined with velvet, a copy of the crown's writ. I knew that seal. Everyone in Thornfell knew that seal. You saw it on tax notices and conscription letters and the little cards they slipped under doors when someone died owing money.

"Inquisitor Ashgrave," he said, and his voice carried in that space the way a knife carries into bread, clean and certain, "speaking for the Crown of the Valley, its regent, its heirs, and its beacon." He unrolled the writ. It glowed faintly. Of course it glowed. Everything down here glowed except the people. "The Crown asserts its lawful claim upon the asset designated the Last Flame, held in collateral under contract dated—" he recited the date, and the date was my ninth birthday, which is the kind of detail the universe includes when it wants you to know it has a sense of humor and it is not a kind one, "—tendered by the signer present."

Murmuring rose from the tiers — not voices, exactly, but the memory of voices, contracts whispering their own terms, thousands of bargains reciting themselves in dead tongues and living ones.

"The Crown further asserts," Ashgrave continued, "that renewal of the lease upon said Flame is necessary to the welfare of the realm, and claims the right of execution of that renewal, requiring the co-signature of the original tenderer, who is—" and here he paused, and I watched him pause, and I understood that the pause was not hesitation but placement, a man setting a stone precisely where he wanted it, "—present, and named."

And the Archivist of Selves turned the great ledger toward me, and the warden on the dais inclined its ruined head, and every mask in that impossible courtroom turned back to me, hundreds of blank faces, and the question came down like weather:

"Signer. State your name for the record."

* * *

**Wren**

I have fought in four wars, been knighted twice — once properly, once as an insult by a dying general who meant it kindly — and I can tell you that no battlefield ever frightened me like that courtroom did, and I wasn't even in it. I was in the gallery of black glass above the tiers, flat against a wall that was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, watching through a seam in the stone.

Getting there had cost me a corridor full of soldiers, one stolen sword, and a promise I made to myself in the dark, which was: *you go back, you get him out, and if you die doing it you will at least die knowing your own name.* Because that was the other thing. Down here, the toll-keeping walls whispered to me constantly, offering fragments of Wren-the-knight back like a pawnbroker laying out rings. *Here. Your mother's face. Here, the oath, the white cloak, here, the order you refused, the dragon kneeling, the sword in your hand.* I could have them. Any of them. All of them. One price.

Later. I told the whispers. After.

Because I had finally worked out the arithmetic that had eluded me since the ditch outside Thornfell: the memories I wanted most weren't old. They were new. Rain on a smuggler road. Theo arguing with a map. Theo lying badly, then well. Two weeks, and the vault-walls could keep every year before them if the price of keeping those two weeks was walking away.

I pressed my eye to the seam and watched them ask Theo for his name, and I felt my hand close on the stolen sword hilt, and I understood — with the particular clarity that arrives only when it is too late to be useful — why the mountain had pulled me here. Not compulsion. Recruitment. It had known, before I did, what I would choose.

Below, Theo opened his mouth to answer.

* * *

**Theo**

State your name for the record.

I want to describe what those six words did to me, but description requires distance and I had none. Let me try anyway, because I was trained as a scribe and trained scribes narrate their own funerals.

My name. Nine years of indenture, and my name was the one thing I owned outright. Not the debt — the debt owned my labor, my next forty years, my mother's grave and my brother's schooling. But *Theo Dunmore*, that was mine, earned letter by letter in the scriptorium, defended against Brother Cassian's gentle corrections and the Order's numbering systems and every smuggler who'd ever called me "scribe" like it was an insult. I had built that name the way you build anything worth having: slowly, stubbornly, out of materials nobody else wanted.

And underneath it, older, wetter, nine years old and crooked: the signature. The same name. The name that sold a soul. The name that saved my mother. Both true. Both mine. The wound and the weapon, forged in the same fire, and now the entire court beneath the mountain stood waiting for me to say it aloud, because down here a name was not a word. Down here a name was a handle, and a handle is how you lift a thing, and everything they intended to do to me — bind me, bill me, renew the lease with my living hand — required the handle first.

Ashgrave wanted it for the crown's claim. The masks wanted it for the rolls. Even the warden — even the tired old remnant of the last dragon, whose soul my childish hand had signed away — waited on it, because a contract without a spoken confirmation is a door without a hinge.

I thought of my mother. Not the deathbed — I'd had enough of the deathbed, it had replayed behind my eyes for two days straight — but earlier. Her hands kneading bread. Her saying my name across the yard, that specific rising note she put on it, half call, half song. If I gave them the name, did I give them that too? Could they reach down through the black glass and take her voice out of my memory and file it among the contracts?

The Archivist's pen hovered over the ledger. Ink darker than the dark.

"Signer," said the warden, patiently, terribly, "the court requires your name."

And here is what I knew, in the marrow-way, the way you know a stair has one more step than your foot expected: the moment I said it, everything was decided. The trial was theater. Contracts were the law here, and a contract needs a name the way a fire needs air, and they were asking me — politely, formally, with hundreds of centuries of precedent stacked in tiers around me — to breathe.

Ashgrave watched me. His face did the thing careful men's faces do, offering nothing, and yet I swear — and I have lied professionally, I know the craft — I swear there was something in his eyes that was not triumph. Something almost like a warning. As if the inquisitor, who had broken forty-one people fiber by fiber, was standing in the one courtroom in the world where he suddenly understood he was not the rope.

I looked at the tiers of masks. I looked at the drifting contracts below the glass, the fish-swarms of other people's bargains, other people's mothers saved and damned. I thought: every one of those began with a name, spoken aloud, in good faith, by someone who didn't know the price.

I thought of Wren, wherever she was, and whether she was watching, and whether she would understand what I was about to do, and whether it mattered, because I was about to stop being a person anyone could find.

The pen waited. The mountain waited. Somewhere above us, impossibly far, the last dragon's soul burned low in its lantern, waiting on a signature twenty years overdue.

"No," I said.

The sound did not echo. The glass drank it.

"I don't give my name," I said, and my voice was steadier than it had any right to be, and I heard myself finishing the sentence before I fully knew it, the way the best lies arrive already finished, "because I'm not the one who owes it."

Every mask in the court turned — all at once, all together, that book-closing sound — and the Archivist's pen stopped above the ledger, and the warden's ancient eyes widened by a fraction that in a creature that old was a scream.

And the black glass under my feet began, very quietly, to forget me.
