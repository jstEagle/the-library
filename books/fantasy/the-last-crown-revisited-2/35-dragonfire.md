## 35. Dragonfire

### Theo

I want to tell you that forgiving yourself is quiet.

It isn't. It is the loudest thing I have ever done, and I have spent my life among bells and chanting and Brother Odell's snoring, which could strip paint from a wall.

The words left me and did not echo. That was the first strange thing. The Undervault swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else—names, years, the memory of rain—but these words it *kept*. They hung in the black glass of the vault like insects in amber. *You were nine. You were afraid. You wanted her to live.* And then, the part that mattered, the part that took everything I had: *I forgive you.*

The lantern of gold fire at the center of the vault—smaller than a loaf of bread, dimmer than a dying candle, holding inside it something older than every mountain in Thornfell—trembled.

"It's listening," Wren breathed beside me. She had one hand on my shoulder. Not gripping. Just there, the way you rest your hand on a doorpost of a house you've decided to stay in.

The warden stood across the lantern from us, or rather was arranged across from us, its shape more suggestion than body—a figure like smoke given the idea of bones. It had been tired for so long, I realized. Tired the way a sentry is tired on the last watch of a war everyone else forgot to end.

"Say it again," the warden said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way contracts do when they're read aloud by someone who isn't being paid to read them. "Not for the record. For the child."

So I said it again. And this time I didn't say it like a scribe copying scripture, careful and correct and dead behind the eyes. I said it like what I was: a person standing in front of the smallest, most frightened version of themselves, telling them the thing nobody told either of us until now.

*You didn't do wrong. You did the only thing love knew how to do. Come here. Put it down. Put it down, little one. It was never yours to carry.*

The lantern cracked.

Not broke—cracked, the way an egg cracks, the way ice cracks on a river in spring, the way a fist unclenches after being closed for three hundred years. Light came out through the seam, thin as thread at first, then wider, and the light was *warm*, which is a stupid thing to notice but I noticed it, because I had been cold since I was nine years old and hadn't known it until that exact moment.

And then the soul of the last dragon came home to itself.

There are no words for it. I am a professional with words; it is the one skill they let me keep, nameless as I now am. But there are no words, so I will give you the closest ones I have: it was like sunrise happening underground. Like every bell in Thornfell rung at once, except instead of sound it rang in your ribs, in your teeth, in the place behind your sternum where you keep the things you'd die before saying. The vault's black glass lit up from within, and I saw that the walls weren't walls at all—they were strata, layers upon layers of contracts, thousands of them, ten thousand, each one a small cruelty sealed in glass—and the light touched them and they began to burn.

Not destroy. *Burn clean.* There's a difference. A contract burning badly leaves ash and grievance. These burned the way a fever breaks—violently, and then with an almost embarrassed gentleness, leaving nothing behind but the person who signed, suddenly lighter, suddenly wondering why their shoulders had hurt all these years.

"The court," Wren said, staring past me into the corridor. "Theo. Look at the court."

I looked.

The masked figures of smoke and bone—the archivists, the toll-keepers, the beautiful terrible clerks of everything owed—were coming apart. Not screaming. Unraveling. Each mask lifted from its face and turned to ash that rose rather than fell, drifting upward through corridors that were already forgetting how to be corridors. One of them, passing close to us, paused. Under the mask there was no face, only a kind of question mark made of shadow. It looked at me—or oriented itself toward me, I should say, because looking implies eyes—and inclined its head. Almost a bow.

Then it went, and took the question with it.

"They weren't evil," I heard myself say. "They were just... the contract's hands."

"Hands can be cut off," Wren said, "or they can put down what they're holding." She squeezed my shoulder. "Yours just put it down."

Above us, the whole mountain groaned. Somewhere far off, stone was deciding whether to be a ceiling or a memory. The freed fire poured upward through the Undervault's veins like water finding its level, like it had been waiting three centuries for permission to move, and everywhere it passed, debts dissolved. I felt them going. I don't know how else to describe it—a sensation like dozens of fingers unhooking from behind your collarbone, one after another, all the way up the dark.

The warden remained. Of course it did. Somebody has to watch the last candle go out.

"You could leave too," I said to it. "Whatever you are. Whatever's left of him."

The smoke-and-bone shape regarded the lantern, which was now less a lantern than a hearth, roaring quietly, gold and alive. "I have kept this post since before your kingdom had a name," it said. "I had forgotten there was anything to do besides keeping it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," it agreed. "But it is what I have." Then, softer, in a voice that for one syllable sounded like something vast and old and glad: "Tell the valley the debt is paid. Tell them he does not blame them for surviving on borrowed fire. Tell them—" and here the voice did break, just slightly, the way great things crack, along their oldest lines— "tell them thank you for remembering him at all."

Then it walked into the light, and the light accepted it, and the vault began to come down around our ears, and Wren grabbed my hand and we ran.

### Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about escaping a collapsing underworld: it's mostly stairs.

All that drama, all that fire, and at the end of it you're just two people taking steps very fast while the architecture files its resignation letter. Theo ran ahead of me through the shaking corridors of black glass, and I noticed—I will always remember noticing—that the glass was clearing as the fire passed through it. Contracts burning off the walls like fog off a morning field. And under the contracts, carvings. Old ones. Dragons. Hundreds of dragons, wing over wing, spiraling up the walls of the deep roads like the mountain itself had once been a nest.

"There used to be more of us," Theo panted, catching sight of them. "Of them. Them. More of them."

"Obviously," I said. "Someone built all this."

We burst out of the deep ways into the outer court, and that's where it got strange, even stranger than everything that had already happened, which frankly had set a high bar.

The court was unmaking itself in order. The great hall of smoke and bone, the tiers where the masks had sat in judgment, the ledgers—gods, the ledgers, shelves and shelves of them rising into darkness—all of it was folding inward like a book closing. But the masks, or what was left of them, were not fleeing. They were filing out. In lines. In *order*. Centuries of bureaucracy do not simply stop; apparently they adjourn.

One of them stopped in front of us. Smaller than the others. Its mask was cracked down the middle, and through the crack I could see—light. Just light, moving the way water moves.

"Toll refunded," it said, in a voice like a page turning.

And it reached out and touched my forehead.

I want to describe what came back, but here's the trouble: what came back wasn't a list. It wasn't *here is the name you paid at the Toll House, here are the three days you lost outside Thornfell, here is the battle where your sword got notched.* It was warmth. It was a kitchen, someone's kitchen, low beams and bread smell, and a woman laughing at something I'd said, and I knew her name for half a second—knew it the way you know a note of music, wholly and briefly—and then it folded itself away again, gently, like a letter returned to its envelope, and the mask withdrew its hand.

"Partial refund," it said. "The rest was spent. Spent coin stays spent." A pause. "Most of it bought you passage out, the first time. You asked it to."

"I did?"

"You were very polite about it," said the mask, and if a mask can sound amused, this one managed it. "Goodbye, knight-errant. You were the worst customer we ever had. You never once accepted the terms."

It bowed, and came apart into ash that smelled—this is true, I swear it on whatever I still own—of woodsmoke and cinnamon, like a hearth in winter, like somewhere warm at the end of a long road.

Then the floor tilted, Theo swore with remarkable fluency for a former scribe, and we ran.

The entrance—the cliff face that hadn't existed yesterday—was ahead of us, daylight beyond it, actual daylight, thin and gray and the single most beautiful thing I had seen in my life. Behind us the mountain roared, and the roar wasn't anger. I know anger; I've fought next to it and against it and been it. This was relief. This was a held breath released after three hundred years.

We came out onto the mountainside into falling snow and falling ash, and above us, above the peak of Thornfell itself, the sky opened.

I have seen dragon-fire exactly once before this, though I didn't remember it until later, and I'll get to that. What came out of the mountain now was not a weapon. Everything the stories say about dragonfire being wrath—wrong. All of it. What rose from Thornfell was a column of gold light wide as a cathedral and it went up and up and up until it punched through the cloud deck, and the snow around us stopped falling and hung in the air, suspended, lit from below like a sky full of slow stars. The ground hummed. My sword, on my back, sang—one clear ringing note, and I felt the notch in the blade smooth itself out like a scar fading.

And in the column of light, for a moment—wings. Vast, vast wings, spread the width of the valley, translucent as the membrane of a soap bubble, gold-shot. Not descending. Not staying. Just showing itself. *I was here. I am going. Look, once, before I go.*

The valley looked. Every soul in Thornfell must have looked; you couldn't not.

Then it folded upward like a hand closing around something precious, and was gone, and the light sank back into the mountain—not extinguished, *settled*, the way a fire settles into coals that will hold heat till morning—and the snow remembered gravity and came down soft around us, and Theo started laughing.

Not the bitter laugh I'd heard from them before, the scribe's laugh, the armor laugh. This one was helpless and wet-eyed and young. They sat down in the snow, right there on the mountainside, and laughed until it turned into crying, and I sat down next to them, because some things you don't stand for.

"We did it," they said finally, which was wrong, and I told them so.

"*You* did it. I hit some people, historically one of my best skills, but the actual work—" I shrugged. "That was yours. The hard kind."

"The kind you can't swing at," Theo said.

"Yeah. The kind I'm no good at." I bumped their shoulder with mine. "Lucky you had a sword along anyway."

"Luckiest thing I ever did," they said, and looked at me when they said it, and I found I had absolutely nothing wry to offer in return. So I just held their gaze back, snow gathering on both of us, ash settling gold in our hair, and let that be the whole conversation for a while.

Below us in the valley, I could see the beacon tower at Highmoor relighting—not by any human hand; the flame simply arrived, leaping up the tower like ivy growing in fast motion—and beyond it, faint on the horizon, other towers, other beacons, one after another catching, a chain of answered letters spelling itself south toward the capital.

Somewhere in that city, I'd have bet my sword, an inquisitor was watching his life's work burn beautifully to nothing.

### Ashgrave

I watched the beacon of the capital reignite from the window of my own office, which I grant you is a poor vantage point compared to a mountainside, but then I had never been a man for vistas. I preferred documents.

The flame came back at the turn of the ninth hour. I know because I was watching the hourglass when it happened, and the sand stopped mid-fall—hung there, golden, every grain lit from within—and then the window went bright and the hourglass finished falling all at once, and outside, the great beacon atop the Spire roared like a bonfire the size of a village, throwing shadows across the city at midnight.

My mandate, I reflected, was void.

This is not a sentence most men get to think clearly. Most men in my position would have been composing excuses already, drafting the report that would somehow make three hundred soldiers' march into a collapsing mountain, the loss of forty-two of them, and the escape of two fugitives read as a triumph. I had drafted such reports myself; I knew the craft. But sitting there with the dragon-light pouring through my window and pooling on my desk like spilled honey, I found I had no appetite for the craft.

Because here is what I learned in the Undervault, in the last hour before the world caught fire: I had read the original mandate. The crown's claim. *The soul of the last dragon, held in trust, renewable at the pleasure of the throne.* Held in trust. I had always taken *trust* to mean ownership dressed politely. Standing in that vault, watching a lantern the size of a loaf of bread contain a being older than my institution, older than the concept my institution enforced—it had occurred to me, briefly, coldly, the way a draft gets under a door, that perhaps the crown was never the owner. Perhaps it was only ever another debtor. Perhaps the entire edifice I served—wards, leases, beacons, the tidy machinery of inherited power—was a tenant's arrangement with a landlord everyone pretended had died.

And now the landlord had risen, forgiven the arrears, and moved out.

What do you enforce, then? Whose property do I seize? You cannot arrest sunlight. Believe me; I considered the paperwork.

A knock. Captain Ferren, snow on his shoulders, ash in his eyebrows, looking like a man who had walked out of a story and didn't expect anyone to believe him. He'd brought back thirty-eight soldiers of the forty-two. Good ratio, for an apocalypse.

"The mountain's open, sir," he said. "Or—closed. Both. The passages are sealing themselves. Smooth stone, like they were never there. Except the main door, on the north face. It stands open. No guard, no door, just... an entrance, and a smell like a forge and summer."

"Casualties?"

"None since the fire. Men who were hurt are—better, sir. Martel had an arrowhead in his thigh since Salt Pass. It's gone. Wound's gone. Scar's gone." Ferren shifted. "He wants to know if that's legal."

"Tell Martel," I said, "that the law and I are currently renegotiating our relationship, and he should enjoy his leg."

Ferren blinked. I imagine it was the longest joke I'd made in his presence, possibly ever.

"And the fugitives? Dunmore and the knight?"

"Gone ahead of us down-mountain. Villagers on the high road say they walked out of the snow laughing, sir. Laughing." He said it like a symptom.

"Let them go," I said.

"Sir?"

"You heard me. Draft it however you like. *Fugitives escaped in the chaos of the Undervault's collapse; pursuit deemed imprudent.* Imprudent is a good word. It suggests wisdom without requiring any." I turned back to the window. Below, the city was waking despite the hour, people pouring into the streets to look at the beacon, and I could hear them—cheering, some of them, and others crying, and others doing the thing crowds do when grace arrives uninvited, which is go quiet all at once and then make noise about ordinary things, bread and weather and children, because ordinary is the only vessel big enough.

"Inquisitor," Ferren said carefully, "with respect. Your career—"

"My career was built on a lease," I said, "and the lease is burned. Do you know what I find?" I watched the beacon throw gold across a thousand rooftops. "Lighter. Considerably lighter. I recommend it."

He left. I stayed at the window a long while. At some point I took out the copy of the soul-contract I'd carried for eleven years—the crown's certified facsimile, seals and ribbons, the document that had defined my office—and found it blank. Not burned. Blank. As if the words had simply gone home.

I fed it to my candle, which was redundant and therefore satisfying, and began the report.

### Maren

I was halfway down the north road when the mountain lit up, and I'll tell you straight: I sat down on a rock like an old woman and watched it, and I cried, and I don't cry, ask anyone, ask especially the people I've robbed.

The fire went up out of Thornfell like the answer to a question I'd been asking my whole life without knowing it. And I thought about the shepherd. Highmoor, forty years of his head traded away for one winter of his wife's breathing, walking us to that door because we promised to tell him what he'd paid. We hadn't gotten the chance. The vault came down, the court came apart, and somewhere in that collapse—

Well.

Turns out the court kept everything, right up until the end. And turns out that when a court burns its books clean, the contents don't vanish. They *scatter*. Ask any smuggler: when a warehouse burns, the goods don't stop existing. They just change hands.

I went back up. Don't ask me why. I'd sold Theo Dunmore's route to a crown inquisitor for amnesty, and I'd hated myself cleanly and efficiently ever since, the way you hate a scar: familiar, functional, yours. Going back up a collapsing holy mountain to dig through the ashes of hell's accounting office was either penance or madness, and in my experience the line between those is drawn in pencil.

I found it in the ruins of the outer court, under a fallen shelf of ledgers, still warm: a jar. Glass, sealed with wax that glowed faintly, and inside it—light. Not firelight. Memory-light, the color of late afternoon on a good day, and pressed against the glass from inside, shapes. A hillside. Sheep. A woman's hands kneading bread. Forty years of a man's mornings, sloshing gently, waiting to be delivered.

There was a scrap of paper tied to the neck of the jar in a handwriting I recognized—careful, cramped, scribe's script, the letters of someone who'd learned to write small so the words would fit in narrow margins and narrow lives:

*Maren — You went back. That counts for something, wherever you keep score. Take this to the Debtor of Highmoor. Tell him everything. He asked, and someone should honor it. — T.D.*

"Pillock," I said, meaning it fondly, which was new for me.

I tucked the jar inside my coat, against my chest, where it warmed like a second heartbeat, and I started down the mountain toward Highmoor, and the snow fell soft, and the beacons burned all down the valley like a string of lit lamps leading anywhere I pleased.

For the first time in twenty years of smuggling, I was carrying something worth more than everything I'd ever stolen, and it weighed almost nothing, and I caught myself humming.

Gods help me. Grace is contagious.

### Theo

We made camp that night in the mouth of the open door, because it was dry, and because neither of us could quite bear to leave yet. The mountain hummed around us, low and content, a hive after the swarm has settled. Through the doorway we could see the deep halls glowing faintly gold, cooling coals, empty of masks, empty of ledgers. Once, in the night, I woke and saw light moving far down in the dark—a slow procession, unhurried, going deeper. Going home, maybe. Or out. Some doors only open from one side.

Wren slept with her sword across her knees out of habit, and her head tipped against my shoulder out of choice, and I sat there between the two and did the arithmetic I'd been doing since I was nine years old, and for the first time in my life the ledger balanced at zero.

No debt. No name on file. No signature anywhere in the world binding me to anything I hadn't chosen.

It should have terrified me. A week ago it would have; I'd have lain awake cataloguing everything a person without papers can't do. Instead I found myself thinking about the smuggler maps I used to draw, and how the best routes were never the marked ones. How the real roads were the ones you found by walking.

In the morning we would go down. There was Cassian to find, or to mourn, and an Order to face, and a family debt that still existed in the mundane world of ink and coin no matter what had burned beneath the mountain. There was a shepherd owed the truth of forty years. There was a life to build out of no materials whatsoever, which struck me as the most interesting commission I'd ever received.

But that was morning's business. For now the snow fell gold-lit past the doorway, the beacon-fires burned down the length of the valley like a blessing being passed hand to hand, and somewhere overhead—too high to see, close enough to feel—a vast wingbeat of warm wind crossed the mountain, once, like a hand resting briefly on the roof of the world before lifting away.

Wren stirred against my shoulder without waking, and murmured my name.

Which was funny, really.

Because I didn't have one anymore—and it fit better than anything ever had.
