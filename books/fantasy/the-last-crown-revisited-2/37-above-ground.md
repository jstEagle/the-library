## 37. Above Ground

**WREN**

I woke up on grass.

Actual grass, with actual dew soaking through my cloak, and a sky overhead so aggressively blue it felt like an insult after weeks of black glass corridors and whispering contracts. The sun sat low and orange in the west. Somewhere nearby, water ran over stones. A bird was making an idiot of itself in a hawthorn bush.

For a long moment I did nothing but breathe and catalogue what I had. Sword: present, notched, still mine. Boots: present, one sole flapping. Memories of the last three days: present, mostly. Memories of everything before that:

That was the interesting part.

The Undervault had burned clean when the dragon's soul tore free of its lantern. Every debt it held went up like dry kindling — I'd felt it go through me, a heat that wasn't pain exactly, more like someone finally setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying. And in that fire, some of what the mountain had eaten out of me had come back. Not all. Fragments. My mother's kitchen, the smell of onions and tallow. The day I took my knight's oath and the way my knees had shook under the ceremonial armor. Maren Voss laughing at me across a smugglers' fire, years before she ever betrayed anyone.

And Theo.

Not fragments of Theo. All of it, somehow, or enough of it: the ambush on the road, the stalemate, the grudging truce, the pass at Salt and Cinder where they'd nearly gone off the edge and I'd caught their wrist and we'd said things to each other that people usually take years to say. The vault. The cell. Running back into a collapsing court for someone I'd known two weeks, because the alternative was walking away from the only person who'd ever looked at the wreckage of my memory and hadn't flinched.

I turned my head. Theo was lying three feet away on their back, hands folded on their chest like an effigy, eyes open and fixed on the sky.

"You're alive," I said. My voice came out scraped.

"I'm not sure that's technically true," Theo said.

I sat up too fast and my head objected. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Theo said, in the careful voice of someone who has rehearsed a sentence until all the feeling has been drilled out of it, "that when the court unmade my legal self, it apparently meant it. No name in any roll. No record. As far as the kingdom is concerned, there was never a T. Dunmore at all. The debt ledger at the Order of the Quiet Flame lists an indentured scribe who—" they paused— "who doesn't exist. Which raises fascinating questions about who owes forty-one years of copying scripture."

"Theo."

"Because that's rather the point, isn't it? If I don't exist, then either the debt doesn't exist—" they stopped. I watched them arrive at the same place I'd arrived somewhere around the third breath of consciousness. "Or I owe it anyway, and there's no me attached to owe it with."

"Which?"

"I don't know." They laughed, short and cracked. "Gods, I don't know. I've spent my whole life being a name on a ledger, Wren. It turns out when you scratch the name out, you're not free. You're just illegible."

I lay back down next to them. The bird kept up its racket. Below us, past a fold of hillside, I could see the valley road and, far off, the grey smudge of Thornfell town, and beyond it the mountain with its new scar — a seam of glassy black rock running down the southern face where the court had been, cooling in the evening air. Travelers would come to look at it. Pilgrims, probably. Someone would build a shrine. Shrines grow on scars like moss.

"How much did you get back?" Theo asked quietly.

I considered lying. Then I considered how tired I was, and how lying to Theo had never once worked anyway.

"Some. Enough. My mother's kitchen. My oath. You." I said that last part flatly, because if I gave it any weight I'd have to deal with the weight. "There are gaps. Big ones. I know I was a knight for eleven years and I can name maybe four of them. There's a whole person I used to be and I've got her scattered like dropped coins."

"And the Archivist offered—"

"The Archivist is ash now. So's the offer." I watched the sky instead of looking at them. "But even if it weren't. Even if I could walk back down there and buy the rest of myself back, piece by piece, memory by memory." I rolled onto my side to face them. "I don't think I want to."

Theo propped themselves up on an elbow. "You don't want your own life back?"

"I want to tell you something my mother used to say, except I only got her kitchen back last night, so I'm telling you something my mother used to say as of approximately twelve hours ago. She said: a soup tastes like what you put in it, not what the recipe says." I gestured vaguely at my own head. "I've been built out of what's left, these past weeks. The stubborn bits. The bits that chose things. And those choices made a person who ran back into a burning court for a scribe who lies badly about courier deaths and maps smuggler routes out of sheer bloody spite." I shrugged. "If I go collect the rest, I get the whole woman back, and she might be someone else. She probably is someone else. Eleven years is a lot of woman."

"And you'd rather keep the stranger you built."

"I'd rather keep the woman who chose you," I said, because apparently I was doing honesty today, all at once, like ripping off a boot with the laces tied. "She's not much. She's got holes in her like a moth-eaten cloak. But every stitch in her is mine."

Theo was quiet for a while. The light went gold, then copper.

"Every stitch is yours," they repeated. "That's more than most people can say. Most people are stitched entirely out of other people's records. Birth rolls. Debt ledgers. Contracts signed by nine-year-olds." They touched their own chest briefly, where the contract-scar had been, though the skin there was smooth now — I'd checked, in the vault, in the firelight, when I thought they were sleeping. Forgiven debts leave nothing. That was rather the horror and the miracle of it.

"Do you want yours back?" I asked. "Your childhood. Before you sold—" I stopped. Reframed. "Before the bargain. Do you remember your mother now? Properly?"

"Yes," Theo said. "All of it. The fire gave it back along with everything else. She sang while she cooked. Badly. She let me win at cards and thought I didn't notice." Their voice stayed level but their eyes didn't. "I remember being nine. I remember the masked visitor. I remember signing." A breath. "And I remember forgiving him. Me. The boy. Standing in front of the lantern and telling him out loud that I forgave the debt, that he'd been a child trying to save his mother, that no court in heaven or under a mountain could make that a sin." They smiled, and it was a real smile, worn thin. "It worked. That's the mad thing. It actually worked."

"So what do you do now?" I asked. "Illegible man. What does freedom look like from the inside?"

"I genuinely have no idea," Theo said, with such cheerfulness that I laughed, and then they laughed, and then we were both lying in wet grass on a hillside above Thornfell laughing like idiots, which I submit is as good a sign as any that we had both been fundamentally altered by recent events.

* * *

**THEO**

We walked down into the valley at dusk, because neither of us wanted to spend another night on the mountain, even a mountain that had recently undergone what I can only describe as structural forgiveness.

The village at the foot of the pass — the one where everyone had paid the memory-toll, where they smiled too much and asked us the same questions in the same order — was changed. Not healed, exactly. When the debts burned, the memories the court held should have burned clean back to their owners, the way Wren's had. But a memory kept in a jar for thirty years comes home musty. The villagers remembered themselves the way you remember a dream: the shape of it, the feel of it, missing pieces here and there. The old woman who had once mistaken me for someone she'd sold grabbed my sleeve in the lane and squinted up at me.

"You're not him," she said. "But you knew him, didn't you. You've got the look of a debt about you. Had the look. Past tense now." She patted my hand with fingers like birch twigs. "Past tense is a lovely tense. Nobody told me. Thirty years I couldn't use it."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody tells you."

They fed us, because villages like that feed strangers; it may be the last instinct the toll leaves intact. Stew, bread, a cheese that tasted mainly of the cellar it had been forgotten in. We ate by their fire while they compared recovered memories aloud like people trading market gossip — *do you remember the miller's dog, the one with the crooked ear? — I remember a wedding, whose wedding was it? — does anyone remember winter?* — and I found I couldn't eat much. Every recovered memory in that room had passed through the same fire that had given me back my mother singing off-key. We were all of us patched now, this whole valley, holding ourselves together with whatever came home.

"They're happy," Wren murmured to me, watching the old woman laugh at something.

"They're patched," I said.

"Patched is a kind of happy."

Later, in the loft where they'd bedded us down on straw that smelled honestly of straw and nothing worse, Wren lay awake beside me and I felt her working up to something. I waited. Waiting for Wren to finish a thought is like waiting for a siege to conclude; you might as well get comfortable.

"If you're legally nobody," she said eventually, to the rafters, "can you legally inherit anything? Earn anything? Marry anyone?"

"I suspect I can do all of those things," I said, "in the sense that no clerk anywhere will be able to stop me, because no clerk anywhere will be able to find me. The kingdom's whole machinery runs on names, Wren. Take away the name and the gears spin on air. I could commit any crime. I could also be robbed, cheated, and murdered with perfect impunity, since there'd be no one to press charges."

"So what you're describing isn't freedom. It's just a bigger smuggling route."

I turned my head to look at her. Firelight came up through the floorboards in thin stripes across her face. "You say the most romantic things."

"I mean it seriously."

"I know you do. That's why I married— " I stopped. We had not. We had absolutely not. I lay there in the dark and listened to my own heartbeat and decided that some sentences, once started, deserve to be finished by braver men. "Ask me again in a year," I said instead. "When I've figured out what I am. Illegibility takes practice."

Wren was silent long enough that I thought she'd slept. Then, softly: "In a year I'll ask you. Write it down somewhere. Oh wait."

"You're unbearable."

"I'm *patched*," she said, "which is a kind of unbearable," and she rolled over, and I lay listening to her breathing even out, and I thought about names.

Here is what I knew about names, having recently lost mine to a fire and gained it back from a woman shouting it across a collapsing court: a name given to you is a handle. It's how institutions pick you up and move you where they want you. Ledger-name, oath-name, debt-name. But a name spoken as a gift — Wren had said *Theo* like she was handing me something warm with both hands — that's different. That doesn't hold you. That reminds you that you're holdable.

I fell asleep planning, which is what I do instead of dreaming.

* * *

**WREN**

Morning came up grey and clean-smelling. Theo was already awake, sitting cross-legged in the corner of the loft, writing.

Not mapping. I'd seen Theo map — the quick economy of it, lines laid down like traps. This was slower. Words, in a small neat hand, on a page torn from the back of the bloodstained map that had started all of it. Fitting. The map had earned its retirement; might as well die as stationery.

"What are you writing?"

"A letter," Theo said, without looking up. "To Brother Cassian."

I went very still. Cassian — the mentor who'd covered for them, whose letters had ended mid-sentence, who'd known about the childhood bargain all along and had spent years standing between Theo and the Order's archive division like a wall that loved someone. We hadn't found a body in the Undervault. We hadn't found anything of the Order down there at all, which was its own kind of answer, and not a good one.

"He deserves to hear it from me," Theo said. "Whatever happened to him. He deserves to know the debt is closed. That it worked." The pen scratched. Paused. "And the Order keeps records, Wren. Even of dead men. Especially of dead men. If Cassian is in their rolls, then somewhere in that scriptorium there's a page with his name on it, and pages with names on them can be *read.*"

"You're going back." I said it flatly, because there was no point dressing it up. "Back to the temple. To Thornfell. Where the Order still wants you for theft, desertion of indenture, and consorting with smugglers, and where half of them saw your face."

"They saw the face of a scribe named T. Dunmore," Theo said mildly. "And I've recently established that no such person exists. I'm curious to see how they manage to arrest a clerical error." They set down the pen and looked at me, and under all the wryness there was something rawer. "He covered for me for years and I never knew. He paid — Ashgrave took him, or the Order did, and either way he paid in my place, and I never got to say thank you or sorry or any of the hundred things. If there's a ledger entry for him anywhere, I want to read it. If there's a grave, I want to stand on it and be rude about the weather. If he's alive—" their voice did something complicated— "then someone owes him an apology, and I'm the only person in the world qualified, since I'm apparently the only person in the world who doesn't exist and therefore can't be punished for trespass."

I got up, joints announcing themselves, and sat down next to him — them, I corrected myself, them, though privately I'd noticed that grief made me think of Theo as *him*, small and nine and signing things in candlelight — and took the pen out of their hand.

"You're not going alone."

"Wren—"

"You walked into a collapsing court and I came back for you, and you think I'm letting you walk into a temple full of inquisitors by yourself? I have a sword and a notched history and absolutely nothing better to do. Besides." I tapped the letter. "I want to meet whoever taught you to lie that badly. I feel he shaped you, and I have grievances."

Something loosened in Theo's shoulders. "He'd like you," they said. "He always liked people who were terrible for me."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said about me in eleven years, including the six I can't remember."

We packed in silence after that. There wasn't much to pack. On the way out of the village the old woman pressed a heel of cheese into Theo's hands and told them, once more, that they had the look of a debt about them, past tense, and Theo thanked her gravely as though she'd bestowed a knighthood.

At the crossroads below the village, the road forked. Left ran down toward the coast and a life with no records in it. Right climbed back up toward Thornfell, its temple, its bells, its ledgers full of ghosts.

Theo stood at the fork a long moment. Then they took out the letter, sealed it with a dab of pine pitch because we owned no wax, and tucked it inside their coat, against the heart, where people keep things they're afraid of losing.

"Right, then," they said. "Let's go close a ledger."

And we turned toward the mountain, and began to climb.
