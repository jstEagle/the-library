## 39. A Road With No Toll

**Theo**

The smuggler roads in autumn smell like wet stone and woodsmoke and, if you know where to look, like the particular sweetness of wild honey left too long on the comb. I know where to look. I spent eleven years learning every fold of these mountains from behind a desk, copying other people's prayers while drawing my own heresies in the margins.

Now I walk them for free.

That's the strangest part, if I'm honest—not the freedom itself but how it feels, which is mostly like walking. I had imagined freedom would announce itself. Trumpets. A weight lifting off my shoulders like a yoke being drawn away by gentle hands. Instead it turns out that freedom is just Tuesday, and your boots are damp, and the person beside you is complaining about the state of her boots, which are also damp, because you both slept in a hollow that turned out to be a stream's old bed.

"You said it was dry," Wren says, holding one boot upside down over the fire. It makes a sound like a small drowned animal being wrung out.

"I said *it looked* dry."

"It *looked* dry. Wonderful. I'll carve that on my grave. Here lies Wren of—of wherever I'm from. She trusted a scribe's eye and died of damp feet."

"Wren of Thornfell," I say. "You're from Thornfell. We established this in Salt and Cinder Pass, remember? You remembered your mother's kitchen. The blue door. The way she hummed when she kneaded bread."

Wren is quiet for a moment, turning the boot over. "The humming," she says. "I've been trying to get the tune back all week. It keeps sliding into a temple hymn instead, which is blasphemy of some kind, surely. Your fault, probably."

"Everything's my fault. It's on the paperwork."

There isn't any paperwork anymore. That's rather the point.

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what nobody tells you about getting pieces of yourself back: they don't fit the same. My memories returned in fragments after the mountain burned clean—the dragon-fire went through the Undervault like a bell through glass, and everything the court had swallowed came back up, changed by the journey. I have my mother's kitchen now. I have the blue door. I have a name I don't use, a father whose face arrives only in profile, and the memory of kneeling before the last dragon with my sword drawn, refusing an order from a man whose voice I still can't place.

I could buy the rest. That's the thing Theo doesn't know I know—or maybe they do know, because Theo knows everything eventually, it's their whole condition as a person. There are archivists in the capital who deal in restored memory now, honest ones, post-collapse, charging honest coin. I have coin. I could walk in with questions and walk out whole.

But here's what else I have: two weeks of road with someone who laughs at their own terrible jokes before finishing them. Two weeks of Theo standing between me and a toll-keeper's price, of them mapping nothing, owing nothing, drawing pictures in the dirt at night just because the dirt was there. If I take back the missing years, I become someone who has known them longer. But she'd be a stranger wearing my face. The woman who chose Theo in a collapsing court of smoke and bone—that one I intend to keep intact.

Besides, the not-remembering has its uses. When Theo tells the story of how they refused to give their name to an entire court of contractual nightmares, I get to hear it fresh every time. Nobody alive can say that.

"Theo," I call across the fire. "Tell me again about the part where Ashgrave's face did the thing."

"He's told it four times today," says Maren Voss, who is sitting with us against every law of nature and at least two laws of men, "and it gets worse every time. His Ashgrave impression sounds like a constipated owl."

"I do not do an impression," Theo says with dignity, poking the fire. "I offer a faithful reconstruction."

"Do the owl," Maren says.

He does the owl.

I laugh until my ribs hurt, and I think: whatever the archivists have locked in their drawers, none of it is worth more than this exact minute. Then I throw an acorn at Theo's head, because affection should keep a person humble.

* * *

**Theo**

Maren stayed three days, which was two days longer than anyone expected and roughly two days longer than Maren wanted anyone to believe she'd wanted. She'd come up the western road with a sealed jar wrapped in wool riding her pack like a sleeping infant, and she set it down in front of the shepherd of Highmoor without ceremony, without apology, and without once mentioning that she'd nearly died getting it.

Tam of Highmoor held forty years of his life in both hands—a jar of grey-gold light that moved like slow water—and he asked us, as we'd promised, to tell him what he'd paid.

We told him. All of it. His wife's last winter, bought back hour by hour; the forty years of himself he'd surrendered so that she wouldn't die alone; the village he'd forgotten raising him, the daughter whose name he'd traded away without ever knowing he'd had a daughter. He listened the way stone listens to rain. Then he opened the jar, and the light went into him like dawn going into a valley, and he sat down hard in the grass and wept for a man named Tam whom he had never met, and then stood up and was him.

His daughter lives two valleys over. He's gone to find her. I gave him directions off no map at all—I just knew the roads, by then, the way you know a song.

As for Maren: she drank our tea, insulted our tea, corrected our route twice, taught Wren three genuinely useful knife tricks and one trick designed purely to maim, and left before sunrise on the fourth day without saying goodbye, which from Maren is a sonnet. She did leave something. A small pouch of coins, tucked under Wren's sword belt where she thought we wouldn't find it until she was miles gone, with a scrap of paper inside in her blunt courier's hand:

*For the road. Don't map anything. You were better at it when you stopped.*

I've decided to be moved by this rather than alarmed that she watched us sleep.

* * *

**Wren**

We're heading north, or we were heading north, or possibly we're heading wherever the weather argues us into. That's the other thing about owing nothing: there's no reason to arrive anywhere. Theo finds this terrifying approximately once a day and wonderful approximately twice a day, and I have made it my personal project to keep the ratio tilted toward wonderful.

Today the weather argued us east, up onto the high moor above Thornfell, because Theo claimed—eyes doing that thing they do, that lit-from-inside thing they'll deny doing—"the light will be extraordinary up there, and also I want to see the temple from far enough away that it's small."

So we climb. The heather is going rust and gold. The wind up here has teeth but honest ones. And below us, by late afternoon, all of Thornfell spreads out like something a careful child built: the town, the terraced fields, the grey bulk of the Order's temple with its thin thread of evening smoke, and beyond it the mountains going blue into blue.

Theo stands at the edge of the hill with their hands in their pockets and looks down at the temple for a long time.

"They'll have burned Cassian's letters," they say at last. "Or archived them. With the Order those amount to the same thing." A pause. "I settled the debt, you know. Before we left. Every copper of the family obligation, paid out of the crown's grudging reward money. The ledger clerk kept checking my figures like I'd made an error in his favor. Eleven years of my life, closed out in an afternoon of arithmetic."

"How did it feel?"

"Like handing back a library book," Theo says. "Enormous, and administrative." They glance at me sideways, and there's the wry twist of mouth that I have, in two weeks, catalogued more thoroughly than I ever catalogued troop movements. "I didn't take the oath again. Brother Edran offered. Very kindly. I said no, and the world continued to exist, which I'm told is the usual result of saying no to the Quiet Flame, though you'd never guess it from inside."

"What will you do now?" I ask. "Truly. No ledger, no order, no name the courts can hold you to. Most people build entire lives out of less scaffolding than you've got."

Theo is quiet long enough that the wind fills the space. Then: "I keep thinking about the shepherd. About how he asked us to tell him what he paid. Not to give it back—just to tell him. Witnessing, Wren. That's the trade I'm good at now. Somebody has to write down what the mountain took, from everyone it ever took from. Not contracts. Stories. The difference matters." They kick at a tussock of heather, embarrassed by their own earnestness, which is the most Theo thing I have ever seen, including the time they argued with a ghost. "Also I want to learn to cook something that isn't porridge. Ambitious, I know. One disaster at a time."

"And I'll teach you knife tricks," I say. "The non-maiming ones. Probably."

"Probably," Theo agrees solemnly, "is my favorite word."

* * *

**Theo**

We camp on the hill because neither of us suggests going down, and not suggesting is its own kind of vow. The stars come out the way they only do above the tree line—shameless, crowded, showing off—and Wren falls asleep mid-sentence, which she does most nights, usually mid-boast, and I sit up a while longer because I am who I am and always will be, nameless or otherwise: a person who watches, and notices, and keeps things.

I notice this:

The beacon in the capital burns bright again—we saw it from the pass two weeks ago, a steady gold star on the southern horizon where for a generation there'd been a guttering cough of light. Crops came back. The wards along the border roads hold. Children in Thornfell are born this year without the grey tremor that touched the last three generations, and the temple has quietly reclassified several centuries of scripture as metaphor, which must have caused convulsions in the archive division, and I would pay real money to watch.

I notice that Wren sleeps with her sword close and her hand loose, and that even asleep she has stopped flinching at nothing, which started happening around the third day out of the mountain and which I have chosen to interpret as a soul deciding it's allowed to rest.

And I notice—because I am watching, and noticing, and keeping—that the moon is not quite full, and that something crosses it.

At first I take it for a bird. Then the shape blocks the stars behind it, and keeps blocking them, and keeps on, a vast slow darkness moving against the wheel of the sky, and my mind, trained for eleven years to categorize and file, offers up *cloud* and then withdraws the suggestion, embarrassed.

It passes over the sun-warmed memory of the day like a hand passing over a page. Wings. A shape like vast wings, wider than the temple, wider than the town below us, unhurried, silent, riding a wind too high and too cold for anything that breathes.

The lantern-fire. The soul I carried out of the dark by refusing to carry it. It went into the world in a blaze that unmade a court and cleaned every debt the mountain held—and apparently it did not go *up* into legend, the way stories go. It went somewhere with weather in it.

My heart is doing something complicated. Part terror, part the specific joy I used to feel finding an unmapped pass—*nobody knows this is here but me*—except inverted, multiplied: *nobody knows this is possible but us.*

"Wren," I say.

She's awake instantly—two weeks of road have taught her body my voice the way it once knew bugle calls. She follows my pointing hand, and I feel her go still beside me, the particular stillness of a soldier seeing something she once drew steel against.

Neither of us speaks for a long moment. Below us Thornfell glitters, small and stubborn and alive. Above us the shape banks once—there is no other word for it, it *banks*, deliberate, almost graceful—and then climbs, and thins, and is gone into the ordinary night, leaving the stars looking slightly rumpled, if stars can look rumpled, and I maintain that tonight they can.

"Well," Wren says finally, her voice rough. "That's going to need a new chapter."

"It's going to need several," I say. "Possibly forty."

She looks at me. In the firelight her face is half shadow and wholly hers, every piece of it chosen, and she smiles the smile she saves for when the world confirms we're right to keep walking through it.

"Then you'd better start writing," she says.

And I do. Right there, on the hill above Thornfell, with damp boots drying by the fire and no debts anywhere in any ledger under any sky, I open the notebook I no longer need and begin at the beginning, which is, of course, a candle, and a ledger, and a boy who wanted more.

The road goes on. It has no toll. We ride it anyway.
