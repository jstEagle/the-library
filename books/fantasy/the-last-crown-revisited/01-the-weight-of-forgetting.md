## 1. The Weight of Forgetting

The widow Maren Ostler smelled of tallow and dried lavender, and her hands would not stop moving.

Orsen watched them from the doorway of her kitchen—the fingers twisting a dishrag into a rope, unwinding it, twisting it again—as if she could wring the memory out through her skin before Orsen even touched her. The kettle on the stove had gone quiet ten minutes ago. Neither of them had moved to pour the tea.

"You're sure," Orsen said. It was not a question so much as a door left open. They always left it open. It cost nothing to ask, and it made the taking cleaner somehow—like a lock that turned willingly.

"He was in the chair." Maren's voice came out flat, rehearsed. She had been saying it to herself for three weeks; Orsen could hear the groove worn into the words. "By the window. He liked the morning light on his hands. And I went to the market, because we needed flour, and when I came back he was still in the chair but there was no—" The rag twisted tighter. "There was no him in it anymore."

"I know what death looks like," Orsen said, gentler than they felt. "You don't have to tell me the shape of it. I only need your permission."

She looked up then, and her eyes were the particular red-rimmed gray of someone who had cried past crying. "It hurts too much to keep. Everyone says it gets softer with time, but no one tells you how long time takes." Her chin lifted a fraction, the pride of a woman who had buried a husband and a son and still kept her kitchen floor scrubbed pale. "I want it gone. All of it. The forty-one years, not just the— not just the end. Can you do that?"

Orsen could. That was the trouble with Hollowmere: everyone assumed the trade was like pulling a tooth, one sharp thing out and the ache gone with it. Nobody understood that memories were load-bearing. Take the grief out of forty-one years and you took the love too, and what remained was a woman with a tidy house and an unexplained hollow where a man used to be.

They had explained this before, early in the trade, back when Orsen still believed explaining mattered. Clients had wept and thanked them anyway. Grief is a stone in the shoe, they'd learned. People will pay good coin to walk without it, and they will not thank you for pointing out that the limp taught them something about their own feet.

"Forty-one years is deep work," Orsen said instead. "It'll leave a mark. You'll know something's missing even if you can't name it. Some people find that worse than the hurting."

"And some find it better." Maren set down the rag at last. Her hands were still. "Which are you?"

"Neither. I'm the one who does the taking." Orsen crossed the small kitchen and knelt by her chair so their eyes were level. Up close she smelled less of lavender and more of the sick-sweet scent of a house where no one had cooked a real meal in weeks. "Hold my hands. Breathe slow. When you feel the pull, don't fight it and don't help it. Let it come like a tide going out."

She held out her hands. They were rough as bark and trembling only slightly now, which meant she had already done the hardest part—the deciding—in the weeks since she'd sent her neighbor's boy running across the valley with word that the memory-thief should come.

Orsen closed their eyes and reached.

The extraction was not magic. That was the first lie everyone told about it, and the second lie was that it was easy. What Orsen did lived somewhere between craft and theft, the way a pickpocket's fingers live somewhere between dexterity and sin. Memory in Hollowmere had a texture—you could feel it once you'd been shown how, and Orsen's grandmother had shown them at nine years old, pressing their small hands to her temples and saying *everything that happens leaves a weight, little bird, and some weights can be lifted*. Grief had a specific grain to it, dense and fibrous like heartwood, and Maren Ostler's grief was old-growth: forty-one years of a man named Doss who whistled off-key and burned the porridge every Sunday and had died in his chair with the light on his hands while his wife bought flour.

Orsen followed the grain down.

It was like sinking into warm water that remembered being a river. Around them—or around Orsen's attention, which was the closest way to say it—Maren's life unspooled: a girl of nineteen laughing under an apple tree, Doss young and skinny with sawdust in his hair; a wedding ring sized twice because her knuckles swelled; a son, Tomas-adjacent, no—a boy called Pell who grew tall and left for the lowlands and wrote letters that got shorter each year until they stopped. The grief was braided through all of it, silver thread in grey wool, and Orsen took hold of the braid's end and pulled.

Maren gasped. Her grip tightened on Orsen's fingers hard enough to grind bone.

"Don't fight it," Orsen murmured, though the words were half-lost in the roar of the tide. This was the part they hated—not the taking but the moment of parting, when the client's whole body understood, before their mind caught up, that something irreplaceable was leaving. Maren's face ran with sudden sweat. Her mouth opened around a sound that might have been his name.

Then it was out.

Orsen sat back on their heels, palms damp, and cradled the extracted weight the way you'd cradle a hot coal wrapped in cloth. To anyone watching, nothing showed—no glow, no shimmer, none of the theatrics the lowland peddlers claimed for their cheaper tricks. But inside Orsen's chest, forty-one years pressed outward against their ribs: apple blossoms, porridge smoke, a whistle gone flat on the high notes. A man dying in a chair with morning light on his hands, seen from a doorway, from a market street, from the far side of closed eyes.

They carried it to the door of Maren's cellar, where they kept the empty flask they'd brought for exactly this purpose. Extraction needed a vessel or it clung—and clinging memories were how you ended up like old Hedda Croft, who'd had three husbands' deaths drawn out of her and still woke screaming, because the thief who'd worked on her hadn't known what he was doing. Orsen knew. Orsen's grandmother had made sure of it, drilling them through a hundred practice extractions on goats and geese and finally, reluctantly, on herself.

*Never carry more than you must*, she'd said. *A memory kept is a debt owed.*

The stopper went in with a soft glass click. In the flask's dark interior, the memory settled like sediment—though Orsen, holding it, felt it lean toward them the way water leans downhill. Forty-one years, wanting to be lived again by somebody. Anybody.

That was the other thing nobody talked about. Memories didn't die when they were taken out. They waited.

* * *

Payment was seven silver marks and a jar of plum preserves, counted out onto the kitchen table with hands that were steady now, eerily calm, already smoothing toward the smoothness of a woman whose worst day had been amputated.

"It won't take full hold for a day or two," Orsen said, pocketing the marks. The preserves they left; they never took food from clients, though they couldn't have said why. Principle seemed too grand a word for it. Habit, maybe. "You'll sleep heavy tonight. If anyone asks about your husband, you'll know the facts of him but they'll sit strange, like a story you heard rather than a life you lived. Don't let them convince you that's wrong. It isn't wrong. It's just what you asked for."

Maren nodded along, and then, at the door, she caught Orsen's sleeve.

"The boy who fetched you—Nils. He said you were the one who did old Croft, and the Ferrow family after the fever year." Her eyes searched Orsen's face with a desperate arithmetic, tallying the town's sorrows against its one quiet stranger. "Half of Hollowmere has been to you, haven't they? Half the town is walking around with holes in them, and nobody says it aloud."

"Hollowmere keeps its own counsel," Orsen said. "That's why people come to me instead of the lowland chirurgeons. Here, forgetting is private."

"And what do you keep?" The question came out of her sideways, sly with the strange clarity of the freshly emptied. "All those years you've taken out of us. Where do they go?"

Orsen opened the door. Evening was coming down over the valley in the usual order—shadow climbing the eastern ridge first, then the rooftops, then the lane—thirteenth-month light, thin and early. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"They go where all payments go," Orsen said. "Into keeping."

* * *

The walk home took twenty minutes along the millrace path, and Orsen spent all twenty doing what they always did after a extraction: inventorying themselves, checking the seams. This was discipline, not superstition. Every thief who handles stolen goods eventually finds some of the goods handling them back. Grandmother Zhou had a rhyme for it—*count your pockets, count your skin, know what's yours and what crept in*—and Orsen recited it now under their breath like a prayer they'd long stopped believing in but couldn't shake.

Skin: theirs. Hands: theirs, still humming faintly with Maren's grief, which would fade by morning. Pockets: seven silver marks, the flask of forty-one years, a stub of pencil, half a wheel of cheese wrapped in cloth that belonged, strictly speaking, to no one since its owner had forgotten buying it.

Home was a squat stone house at the valley's edge, close enough to the ward-line that the air tasted faintly of iron on damp nights. Orsen had inherited it along with the trade and the warnings, and like everything else inherited, it fit badly and was loved anyway. Inside, they hung their coat, fed the banked stove, and went down the ladder to the cellar.

The cellar was where the keeping happened.

Shelves lined the walls, floor to ceiling, and on the shelves stood flasks—hundreds of them, glass catching the lantern-light like a congregation of dull stars. Each one labeled in Orsen's careful hand: *Ostler, D., 41 yrs, grief*. *Ferrow, the three daughters, fever-year*. *Hedda C., remainder*. *Vess, Alderman, one afternoon, unspecified shame*—that last one paid double, because Vess had refused to say what he wanted forgotten and Orsen had charged accordingly for the mystery.

This was the second lie of the trade, the one Orsen told only to themselves: that the memories were stored, inert, waiting for a client who might someday want to buy one back. Nobody ever bought them back. Not once in eleven years. People came to forget, and having forgotten, they stayed forgotten, because a hole in you heals over smoother than a scar.

So the cellar was not storage. Orsen knew that. It was a hoard. And a hoarder who steals grief for coin and keeps it in the dark is either an archivist or a dragon, and dragons, whatever else the stories said, were never comfortable.

They set Maren's flask on the newest shelf, between the fever-year sisters and a small unlabeled vial that Orsen touched once, briefly, before turning away. The unlabeled vial was the rule-breaker, the one thing they'd kept out of their own head instead of another's: a single hour from childhood, age six, a kitchen that smelled of cardamom, a woman's voice singing low. Grandmother Zhou had ordered it removed the winter before she died. *Too heavy for you, little bird. Keep it in glass where you can see it but it can't see you.* Orsen had obeyed, because they always obeyed her, right up until the day obedience stopped mattering.

They did not uncork it. They never uncorked it. But some nights—most nights—they stood in front of it a while, the way Maren had stood outside her husband's chair.

* * *

Sleep came late and shallow, the way it did after deep extractions. Orsen dreamed in borrowed fragments: a whistle going flat, porridge smoking, morning light lying across still hands. Not theirs, any of it. They woke before dawn with the taste of someone else's loss in their mouth and lay staring at the ceiling, counting the sounds of Hollowmere waking—the millwheel's first groan, cart wheels on the frost-stiff lane, the bell at the meetinghouse that rang thirteen times on the thirteenth of every month, an old custom nobody remembered starting.

Thirteen times. The bell finished, and Orsen lay listening to the silence after, and found it wrong.

Not wrong exactly. Thin. Like a note held a beat too long by someone who'd lost the rhythm.

They got up, dressed, and climbed the hill behind the house before breakfast, because unease in Orsen had always translated directly into motion. The hill was called Wardrise, and it earned the name: from its crown you could see, on clear days, the whole circuit of the valley's guardians. Thirteen ward-stones, each taller than a man, standing sentinel in a rough ring miles apart, older than the town, older than the war, placed—so the story went—by the peacemakers at the war's end to fold Hollowmere out of the world's sight. No mage could scry past them. No army had ever found the road in. For ninety years the valley had slept behind its ring of stones like a purse sewn shut, and the people inside had gotten rich on the one commodity the outside world could never tax: the freedom to forget.

Grandmother Zhou had walked this hill every month of her life until the winter she died. *Count them, little bird,* she'd say, her breath steaming. *Twelve for the months, and the thirteenth for the price. Never forget there was a price.*

Orsen counted.

One, by the millrace, mossed green to the shoulder. Two, on the western slope. Three through eight, marching the ridgeline, patient as teeth. Nine, ten, eleven, twelve—each one humming, when Orsen laid a palm against it, with the faint subterranean vibration of a thing doing its work. The hum had been background noise their whole life, as unnoticed as heartbeat.

The thirteenth stood alone on Wardrise itself, nearest the house, and Orsen had saved it for last out of habit, the way you save the stair you climb daily.

It was cracked.

Not chipped—cracked. A fissure ran from its crown to its base, fine as a hair but unmistakable in the dawn light, and where the crack gaped widest, a fleck of the stone's inner face glittered oddly, like mica, like glass, like—

Like the inside of a flask.

Orsen's hand rose and hovered an inch from the fissure without touching. The stone's hum was still there, but changed: thinner, arrhythmic, a heartbeat with a skipped beat. And beneath the hum, so faint Orsen might have imagined it, came a sound they had no honest name for, though their body recognized it instantly and coldly, the way a body recognizes the smell of smoke.

It sounded like something remembering.

Orsen stood on Wardrise in the grey light with their hand hovering and their breath fogging, and heard their grandmother's voice as clearly as if she stood behind them: *Twelve for the months, and the thirteenth for the price.*

Below in the valley, the meetinghouse bell began ringing again—off schedule, ragged, someone ringing it wildly—and Orsen turned toward town, toward the sound, and forgot, for one crucial moment, to finish the count.

When they looked back at the stone, the crack had widened by the width of a fingernail.

And the sky above the eastern ridge, where the last stars of night should still have been hanging, was—empty.
