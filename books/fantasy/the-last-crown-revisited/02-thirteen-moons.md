## 2. Thirteen Moons

The ward-stones of Hollowmere had names, once. My grandmother swore they did—thirteen names in a dead language she refused to teach me, saying that a thing named too often wears thin, like a coin passed through too many hands. She'd been gone eleven years by the time I started counting them myself, and I counted the way she never did: quickly, carelessly, the way you count fence posts on a road you expect to walk again tomorrow.

I was walking home from the widow's house with her coins warm against my chest and her grief still souring my mouth, when I decided to count.

It wasn't ritual. It wasn't even habit. It was something closer to restlessness—a feeling like a splinter working its way toward the surface, an itch beneath the ordinary evening. The light was doing something strange. Autumn came early to the valley and lingered late, hemming us in with fog that ate sound and softened edges, but this evening the light fell long and amber and wrong, gilding things that should have been left gray. The millpond looked like hammered copper. The crows on the chapel roof cast shadows twice their size.

I took the low road, the one that looped along the valley's eastern rim where the first stones stood, because it added ten minutes to my walk and cost me nothing but the company of my own thoughts—and those were poor companions after a job like the widow's. Her name was Marta Ellery. Her husband had been dead four years, drowned in the millrace, and what she'd wanted to forget wasn't the drowning itself but the last thing he'd said to her at breakfast that morning: *If the water's high, don't wait for me.* She'd obeyed him for four years without knowing why the obedience gnawed at her, and then she'd come to me with her savings in a flour sack and asked me to take the sentence out of her head so she could stop hearing it.

I'd done it. Clean extraction, clean seam. She'd walked home lighter, humming.

And I'd walked away heavier, carrying a dead man's last words in a glass vial against my ribs alongside eleven other people's worst mornings, and I told myself—as I always told myself—that this was mercy, that someone had to be the valley's drain, that what pooled in me would otherwise pool in them.

My grandmother would have said: *a thief who robs grief is still a thief.*

She said a great many things, my grandmother. Most of them in a voice I could no longer conjure honestly, which is perhaps why the warnings survived better than the warmth.

* * *

The first stone stood where the low road bent toward the river flats, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, taller than a man and shaped like nothing so much as a finger pointing accusingly at the sky. Ward-stone, we called them, though nobody under sixty knew what the wards actually warded *against*. They kept magic out—that much everyone agreed on. The valley sat inside a ring of thirteen of them like a cupped hand, and inside that ring, nothing burned unless you lit it, nothing floated unless you threw it, nothing whispered to you in voices that weren't there. Outside the ring was the war. Inside was Hollowmere, where the most exciting event of any year was whether Alderman Vess would finish his harvest speech before the cider ran out.

I put my palm flat against the first stone, the way children here were taught before they were taught their letters. Cold. Damp. Utterly indifferent. The stone drank the touch and gave back nothing, which was correct. That was the whole arrangement: we gave the valley our curiosity and the stones gave us our sleep.

Second stone, past the fallen oak. Third, where the sheep path forked.

I counted the way you count stairs in the dark—not because you doubt yourself, but because doubting is easier than thinking about anything else. Eleven vials in my coat. A widow's silence in my blood. And somewhere behind my sternum, that splinter-itch getting worse with every stone that answered my touch with the same dumb, faithful coldness.

The seventh stone had a bird's nest wedged in a hollow near its crown. The ninth wore lichen like a green ruff. The eleventh had been struck by lightning sometime before my grandmother's grandmother, judging by the scar, and still stood, and still worked, and I touched it anyway, and it gave me nothing, and I loved it a little for that.

It was the thirteenth stone that stopped me.

* * *

You have to understand: the thirteenth stone was the one every child in Hollowmere learned to fear and love in equal measure, because it was different. It didn't stand at the valley's rim with its brothers and sisters. It stood alone on the knoll above the old Zhou property—the house my grandmother left me, sagging and stubborn, with its root cellar full of jars I'd never opened and its attic full of boxes I'd never dared. The thirteenth stone was ours in the way that a lighthouse belongs to the family who keeps it, except nobody in living memory had done any keeping. It simply stood there, blacker than the others, smoother, as though it had been poured rather than quarried, and it hummed.

Not loudly. Not always. But if you pressed your ear to it on certain nights—nights when the thirteen moons of the old calendar aligned in their slow wheel, or so my grandmother claimed—you could feel it. A vibration like a held breath. Like something enormous remembering it was supposed to be asleep.

Grandmother had a game she played with me when I was small enough to be carried home from the fields. She'd hold up her fingers, all ten and then fold two down, and say, "Thirteen moons, Orsen. Thirteen stones. Count them every season, and if ever one of them answers you—" and then she'd stop, every time, and laugh, and say, "Well. Then you'll know it's time to ask your grandmother some very good questions."

She died before I thought of any questions worth asking. That's the trick of warnings: they're priced for later, and later always arrives broke.

I climbed the knoll with the amber light dying behind the western ridge and my breath starting to show, and I put my hand on the thirteenth stone, and it did not answer me.

It cracked.

No—it had cracked. The difference mattered, the way the difference between falling and having fallen matters when you're already at the bottom. A fissure ran from the stone's base up its flank, fine as a hair, fine as the seam of an extraction, so thin I might have invented it, might have been seeing the shadow of the bramble thrown across it by the last of the light. I pressed my thumb into it. The groove was real. Cold air breathed out of it, faintly, rhythmically, like the stone was exhaling through gritted teeth.

And underneath my feet, faint enough that I could have called it imagination, the ground hummed.

I snatched my hand back. Stood there in the blue dusk with my heart going like a drum in a parade and told myself the reasonable things. Stone cracks. Stone settles. Stone has stood for centuries holding back whatever the war couldn't kill, and things that stand that long develop flaws the way old men develop coughs—one flaw, survivable, ignorable. Vess would send someone to look at it. We had a mason. We had mortar. We had, if it came to it, the accumulated ignorance of six generations, and ignorance had kept us safer than any army.

But my hand wouldn't stop remembering the exhale.

* * *

I went inside and lit the lamp and did what I always did when the day had left something in me I didn't want: I worked. The vials came out of my coat, eleven of them, and I set them in their rack in the cellar's cool dark—each one labeled in my own cramped hand. *Marta E., the sentence.* *Joren P., the fire, age nine.* *Sela M., her sister's face at the end.* A little museum of other people's mercies, and mine to keep or sell; extracted memories kept, if you stored them right, and I kept them, though I'd never once examined why. Selling a person's pain felt like robbery. Keeping it felt like—what? Custody? Hoarding? I didn't have the word yet. I wouldn't have it for a while.

That night I went up to the attic.

I'd been up there maybe five times in eleven years, each time for something practical, each time leaving faster than I'd come. Grandmother's boxes lined the walls under dust sheets, and I'd made a private treaty with them: they stayed closed, I stayed sane. But the treaty had a clause neither of us had written, and standing there in the lamplight with the memory of a cracked stone breathing cold air into my thumb, I invoked it.

Her writing desk sat under the east window. Locked, always locked, key presumed lost. I picked it in ninety seconds, because I am a thief, and thieves are honest about exactly one thing.

Inside: papers. Bundles of them, tied with string gone soft as cloth. Letters in her handwriting, dense and slanting, and not letters really—more like ledger entries crossed with scripture. Dates going back further than I'd imagined her alive. Names I didn't recognize. And on the topmost page, written larger than the rest, underlined twice:

*Thirteen stones, thirteen seals. They are not walls. They are wounds, dressed. Ask what bled.*

Below that, smaller, dated the winter before she died:

*The wheel turns. When the thirteenth moon completes its count, the seals will want settling. If I am gone, whoever finds this must go to the stone and listen. Not touch. LISTEN. If it sings, the Crown is waking. If it is silent, we have another generation. God forgive me for hiding it so close.*

*Crown.*

The word sat on the page like a spider. I read it three times and it refused to mean anything, the way words refuse when they're carrying more weight than their size suggests. There was no crown in Hollowmere. There was a king far away who hadn't sent so much as a tax collector in living memory; there was the war, finished before my mother was born; there were stories, and stories were the valley's second crop.

But my grandmother had not written a story. She'd written instructions. *Go to the stone and listen. Not touch.*

I had touched it. I had put my thumb in its wound and felt it breathe.

I sat on the attic floor until the lamp burned low, reading pages I only half understood—fragments about a *Last Crown*, about bearers, about memories taken and kept and worn, about a valley chosen not because it was safe but because it was *hidden*, hidden the way you hide a thing inside a thing, a jewel sewn into a coat's lining. Our invisibility, the thing we praised ourselves for, the wards we thanked for our peace—apparently we weren't the protected. We were the *wrapping*.

And somewhere in those pages, over and over, in margins and between lines, her oldest refrain surfaced, the one she used to sing-song at me while shelling peas, the one I'd thought was just an old woman's nonsense rhyme:

*Count the stones, count the moons,
count them true or count them none.
When the thirteenth moon comes round,
the thirteenth stone will come undone.*

I'd sung it back to her as a child, laughing. She'd never laughed with me. She'd watched me with an expression I now, finally, had the vocabulary for, sitting on that cold attic floor:

She'd been waiting for me to stop laughing.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Toward the coldest hour, I took the lamp back up the knoll, because I am my grandmother's grandchild and apparently incapable of leaving a locked box locked, and I stood before the thirteenth stone and did as her letter commanded.

I listened.

The valley held its breath around me. Fog lay in the hollows like spilled milk. Somewhere a dog complained and gave up. And the stone—

The stone exhaled. That same rhythmic seeping cold, but now, in the deep quiet, with the lamp flame bending toward it as though leaning in, I caught what lived inside the breath. A sound below sound. A resonance with weight to it, patient and vast and terribly awake, like a sleeper turning over in a bed too small for it. And threaded through it, unmistakable, impossible—

a hum. Almost melodic. Almost a song.

I stumbled back off the knoll with the lamp guttering and my skin crawling, and behind me the crack in the stone seemed wider in the lamplight than it had in the dusk—or perhaps the dusk had been kinder, or perhaps I had. Above the valley's western rim, through a tear in the fog, the stars were coming out one by one, faithful as ledger entries, and I counted them without meaning to, the way I counted everything now, and got a number, and got it right, and hated how much comfort I took from a sky that was behaving.

*One season,* I told myself. *One season to learn what she hid, before the wheel finishes turning.* The thirteenth moon was coming—I'd seen it rising fat and late these past weeks, a moon I'd never noticed before because I'd never had reason to count the nights, and it hung now above the ridgeline like an eye opening slowly, patiently, over a valley that believed itself asleep.

I went inside. I barred the door, which I had never once done in my life, because the valley needed no bars, that was the entire point of the valley.

And as I lay in the dark listening to my own heart count instead of stones or stars or moons, three things settled on me with the certainty of frost:

Safety, my grandmother had tried to tell me, has an expiry date.

The wrapping was tearing.

And somewhere beyond the hills, past the dead wards' sleeping ring, something had begun to look for what she'd hidden—

and I had just spent my whole life learning how to make things stay lost.
