## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The thirteenth moon hung low over Hollowmere that evening, swollen and pale as a fish's belly, and Orsen Zhou counted it the way their grandmother had taught them—without looking directly at it.

*Count by its light on things,* she'd said, when they were small enough to sit on her knee without either of them minding. *A stone that watches you back is a stone you don't want watching.*

So Orsen counted the moonlight instead: silvering the eaves of the tannery, pooling in the wheel ruts of Miller's Row, laying a thin blade of white along the windowsill of the widow Maren's house, where Orsen sat drinking tea they didn't want and being paid for patience they were barely pretending to have.

"You'll want to swallow it quick," Orsen said. "Like bitter medicine. The slow way only makes it worse."

Maren Ashby sat across from them with her hands wrapped around her own cup, though the tea had long gone cold. She was sixty if she was a day, with a face like crumpled linen and eyes that had stopped sleeping sometime around the frost-melt. Three weeks ago her husband had drowned in the millrace, and three weeks ago something had lodged itself behind her ribs that no poultice, prayer, or priest could shift—the memory of finding him, of his hand half-raised out of the water as if he'd been reaching for the bank even after the end.

"I don't know," she whispered. "If I give it up, it's like he never—like I'm saying it didn't matter."

"It matters," Orsen said, because that was the lie they'd gotten good at. "It matters so much it's killing you. That's why it has to go."

They set out the tools on the table between them: the shallow copper bowl, the vial of grey glass, the needle-thin silver probe that caught the candleflame and held it. Maren watched the probe the way people watch snakes.

"Does it hurt?"

"No." Another lie, worn smooth as riverstone. "It feels like setting down something heavy. Most folk say afterward they can't remember what all the fuss was about."

Which was true, in its way. That was the cruelty of it, or the mercy—Orsen had never decided which. Memory taken cleanly left no hole. It left a smoothness, a place where the past should have snagged but didn't. People walked away from Orsen's table lighter, and emptier, and grateful, and none of them ever quite knew why the gratitude tasted strange.

Maren closed her eyes. "Do it."

Orsen pressed two fingers to her temple, found the memory by its heat—it burned, this one, hotter than most—and drew it out through the probe and into the vial in one long unbroken pull, like thread from a spool. Maren's breath hitched once. Her shoulders dropped an inch. When she opened her eyes they were clear and calm and slightly, unforgivably vacant.

"There," Orsen said, stoppering the vial. "He'll still be gone tomorrow. But you'll be able to get up tomorrow too."

"Thank you." She said it with real warmth, pressing coins into Orsen's palm—more than the agreed price, and Orsen let her overpay because letting people overpay was also part of the trade. "I feel... lighter. I can't think why I waited so long."

"No," Orsen said softly, pocketing the payment. "You wouldn't."

Outside, the night smelled of woodsmoke and turned earth, and the thirteenth moon laid its pale light across Hollowmere like a sheet over furniture in a house nobody lived in anymore. Orsen walked home through streets they knew better than their own face—they hadn't seen their own face properly in years, not since they'd sold the mirror-memory along with everything else—and felt the particular loneliness of their profession settle over them like the cold coming off the ward-stones.

Thirteen stones ringed the valley. Thirteen moons crossed its sky. Thirteen wards woven by hands that had known what they were hiding from, keeping magic out and Hollowmere invisible and everyone inside safe, safe, safe, in a town so far beneath the world's notice that even grief was the worst thing that ever happened here.

Orsen unlocked their door. Set the widow's memory on the shelf with the others—forty-one vials now, forty-one little grey sunsets, each one somebody's worst hour sealed in glass and waiting for a buyer from beyond the border who would never, ever come, because nobody came to Hollowmere, that was rather the point.

They were lighting the lamp when the first star went out.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years when people still asked Orsen to tell it—they would say the stars went out all at once, every one of them, like a thousand candles pinched between a thousand unseen fingers. But that wasn't quite how it happened. It happened fast, faster than counting, but it happened in order, and Orsen saw the order, because Orsen was standing at their window with the lamp unlit and the whole sky spread out above the valley like a ledger waiting to be written in.

The western stars went first. One, then a handful, then a constellation's worth, winking into nothing so smoothly that for a heartbeat Orsen thought it was cloud. Then the dark came eastward across the heavens like ink spilled on cloth, swallowing star after star, and there was no cloud, there was nothing, there was only black where black had never been—not the soft black of a cloudy night but an absolute, greedy, finished black, a black that ate the sky and left no crumbs.

And then the thirteen moons went out too.

All of them. All at once. Even the fat pale thirteenth, hanging low over the eastern ridge, which simply ceased, mid-shine, the way a voice ceases mid-word.

Orsen stood very still in their dark kitchen and heard their own heart going like a fist on a door.

The valley had never been truly dark at night. Thirteen moons meant there was always light somewhere—a night with no moonlight at all was not a night anyone in Hollowmere had ever seen, not in living memory, not in grandmother's stories, not in the oldest carvings on the well-heads. The sky above the valley was a constant, a covenant, the one thing in Orsen's entire life that had never once changed its mind.

Now it was gone. Now the darkness outside the window was total and close and *wrong*, pressing against the glass like something with weight.

"Grandmother," Orsen said aloud, to the empty room, the way they always did when the old warnings surfaced. *When the sky forgets us, child, the wards have forgotten us first.* They'd thought those were just words, the kind of thing old women said to make children behave. Old women who'd come to the valley from nowhere, with burn-scarred hands and a name nobody else had, who never spoke of before, who checked the ward-stones every season of her life until her knees gave out and made Orsen do it after, and Orsen had done it lazily, carelessly, ticking the ritual off like sweeping a floor—

The thirteenth stone was cracked. They'd seen it themselves, weeks ago, and told no one, because telling someone would make it real, and because some traitor corner of them had hoped that if they ignored it, it would ignore them back.

Outside, dogs began to howl. Not one dog—all of them, every dog in Hollowmere at once, a rising ragged chorus of animal terror that needed no language and offered no comfort. Somewhere down the lane a shutter banged. A child started crying, high and continuous, the cry of a child who has woken into a nightmare and cannot be convinced otherwise.

And then the wards broke.

There was no other word for it. Orsen felt it before they heard it—a pressure change, a popping in the ears, the sensation of a held breath finally released—and then came the sound: a vast, distant, crystalline *crack*, rolling across the valley from ridge to ridge, echoing off the mountainsides, and under the crack a higher sound, a shimmering, splintering cascade like a chandelier coming down, like a window the size of the sky giving way all at once into ten million falling pieces.

Glass. It sounded exactly like breaking glass, except the glass was the border of the world, and it did not fall so much as *unmake*, and when the echoes faded the silence underneath them was the deepest silence Orsen had ever heard. The silence of a room where something has been for so long that its absence is louder than its presence had ever been.

The wards were down. Hollowmere was visible. The valley was open.

Orsen's legs carried them outside before their mind caught up. The lane was filling with neighbors—nightshirts and shawls and lanterns that shook in trembling hands, faces tilted up toward a sky with nothing in it, mouths opening and closing around words that wouldn't come. Old Tam Weller stood barefoot in the road in his nightclothes, pointing straight up and saying *look, look, look* in a flat repeating whisper, as if the looking might summon back what had been looked at. The baker's wife had her children by the wrists and was pulling them toward the chapel as if walls would help. The miller came stumbling up from the millrace shouting something about the water running backward, and whether that was true or madness Orsen couldn't say, but nobody laughed, because laughter had left the world along with the stars.

"The wards," someone said. "The wards are gone."

"They can't be gone. They've been there forever."

"That's what forever means, isn't it? Until it isn't."

"Who did this?" That was Alderman Vess, pushing through the crowd in a formal coat thrown over his nightshirt, his chain of office glinting dully in the lightless dark—he must have grabbed it in the dark by feel, Orsen thought wildly, the fool had reached for his *badge* while the sky died. "Who's done this? Answer me!"

Nobody answered him, because nobody knew, and Vess's voice climbed anyway, pitched high with fear dressed up as authority: "Everyone remain calm. Remain indoors. This is—" He faltered. Above him, the empty sky went on being empty, patient as a debt. "This is temporary. The council will—"

"The council will what?" Tam Weller said, and his whisper-flat voice cracked open into something rawer. "You going to put the stars back, Alderman? You got them in your strongbox?"

"Tam—"

"My grandfather helped cut those ward-stones." Tam's voice carried down the whole lane now, shaking. "My *grandfather*. He said they'd hold past his grave and past mine. What else did he say, eh? What else is coming through tonight?"

That question landed in the crowd like a stone in still water, and Orsen watched the ripples spread—watched fear curdle visibly, neighbor to neighbor, wonder dying in real time. Because that was the thing about the valley's safety that no one said aloud: the wards kept Hollowmere hidden from the world, yes, but everyone knew, in the bone-deep way of inherited knowledge, that hiding ran both directions. Whatever the founders had fled from, whatever the war had been, whatever their grandmother's generation had bled to end—it had stayed out there because the wards held. And now the wards were broken glass, and the night was absolute, and anything at all could be walking down the mountain passes toward them, and every soul in that lane was thinking it, and none of them dared say it, until a woman near the chapel—Maren Ashby, Orsen realized with a lurch, sweet forgetful Maren, whose worst memory Orsen had bottled three hours ago—said it in a small clear voice:

"What's coming?"

And the silence that followed was worse than the howling dogs.

* * *

Orsen didn't stay for the argument. They slipped out of the crowd the way they slipped out of everything—sideways, quietly, a thief of attention as much as anything else—and walked home fast with their collar up against a cold that had teeth in it now, a cold with intent.

The shop smelled of copper and candlewax and the faint mineral tang of stored memories. Forty-one vials gleamed dully on their shelves. Orsen lit the lamp with hands that wanted to shake and mostly didn't, and stood in the middle of their own front room feeling the floor tilt under them.

Here was the arithmetic they couldn't avoid. The wards were down. Which meant the valley was findable. Which meant that sooner or later—days, weeks, but certainly within a season—someone from outside would come. Someone with coin, someone with questions, someone with the kind of power that broke borders as casually as breaking bread. And when they came, they would find a town of soft, frightened people who hadn't seen magic in three generations, and a memory-thief with forty-one vials of stolen grief sitting in plain view like a sign that read *trade here*.

Orsen looked at the shelves and, for the first time in eleven years of trade, felt sick looking at them.

Because here was the other arithmetic, the one they'd been refusing since the night they'd found the crack in the thirteenth stone. Grandmother had made them count the ward-stones every season. Grandmother had made them memorize the maintenance rites and then, in her last year, had gripped Orsen's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *if a stone ever fails, you run east, you hear me, you don't pack, you don't warn anyone, you run east and you keep running*—and Orsen had nodded and filed it away with all her other ravings, the burn-scarred hands, the nightmares, the way she'd flinched at the sight of anything gold.

Run east. Away from the passes. Toward the thirteenth stone, which sat on the eastern ridge like a rotten tooth in the valley's jaw.

Orsen blew out the lamp. Stood in the dark. Listened to the dogs, quieter now, whining more than howling; to the distant murmur of the town still gathered in the lanes; to the enormous ringing absence where the sky used to be.

They should run. Every instinct honed by eleven years of comfortable invisibility said *run, pack nothing, trust no one, survive*. That instinct had kept them fed and free and untouched through a decade of taking other people's pain and selling it, and it had never once cost them a night's sleep.

But their feet, without asking permission, were carrying them to the window. And their eyes, without asking permission, went east—to the black ridge where the cracked stone stood, where grandmother's warnings lived, where something in the deep animal cellar of Orsen's mind was already whispering that the wards hadn't simply failed.

They'd been *opened*. From outside. Deliberately. And whatever had opened them was already coming down the mountain, and it was coming toward Hollowmere, and Orsen understood—with the same cold certainty they felt when a mark was about to name their own price—that whatever it was, it was coming for something. Something specific. Something that had been hidden in this valley for three generations, sleeping under thirteen stones and thirteen moons and a sky full of borrowed light.

Something that, judging by the ache suddenly spreading through Orsen's chest like frost through a windowpane, might have their name on it.

A knock at the door.

Three blows, hard and desperate, and then the sound of a body sliding down against the wood—the heavy wet slump of someone who had been holding themselves upright by will alone and had just run out of will. Through the door, in a voice hoarse with pain and thick with an accent Orsen had never heard inside these borders in their life, came four words:

"Please. They're behind me."

Orsen's hand found the latch.
