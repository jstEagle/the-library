## 4. A Stranger at the Door

The rain found Ilse three miles from Hollowmere, and it did not fall so much as hunt her—slanting, needling, finding every tear in her cloak as though the sky itself had been briefed on her crimes. She had been walking for two days on a leg that no longer entirely belonged to her. The wound in her thigh had gone past pain into something colder and more conversational, a dull voice that spoke to her with every step: *sit down. Rest. Just for a moment.* She knew what that voice meant. She had heard it from dying soldiers on the Ashfield road, men who sat down for a moment and never stood again.

So she did not sit. She put one foot in front of the other and counted them, because counting was a thing she could still do that required nothing of her but stubbornness.

*Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve.*

Above her, where the stars should have been, there was nothing.

She had seen it happen—the whole sky going dark at once, not clouded over but *erased*, as if some enormous hand had wiped the night clean and forgotten to paint it back. She had seen mages twice her age weep at the sight. She had felt the wards fail an hour later, a sound like a wineglass breaking somewhere behind her eyes, and she had understood, with the clarity that only terror provides, that the valley's long invisibility was over. That everything hidden was about to be found.

Including her.

*Four hundred and thirty.*

The shard rode against her sternum, wrapped in three layers of linen and one of oiled silk, bound tight because it hummed. It had begun humming three days ago, shortly after the stars went out, and the sound was not sound exactly—it was more like remembering something you'd never known, a vibration in the teeth and the breastbone, a whisper made of other people's lives. The enforcers' ledger called it Fragment One of the Last Crown. The old texts called it the Memory of Kings. Ilse, who had carried it out of the vault at Cindral Keep with a dead archivist's blood still warm on her hands, called it *the thing that is going to get me killed.*

It had gotten her this far. That was more than she'd expected.

*Four hundred and fifty.*

Then, through the rain: light. A single window, yellow and ordinary, glowing at the edge of a town that should not have existed. Hollowmere. She had memorized its position from Sefa's stolen maps—a valley town, ward-shielded, thirteen stones, population small enough that a stranger would be noticed within a day. Bad ground for a fugitive. But her leg had stopped taking votes, and the choice between being noticed and being dead was not a difficult one.

She came down the slope toward the light, and the last hundred yards took her longer than the last mile had. The wound had opened again; she could feel it sliding, warm under the bandage, and the cold had gotten into her fingers so thoroughly that they seemed to belong to someone else. The town resolved out of the rain in pieces—a well, a shuttered smithy, a lane of low stone houses—and the lit window belonged to the last house on the lane, set slightly apart, with a herb garden gone ragged under the weather and a door of grey oak.

Ilse remembered thinking, absurdly, that she should knock politely. She remembered raising her hand.

She did not remember falling.

---

Consciousness returned in fragments, which was fitting, given what she carried.

Warmth first—that was the shock of it. Warmth, when she had resigned herself to dying cold. A floor beneath her cheek, wooden, swept clean. The smell of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, underneath it, something sharper: astringent herbs, the smell of a wound being tended. Hands at her leg. Not gentle, exactly. Efficient.

"Stop moving or I'll sew through your bone."

A voice. Younger than she'd expected. Flat, unimpressed, the voice of someone who had seen wounds before and declined to be moved by any of them.

Ilse opened her eyes. Firelight swam, then steadied. Above her leaned a figure wrapped in a blanket over a nightshirt, hair tied back carelessly, face narrow and watchful and lit from below by the hearth. Their expression was the expression of a person doing arithmetic they didn't like.

"You're not from here," the figure said.

"No," Ilse managed. Her throat was sand. "Water."

A cup was pressed to her lips—brusquely, but it was pressed, and she drank until it was taken away. Then the figure sat back on their heels and studied her, and Ilse studied them back, because studying people was the one skill the towers had left her with.

Young. Mid-twenties, perhaps. Calluses on the hands in the wrong places for a farmer—between the fingers, along the thumbs. The hands of someone who worked with small tools, precise ones. And around their neck, half-hidden by the blanket's collar, a cord bearing a plain iron ring worn smooth with handling. An extractor's token. Ilse had read enough ledgers to know what that meant. In the border valleys, memory-work was a trade like tanning or smithing—unglamorous, necessary, quietly despised.

*A memory-thief,* she thought. *Of all the doors in all the valley.*

"Ilse," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "My name is Ilse."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to." She tried for a smile and suspected she achieved something closer to a grimace. "Everyone asks eventually. Better to get it over with while I'm too weak to lie convincingly."

Something flickered across the young thief's face—not amusement, quite, but a neighbor of amusement. "Orsen," they said finally, as if paying out a coin reluctantly. "This is my house. You bled on my floor, which means you owe me, which means you'll answer questions. Starting with—" their eyes dropped to her chest, where the linen wrappings showed through the torn cloak, "—what's under those bandages. And don't say wounds. I've already treated the wounds. There's something else. I could feel it while I was working. Like holding a beehive made of whispers."

Ilse's heart stumbled. She kept her face still through long practice, but inside, calculation ran fast and cold. Tell them nothing. Take the shard and go—except she couldn't take the shard and go; her leg had other opinions. Refuse, and risk the thief deciding a stranger bleeding secrets onto their floor was safer handed over to whoever came asking. Because someone would come asking. The enforcers did not lose Crown fragments; they lost people.

And yet.

She looked at Orsen's hands—steady, competent, stained to the wrist with her blood—and thought: *they could have let me die on the step. One door, unlatched, and the rain does the rest. No one in this valley would ever have known.*

"Why did you open the door?" she asked instead.

Orsen's jaw tightened. "You didn't knock. You fell against it. There's a difference."

"There isn't, and we both know it." Ilse pushed herself up on her elbows, and the room tilted, and she rode the tilt with her teeth set. "You saw a stranger dying in the rain and you opened the door anyway. In a valley that has spent sixty years teaching itself that strangers are the price of safety. So either you're a fool—"

"I'm not."

"—or you remember what it's like to need a door opened." She held their gaze. "Which is it?"

For a moment the fire popped, and rain ticked against the shutters, and Orsen said nothing at all. Then they stood, abruptly, and crossed to the hearth, and stirred a pot that needed no stirring.

"My grandmother used to say," they said, to the pot rather than to her, "that the wards keep out more than magic. They keep out having to decide. Out there, every stranger is somebody's problem. In here, nobody's anything. It's a comfortable way to live." They glanced back at her, and their eyes were older than their face. "I hate comfortable things. They rot."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give." They ladled something from the pot—broth, thin and steaming—and brought it over, and set it down beside her with the careful roughness of someone who resented their own kindness. "Drink. Then talk. What are you running from?"

"The crown," Ilse said. "What's left of it."

Orsen went very still.

"In the capital, when the last king died without an heir, everyone said the age of crowns was over. It wasn't. It went underground. There are remnant enforcers—maybe two hundred of them, maybe fewer—who kept the old oaths and the old ledgers, and they've spent years hunting everything the Crown ever touched. Records. Relics. People." She paused. "People whose families touched it."

"Whose families—" Orsen started, and then stopped, and Ilse watched something move behind their eyes like a fish turning in dark water. But they didn't finish the question, and she didn't offer, because the shard chose that exact moment to hum.

Loudly.

It had never done that before. Three days of a whisper, and now, in this small hot room, the vibration rose through the linen wrappings like a struck bell, and both of them heard it, and both of them froze. The firelight seemed to bend toward it. And Ilse—Ilse *felt* it reach, the way a drowning man reaches, felt it strain against its bindings toward something in the room, and it took her a heartbeat of horror to understand what.

Not the hearth. Not the house.

*Orsen.*

"What," Orsen said quietly, "is that."

Ilse's mouth was dry. Every instinct she owned—the instincts that had gotten her out of Cindral Keep alive, past two checkpoints, over the mountains—screamed at her to lie. To say it was a relic, a trinket, a cursed heirloom, anything that made it small. Small things got thrown in rivers. Big things got hunted across kingdoms, and anyone who had touched them got hunted too.

But the shard was still humming, and it was humming at Orsen, and lying to the one person it wanted would be like lying to a lighthouse about the sea.

"It's a fragment," she said. "Of the Last Crown."

She watched the words land. Watched Orsen's face do several things very quickly and settle on blankness, which was its own kind of tell.

"The Crown was destroyed," Orsen said. "Every child in the valley learns it. The last bearer broke it rather than let the war continue, and the pieces were scattered, and the wards were raised, and we've been safe for sixty years."

"Yes."

"Then how—"

"How do I have a piece of a broken thing?" Ilse laughed, and it hurt her ribs. "Because broken things don't stop being what they are. They just wait. This one waited in a vault under Cindral Keep for sixty years, catalogued and guarded and *hungry*, and three weeks ago I opened the wrong door looking for something else entirely, and it—" she searched for the word and hated that the truest one was also the maddest one, "—it *chose* me. It started humming the night the stars went out. It's been pulling me north ever since. I told myself I was choosing the route." She looked down at the wrappings over her heart. "I'm starting to doubt that."

Orsen had gone pale. Not the pallor of fear—or not only that. The pallor of recognition, of a person hearing a story and finding themselves already written into it.

"It's humming *now*," Orsen said. "Why is it humming now? It wasn't humming when you collapsed."

"I don't know."

"You know. I can see you know. You have the face of someone doing sums they hate." Orsen crouched, bringing us eye to eye, and up close Ilse could see the exhaustion under their composure, the loneliness worn smooth as a river stone. "I pull memories out of people for a living. I know what evasion looks like. So I'll ask once, plainly, and you'll answer plainly, or you can take your beehive and your ruined leg back into the rain. Why is it humming at me?"

And there it was. The door within the door.

Ilse thought about the ledger she'd read by candlelight in the archive, the page she'd torn out and burned and regretted burning. *Zhou, Maren. Bearer, final. Status: deceased. Kin: one grandchild, location unknown, valley wards presumed sufficient.* She thought about the way the shard had strained toward this house before she'd even seen its window. She thought about a grandmother who had supposedly died a simple herb-woman, and a Crown that had supposedly been shattered by a nameless hero, and sixty years of stories that fit together far too neatly to be true.

If she said it aloud, she would make this stranger a target. The enforcers' ledger had a column for kin. She had read it. She knew how the column was filled.

If she didn't say it aloud, the shard would say it for her, sooner or later. Things that hungry were not discreet.

"Your grandmother," Ilse said. "What was her name?"

The silence stretched. Rain on the shutters. Fire settling. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"Maren," Orsen said at last, barely above a whisper. "Maren Zhou. She raised me. She died four years ago." Their eyes narrowed. "Why does a fugitive from the crown's remnants know to ask about my grandmother?"

"Because," Ilse said, and gentled her voice as much as her shredded throat allowed, "in the vault under Cindral Keep, in the enforcers' own ledger, in ink that has not faded in sixty years, there is an entry that reads: *Zhou, Maren. Last Bearer of the Crown. The one who hid it.*"

She watched the sentence go into Orsen like a blade going into water—no splash, just a slow dark spreading.

"And the fragment," she finished, "has been humming at you since the moment I crossed your threshold. Whatever your grandmother hid, Orsen Zhou—I don't think she hid it from the world."

She let that sit. Let it turn over.

"I think she hid it in *you*."

Outside, the rain kept falling on a valley that no longer had its wards, and against Ilse's heart, soft and patient and terribly awake, the shard began to hum a little louder—as if it, too, had finally come home.
