## 5. What the Shard Remembers

The shard sat on my kitchen table like a thing that had always lived there.

That was the trouble with it. Ilse had been asleep in my spare room for the better part of a day—Maren's old room, the one I'd stopped opening after the funeral—and still I couldn't stop looking at the fragment she'd carried through the dead wards and half-dead herself. It was no bigger than a man's thumb, a sliver of something that might have been glass if glass had ambitions. It caught the lamplight wrong. Light went *into* it and didn't come back out the same. It came back older.

I'd made a living off memories for eleven years. I knew what recollection looked like when it was bottled, bled, or bound: the amber of a jarred first kiss, the gray silt of a grief too heavy to carry. I'd held ten thousand of them in my hands. None of them had ever looked at me back.

This one did.

"You shouldn't touch it," Ilse had said before sleep took her under, her voice fraying at the edges like rope dragged over stone. "Not without me awake. It's not—it doesn't work the way your extractions do. It's not a container. It's a *mouth*."

A mouth. A thing that ate. I'd filed the warning away with all the other warnings I'd spent a lifetime not heeding, and now, past midnight, with the valley's thirteen stones silent as gravestones outside and no stars above them, I sat alone with a piece of the Last Crown and a cup of tea gone cold enough to grow its own opinions.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a memory-thief: you get very good at other people's lives and very bad at your own. I could tell you the exact color of the ribbon Maren wore to her wedding—cornflower, with a loose thread at the bow, because she'd bought it secondhand from the Vess estate sale and never noticed—but I could not tell you the name of my own first friend. I'd sold it at nineteen for coin enough to fix the roof. There was a hole where that child should be, smooth-edged, comfortable, the way a missing tooth stops hurting once your tongue gives up checking.

My grandmother used to say the valley's peace was bought the same way. *We paid in remembering, Orsen-girl. Everything safe costs something.* I'd thought it was just the way old women talked.

The shard hummed. Not sound, exactly—more a vibration in the teeth, in the small bones behind the ears, the frequency of a word spoken just below hearing. I'd felt it faintly since Ilse collapsed on my doorstep, but tonight it was louder, and tonight it was pointed. At me. The way a compass needle is pointed, or an accusation.

"Just looking," I told it, because I'd started talking to objects somewhere around the time the stars went out and I hadn't yet decided to be embarrassed about it.

It pulsed.

I should have woken her. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the world keeps of my poor decisions: *she should have woken the mage.* But Ilse slept like someone who hadn't slept in weeks, and the enforcers were coming—I'd seen their lanterns from the ridge at dusk, a slow constellation crawling along the border road—and whatever answers this thing held, we would need them before those lanterns reached my door.

So I did what I do best. I did what I've done to widows and cowards and men who wanted to forget the faces of the children they'd failed. I reached out, and I took.

* * *

The coronation came first.

Not gradually—that's the lie I'd have expected from my own trade, the gentle fade-in, the courtesy of transition. No. One moment I was sitting at my table; the next I was kneeling on stone so cold it burned, and the smell hit me like a wall: incense and tallow and the copper tang of a thousand bodies pressed too close, unwashed and ecstatic. Bells. Bells everywhere, so many bells they stopped being music and became weather.

And above me—above *him*, the body I was wearing, because I was inside someone, I was inside a king—a crown was descending.

I have extracted the memory of a hanging. I have taken a mother's memory of the fever-night, a soldier's memory of the order he obeyed. I thought I knew what weight looked like on a human head. I was wrong. The Last Crown settled onto the king's brow and the whole cathedral *inhaled*, every soul in it at once, and I felt what he felt: not triumph. Hunger. A vast, patient hunger waking up, unrolling through him like roots through soil, and underneath the hunger—

Memories. Not his. *Other people's.*

They poured into him the moment the metal touched skin. A queen drowning in a lake she'd ordered drained. An emperor counting plague-dead by candlelight, tallying them like debts. A boy-king on a throne too tall, swinging his feet, waiting for a mother who was never coming back. Centuries of them, generations of rulers, all of it flooding down through the Crown into the fresh vessel, and the king beneath me—we—gasped, and laughed, and wept, all three at once, because he wasn't becoming a ruler. He was becoming *every ruler*. All of them at once. All of them forever.

*I will never be lied to again,* he thought, and the thought wasn't entirely his, and that was the most frightening part—the way the Crown's wanting fit so neatly over a man's own wanting, like a glove already shaped to the hand.

Then the bells changed key, and I understood, with the king's own knowledge, that this was the ninth coronation. Eight bearers before him. And somewhere in the pews, a woman with gray eyes was watching, and the king's borrowed memories flinched away from her face—

* * *

The betrayal came without transition either, but slower, the way rot is slow.

I was the same king, years deeper into the reign. The Crown's memories had grown in him like ivy grown into brick—you couldn't say anymore where the man ended and the relic began. He stood in a war tent lit by a single lamp, and across the map-table from him stood the gray-eyed woman, older now, and the king loved her. That was the terrible engine of it. He loved her the way you love the last person who knew you before.

"You're not listening anymore," she said. "You listen to *them*. The dead ones. You take their counsel and call it wisdom."

"They were rulers," the king said, and heard the echo in his own voice, the plural hiding inside the singular. "They ended wars."

"They ended wars and started them and burned cities and buried rivals, and you drink all of it every morning like water. When did you last decide anything *alone*?"

He reached for the Crown's certainty then—I felt him reach, felt the vast chorus rise up to answer her, eight lifetimes of authority aligning behind his tongue—and she saw it happen. Saw his eyes change. Whatever she'd come to say, whatever plea or warning or confession, she swallowed it, and her hand went to the knife at her belt, and the king's heart broke even as the Crown's cold arithmetic noted, calmly, *threat.*

What happened next happened fast. She moved first—of course she moved first, she'd known him before the bells—and the blade went in under the ribs, angled up, the way you kill something that has more than one life in it. And the king caught her wrist. And the Crown *poured*.

I felt him do it. Felt him reach into her the way I reach into clients, except there was no jar, no mercy, no clean severance—he took everything. Her childhood. Her name. The color of the ribbon at her wedding. He took it all in a long silver pull and left her standing there with empty eyes, a woman-shaped room with nothing in it, and the last thing to go, the thing she fought hardest to keep, was—

* * *

Me.

A kitchen. My kitchen—or its bones, its shape, though the table was different and the window sat where my shelf sits now. A woman with gray eyes stood at a stove, and a child sat on the floor at her feet playing with wooden buttons, and the woman was humming, and I knew her hands before I knew her face. I'd know those hands anywhere. They'd braided my hair. They'd pulled thorns from my palms. They'd taught me, when I was twelve, how to hold a memory gently by its edges.

My grandmother. Younger than I'd ever known her, younger than any photograph, and she was humming the tune I still hum when I'm working, and at her feet the child looked up—

And I was looking at myself.

Small. Maybe four. Hair in the kind of braid only she could do. The child laughed at something and the sound went through me like a spear through water, because I knew that laugh. I'd heard it in dreams I'd assumed were invented. And the grandmother turned toward the door—toward *me*, somehow, across forty years and the impossible geometry of a stolen memory—and her expression was not surprise.

It was guilt.

"Orsen," she said, and the name in her mouth was both endearment and apology, "you weren't supposed to see this yet."

The kitchen shattered.

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor beside the table, which meant at some point I'd fallen out of the chair, and my tea had spilled across the boards in a slow dark star. The shard lay a foot from my hand, dim now, sated, or perhaps just finished with me for the moment. My whole body shook with the fine tremor of a struck bell.

Outside, Hollowmere's dogs had started up again. They'd been doing that since the stars vanished—crying at nothing, at the empty sky, at whatever it is animals sense when the rules change.

I lay on my cold floorboards and tried to assemble myself, and found the pieces didn't fit the way they used to.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I cannot unsay even to myself. Memory-work has rules. Every extractor knows them the way sailors know weather. You can take what a person remembers. You can give it back. What you cannot do—not ever, not for any price—is find yourself inside someone else's past. The self is a locked room. Eleven years, ten thousand extractions, and I have been inside grieving widows and dying soldiers and one alderman's shameful election night, and never once, not in the deepest dive, have I met *myself* walking around in there.

But the Crown isn't someone. The Crown is everyone who ever wore it, layered like river-stone, and somewhere in those layers, in the memory of a gray-eyed woman the ninth king emptied out like a purse—

There was a kitchen. And a child. And the child was me.

Which meant my grandmother had been in that tent. Had held the knife. Had lost everything she was to the thing she'd tried to kill—which meant the woman who raised me, who taught me to hold memories by their edges, who filled our house with warnings about the wards and the war and the price of safety, had been rebuilt afterward from scraps. From whatever the Crown deigned to leave. From whatever *someone* stitched back together.

Or.

Or the other possibility, the one squatting in the corner of my skull like a debt collector: that the extraction trade itself, the whole quiet economy of Hollowmere, the jars on my shelves and the coins in my pocket—all of it traced back to her, to the final bearer of the Last Crown hiding in a memoryless valley, and that the memories I'd been pulling out of my neighbors for eleven years weren't ordinary memories at all. That some of them had passed through the Crown. That some of them had passed through *me*, long ago, before I was old enough to hold anything by its edges.

That the holes in my own history weren't sold. They were *taken*—by her, or for her, or as payment for something I'd been too young to understand I owed.

I got up. My legs worked badly. I crossed the hall to Maren's room, where Ilse slept, and stood in the doorway with my hand on the frame, and almost woke her. Almost asked her what the Crown was, really, and what it did to the people around it, and whether a person could be entangled with a relic the way a fly is entangled with amber—preserved, displayed, owned.

I didn't wake her. Her breathing had finally gone deep and even, the wounded rhythm smoothing out, and she'd earned the hour.

Instead I went to the pantry. To the back wall, behind the preserved plums, where I keep the jars that aren't for sale—the ones I couldn't bring myself to sell and couldn't bring myself to open. Eleven of them. One for each year of the trade, more or less, though three of them are older than the trade, older than my license, older than my name for myself. Grandmother's jars. I'd always assumed they held her memories, surrendered at the end the way valley folk surrender things.

I took down the oldest one. The glass was dark, clouded, and the memory inside didn't drift the way memories drift. It sat at the bottom like a stone. Like a sliver.

Like a shard.

Under the lamplight, turning it slowly, I watched the light go into it and not come back out the same. It came back older.

From the spare room, faint through the wall, Ilse began to scream.
