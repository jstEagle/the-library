## 6. Hollowmere Turns

Tomas Ferrow had lived in Hollowmere for thirty-one years, and in all that time the town hall had never once been full. It was a modest building—timber frame, slate roof, a bell that rang only for fires and funerals—and its benches could seat perhaps sixty people. Tonight there were more than a hundred crammed inside, standing along the walls, spilling out onto the steps, and the air had gone thick with wet wool, tallow smoke, and fear.

He stood at the back where he always stood, arms crossed over his barrel chest, watching Alderman Vess work the room like a man kneading dough.

"The stars," Vess was saying. He didn't need to raise his voice; the hall did it for him, carrying his words from mouth to mouth until everyone had heard them twice. "The stars went out. Every one of them. And you all heard what came after—the sound. Like glass. Like the sky itself breaking." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit. "Thirteen generations, this valley has been hidden. Thirteen generations of peace because we kept our heads down and our borders sealed. And now—" he spread his hands, a gesture of terrible reasonableness, "—now the wards are dead, and a mage is sleeping under one of our roofs."

"She's not sleeping under *my* roof," someone called from the middle of the room. Marta Kell, the cooper's wife. Her voice was thin but steady. "She's under Orsen Zhou's."

"And Orsen Zhou is one of us," Tomas said.

It came out louder than he'd intended, and a hundred faces turned toward him. He felt the heat climb his neck. He wasn't made for this—wasn't made for speaking, for councils, for being looked at. He was made for his forge, for the honest argument between hammer and iron, where you always knew which one would win.

But Orsen was his oldest friend. Had been since they were six years old and Orsen had pulled him out of the millrace by the collar, half-drowned, and never once mentioned it afterward except to charge him a copper every year on the anniversary. You didn't abandon a person like that. Not when the whole town had gone strange.

Vess found him with his eyes. The alderman was a narrow man, gray as ash, with a face like a folded letter—everything important hidden in the creases. "Nobody's saying Orsen isn't one of us, Tomas."

"You're saying it with your eyebrows."

A ripple of nervous laughter moved through the crowd, and Vess absorbed it without flinching. That was the thing about Vess that had always unsettled Tomas: nothing landed on him. Insults, griefs, accusations—they slid off like rain off oiled canvas.

"I'm saying," Vess continued, patient as erosion, "that there are riders at the border pass. Six of them, in crown colors, carrying a banner nobody has seen in thirty years. They sent a herald down this afternoon while most of you were hiding behind your shutters. I spoke with him." He paused, and Tomas watched the crowd lean in despite themselves. Fear made people lean toward the thing they feared, like moths making for the flame that would kill them. "The herald gave us until moonrise tomorrow. Surrender the fugitive, and the crown's remnant will consider the matter closed. Refuse—" he shook his head slowly, "—and they come in. All of them. And not just for her."

"Not just for her" hung in the air like smoke. Tomas saw people glance at their neighbors. Saw old Widow Anselm pull her shawl tighter. Saw the Halloway brothers, who hadn't spoken to each other in four years over some dispute about a fence line, exchange a look of perfect agreement—for the first time in years, united by the desire to give somebody else to the wolves.

That was how it started. Tomas had read enough history—or enough of the stories his grandmother told, which amounted to the same thing—to know how it started. First you gave them the stranger. Then the stranger's friends. Then the ones who argued too loudly against giving anyone up. Fear was a fire that ate outward, and it always started with someone standing at the edge of the circle, someone who already stood slightly apart.

Someone like Orsen Zhou, who took memories out of people's heads for coin, and whom half the town trusted precisely because the other half didn't.

* * *

The council session proper began an hour later, after the crowd had been persuaded—shoved, more accurately—out into the night. Only the seven council members remained, seated around the long table beneath the mounted antlers of a stag hunted before Tomas was born. Vess at the head. Old Mother Quill, who was mostly deaf and mostly asleep. The two Tarrow sisters, who finished each other's sentences and each other's grudges. Farmer Doss, whose face was a permanent apology. Young Pell Wick, barely thirty, who had been put on the council because nobody else wanted the winter road survey. And Tomas, who had been put on it because Orsen had once fixed Vess's daughter's memory of a dead dog—a kindness, freely given—and Vess had apparently decided a debt was best repaid by proximity.

"We should hear from those who've actually seen the woman," Vess said. "Tomas. You've known Orsen longest. What can you tell us?"

Tomas had spent the hour preparing an answer. He discarded all of it.

"That she's hurt," he said. "Bad. Wound in her side, fevered. Orsen's been boiling willow bark since dawn. And she's scared out of her mind, which for a mage is saying something, because from everything I've ever heard, mages don't scare easy."

"Where did she come from?" Pell Wick asked. He had a notebook out, writing everything down, which Tomas found both admirable and deeply stupid.

"Over the border, obviously. Before the wards fell."

"Before?" One of the Tarrow sisters—Rilla, the meaner one—sat forward. "Then she came through the wards. Through them. Nothing comes through the wards. That's rather the entire point of the wards."

"Maybe the wards were already failing," Tomas said. "Maybe that's why the thirteenth stone cracked. Maybe she came through the crack."

He regretted it immediately. He could see the word land—*crack*—and travel around the table, gathering weight. The thirteenth ward-stone's fracture was common knowledge by now; Orsen had counted the stones the morning after the stars vanished, and Orsen counting things was like water running downhill, inevitable and impossible to stop. But hearing it said aloud in the council chamber was different. Hearing it said aloud made it real.

"The stone is irrelevant," Vess said smoothly, and Tomas understood suddenly, with a cold feeling in his stomach, that Vess had wanted exactly this. Wanted the crack discussed. Wanted the fear fed. "What matters is the choice before us. We surrender the fugitive, or we don't. Let's speak plainly about what refusal means."

"Let's speak plainly about what surrender means," Tomas shot back. "You met the herald. What did he promise, exactly? That they'd take her and ride away? That's not how the crown worked, Vess. My grandmother remembered the last time. They take the fugitive, then they ask who sheltered her. Then who fed her. Then who argued for her in council. They have ledgers." He didn't know why he was so certain of this. He only knew the certainty sat in his chest like swallowed iron. "They write names down. Names are how they ruled, before the valley hid. A ledger doesn't forget, and neither do they."

"Then we give them the mage and disavow all knowledge," Rilla Tarrow said. "We say she forced her way in. We say we were about to turn her out ourselves."

"They'll know we're lying."

"How?"

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer was: they'd know the way powerful people always knew. The way the crown had known things, back when there was a crown. And he couldn't explain that to a room full of people who had spent their whole lives believing that invisibility was the same thing as safety.

Mother Quill woke up. This was an event, like an eclipse. She pushed back her chair, squinted around the table with milky eyes, and said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"When I was a girl, before the hiding, my mother told me about the crown's men. She said they never lied to you. That was the worst part. They'd stand in your doorway and tell you exactly what they were going to do, polite as priests, and then they'd do it." She settled back. "I'd think hard about trusting the politeness of men in crown colors."

Silence. Even Vess seemed to weigh that. Then Doss, apologetic as ever, cleared his throat.

"Alderman... I don't want trouble. Nobody wants trouble. But my boy is seven years old and he cried himself sick last night because the stars are gone. He thinks the world ended. If handing over one stranger—one *mage*, one of *them*—makes the riders go away..." He spread his hands. "I have to look at my boy's face in the morning."

"There it is," Tomas said quietly.

"Tomas—"

"No, there it is. That's the whole thing, laid out plain. One stranger against your boy's nightmares. And it sounds reasonable. It sounds *very* reasonable. Until you remember that the strangers always start as one." He stood, because sitting felt wrong for what he had to say next, and his knees complained about it. "My grandmother used to say: a town is a rope. Every family's a strand. Twist them together and you can hang a gate with it. Start pulling strands out one at a time and pretty soon all you've got is string, and string breaks." He looked around the table, at each face in turn. "They're asking us to hand them a strand. And we're going to spend the rest of our lives wondering which strand they come for next."

Vess regarded him for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted.

"Noble words, Tomas. Truly. But nobility doesn't stop arrows. Here is what I propose." He turned to the table. "We send a delegation to the border at first light. Not to surrender her—not yet—but to negotiate. To buy time. To learn what they truly want. Meanwhile, no one speaks of the fugitive outside this room. No one visits Orsen's house. We isolate the problem, contain it, and decide with clear heads. All in favor?"

Hands rose around the table. Doss. Both Tarrows. Pell Wick, after a guilty glance at Tomas. Mother Quill raised hers too, though whether in favor or to ask for more tea was unclear.

Vess looked at Tomas last, expectant, and something in that expectation made Tomas's skin crawl. As if Vess had already counted the votes and Tomas's hand was merely ceremonial.

"All in favor," Vess said, before Tomas could decide. "Carried. Meeting adjourned."

* * *

Tomas didn't go home. His feet carried him through the dark streets on the path they'd carried him down ten thousand times before—past the well, past the shrine with its unlit candle, up the crooked lane to the small house at the edge of town where lamplight still burned in the window.

Orsen answered the door before he knocked. They always did. Thirty years of friendship and Tomas had never once managed to surprise them.

"Well?" Orsen said. They looked exhausted—hollow-eyed, ink-stained fingers, a smear of Ilse's blood still dried brown on their cuff. Behind them, through the doorway, Tomas could see the stranger-mage asleep on the settle, breathing shallowly, one hand even in sleep curled protectively around something at her breast. Something that, in the lamplight, seemed to glint with a light that had no business existing in a world with no stars.

"They voted to send a delegation to the border," Tomas said. "To negotiate. Vess is leading it himself."

Orsen's eyes narrowed. "Negotiate what?"

"That's the thing. I don't think it's negotiation. I think Vess wants to talk to them alone." Tomas rubbed the back of his neck. "Orsen, I've known the man twenty years. He's frightened, yes, same as everyone. But it's more than that. He's *organized*. Did you know he called the council session within an hour of the herald leaving? Before most folks even knew riders were at the pass? He had the agenda written out. He had the votes lined up. Doss, the Tarrows, Pell—all of them nodding along like puppets." He hesitated. "And when I argued, he looked at me like—like a man checking an item off a list."

Orsen was quiet for a moment. In the silence, the mage on the settle stirred and murmured something in a language Tomas didn't know, and the thing clutched against her chest pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat.

"Did you see the thirteenth stone?" Orsen asked abruptly.

"What? No. Why?"

"It's hollow." Their voice was very quiet. "There's a vault inside it, Tomas. And I think—I think whatever's in there is why the wards fell. Why the stars went out. I think Hollowmere was never what we thought it was. I think the hiding was built on top of something, and the something is waking up." They looked past Tomas, out at the starless black above the rooftops, and for a moment they looked less like the sharpest person in the valley and more like a child lost after dark. "And I think Vess knows more than he's saying. He asked me yesterday—casual, like asking about weather—whether my grandmother ever left any papers. My grandmother, Tomas. Who's been dead eleven years."

The cold feeling in Tomas's stomach returned and settled in for a long stay.

"So what do we do?"

Orsen turned back, and the sharpness came back into their face like a blade coming off a whetstone. "Tomorrow, when Vess's delegation rides out, I want you to follow them. Keep far back. Don't be seen. I want to know what's said at that border—and I want to know if Vess says anything to the riders that he didn't say to the council."

"You want me to spy on the alderman."

"I want you to watch my back," Orsen said. "Like you've done since the millrace. Except this time I'm asking first, and it'll cost you more than a copper."

Tomas looked at his oldest friend—at the blood on their cuff, at the stranger sleeping behind them holding a piece of a world Tomas didn't understand—and thought about ropes and strands and string.

"Fine," he said. "But I'm charging you interest."

Outside, far away at the edge of hearing, something at the border pass began to drum. Slow. Patient. Like a heartbeat counting down to moonrise.
