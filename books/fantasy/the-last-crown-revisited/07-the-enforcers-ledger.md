## 7. The Enforcers' Ledger

Captain Reye had crossed the Ashen Pass at dawn, and by the time the valley opened beneath her like a held breath, she had already counted the ward-stones twice.

Thirteen of them. Old ones, older than the road, older than the crown they'd been cut from. They stood along the border like a fence of gray teeth, and the grass grew right up to their bases as though the land itself had agreed to forget what lay beyond them. Reye did not forget things. That was the whole of her profession.

"Stones are dead," said Corporal Hask, riding at her left. He said it the way men report weather—flat, unbothered, already thinking about his supper.

Reye looked again. He was right. For three hundred years the ward-stones had hummed, a sound so low most people never heard it consciously but felt it in their teeth, in the backs of their eyes, a constant reminder that here was safety and there was everything else. Now the stones stood silent. Frost clung to their southern faces in the warm season's first week, which meant nothing good.

"The ledger says this valley was warded by the Crown's own fragments," Reye said. "If the wards are dead, then either the fragments have moved—" She let the sentence hang. Hask knew how it ended. *Or someone has moved them.*

They rode down into Hollowmere with six riders behind them and the black-and-silver banner of the Remnant furled tight on its pole, because Reye had learned long ago that an unfurled banner made towns defensive and a furled one made them curious, and curious people answered questions. The town sat in the crook of the river like something asleep: slate roofs, smoke rising straight in the windless morning, a market square where a handful of stalls were being set out by people who did not yet know their world had ended in the night.

She could see it in their faces as the column entered the square—the moment each of them looked up, saw the sky, and remembered. The stars had gone out over Hollowmere just as they had gone out everywhere else within a hundred leagues of the broken wards. People who had lived their whole lives under a ceiling of lights now stood under a blank black cloth, and you could watch the fear move through them like wind through wheat.

"Post at the four corners," Reye told Hask. "No one leaves the square. No one is harmed. We are guests."

Hask's mouth twitched. "Guests."

"We are *polite*," Reye corrected, "which is different, and more frightening."

* * *

The alderman came out of the council house so fast he nearly tripped on his own robe. Vess was a soft-bodied man with a clerk's hands and the eyes of a man who had spent years practicing an expression of harmless worry until it had grown into his face permanently. He wrung those clerk's hands as he approached her horse, and Reye noted, without any particular surprise, that he did not ask who she was or why she had come.

"Alderman Vess," she said, dismounting. "You received our letter."

"Three days ago, yes, Captain, yes—we've done everything asked, the town has been very cooperative, very cooperative indeed—"

"You knew we were coming." Not a question. She watched him carefully. "And you didn't warn your people."

"They'd only panic! There's been enough panic since the stars—" He caught himself, glanced around, lowered his voice. "Captain, if I may speak plainly."

"You may not," Reye said pleasantly. "But you will anyway. You always do."

Something flickered across Vess's face—hurt, quickly buried, and beneath the hurt a flash of resentment so old and worn smooth with handling that it barely registered as emotion anymore. It was the look of a man who had been useful for a long time and resented being reminded of it.

"The ledger, Alderman," Reye said. "The names."

Vess produced it from inside his robe—a slim book bound in gray leather, its pages soft with age—and Reye took it with both hands, because some objects deserved ceremony even when they deserved nothing else.

The Ledger of Contacts. Every soul in the valley who had ever touched Crown-magic, whether knowingly or not. Three centuries of careful record-keeping, maintained first by the Crown's archivists and, after the fall, by the Remnant's agents embedded in every town from here to the coast. Names in faded iron-gall ink. Grandmothers who had carried messages. Farmers who had plowed up a shard fragment and, not knowing what it was, kept it on a mantelpiece until their hair fell out. Children born too close to the stones.

Reye turned the pages slowly. This was the part of the work she hated least and understood best: the reading of names. A name was a person reduced to their smallest true shape. Everything else—love, grief, the smell of their kitchens—was commentary.

"Zhou," she said.

Vess went still. "I'm sorry?"

"There's a Zhou family in this town. Still is?" She looked up. "The old woman. Maren Zhou."

"She died," Vess said. "Years ago. Winter fever."

"And her family?"

"A granddaughter. Grown now." Vess's hands resumed their wringing. "Keeps to herself. Does odd work. Memory-work, the folk call it—extracting recollections, selling them to people who want to be rid of them. Unlicensed, of course, but the valley never much cared about licenses."

Memory-work. Reye felt the word settle into place beside the other facts she'd gathered on the ride down: the cracked thirteenth stone reported by a shepherd two months ago; the letter from Vess describing a stranger seen at the edge of town; and now, last night, the stars going out all at once, like candles pinched by a single hand.

"Maren Zhou," she said again, and read the entry aloud, because she wanted Vess to hear it in her voice, wanted to see whether he flinched. "*Final bearer of the Last Crown. Removed the relic from the field of its making. Assisted in the construction of the valley wards. Status: deceased. Descendants: one.*"

Vess flinched. Good.

"Do you understand what that means, Alderman?" Reye closed the ledger gently. "It means the woman who hid the Crown from us lived forty years in your town, bought bread from your bakers, and died in her bed while my predecessors' agents drank tea with her neighbors and wrote down everything she said. And it means her granddaughter—who makes her living cutting memories out of people's heads—is the single most likely person alive to know where that Crown is sleeping."

"I'm sure Orsen knows nothing—"

"*Orsen*," Reye repeated. "You know the granddaughter well."

"We all know everyone here, Captain, it's a small town—"

"Yes." Reye smiled at him, and watched him take an involuntary half-step back. "It is. Small towns are the easiest places in the world to keep a ledger. Everyone knows everyone. Everyone remembers everything about everyone. All it takes is one man willing to write it down."

She handed the book back. "Where does the granddaughter live?"

* * *

They found the house on the lane above the mill, and Reye walked its perimeter herself while Hask knocked—or rather, while Hask stood before the door looking official, because no one answered.

The door was unlocked. Inside: a cold hearth, a table with two cups on it, one of them still holding tea gone cloudy with sitting. A bed unslept-in. On the desk, a tray of extraction tools laid out with a craftsman's neatness, and beside the tray, a single sheet of paper covered in a hurried hand.

Reye read it standing very still.

*Gone to Cindral. Don't look for me. — O.*

She read it twice. Then she set it down exactly where it had been, adjusting its angle to match the dust shadow, because a room was also a ledger and she did not write in other people's books.

"Hask."

He appeared in the doorway. "Ran?"

"Two cups. One guest. Tea still wet when we rode in—they left within the hour, maybe less, maybe as we were entering the square." She moved to the window. From here she could see the road south, the one that wound out of the valley through the pass toward Cindral, the border town where everything in this region was eventually bought, sold, or stolen. "A memory-thief, running with a stranger, toward the biggest memory-market on the coast. Tell me what that tells you."

Hask considered. "That they're selling something."

"That they're *carrying* something worth selling." She tapped the window frame. "Nobody flees toward a market town unless they have goods. And nobody runs from enforcers with goods unless the goods are hot." She turned. "Saddle up. Six riders stay in Hollowmere under Sergeant Ives—quietly, friendly, buy things, learn things. Vess will help them. Vess always helps."

"And us?"

"Cindral. Fast." She paused at the door and looked back once at the little cold room, at the two cups, at the neat tray of tools. Somewhere in her chest, in the place where she kept the things she refused to examine professionally, something stirred—an image of a girl learning her grandmother's trade at this very desk, patient hands, patient knives.

She put it away. The ledger did not have a column for sympathy.

"One more thing," she said. "When we find them—and we will find them—I do the talking. If the granddaughter runs, no one draws. Dead thieves can't tell us where the Crown is, and a frightened thief tells worse stories than a calm one." She stepped out into the starless morning. "We are polite, Corporal. Politeness is how the Remnant wins. Fear burns out. Courtesy compounds."

Behind them, in the square, Alderman Vess was already talking to Sergeant Ives, hands wringing, voice low and helpful, giving away his town one neighbor at a time.

Reye mounted, and did not look back again.
