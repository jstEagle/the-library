## 8. Running the Wards

The moon was a coin spent badly, half-worn, hanging over Hollowmere like something someone had forgotten to put away. Orsen counted its light the way they'd once counted coins—out of habit, out of hunger—and found it wanting. It didn't matter. Tonight they needed darkness more than silver.

They packed by touch.

The house had been their grandmother's before it was theirs, and every shelf and floorboard knew the shape of her hands better than it knew Orsen's. The loose stone behind the hearth came away with the same reluctant groan it always made, and Orsen lifted out what remained of their savings: a leather pouch of valley-minted marks, a few foreign coins Ilse had identified with a scholar's squint as "worth killing for, ideally not us," and a thin gold band that Orsen had never been able to sell because touching it felt like being watched.

They left the ring in the stone's hollow. Some inheritances were meant to stay buried.

Ilse sat on the edge of the bed, the shard wrapped in strips of blanket against her ribs, watching Orsen pack with the particular stillness of someone who had learned that stillness kept her alive. Her wound had stopped seeping through the bandages that morning, which Orsen chose to interpret as good news rather than as the body simply running out of blood to give.

"You're doing it again," she said quietly.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you decide everything alone and then inform us afterward like weather." She tilted her head toward the window, toward the town beyond it. "Tomas is downstairs. He's been downstairs for an hour. You haven't told him we're leaving tonight."

Orsen tied off the pack. "If I tell him, he'll argue."

"If you don't tell him, he'll follow you anyway, badly, and get himself caught." Ilse's mouth curved—not quite a smile; smiles cost her something now, Orsen had noticed. Everything cost her something. That was what the shard did to people who carried it: it made them pay attention, and attention was expensive. "You're a thief, Orsen Zhou. Steal yourself some honesty."

"I'm not a thief," Orsen said, and heard how the words sounded even as they left their mouth. Old words. Practiced words. *I extract recollections. I provide a service.* The vocabulary of a person building a wall out of euphemism, brick by brick, year by year. "I'm—" They stopped. Outside, far off toward the border, something howled, and it wasn't a wolf. Wolves had gone quiet three nights ago, when the stars went out and the wards died with them, and the things that had replaced the wolves' chorus had no interest in being mistaken for anything harmless.

"We're leaving," Orsen said instead. "Tonight. Before Reye's enforcers stop waiting politely at the border and start walking up the road."

Ilse nodded slowly. "And Tomas?"

Orsen closed their eyes briefly. "Get your boots."

* * *

Downstairs, Tomas was sitting at the kitchen table with his elbows planted and his big hands wrapped around a cup of tea he hadn't touched, because the tea had gone cold an hour ago and Tomas was many things but never wasteful. He was a head taller than Orsen and built like the doorframes of the houses he repaired for a living, and he had the kind of face that made strangers tell him their troubles within minutes—which was, Orsen reflected, probably why Alderman Vess tolerated him at council meetings: fear needed a friendly face to hide behind.

He looked up when Orsen came down, and his expression said he already knew.

"You absolute mule," he said fondly.

"Tomas—"

"No, let me finish." He stood, joints cracking, and crossed to the corner where his own pack already waited—packed, buckled, obviously prepared days ago. "You were going to slip out the back past the tannery and take the shepherd's cut north because you think I don't know about the shepherd's cut. I fixed the fence along it in spring, Orsen. I know my own fences."

Orsen stared at him. "I wasn't going to take you."

"I know." Tomas picked up his pack and slung it over one shoulder, and the gentleness in his voice was somehow worse than anger would have been. "That's what makes you such a pain in the ass to love. You've got this ledger in your head—you keep it for everyone. What you owe, what you've taken, who's owed what. And you've decided you owe me nothing but safety." He shook his head. "Terrible accounting. Worst bookkeeping in the valley. I'm coming."

"They want the mage," Orsen said. "They'll want whoever helped her. Vess is already drawing up lists—I saw the council scribes copying names today. Yours will be on it if you come."

"It'll be on it if I stay." Tomas's jaw set. "Vess asked me point-blank yesterday if I thought the stranger should be surrendered. I told him sanctuary means something or it means nothing, and he wrote something down while I said it. He *wrote it down*, Orsen." He stepped closer, lowering his voice. "Whatever's coming, it isn't staying outside the valley anymore. The stars went out. The wards are dead. There's no *outside* to be safe in. There's only choosing which side of the door you're standing on when it opens."

From upstairs came the soft tread of Ilse descending, and then she was in the doorway with her hood up and the shard humming faintly against her—a sound Orsen could feel in their teeth more than hear, like a word remembered halfway.

"So," Ilse said. "The family meeting has concluded?"

"This isn't a family," Orsen said automatically.

"No," Tomas agreed cheerfully, opening the back door onto the cold. "It's worse. It's neighbors."

* * *

Hollowmere at night, without its stars, was a town holding its breath.

Lamps burned behind shutters—people had started keeping lamps lit all night since the sky failed, as if flame could stand in for constellations—and the streets between them lay in ribbons of orange and black. Smoke from banked fires smelled of wet ash. Somewhere a child cried and was hushed, and somewhere else a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing, at everything, at the missing sky.

Orsen moved through it the way they'd moved through it a hundred times before: close to walls, weight on the outside edges of their feet, breath shallow. But tonight the skill felt different in their hands. Tonight they weren't slipping past sleeping neighbors to steal from them. Tonight they were slipping past sleeping neighbors to leave payment on their doors.

The first house was the widow Maren's.

Orsen stood in her lane for a long moment before they could make their feet move. Three nights ago—or four; time had gone strange since the sky broke—they had sat across from her at this very table while she wept and paid them thirty marks to take away the memory of her husband's last day, the fever, the raving, the smell of the sickroom that clung to her hands no matter how she washed them. Orsen had extracted it cleanly, professionally, and walked home through the dark feeling nothing, or feeling the specific nothing that was their trade's whole wages.

Now they knelt at her door and drew out their purse.

Thirty marks back. Plus interest—the foreign coins Ilse had valued, enough to buy a winter's wood. Plus, folded around them, the note Orsen had written and rewritten seven times until the words stopped sounding like a confession and started sounding like the truth:

*I took something that was yours to keep or lose. I'm sorry. It can't be returned, but it shouldn't have been sold.*

They tucked the pouch into the crack of the door where Maren would find it at first light, and knelt there a moment longer than necessary, forehead nearly touching the wood.

Behind them, Tomas waited without comment. Ilse waited without comment. The shard hummed against the cold air like a struck bell fading.

The second house was the cooper's. The third, the ferryman's. The fourth belonged to a man whose name Orsen had never learned, only the memory they'd sold him—his daughter's face, purchased back after he'd traded it away in grief-drink one terrible winter. Orsen left double at his door. At every door they left something, working down the street they knew by heart, the route of their whole career laid out in lamplit thresholds: *this house, this price, this sin.* Their purse grew lighter with each stop until it held nothing but the gold band they couldn't sell and a single copper they'd saved for luck, and Orsen found that the emptiness of it sat strangely well in their palm. Lighter than they'd felt in years.

"You could have just left," Tomas murmured as they worked. "We could be over the ridge by now."

"And carry it?" Orsen said. "All of it? I've spent ten years carrying other people's worst days in glass vials so they wouldn't have to. You think I can't carry this?"

"That's exactly what I think," Tomas said. "I think you'd rather bleed than admit you're bleeding. But go on. Pay your debts. I'll watch the lane."

* * *

The thirteenth ward-stone stood on the hill above the eastern fields, and they reached it an hour before dawn.

Up close, in the starless dark, the crack in it looked less like damage and more like a wound that had finally finished opening. Orsen had noticed it weeks ago—one hairline seam among the stone's old carvings, fine as a scratch on glass—and had told themselves it was frost-heave, age, nothing. Now the seam gaped wide enough to fit two fingers, and the air around the stone was wrong. Not cold. Empty. Like standing next to a doorway that opened onto nowhere at all.

Beyond it, the valley should have hidden them. For as long as anyone living could remember, Hollowmere had been unfindable—maps skipped it, roads bent around it, armies marched past its borders without ever seeing the smoke of its chimneys. Grandmother had called the thirteen stones "the town's closed eyes." Sleep, children, she'd say, tapping each stone in turn on festival days. Nothing sees a sleeper.

The stones weren't sleeping now. They were dead, all thirteen of them, and the world had noticed. Orsen could feel it the way you feel a stranger's gaze across a market square—that prickle of being *located*.

Ilse approached the cracked stone slowly, reverently, the shard in her arms rising to a higher hum until the sound filled Orsen's skull like a tuning fork pressed to bone. When she laid her free hand against the granite, her breath caught.

"It's hollow," she whispered.

"What?"

"The stone. Under the carving—it's hollow. There's a space inside." She pulled her hand back as if the rock had bitten her, and her eyes, when she turned, were wide and lit with a fear that had nothing to do with enforcers. "Orsen. Your grandmother's mark is on the seam. The same sigil that's branded into the shard's casing. She didn't just live here. She *built* here. This whole valley—these wards—" She stopped, swallowed. "She was Crown-borne. Someone who carried its magic. And she hid it inside thirteen stones and called it safety."

The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the dead ward-stones, and for a moment—Orsen would swear this later, under oath if it came to that—for a moment the wind sounded like thirteen voices exhaling at once, tired and ancient and done.

"We need to go," Tomas said softly. "There are riders on the border road. I saw their lanterns from the last roof. Six of them, maybe eight. They're not moving yet, but they're *arranged*, Orsen. That's military."

Orsen looked back down at Hollowmere one last time. The little town crouched in its bowl of dark hills, lamp-lit and shuttered and pretending hard to be asleep, full of people who had bought forgetfulness from Orsen Zhou because forgetting was cheaper than grieving. Full of doors Orsen had just paid back and could never truly repay.

"Then let's not be findable," Orsen said, and turned north, toward the pass, toward Cindral and whatever waited beyond it. "Keep off the road. Single file. If we're seen, we're pilgrims going to pray at Ashfield."

"Pilgrims," Tomas repeated, falling into step. "Nobody's prayed at Ashfield in forty years."

"Then nobody will look for pilgrims there."

They went up through the bracken in the gray hour before false dawn, three shadows climbing away from the only home two of them had ever known. Behind them the valley lay silent and exposed beneath its murdered sky, thirteen dead sentinels staring blind at the border road where the lanterns of the crown's remnant waited, patient as arithmetic.

Ahead, over the northern ridge, the first smudge of Cindral's distant lights glimmered—border-town lights, lawless lights, the lights of a place where memories hung in shop windows like cuts of meat.

Orsen touched the single copper in their pocket once, for luck, and did not look back again.

They were a mile into the high ground when Ilse, walking beside them, spoke without turning her head.

"There's something you should know before Cindral," she said. "About the shard. About what it does when it gets close to the memories it lost." A pause. The hum against her ribs deepened, and Orsen felt it answer something in their own chest—an echo, a recognition, cold as water down the spine. "It's been calling since the moment we met, Orsen. And I think—I think it knows your name."

The northern dark waited, and the road went down toward it.
