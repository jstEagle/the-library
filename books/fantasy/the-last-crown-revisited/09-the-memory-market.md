## 9. The Memory Market

The road down from the valley was longer than Orsen remembered.

That was the joke of it, of course — they couldn't remember it. The wards had kept Hollowmere invisible not just to the world but to itself; the paths out were roads Orsen had walked a dozen times as a child and now walked like a stranger, Tomas's lantern swinging ahead through the pine dark while Ilse limped behind them with the shard wrapped in three layers of cloth against her chest. Every mile, the shard hummed a little louder. By the time they crested the last ridge and saw the valley of Cindral spread below — lamplit, sprawling, awake at an hour when Hollowmere would have been asleep for generations — the humming had become almost conversational.

"It knows where it is," Ilse said quietly. "Or something here knows *it*."

"Comforting," said Tomas.

"Nothing about this is comforting," Orsen said. "Let's go."

Cindral sat on the border the way a spider sits at the center of its web: patient, glistening, aware of everything that touched its edges. It had been the nearest town to the valley all Orsen's life, close enough that traders came up twice a season, far enough that no one from Hollowmere ever bothered going unless they had to. Orsen had been exactly once, at nineteen, to sell their first extraction — a soldier's memory of a burning village, bought off a drunk deserter who wanted it gone more than he wanted coin. They'd taken it to a broker in a back room, gotten twelve silver, and never thought about what happened next.

Now, walking into the town proper, they understood what happened next. Everything happened next. All of it, forever, in public.

The market didn't keep hours because memories don't sleep. Lanterns hung on every stall in strings of red and gold, and beneath them, vendors called out their wares the way fishwives call out the morning catch. *First kisses, gently used. A mother's lullaby, still warm. Ten years on a fishing boat — you'll smell the salt for weeks, guaranteed.* Between the stalls moved the customers: rich merchants' wives sampling other women's weddings, young men buying courage by the vial, an old man weeping openly over a small glass phial that presumably held someone else's daughter.

Orsen stopped in the middle of the street.

They had known, abstractly, that this existed. Everyone in Hollowmere knew — the wards kept magic out, and with it went the trade in extracted recollection, which was why Orsen's profession lived in a legal gray country all its own. Knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals. Seeing it was like hearing, for the first time, the sound your own knife makes.

"You've gone pale," Ilse observed.

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of old candle wax."

"Tomas," Orsen said, "tell her I'm fine."

"He's the color of old candle wax," Tomas told her.

They found an inn near the market's edge, a place called the Ledger & Lamp whose sign showed a quill crossing a wick. The innkeep, a broad woman with ink-stained fingers, took their coin without asking questions — the first mercy Cindral offered — and gave them a room with two cots and a window overlooking the market square. Ilse collapsed onto a cot and was asleep within minutes, the shard bundled against her ribs, her breathing shallow and feverish. The wound in her side had been bound in Hollowmere, but binding wasn't healing, and healing wasn't something any of them could do here without drawing eyes.

Orsen stood at the window a long time, watching the market turn over its endless inventory of other people's lives.

Below, a vendor was demonstrating a memory to a crowd. He'd cracked a phial against his temple — that was how it worked, Orsen knew, contact and intent — and now his face ran through expressions like weather: terror, wonder, grief, laughter, all of it borrowed, none of it his. The crowd oohed. A woman in furs pressed forward to buy. The vendor wiped his temples and smiled with his own face again, or what passed for it.

"They're not all stolen," Tomas said from behind him. He'd come to stand at the window too, arms crossed. "Some people sell willingly. You know that better than anyone."

"Willingly." Orsen tested the word and found it rotten. "The widow who paid me last week — she was willing. She cried the whole time and thanked me after. That's willing." They turned from the window. "I always told myself I was doing them a kindness. Taking the weight off."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at it from the other side of the counter, aren't I."

Tomas was quiet a moment. "You could stop," he said. "When this is done. Whatever this is."

"When this is done I'll be lucky to be alive. Let's start there." But the words came out softer than Orsen meant them, and Tomas heard the softness, and smiled the way old friends smile when they've won an argument neither person admitted was happening.

* * *

They went into the market at dawn, when the lanterns burned low and the first grey light made everything look honest.

Ilse stayed behind — she could barely walk, and the shard drew attention like a wound draws flies. She'd given Orsen instructions anyway, delivered in the clipped voice of someone who has decided not to be afraid: buy willow bark and honey for the fever, buy nothing else, talk to no one, and above all touch nothing in the vendor cases. "Memory sticks to skin," she'd said. "Yours especially. Yours is already tangled in this. Don't give it more thread."

So of course, within the hour, Orsen touched something in a vendor case.

It wasn't deliberate. That was the thing they'd tell themselves later, walking back through the events like a man checking his pockets after a theft. The market at dawn was quieter, the vendors drowsing on their stools, the crowds thin to stragglers and early bargain-hunters. Orsen had bought the willow bark. They had bought the honey. They were on their way back, keeping to the edges, head down, invisible — invisibility was the one craft Orsen had mastered before ever learning memory-work, the family inheritance nobody spoke of — and then they passed a stall near the square's north corner and stopped as if they'd hit a wall.

The stall was smaller than most. Its awning was patched in three places. Behind it sat an old woman with a face like a walnut shell, sorting phials into rows with the unhurried precision of someone who had done it ten thousand times. And in the case before her, laid out on faded blue velvet under a pane of scratched glass, were memories.

Ordinary ones, at a glance. That was the horror of it — how ordinary. Phials of summer light. Phials of bread baking. A phial labeled *FIRST SNOW, AGE SIX*, another labeled *FATHER'S HANDS*. The labels were written in a careful, childish hand, the kind of hand someone uses when they're cataloguing treasures.

Orsen leaned closer. Just to look. Just to pass the time.

And saw their own name.

Not their name — their grandmother's name, written on a slip of paper pinned inside the case, in handwriting Orsen knew the way you know a scar. *Maren Zhou — estate, partial lot.* Beneath it, a dozen phials arranged in a fan, each tagged in that same careful script:

*Girl, age four, riverbank.*
*Girl, age five, learning letters.*
*Girl, age six, the thirteenth stone.*
*Girl, age seven, grandmother's kitchen.*

The world tilted. Orsen put a hand on the case's edge to steady themselves, and their bare fingers brushed the glass, and —

— cold river water over their shoes, Maren laughing somewhere upstream, *mind the current, little fish* —

— the taste of chalk dust and triumph, the first word spelled right, *Z-H-O-U* —

— standing before the great grey stone at the valley's edge while Maren's voice said, solemn as scripture, *this one matters most, Orsen, remember that I said so* —

— cinnamon, flour, a wooden spoon too big for a child's fist —

Orsen snatched their hand back. The old woman looked up.

"Careful," she said mildly. "Skin remembers."

"What—" Orsen's voice came out wrong, scraped. They tried again. "Where did these come from?"

The old woman shrugged. "Estate sale. Woman died — well, woman didn't die, woman *left*, which around here amounts to the same thing. Landlord seized what she left behind. Memories count as property in Cindral, same as furniture. Same as children, once upon a time." She said it without bitterness, the way you recite a rule of weather. "These came up the mountain road maybe twenty years ago. Good stock, that lot. Old extraction work — clean edges, no bleeding. Whoever did that work knew their craft."

Twenty years ago. Orsen did the arithmetic and felt sick. Twenty years ago, Orsen had been seven years old, and the valley had been whole, and their grandmother had been alive, and someone had stood in Maren Zhou's kitchen with a bowl and a needle and taken these things out of a child's head — or out of Maren's, Orsen realized with a fresh lurch, because some of the phials weren't the girl's memories at all. The tags said so. *Maren, age thirty-one, holding her daughter.* *Maren, age fifty-four, burying her husband.*

Someone had harvested a whole life. A child's life and a woman's life, both, and sold them piece by piece across a counter in a border town, and the pieces had ended up here, on blue velvet, priced at eight silver each.

"How much?" Orsen heard themselves say. Their hand was already moving toward their purse. Eight silver apiece, thirteen phials visible, more in the drawers beneath — the old woman was opening them now, sensing a buyer, laying out row after row. *Girl, age nine, first lie. Girl, age ten, the widow's house. Girl, age eleven—*

"Two hundred for the full lot," the old woman said. "They're estate goods, I can't go lower. There's a gentleman asked after them last month — collector, pays well for coherent sets. If you're haggling, I'd haggle fast."

"A collector," Orsen said. The word arrived cold. "What collector?"

"Fellow named Quill. Wears grey. Buys anything with the name Zhou attached, or the valley, or—" the old woman squinted at Orsen, really looked, for the first time, "—well. Now that I look at you. You've got her jaw."

The market noise seemed to fall away. Somewhere a vendor laughed; somewhere a bell rang the hour; somewhere Ilse lay fevered in a room above the Ledger & Lamp with a fragment of the Last Crown humming against her ribs, calling to its missing pieces, calling, Ilse had said, *to Orsen*.

"My grandmother," Orsen said carefully, "sold these?"

"Sold them, lost them, had them taken — depends who tells it." The old woman closed the drawer with a click that sounded final. "There was a man came through, back then. Grey coat. Paid the landlord directly, I heard. Bought the lot before it ever reached a case like mine — except she hid a few, I think. Sold those herself, quiet-like, to feed the girl." Her eyes flicked to the fan of phials on the velvet. "These are the hidden ones, see. Came back on the market when the buyer who had them died. Funny how things circle. Like water."

Like blood, Orsen thought. Like debt.

"I'll take them," they said. "All of them. Two hundred."

The old woman studied them a moment longer — the jaw, the eyes, whatever else in Orsen's face answered to a dead woman's name — and then, unexpectedly, she shook her head.

"No."

"No?"

"Not today." She began closing the case, sliding the velvet tray inward, covering the phials one by one like a mother tucking in children. "Come back tomorrow, dawn, before Quill's man makes his rounds. Bring two hundred and I'll forget your face. Today, if I sell to a Zhou out of an open case, half this market hears of it by noon, and grey coats travel fast when they hear that name." She looked up, and her walnut-shell face was suddenly, terribly kind. "Your grandmother sold me a memory once, girl. Winter before she left. I never asked what it was. I never watched it. But she told me — *if anyone with my blood comes looking, hold the rest for them.*"

She pushed the case fully shut. The latch fell with a small sound like a door closing on a tomb.

"Dawn," she said. "Don't be late, and don't be followed."

* * *

Orsen bought the honey. They bought the willow bark. They walked back to the inn with their hands steady and their mind on fire, past stalls selling other people's childhoods by the inch, past a grey-coated man at the square's edge who stood very still and watched the north-corner stall with the patience of a man being paid to watch it.

At the inn, Ilse was sitting up. Fever-bright, sweating, but upright, and the moment Orsen came through the door she read their face the way Orsen read a client's — instantly, completely, and without mercy.

"You found something."

"I found me," Orsen said. They set the willow bark on the table. "Thirteen phials of it. On sale. Eight silver apiece."

Ilse went very still. Even the shard seemed to quiet against her chest, listening. "Your memories. From the valley. Someone extracted them—"

"When I was a child. Before the wards finished settling, I think. Before I could have known or consented or even remembered it being done — because they took the remembering too." Orsen sat down heavily on the edge of the cot. The mattress ropes creaked. "She hid some. Sold a few to feed me, the vendor says. Hid the rest. Left word they should come back to me." They laughed, and it came out ugly. "My own trade. My whole life, I've sold people back their worst days and told myself it was mercy. And my grandmother was doing the same thing in reverse — selling the best of me to keep the worst from someone's hands. Keeping the *thirteenth stone* from someone's hands." They looked up. "Ilse. One of those phials is tagged 'girl, age six, the thirteenth stone.' I touched the case and I remembered standing in front of it. My grandmother's voice saying it mattered most. Saying *remember that I said so*."

Ilse's breath caught. "The vault," she whispered. "The Crown was split into fragments. Thirteen of them, some say. Hidden where no one would think to look." Her eyes went, involuntarily, north — toward the mountains, toward the valley, toward the great grey stone Orsen had touched once as a child and never thought of again until tonight. "She didn't just hide the Crown, Orsen. She hid it *inside the wards*. Your valley isn't protected by accident. It's protected by design. The ward-stones and the shards are—"

"The same thing," Orsen finished. The words tasted like river water and chalk dust and cinnamon. "I know. I'm starting to know."

Outside, dusk was coming down over Cindral, and the lanterns were lighting one by one, and at the north corner of the square the grey-coated man had finally moved — was walking, unhurried, toward the old woman's stall.

Orsen was on their feet before they'd decided to stand.

"Dawn," they said. "She said dawn. But he's not waiting for dawn."

* * *

The market at night was a different animal — fewer buyers, more sellers, the lanterns turned low and amber, deals happening in shadows between stalls. Orsen moved through it the way they'd moved through Hollowmere's back lanes for fifteen years: unremarkable, unhurried, a shadow among shadows. Behind them, Tomas kept pace with the silent competence of a man who had carried contraband cider since he was twelve. Ilse had insisted on coming, fever or no fever, shard or no shard, wrapped in Tomas's spare coat with her hand pressed to her bandaged side.

The old woman's stall was shuttered and dark. The grey-coated man stood before it, knocking — politely, patiently, the knock of a man who has never once needed to break a door.

Then he stopped knocking. He cocked his head. And slowly, deliberately, he turned and looked directly across the market square at Orsen.

He smiled.

In his hand, catching the lantern light, glinted a long steel instrument Orsen knew intimately — the extraction needle's larger cousin, the harvest-spoon, the tool collectors used when a whole head was worth more than a single memory.

"Well," Ilse breathed beside them, and the shard against her chest began, softly, unmistakably, to sing.
