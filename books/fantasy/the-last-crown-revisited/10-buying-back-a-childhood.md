## 10. Buying Back a Childhood

The vendor's stall smelled of cedar oil and old paper, the way every memory did once it had been properly cured. Orsen stood in the crowd outside Cindral's Memory Market and stared at the glass case that held their own life.

*Orsen Zhou, age six. First snowfall in Hollowmere. The taste of honey bread. A woman's hands braiding hair.*

Their name. Their handwriting on the little parchment labels, because vendors always copied the seller's own descriptions — and Orsen had written those descriptions themselves, three years ago, when they'd sold the lot to a traveling broker for enough coin to fix the roof. They hadn't known where the broker was headed. They hadn't cared. That was the trade: you took what people gave up and you didn't ask where it went.

They had never imagined standing on the other side of the glass.

* * *

Cindral was everything Hollowmere pretended not to be. Where Hollowmere kept its forgetting quiet — a whispered appointment, an envelope of coins left under a loose flagstone — Cindral shouted it from awnings and painted signs. MEMORY, FAIRLY PRICED. GENTLE EXTRACTION, LICENSED. WE BUY PAIN. The border town sat just past the valley's edge, close enough to serve the ward-protected towns that wanted their griefs lifted and far enough that no one asked too loudly why the valley folk came down in such numbers, year after year, with pockets full of silver and eyes full of things they couldn't wait to lose.

Orsen knew this town. Had walked these stalls a dozen times with a satchel of other people's worst days, selling wholesale to brokers who never blinked. It had felt like honest work. Clean work. You didn't take anything anyone wasn't desperate to be rid of.

Now, looking at their six-year-old self sealed behind glass like a beetle in amber, *clean* seemed like a word someone else had invented.

"You're staring hard enough to crack the case," Tomas murmured beside them. He'd pulled his hood low and affected the slouch of a laborer, which fooled no one; he stood out in Cindral the way a church bell stands out in a barnyard. "Is that—"

"Mine." The word came out flat. "That one's mine."

Ilse drifted closer, keeping her bandaged hand tucked inside her coat. In daylight, without the panic and the blood, she looked younger than Orsen had first thought — mid-twenties maybe, with the kind of tiredness that sleep doesn't touch. Her eyes went to the case, then to Orsen, and something in her expression shifted. Recognition, almost. The look of someone seeing a lock turn inside a person's face.

"The whole tray," she said quietly. "Those are all yours."

"Three years ago I sold eleven memories to a broker named Dannel Vos. Winter stock. Nobody buys much in winter, so I let go of the warm ones." Orsen heard their own voice and hated how reasonable it sounded. "I needed the money for the roof."

"For the *roof*," Tomas repeated.

"It was a bad winter." It sounded pathetic now. It had sounded practical then. That was the trick of the trade, Orsen realized — the trick they'd been running on themselves as much as on anyone else. Every extraction felt necessary at the time. Every loss felt light until you saw it laid out in a stranger's shop with a price tag.

The vendor, a soft-bellied man with ink-stained fingers, noticed their attention and brightened. "Fine stock, that tray. Winter memories keep beautifully. The honey bread one is my best seller — everyone wants to remember tasting something good." He tapped the glass above the smallest vial. "Two crowns, and I'll throw in the snowfall."

Orsen looked at the vials. Eleven of them, each holding a curl of silver-grey light that moved when you weren't watching directly. Each one was them. Each one had been *taken* by them — by their own hands, their own needle-craft, their own steady professional breath. Somewhere in Hollowmere right now were dozens more people walking around lighter than they should be, carrying gaps shaped like Orsen.

"We can't buy them," Ilse said under her breath. "We spent nearly everything getting here, and if Reye's enforcers are asking questions at the gates—"

"They will be," Orsen said. "Reye travels with a ledger. Ledgers get read to gate guards."

"So we steal them," Tomas said.

Orsen turned. Tomas had been their oldest friend since before either of them could name the feeling, and in all those years Orsen had never once seen him suggest theft with a straight face. His jaw was set. His ears were red.

"You hate stealing," Orsen said.

"I hate *you* being sold." He said it simply, the way he said true things, and then seemed embarrassed by it and studied the cobblestones.

* * *

They watched the stall through the afternoon, the way Orsen had once watched grieving widows' houses through the evening. Old habits. The vendor — his sign said MERRO — closed at dusk, but he left his cases locked inside a rolling chest that went with him to a room above the Feathered Goose, and the window of that room faced the stable yard, and the stable yard had a wall a child could climb.

"I'll do it," Ilse said.

"No."

"You don't trust me."

"I don't trust anyone near a case of my childhood with mage-hands." Orsen flexed their fingers, feeling the old calluses from the extractor's needle. "Besides, this is a thief's job. And tonight—" they exhaled, "—tonight I'm robbing myself. Seems only fair somebody does."

Tomas insisted on coming as far as the wall. Ilse insisted on watching the street. Orsen climbed alone, in the dark, with the moonless sky pressing down like a lid — because there was no moon now, none anywhere; the night since the stars went out had been one long unbroken dark, and Cindral's lamps burned all hours against it, and nobody slept well, and everybody talked about the sky in the hushed voice people used for plagues.

The window latch gave to a thin blade. Merro snored like a landslide. The chest's lock was better work — two tumblers and a false gate — but locks were the other half of Orsen's education, the half nobody paid for. It opened in ninety heartbeats.

Eleven vials, cool in their padded slots. Orsen lifted them out and, because they were who they were, left coins. Not the asking price. More. It made no sense and they did it anyway, arranging the crowns neatly where the vials had been, and only understood while climbing back over the wall that they were doing what they'd done on every door in Hollowmere the night they fled. Guilt made tangible. Payment left where payment was owed.

Some debts, though, couldn't be settled with coins on a sill.

* * *

They didn't open them in the room. Too many walls with ears in Cindral, Ilse said, and Orsen believed her — believed everything she said about listening lately, because she flinched at sounds the rest of them couldn't hear, some mage-sense still tuned to pursuit. So they walked out past the last lamps, up the scrubby hill north of town where the grass grew wild over the ruins of an older road, and there, under the starless black, Orsen sat with eleven small deaths in their lap.

"You don't have to take them all at once," Tomas said.

"If I take them one at a time I'll stop. I'll tell myself it hurts too much, come back tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow." Orsen uncorked the first vial. "I've met me. I know how I am."

Memory returning was nothing like memory leaving. Leaving was a cold thread behind the eye and then a smooth blankness, almost pleasant, like a field after harvest. Returning was a *flood*. The first vial hit them like falling through ice — snow, real snow, fat flakes catching in a red scarf, and the taste of honey bread so vivid their mouth flooded and their eyes stung, and their grandmother's laugh—

Grandmother.

The second vial: braiding hair. Big hands, knuckles scarred, working strands of Orsen's hair with absurd gentleness. A voice humming a tune with no name. *Hold still, little wren, or you'll wear more knots than the rope on our door.* The smell of the workshop — stone dust and lamp oil and something underneath, something metallic and old, like a key kept too long in a fist.

The third. The fourth. By the fifth Orsen had stopped trying to stay upright and lay in the wet grass, gasping, while their childhood poured back into them and rearranged the furniture of who they were. Tomas crouched beside them saying their name. Ilse stood apart, very still, watching with an intensity that would have frightened Orsen if Orsen had been capable of noticing anything beyond the flood.

Because the memories weren't what they'd sold.

When Orsen had extracted them — professionally, cleanly, the way they'd been taught — they'd taken the surface of each moment. The snow. The bread. The braid. But a memory extracted whole keeps its roots, and roots go deep, and now that the memories were home again the roots came with them, dragging up everything the extraction had cut away.

And the roots all led to Grandmother.

Not the grandmother of the public story — not Maren Zhou, the widow who'd ended the war, the woman whose name Hollowmere spoke softly the way you speak of weather that killed someone once. The private one. The one who worked late in a cellar Orsen had never been allowed into. The one whose scarred knuckles Orsen suddenly remembered were scarred from *burns*, from gripping something hot, something that sang. The one who had sat seven-year-old Orsen down on the workshop steps and held their face in those burned hands and said:

*"Listen to me, little wren. One day someone will come asking about stones. Thirteen stones in a ring, and one of them different from the others. If I'm gone, and someone comes asking — you run. You don't ask what they want. You run, and you don't stop until the valley is behind you."*

Seven years old, and terrified, and obedient — and then, because children are built to survive their own terror, Orsen had *forgotten*. Not naturally. They remembered now the sensation of choosing to forget, or rather of letting Grandmother choose for them, her thumb cool against their temple, a whisper in a language that tasted like metal: *sleep, wren, and wake lighter.*

She had erased herself from her own grandchild. Carefully. Lovingly. The way Orsen later learned to erase grief from widows.

"Oh," Orsen said aloud, to the dark, to no one. "Oh, you *liar*."

"What?" Tomas's grip on their shoulder. "What is it?"

"She knew." Orsen sat up. The hill tilted and steadied. "She knew this day would come. She *prepared* for it. She hid things in my head and then hid the hiding." The last vials waited, and Orsen drank them down like medicine, all four in quick succession, and the final flood was the worst because it was the deepest:

A cellar. Age nine, having followed a forbidden smell of singing metal down stairs that were supposed to be locked. Grandmother standing before a stone the size of a door, a cracked grey monolith veined with light, and pressed into a hollow at its center — a shape Orsen knew. Knew from Ilse's shard, from the fragment humming in the lining of Ilse's coat even now, thirty feet away on this same hill.

A piece of the Last Crown.

*"You shouldn't be here."* Not angry. Afraid. Grandmother had never once raised her voice at Orsen, and in this memory her voice shook. *"But you're here, so look. Look at it and remember, because I may need you to remember when I cannot."*

*"What is it?"*

*"It's the reason the war ended. And the reason it must never start again."* She had touched the stone the way you touch a sleeping animal. *"Every ruler who ever wore the Crown poured what they knew into it. Every secret, every betrayal, every grave they dug. It remembers them all, wren. And it has learned, across all those lifetimes, to want. Your grandfather thought we could cage that wanting. He built the wards from its broken pieces — thirteen stones, thirteen fragments, a ring of forgetting around a valley full of people who deserved to forget."*

*"Forget what?"*

Grandmother had smiled then, and the smile had been the loneliest thing nine-year-old Orsen had ever seen.

*"Me," she said. "It lets me forget me."*

* * *

Orsen came back to the hilltop on their hands and knees, shaking, grass-stained, weeping without any decision to weep.

Tomas was talking — had been talking for a while, probably. *"—Orsen, hey, breathe, you're scaring me, what did you see—"*

Ilse hadn't moved. She stood at the crest of the hill with the wind pulling at her coat, and her face in the lamp-glow from the town below wore an expression Orsen had last seen in the shard's vision: the look of a coronation witness, of someone watching power arrive where it wasn't expected.

"You saw the vault," Ilse said. Not a question.

"The thirteenth stone." Orsen wiped their face with a sleeve. "It's real. It's *hollow*, isn't it? There's a hollow in it, and something fits there." They got to their feet, unsteady, furious, grieving. "She was the Crown's bearer. The last one. She broke it herself — she and my grandfather — and she built her whole life in that valley around hiding the pieces, including hiding the truth inside her own family. Inside *me*. She carved up my childhood to keep me safe and never told me, never asked, just—" Orsen's voice cracked. "She treated me like one of her vaults."

"That's not all she treated you like." Ilse came down the slope slowly. "Think about your trade. Think about how good you are at it. Extraction isn't a common gift, Orsen. It runs in lines. It runs in *blood*."

The words landed and kept landing. Orsen thought of their hands, steady around a needle since they were fifteen. Thought of how naturally the craft had come, how the neighbors said *she has a gift, Maren's grandchild has the gift*, and how everyone laughed because everyone assumed the gift was a joke — the war-hero's heir, taking out trash-memories for pocket change.

It wasn't a joke. It was an inheritance. The Crown's bearer's blood, learning to handle memory the way the Crown itself did — pouring it out, sealing it away, hoarding what others surrendered.

"She trained me without training me," Orsen whispered. "She left me the tools and buried the blueprint."

"And the Crown knows," Ilse said. "That's what I came down the hill to tell you, and I'd hoped — I'd *hoped* to say it gently, later, when you'd slept. The shard wakes a little more each day. It hums louder when you're near. When you touched it in your house, it wasn't showing you visions, Orsen. It was *recognizing* you. It's been calling since the wards fell, and it isn't calling for a thief, or a fugitive, or a hero." She stopped an arm's length away. In the dark her eyes caught the distant lamplight and held it. "It's calling for its bearer's blood. For the only line that ever mastered it."

The starless sky pressed down. Below, Cindral glowed like a bed of coals, ten thousand people sleeping off their fears, and somewhere among them, Orsen knew with cold certainty, Captain Reye was reading names from a ledger — and had reached, by now, the name Maren Zhou.

Orsen closed their fist around the empty vials until the glass bit.

"Then we'd better move," they said, "before it stops calling and starts *taking*."
