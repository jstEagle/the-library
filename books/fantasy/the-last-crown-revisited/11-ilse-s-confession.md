## 11. Ilse's Confession

Ilse had counted the cracks in Orsen Zhou's ceiling forty-one times before she decided to tell the truth.

She knew them all now. The long fissure above the hearth that branched like a river delta. The hairline seam that ran from the window to the corner, thin as a spider's thread. The place where plaster had bulged and never been smoothed, casting a shadow shaped like a sleeping animal when the fire was low. She had memorized this ceiling the way she'd once memorized the constellations of the outer world—back when there were stars to memorize—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, that she could not spend another night in this house carrying what she carried.

The shard sat on the table between them, wrapped in three layers of cloth, humming.

It always hummed. She had thought at first it was her own blood she could hear, her own fear translated into sound by an exhausted mind. But no. Three days in this valley, three days of Orsen bringing her broth and checking the bandages on her side and asking careful, gentle questions that were really interrogations, and the hum had not stopped once. It grew louder at night. It grew louder whenever Orsen came near.

That was the part she couldn't explain away anymore. That was the part that had finally broken her silence.

* * *

Orsen was awake. Of course they were. Ilse had learned that Orsen Zhou slept the way a knife sleeps—lightly, and only because sharpening took effort. They sat across from her in the small kitchen with a cup of something bitter steaming between their hands, watching her with those flat, patient eyes that gave away nothing. A memory-thief's eyes. Eyes that had looked into hundreds of minds and learned to keep their own face still.

"You're going to do it tonight," Orsen said. Not a question.

"Do what?"

"Whatever you've been circling for three days. You look like a woman standing on a bridge deciding whether to jump." They sipped their drink. "I've watched people make that decision before. I know the face."

Ilse almost laughed. Almost. "You watch everyone that closely?"

"It's how I stay alive."

"And how does that work out for you? Staying alive?" She gestured weakly at the shuttered windows, at the darkness beyond where stars should have been and weren't. "The wards are dead, Orsen. The sky is empty. Whatever you were alive *for* — I don't think it applies anymore."

Something flickered across Orsen's face. Not anger. Something older and more tired than anger. "No," they admitted. "I don't suppose it does."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and went silent, as if even the animals of Hollowmere understood that loudness was dangerous now. Ilse pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders—the wound in her side ached dully, a hot coin pressed against her ribs—and made herself look at the wrapped shape on the table instead of the ceiling.

"I stole it," she said.

Orsen didn't move. Didn't blink. But Ilse had spent two years learning to read the silences of frightened people, and she saw the way the room seemed to lean in around them both.

"The shard," she continued. "From the enforcers. I wasn't fleeing some random purge, some border skirmish. I was inside Vaskar Hold. The remnant's fortress in the eastern passes." She swallowed. "I was one of them, Orsen. Before I ran. I wore the gray-and-gold. I kept their ledgers."

* * *

The lie of it—that she'd been merely a victim—had been eating her since the night she collapsed at Orsen's door. So she told all of it now, in order, the way you pull a splinter: slowly, and all at once.

Her name had been different then, or rather, her name had been nothing at all. Enforcers of the Crown's remnant surrendered their names when they took the oath; they became designations. She had been Nine-of-the-Eastern-Table, a junior archivist, which meant she catalogued what the remnant confiscated. And what the remnant confiscated, more than anything else, was memory.

"They don't just hunt mages," she said. "They hunt *remembering*. Anyone who touched Crown-magic, anyone who might have inherited it, anyone who dreamed too vividly about the old kings. They extract first and ask questions after. There are rooms in Vaskar Hold lined floor to ceiling with vessels—glass, crystal, hollowed stone—each one holding a life. Whole childhoods. Whole marriages. Griefs that belonged to someone once." Her voice caught. "I shelved them. Alphabetized grief. For six years."

"Why?" Orsen asked quietly. "Why does the remnant want memories?"

"Because the Crown wants memories. The remnant serves the Crown—or what's left of it, or what it's becoming, I honestly don't know if there's a difference anymore. The Last Crown holds every ruler who ever wore it. Every memory, every decision, every sin. That's its power. It doesn't just confer authority—it confers *certainty*. Put it on your head and you suddenly know how to win wars, how to rule provinces, how to break rebellions, because thirty kings' worth of doing exactly those things lives behind your eyes." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what the training manuals called it? *The Inheritance.* We were taught to revere it. To guard it. To believe that whoever bore it last died protecting us all."

"But someone didn't," Orsen said.

"No. Someone hid it. In this valley, as far as I can piece together—which we'll come back to, because that's the other thing I have to confess." She steadied herself against the table. "Two months ago I was assigned to a transfer detail. A fragment of the Crown had been recovered from a ruin in the north—buried, sealed, and the seal was old enough that whoever built it predated the remnant itself. Protocol said the fragment went to Vaskar for study. Four enforcers and an archivist. Me. I rode in the last wagon with the reliquary case, and for three hundred miles, Orsen, I listened to it hum."

"The way it hums now."

"Worse. Louder, every mile closer to home." She unwrapped one corner of the cloth, just enough to see the sliver of dark metal beneath, veined with light that moved like slow blood. "And it wasn't just sound. It was—*attention*. Like being watched by something that had been asleep for centuries and found me interesting. On the fourth night I put my bare hand against the case to check the warding seals, and it showed me things. A coronation. A betrayal. A woman in a crown standing in a burning field, laughing, laughing—" She shut her eyes. "I decided somewhere on that road. I think I decided before I ever left Vaskar, and the road was just the coward's delay."

"What happened?"

"I drugged the other four's evening ration. Crude work—they trained us better—but I'd been planning in my head for so long that my hands shook from practice alone. Then I took the case, the fragment, and the fastest horse, and I ran west. Toward the border. Toward the one place in the world the remnant couldn't follow." She opened her eyes and met Orsen's gaze directly. "Toward the memoryless valley. Because I'd read the old reports, and I knew the wards here suppressed Crown-magic. I thought—I *hoped* —that inside your borders, the thing would go quiet. That I could bury it. Disappear. Start being a person again instead of a number."

"And did it go quiet?"

Ilse looked at the shard. At Orsen. At the space between them, which was exactly the width of a kitchen table and exactly the width of everything she hadn't yet said.

"No," she said. "And that's why you need to hear the rest."

* * *

She stood, because sitting made the telling feel too much like confession to a priest, and she had no priest, and she did not want absolution—she wanted a witness. She wanted someone else to carry the weight of knowing what she knew.

"Inside these wards, the fragment should be mute. Dormant. Dead weight. That's what the reports promised, and for the first day, it mostly was. But then—" She flexed her bandaged hand. "Then it started reaching again. Faintly. Like a voice through a wall. And I noticed something I should have noticed on the wagon, but I was half-dead and half-mad and I missed it. The hum changes when you're near."

Orsen went very still.

"Not when Tomas visits. Not when the alderman's people come knocking. When *you* cross the room, the pitch rises. When you sleep in the next chamber, I can feel it through the wall, straining toward you like a dog at the end of a chain." She let the words land. "Three nights ago I tested it. I held the shard and watched you walk past the doorway. Orsen—it *lit up*. The veins in the metal moved. It turned toward you the way a flower turns toward sun."

"That's impossible," Orsen said. Flatly. Automatically. And then, worse, quieter: "Unless."

"Unless," Ilse agreed. "Unless there's a reason the Crown's fragment knows you. Unless there's a reason you—a memory-thief in a hidden valley, of all the trades in all the towns in the world—can touch it without screaming. I've seen enforcer handlers wear gloves of warded leather just to transport the thing. You picked it up with your bare hands on the night I arrived and your skin didn't blister."

"How do you know I didn't scream?" Orsen's voice had gone carefully light, and Ilse recognized the technique because she'd used it herself in interrogation briefings: deflect with a joke, buy time, retreat. But there was a crack under it. She'd seen the same crack three days ago, in the first hour, when Orsen had touched the shard and gone white as bone and muttered something about a child in a green coat.

"Because I was watching," Ilse said gently. "Because I've spent six years studying what Crown-magic does to people, and I know recognition when I see it. That shard didn't hurt you, Orsen. It *greeted* you."

Silence. The fire settled. Outside, Hollowmere lay dark and starless, thirteen stones' worth of protection gone, and Ilse watched the truth arrive in Orsen Zhou's face like weather crossing a plain—denial first, then calculation, then fear, then something underneath all of it that looked almost like relief, which frightened her more than any of the rest.

"My grandmother," Orsen said at last. The words came out stiff, dredged from somewhere deep. "Maren Zhou. She raised me. She was—strange. Strict about certain things. She never let me leave the valley. Never let me ask about the outside. And she had this habit, when she thought I wasn't looking, of touching the doorframe of our house. All four corners. Every time she passed. Like she was checking a ward." Their jaw tightened. "When I was eight, she caught me with a shard of green glass I'd found in the creek bed. She burned it in the stove and then she extracted the memory of finding it—herself, by hand, no payment, no consent. I woke up the next morning with a hole in my head and a feeling like I'd lost a word I needed. I hated her for years for that. I became *this*—" a bitter gesture at the room, the trade, the whole hollow life, "—partly to spite her. Taking memories apart. Learning how they're stitched. Proving nobody had the right."

"And now?"

"Now I'm wondering whether she wasn't hiding something in me. Whether the hole she cut wasn't cruelty but surgery." Orsen's hands had begun to tremble, very slightly, and they clenched them to stop it. "You said the fragment calls to its missing pieces."

"It does. I'm sure of it now. It's waking, Orsen—waking faster every day the wards stay dead. And it's calling for two things. Its siblings—the other fragments, wherever your grandmother scattered them." Ilse paused. "And its bearer's line. Whoever hid it. Whoever was meant to keep it safe. Whoever's blood remembers what the rest of us were forced to forget."

"Say it plainly."

"I think Maren Zhou was the Crown's final bearer. I think she broke it and hid the pieces here. And I think she cut pieces out of her own grandchild's memory to keep the Crown from finding its way home through you." Ilse's voice dropped. "And I think the wards falling wasn't an accident. I think the Crown has been looking for this valley for sixty years, and the moment it woke up hungry enough, it reached out and broke the lock."

The cup in Orsen's hands rattled against the saucer. They set it down with exaggerated care, the way people do when precision is all that's holding them together.

"You realize what you're saying," Orsen said. "You're saying I'm not a thief who happened to find a fugitive. You're saying I'm the destination. That everything that's coming—enforcers, hunters, whatever broke the sky—is coming *here*, for *me*, and it's coming because of a war my grandmother started and a secret she buried in my skull without my permission." A short, terrible laugh. "She stole from me first. Before I ever stole from anyone. Maybe that's where I learned it."

"Orsen—"

"No. You confessed. Let me." They stood and walked to the window, and Ilse saw their reflection in the black glass—young and old at once, lit by firelight on one side and nothing on the other. "I've taken hundreds of memories. Hundreds. I told myself it was mercy. People *paid* me to forget. The widow Marta paid me to forget her husband dying, and I took her gold and told myself the grief was hers to spend however she liked. But you've just described a machine that eats remembering. Rooms full of shelved lives. An empire built on holes in people's heads. And I have to sit here and admit that I built myself a small, tidy version of it right here in Hollowmere, and called it a living."

"That's not the same thing."

"Isn't it? You alphabetized grief in a fortress. I priced it by the vial in a kitchen. We both made forgetting into a trade." Orsen turned from the window, and their eyes were wet and furious and more alive than Ilse had yet seen them. "So here's my confession to match yours, mage: when I touched your stolen shard and saw that child in the green coat, I recognized her. Not remembered—*recognized*. The way you recognize your own handwriting. And I've spent three days pretending I didn't, because the alternative is admitting that the most important thing about me has been missing since I was eight years old, and my grandmother took it, and I can't get it back because I sold the tools of my own trade to every desperate soul in this valley and none of them were selling."

The room rang with it. Ilse found she was shaking—not from the wound, not from fear, but from the strange, awful grace of being believed. She had rehearsed this confession for three days and imagined every outcome: exile, betrayal, a knife in the dark. She had not imagined this. She had not imagined Orsen Zhou meeting her guilt with guilt of their own and calling it even.

"Then we get it back," she said.

"How? The extraction's done. You can't un-cut a memory any more than you can un-ring—"

"You can't," Ilse agreed. "But the Crown can." She laid her hand flat on the table, beside the wrapped fragment, not touching it. "That's what the makers built it for, before it curdled into what the remnant worships. A vessel for keeping. Everything it ever held is still in it, Orsen. Every memory it hoarded, every memory its bearers fed it—including, I'd wager, whatever your grandmother fed it before she broke it. Including, possibly, the piece of you she carved out and locked away. The fragment isn't just calling to you. It may be calling back something of yours."

Orsen stared at her. "You want me to use it."

"I want you to understand that we don't have a choice. The remnant knows the fragment crossed the border—they'll have tracked the wagon, they'll have hanged my four colleagues' ghosts over the failure, and Captain Reye does not lose things. Enforcers will reach this valley within days. When they do, they won't just want the shard. They'll want the thief who took it and the bloodline that hid it. Your grandmother's name will be in their ledger, Orsen. Which means *yours* is." She held their gaze. "We can run, and run badly, with a hole in your head and a compass in mine. Or we can find out what you actually are before they arrive—and I promise you, whatever's in your missing years is worth more to us right now than an army."

For a long moment Orsen said nothing. Then, slowly, they crossed the room and sat back down at the table, across from the humming, wrapped thing, and Ilse watched them look at it the way a person looks at a locked door they've walked past their whole life without knowing it was theirs.

"If I open that," Orsen said quietly, "and I find out my grandmother was a monster—or that *I'm* something monstrous—"

"Then you'll know," Ilse said. "That's more than either of us had yesterday."

Orsen's hand hovered over the cloth. The hum swelled, soft and insistent, a tuning fork struck against the bones of the world. And in the silence between heartbeats, Ilse heard it speak—not in words, never in words, but in that wordless pressure of attention she'd felt on the road west:

*Closer. Closer. Come home.*

Outside, far off toward the valley's edge, a sound rolled through the dark—deep, rhythmic, deliberate. Hooves. Many of them, moving fast along the border road.

Orsen's hand froze midair. Their eyes met Ilse's, and neither of them said what they were both already thinking:

They had run out of time to decide.
