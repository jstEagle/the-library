## 12. The Puppet Alderman

Captain Reye had learned long ago that the crown's true power was not in its soldiers. It was in its paper.

She sat in the command tent outside Cindral with the Ledger open on her knees, its vellum pages soft as skin from a century of handling. The ink never faded. That was the first thing every new enforcer noticed and the last thing they understood: the Ledger wrote itself. Names appeared in a hand no living scribe could imitate, each entry arriving the moment someone touched Crown-magic—willingly or otherwise. Reye had watched names bloom onto the page mid-sentence, watched them darken when their owners drew closer to the shards, watched three of them go grey over the years, which meant dead, which meant the Ledger kept accounts even past the grave.

There were four hundred and eleven names in it now. She had read every one. Twice.

The tent flap lifted and Sergeant Corvane ducked in, bringing the smell of woodsmoke and wet horse with him. He was young for the rank, ambitious in the quiet way Reye tolerated, and he carried her supper on a tin tray as if the gesture were routine rather than surveillance. Everyone in the remnant watched everyone else. It was how the crown survived its own shrinking.

"Report," she said, not looking up.

"Cindral's gates are open. Market town, no walls worth the name. The ward-refugees have been coming through for six days—Hollowmere folk mostly, and some from the eastern hollows." He set the tray down. "And the border wards are confirmed dead, Captain. All thirteen stones silent. Survey team reached the thirteenth this morning."

Reye turned a page. "And?"

"The stone's cracked. Has been for years, by the look of it." Corvane hesitated. "It's also empty."

That made her look up. "Empty?"

"Hollow, sir. There's a cavity inside, old workmanship, and scorch marks where something was sealed and then removed. Recently removed, if you ask me. Within the month."

Within the month. Reye closed the Ledger and let her hand rest on its cover, feeling the faint warmth there, like a sleeping animal. The fragment Ilse Varn had stolen from the vault at Karst was one piece. The survey suggested there had been another, hidden in the valley itself—which meant the woman who had hidden it, the one whose name sat at the top of the Ledger's second page in letters darker than any others, had been more than a hiding place. She had been an architect.

*Maren Zhou.* Dead eleven years, but the Ledger did not care. Her name was still the darkest thing in it.

"And the targets?" Reye asked.

Corvane produced his notebook. "Varn entered Cindral two nights ago, seen at the Lantern Gate with a local man matching Zhou's description and a third, unidentified, heavyset, valley accent. They went into the undercity. My informants lost them below the Tannery Row."

"Informants," Reye said, "or informant."

Corvane's jaw tightened. He knew what she knew. "One informant," he admitted. "Reliable. Long-standing."

"Alderman Vess."

* * *

Reye walked the camp after dark, because she always did, because troops trusted a captain they saw in the cold more than one they heard in the warm. Two hundred enforcers remained of the nine hundred the crown had once fielded. Their armor was patched, their pay three months in arrears, their orders coming from a regency council that met behind locked doors in a capital most of them had never seen. They were the remnant of a remnant, guarding the memory of a king who had been dust for sixty years—and yet the Ledger stayed warm, and the shards called to each other across the miles like tuning forks, and so the work continued.

At the edge of camp, under the single lamp permitted near the horse lines, sat the man himself.

Alderman Oren Vess of Hollowmere had traveled three days to meet her, wrapped in a good coat gone shiny at the elbows, and he looked precisely like what he was: a small official grown fat on small authority, terrified of everything larger. He rose when she approached, then seemed unsure whether rising had been correct, and half-sat again before committing fully to standing.

"Captain Reye." His voice was dry as chaff. "I trust—the council hopes—I trust the situation is being handled with discretion."

"Sit down, Alderman."

He sat. She remained standing, which was a language he understood fluently.

"You've served the crown a long time," Reye said. "Thirty-one years as alderman. Before that, a tax assessor. Before that—" she let the pause do the work— "well. The Ledger remembers further back than I do."

"I've done nothing but protect my town." The words came fast, rehearsed. "When Maren Zhou brought her dangerous cargo into our valley, when she built those wards and filled people's heads with ideas about forgetting, someone had to keep the crown informed. Someone had to be loyal. If I hadn't sent the lists—"

"The lists." Reye crouched, bringing her eyes level with his, and enjoyed the way he flinched. "Let's talk about the lists, Alderman. Every household in Hollowmere. Every name, every age, every trade. Who could extract memory, who had ever left the valley, who complained about the wards. Thirty-one years of correspondence, folded into grain shipments and tax returns. Do you know what we call that kind of service, in the remnant?"

"I—"

"We call it *feeding*." She stood. "You fed us your town, Alderman, one name at a time, and in exchange we let your little valley stay invisible, and you got to be the biggest man in a town that didn't know it was being farmed."

Vess's face had gone the color of tallow. "The agreement was protection—"

"The agreement was *silence*." Reye let the word fall between them like a coin on stone. "Your town forgot its own history because we paid you to help us make them forget. And now the wards are dead, the stars are out, and a thief is running through your streets carrying a piece of the Last Crown, and you've come crawling to my tent asking whether the situation is being handled with *discretion*."

"They say she has a shard," Vess whispered. "Is it true? Is it true about the Crown?"

"It's true." Reye watched him absorb it, watched the fear rearrange itself into something worse: appetite. She had seen that transformation before, in better men. "Which brings us to why you're actually here. You didn't walk three days through bandit country for reassurance. What do you want, Oren?"

He wrung his hands. When he spoke, it came out in a rush, the way confessions do. "The council wants the stranger surrendered. The town's frightened—stars gone, wards dead, and now strangers in the night. I can deliver her. I can deliver all three of them. But I want assurances. For me. For my position. When order is restored—"

"When order is restored," Reye said, "there will be no positions. Not yours, not mine. There will be the Crown, and whoever wears it, and everyone else will be exactly what they were before, except they'll remember why they should kneel." She turned away, done with him, then paused. "But yes, Alderman. Bring me the thief and her companions, and the remnant will remember who fed it. We always remember. It's rather our specialty."

Behind her she heard him exhale, heard the small wet sound of a man swallowing his own conscience whole, and thought, not for the first time: *the crown never needed armies. It needed men like this, in every town, in every generation, hungry enough to sell their neighbors and grateful for the chance.*

* * *

The message from Vess came at midnight, passed through the same grain-merchant channel he'd used for thirty-one years, and it was beautifully specific. Three travelers, undercity, Tannery Row, sheltering with the strays. A mute mapmaker. Twin forgers. An old soldier named Bren—Reye's pen stopped over that name—Bren Halloway, Eleventh Memory Regiment, listed in the Ledger since the purge at Redford Ford.

So the old soldier had found them. Of course he had. Halloway had spent fifteen years looking for the officer who erased his unit, and now he'd attached himself to the very fugitives that officer was hunting. The world arranged itself into these ironies if you paid attention, and Reye had made a career of paying attention.

She stood over the campaign table while Corvane and her other officers bent around it, and moved wooden markers across the map of Cindral with two fingers.

"Undercity entrances," she said. "There are seven. The Lantern Stair, the Tannery gate, the culvert under the granary, and four others the locals think we don't know about. We know about all of them—we built half of them, back when Cindral was a garrison town. Corvane, take forty men and seal the four minor entrances tonight. Quietly. No torches, no challenge-cries. If anyone comes out, follow, don't take."

"And the major entrances, Captain?"

"Left open." She set a marker on the Lantern Stair. "Seal them at dawn. You don't corner rats in their own warren—you close the doors and wait for them to come up for air. They need food, they need news, and above all they need to move south toward Vael. Every road south runs through the market square."

Lieutenant Ashka, grey-haired and skeptical, frowned at the map. "And if they don't run? If they dig in with the strays?"

"Then we burn Tannery Row and sort the ashes." Reye said it flatly, without relish, and watched her officers accept it the way soldiers accept everything: as weather. "But they won't dig in. The Zhou woman is a courier by instinct—she moves, she pays her debts, she keeps moving. The Varn woman is wounded and getting worse; I've seen shard-fever before, and it doesn't improve underground. They'll run. Probably within two days."

She straightened. One marker remained in her hand, and after a moment she placed it herself, alone, at the southern edge of the map: the river ford, twelve miles south of Cindral, the only crossing deep enough to matter this time of year.

"If I were a thief with a relic and a dying mage," she said quietly, mostly to herself, "that's the road I'd take."

Ashka caught it. "You want the ford held."

"I want the ford *watched*. Nothing taken, nothing given away. I want to know the moment they touch that water." Reye picked up the Ledger from its stand, and felt it warm against her palms, warmer than it had been in weeks, warming as the fragments drew together somewhere in the dark beneath Cindral. "Because when they cross, we won't be chasing thieves anymore. We'll be escorting the Crown home."

* * *

She slept badly, which was unusual, and woke before the horn, which was not, and sat in the tent's lamplight with the Ledger open to the newest page.

Three fresh names had bloomed overnight in that handwriting no scribe could imitate. *Sefa of the Ash Roads. Bren Halloway. Tomas Merrick of Hollowmere.* The strays, written into the account. And beneath them, older entries she had read a hundred times, the ones that had started all of this:

*Maren Zhou.* Dark as a bruise.
*Ilse Varn.* Darkening daily.
*Orsen Zhou.* The ink still wet, still spreading, darker this morning than it had been the night before.

Reye ran her thumb along that last line and felt the Ledger hum against her skin, faint and patient, like something breathing in its sleep. Grandmother, granddaughter, and a stolen Crown between them. Eleven years ago Maren Zhou had died with her secrets intact, and the remnant had assumed the hunt was over. Now her blood was carrying the pieces north to Vael, and every mile the ink grew darker, and Reye understood—with the cold clarity that had kept her alive through three purges and one mutiny—that she was not hunting a thief at all.

She was watching a coronation try to happen.

Outside, dawn came grey over Cindral's rooftops, and the first reports arrived from the sealed gates: movement below, lantern-light in the deep galleries, the strays scattering like startled birds. And one more report, from the granary culvert, delivered breathless by a corporal too junior to know how to soften bad news.

"Captain—a boy came up the stair just before we closed it. Local lad, said he was fetching medicine for his mother. We let him pass." The corporal swallowed. "He was carrying a message tube. Merchant seal. We couldn't stop him without breaking cover, and—"

"Whose merchant seal?" Reye was already reaching for her boots.

The corporal told her, and it took a moment for the name to land, and when it landed she laughed—one short, hard note with no humor in it at all.

Grain-merchant channel. Vess's channel. Which meant either the alderman had sent a second message she'd never see, or someone else had gotten hold of his seal, or—

Or the boy had been one of theirs. Orsen Zhou, the extraction-man, the invisible thief, walking straight up a sealed stair with a forged seal and a face like anyone's, buying his friends an hour with nothing but nerve.

"Corvane!" Her voice carried across the waking camp. "Saddle the horses. All of them. They're moving early, and they're smarter than we priced in." She buckled her sword-belt and took up the Ledger, warm as a heartbeat against her ribs, and stepped out into the grey light where two hundred riders were already turning toward the river.

Twelve miles to the ford. She intended to arrive before the water did.
