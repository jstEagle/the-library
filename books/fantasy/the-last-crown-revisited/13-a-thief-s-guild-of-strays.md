## 13. A Thief's Guild of Strays

Tomas had never been good at following. That was the whole joke of his life, really—Orsen was the one who'd learned to move through Hollowmere like smoke, quiet and unseen, and Tomas was the one who'd gotten caught at everything: stealing apples, kissing the miller's daughter, sneaking into the alderman's cellar the night Vess was elected. He was six feet of elbows and bad luck, a man whose footsteps announced him to every room he entered. So it figured that when they finally needed to disappear, he was the one who stuck out.

Cindral made that worse. The border town was all vertical—a canyon of leaning houses stacked three and four deep, bridges slung between rooftops, staircases that went up into nothing. Everyone in the upper city moved like they had somewhere to be. Everyone in the undercity moved like they were being followed. Tomas was both, and he was loud about it.

"Stop walking like that," Orsen muttered, not turning around.

"Like what?"

"Like you're sorry to be here."

"I *am* sorry to be here. This place smells like fish and regret."

"It smells like people who don't want to be found," Ilse said from behind them, her hood up, her left arm still bound where the enforcers' iron had burned her crossing the wards. "Which means we fit right in. Try to look grateful."

Tomas tried. He mostly managed to look like a man lost on his way to a funeral, which, in the undercity of Cindral, was apparently close enough to blend.

Orsen led them down a stair that wasn't on any map—Tomas knew because he'd checked the one map they had, a water-stained scrap Ilse had bought from a beggar with too many teeth—and through an archway choked with moss, into a courtyard where the sky was a distant silver coin at the bottom of a well of buildings. Laundry hung between the walls like the flags of a country that had never been named. Somewhere above, a fiddle played something slow and off-key, and nobody stopped it.

In the middle of the courtyard, a woman sat on an overturned crate, drawing.

She was maybe forty, broad-shouldered, with ink to her elbows and a leather strap of pens across her chest like a soldier's bandolier. She didn't look up when they entered. She was drawing the courtyard itself—the laundry, the archway, the three strangers who had just walked into it—and she drew them with a few fast strokes, quick and exact, before Tomas had even finished deciding whether to be nervous about it.

Then she looked up, and her eyes went straight past him to Orsen, and then to Ilse, and something in her face shifted. She set down her pen. She touched two fingers to her throat, then opened her hand flat toward the far wall, where a door stood half-hidden behind a rain barrel.

She didn't say a word. She never would, Tomas would learn. But her hands said plenty.

*You're the ones from the valley,* she signed, and Ilse, to Tomas's astonishment, signed back—clumsily, like a woman remembering a language she'd learned in a war. The woman's eyebrows rose. *You knew the hand-speech?*

*The army used it for silent orders,* Ilse signed. *I've forgotten most of it.*

*Then you'll relearn it,* the woman signed, and stood, and offered her ink-stained hand to Orsen. *My name is Sefa. I draw the way out of places. You look like people who need one.*

* * *

The door behind the rain barrel led down, and down, and down, into a cellar that had been dug out and dug out again until it was less a room than a warren—candlelit, warm, smelling of stew and lamp oil and the particular staleness of air that had been breathed by too many people for too long. There were cots along the walls. There were crates of forged documents stacked like firewood. There was a stove, and on the stove, a pot, and around the pot, people.

"Visitors," announced a young man with flour on his sleeves, looking up from a ledger. He had a twin—identical, down to the flour—seated across from him, copying a seal onto a passport with the steady hand of a surgeon.

"Refugees," the first twin corrected.

"Guests," said the second.

"Liabilities," muttered a voice from the corner, and that was the old soldier, though Tomas didn't know it yet. All he saw was a grey man with a grey beard sitting on a grey cot, sharpening a knife that was already sharp, watching them the way a man watches weather he doesn't trust.

"They're from Hollowmere," Sefa signed, and then, for the room's benefit, said it aloud in her low, cracked voice—she could speak, Tomas learned later, a single word at a time, hoarded like coins, spent only when signing wouldn't do. "Valley. Wards are down."

The room went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says the name of a dead person.

"The valley's real, then," said the flour twin. His brother elbowed him.

"It's real," Orsen said. "It was real right up until the stars went out."

"Stars went out all over," the old soldier said without looking up from his knife. "Whole sky. I was on the wall at Cindral when it happened. Forty years a soldier, and I never saw men break like that. Not from fighting. From *looking up*." He finally raised his eyes, grey as his beard, and fixed them on Ilse. "You're the mage. The one the criers are calling a crown-thief."

"Ilse," she said. "Yes. And no. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated," the soldier said. "That's how you know it's true. Lies are simple." He went back to his knife. But he shifted on the cot, just slightly, making room on the bench beside him, and Tomas understood that this was the man's whole heart laid open: suspicion and welcome, in the same motion, because he'd learned somewhere that you couldn't afford to feel only one of them.

The flour twin—his name was Perrin, and his brother's was Aldous, and they would spend the next three days refusing to tell Tomas which was which just to watch him flounder—shoved a bowl of stew into his hands before he'd even sat down. "Eat. You look like a man who's been walking on worry."

"I have been walking on worry."

"Then eat the worry. It's got barley in it."

Tomas ate. It was good stew. It was, in fact, the best thing he'd eaten since the stars vanished, and he was embarrassed by how much that mattered to him, and how his eyes stung a little over the steam, and how he covered it by blowing on the spoon like it was too hot when it wasn't.

Across the cellar, Orsen was watching him. His oldest friend, the person he'd known since they were both small enough to hide under the same blanket during thunderstorms, and Orsen's face did the thing it always did—the thing where all the sharp edges went soft for exactly one second before being hastily reassembled. *You're crying into your stew,* that face said.

*I am not,* Tomas's face said back.

*You absolutely are.*

*It's the barley.*

* * *

They stayed. That was the thing Tomas kept waiting to be decided—the staying—and it never was, exactly. Nobody voted on it. Nobody drew up terms. One night became two, two became five, and the cellar of strays simply made room, the way water makes room, the way a family makes room, without ever admitting that's what it was doing.

Tomas learned the shape of the place.

He learned that Sefa rose before anyone else and drew by candlelight—maps, always maps, the undercity, the border roads, the dead wards of the valley rendered in fine grey lines like veins on the back of a hand. She kept them rolled in oilcloth tubes labeled with small painted symbols instead of words, because, Aldous explained quietly, "paper burns, but nobody thinks to burn a picture of a bird." She communicated in a shorthand of gestures she'd invented with the twins, a language of flicks and points that could convey *the patrol changed its route, we move at dusk* in under two seconds. When Tomas asked her—through Ilse, translating—why she didn't speak, she looked at him for a long moment. Then she drew, fast, on a scrap: a mouth, and over it, a hand. And under that, in careful letters, because she saved her written words for what mattered: *They took the names. They didn't get the maps.*

He learned that Perrin forged documents and Aldous forged seals, and that the division was sacred, and that each believed the other's work was the harder one, and that they argued about it every single evening with the contentment of men who had never once in their lives been lonely. They had been turned out of a university in the capital for forging a diploma—for a *friend*, they insisted, a friend who couldn't afford one—and had drifted to Cindral the way driftwood drifts, and had discovered that a town full of people fleeing a collapsing order needed exactly what they could do. "Everyone here is someone else, on paper," Perrin told Tomas proudly. "It's the most honest place I've ever lived."

He learned that the old soldier was called Bren, that he'd served in the last years of the border garrisons, and that he drank his tea like it had personally wronged him. Bren spoke in short sentences and long silences, and Tomas, who was constitutionally incapable of silence, kept filling them, and Bren kept letting him, and somewhere in the third day Tomas realized that this was the old man's version of conversation: he *listened*, wholly, the way stone listens to rain. On the fourth evening, without preamble, Bren said: "Your valley. Your grandmother's generation built those wards. My unit helped carry the stones." He turned his cup. "We were told we were building a wall against a storm. We were building a hiding place. Took me thirty years to decide which one I'd helped make." He looked at Tomas. "Your friend with the shard. Is she running from the crown, or toward it?"

"Neither," Tomas said. "She's running *ahead* of it. There's a difference."

Bren considered this. "There is," he allowed. "It's a rare person who knows the difference about themselves." And he refilled Tomas's cup, which Tomas would later understand was the equivalent of a knighthood.

And he learned—this was the hardest one, the one he turned over at night on his cot between Aldous's snoring and the drip of the cellar's far wall—he learned what Orsen had done in Hollowmere. Not from Orsen. Orsen told him nothing; Orsen told everyone nothing; that was Orsen's whole architecture. He learned it from Ilse, on the fifth night, sitting on the cellar steps while the twins argued about wax and Sefa's pen scratched in the dark.

"The memory trade," Ilse said. "You knew?"

"I knew Orsen had a trade. Everyone in Hollowmere knew that. Nobody asked what it was. That's the valley for you—we were all so proud of not asking questions. Called it peace." Tomas turned his own cup, the way he'd seen Bren do. "What did they take? The people who paid?"

"Painful things. Grief, mostly. A widow came to them the day before everything broke. Paid to forget her husband's last words." Ilse's voice was flat, careful. "Orsen took the memory and took the coin and sent her home lighter. And I watched it happen, and I thought: *this is mercy*. And then I thought: *this is theft*. And I still don't know which one it was."

"Both," Tomas said. "It's both. That's Orsen. Everything they do is both." He laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob. "I used to think I was the bad one, you know. Orsen was the quiet one, the careful one, the good student. I was the one who got caught. And now the wards are down and the stars are gone and it turns out my careful, quiet, good friend has been robbing grief from widows for ten years, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't even know if I'm angry. That's the worst part. I'm not even angry. I'm just *sad*, and I don't know for who."

"For everyone," Ilse said. "That's what it's like, when someone you love turns out to be a whole person. You grieve the simple version."

Tomas sat with that for a while. Above them, faintly, the fiddle was playing again, the same slow off-key tune.

"Orsen's going to have to answer for it," he said finally. "Not to enforcers. To *them*. To the people. When the memories come back—Ilse, I've seen how Orsen looks at that shard. Like it's a mirror they can't put down. Whatever's in there, it's going to make them answer."

"Maybe that's why the shard chose them," Ilse said quietly. "Not the powerful. The accountable."

* * *

On the sixth night, the undercity threw a feast.

That was the word for it, anyway. In truth it was forty people in a cellar with a second pot of stew and a jug of plum brandy that Aldous produced from somewhere with the shameless flourish of a magician, but Tomas had been to grand feasts in Hollowmere—harvest feasts, warding-day feasts, the alderman's annual dinner where everyone sat stiff as cutlery—and none of them had ever felt like this. This felt like a fort. Like a fire in a rainstorm. Like the thing people build when the world has told them, in every voice it has, that they don't get to belong anywhere.

Perrin taught Tomas a drinking game involving forged passports and increasingly elaborate lies about the identities printed on them. Sefa sat in the corner and drew the whole party, and when Bren leaned over her shoulder to look, she turned the page and showed him instead a drawing she'd made days ago: the old soldier asleep on his cot, knife under his hand, and above him, drawn with a few impossibly tender strokes, the shape of a wall with the stars coming out over it. Bren looked at it a long time. He didn't say anything. He touched the paper once, lightly, like touching a scar, and nodded at her, and Sefa nodded back, and Tomas understood he had witnessed a whole conversation, a whole *friendship*, conducted entirely without a word.

Ilse laughed that night. Tomas noticed it the way you notice the sun coming out—not because it was loud, but because of how long it had been since the last one. She sat between the twins with a cup of brandy she wasn't drinking and told a story about the war, a funny one, a story about a supply sergeant and a mule and a crate of ceremonial banners, and she did all the voices, and the cellar roared, and Ilse looked around at the roaring like a woman checking whether it was real, and then laughed herself, really laughed, and Tomas felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the night the sky went dark.

Orsen sat apart. Orsen always sat apart. But not far apart—not *away*—just at the edge of the firelight, on a crate, turning the shard over in their hands, and when Tomas caught their eye across the room, Orsen didn't look away. They held it. And then, slowly, they lifted the shard an inch, a toast, to him, to the cellar, to all of it.

Tomas raised his cup back. *We're still us,* he tried to say with his face. *Whatever you did, whoever you turn out to be. We're still us.*

Orsen's face, in the firelight, said: *I don't know who us is.*

Tomas's face said: *Then we'll find out together, idiot.*

It was, he thought later, the most honest conversation they'd had in years.

* * *

It was near midnight when the cellar went quiet—when the twins had gone up to their loft, when Bren had turned to the wall, when the fiddle above had finally stopped—and Tomas, who could not sleep in strange places, went looking for Sefa because she was always awake and never minded company.

He found her at her drawing table with every lamp lit, and she was not drawing the undercity.

She was drawing the valley. Hollowmere, the thirteen stones, the roads out—and beyond the valley, past the border, a route rendered in her fine grey hand: rivers, ridgelines, a causeway, and at the end of it, marked with a symbol he didn't know—a circle, broken, with a crown-shaped gap in the broken part—a place he had never heard of and that made the hair on his arms stand up anyway.

Sefa looked up at him. Then at the map. Then she did something he had never seen her do.

She hesitated.

Then she pulled a second sheet from beneath the first, and laid it beside it, and Tomas saw that the route continued—past the broken-circle place, through mountains, to the coast, to a city drawn half-submerged, its towers rising out of water, and at its heart a single small shape that Sefa had drawn over and over again in the margins of all her maps, he realized now, without ever explaining it: a crown, whole.

She looked at him, and her hands moved, slow and deliberate, so that even he could follow.

*The Crown was made here,* she signed. *And it can only be unmade here.*

Then her pen went to the first map, the route through the valley's edge, and tapped one place on it—a river crossing, a ford—and her face, for the first time since he'd known her, went grim.

*But first,* her hands said, *we have to cross Ashfield. And someone is already waiting there.*

Tomas looked at the ford, at the small careful strokes that meant reeds, that meant shallow water, that meant—though he couldn't have said how he knew—ambush.

He went to wake Orsen.
