## 15. Ashfield Crossing

The ground began to whisper before they reached it.

Ilse felt it first in her teeth—a low vibration, like a tuning fork struck somewhere far below the soil. She stopped at the crest of the ridge and looked down at the plain that Sefa's map called Ashfield Crossing, and understood why the mute mapmaker had drawn it with a border of small black strokes, like flies around a wound.

"What is it?" Tomas asked behind her. He was carrying Bren's pack along with his own; the old soldier's knees had been complaining since the ford two days back.

"You'll feel it in a moment," Ilse said. "When you do, don't fight it."

They came down through gorse and broken wall into a landscape that had once been farmland and was now something else—something that remembered being farmland but could not quite commit to it. The fields were green enough. The hedgerows had grown back crooked and strange, bending inland as if flinching from something out on the plain. And everywhere there were stones: not natural ones, not boundary markers, but slabs of pale rock jutting from the earth at angles no geology would choose, each one carved with names so old the letters had gone soft as scars.

The whispering grew louder.

Orsen felt it next. Ilse watched it happen—the thief's careful face going slack, their hand drifting up to press against their own sternum as though checking for a door that had opened there. "It's like the shard," Orsen said quietly. "But spread over everything. Like the whole valley of Hollowmere, if the whole valley were—" They stopped, swallowed. "What happened here?"

"The last battle of the war," Ilse said. "The one your grandmother helped end." She kept walking. If she stopped moving she would have to start feeling, and feeling could wait until nightfall, or forever, whichever came first. "Forty thousand soldiers. Two crowns' worth of mages. And when it ended, nobody buried them properly. There wasn't time. The Crown's forces were retreating, the alliance was fracturing, and the dead were left where they fell."

"And the ground took them," Bren said. His voice had changed too—flatter, older. Ilse glanced back and saw him walking with his chin tucked, eyes on the path, the way men walk who have learned that looking at horizons invites them to look back.

"The ground took them," she agreed. "Memory soaks. That's the first thing they teach you about magic, before they teach you anything useful. Grief is the heaviest thing a mind can hold, and minds break open here, and what spills doesn't vanish. It goes into the dirt. Forty years later, the dirt is still full."

Sefa walked beside her, silent as always, but her chalk was already out, sketching quick shapes on her slate: a field, then arrows, then a cluster of small figures huddled together. *Stay close,* the drawing said. *Don't wander.*

They crossed the first stone field in a loose knot, shoulders touching, and that was when the memories started.

* * *

It began gently, the way drowning begins gently—with water that seems almost warm.

Ilse smelled woodsmoke that wasn't there. Then bread. Then the particular sour-sweet smell of a barracks kitchen at the end of a long winter, and she was twenty-two years old again, laughing at something Danya had said, Danya with her terrible braids and her beautiful handwriting, both of them scalding their fingers on chipped mugs while the snow came down outside the garrison windows—

She caught herself by grabbing Tomas's arm hard enough to make him yelp.

"Sorry," she breathed. "Sorry. It does this. It finds whatever you're carrying and it plays it back."

"It found my mother's garden," Tomas said. His voice wobbled. "I was six. I could smell the—there were gooseberries, I haven't thought about gooseberries in thirty years, I'd forgotten we ever had a bush at all, and now I can taste them." He laughed, wet-eyed and startled. "Is it always like this? Things you forgot?"

"That's how it gets its hooks in," Ilse said. "It starts kind."

It did not stay kind.

Fifty paces further on, the field shifted under them like weather, and the warmth went out of the air. Ilse felt it coming and tried to brace, and bracing did nothing at all. The smoke smell returned, but now it was burning thatch and burning hair, and the laughter in the barracks kitchen became screaming, and Danya's terrible braids were soaked dark and stuck to her neck, and Ilse was kneeling in mud holding a head together with both hands because someone had told her once that you should keep the pieces close, keep the pieces warm, as if a person were an egg, as if a person could be—

"Ilse." Orsen's voice, very close. "Look at me. You're here. You're on the grass. Count the stones with me—one, two—"

"There were forty thousand," Ilse heard herself say. Her mouth felt like it belonged to someone else. "I only knew eleven of them personally. Do you understand? Eleven. And I remember all eleven perfectly, every face, and I cannot remember a single one of the other thirty-nine thousand nine hundred and eighty-nine, and some nights that's the part that breaks me—not the ones I lost, but the arithmetic. The sheer size of what I got to stop counting."

Orsen took her hands. Their palms were rough from years of extraction work, callused in patterns Ilse was beginning to recognize, and the familiarity of it—an anchor made of another person's ordinary body—brought her back to the grey daylight and the crooked hedgerows and the four faces watching her with varying degrees of alarm.

"Grief shared becomes grief survivable," Orsen said. "Someone told me that once. I think it was me, actually. I think I stole it from a widow in Hollowmere and never paid her back." A ghost of a smile. "Come on. We cross together. Nobody walks alone in here. That's the rule now."

"That's a good rule," Bren said gruffly, and offered Ilse his arm like a gentleman at a dance, which was so absurd that she laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been sealed since the war, and she took his arm, and they walked on.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a collapsed granary, because Sefa's slate insisted it was the only structure within three miles whose walls still trusted themselves to stand. No fire. Bren argued for one and Sefa drew a picture of smoke rising above the ruin, and then, beneath it, a row of small hooded figures watching the smoke, and Bren conceded with the grace of a man who had been outvoted by better scouts before.

They sat in a ring instead, shoulder to shoulder, sharing cold food, and the field whispered around them in the dark like a tide.

"It's worse at night," Tomas said. "Can anyone else hear it?"

Everyone could. The whispers had a rhythm now, almost words, almost names. Ilse wondered if it was worse for the others because they were hearing strangers, or worse for her because she was hearing friends.

"Danya," she said, without deciding to. The name simply left her, the way a splinter leaves a finger that has finally been squeezed right. "Her name was Danya Voss. She was a signal-mage. Second company, eastern front. She could read smoke like other people read books—she'd take one look at a burning village and tell you how many people had gotten out, based on how the columns bent." Ilse turned her mug in her hands. "She died at the crossing. Not this crossing. A river crossing, north of here. Holding a signal line open so three hundred people could get across a bridge before it burned. She held it for eleven minutes. The record for a signal-mage under fire is four."

Nobody spoke. In the dark, Sefa's chalk moved softly against slate.

"After the war I went back for her," Ilse continued. "You're not supposed to. Everyone tells you not to go back, the ground takes things, the ground keeps things, you'll drown in it. But I went back, and I stood on the bank of that river, and do you know what the ground gave me?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "It gave me her last thought. Just the one. The ground had soaked up thousands of deaths and it gave me hers, because I asked, because I wanted her, and the ground is generous like that—it gives you exactly what you ask for and nothing else, no context, no mercy. Her last thought was relief. She was relieved. Because the last boat made it. Three hundred people, and the last boat made it, and she thought: *good*, and then the bridge came down, and that word—*good*—is the last thing she ever thought, and I carry it, and the ground carries it, and sometimes I can't tell which of us is holding it and which of us is just visiting."

Silence. Then Orsen, quietly: "My grandmother never talked about the war. Not once. I used to think it was because she didn't remember it." A pause with weight in it. "Now I think it's because she remembered it too well."

"She was there," Ilse said. "At Ashfield. On the alliance side. She was the one who—" She stopped herself. Some things were not hers to tell. Some things belonged to Orsen and to whatever waited in the thirteenth stone, and she had already given away more of other people's secrets than any one person should carry.

"The one who what?" Orsen asked.

"Ask her yourself," Ilse said. "If we find what we're looking for, you'll be able to."

Bren cleared his throat, and the sound was so deliberately ordinary that everyone turned toward it gratefully. "First war I fought in," he said, "I was seventeen, and I cried the entire first week, and a sergeant told me that was fine, that crying was just the body remembering faster than the mind could. Said the ones to worry about were the ones who didn't cry." He looked out at the dark field. "Never forgot that. Never decided if it was wisdom or just something old men say to boys so the boys will keep fighting." He shrugged. "Anyway. I'm crying a little right now, in case anyone's keeping count, and I'd thank you all to pretend otherwise."

"We are pretending otherwise," Tomas said solemnly, "very badly," and Bren cuffed him, and the tension broke the way ice breaks—all at once, everywhere, harmlessly.

Later, when the others slept, Ilse sat awake with her back against the granary wall and let the field talk to her.

It showed her the barracks kitchen again. It showed her Danya's handwriting on the last letter Ilse had never answered, because answering meant admitting the war was real. It showed her the moment she had run—not the dramatic running, the fleeing-across-borders with enforcers behind her, that was later—but the first running, the quiet running, the morning she had looked at her commission papers and discovered she could not read them anymore, the words sliding off the page like rain off glass, because her mind had begun protecting itself the only way it knew how: by forgetting. By carving pieces out of itself and burying them, one by one, until there was barely enough left of her to hold a pen.

That was the crime they'd branded her for, in the end. Not theft. Not treason. *Self-mutilation of memory.* The Crown's law held that a citizen's recollections belonged to the realm, and Ilse had spent five years stealing herself back piece by piece, hiding the stolen years inside wards and hollow stones, until the night she'd taken the shard—and everything changed, and everything got worse, and here she was, sitting on a battlefield that remembered better than she did, listening to the dead whisper names she'd carved out of her own skull.

The ground was generous. The ground gave her exactly what she asked for.

She was still sitting there, tears moving silently down her face in the starless dark, when she noticed Orsen was awake beside her. Had been, maybe, for some time.

"How much of that did you hear?" Ilse asked.

"All of it. The field broadcasts pretty wide at night." Orsen pulled their blanket tighter. "For what it's worth—I've spent ten years taking memories out of people's heads for money, and telling myself it was mercy, and maybe some of it was. But I never once put one back." They were quiet a moment. "Tomorrow, when we're off this field, I want to try something. With you. If you'll let me. I want to see if I can give some of yours back. The ones you buried. I'm not promising anything. But I've been thinking about what the shard showed me, about how my work and the Crown's work are... related. Same craft. Different ledger." A dry, self-mocking breath. "Maybe it's time the craft did something kind."

Ilse looked at them—at this thief she had fallen into the lives of, this stranger with her grandmother's secrets sleeping somewhere in their skull—and felt something shift in her chest that was not the field's doing.

"Yes," she said. "All right. Yes."

* * *

They crossed the worst of it at midmorning, a stretch Sefa had marked on her slate with a dense black hatchwork and a single drawn figure kneeling. None of them asked what the figure meant. They linked arms instead—Tomas on Ilse's left, Bren on her right, Orsen and Sefa forming the outer points of the chain—and they walked through the thick of the drowned memory like wading a river in flood.

It hit them all at once, and it hit them differently, and that was the mercy of it: because they were linked, they shared it.

Ilse felt Tomas's mother's garden, gooseberries and all, superimposed over her own burning barracks. She felt Bren's sergeant's voice saying *crying is just the body remembering*. She felt Sefa's silence—which was not empty at all, which was full to bursting, which was a dam built word by word over years—and she felt Orsen's loneliness, vast and tidy, a house with one chair in it.

And they felt hers. All of it. The kitchen, the letter, the bridge, the word *good*.

She felt them receive it, and she felt them hold it, and none of them flinched away.

When they came out the far side of the hatchwork, onto the long slow rise beyond the battlefield, Tomas was crying openly and pretending not to, and Bren was pretending harder, and Sefa had stopped to draw something on her slate: eleven stick figures standing in a chain, and above them, written in her careful hand, the only words she ever wrote:

*Not alone.*

Ilse looked back once, from the top of the rise. The field lay green and whispering behind them, forty thousand names soaking in the dirt, patient as rain. Someday, she thought, if the world became the kind of place where such things were done, someone would come here and listen to all of it, and write it down, and give the dead back their arithmetic. Maybe that was what the Crown had been supposed to be, before it curdled. Maybe that was what Orsen intended to make of its corpse.

She was still thinking this when Bren, who had gone very still beside her, said in a voice she had not heard from him before—flat, and old, and cold:

"Well," he said. "Of all the cursed luck."

He was looking down the far slope, toward the river road, where a column of riders had appeared out of the heat haze. Grey cloaks. Ledger-books in saddle cases. At their head, a woman with a captain's bearing and a face like a closed door.

"I know that officer," Bren said. "Captain Reye. Third Memory Regiment." His jaw worked. "She's the one who ordered my unit erased."
