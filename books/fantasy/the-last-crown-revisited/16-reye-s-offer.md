## 16. Reye's Offer

Captain Maren Reye had learned long ago that the best place to catch a thief was not at the scene of the crime but at the moment of kindness. Thieves were careful with locks and ledgers. They were careless with each other.

So she waited three days.

She had watched the group move through Cindral's undercity with the patience of a woman reading a slow ledger: the mage Ilse with her bandaged side and her too-careful hands; the big friend Tomas who laughed too loudly in doorways because he was afraid of silence; the mute mapmaker Sefa whose drawings were worth more than any of them knew. And at the center of it, Orsen Zhou—memory-thief, valley-born, grandchild of the name that had started this whole miserable hunt—moving through the refugee warrens like someone trying to be small enough to disappear.

Reye did not believe in disappearing. She believed in paperwork, patience, and pressure applied at the exact point where a person's principles met their love for other people.

The opportunity came on the third night, when Orsen left the safehouse alone to buy bread. Alone. Carrying coin. Looking, for one unguarded hour, like an ordinary person doing an ordinary errand in a border town that had never heard of crowns or wards or the price of forgetting.

Reye followed them into the market district, past the shuttered stalls, down a lane where the lamps burned low and the smell of river mud rose thick from the stones. She let her boots announce her—deliberately, evenly—until Orsen turned, saw the grey coat and the crown-stamped clasp, and went very still.

"Captain," Orsen said. Their hand drifted toward their belt, toward whatever small extraction-tools they carried, and Reye shook her head once.

"You'd need both hands and ten seconds," she said. "I'd need half of one." She nodded toward the mouth of the lane, where two of her enforcers stood in the shadows—not blocking it, just occupying it, the way a signature occupies a page. "But I didn't come here to fight you. I came to talk. Ten minutes. After that, you can walk away, run away, scream for your friends—I don't care. I only ask that you hear me out."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll have wasted a perfectly good ambush." Reye gestured to a stack of crates beneath a sagging awning. "Sit. Eat your bread. I'll even buy the next loaf."

Orsen did not sit. They stood with their back against the wall, bread held against their chest like a shield, and studied her with eyes that had clearly spent a lifetime measuring what people wanted before they asked for it. "You've been following us since Hollowmere."

"Since before Hollowmere. Since the wards fell and every star over this continent went dark in a single night." Reye leaned against the opposite wall, arms crossed, giving them space the way a good interrogator gives space—not out of kindness, but because corners make people lie and open ground makes them think. "Do you know what happened that night, Zhou? Officially? Nothing. The crown's remnant council issued a statement about 'atmospheric disturbance.' Atmospheric. As if the sky itself had committed treason."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, we lost the single most dangerous object ever made by human hands, and everyone who knows anything has been tearing the world apart looking for it." She tilted her head. "You're carrying part of it right now. In that satchel. I can feel it from here—it hums like a struck bell that never stops ringing. Don't bother denying it. I've spent twenty years around Crown-magic. It leaves a taste."

Orsen's jaw tightened. "If you know what it is, then you know why it can't go back to your council."

"I know what your grandmother thought it was," Reye said, and watched the word *grandmother* land like a stone dropped into still water. "Elin Zhou. Final bearer. Keeper of the hiding. Her name is on the first page of my ledger, written in ink older than either of us. Do you want to know what else is on that page?"

"No."

"She wrote a confession." Reye said it flatly, without triumph. "Forty years ago, when she sealed the Crown in your valley, she also filed testimony with the remnant registry—a legal formality, nothing more, the kind of thing archivists demand and no one reads. I've read it. Every word. Do you know what she confessed to, Zhou? Not theft. Not rebellion. She confessed to *mercy*. To taking the thing that eats memories and burying it under thirteen stones so that no one—crown, council, king, or god—could wear it again."

The lane was quiet except for the river and the distant cough of the city settling into night. Orsen hadn't moved, but something behind their eyes had shifted—the look of a person recalculating.

"You're telling me this why?"

"Because I'm about to offer you a bargain, and I want you to understand that I'm not lying to you. Lying would be easier. Lying is what my superiors would do." Reye pushed off the wall and stood straight, letting the full weight of the grey coat settle on her shoulders. "Here is the offer. You give me the shard. All of it—the fragment Ilse stole, anything else you've gathered, and any knowledge of where the remaining pieces are hidden. In exchange, I give you my word as a captain of the remnant crown: full amnesty. For you, for the mage, for Tomas, for the mapmaker. And for Hollowmere."

* * *

She watched the word hit him. *Hollowmere.* Not his own safety—he'd already priced that cheap, she could see it in the way he'd walked into her ambush without flinching—but the valley. The thirteen stones. The town full of farmers and widows and children who had never touched Crown-magic in their lives and were currently sitting inside a broken ward-ring with no stars above them and enforcers at every pass.

"The valley isn't yours to amnesty," Orsen said quietly.

"No. But it's mine to burn." Reye let the honesty sit there between them, ugly and plain. "That's the truth of my position, and I won't dress it up. My orders are to recover the shard by any means necessary. If I fail, the order passes to Captain Doss, and Doss doesn't wait three days in a marketplace buying goodwill. Doss burns the undercity of Cindral to flush you out, and when that fails, he goes to the valley, and he asks the ward-stones questions with fire until someone talks." She paused. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you because you deserve to make this choice with the whole ledger open."

"You said amnesty for the valley. Amnesty implies guilt."

"Amnesty implies *records*." Reye smiled without warmth. "Every soul in Hollowmere exists in a registry somewhere, Zhou. Your grandmother saw to that when she hid the Crown there—she knew a hiding place needs to be officially boring, which means officially documented. Right now those documents mark the valley as a site of interest. With my endorsement, they become a site of closure. Closed files. No further inquiry. Farmers stay farmers. Children stay children. The thirteenth stone stays cracked and nobody comes to ask why."

"And all it costs is the shard."

"All it costs is the shard."

Orsen looked down at the bread in their hands. When they spoke again, their voice had gone careful, the way voices go when someone is walking across ice. "And the Crown itself? The rest of the pieces? If I give you this fragment, your council reassembles it. That's what they want. That's what this hunt *is*."

"That's what some of them want," Reye said.

"But not you."

It wasn't a question, and Reye found, to her considerable irritation, that she didn't have a clean answer ready. She had prepared for suspicion, for bargaining, for tears, for a knife. She had not prepared for a memory-thief looking at her with the flat, patient attention of someone who extracted truths for a living and knew exactly what evasion sounded like.

"I've served the remnant crown for twenty-two years," she said finally. "I was a soldier before that, in the last years of the war, back when the Crown sat on an actual head and the world still had kings. I've seen what it does to the people who guard it. We start as wardens. We end as priests. Somewhere along the way, protecting the relic becomes worshipping it, and questioning that becomes heresy, and heresy gets your name written in a ledger." She tapped the leather book at her hip. "I keep this ledger. Do you understand what that means? I am the person who decides which names get hunted. Twenty-two years, and I have never once been allowed to cross a name off."

"So cross mine off."

"I'm offering to."

"No." Orsen pushed off the wall, and for the first time they stepped toward her rather than away, and Reye's hand moved instinctively toward her sword before she caught herself. "You're offering to trade. My friends' freedom for a piece of a thing that eats minds. And you want me to believe you'll honor it because—" they gestured at her, at the whole grey architecture of her authority, "—because you're tired? Because you have doubts? Captain, I rob people of their worst memories for coin. I know what mercy looks like when it's being sold. It looks exactly like this. Reasonable. Priced fairly. Available for a limited time."

For a moment—just a moment—Reye wanted to laugh. The boy had teeth. Elin Zhou's blood, no question about it.

"Then refuse," she said. "Refuse, and tell me what your plan is. Keep running. Keep the shard warm. Wait for Doss, or wait for the Crown itself to finish waking up—you know it's calling to its pieces, don't you? You've felt it. Dreams that aren't yours. Memories surfacing that belong to dead kings. The shard wants to go home, Zhou, and home is wherever the other pieces are, and every day you carry it, you're leading it—and everything hunting it—straight to the rest." She stepped closer, close enough now that she could see the exhaustion under their defiance, the bruised circles of someone who hadn't slept properly since the stars went out. "I'm not asking you to trust the crown. I'm asking you to do arithmetic. One shard, surrendered, versus a valley burned and a world drowned in a relic's hunger anyway. Which number is smaller?"

Orsen was silent for a long time.

The river murmured. A lamp overhead guttered and steadied. And Reye watched—she was trained to watch—the exact moment the arithmetic landed. She saw it in the way their grip loosened on the bread. In the way their gaze drifted, just slightly, toward the direction of the undercity, toward the safehouse, toward the sleeping friends whose names were already, in her mind, arranged neatly beside the word *amnesty*.

They wavered. God help them, they wavered, and Reye felt the old cold satisfaction begin to rise in her chest—

—and then Orsen looked up, and their eyes were wet, and furious, and absolutely clear.

"My grandmother hid that thing," they said, "under thirteen stones, in a valley she made invisible, and she spent the rest of her life watching it, and she never told a living soul, not even her own blood, because she knew that anyone who knew would become exactly what you are right now—someone standing in a dark alley offering a reasonable price for the end of the world." Their voice cracked and they let it. "You're not lying to me, Captain. That's the worst part. You mean every word. You'd honor the amnesty. You'd protect the valley. And in five years, maybe ten, your council would find the next piece, and the next, and they'd assemble it in some vault with your seal on the transfer papers, and some frightened man or woman would put it on their head because putting it on would solve everything, wouldn't it? One mind holding every ruler's memory. No more manipulation. No more forgotten treaties. No more war." They shook their head slowly. "My grandmother read that same promise. It's written in the Crown's own voice. It always sounds like mercy."

Reye said nothing.

Because the terrible thing—the thing she would carry back to her tent that night and turn over and over like a stone with a sharp edge—was that Orsen Zhou had just described her own private thoughts more accurately than she had ever described them to herself. The doubt she'd been nursing for months, the crack in her faith she'd shown no one, laid bare in a lamplit alley by a thief half her age.

"How do you know that?" she asked quietly. "About the promise. About the Crown's voice."

Something flickered across Orsen's face—guilt, or fear, or recognition—and they glanced involuntarily toward the satchel.

And Reye understood, with the sudden cold clarity of twenty years of ledgers, that the shard had already spoken to them. That the boy standing in front of her had heard the same seductive whisper she'd been hearing in her dreams since the wards fell. That they were not two negotiators across a table but two people hearing the same voice, and the only difference between them was which of them had decided not to answer.

"You're going to Vael," she said. It wasn't a guess. She watched them flinch. "The drowned citadel. Where it was made. That's where it can be destroyed—isn't it? That's what the message in the thirteenth stone says."

"We never—"

"Don't." Reye raised a hand, and for a moment she was not a captain but simply a tired woman in a dark street, forty-nine years old, holding a ledger full of names she'd never wanted. "Don't insult us both. Here's what happens now. You walk back to your friends, and in the morning you leave for Vael, and I will report to my superiors that the trail went cold at Cindral. I'll falsify the route. I'll buy you a week, maybe two, before Doss picks up the scent." She reached into her coat and set something on the crate between them: a slim iron key stamped with the crown's quartermaster mark. "Gate key. Eastern post. The guards there answer to me."

Orsen stared at the key. At her. "Why?"

"Because I offered you a bargain and you showed me the flaw in it," Reye said. "The flaw being that the crown cannot be bargained with, only buried—and apparently I'm not the first Zhou to figure that out." She turned to go, then stopped, her back to them, and spoke the last words quietly enough that the river nearly took them. "The ledger stays open, Zhou. Your name, your friends', the valley's—all of it, still written, still mine to answer for. If you fail in Vael, I lose the only reason I have left to hold the pen. So don't fail."

She walked away down the lane, boots steady, coat grey in the lamplight, and did not look back. Behind her she heard Orsen stand motionless for a long moment—she counted the seconds, an old habit, thirty-one of them—before footsteps hurried away toward the undercity, toward the safehouse, toward the friends who would need to be told that their route to the drowned citadel now ran through the conscience of a crown captain.

Reye rounded the corner, signaled her enforcers to fall in, and opened her ledger as she walked.

On the first page, beneath the ancient ink of Elin Zhou's confession, she wrote a single line in her own hand:

*Day 1. The grandson refused. Thank the dark, he refused.*

Then she closed the book, and began planning how best to lie to her superiors.
