## 18. Thirteenth Stone

The detour cost them two days.

Sefa marked it on her cloth map without a word—she never used words, only ink and gesture—and when Orsen traced the line she'd drawn east of Ashfield Crossing, up into the teeth of the Grayfell hills where no road went and no sensible person would follow, they understood immediately what she was showing them.

The thirteenth stone.

They had all felt it since the wards died. Not heard, exactly, not seen—but felt, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Thirteen ward-stones had ringed the valley for as long as anyone could remember, sunk into hilltops and riverbeds and one inconveniently placed in the market square of Hollowmere itself, and for generations they had been furniture. Scars on the landscape. Children dared each other to touch them and nothing happened, because nothing ever happened in the valley, that was the whole point of the valley.

Now twelve of them were just rock. Dead, inert, ordinary.

The thirteenth hummed.

Orsen had felt it from three miles out, a pressure behind the eyes like a song remembered but not quite recalled. By the time they crested the last ridge, their teeth ached with it. Ilse stopped walking so abruptly that Bren nearly collided with her back.

"You feel it too," Orsen said.

"I've felt it since Cindral." Ilse's voice was thin. She had not slept well in days; the shadows under her eyes had gone from gray to bruise-purple, and she kept her left hand—the one that had carried the first shard—wrapped in bandages though the wound beneath had long since closed. "It's been calling me. I assumed it was calling the fragment I stole." She looked at Orsen, and there was something in her face Orsen had learned to dread over the past weeks: the look of someone about to say *and also you*. "It isn't. It's calling both of us."

Tomas shifted the pack on his shoulders and said nothing, which was unlike him. Since the ford—since Sefa's capture and the loss of the first shard into Captain Reye's hands—he had been quieter than Orsen had ever known him. He kept looking at the eastern horizon as if expecting Hollowmere's militia to crest it at any moment. Maybe he was. Orsen hadn't asked. Some questions you saved for when you had the strength to survive the answer.

The stone stood at the crown of the hill like a broken tooth.

It was taller than the others—or it had been, once. Now its top third lay shattered around its base, great slabs of pale granite fanned outward like petals of a flower that had bloomed violently and died. The crack that ran through what remained glowed faintly, a seam of light the color of candle-flame seen through closed eyelids, and the humming came from inside it. From *inside* it. Orsen realized that with a lurch, because stone did not hum from inside, and hollow things did not sing.

"Grandmother brought me here," Orsen said. The words came out before they decided to. That was happening more often now, memory surfacing like a drowned body, unbidden and unwelcome. "When I was small. Eight, maybe nine. She stood right there—" they pointed at a flat shelf of rock to the west, "—and made me count the stones. All thirteen. And when I got to this one she put her hand on my shoulder and said, *count it twice*."

"Count it twice," Bren repeated. The old soldier had his hand resting on the knife at his belt, an affectation Orsen had never once seen him act on. "Why twice?"

"Because thirteen is a bad number to be wrong about," Orsen said. "That's what she told me. She laughed when she said it. I thought it was a joke." They looked up at the cracked monolith, at the light bleeding from its wound. "She didn't joke. Not really. Everything funny she ever said was a locked door wearing a clown's hat."

Ilse stepped forward. The closer she came to the stone, the more the bandaged hand trembled, and Orsen saw why: the light in the crack pulsed, slow and rhythmic, and each pulse landed in time with Ilse's heartbeat. Or theirs. It was hard to tell whose rhythm the stone had decided to keep.

"How do we open it?" Tomas asked.

"I don't know that we do," Orsen said. "I think—" They climbed onto the fallen slabs, boots scraping on granite dust, and pressed their palm flat against the glowing seam.

The world folded.

Not darkness—*folded*, the way a letter folds, the way a map folds, all the distances collapsing at once until Orsen stood somewhere else entirely and had always stood there. A kitchen. Wood smoke and rosemary. A woman with silver-streaked black hair and forearms like a dockworker's standing at the stove with her back turned, and Orsen knew her before she turned around, knew her the way you know your own hands, and their throat closed around a name eight years unspoken.

*"You're early,"* Maren Zhou said, without turning. *"I expected you at the stone, not in my kitchen. But then, you always did take shortcuts."*

"Grandmother." Orsen's voice cracked on it. "You're dead."

*"Am I?"* She turned then, and her eyes were the exact brown of Orsen's own, and she smiled the way she'd smiled whenever young Orsen brought her something stolen—a pocket watch, a neighbor's grief sealed in a glass vial, anything shiny and ill-gotten. Pleased. Unsurprised. Slightly disappointed in the method. *"Sit down, little thief. We have less time than the stone will make it feel like, and I have been waiting a very long while to say this."*

* * *

They came back to themselves on their knees in the granite dust, gasping, Ilse's hand clamped around their wrist hard enough to bruise.

"What did you see?" she demanded. "What did it show you?"

"A kitchen." Orsen pushed upright. Their legs shook. "Rosemary. My grandmother."

"The woman from the ledger," Bren said quietly. "Maren Zhou. Final bearer."

"She wasn't final," Orsen said. "She was *first*. First and last. She—" They stopped. Started again. The words felt like handling broken glass. "She built the valley. Not the houses. The hiding. The wards. Thirteen stones, thirteen fragments of the Crown, ground down and set into granite and buried across the borderlands so that no one—not the enforcers, not the heirs, not the Crown itself—could ever find the whole of it again. The valley isn't protected by magic. The valley *is* the magic. We've been living inside the corpse of the thing everyone's hunting."

Silence on the hilltop. Wind moving the dry grass in long silver strokes. Below them, far south, the land fell away toward Ashfield and the drowned fields beyond, and somewhere out there Reye was marching with the first shard in her keeping, and somewhere beyond *that*, the remnant crown sat on its throne of ledgers and fear, missing pieces and growing hungry.

"That's why the stars went out," Tomas said slowly. He had gone very pale. "When the thirteenth stone cracked. The wards weren't a wall around the valley. They were a blindfold over the whole world. As long as the fragments held, nobody outside could look *here* and see anything worth seeing. The moment one broke—"

"The blindfold slipped," Orsen finished. "And everything that's been hunting the Crown for forty years looked up and saw a valley that wasn't supposed to exist."

"And it called to you," Ilse said. Her voice had gone strange, reverent and afraid at once. "The shard in my pack—it woke the night the stars vanished. I thought it was waking for me. It was waking for its keeper's blood. For you." She knelt beside the cracked stone and laid her bandaged palm against the granite, mirroring Orsen. "It's a vault. Can't you feel it? There's a door."

There was a door. Once Orsen knew to look, it was obvious—the way obvious things are obvious only after someone names them. The seam of light wasn't a crack at all. It was a keyhole, tall and narrow and shaped exactly like nothing, shaped like absence, shaped like the space where a piece of something ought to be.

"It needs a fragment," Orsen said. "And ours is gone. Reye has it."

"No." Ilse pulled the bandage from her left hand.

The skin beneath was unbroken—but it was not empty. Faint lines traced across her palm and fingers, luminous, branching like frost or lightning or the veins of a leaf: the ghost of the shard's shape, burned into her flesh during the weeks she had carried it. During the ford, when the enforcers took the crystal itself, the shard had poured whatever it was made of into the nearest vessel rather than be captured.

Into her. Into the thief who had stolen it.

"I didn't tell you," Ilse said, not meeting Orsen's eyes. "Because I didn't understand it. And because I was afraid you'd look at me the way everyone looks at mages who start absorbing relics." She laughed, brittle. "Also because it doesn't hurt, which felt like the worst possible thing to admit. It should hurt. It feels like being *remembered*."

"You should have told us," Tomas said.

"Yes," Ilse agreed simply. "I should have. I'm telling you now because if we stand out here debating honesty while the enforcers close the distance, we'll all die honest." She raised her luminous hand to the seam of light. "May I?"

Orsen hesitated. Every instinct honed by twenty years of the memory trade said *don't let a mage touch the mysterious singing door with her haunted hand*. But those same instincts had kept them alive in Hollowmere by keeping them alone, and alone was the one thing they had stopped being.

"Do it."

Ilse pressed her palm flat against the light.

The stone opened the way a chest opens, the way a fist opens, the way grief opens—suddenly, entirely, with a sound like a long-held breath finally released. Granite split along seams that had waited decades for permission, and the whole face of the monolith swung inward on hinges of light, exhaling air that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and cold deep earth.

Inside was a chamber the size of a modest room, lined in the same pale granite, and it was almost empty. Almost. In the center, floating a hand's width above a pedestal of fused glass—fused, Orsen noted distantly, as if by fire—hung a shard of crystal the length of a forearm, turning slowly, drinking the dimness and giving back a glow the color of dawn through fog.

And beneath it, on the pedestal, lay a letter. Sealed with wax the color of dried blood, stamped with a sigil Orsen knew better than their own name: a circle of thirteen stones around a single open eye. Their grandmother's mark. The one she'd burned into the bottom of every preserves jar in Hollowmere.

Nobody moved for a long moment.

"Legacy," Bren said at last, gruffly, "is just orders from the dead."

"It can be a hand on the shoulder," Orsen said. "If you're lucky. If she loved you." They stepped down into the vault, and the air closed around them warm despite the depth, and they took the letter, and the wax broke under their thumb like old ice.

*My dear thief,*

*If you are reading this, then the thirteenth stone has cracked, the stars have gone out, and you have come home the long way round—which means you are older than I wanted you to be when you learned any of this, and braver than I had any right to expect, and angrier, probably. Good. Anger is fuel. Just don't let it drive.*

*I hid the Crown because it cannot be worn. Not safely. Not by anyone. I wore it for eleven months, and I remember everything, and that is precisely the problem—you will understand soon, and I am sorry for it, and I would spare you if sparing you were possible.*

*Two shards lie ahead of you: the one a thief carries in her skin, and the one sleeping under the water at Vael, where the makers forged the whole cursed thing. The stone will show you the rest. Trust the mute one's maps—they are truer than mine were. Trust the soldier; he remembers more than he knows. Trust the mage, even after she lies to you, because she will lie to you, and it will matter, and she will choose you anyway. That is what found family is: people who keep choosing.*

*Trust yourself least and most. You have spent your life selling other people's memories and pretending yours were empty. They were never empty, little thief. You are the last lock I built.*

*Come to the water. Bring everything. Break it beautifully.*

*— Your grandmother, who counted you twice*

Orsen read it three times. Around them the others watched, and the second shard hung turning in its cradle of light, and far away, at the bottom of the world, something drowned began—very softly—to sing.

"Well?" Ilse asked.

Orsen folded the letter with steady hands and put it against their heart, next to the ledger page bearing their grandmother's name. "She says we're going to Vael." They looked up, and found they were smiling, and that the smile hurt. "And she says trust you. Even after you lie to me."

Ilse's face went white.

"So," Orsen said, turning back toward the light of the doorway, toward the waiting dark of the hills. "Before we walk another mile—what is it you haven't told me?"
