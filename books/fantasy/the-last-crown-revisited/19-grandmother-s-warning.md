## 19. Grandmother's Warning

The vault did not want to be opened gently.

The second shard sat in a cradle of black iron at the chamber's heart, and when Orsen reached for it, the air itself pushed back—thick, resistant, like wading into a river that remembered being a glacier. Ilse stood at their shoulder, her breath shallow. Tomas hung back by the stair, torch raised, casting long shadows across walls that were not walls at all but compressed layers of ward-glass, banded like the rings of an ancient tree.

"Orsen," Ilse said quietly. "Wait. Look at the cradle."

Orsen looked. Beneath the shard, set into the iron, was a plate of hammered silver gone green with age. Words were etched there in a hand Orsen knew better than their own—the looping, impatient script that had filled the margins of every recipe book and seed ledger in the house on Lantern Row.

*If you are reading this, I am dead or worse, and you have inherited my worst decision.*

Orsen's throat closed. Around them the chamber seemed to lean closer, the ward-glass humming faintly, as if the valley itself had been waiting years for someone to read this aloud.

*My name—my true name, the one they gave me in Vael before it drowned—was Maren of the Last Assembly. I was the Crown's final bearer. Not its queen. Its bearer. There is a difference, and the difference cost me everything, and now it will cost you something too, because you are reading this, which means you could not leave well enough alone.*

A sound escaped Orsen—half laugh, half sob. *Could not leave well enough alone.* Grandmother had said it about the jam kettle, about locked sheds, about young Orsen prying at floorboards. She had been talking about herself all along.

"She built this," Orsen whispered. "She built the whole valley on top of it."

"The thirteenth stone," Ilse breathed. "It isn't a ward-stone. It never was. The others anchor it." Her hand hovered near the shard without touching. "She hid the Crown inside her own protection and made the hiding place look like part of the fence."

Tomas came down the last steps slowly, torchlight trembling on his face. "What does it say?"

Orsen read on.

*I took the Crown from the burning of Vael because no one else would carry it and because I was young enough to believe I could outlast it. It cannot be worn safely. It cannot be hidden safely either—that is the trap, so listen, child, and do not flinch.*

*The Crown remembers everything its bearers ever knew, and it wants to be whole again. Shards left apart grow restless; they call to each other across any distance, through any wards, the way a severed hand still itches. I split it into three and scattered them: one I carried into exile until I could not bear its whispering and sealed it away; one I sank here beneath the thirteenth stone, wrapped in the strongest wards my assembly could weave; and the third—*

Here the script changed. The letters grew smaller, cramped, pressed hard enough into the silver to tear it in places.

*—the third was inside me. What I extracted from myself in this valley, what I sold piece by piece over thirty years to buy Hollowmere its peace, was not only my guilt. It was the third shard, broken down into memory-sized pieces and scattered among strangers. Among you. Whoever bought my memories bought fragments of the Crown without knowing it.*

The chamber tilted. Orsen put a hand against the cold ward-glass to steady themselves and felt the hum travel up their arm, into their chest, where something behind their ribs answered—a resonance they had been feeling since the night the stars went out and had refused, until this moment, to name.

"I sold them," Orsen said. Their voice sounded far away. "In Cindral. My own childhood. I stole it back, and I thought—" They stopped. Thought what? That it had been coincidence, a thief's luck, that the memories in that vendor's case had hummed against their palms like a struck bell?

Ilse was watching them with terrible gentleness. "That's why the first shard wakes for you. Why it showed you the coronation. You've been carrying Crown-matter since before you could walk."

"Everyone in Hollowmere has," Tomas said. His voice cracked on it. "Half the town sold memories to your grandmother. Half the town sold memories to *you*, after. We've been passing pieces of the thing around like coins."

*You will be tempted,* the message continued, *to reunite it. The shards will make you tempted—they are good at wanting things on your behalf. Do not. If all three are joined, the Crown becomes whole, and a whole Crown chooses a bearer whether a bearer consents or not. And hear this plainly, because I loved you and love is the only reason I write it: whoever unites it will be consumed by every ruler's memory at once. Not haunted. Not instructed. Consumed. Forty-one kings and queens and the things they did, all of it arriving in one skull in a single moment, wearing that person like a glove. I watched it happen to the last man who tried. He smiled the entire time he burned a city, because some queen three hundred years dead thought it was a good idea, and he no longer had room in himself to disagree.*

Orsen read that paragraph twice. Then a third time, because the mind kept sliding off it, refusing the weight.

"Consumed," Tomas repeated flatly. "Not killed?"

"Worse than killed," Ilse said softly. "Killed leaves a body. This leaves a puppet that thinks it's a person." She looked at the first shard, bound in cloth at her hip, and Orsen saw her hand press against it—not guarding it, holding it down. "I felt it try. On Ashfield, in my sleep, it arranged my dreams into a throne room and waited to see if I'd sit."

*There is one way to end it truly,* the silver read. *The Crown can only be unmade where it was made—in the forge-halls beneath the citadel of Vael, where my assembly poured memory into gold and believed we were building a shield for humanity instead of a mouth. The roads to Vael drowned when we broke the levees to stop the war. The causeway remains, under the water, and it opens only for Crown-matter. Which means, cruelly, that the very thing we must destroy is the key that lets us reach the place to destroy it.*

*So. Carry it if you must. Let it open the doors. But understand the arithmetic before you take the first step:*

*To reach Vael, you must gather what is scattered.*
*To gather what is scattered is to arm it.*
*And to destroy it, someone must hold the whole of it at the moment of breaking—and whatever is in a person when the Crown shatters stays in them. Every memory it hoarded, released through the breaker's hands first. Every king. Every fire. Every grave.*

*I could not do it. I stood in the forge-hall sixty years ago with the hammer in my grip and I could not, because I was afraid, and I told myself the wards would hold forever instead. They are holding now because I lied to myself beautifully. Do not inherit my lie. Inherit my hammer.*

Something clinked against the inside of the cradle as Orsen shifted the plate aside. A small object, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. Orsen unwrapped it with fingers that did not feel like their own.

A hammer. Not large—a smith's finishing hammer, the kind used for detail work, for setting stones in jewelry. Its head was plain iron, but the handle was wrapped in wire, and wound into the wire, thread by patient thread, were strands of grey hair. Grandmother's hair. Dozens of turns' worth. An anchor, Orsen understood suddenly—the way memory-workers anchored a client during extraction, something human to hold onto when the current tried to sweep them away.

She had prepared this. Decades ago. She had wound her own hair around a hammer and put it in a hole in the ground and then walked home and made dinner and never said a word, for forty years, while Orsen grew up at her elbow learning to extract grief from widows.

"She knew it would be me," Orsen said. It wasn't a question. "She knew, and she didn't warn me, she just—left tools."

"She warned you," Ilse said. "This is the warning. Some warnings can't be spoken aloud in a valley full of people who might sell what they overhear." Her eyes went to the shard, then to Orsen. "She couldn't tell you the truth because the truth was made of Crown-matter. Speaking it might have called the shards sooner. So she wrote it where only someone who chose to dig would find it."

Orsen looked at the hammer lying in their palms. Small. Absurdly small, for what it was asked to do. The wire-wrapped handle was warm, though the chamber was cold enough to fog their breath.

"There's more," Tomas said. He was crouched by the wall, angling his torch. "Under your grandmother's writing. Someone added to it."

Orsen knelt. Below Maren's cramped script, in different ink, faded nearly to nothing, ran a second message in a rounder, more careful hand:

*Assembly record 114: the forge of Vael was built with a failsafe. The makers feared their creation too. The shattering must occur within the primary crucible, and the crucible requires a living bearer's consent to unlock. Consent given freely. It cannot be forced, coerced, or tricked. The Crown knows the difference. It has always known the difference between a person who serves it and a person who chooses against it.*

"That's why she couldn't do it," Ilse whispered. "It wasn't fear. Or—it was, but not only. The Crown would have tasted her hesitation. It refuses breakers who aren't certain."

"So whoever breaks it has to mean it," Tomas said. "Completely. No doubt. While every dead ruler is screaming in their head."

"Yes."

"And they have to get there alive," Orsen added, "through drowned roads, past enforcers, carrying the thing that wants to wear them."

Silence settled over the vault like silt. Above them, muffled through thirteen layers of earth and glass, wind moved over the valley—the ordinary sound of home, except home was no longer invisible, and somewhere beyond the hills men with ledgers were asking neighbors about a thief, a mage, a stonemason's apprentice.

Orsen stood. Took the second shard from its cradle, and the moment it left the iron, the two shards—one in their fist, one against Ilse's hip—began to sing to each other, a thin high note below hearing, felt in the teeth. The cracked ward-glass around them flickered with light that had no source.

"It knows," Ilse said. "It's been calling, and now half of it is awake in the same pair of hands."

"Then we don't wait for it to finish waking." Orsen wrapped the second shard beside the first, and the singing dimmed to a pulse, a heartbeat in a bundle of cloth. They took the hammer last. It fit their grip as if the wire had been wound to their fingers specifically—which, they realized, looking at the size of those careful loops, it possibly had. Grandmother had modeled the wrapping on a child's hand. Orsen's hand, measured against a hammer while they slept, or played, or pried at floorboards.

The stairs back up seemed longer than the stairs down. When they emerged into the night, the sky was still starless, still wrong, but the clouds had thinned enough that Orsen could see where stars should be—a darkness shaped like absence, like a page held up to the light to show the watermark of what had been written there.

They camped in the ruin of the old shepherd's hut a mile east, and Orsen sat apart with the silver plate on their knees, reading the message again by coals-light, committing it to the one vault no enforcer could raid. Near midnight, Tomas stirred across the fire. He'd been quiet since the vault—too quiet, turning something over, and Orsen had assumed it was grief or dread.

But when Orsen glanced over, Tomas wasn't looking at the fire or the shards or the road ahead. He was looking south, toward Hollowmere, and his face wore the particular expression of a man composing words he hoped no one else would ever hear.

"Tomas," Orsen said. "What are you thinking?"

He started. Smiled the smile he'd used since they were eight years old, the one that meant *nothing, brother, all's well.*

"Thinking it's a long walk to Vael," he said.

It was a good lie. Tomas had always been good at lies, the way kind people often are—practiced at them, apologetic about them. Orsen let it pass, because they were tired, and because they trusted him, and because the alternative question—*who are you planning to talk to?*—was unthinkable.

Orsen looked back at the coals and did not see the answer Tomas gave the dark when he thought everyone slept: none at all. Just a man, awake, staring at a sleeping town on the horizon, weighing one kind of betrayal against another, and beginning, very quietly, to lose.
