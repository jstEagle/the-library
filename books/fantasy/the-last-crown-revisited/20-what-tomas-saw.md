## 20. What Tomas Saw

Tomas had never been good at keeping his hands still.

It was a thing his mother used to say when he was small—that the boy couldn't hold a secret the way he held a hammer, that secrets slipped through his fingers like water and left him standing in a puddle of his own telling. She'd said it laughing, mostly. But she'd said it.

He thought about her now, sitting on the cold stone lip of the cistern behind the safehouse in Cindral's undercity, turning a scrap of ward-paper over in his restless hands. The paper was thin as onion skin, gray where it should have been white, and it carried a message exactly once—Sefa had drawn him a diagram of how to fold it so the words would travel. Fold toward the heart, crease along the old ward-line, whisper the name of the place you wanted it to go. Sefa couldn't speak, but her hands said everything, and she had taught him this folding in the space of an afternoon with the patience of someone who had spent her whole life being understood without words.

She didn't know he'd taken one of the papers from her satchel.

Or maybe she did. That was the thing about Sefa. Her eyes followed you with a kindness that felt worse than suspicion.

The undercity hummed around him—the twin forgers arguing in their workshop two doors down, the smell of hot metal and tallow, the drip of water finding its patient way through stone that hadn't been dry in a hundred years. Refugees' architecture, Bren called it. Everything built by people who expected to leave.

Tomas folded the paper toward his heart.

He told himself it was for Hollowmere. That was the truth, or close enough to the truth that he could carry it without it burning through his pockets. The town was sitting there on the edge of everything—Vess posturing, the enforcers gathering at the border like crows on a fence—and Orsen was half a world away from home chasing fragments of a crown that ate kings, and nobody, *nobody*, was thinking about the people who'd stayed.

His mother stayed. Old Maretta who'd taught him to swim in the millpond stayed. The widow Ilse had healed, whose name Tomas still didn't know, stayed. Four hundred souls behind thirteen stones, eleven of them whole, and Alderman Vess holding council meetings that ran longer and longer and came out stranger every time, if the rumors drifting down the trade road were true.

One letter. One warning. *Be careful of Vess. Watch your names. Lock your doors at night.* Nothing about routes, nothing about Vael, nothing that could hurt anyone.

Tomas was a carpenter. He knew all about load-bearing walls, about how a structure stood or fell depending on which parts you asked too much of. He told himself this letter was a beam, not a burden.

He whispered *Hollowmere* into the fold, and the paper dissolved against his palm like frost in sunlight, and he sat in the dark feeling lighter and heavier at once.

* * *

Three days later, they moved.

The plan had been settled around Bren's fire: north through the drowned quarries, then east along the ridge above the Ashen Vale, skirting the old garrison road entirely because Reye's patrols would be thick as flies along anything that had ever been paved. Sefa had drawn it out on the floor in chalk—a winding thread through country so broken even the maps had given up on naming it. It would take six days. It would take them within sight of Hollowmere's northern hills, close enough that Tomas's chest had gone tight and stupid with homesickness when she traced it.

"Here." Sefa tapped a junction of chalk lines, then drew a small circle and frowned at it. She looked up at Orsen, and her frown deepened, and she made a quick gesture—flat hand, chopping motion.

"She says we shouldn't linger anywhere near the town," Ilse translated softly. "Not even to look at it."

"I wasn't going to suggest—" Tomas started.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't been going to suggest it. He had only been *thinking* it, loudly, the way he thought everything, and apparently four people who had known him less than a month could already read the shape of his silences.

"We're not going home," Orsen said quietly. There was something in their voice Tomas had learned to hate hearing—the sound of someone bracing. "We go around."

"Around," Tomas agreed. "Fine. Around."

That night he slept badly, dreaming of the millpond and of his mother standing on the far bank wearing Vess's chain of office, calling his name across water that kept getting wider.

* * *

It happened on the second night of the march, and afterward—for years afterward, though he never said so to anyone but Orsen, once, very late, very drunk on Bren's terrible plum spirit—Tomas would swear he never meant it. Would swear it the way you swear you didn't mean to lean your weight against a rotten rail. You didn't *mean* it. The rail broke anyway, and what went over it went over because of you.

They had camped in the shell of a shepherd's cot, walls waist-high, roof long since gone to moss and sky. Rain came down in a fine gray veil, not enough to soak you, enough to make everything whisper. Bren had first watch. Ilse was curled against her pack with the shard wrapped in oilcloth beside her head, and even through the wrapping Tomas could feel it sometimes—a faint warmth, a hum below hearing, like a hive dreaming.

Orsen sat awake across the coals, turning their grandmother's letter over and over. They'd been doing that since the vault. Reading it, refolding it, reading it again, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder on the hundredth pass.

"You should sleep," Tomas said.

"So should you."

"I'm not the one carrying a dead woman's homework."

Something almost like a smile crossed Orsen's face. Then faded. "Tomas. If I don't come back from Vael—"

"Don't."

"—if I don't," Orsen went on, with the stubbornness Tomas had loved since they were both children falling out of the same apple tree, "I need to know someone will go back. To Hollowmere. To tell them what happened to me. Not Vess's version. The true one."

Tomas looked at the coals. His hands wouldn't keep still; he picked up a stick and began stripping its bark in long curls, the way he did when his thoughts got ahead of his mouth and he needed something to suffer in their place. "You're coming back yourself. So write your own letter."

"I'm serious."

"And I'm tired." He snapped the stick. "And you're not dying in a flooded citadel, because I refuse to carry your pack home. You know how much stuff you have."

Orsen laughed—a short, surprised sound—and for a moment it was just the two of them under the rain, twelve years old again, and the world was a valley and an orchard and nothing else.

That was why it hurt so much later. Because right before he ruined everything, it had been *good*.

* * *

He took out the second ward-paper while the others slept.

He'd been drafting it in his head for three days, the way he drafted joints before cutting—measuring twice, cutting once. This one was different from the first. This one was practical. This one said: *We are alive. We are well. Do not believe anything Vess tells you about us. We are coming back when it is finished.*

That was all. He checked it against itself a dozen times. No route. No destination. No mention of Vael, no mention of shards, no mention of the Crown except as *it*, vague as weather. He was proud of how clean it was. He read it by coal-light until the words wore smooth.

Then, because the paper required a name whispered into the fold, and because he was exhausted and homesick and his mother was on the other end of that whisper, he added a line he hadn't planned:

*Mother—we come by the high ridge past the millpond. Save us supper.*

He told himself it was harmless. Who would see a private letter between a mother and her son? Who in Hollowmere would care about a ridge and a millpond?

He whispered *Hollowmere*, and the paper melted against his heart, and he lay back on wet ground and slept better than he had in weeks, which he would remember later and hate himself for.

* * *

The ridge past the millpond.

Tomas woke at dawn with the phrase already souring in his mouth, the way bread goes stale overnight without any visible change. He lay looking at the gray sky through the missing roof and tried to remember the exact wording of the letter, and found he could recite it perfectly, every word, including the ones he wished he could peel off like bark.

*High ridge. Millpond. We.*

Not *I*. *We.* Which meant Orsen. Which meant Ilse, and the shard humming beside her sleeping head. Which meant the group's position, its direction, its pace—all of it folded into eight innocent words and delivered to a town whose council chambers belonged to Alderman Vess.

Tomas sat up so fast his neck cracked.

Bren looked over from the ashes of her watch-fire. "Nightmare?"

"No," Tomas said. "Yes. I have to—there's something I have to fix."

"Fix what?"

But he was already on his feet, already digging in his pack for another ward-paper, and his fingers were shaking so badly he tore the first one, and Sefa stirred and watched him with those kind, terrible eyes, and he knew—he *knew*—that she had counted her satchel weeks ago and knew exactly how many sheets were missing and had said nothing, because she had believed he would use them well.

He couldn't send a second letter. That was the trap of it, the beautiful ugly engineering of it: ward-paper carried one message, one time, one direction. Whatever he'd sent was already walking. He might as well try to recall an arrow by shouting at it.

"Tomas." Orsen's voice, quiet, awake after all. Of course. Of course Orsen never really slept these days. "What did you send?"

"Nothing. A note home. Just—to my mother, just to say—"

"What did you send?"

He could have lied. He wants that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the world keeps of Tomas of Hollowmere: he considered lying, weighed it in his carpenter's hands, and found it too heavy. He put it down.

"I told my mother our route," he said. "By accident. Part of it. The ridge past the millpond—I grew up there, I wasn't thinking, I just—it was supper, I said save us supper, and—" His voice cracked like green wood. "Orsen, I swear on the orchard, on my mother's grave-to-be, on everything, I didn't—"

Orsen had gone very still. Not angry-still. Worse. The stillness of someone doing arithmetic.

"How long ago?" Orsen asked.

"Four days."

"Who reads the letters that come into Hollowmere?"

Nobody answered, because everyone knew. The rumor from the trade road. The council meetings that ran long and came out strange. The alderman who'd spent thirty years keeping the valley's ledgers and had lately started keeping other kinds.

"Vess," Ilse said softly, and the shard beside her hummed, as if it too had done the arithmetic.

* * *

They argued for an hour in the rain.

Bren wanted to double back and intercept whatever Vess might send—ambush the ambush, turn the betrayal inside out. Sefa shook her head and drew furiously in the mud: a ridge, a road, a fork, and X marks scattered like dropped grain, meaning *too many places, too little time*. Ilse argued for pressing on faster, outrunning the news. And Orsen sat apart with the grandmother's letter in their lap and said nothing at all, which frightened Tomas more than shouting would have.

In the end Orsen looked up, and their face was the face of someone who has decided to carry something heavy alone rather than watch others stagger under half of it.

"We change the route," they said. "Sefa—can you find us another way? Something uglier?"

Sefa nodded slowly. Then she paused, chalk hovering, and drew something else: a question mark, and beside it, a small figure with a crooked staff—the sign she'd invented for Vess, whom she'd never met, whom she distrusted purely on the evidence of other people's silences.

"He'll send word to the enforcers," Tomas said hoarsely. "If he's been selling names—if he reads my letter—"

"Then we assume he already has," Orsen said. "Assume Reye knows where we sleep before we do. Assume every friendly face is priced." They folded their grandmother's letter away, and when they stood, they wouldn't look at Tomas, and that was the worst of it—not anger, just the careful absence of it, the way you work around a wall you've decided not to trust your weight to anymore.

"I can go back," Tomas said. "Alone. Intercept it. Talk to my mother, warn the town properly, fix what I—"

"No."

"Orsen—"

"*No.*" And there was the anger finally, brief and blazing, gone as fast as it came. "You've done enough fixing for one week."

Tomas stood in the rain and let that land. He deserved it. He catalogued the ways he deserved it, thoroughly, like a man itemizing tools he'd lost.

But underneath the shame, something else had begun to burn—a small, stubborn heat, the heat of a man who has broken a joint and intends, whatever it costs, to re-cut it true. He had betrayed them by loving badly. Fine. Then he would spend the rest of this road loving carefully. Watching Vess. Watching the ridgelines. Carrying the weight of what he'd seen so no one else had to stumble over it blind.

He picked up his pack. Nobody handed him his share of the morning's bread; he took it himself from the pile, and Bren watched him do it, and after a moment she tore her own piece in half and gave him hers anyway, wordless, which was its own kind of verdict: *guilty, and fed.*

They walked. Behind them, far to the south, past hills none of them could see, a letter's worth of innocence was already moving through the hands of men who were not innocent at all.

* * *

On the fourth night after, they saw the fires.

From a shoulder of high ground Sefa had chosen—she chose all the high ground now, always with a view of the road behind them—the group looked down into the Ashen Vale and saw lights strung along the old garrison road like beads on a black wire. Too many lights. Campfires in disciplined rows, and among them, moving with a precision that made Bren mutter a soldier's curse, the glint of lanterns on armor.

Enforcers. A full company, and heading not along the vale but angling north. Toward the ridge. Toward the millpond. Toward the route that existed nowhere on any map except in the mind of a carpenter who had missed his mother's cooking.

"They haven't seen us," Ilse breathed.

"Not yet," Bren said. "But they're not hunting the vale. They're hunting the high ground." She turned, and in the fireless dark her eyes found Tomas and held there—not cruelly, just steadily, the way you look at a wall you're deciding whether to trust. "Somebody gave them a map."

Tomas's stomach dropped through the mountain.

Because he was looking at the fires, counting them, and doing the thing he could never stop doing—doing arithmetic with his hands, his eyes, his whole unlucky mind—and the numbers came out wrong. The company was too big, too well supplied, too *far along* for a four-day-old letter, unless Vess had ridden hard the night it arrived, unless the enforcers had moved before Tomas had even finished feeling guilty—

Unless, said the cold voice in his head, there had been another letter. Another pair of hands that wouldn't stay still.

"Sefa," he said, and his voice came out strange. "Your satchel. Count your ward-papers."

She was already opening it. Her fingers moved through the gray sheets once, twice. Then she stopped, and looked up, and her face in the starless dark said everything her voice never could.

One sheet missing. But not one of hers.

Someone else in the undercity had learned the folding. Someone with kind eyes and access to their plans, someone who'd sat through the same chalk-drawn routes, and as Tomas stood there with his own guilt suddenly sharing a bed with a much older fear, Ilse spoke the thought aloud, softly, the shard at her side beginning to hum like a struck bell:

"The mapmaker taught the whole safehouse that trick. And somebody taught it to Hollowmere."

Far below, the first of the fires began to move—north, unhurried, certain—like a finger drawing a line straight through the dark toward everything Tomas loved.
