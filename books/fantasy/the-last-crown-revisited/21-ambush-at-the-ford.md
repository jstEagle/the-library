## 21. Ambush at the Ford

The river announced itself before we saw it—a low rush threading through the trees, the smell of wet stone and cold iron in the water. After three days of ash-dust and silence, it sounded like a promise.

Sefa heard it first. She stopped mid-stride, tilted her head the way she did when she was reading something none of us could see, and then she was running—not away from the sound but toward it, map case bouncing against her hip, her whole mute body lit up with a joy that needed no words. Bren laughed, an actual laugh, rusty as an old gate.

"She hasn't seen open water since Cindral," he said to me. "Let her have it."

I let her have it. That is the thought that would not leave me afterward, the one I turned over like a coin worn smooth: *I let her have it.* Three days of marching through country where every hill looked like a held breath, and when the land finally offered us something gentle, I said yes. I was tired. Ilse's fever had broken only that morning, and Tomas's limp had gone from favoring his left leg to favoring his pride, which meant he'd stopped complaining, which meant he was worse than he let on. And I wanted—gods help me—to believe the map Sefa had drawn for us was true all the way down. The route through Ashfield Crossing had cost us a night of ghosts. The ford was supposed to be the reward on the other side of grief.

The Serpent's Tooth ran shallow and wide at the crossing, braided over pale gravel, easy water. Sefa reached the bank first, crouched, and put both hands in up to the wrists. When she turned back to us she was grinning around the wordless shape of it, water dripping from her chin because she'd splashed herself in the face like a child, and for one whole moment I saw what Tomas must have seen in her from the beginning: not the mute mapmaker who'd lied with lines on vellum, not the refugee with nothing, just a person standing in a river in the morning light.

Then Ilse screamed.

Not a word. A raw, animal sound, and I knew before I understood—I knew the way you know a memory isn't yours, all at once and from the inside. The shard. It hung against her chest on its cord of braided leather, and it was singing. I could feel it even from twenty feet away, a vibration in my teeth, in the fillings of my old griefs, a note so deep it wasn't sound at all but recognition. The shard knew where it was. Or it knew what was coming.

"Off the water," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard, the voice I used on customers who wouldn't stop talking. "Everyone off the bank. Now."

Bren moved first—he'd been a soldier too long to argue with flat voices—and hauled Tomas back by the collar. Sefa stood up out of the shallows, confused, hands still cupped with river. Ilse was clawing at the cord around her neck like it had become a noose.

"It's calling," she gasped. "Orsen, it's *calling*, they can feel it, they've felt it for miles—"

"How far?"

"I don't—" Her eyes went wide and fixed on something over my shoulder, upstream, where the trees grew thick and the morning light lay in long gray bars between the trunks. "Too close."

I have spent my life reading people in the half-second before they decide what to do with you. The widow's flinch before she named her price. Alderman Vess's smile arriving a beat after his fear. So I read the treeline, and the treeline told me nothing, and that was the worst part—the absence, the professional blankness of ground that had been swept clean of everything alive except what was waiting for us.

"Ford or fight," Bren said quietly beside me. He'd drawn his knife, the old soldier's knife, more ritual than weapon. "We can't outrun them to the trees. Water slows arrows."

"And puts a river at our backs," Tomas said. His voice was steady. His knuckles weren't.

"There's a gravel bar," Sefa signed, fast, urgent, her hands cutting shapes I'd learned enough to follow. *Midstream. Rocks. We hold there.*

It was a good plan. It was the best plan available, which is a thing soldiers say, and I understood then why they say it with that particular flatness. We waded in.

* * *

The water was colder than it had any right to be in late summer—snowmelt from somewhere high, carrying winter down to us. It hit mid-thigh, then waist-deep in the channel, and the current grabbed at our legs like hands checking our resolve. Ilse went gray-lipped and shaking; whatever the fever had left in her, the cold meant to finish. Bren carried his pack above his head. Tomas cursed with inventive fluency, which was how I knew he was frightened, because Tomas only got poetic when he was scared.

Halfway across, on the gravel bar Sefa had promised—a spine of pale stone breaking the current, barely long enough for five people to stand abreast—we stopped, and the world held its breath with us.

"They'll want the shard intact," Ilse said. Her teeth were chattering. She'd pulled the cord free and was holding the shard in both fists now, and it pulsed against her palms like a second heartbeat, slow and vast and patient. "They won't loose arrows at me while I'm holding it. Reye knows what a stray bolt could do."

"You're assuming Reye leads them," I said.

"Aren't you?"

I wasn't going to answer that. I didn't have to. Because that was the moment the trees gave up their pretense and the enforcers came out of them.

Twelve of them. No—fourteen, moving in two files along both banks, in the gray-and-bone livery I remembered from the ledger-captain's column at Hollowmere's border, except these wore no captain's sash. They spread out with the unhurried precision of people who had done this a hundred times and never once been surprised by the outcome. Crossbows, but held low. Nets coiled at two belts. Iron manacles, dull and dark, and my stomach dropped through the cold water when I saw them, because I recognized the make. Grandmother's stories. Manacles for mages. For *bearers*.

A woman stepped forward on the north bank. Tall, helmeted, face hidden behind a visor slit that showed nothing human. She raised one gauntleted hand, and every crossbow on her side rose with it, smooth as tide.

"Surrender the fragment," she said. Her voice was wrong—not muffled, exactly, but flattened, like speech passed through paper. "Surrender the bearer. You will be led to processing. Resistance is recorded."

"Recorded," Tomas repeated under his breath. "Gods. They even threaten in inventory."

"We're in the middle of a river," Bren called back, conversational, awful. "You want to come discuss terms in the water?"

The visor turned toward him. Turned toward me. Held.

And then, downstream, the light changed.

I don't know how else to describe what happened next, except that the shard decided it was done being carried. Ilse made a sound like she'd been struck, staggered on the gravel, and the fragment in her hands blazed—not with fire but with *remembering*, with images pouring off its facets faster than air could hold them: a coronation in a drowned hall, a city burning in colors that don't exist, a child's hand pressed to a kitchen door, *my* child's hand, *my* door—and the light of it swept the river like a lighthouse beam, and every enforcer on both banks took one step backward.

One step. That's all it bought us.

"Loose," said the flat voice, and the world broke.

* * *

Later, trying to remember the fight, I would get it in pieces, the way a shattered window gives you the garden. This is the order I've settled on, though I don't trust it:

Bren shouting *down* and hauling Ilse behind the gravel bar's spine as bolts hissed overhead and slapped into the water. Tomas, impossibly, laughing—one short bark of disbelief—as a bolt buried itself in the pack he'd been holding over his head. Me, flat against wet stone, watching Sefa.

She hadn't gotten behind cover. She was standing in the shallows at the bar's downstream tip, and her hands were moving—fast, fluent, furious—signing at the enforcers on the south bank as if they could read her, as if language were a bridge anyone could cross if they only tried. And maybe some of them could. Maybe that was the point. Two of the south-bank enforcers hesitated, crossbows dipping, heads tilting at her shapes the way you tilt at a foreign tongue, and in that hesitation Sefa snatched something from her belt—a flare-stick, one of the forger twins' gifts, red as a wound—and cracked it against a stone and hurled it upstream into the dry brush on the north bank.

Fire caught with a sound like a drawn breath.

Smoke rolled thick and acrid across the water, and the north file broke formation—just a few steps, just a stumble—but a few steps is a country in an ambush. "Go!" Bren roared, shoving Ilse toward the south bank, toward the smoke, because the south enforcers were still reorganizing and the fire had cut the wind wrong and—

That was when the net came.

I never saw who threw it. I saw it land. Weighted iron mesh, spinning out of the smoke on the north bank with a soft, terrible grace, and it took Sefa across the shoulders and drove her down into the shallows. The flare-stick's red light washed over her as she went under, and her map case burst open, and her maps—the routes, the vault-roads, the drowned causeway to Vael drawn in her own careful hand—fanned out across the current like a flock of released birds.

"No—" Tomas was already moving, plunging off the bar, and I grabbed him, and he fought me, actually fought me, this gentle limping man suddenly strong with a strength I'd only seen in him once before, the night he'd stood between me and Alderman Vess's mob. "She can't call for help, Orsen, she can't even—"

"I know." My arms around him. My face in his wet hair. "Tomas. *I know.*"

On the far side of the smoke, gauntleted hands dragged her up out of the water. She fought—she fought with everything, twisting, signing even then, even with her arms pinned, her fingers spelling defiance into the air over her captor's shoulder as if the whole world were listening. One enforcer gathered the floating maps out of the current with terrible patience, one by one, pressing the water from each sheet before folding it away.

And Ilse—Ilse was on her knees on the south gravel with the shard raised above her head in both hands, and I saw what she meant to do, and I screamed *no* across the whole width of the river, because I understood in that instant what smashing it here would mean: every memory in the fragment loosed wild into the water, into the ground, into *us*, unchosen and unbearable, the Crown's hunger scattered like plague. But she wasn't striking it down. She couldn't. Her arms shook and would not fall, and the shard sat in her grip glowing like a captive star, and the flat voice on the north bank said, almost gently:

"The bearer cannot destroy it. None of you can. That is rather the point of it."

Then the second net came, and the first enforcer to reach our bank was not an enforcer at all.

* * *

She walked out of the smoke without a helmet, gray cloak streaming, and I knew her before her boots touched the south shore—knew the set of her shoulders, the ledger-straightness of her spine. Captain Reye crossed the last of the shallows alone, unarmed, hands empty and visible, and stopped three paces from Ilse as if the battle were a formality already concluded somewhere else.

"Captain," Bren said. His knife was out. His voice had gone somewhere I hadn't heard it go before, down past anger into older, darker country. "Captain *Reye*."

"Bren." She said his name like a report. Like a casualty list. Then her eyes found mine over the water and the smoke and everything burning, and she said, "Zhou. Give me the fragment and your people walk. All of them. Even the mute. Even the traitor's friend. I keep the terms I offer—you've seen the ledger, you know I do."

"And Sefa?" My voice cracked on her name.

Reye didn't look at the north bank. That was the answer. That was the whole of the answer, written in what she refused to see.

Ilse rose slowly, shard cradled against her chest, fever-bright eyes fixed on Reye. "You'll take it and give it to whoever holds your leash," she said. "And they'll wear it. And everything starts again."

"That is above my rank," Reye said.

"Nothing is above your rank anymore," Ilse said. "That's what the Crown does. It eats upward."

For one moment—one suspended, impossible moment—something moved behind Captain Reye's eyes. Doubt, or its ghost. I have spent my life reading faces, and I will swear until I die that I saw it: a hairline crack, a thirteenth stone splitting. Her gaze dropped to the shard in Ilse's hands, and her lips parted as if to say something that had never appeared in any ledger—

And the whistle sounded from the north bank. Two notes, sharp, final.

Reye's jaw closed like a gate. "Last chance, Zhou. The fragment. Now."

The smoke shifted. Four enforcers were wading toward us, nets ready, and behind them, through the thinning haze, I could see Sefa bound and hooded on the north bank, small and silent as a folded letter. Tomas made a sound beside me that I will hear for the rest of my life. Bren's knife-hand trembled—not from fear; from arithmetic, from counting bodies and finding ours wanting.

Ilse looked at me. Not at Reye. At me. And she opened her hands.

She didn't throw the shard to Reye. She threw it to *me*—a flat, desperate arc over the water—and time went strange and syrup-slow, the fragment tumbling end over end, throwing its stolen coronations across the river surface, and every eye on both banks followed it, and in that stretched and golden half-second I understood the whole trap at once: they had never needed to catch Ilse. They had needed only to make her afraid enough to pass it on, because the shard *chose* its bearer, and a shard passed in desperation might pass its claim to anyone—to a memory-thief, to the granddaughter of the last woman who'd ever hidden it—

My hands closed around it.

The world detonated into remembering. A thousand years poured through my palms—crowns set on brows, oaths sworn and broken, cities loved and burned, and underneath it all, faint as a watermark, a kitchen door and a woman's voice saying *hide it where no one remembers to look*. I went to my knees in the shallows and the river roared and the shard sang and somewhere very far away Reye was shouting orders and Bren was screaming my name.

When I surfaced from it, gasping, blind with borrowed centuries, the enforcers were already on us. There was no fight left to have. They took the shard from my hands with a reverence that sickened me more than violence would have—two of them, gloved, handling it like a relic—and they bound Ilse's wrists with the dark manacles, and they did not take me, and they did not take Tomas or Bren, and I understood that too, then: *the bearer cannot destroy it, none of you can.* They had what they came for. We were inventory, recorded and released.

The last thing I saw, as they marched her up the north bank into the trees, was Sefa turning her hooded head toward the sound of my voice—because I was shouting her name, I was still shouting it, I have not stopped shouting it—and her bound hands answering, one last time, in the old quick signs of home:

*Follow the water. I left you the way.*

Then the smoke swallowed them both, and the river ran on, indifferent and bright, carrying nothing but light.

* * *

We stood in the shallows for a long time after. Nobody spoke. The fire in the north-bank brush burned itself out against the damp, hissing, apologetic.

It was Tomas who finally moved—wading to the gravel bar, kneeling, gathering from the wet stone the only thing the enforcers had left behind: a single corner of one of Sefa's maps, torn loose in the struggle, waterlogged, half-blurred. He smoothed it against his knee with both hands, so carefully, the way you'd handle something living.

"It's the causeway," he said hoarsely. "The drowned road. Look—it's marked. She drew it twice." He looked up at me, and his face was wrecked and resolute, and I knew that whatever he was about to say had already cost him something. "Orsen. If they're taking her and the shard toward the crown remnant's seat—if Reye follows protocol—they'll pass within a night's march of the flooded road to Vael. Sefa knew that. That's why she drew it twice." His voice steadied. "They think they've won the race to the vault. They haven't. We know a door they don't."

Bren spat into the current. "Three of us. No mapmaker. No mage—Ilse's chained in iron, and even free, she's half-dead." He sheathed his knife with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a verdict. "You're proposing we follow fourteen enforcers into the drowned citadel to steal back a prisoner and a piece of a god."

"Yes," Tomas said simply.

I looked down at my empty hands. They still hummed. A thousand years of rulers sat in them like an ache, and beneath the ache, patient as groundwater, the grandmother's voice: *hide it where no one remembers to look.* And beside it, newer, sharper, spelled in the air by bound and hooded fingers: *follow the water. I left you the way.*

Ilse was theirs. Sefa was theirs. The first shard was theirs.

But the second shard still slept hollow in the cracked heart of the thirteenth stone, two days' hard march back the way we'd come—and somewhere in the dark of my own stolen memories, the door to Vael was waiting to be unlocked by a thief who finally had nothing left to lose.

"Get your packs," I said, and the river carried my voice away like a promise. "We're going to Vael."
