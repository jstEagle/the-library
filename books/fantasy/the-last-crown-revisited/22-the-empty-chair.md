## 22. The Empty Chair

The shard sang to Reye all night.

She had told herself it wouldn't. She had wrapped it in three layers of oiled leather, bound it with iron chain, and locked it in the strongbox beneath her command tent, and still it sang—low, patient, a note felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ear. Like a hymn hummed by someone standing just behind your shoulder. Like your mother's voice through the wall of a house you no longer lived in.

Reye sat at her field desk with the ledger open before her and did not read it. The ledger was her life's work, in its way: forty years of names, forty years of ink. Everyone who had ever touched Crown-magic, catalogued by order of the Regency Council, cross-referenced by bloodline, by trade, by proximity to the old vaults. Her superiors called it the Ledger of Contamination. She had never liked that word. *Contamination* implied purity somewhere, and in thirty years she had not found any.

Outside, the camp of the Ninth Remnant settled into its night rhythms. Cook fires guttering. The river muttering over its stones—the same river where, six hours ago, her riders had swept down on a cart, a mute girl, and a thief who fought like someone who had learned brawling in churchyards. They'd taken the mapmaker alive because orders said take them all alive. They'd taken the shard because orders said nothing mattered more than the shard.

They had not taken Orsen Zhou. Or the mage. Or the big friend with the soft hands and the terrible throwing arm. The ford had been chaos, torchlight on black water, and then the fog off the river had thickened like something breathing, and by the time it cleared there were hoofprints and blood on the far bank and no fugitives in either direction.

Reye should have been furious. Her aide-de-camp, young Lieutenant Corvas, kept glancing at her as if waiting for the fury, the way soldiers watch weather.

"Captain," he said finally. "Interrogation of the prisoner is prepared for dawn."

"Mm."

"And the shard?"

"The shard is where I put it." She closed the ledger. "Go sleep, Lieutenant. That's an order you can actually follow."

He saluted and left, boots squelching in the mud, and Reye sat alone with her lamp and her forty years of names and the singing that would not stop.

*Come and see*, the shard did not say, because shards did not speak. But it was a thought-shaped absence in the room, the way an empty chair is shaped by whoever ought to be sitting in it.

She got up. She took the strongbox key from around her neck. And she told herself—as she had told herself every hour since the ford—that she was only checking the seals.

* * *

The leather wrapping came off easier than it should have. The iron chain she left on; some part of her wanted the weight visible, wanted proof of procedure, of containment, of the clean bright line between *custody* and *communion*. But the leather she unwound layer by layer until the shard lay bare on her desk under the lamp, and she understood immediately why the Zhou woman's grandmother had hidden this thing in a valley with no memories and why the Council kept it behind seven doors and why Ilse Varn—the archivist, the traitor, the girl Reye had once signed commendation papers for—had bled to steal it away.

It was beautiful the way wounds are beautiful. A splinter of the Last Crown, perhaps a finger's length, faceted like cut glass but darker, deeper, light going into it and not coming out so much as coming back *changed*. Gold ran through it in veins that moved when she wasn't looking directly at them. And inside—

Inside were faces.

Not reflections. Faces. A woman crowned with wheat. An old man weeping at a window. A child on a throne too large for him, swinging legs that didn't reach the floor. They surfaced and sank like bodies in dark water, and each one looked at her—not at the air, not past her—at *her*, with the specific attention of someone who has read a document bearing her name.

Reye had interrogated two hundred people in her career. She knew what it was to be seen by someone with something to hide. This was different. These faces had nothing to hide. They had everything, and they were deciding how much to give her.

"You're evidence," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the tent. "You are inventory. You are item one of one in a chain of custody that ends in a sealed chamber in the capital, and you will sit there until the Council decides—"

The child on the throne stopped swinging his legs.

And looked at her with her father's eyes.

Reye went very still. That was impossible. Her father had been a ferryman on the Sorn, dead twenty-two years, uncrowned, unlettered, a man whose entire legacy was a scarred rowboat and a daughter with a chip on her shoulder the size of a regiment. He had never touched Crown-magic. His name wasn't even in the ledger.

But the face wore his tiredness. The particular tiredness around the eyes of a man who rows against the current for other people's coin, and smiles anyway, and never tells you what he dreams about at night.

"What do you want," Reye whispered.

The shard pulsed once, warm as a hand on her wrist, and showed her.

* * *

It was not a vision the way the chaplains described visions—no trumpet-blast revelation, no burning text. It was memory, worn smooth as river stone from centuries of keeping. She stood in a hall she had never seen and somehow knew: the Sunken Hall of Vael, though in the memory it was not sunken at all. Light came through high windows in long gold bars. Banners hung from rafters, faded to ghosts of their colors. And in the center of the floor stood a chair.

An empty chair.

Simple, wooden, unadorned—the kind of chair a farmer would recognize. Around it, the whole apparatus of a kingdom: ministers in silk, guards in polished steel, scribes with fresh ink, a herald with a scroll. All of them arranged toward the empty chair the way iron filings arrange toward a lodestone. No one sat in it. Everyone orbited it.

A voice beside her—inside her—one of the keepers, one of the makers' hands: *This is the last design. We built it to remember so they would not have to fear forgetting. A ruler who remembers everything cannot be lied to. Cannot be flattered into folly. Cannot be made a puppet, because puppetry requires gaps, and we have closed every gap.*

*Then why is the chair empty?*

*Because the Crown is not finished choosing.*

The scene shifted. Time poured through it like sand. The hall changed hands, changed banners, changed languages—and the chair remained. Always the chair. And always around it, the slow accretion Reye recognized from her own ledgers: more names added to lists, more doors built, more silence mandated. She watched a queen consult the Crown before signing a treaty and sign well, and the kingdom prospered, and the next queen consulted it before appointing her advisors and appointed *its* preference, and the kingdom prospered, and the third queen consulted it before speaking to her own daughter, and the daughter grew up fluent in a wisdom not her own, and the kingdom prospered, prospered, prospered, and somewhere in the long prosperity the question stopped being asked:

Whom does the Crown serve?

The maker's voice again, older now, frayed: *We meant it as armor. When did armor begin giving orders?*

*When we started obeying it,* Reye thought, and the shard went quiet, satisfied, the way a teacher goes quiet when the student arrives unassisted at the lesson.

She came back to herself with her hand flat on the shard and the iron chain coiled loose around her fingers, and she could not remember unlocking it.

Her heart slammed. She snatched her hand back, stood, knocked the chair over behind her, and stood in the wreckage of the noise breathing like a woman surfacing from deep water. Outside, a sentry called a query; she answered—"Nothing. Dropped my cup"—and heard the sentry's shrug in his footsteps fading away.

On the desk, the shard glowed faintly gold, patient as banked coals.

Reye righted her chair. Sat. Pressed her palms flat to the wood until they stopped trembling.

Here was the thing she had built her life on and had never once said aloud, not even to herself, not even in the privacy of her own skull: the Regency Council that gave her orders had produced no heir in nineteen years. The old king was ash in a jar. The Council ruled in the throne's name, and the throne stood empty in the capital, and every edict, every purge, every name burned out of a village's records—all of it was done in service of a chair that no living person occupied. She had assumed, the way soldiers assume, that above her, above the Council, above the seven doors, there was a plan. A purpose. Someone, somewhere, actually steering.

But the shard had shown her the shape of the machinery, and the machinery had no hands on it. Only orbiting bodies, arranged toward emptiness. The Council didn't serve a king. They served the *idea* of a king, and the Crown served itself, and everyone below served everyone above, and the whole tower leaned over nothing.

And now the Crown was waking. She had felt it wake. It had looked at her with her father's eyes because it had learned, across centuries, exactly how to be looked at. It was not calling to the worthy. It was calling to the *reachable*—and Captain Maren Reye of the Ninth Remnant, keeper of the ledger, servant of the empty chair, was reachable.

Corvas would say it was sorcery talking. The chaplains would say it was corruption. Her own training manual, which she had helped write, said that prolonged contact with Crown-magic induced confabulation, grandiosity, and loyalty inversion, and that any officer experiencing such symptoms must report immediately for severance review.

Report immediately.

She looked at the ledger. Forty years of names. Her grandmother's generation was in it—whole towns, entered in columns like grain shipments. Page after page of people who had never worn a crown, never held power, never threatened anything except the tidy story the capital told about itself. She had entered half those names herself. She had told herself it was mercy: better the ledger than the pyre. Better catalogued than culled. She had believed the Council collected these names the way the Crown collected memories—to protect, to preserve, to prevent manipulation.

But the Crown collected memories to *own* them. And the Council collected names to own the named. She saw it now with terrible clarity, the way you see a room clearly only when the lamp gutters: the ledger was not a record of contamination. It was a harvest list. And Hollowmere—that little invisible valley with its thirteen stones and its memory-thief—had stayed free for sixty years only because one woman had been clever enough to hide it, and that woman's name was in Reye's book, entered in her own handwriting, and enforcers were already marching toward her grandchildren.

Reye opened the ledger to the Z section. Zhou, Oren. Zhou, Marta. Zhou, Edeline—the grandmother, flagged in red: *Bearer-class. Vault knowledge probable. Priority: retrieval or termination.*

Retrieval or termination. She had written that flag eleven years ago. She remembered the day. It had rained. She'd had tea afterward.

The lamp flickered. The shard hummed its single patient note.

"No," Reye said—to it, to herself, to the whole descending weight of the thing. "I don't know yet what I am. But I know what I'm not. I'm not a door that opens twice."

She wrapped the shard back in its leather. Chained it. Locked it. Hung the key around her neck, where it lay cold against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

Then she sat back down and did the most dangerous thing she had done in forty years of dangerous things: she began, in pencil, in the margin of page nine hundred and twelve, beside the red-flagged name of Edeline Zhou, to draft a letter of objection to the Regency Council. One line. Then another. Not treason—objection. There was a difference. There had to be a difference, or there was nothing left of her at all.

Outside, the east was graying. Dawn, and with it the interrogation of the mute girl—the mapmaker who had stared down four armed riders with nothing but her eyes and won, somehow, because her riders came back spooked and would not say why.

Reye blew out the lamp. In the gray half-light, the strongbox sat silent, and the empty chair across the tent—she kept it for visitors who never came—stood in the corner like a question she could no longer avoid.

Whom does the Crown serve?

By noon, she intended to ask the prisoner. The mute girl drew instead of spoke, they said. Drew pictures that told truths.

Reye picked up her sword belt and buckled it on with steady hands, and told herself the tremor she almost felt was only the cold.
