## 23. Interrogation of Sefa

They took her hands first.

Not bound—*bagged*, soft leather sleeves that swallowed her fingers to the wrist, cinched at the elbow. The enforcer who did it was careful, almost gentle, the way you handle something valuable you've been told not to break. Sefa understood the message in that gentleness. They knew what she was. The mute mapmaker of Cindral's undercity, the woman who drew routes no one else could find, the one whose silence everyone mistook for emptiness.

She let them take her hands. She had counted the cost of the ford before the ambush ever came—had seen the riders in the reeds three heartbeats before Orsen shouted—and she had made her choice in those three heartbeats: *scream loud enough with her body to buy the others the river.* So when the net came down and the shard was torn from Ilse's pack and Bren went into the water swinging a boat oar like a war-god, Sifa had simply stopped fighting, gone limp, made herself easy to carry away.

A captured mapmaker was worth more than a dead one. Everyone knew that. Even the crown.

Even Captain Reye.

* * *

The room they kept her in was dry, which surprised her. She had expected a cellar, damp stone, the smell of standing water. Instead: a converted granary above the ford garrison, whitewashed walls, one high window through which afternoon light fell in a slanted gold bar full of drifting dust. A table. Two chairs. A pitcher of water, two cups.

Almost courteous.

Sefa sat in one chair and studied the room the way she studied everything—not with her eyes alone but with her whole attention, the way a river studies a bank. There were scuff marks on the floorboards where furniture had been dragged. A dark ring on the table where a hundred cups had left their ghosts. A hairline crack in the plaster shaped, if you looked long enough, like the river delta south of Cindral, the one that had silted shut eleven years ago and stranded half the fishing fleet.

She had drawn that delta once, from memory, for a smuggler who needed to know where the old channel ran. He had paid her in salt fish and a bolt of blue cloth. She still had the cloth.

The door opened. Captain Reye came in without her helmet, and behind her a younger enforcer carried a board, an ink pot, and a stack of clean paper.

Reye sat across from her. For a long moment neither of them moved. The light crawled along the floor.

"You don't talk," Reye said at last. It wasn't a question. "I've read your file. The guild calls you the Silent Cartographer. There are men in this company who think that means you have nothing to say." She turned the pitcher slightly, aligning its handle to the table edge—a small precise gesture, the gesture of a woman who aligned things. "I know better. Silence is a language. I'd just like to establish which dialect."

Sefa tilted her head.

"We want the route," Reye said. "The one the mapmaker of the undercity drew for them. The drowned road to Vael. We know it exists because our own archives have fragments of it, and the fragments match nothing on any living chart—which means someone alive has reconstructed it." Her eyes were pale gray, patient as winter. "You drew it for them. I'm not asking whether. I'm asking *how much of it is true*, and how much of it is a trap you built for them, and whether the trap has room in it for me."

That last sentence landed strangely, and Sefa saw that Reye knew it had. The captain folded her hands and waited.

Sefa looked at the paper. At the ink pot. At her own arms, wrapped in leather, resting on the table like two dead branches.

Then she looked up and held the captain's gaze, and smiled—the small, closed-lip smile of a woman agreeing to terms.

Reye nodded to the younger enforcer. "Cut the sleeves."

* * *

Her hands free, flexing blood back into her fingers, Sefa reached for the paper the way other people reached for air.

The first thing she drew was not a map.

It was a face.

She worked fast, in the quick confident strokes of someone who had spent thirty years making images carry what her voice could not. The face was gaunt, hollow-cheeked, with eyes that had been burned out of the drawing entirely—two smudged voids where eyes should be. Below it, in the picture-language the undercity used among the voiceless and the foreign-born, she added a figure kneeling, and a second figure standing over it holding a ledger, and above the ledger a symbol every refugee in Cindral knew: the open book with the quill through it. The mark of the Enforcers' Registry.

She turned the page around and slid it across the table.

Reye looked at it for a long time. The younger enforcer shifted by the door.

"That's not what I asked for," Reye said quietly.

Sefa took the page back, and beneath the hollow-eyed face she drew a second figure: a soldier. Then a line of soldiers behind him. Then, with two strokes, she erased them—her palm sweeping the charcoal across the page until the ranks became a smear, a fog, a blankness. Above the smear she wrote, in plain block letters learned from years of signing her work:

**WHO GAVE THE ORDER?**

She pushed it back.

The silence in the room changed quality. The younger enforcer had gone very still. And Captain Reye—Captain Reye looked at the smeared soldiers for longer than interrogation protocol allowed, and something moved behind her gray eyes like ice shifting on a river, a deep groan of weight deciding whether to hold or go.

"You've done your research," Reye said finally. Her voice was level, but Sefa heard the plank beneath it creak. "Or you're guessing. Memory erasure details are sealed. Most people outside the company don't even believe we do it."

Sefa met her eyes and did not blink, and in that unblinking look she gave the captain everything: *I believe it. My friends believe it. An old soldier drinks with us in the undercity who cannot remember his own mother's face, and he remembers yours.*

She picked up the charcoal again. Drew a third image, quickly, before the moment cooled: a bridge over a river, and on the bridge a single figure with a captain's braid, and beneath the bridge, hidden in the pilings, a small stick-figure with a bundle—herself, with the maps. And an arrow from the captain's eyes to the bundle.

*I see everything,* the picture said. *Including what you're hiding.*

Reye stood abruptly. The chair scraped.

"Out," she said to the younger enforcer. "Wait in the hall."

He hesitated. "Captain—"

"*Out.*"

The door closed. The gold light lay between them like a blade laid flat.

"Here is what happens now," Reye said, leaning both fists on the table, close enough that Sefa could smell iron and road dust on her. "In one hour my superiors arrive from the border with a truth-reader. When it touches you, it will pull the route out of your head regardless of what you draw or refuse to draw. Do you understand? Your little pictures are brave. They are also irrelevant. In one hour you will have no secrets left, and then you will wish—" She stopped herself. Jaw tight. Restarted. "You will be handled. That is all I am permitted to say about it."

Sefa looked at her.

Then, slowly, deliberately, she turned to a fresh sheet, and began to draw.

She drew the granary room. The table. The two chairs. The window with its bar of light. She drew herself sitting in it, charcoal-stained fingers moving. And then she drew the same room again—but the walls were thin as eggshell, and through them, rendered in fine cross-hatching, she showed everything beyond: the hall outside, the garrison yard, the stables, the river, the ford, the reed beds where the ambush had sprung, and far downstream, small as a seed, the undercity of Cindral with its thousand lamps.

On the second drawing she marked one point. The hall outside the door. Where the young enforcer waited.

And from that point, a dotted line, snaking down through the building, out through the stable wall—drawn with the loving precision of a woman who had spent her life finding the seams in fortresses—out to the horse lines, out past the sentry schedule, which she sketched from having counted the watch rotations during her capture, out to the reeds.

An escape route.

She turned it around and tapped it twice with one stained finger. Then she pointed at Reye. Then at the route. Then she raised her eyebrows: *Well?*

"What—" Reye stared at the page. "Are you *mocking* me?"

Sefa shrugged elaborately, palms up, and grinned.

Because here was the thing the file had gotten wrong, the thing the guild knew and the crown would never understand: Sefa did not need to speak to lie, and she did not need to speak to tell the truth, and most importantly she did not need to speak to make a person stand in front of a choice so naked that refusing it felt like answering it anyway.

The route was real. Every line of it was real; she had mapped the garrison since the moment the sack went over her head, the way she mapped everything, compulsively, helplessly, the world insisting on becoming lines in her mind. If Reye wanted to use it, she could use it. If Reye reported it, she proved she had looked at treason. If Reye destroyed it, she destroyed the only copy of a route her own side might someday need.

And meanwhile—an hour passed. And another. And the truth-reader did not come, because nothing travels as fast as its reputation, and reputation was all Sefa needed to steal time.

Time was the whole drawing. Time was the map.

Every minute Reye spent staring at charcoal was a minute Orsen and Ilse and Bren and Tomas spent putting distance between themselves and the ford, running the drowned road toward Vael with the second shard against Orsen's chest. She could not say this. She did not need to. She only had to keep the captain looking at the page.

So she kept drawing.

* * *

They brought her bread and cheese at dusk, which she interpreted as either respect or a delay, and she ate methodically while Reye stood at the window with her back turned, and the gold bar of light went copper, then ash.

Twice more Reye tried questions. Once about the group's composition—Sefa answered with portraits, warm ones, infuriatingly fond ones: Orsen mid-laugh, Ilse scowling over the shard, Tomas asleep against a saddle, Bren teaching her dice games with his big scarred hands. Drawings like love letters. Reye looked at them too long each time.

Once about Vael itself. To that, Sefa answered honestly, because honesty was also a weapon with a delayed fuse: she drew the drowned citadel as the old charts described it, towers below the waterline, gates that opened to memory instead of keys. Let the crown send its whole army into a place where every secret a bearer ever kept comes flooding up their throat. Let them drown in it. She even drew little figures wading in, enforcers with ledger-books held over their heads like drowning men clutching ledgers, and despite everything, despite the stakes, she caught herself smiling as she shaded the water.

Near midnight, the door opened without a knock, and a man came in whom Sefa had never seen but recognized instantly from a hundred refugees' nightmares: robed, gray-faced, carrying a case of instruments, with eyes that moved over her like fingers counting coins. The truth-reader. Arrived at last, hours late, escorted by outriders who had ridden hard enough to lather their horses.

"Captain," the man said, "we can begin."

"Yes," Reye said.

And then she did something Sefa would spend the rest of her life turning over and over, the way you turn a strange coin found in the road.

She stepped between Sefa and the reader.

"One matter first," Reye said, and her voice was perfectly flat, perfectly professional, and her hand rested on the table directly beside the stack of drawings—the escape-route drawing, still there, still visible, still unreported. "Before you open her head, you'll want to review what she's already given up voluntarily. She's cooperative. More than cooperative. She's been mapping *for* us since the ford." She gathered the pages together, squared their edges with a single crisp tap, and tucked them under her arm—including, casually, indistinguishably, the drawing with the dotted line to the reeds. "I'll brief you on the contents myself. Alone. Company intelligence isn't shared outside the chain of command—you wrote the regulation yourself, Magister."

The truth-reader's mouth thinned. "The prisoner—"

"The prisoner has been awake for thirty hours and questioned for twelve," Reye said. "She goes nowhere. Post your guards. She'll keep till morning." A pause, one beat too long, the kind of pause Sefa could have drawn: *unless you intend to accuse a captain of the crown of hoarding intelligence.* "Unless you'd like to put that in writing."

The magister looked at Reye, and at Sefa, and at the space between them where something unspoken stood like a third person in the room. Whatever he saw, he decided it was smaller than his career.

"Morning," he said, and left.

The door closed. The lamp guttered. Sefa sat very still, listening to the boots fade, feeling the shape of what had just happened settle over the room like snowfall.

Reye stood with her back to her, the drawings under her arm.

"I don't know what game you're playing," the captain said quietly, without turning. "I don't know if you planned this, or if you're simply the most stubborn piece of territory I've ever been ordered to take. But tomorrow morning that man opens your skull like a drawer, and everything in it—your friends, your routes, your ridiculous little portraits—he reads it all out loud to men who burn villages for less than a dotted line." Now she turned. Her gray eyes in the lamplight were the color of the river before ice breaks. "So tonight, cartographer, you're going to draw me one more picture. And you're going to make it good enough that I can show it to him and buy you one more day. Because one more day might be all they need—or all *I* need. I haven't decided which. Have you?"

Sefa looked up at her for a long, long moment.

Then she pulled a fresh sheet of paper close, and chose her charcoal, and began—to draw the drowned road to Vael, true and complete, every gate and every seal, exactly as she had promised it to no one.

Exactly as it would lead the entire enforcer column, by morning, straight into the flooded halls of Vael—

—where Orsen was already waiting, and where the Crown itself, waking shard by shard, was beginning to call.

She drew fast, and she did not smile, and somewhere in the walls of the old granary the river murmured against the pilings, carrying her silence downstream toward her friends like a message written in water.
