## 24. The Drowned Road

The sea had taken the road a thousand years ago and never given it back, but it had left a spine of black stone running beneath the water like something swallowed whole. We found it at low tide, or what passed for low tide in this drowned stretch of coast—Bren spotted it first, standing hip-deep in the shallows with his boots slung over one shoulder, pointing at a line where the waves broke wrong. Not against rock. Against architecture.

"There," he said. "See how the foam sits? It's sitting on steps."

I waded out beside him and put my palm flat on the surface, and under my hand, through a finger's width of cold seawater, I felt carving. Grooves. Letters. My fingers knew them before my eyes could have—the way you know the shape of your own name written by someone else.

"Orsen." Ilse had come up behind me, the first shard wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant she didn't trust herself to hold. "What is it?"

"It's a door," I said. And then, because the knowledge arrived with the taste of salt that wasn't in the air, a memory of standing somewhere high and dry while a man with ink-stained hands explained the making of roads: *We built them to open for blood, not keys. The makers were proud of that. They thought it would keep the Crown out of the wrong hands.* "It's a door that opens for me."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Sefa stood on the shingle behind us with her sketchbook held against the wind, already drawing the causeway's line as it vanished into the grey water, and her pencil made a sound like a small animal worrying at something. Tomas shifted from foot to foot. Bren watched the horizon, where the light was going the color of old bruises.

"The tide comes back in four hours," Bren said finally. "Whatever we're going to do down there, we do it fast."

* * *

The road descended in long flights, each landing marked with pillars that broke the surface like the ribs of some enormous patient beast. As we went deeper the water rose—from our ankles to our knees to our waists—and the wind died, and the sound of the world narrowed to our own breathing and the slap of water against stone.

The first sealed door stood at the bottom of the third flight.

It was bronze, or had been once; now it was green as pond-silt, twelve feet tall, worked with a relief of interlocking circles. No handle. No lock. Just the circles, turning slowly when I looked at them—or seeming to turn, the way patterns move when you stare too long.

"I can't feel any mechanism," Ilse murmured. Her fingers hovered over the metal without touching. "There should be something. A ward, a counterweight, *anything*. This isn't locked, Orsen. It's just... closed."

"Then it's waiting for permission." I heard my grandmother's voice say it inside my skull, in the tone she'd used for things she considered obvious: *Everything in Vael waits for permission. That was its genius and its sickness.* I set my hand flat against the green bronze.

The memory came up through my arm like water through a well-rope.

Not mine—that was the vertigo of it, the thing I'd been learning to bear since the shard woke. It belonged to a woman with cropped hair and builder's calluses, standing exactly where I stood, pressing her palm exactly where mine was. *Third gate of the drowned approach,* she thought, in the brisk interior voice of someone checking off a list. *Sealed to all claimants. Opens only for the unremembered—for those who gave their pasts away to build the wards. Let them pass. Let them come home.*

The door sighed. Not groaned—*sighed*, the way a person sighs when they've been holding their breath for a very long time, and the circles spun apart like an eye opening, and the bronze swung inward on hinges that had no business working after a millennium underwater, and did not let in a drop of the sea.

Beyond it: stairs going down, dry, lit by nothing we could name.

"Well," Tomas said, after a silence. "That's either wonderful or terrible."

"It's both," I said. "Come on."

* * *

The passage beyond the first door ran straight as a spear-shaft, and every twenty paces another door waited, and every door wanted something different from me.

The second opened for grief. I touched it and remembered—I mean *remembered*, in the raw first-person way of the returned memories, not the distant third-person way of the ones I'd sold—a funeral in Hollowmere, my grandmother's funeral, rain coming down through the pines, and my own hands too numb with cold and loss to close the coffin lid properly so Tomas had reached over and helped me, wordless, and the wood had scraped, and that scrape lived in me still. The door drank the memory the way dry ground drinks rain. It opened. The memory stayed. I checked, afterward, frantically, patting around inside my own head like a man checking his pockets—and it was still there, the rain, the scrape, Tomas's sleeve dripping beside mine. The door hadn't taken. It had only *read*.

"They're not locks," I told the others, when I could speak again. "They're witnesses. The makers built the road so that only people who had actually lived could walk it. You can't fake your way down here with a stolen key. The road checks you against yourself."

Ilse looked at me with an expression I couldn't read—something between pity and fear. "And if someone came down here with no memories at all?"

"Then the doors stay shut forever," I said. "Which was the point."

Sefa tapped my elbow. She had her sketchbook out and showed me a drawing: a stick figure walking down a stair, and above it, a series of small doors, each with a mark—she'd been keeping count, and noting which emotion each door took from me, reading it somehow off my face faster than I could name it myself. Under the last drawn door she'd written, in her careful hand: *how many?*

"How many what?" I asked.

She pointed down the dark, then drew a long line of doors stretching past the edge of the page, and then drew the same figure again at the end of it—but smaller. Thinner. And she looked at me with her level, wordless eyes until I understood.

How many pieces of myself did the road want? How much of me would be left at the bottom?

"I don't know," I said honestly. "But I've spent ten years selling my memories piece by piece to strangers. At least these doors give something back. Light. Passage. A road under the sea." I tried to make it a joke and mostly failed. "Best trade I've ever made."

Bren grunted—the soldier's laugh, the one that means *I hear you lying to keep the rest of us calm, and I'll allow it.* He took point anyway, sword drawn, because some habits are load-bearing.

* * *

Between the fifth and sixth doors, the road changed.

The walls stopped being dressed stone and became glass, or something like it—thick, cloudy, faintly luminous, and behind the glass there was water, and in the water there were shapes. We all saw them at once and all pretended, for a few paces, not to. Fish, we told ourselves. Statues. Driftwood.

They were neither. They were people—or the memory-echoes of people, pressed into the glass like flowers in a book, thousands of them, faces turned outward, mouths moving in slow silent speech. Not dead. *Stored.* The way the Crown stored. The way the ward-stones stored.

"Don't look at their faces too long," Ilse said quietly. She had gone pale. "Some of these are war-dead. The makers archived everyone they could reach before the end. It was mercy, at the time. It was—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It became something else. I've read about this gallery. In the enforcer archives they call it the Congregation. Reye's superiors think it's a weapon. A reserve of souls they can draw on."

"What do *you* think it is?" Tomas asked.

Ilse walked a few more paces, looking at the drifting faces. One of them—a young man with a farmer's sunburn—turned his head, slowly, to follow her as she passed, and she didn't flinch, but her hand found the shard through the oilcloth and gripped it.

"I think it's a warning," she said. "I think somebody in Vael, near the end, understood what storing people does to them, even kindly. Even mercifully. And they put them here where anyone walking to the Crown would have to see." She glanced at me. "Every ruler who came this way had to walk past the faces of everyone they ruled. To remember what power costs. Most of them, apparently, managed to forget."

The sixth door, when we reached it, didn't ask for grief or love or anything clean. It pressed against me and pulled, and what it pulled was shame—one specific shame, small and hot and humiliating: the night I'd shorted the widow Maren, chapter one of my own private ledger, taking double payment for a single extraction because I was cold and hungry and she wouldn't know the difference. I felt the door take the memory's weight, weigh it, find it sufficient—and I stood there burning with it in front of three people I loved, unable to hide, and hating that the road had chosen *that* one to show.

Sefa squeezed my hand. She didn't ask. That's the thing about Sefa; she never asks for what she already knows.

The door opened. We went through. My face stayed hot for another hundred paces.

* * *

The seventh door nearly killed us.

Later, piecing it together, I understood what happened: the road had been checking me against the memories I carried, and the closer we got to Vael, the more the Crown's shards resonated, and the resonance woke things in the archive-walls. When I touched the seventh door, the faces in the glass all spoke at once—not aloud, but directly, the way memories arrive—and for a moment I was a hundred people drowning in a hundred different centuries.

I went down. The water on the floor—it shouldn't have been there, the road was dry, but the road was dreaming and dreams leak—closed over my face, and it was warm and it tasted of iron and incense and someone else's terror.

Hands pulled me up. Bren, one arm hooked under mine, hauling me back from the door with a strength that had nothing to do with kindness and everything to do with the reflexes of a man who has dragged bodies out of worse places. Ilse had the shard out of its wrapping and pressed to my chest and the hum of it cut through the chorus like a blade.

"Breathe," she was saying. "Orsen. Breathe. You're you. Say it. Say your name."

"Orsen Zhou," I gasped. "Of Hollowmere. Memory-thief. Son of nobody. Grandson of—" and I stopped, because the sentence ended differently now than it had a month ago, ended in *the last bearer of the Last Crown*, and saying it made the walls brighten, made every face in the glass turn toward me at once—

"—grandson of a stubborn old woman who hid the worst thing in the world under my bed," I finished, "and left me the bill."

The faces settled. The light dimmed to its ordinary ghost-glow. The seventh door, unopened, simply dissolved—no ceremony, no judgment, just a soft collapse into mist, as if it had decided the performance was over and we'd earned the rest of the descent.

Tomas sat down hard on a step and put his head between his knees. "I hate this road," he announced to the floor. "I want it noted. Officially. For posterity."

"Noted," said Bren.

Sefa drew a small picture in her book and showed it to me: a heart with a crack in it, mended with stitches of gold. Then she crossed out the gold and redrew the stitches in plain brown thread. *You don't need to be beautiful about it,* the picture said. *Just mended.*

* * *

The ninth door was the last. Beyond it, according to Sefa's maps—the true maps, the ones she'd redrawn from fragments recovered across half the continent—the road ended at the antechambers of Vael itself, the drowned citadel rising out of the seafloor ahead like a mountain that had lost an argument with the ocean.

This door was smaller than the others. Human-sized rather than processional. Plain, almost humble, though the bronze had the same deep green age. And when I laid my hand on it, it didn't pull any memory at all.

It gave one.

I stood in a room I recognized with a jolt that went all the way to my teeth: my grandmother's kitchen in Hollowmere, the copper pots, the smell of cardamom. She was younger in this memory—younger than I'd ever known her—and she was talking to someone off to my left, someone whose presence I could feel like heat from a stove but whose face the memory refused to hold. A maker. One of the last.

*"The valley will be safe,"* the voice was saying. *"Thirteen stones, thirteen seals, the Crown broken and scattered among them. Your descendants will live their whole lives and never know what sleeps under their feet. That's the gift."*

*"And the cost?"* my grandmother asked. She always asked that. I could hear it in her voice—the habit of a woman who had learned, in whatever years had made her, that gifts always had invoices attached.

*A pause. The sound of the sea, impossibly, in a kitchen a hundred miles inland—memory doesn't care about geography.* *"One of your line will have to come back someday. When the seals fail. Someone with the right holes in them. We've arranged the road to know them. But understand, bearer: the road will test them with their own life. Every door will cost. If they arrive at the antechamber with nothing left—"*

*"Then they weren't strong enough,"* my grandmother said, *"and the Crown stays drowned, and that's that."*

*"Is that acceptable?"*

Another pause, longer. And then my grandmother laughed—that short, bark-like laugh that had scared me as a child and that I would have given a year of my life to hear again—and said:

*"Nothing about any of this is acceptable. That's why it has to be done by someone who knows the difference."*

The memory ended. The door stood open.

I stood in front of it for a long time. Behind me, the others waited—they'd learned, on this road, when to wait—and the sea murmured overhead through a thousand feet of glass and stone, and somewhere far below, the Crown was humming to itself in the dark like a thing dreaming of hands.

"She knew," I said finally. Not to anyone. To the door. To her. "She knew it would be me. She *built* the road knowing. All those years of warnings about the ward-stones, all those stories she told sideways and never straight-on—she was leaving me instructions the only way she could, because the makers' magic wouldn't let her write it down."

Ilse came and stood beside me. "Does that make it better or worse?"

"Yes," I said.

She almost smiled. "The makers' records are ahead. Whatever questions you're carrying, the antechambers might actually answer some of them. That's more than most of us get."

I looked at the open door—at the darkness beyond it, where the drowned citadel waited with its hoard of every ruler's certainty and every subject's grief—and I thought about the widow Maren, and the price list I used to keep, and the thirteenth stone cracked like an egg, and my grandmother laughing at the unacceptable world.

Then I stepped through, and the road let me, and the last door closed at our backs with a sound like a book shutting, and below us, rising out of the dark, the lights of Vael began—slowly, one window at a time—to wake.
