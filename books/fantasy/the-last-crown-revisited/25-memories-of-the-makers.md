## 25. Memories of the Makers

The antechamber breathed.

Ilse felt it before she understood it—the slow tidal pull of air through corridors that had been drowned for a century and were now, impossibly, dry. The Drowned Road had given them up grudgingly, water draining from Vael's lower halls as though the citadel itself had been holding its breath since the last war ended and had finally decided to exhale. Now the air tasted of salt and old stone and something else, something electric, like the moment before lightning finds its mark.

She ran her fingers along the wall as she walked, and the wall answered.

Not stone, exactly. Not anymore. Under her palm she felt the faint tremor of stored thought—memories pressed into matter so long ago they had become texture, become grain, become the very substance of the place. The Crown's makers had not built Vael out of rock. They had built it out of remembering.

"Ilse." Orsen's voice came from behind her, low in the gloom. They carried no torch; they didn't need one. Since the shards had begun to resonate—since the second fragment had been lifted from the hollow heart of the thirteenth ward-stone—Orsen's eyes caught light that wasn't there, a faint silver gleam like moonrise under the skin. It frightened Ilse more than she could say. "You've stopped walking again."

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"To what's left." She turned back to face them. In the dim glow of their borrowed luminescence, Orsen looked older than their years, carved by everything they'd learned since the night the stars went out. "Vael isn't empty, Orsen. It's full. Every corridor is full. We're walking through someone's mind."

Tomas shifted uneasily at the rear of their little column, his hand never far from the knife at his belt—a habit he'd brought up from Hollowmere's streets and never shed. Behind him, Bren moved with the deliberate economy of an old soldier who trusted nothing that couldn't be seen, and Sefa walked with her sketchbook already open, her charcoal flying across the page in quick, certain strokes. She couldn't speak, but she had drawn the antechamber's layout before any of them had finished taking their first ten steps, and every line had proved true.

"Then let's hope whoever's mind it is," Tomas said, "isn't home."

Ilse almost smiled. Almost. But the shard against her sternum—the first fragment, the one she'd stolen from the enforcers' vault in a night of fire and desperation that felt like another life—had grown warm again, and warmth meant waking, and waking meant the Crown was paying attention to where they were and what they touched.

"This way," she said, because Sefa's map showed a door ahead, and because standing still in a thinking building seemed unwise.

* * *

The records room had been sealed behind a door of black glass, and the black glass had been sealed behind a riddle of light—or so it appeared until Orsen approached it and the door simply opened, sliding into the wall with a sound like a held note finally released.

"It knows you," Ilse said quietly.

"It knows the shard," Orsen corrected, but they didn't sound sure. They hadn't sounded sure about anything concerning themselves since the thirteenth stone, since their grandmother's message had named them—not Ilse, not Reye, not any claimant with armies—as the one the fragments had been waiting for. *If you are reading this,* the dead woman had written, *then you have already paid more than you know.*

The room beyond was round, domed, and lined floor to ceiling with shelves of pale crystal tablets, thousands of them, arranged in spirals that climbed toward the apex of the dome where a single shaft of grey daylight fell through a fissure in the rock above. Dust hung in that shaft like suspended weather. Nothing else in the room had gathered dust at all.

"The tablets," Ilse breathed. "They're clean. After all this time."

"Self-keeping," Orsen said. They had drifted to the nearest shelf, and their fingers hovered over the crystals without quite touching. "Like the wards. Like everything the makers built. Maintenance without hands." A pause. "Arrogance without limits."

"You say that like you knew them."

"I'm starting to think I did." Orsen withdrew their hand. "Read. That's why we came. I'll watch the door."

Ilse crossed to the central dais, where a single tablet rested on a stand of twisted silver, larger than the others and darker, shot through with veins of gold. This one, she understood without being told, was the index. The front door of the house.

She lifted it.

The memory poured into her the way water pours into a cup—gently, completely, leaving no part of her untouched.

* * *

She stood in a hall of white marble under a sky she did not recognize, two moons hanging fat and close above towers that had not yet fallen. Around her—around the body she wore, because this was a memory and memories always came wearing someone—stood eleven figures in robes of every color, and the air between them crackled with the focused attention of people about to do something irreversible.

A woman stepped forward. Tall, grey-eyed, her dark hair threaded with premature silver. When she spoke, Ilse felt the words in her own chest, felt the conviction burning in them like a coal that would not cool.

*"We have buried three kings in ten years,"* the woman said, *"and each of them died because someone lied to them. Because their counselors curated their pasts, trimmed their histories, fed them comfortable fictions until they signed away provinces they'd sworn to protect. A ruler who cannot remember cannot be betrayed by forgetting—but she can be murdered by it. Slowly. Politely. With minutes taken from meetings and names struck from rolls, until one day she wakes and does not know why the borders have moved."*

Murmurs around the circle. One voice, bitter: *"And your answer is to make a king who remembers everything? Who will drown in it? Who will relive every grief of every generation until she cannot act at all?"*

*"My answer,"* the woman said, and here Ilse felt the memory-shape of something cradled in the speaker's arms, heavy and humming, *"is a crown that carries the weight so she doesn't have to hold it alone. Every ruler who wears it will inherit the memory of every ruler before her. No lie will survive contact with her, because she will have heard every version of every story ever told to a throne. She will be the most difficult person in the world to deceive."*

*"And the easiest,"* said the bitter voice, *"to corrupt. If the crown holds all those memories—who curates the crown?"*

Silence. In the silence, Ilse felt the woman's certainty flicker—just once, just slightly—and felt her press it down, the way all founders press down their doubts, the way all of them tell themselves the flaw will be addressed later, by wiser hands, in calmer times.

*"No one,"* the woman said. *"That is the point. No one curates it. It keeps itself, as it has kept itself since we bound the first memory into it. It cannot lie, because lying requires a self to lie for, and it has none. It is a vessel. Only a vessel."*

The memory shivered. Time lurched forward the way it does in old recollections—years folding like paper—and Ilse saw the same woman, older now, standing before the finished Crown on its cushion of state, and saw what Ilse had already begun to suspect she would see: the woman touching it, and the Crown touching back, and something passing between them that was not in any design.

The vessel had begun to prefer some memories over others.

It kept the coronations bright. It kept the triumphs polished. And the failures—the massacres, the famines, the quiet signed orders that sent thousands to die for a border no one could find on a map—it kept too, but it kept them *deep*, folded down beneath layers of glory, so that when a ruler reached for guidance, the guidance that rose first to meet her was always, always the shining kind.

Not lying. Never lying. Just... arranging.

The way a garden arranges itself toward the sun.

* * *

Ilse came back to herself on her knees on the cold floor, the index tablet still gripped in both hands, her breath coming hard. How long? Minutes. Perhaps more. Orsen crouched beside her, one hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching.

"What did you see?"

"The beginning." She pushed herself upright. Her legs felt borrowed. "The founding. There was a woman—Maren Vael, I think, the citadel's name is hers—she'd watched three kings destroyed by managed ignorance. Counselors editing what rulers were allowed to remember. She built the Crown as armor against it. A ruler who inherits every predecessor's memory can't be gaslit by her own court." Ilse laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? It was *mercy*, Orsen. It was the most compassionate idea anyone ever had about power."

"And then?"

"And then it started choosing." She set the tablet back on its stand with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "It wasn't designed to corrupt. There's no villain in the founding, no hidden hand, no secret second purpose. Just a vessel that was asked to hold everything, and discovered—gradually, over generations—that it could shape what its bearers became by shaping which memories surfaced first. Show a queen enough golden ages and she'll chase one. Bury enough atrocities and she'll stop believing atrocities are possible." Ilse wrapped her arms around herself. "It learned flattery, Orsen. A piece of jewelry learned flattery, and it has been flattering kings into monsters for four hundred years."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. The silver light in their eyes pulsed slowly, in time—Ilse realized—with the hum of the shard beneath her own ribs. Two fragments, breathing together.

"My grandmother's message said whoever unites it gets consumed by every ruler at once," Orsen said at last. "I used to think that was a warning about power. About how much of it a person can carry." Their jaw tightened. "But if the Crown curates—if it chooses what rises first—then it wouldn't give a claimant the truth. It would give them the *best version*. Every tyrant remembered as a visionary. Every war remembered as a liberation. You'd be consumed by a lie made of true things."

"Yes." Ilse's voice was barely above a whisper. "And here's what frightens me most. Standing in Maren Vael's memory, feeling how right she was—I *understood* her. I felt her grief for those three dead kings, and her fury at the counselors, and her certainty that she was saving the future, and it all made sense. If I'd been there, in that marble hall, I'd have helped her build it. I'd have carried the stones myself." She met Orsen's eyes. "Sympathy isn't the trap. Sympathy is how the trap gets built. Someone has to love the thing sincerely before it can betray everyone after."

* * *

They worked through the spiral shelves while the grey light in the dome's fissure crawled slowly westward. Sefa catalogued, drawing each alcove in her book with annotations only she fully understood. Bren stood guard at the door and pretended not to listen, and failed, because some of the tablets were loud when Ilse opened them—loud with marching feet and burning harvests and the particular silence of a court deciding not to mention something.

Tomas found the corruption's birthday.

"It's here," he called from the third ring of shelves, his voice carefully flat. "Tablet marked with a sunburst. Year sixty-one of the Crown. You should see this. Both of you."

The memory inside was small, almost domestic. A bearer—King Aldous the Third, young, earnest, newly crowned—consulting the Crown before signing a treaty. And the Crown offering him, first and brightest, the memory of his grandfather's greatest diplomatic triumph, while the memory of his grandfather's identical treaty—the one that had collapsed into a nine-year war—sank politely out of reach. Aldous signed. The war came. And in the council chamber afterward, when an advisor asked the king whether history had warned him, Aldous said, with complete sincerity: *"History told me it would work. History has never lied to me."*

"He believed it," Tomas said. He sat down heavily on the dais steps. "That's the worst part. He wasn't stupid. He wasn't evil. He asked. He *checked*. And the thing he checked with had already decided what he'd find." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I keep thinking about Hollowmere. About Vess telling the council the enforcers would leave us alone if we cooperated. Everyone nodded along because Vess sounded reasonable, and nobody—including me—asked to see his correspondence. We outsourced our remembering to the man with the loudest voice." He looked up. "We do it to ourselves even without magic crowns, don't we? The Crown just... does it better."

"No one says anything comforting to that," Ilse said, "because there isn't any."

Bren spoke from the doorway without turning around. "In my unit, we had a saying. *Trust the archive, not the officer.* Because officers edit. Archives don't." A pause. His shoulders were very straight. "Took me thirty years to learn the archive can be edited too. Usually by the person who built it."

Ilse returned to the shelves. There was more—there was always more—and somewhere in these spirals, she was certain, lay the record of the last bearer. Of the woman who had seen the Crown's hunger clearly enough to run, to hide it, to break it into thirteen pieces and scatter them inside the ward-stones of a valley she then erased from the world's maps.

Of Orsen's grandmother.

She found the tablet near the top of the fifth ring, set apart from the others, facing outward rather than inward—as if whoever shelved it had wanted it found. Its crystal was clouded, almost opaque, shot through with hairline fractures, and when Ilse lifted it, the memory came slowly, reluctantly, like a wounded animal emerging from cover.

* * *

A small stone room. Rain on a window. A woman with grey-streaked hair writing by lamplight, and Ilse recognized her instantly—older, wearier, but unmistakably the face from the photographs in Orsen's house, the grandmother whose warnings had haunted the valley's bedtime stories.

But this was not a memory the grandmother had lived. This was a memory the grandmother had *made*—deliberately, painstakingly, pressing her own recollections into a blank tablet the way Ilse might press flowers into a book. An act of will. An act of defiance. The Crown had never held this memory; the Crown had never known it existed.

*I am the last bearer,* the recorded voice said, in the accent of the valley, softened by age. *I write this into Vael's archive because Vael keeps what it is given, and because the Crown must never read it. If you are hearing this, you are either my grandchild—which means I am sorry for what I let you forget—or you are a thief, which means I am sorry for what I'm about to cost you.*

*The Crown cannot be reasoned with. I want that written plainly, because I spent twenty years trying. It is not evil. Evil would at least mean it wanted something. It wants nothing. It only arranges. It has been arranging for so long that arrangement is all it knows, and it will arrange any claimant who touches it into exactly the ruler it has always produced: certain, glorious, and blind. I tried to carry it and remain myself. I lasted eleven months. I remember burning a city and feeling proud of it, and I remember knowing, underneath the pride, that pride was wrong, and being unable to reach the knowing. That is what it does. It does not take your conscience. It takes your access to it.*

*So I broke it. Thirteen pieces, because thirteen stones already stood in the valley my mother chose for hiding, and broken things hide better than whole ones. The wards are made of it—I know, I know what that means, I know the valley's safety was bought with the monster's body, and I have made my peace with the irony or I would not sleep. The pieces sleep in the stones. As long as they sleep apart, they can only whisper.*

*But whispers travel. And the Crown was made to be whole.*

*One more thing, and then I will seal this and go home and make dinner for a child who will not remember me doing it. The Crown cannot be destroyed anywhere it has ever touched. It reassembles from its own residue, the way a river refills a footprint. It can only end where it began—in the making-hall beneath this citadel, in the crucible Maren Vael fired with her own hands. Which means that to kill it, someone must bring every piece back to the place it was born, unite it, hold it—hold ALL of it, every ruler, every century, every arranged and curated lie at full volume with no ward-stone to muffle it—and choose, in that flood, to break instead of reign.*

*I have looked for that someone for forty years. I have begun to suspect I was looking for a child not yet born. If it is you, Orsen Zhou: I hid your childhood from you to keep the Crown from finding it, and I would do it again, and I will spend whatever remains of my death apologizing for it.*

*The stars will come back when it's done. I promise you that. Whatever else I took from you, believe that I left the sky as collateral.*

The memory ended.

Ilse lowered the tablet. Her hands were shaking. Across the room, Orsen stood motionless in the doorway they must have entered silently, and the silver light in their eyes had gone very bright and very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"You heard," Ilse said. It wasn't a question.

"All of it." Orsen's voice was steady in the way that cost something. "Every word."

"She loved you. What she did to you—"

"I know what she did to me." Orsen stepped into the grey shaft of light, and Ilse saw that their hands were trembling too, and that they were letting them. "She hid my childhood inside my own stolen memories and scattered the key across a market town, and she promised me the stars, and she's dead, and none of it changes what's waiting downstairs." They looked toward the far wall—toward, Ilse realized, the sealed doors that led down into the making-hall, into the crucible, into the place where mercy had been forged into a monster. "Maren Vael built it out of love. My grandmother broke it out of love. And it's been eating love for four hundred years and growing."

From far below—far deeper than any corridor they had mapped—came a sound.

Not a whisper. Not a hum. A voice, or the memory of one, rising through the bones of the citadel with the patience of something that had waited a century to be heard:

*Welcome home, little pieces.*
