## 26. Reye Defects

The cell smelled of river water and old stone, and Captain Maren Reye had been standing outside it for the better part of an hour without opening the door.

Through the barred window set high in the wall—Vael's drowned outskirts had been a customs house once, before the causeway sank, and its cells still held the architecture of small cruelties—she could hear the mute mapmaker moving. Not pacing. Pacing was what prisoners did when they still believed someone might be persuaded to let them out. This one drew. The scratch of charcoal on paper came in steady strokes, unhurried, like a woman writing letters she knew would be read.

Reye had interrogated forty-one people in her career. She had a method: strip them of time first, then light, then names, until they gave you their memories just to prove they still had any. It worked on everyone eventually.

It had not worked on Sefa.

The woman sat cross-legged on the floor when Reye finally opened the door, surrounded by drawings laid out in careful rows. She did not look up. Her hands kept moving, quick and certain, and Reye stood in the doorway feeling like a man who had come to arrest a river.

"You know I can't let you go," Reye said.

Sefa's charcoal paused. She looked up then—dark eyes, a face weathered by roads Reye would never walk—and lifted one of the drawings.

It was Reye herself. Rendered in a dozen strokes: the captain's coat, the ledger under her arm, and behind her, drawn in lines so faint they were almost not there, a gallows. Not for the prisoner. For the officer.

"Threats," Reye said flatly. "From you. That's new."

Sefa shook her head once. She turned the paper over and drew, fast, right there on the floor while Reye watched: a ledger burning. Then a hand—the mute woman's own hand, unmistakable by the scar across two fingers—holding it open toward another figure. A second figure taking it. And above them both, sketched in three strokes that somehow conveyed an entire sky full of vanished stars, a question mark made of falling light.

*What will you do when the ledger is ash? Who are you then?*

Reye looked at the drawing for a long time.

"I was going to ask you about the route through the causeway," she said. "The sealed doors. Zhou's memories open them—I know that now. I wanted to know if there was another way in."

Sefa tilted her head. *Why?*

"Why does it matter why?"

The mapmaker picked up her charcoal again and wrote, in a neat cartographer's hand directly on the stone floor: *Because you asked me instead of the shard. The shard would have told you anything. I can only tell you true things.*

"That," Reye said quietly, "is exactly the problem."

She left the door unlocked when she went. She told herself it was a test—that a prisoner who ran would confirm she couldn't be trusted, and one who stayed might be worth more than her silence. Both halves of the sentence were lies, and Reye had been in the business of lies long enough to recognize her own handwriting on them.

Behind her, the charcoal never stopped scratching. Steady as a pulse. Steady as something that intended to go on living.

* * *

Her tent that night held two things she could not reconcile, and she laid them out on the field desk like a surgeon laying out instruments.

On the left: the ledger. Forty years of entries in her own hand and her predecessors'. Every name the crown had ever flagged for contact with Crown-magic. Every memory extracted, purchased, or erased. Every village paid to forget and every family paid to be forgotten. She had inherited it at thirty-two from Captain Doss, who had inherited it from a woman whose name had been scraped off the flyleaf, and in eleven years she had added four hundred and six entries without once reading the book straight through. You didn't read a ledger like this straight through. Reading it straight through meant seeing its shape, and its shape was a mouth.

On the right: the shard.

It lay in its padded case where she'd kept it since the ford, and it hummed. Not audibly—she'd checked, twice, with her best listeners pressed against the case—but in the teeth, in the fillings of old wounds, in the marrow. The men complained of headaches when they stood near it. She had ordered it moved to the far end of camp, and then found herself walking to the far end of camp at night without deciding to, the way a compass needle finds north without opinions on the matter.

Tonight she opened the case.

The fragment of the Last Crown sat in its cradle of black velvet like a piece of midnight cut to size, and it was warm, and it was waiting. She knew that much now. She had known it since the interrogation tents downstream, where her own officers touched it and came out quieter, smoother, agreeing with each other in voices that no longer quite belonged to them. The Crown did not need to be worn to work on you. Proximity was enough. Conviction was enough. All it needed was a person who wanted certainty, and every soldier she had ever commanded wanted certainty the way lungs wanted air.

*Whom do you serve?*

The question had been eating her since the ford. She turned the ledger to the page she'd marked and read the entry again, the one that had started all of this rot in her chest:

**ZHOU, VESPERINE — final bearer. Status: concealed asset. Directive: recover Crown fragments; neutralize bearer line.**

Neutralize bearer line. Four words, bureaucratic as a supply requisition, and they meant: kill the grandmother, and anyone who came after her. They meant Orsen Zhou, who had been a child when the order was written. They meant that Reye had spent eleven years hunting a bloodline on behalf of a throne that had been empty for a generation, executing directives issued by ministers who served a Crown that served nothing but its own continuation.

She had asked her superiors, once, early on—who sat the throne these days? Who wore it? The answer had come back in the smooth ministerial grammar she now recognized as the Crown's own voice seeping upward through the hierarchy like damp through plaster: *The Crown holds itself. The realm is governed in trust.*

In trust. For whom? Until when? Trusted by whom?

No one had ever answered, because there was no answer. There was only the machine, turning, and she was a gear in it, and gears did not ask questions. Gears that asked questions got replaced. She had seen it happen to Doss, in the end—Doss, who had grown soft, who had begun keeping private copies of pages, who had died in a fall from his own balcony with his private copies missing and his funeral attended by ministers who smiled too easily.

Reye closed the ledger.

Then she opened it again, took out her flint and steel, and set fire to the corner of the first page.

It burned slowly, the way old paper does, reluctant, each name flaring up gold before it went to black. She fed it page by page into the brazier, watching four hundred and six entries and four hundred years of erasure curl and lift and vanish through the smoke-hole into a sky with no stars to witness it. Some part of her expected to feel freed. She mostly felt hollowed—a building after the tenants leave, still standing, still holding the shape of everything that had lived in it.

But underneath the hollowness, faint as the shard's hum, something else. Something that felt, dangerously, like the beginning of a decision.

When the last page was ash she sat for a long while with her hands over the brazier's dying heat, and then she said aloud, to the empty tent, to the empty throne, to whatever fragment of midnight was listening from the far end of camp:

"No."

It was the first order she had given in eleven years that no one was there to obey. It would not be the last.

* * *

She went to Orsen Zhou alone, at dawn, without escort, which every instinct honed by eleven years of command screamed was madness and which some newer, rawer instinct insisted was the only way it could be done.

They were camped in the shell of the customs house with the strays—the big friend Tomas keeping watch on the causeway road with a fisherman's patience, the old soldier Bren sleeping sitting up with his sword across his knees the way men sleep who have learned that lying down is a luxury, Ilse the fugitive mage awake and rigid the moment Reye's boots sounded on wet stone. The mage moved between Reye and Orsen like a drawn blade, and the shard-fragment at her belt sang a note only Reye's teeth could hear.

"Captain." Orsen Zhou stood in the grey light with their thief's stillness, hands loose and visible, eyes doing the arithmetic Reye had seen them do at the ford: exits, odds, the weight of the people behind them. "You're either very brave or very stupid, coming here without your soldiers."

"Both, probably." Reye stopped ten paces off. Close enough to talk. Far enough that Bren wouldn't feel obliged to make history. "I've brought you two things. One of them you want. One of them you're going to hate."

She set the padded case on the ground between them and stepped back from it.

Orsen didn't move. "That's a trick."

"It's not. Open it."

Ilse opened it—of course she did, snatching it up like a mother reclaiming a stolen child—and the moment the case cracked, the hum filled the room, and Reye watched the mage's face change as the fragment recognized... what? Its twin? Whatever Orsen carried hidden in their own head? The mage looked at Orsen, and Orsen looked at the shard, and between them passed something Reye couldn't see and suddenly, desperately, wanted to.

"The first shard," Ilse said slowly. "Taken at the ford. You're giving it back."

"I'm lending it," Reye said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm not done deciding whose side I'm on. That's the thing you're going to hate."

Tomas had come down from the road, silent as fog, and now stood filling the doorway with his arms crossed. Bren hadn't moved, but his eyes had opened, and they were fixed on Reye with a hatred so old and specific it had its own weather. She met it. She owed him that much and more, though he didn't know yet how much, and she wasn't sure she could survive saying it aloud.

"Talk," Orsen said.

So she talked. She told them about the ledger—told them it was ash, told them what had been written in it, told them the word *neutralize* and let it sit in the room like a corpse. She watched Orsen's face when she said their grandmother's name, and it was worse than grief; it was confirmation, the terrible relief of a suspicion finally given permission to exist. She told them about the ministers and their smooth grammar, about the throne that held itself, about Doss and the balcony. She told them about her officers downstream who touched the shard and came back smoother, and how she had begun to understand that the Crown didn't corrupt people the way poison corrupts blood—it corrupted them the way a current corrupts a swimmer, gently, inevitably, offering rest to anyone too tired to keep swimming against it.

"And your superiors?" Ilse asked. "When they learn the ledger burned, the prisoner walked, and their captain handed the shard to thieves—"

"They'll learn I'm dead," Reye said. "Officially. There's a version of events I can construct that buys us days, maybe a week. After that, they send the Second Cohort, and the Second Cohort doesn't negotiate, doesn't keep ledgers, and doesn't leave witnesses. Which means whatever you're going to do in that citadel—" she nodded toward the drowned towers visible down the flooded causeway, black teeth against the brightening sky "—you do it soon, and you do it with me, because I'm the only person left alive who knows how the Cohort thinks, moves, and kills."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the ruined floors below, patient as a clock.

"Why?" It was Orsen who asked, and they asked it the way Sefa had drawn it: not *why should we trust you*, but *what are you, when the ledger's ash?*

And Reye found—to her surprise, standing in a ruin at dawn among people she had hunted and hurt and herded like inventory—that she had an answer. It had been forming all night in the brazier smoke, and it came out of her rough and unpolished and true.

"I spent eleven years believing I was protecting people from the Crown's power," she said. "Orderly extraction. Regulated forgetting. Better we hold the knife than warlords and kings, that was the doctrine, and I recited it until I believed the recitation. But I've been reading what I burned, Zhou. All those names. Your grandmother's name. Yours. And I understand now that the Crown never protected anyone from anything. It protected *itself*, using us as its hands, and the worst crime of my life is that I was good at being its hand." She looked at the shard in Ilse's grip, humming its hungry note. "I can't unburn the ledger. I can't give back what was taken. But I can stand between the Cohort and whatever you're trying to do, and I can do it better than anyone breathing. That's not surrender. That's a wager. I'm betting my life that you're trying to end this rather than inherit it. Prove me right."

Orsen studied her for a long moment. Then they crossed the ten paces, crouched, and picked up the empty case, weighing it in their hands.

"A wager," they said. "Fine. But understand the terms, Captain. If it comes down to the Crown versus the people in this room, I already know which side I'm on. Make sure you do too."

"I've known for one night," Reye said. "Ask me again in a week."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the thief's face—there and gone, like a fish under ice. Behind them, Ilse lowered the shard, and Bren rose to his feet, and for one suspended heartbeat Reye thought the old soldier was going to run her through where she stood.

Instead he said, in a voice like gravel settling: "You'll tell me. When you're ready. What happened to my unit." It wasn't a question. "And then maybe I'll decide whether you're a person or just a uniform that learned to talk."

"That's fair," Reye said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it.

Down the causeway, the sun cleared the ridge and struck the drowned citadel of Vael, and every window of the sunken hall lit up at once with reflected fire—and beneath their feet, beneath the floodwater, beneath forty fathoms of dark, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to sing.

Every face in the ruined customs house turned toward the sound. The shard in Ilse's hands blazed like a coal.

"It knows we're coming," Ilse whispered.

"Then we'd better not keep it waiting," said Orsen Zhou—and led the way down into the water.
