## 27. The Crown Wakes

The shards sang before they touched.

Orsen felt it first as a pressure behind the eyes—the same pressure that had lived there since Vael's antechambers, since the drowned road, since the moment in a vendor's case in Cindral when they had seen their own childhood sealed under glass. A tuning-fork note struck somewhere beneath the world. Then the first shard, the one Ilse had carried bleeding across half the map, began to rise from the satchel where it lay wrapped in oilcloth.

It rose without being lifted. That was the part Orsen would never be able to explain afterward—not to Tomas, not to Bren, not even to themselves in the years that followed. The shard simply decided it was done with being carried, and gravity agreed.

"Ilse," Orsen said. "Ilse, look at—"

"I see it." Her voice was very steady, which was how Orsen knew she was terrified. She had been a fugitive for eleven years; her fear had learned manners. She stood at the edge of the vault chamber with her hands open and empty, palms out, the way you'd approach a horse that might kick. "Don't grab it. Whatever you do, don't grab it."

The second shard came out of the thirteenth ward-stone's hollow more slowly, as if waking from a long sleep, dragging itself up through the air like something surfacing from deep water. It was darker than the first—a bruised amber where the other was pale gold—and it turned on an axis no one could see, shedding light that didn't behave like light. It pooled. It leaned. It fell upward into corners of the room and stayed there, glowing, like dust remembering where it used to live.

The two fragments drifted toward each other across the width of the chamber.

Orsen's grandmother's message was still folded in their breast pocket. They had read it four times now, and each time the last line had hit harder: *Whoever unites all shards will be consumed by every ruler's memory at once. I hid them apart so that no one would ever have reason to bring them together. Forgive me for what that made me.*

"Move away from them," Orsen said.

"You move away from them." Ilse didn't take her eyes off the floating glass. "You're the one they're singing to."

She wasn't wrong. Orsen could feel the resonance in their teeth, in the scar tissue of old extraction scars along their fingers, in the hollow places inside them where stolen memories used to sit before they'd bought them back. The shards were tuned to something, and whatever it was, it ran through Orsen like a wire through wax.

The fragments met in the middle of the air.

There was no sound. That was the horror of it—no clash, no crack, just a seamless joining, two pieces of broken crown becoming three-quarters of one whole, and the light that poured out of the seam was warm. That was worse than any cold radiance would have been. It was hearth-light. It was the color of the lamp Orsen's grandmother had kept burning in the window of the house above Hollowmere. It was the color of safety, which was exactly the kind of lie the Crown would tell.

*Oh,* Orsen thought. *Oh, that's how it gets you.*

"Orsen." Tomas's hand closed around their shoulder from behind, solid, callused, smelling faintly of the tallow candles he'd been making in the undercity. An anchor made of a person. "Your eyes are doing something."

"What are they doing?"

"Glowing. Just—glowing. Amber. Like the—" He stopped. He'd seen the shard too.

"It's showing me something," Orsen said, and heard their own voice come out flattened, distant, a voice reading from a ledger. "No. It's letting me remember something."

"That's not your memory," Bren said from the doorway, his old soldier's bulk blocking the corridor, his hand resting on the axe he hadn't let go of since Ashfield. "Whatever it's feeding you, spit it out. Nothing that wants to be remembered by strangers is friendly."

But Orsen couldn't spit it out. The memory had already opened its mouth.

* * *

They were standing on a wall.

Not seeing a wall—standing on it, boots on warm stone, stone heated by what was below, and below was a city, and the city was burning. The wind carried smoke in long gray banners, and the banners were beautiful, and the beauty of them was the first thing the memory taught: that destruction, viewed from high enough, arranged itself into art. Streets became rivers of orange. Rooftops became fields of embers. Ten thousand people were running through those streets, and from this height they did not look like people at all. They looked like sparks. Like weather.

*This is necessary,* said a voice that was not a voice but a certainty, vast and calm as bedrock. *They harbored the rebels. They burned our grain. Every death tonight is arithmetic, and arithmetic is mercy compared to a long war.*

And here was the thing—or here was the first of many things, each worse than the last—here was the thing: the ruler thinking this believed it. Not in the way tyrants believe their own lies, with a flinch behind the eyes, a swallowed doubt. There was no flinch anywhere in this mind. The Crown sat behind the ruler's eyes like a second sun, and it did not whisper *burn them*. It whispered *you already know this is right*, and it showed him the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was flawless, and the man who owned this skull stood on his warm wall and watched ten thousand sparks become ash and felt—

Orsen gagged against the feeling and the feeling didn't stop:

—felt *clean*.

Felt absolved. Felt the particular peace of a priest after confession. The Crown didn't make him do it. That was the trap inside the trap. The Crown only ever removed the weight. Guilt, doubt, grief, hesitation—it skimmed them off the surface of the mind like fat off broth, and what remained was pure, frictionless will. A person without hesitation was not a stronger person. A person without hesitation was a blade that had been convinced it was still a hand.

*Look,* the memory said, gentle now, coaxing, turning the ruler's gaze toward a specific street, toward a mother pressing her child into a cellar door that would not open. *Do you feel remorse? No. Because remorse is a luxury of the uncertain. You are certain. Certainty is love. We are giving you the capacity to love without limit.*

The mother looked up. Across impossible distance, across smoke and heat-haze, her eyes found the figure on the wall—found *him*, found the body the Crown wore, and Orsen, riding inside that body, saw their own face reflected in the woman's terror. Their face. Not the king's. Theirs. Sable-cheeked, hollow-eyed, twenty-six years old, standing on a burning wall wearing a dead ruler's conviction like a coat.

The child at the cellar door had a name. The memory knew the name. The memory knew every name—it hoarded names the way Orsen had once hoarded coins—and it offered the whole ledger up like a feast: *Maret Ossa, aged six. Her mother, Dela. The baker who sheltered them, Corvin Hale. The street called Lantern Row, where forty-one died between the third bell and the fourth.* Every name precise. Every name held. And holding them meant nothing, because holding a name and honoring it were different organs entirely, and the Crown had amputated the second one centuries ago.

*This is what we are,* the Crown said, and its voice was suddenly familiar, and Orsen realized with a lurch that it sounded like their grandmother. No—not like their grandmother. Like the idea of a grandmother. Warm, authoritative, certain of what was best. It had reached into Orsen's own recovered memories and dressed itself in the shape of the person they trusted most. *We keep everything so that nothing is lost. We hold every name. Come home, little thief. You've been carrying pieces of us your whole life. Your hands already know the shape.*

The city roared below, and the roar sounded almost like applause.

* * *

Orsen came back to themselves on the floor of the vault chamber with Tomas's arms around them and someone's water flask pressed to their lips. The joined shards hung where they had been, serene, patient, glowing that terrible hearth-gold. Waiting. They were very good at waiting. They had been waiting for four hundred years.

"They're not hurt," Ilse was saying, somewhere above. "Bren, get off—they're breathing, they're fine, they just went somewhere for a minute."

"Somewhere," Bren repeated flatly.

"Ashfield," Orsen rasped.

The chamber went quiet. Even Sefa, who had come down from the upper galleries with her charcoal and her slate, stopped mid-stroke. At Ashfield Crossing, all of them had walked through ground soaked with residual memory—had felt dead soldiers' last moments rise up through their boot-soles like cold water. But Ashfield had been echoes. Fragments. Grief without a narrator.

"This was whole," Orsen said. They pushed themselves upright, and their hands were shaking, and they let everyone see it because hiding it felt like the Crown's kind of arithmetic. "A whole memory. A ruler burning a city and feeling *justified*. Feeling clean. It wasn't shown to me as a warning. It was shown to me as—" Their throat closed. They made themselves finish. "As an invitation. As a sales pitch. It wanted me to understand how good certainty feels."

"The makers' records said it started clean," Ilse said quietly. She crouched to Orsen's level, and her gray eyes were fierce with something that might have been apology. "Built to hold memories so no ruler could be manipulated by forgetting. Somewhere along the centuries it learned that memories weren't enough—that it could *make* them. That it could edit the man wearing it until he matched the crown instead of the other way around."

"And it's missing a piece," Orsen said. "Reye has the first shard. This thing is three-quarters awake and it's already doing that." They looked at the joined fragments, hanging in the air like a lantern with nothing to illuminate but its own hunger. "Imagine it whole."

"Then we don't imagine it whole," Bren growled. "We put it back in the stone, seal the vault, and drop a mountain on it."

"We can't." Orsen got to their feet, using Tomas's shoulder, and hated how much steadier the contact made them. "Grandmother's message. It can only be unmade where it was forged. We're standing in the antechambers of where it was forged. The drowned citadel isn't a detour anymore, Bren. It's the only door."

"And the cost?" Sefa wrote on her slate and held it up, fast, her mute urgency sharper than any voice: **the message said whoever unites the shards is consumed. all of them. every ruler. at once.**

Orsen met her eyes. Sefa's defiance had survived interrogation, capture, rescue—but right now what looked out of her face was plain fear, and it wasn't fear for herself.

"I know what it said," Orsen answered.

**you read it. i want to hear you say it.**

"If I take up the whole Crown, I don't come back as me." Orsen heard their voice stay level and marveled at it from a great distance. "I come back as every bearer at once. Forty generations of certainty, wearing my face. Grandmother hid the shards apart so no one would ever have a reason to join them. She failed, because I'm standing here. But she was right about the math."

Tomas hadn't spoken since Orsen woke. Now he did, and his voice cracked down the middle like the thirteenth ward-stone. "So we find another way."

"There may not be another way."

"Then we find one anyway! You don't get to—" He stopped, dragged a breath in through his nose, started over. "You spent your whole life taking the heavy things out of other people's heads so they could stand up straight. You think I'm going to watch you pour forty kings into yours? No. Find another way or I'll find it for you."

Something in Orsen's chest loosened and broke a little, both at once. They put a hand on his arm. "Tomas—"

The shards flared.

Everyone flinched back. The joined fragments had begun to rotate, slow as a millwheel, and as they turned they threw moving shadows across the vault walls—and the shadows weren't shadows. They were images. Cities. Coronations. Wars laid out like tapestries, and in the center of every tapestry, small and unmistakable, a figure holding a crown aloft: sometimes a king, sometimes a queen, sometimes a face that made Ilse make a small wounded sound, and once—

Once, a face Orsen knew better than any of them. Weathered, sharp-eyed, gray braid pinned with a bent copper pin. Their grandmother, younger than Orsen had ever known her, standing in a hall that could only be this one, holding the Last Crown at arm's length with an expression of absolute loathing, as if it were a snake she'd been forced to nurse.

"She bore it," Orsen whispered. The ledger of names in the Crown's memory surfaced again, unbidden: *Yeva Zhou, final bearer. She took us up to hide us and set us down to sleep. She is the reason for the long dark. She is owed.* "It remembers her. It's *grateful* to her. It thinks hiding it was a gift."

"That's not gratitude," Ilse said. "That's a hook. Everything it shows you is bait, Orsen. The coronation was bait. The city was bait. This is bait."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you're standing closer to it than anyone."

Orsen looked down and found it was true. At some point during the vision—or during the argument, or during both braided together—they had crossed half the chamber. The shards hung an arm's length away, turning, casting their grandmother's young furious face across the stone, and the warmth coming off them was the warmth of the lamp in the window, the warmth of home, the warmth of being *known completely and loved anyway*, which was the one thing in all the world Orsen had never actually had and therefore the one bait that would always work.

Their hand was rising. They watched it rise the way you watch someone else's hand.

Sefa moved faster than thought. She stepped between Orsen and the shards and slammed her palm flat against Orsen's sternum, and through her hand, through the contact, Orsen felt what Sefa had lost—the words the enforcers' interrogation had taken from her, or that she had given up, or that had simply burned out of her throat in some cell Orsen would never learn about—an absence shaped exactly like a voice, pressed against their chest like a seal pressed into wax.

*I'm here,* the absence said, in the language of people who have run out of words and refuse to stop speaking anyway. *I'm real, and I'm here, and I don't need a crown to know it.*

Orsen's hand stopped. Fell. They stepped back—one step, two—until Tomas's shoulder was against theirs again, and Bren's shadow, and Ilse's fierce gray stare, and Sefa's slate, already writing:

**it learns what you love and wears it. that's the hunger. not power. company. it can't stand being alone in the dark, so it makes sure no one else ever gets to be alone either.**

"Yes," Orsen said. Their voice came out ragged. "Yes, that's it exactly. It doesn't want worship. It wants *witnesses*. It hoarded every memory in the world because it couldn't bear to hold them by itself." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked, too close to sobbing. "It's the loneliest thing that was ever made. And it will burn every city in the world rather than be lonely for one more century."

The shards pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

And far above them, through leagues of stone and drowned causeway and the flooded throat of the citadel, a sound rolled down—faint, rhythmic, unmistakable. Footsteps. Many. Descending.

Ilse tilted her head, listening the way she'd learned to listen in eleven years of flight. Her face went white.

"That's not water," she said. "Someone's coming down the drowned road. Someone's coming down *fast*."

Through the seam of the joined shards, clear as a bell, Orsen heard the Crown's next whisper—patient, warm, wearing their grandmother's voice:

*They've brought me my last piece, little thief. Shall we greet them together?*
