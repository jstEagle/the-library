## 28. Vess's Bargain

Tomas felt the militia before he saw them—a tremor in the drowned causeway stones, faint as a heartbeat through the floor of the world. He had been keeping watch at the eastern arch of Vael's sunken hall, where the water lapped black and patient against steps that no living feet had climbed in three hundred years. The tremor came again. Rhythmic. Booted. Many.

He turned to wake the others and found Sefa already awake, already drawing.

She worked by the light of Ilse's shard-lantern, her charcoal flying across a scrap of sailcloth: a line of figures descending the causeway, and above them a shape Tomas knew too well—the broad silhouette of Alderman Vess, hat brim like a knife-blade, staff of office held out before him like a lantern he did not trust to light anything.

"He brought them here." Tomas's voice came out flat, which was how it always went when the worst thing happened; his voice went flat and his hands went cold and some quiet part of him stood aside and watched. "He actually brought them."

Sefa underlined the figure with two hard strokes. Then she drew something else: the militia behind Vess, and among them—she tapped the page twice, urgently—faces. Familiar ones. The cooper's sons. Old Marta from the mill. Denna Hale, who had once paid Orsen to forget a wedding.

*Neighbors*, she wrote beneath, in her careful hand. *Not soldiers.*

Tomas looked at the drawing for a long time.

That was the trouble with Hollowmere, he thought. It had never had soldiers. It had farmers with pikes they'd taken down from barn rafters, and shopkeepers with hunting bows, and boys who'd come because Vess told them the valley's safety could be bought back if only they did this one hard thing. They were not an army. They were a town wearing armor that didn't fit, marching on a drowned city because they were afraid, and fear made people do arithmetic that didn't add up.

He thought about waking Orsen. He thought about it for exactly as long as it took him to remember what he himself had done—the letter he had sent to Hollowmere, the route he had given away, all of it meant to protect the town and instead a blade pointed at the people sleeping ten yards behind him. If he woke Orsen now, he would have to stand there while Orsen read the truth in his face. Again.

Instead he said, "I'll go up. I'll talk to them."

Sefa caught his sleeve. She shook her head, then drew quickly: Vess, and beside him a second figure she rendered smaller, hooded, hands bound.

"Who—" He leaned closer. The charcoal smudged, but the shape was unmistakable. "Is that Bren?"

She nodded once, grim.

The old soldier had gone out at dusk to walk the perimeter—he never slept well inside walls anymore, he'd said, not since Reye's officers had scraped his memories clean as a butchered carcass—and now he wasn't coming back. Vess hadn't just marched a militia into the ruins of Vael. He'd arrived holding a hostage and a bargain, and he'd chosen the one man among them whose memories had already been stolen once.

Tomas closed his eyes. *This is what you did,* said the quiet part of him that watched. *You showed him the door.*

"All right," he said aloud, to Sefa, to himself, to nobody. "Then we don't let it happen twice."

* * *

He went up the causeway alone, hands open and empty, calling out his own name while he was still forty paces from the torchlight so no nervous boy would put a bolt in him. The militia had formed up where the flooded road widened onto dry stone, torches guttering in the salt wind off the deep water, and at their center stood Alderman Vess with Bren kneeling at his feet, wrists bound, one eye swollen shut and bleeding.

"Tomas Weaver," Vess said, and his voice carried the particular warmth of a man greeting a neighbor he intended to ruin. "Still running errands for thieves, I see."

"I'm running errands for my friends, Alderman." Tomas stopped at the edge of the torchlight. He kept his hands visible. He counted the militia—thirty-one, maybe thirty-three, half of them faces he'd known since childhood—and he counted the crossbows, fewer than the men, which meant most of them had come armed with nerve alone. "Let Bren up. He's sixty years old and he fought your father's war."

"He's a collaborator. They're all collaborators." Vess shifted his weight, and Tomas saw the truth of him plainly in the torchlight: the man was afraid. Not performing it, not wielding it—*inhabiting* it, the way a snail inhabited its shell, so that every gesture bent around the fear inside him. Thirty years of feeding names to the crown's ledger-men, thirty years of telling himself it was protection, and now the wards were dead, the stars were dark, and the protection he'd sold his neighbors' secrets for had evaporated like morning fog. So he'd come to buy it back. With interest.

"You've seen what's happening, Tomas. The world outside remembers us now. Every road east is crawling with enforcers, and every one of them has a ledger with our names in it." He raised his staff of office, its iron band catching the firelight. "But the crown remembers its bargains. Bring me the mage and the shard-bearer, hand over the fragment, and the valley's protections are restored. Wards rebuilt. Borders sealed. Hollowmere invisible again, safe forever. That's the offer. That's the *only* offer, and I am the only man standing between these good people and the alternative."

"The alternative being?"

Vess smiled, and it was worse than shouting. "Captain Reye is a reasonable woman. But reason has limits."

Tomas looked past him, at the militia. At Denna Hale, whose knuckles were white around her bow. At the cooper's younger son, who couldn't have been more than seventeen and whose eyes kept flicking toward the dark water as though the drowned city might swallow him for trespassing. At old Marta, who had buried a husband and both sons in the last war and who had once, years ago, sat across Tomas's kitchen table and wept over tea because she couldn't remember her sons' voices anymore—not because anyone had stolen them, but because grief had worn them smooth.

"Marta," he said. "Do you know why the stars went out?"

"Don't you preach at me, Tomas Weaver."

"Do you know why?" he pressed, gentle. "Because the thing your alderman wants to hand over—that crown, those shards—it ate the sky. That's what it does. It takes. It took every ruler's memory and it hoarded them until the hoard got so heavy it cracked the wards that hid us, and now Alderman Vess wants to feed it two more people so it will spit our little valley back out and pretend nothing happened." He spread his hands. "It won't pretend. It never pretends. It just gets heavier."

"Hollowmere has been safe for sixty years," Vess snapped. "Safe under *my* stewardship. Your grandmother trusted me, girl—or have you forgotten that too, along with everything else?"

And there it was. Tomas saw it land, saw Orsen flinch somewhere behind him in the dark—he'd come up without being called, of course he had, silent as a thief—and saw Vess realize a half-second too late what he'd said and to whom.

Orsen stepped into the torchlight.

They looked terrible, Tomas thought. Three days without real sleep, salt-crusted, hollow-eyed, with the shard humming against their chest like a caged bird. But when they spoke, their voice was steady.

"My grandmother trusted you," Orsen said. "Yes. She hid the Crown in our valley and she trusted one man to keep the secret, and that man spent thirty years selling our neighbors' names to the very enforcers she built the wards against." They walked forward, unhurried, and the militia parted without quite meaning to, the way water parts around a keel. "So let's do the arithmetic, Alderman. You want to trade me for the wards. Say it plainly. Say it in front of Marta. In front of Denna. Tell them exactly what price you've set on a life."

"It's one life," Vess said. His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it, Tomas could see that, hated the crack and feared it, and fear made him louder. "One life against four hundred. Any honest person would make that trade."

"One life," Orsen agreed. "Mine. And after me, whose? Because the Crown doesn't stop wanting, Vess. It doesn't get full. And the men who served it—they don't either. You'll hand me over, and in a year they'll want the mapmaker. In two, the soldier. In five, everyone in this valley who ever touched a memory, which by Captain Reye's ledger is nearly all of you." They stopped an arm's length from him. "Your grandmother's name isn't on that ledger. Mine is. My mother's is. Where does it end?"

Silence, except the water and the wind and the guttering torches.

It was Denna Hale who broke it. She lowered her bow—not all the way, but enough that the point aimed at mud rather than flesh—and said, quietly, "My sister sold Orsen a memory once. A bad one. And Orsen took it fair and gave change honest, and never once used it against her." She looked at Vess. "Can you say the same, Alderman? About any of us?"

Vess's jaw worked. Behind him, Bren lifted his head, blood crusting at his brow, and laughed—a low, ruined sound.

"There it is," the old soldier rasped. "There's the whole war, right there, in one question. Whether the man with the ledger knows what's in it."

"Enough." Vess's composure finally tore, and underneath it was just a frightened old clerk of a man, clutching his iron-banded staff like a rosary. "Enough talk. Seize them. Both of them. Anyone who resists—" He faltered, looking at the faces of his neighbors. "Anyone who resists will answer to the crown."

Nobody moved.

Thirty-three Hollowmere folk stood in the wind off the drowned sea, and not one of them took a step. The cooper's son looked at his boots. Marta looked at the sky, at the starless hole where the stars should be, and something in her face changed—something long deferred finally arriving.

"We followed you because you said this would bring the safety back," she said slowly. "But I heard what the girl said. I heard it plain. And I'm thinking about my boys, Alderman. I'm thinking about how I couldn't hold their voices in my head anymore, and how much I would give, now, to have even the bad days back. Even the worst day of their lives back." Her voice thickened. "And this thing you want to give them—it *keeps* all that. It keeps everything. Every memory anyone ever gave it. And you want to feed it more?"

"The wards—"

"The wards are dead, you foolish old man." Marta's voice rose, cracking wide. "They've been dead since the stars went out, and you knew it, and you lied to us on the road here. Didn't you. Didn't you."

Vess said nothing. And that nothing was the loudest answer in the history of Hollowmere.

Tomas watched the militia understand it, watch it move through them like a wave through standing grain. Denna set her bow down entirely. The cooper's son unstrung his. One by one, torches were lowered, and Vess stood alone in a ring of his neighbors' silence, holding a staff of office that suddenly weighed as much as a tombstone.

"You're making a mistake," he whispered. "All of you. When they come—and they *will* come—I won't be able to protect you."

"No," Tomas said softly. "You won't. You never could. That was the whole lie, Alderman. There was never any protection for sale. There was only fear, and you were the one collecting the payment."

For a moment, Tomas almost pitied him. Almost. Then Vess's eyes went past them all, toward the black arches of Vael, toward the place where the shard in Orsen's coat had begun—not subtly, not gently—to *sing*, a thin high resonance that made teeth ache and torch flames lean sideways, and Tomas saw the fear in the old man's face transmute into something hungrier and more desperate.

"If I can't sell you to the crown," Vess breathed, "then perhaps the crown doesn't need me to deliver you at all. Perhaps it only needs me to *open the way*."

He raised the staff of office high, and its iron band flared with a light that was not firelight, had never been firelight, and far below in the sunken hall, Tomas felt the water begin to move—all of it at once, all of it *toward* them—as something ancient in the drowned city answered the call of its oldest servant's key.

"Run," Bren roared, surging up despite his bonds. "Run, you fools, the vault doors are opening—"

The sea came up the causeway like a wall with a grudge.
