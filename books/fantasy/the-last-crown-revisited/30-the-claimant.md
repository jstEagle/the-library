## 30. The Claimant

The Crown called her by name.

Not *Ilse*—not the name her mother gave her in a fishing village that no longer appeared on maps—but the older name, the one stitched into her bones before she was born. It spoke it through the sunken hall of Vael the way a bell speaks through water: slow, resonant, impossible to ignore.

*Heir.*

She stood at the edge of the flooded antechamber with the first shard burning cold against her sternum where she'd carried it for a hundred days and a hundred nights, and she understood, finally, what the theft had cost her. Not safety. Not sleep. Those were small prices. What it had cost her was the right to be ordinary. The Crown had felt her hands close around its fragment in the enforcers' vault beneath Cindral, had felt her blood on its broken edge when she cut herself climbing out, and from that moment it had been waiting for her the way a door waits for a key.

"Ilse." Orsen's voice, low, at her shoulder. "Don't listen to it."

But that was the terrible thing. She couldn't not listen. Listening was all she had done since the night the stars went out.

* * *

It began as warmth.

The hall of Vael had been drowned once and drained twice; the makers had built it to survive both. Water still pooled ankle-deep across the mosaic floor, and in every puddle the light moved wrong—not reflecting the glow of Orsen's lantern but throwing light of its own, gold shot through with violet, the color of memories Ilse didn't own yet. The assembled shards hovered above the dais at the hall's center, three fragments turning slowly around an absence shaped like a crown, and the absence was the loudest thing in the room. It pulled. It pulled at her the way the tide pulls at a hull, patiently, without malice, certain of its own strength.

And into that pull poured the rulers.

She felt the first of them like a hand laid flat against the back of her neck: a woman in bronze, four thousand years dead, standing on a wall at dawn while her enemies' fires burned out below her, feeling not triumph but the vast gray relief of a war finished. Then another—a boy-king crowned at nine who had learned to make his voice steady by pretending his dead father stood behind him, until the pretense wore grooves so deep he could no longer hear his own thoughts over the echo. Then a queen who had wept only once in sixty years of rule, alone, in a garden, and had made even that single grief into policy.

They came gently. That was the trap's genius. They did not seize her; they *offered*. Here is certainty, they said. Here is the end of doubt. You have run since you were nineteen years old—you stole because you were afraid, you fled because you were afraid, you have slept in ditches and under false names because every authority in the world wanted your head—and we are offering you the one thing no fugitive ever has.

A throne no one can take from you.

"Her eyes," Sefa said. The mute mapmaker's voice was a rare thing, hoarse from disuse, and when she used it people listened. She was pointing at Ilse's face. "Look at her eyes."

Ilse hadn't known they'd changed. She saw it now in the black water at her feet: her own reflection wearing irises of hammered gold, and behind them, layered like panes of stained glass, faces that were not hers. The bronze woman. The boy. The queen. Dozens more, hundreds, receding into a depth no human eye should hold.

She should have been horrified. She was—faintly, distantly, the way one is horrified by news from a far country. But closer than the horror, warmer than it, was the certainty. That was the thing no one warned you about. Not power. Power was just weight. It was *certainty* that the Crown offered, and certainty felt exactly like coming home after a long time drowning.

*You have doubted everything,* the rulers murmured together, in a voice that was somehow also her own. *Doubt is the tax the powerless pay. We abolished it.*

* * *

"Ilse." Orsen again, closer now, wading toward her through the shallow water. Their hand found her wrist—the grip of someone who has pulled drowning strangers from rivers before and does not intend to lose the habit. "Whatever it's showing you, it's showing you a lie. I've touched the shard too. I know how good it feels."

"You don't." Her voice came out strange, doubled, as if two women were speaking through one throat. She tried to shake free and found she didn't actually want to. That frightened her more than anything else. "Orsen, you've never ruled anything. You've never had to decide which village burns and which one doesn't. You've never sent anyone anywhere. You've spent your whole life taking things from people one memory at a time—I've seen your ledgers, love, don't look at me like that, I read them while you slept—and you did it because deciding nothing kept you safe. This—" she lifted her chin toward the hovering shards, and the gesture was not entirely hers; some bronze-clad ghost had taught her shoulders how to carry command, "—this decides *everything*, once, forever, and never asks you to choose again."

"That's not mercy," Orsen said quietly. "That's just a bigger cage."

"And yours was smaller!" The words tore out of her with a force that surprised them both, echoing off the drowned pillars. "At least this cage has a view."

Bren had moved without her noticing—the old soldier always did, a habit of forty years that no amount of forgetting could fully erase—and now he stood between her and the dais, planted like a fence post, arms crossed. He didn't threaten. He simply occupied space, the way he had at Ashfield Crossing when the residual memories rose off the battlefield like swamp gas and half of them had gone mad with borrowed grief. He had talked her down that night too. *Name five things you can see,* he'd said. *That's how you stay yourself. The ground remembers so you don't have to.*

"Five things," he said now.

"What?"

"Five things you can see. Right now. Go on."

Ilse laughed—a cracked, ugly sound—and then, because Bren's face did not change, because he would stand there until the citadel finished flooding if that was what it took, she looked.

"The water. Your ugly coat. Orsen's lantern. The mosaic—it's a map, isn't it, under the muck, it's a map of the old world. And—" her gaze snagged on the last one, and something in her chest twisted, "—Tomas. Standing in the doorway looking like he's about to be sick."

"Aye, that's him." Bren nodded slowly. "Now name the faces in your eyes. Can you? All of them?"

She reached for them and found—horror arriving late, arriving properly—that she could not count them. They shifted when she tried to number them, the way stars shift when you stare directly at their constellation. There had been four thousand rulers. Perhaps more. Each one certain. Each one certain in a way that had ground whole nations beneath it like millstones, and none of them, not one, had ever once doubted enough to stop.

*We never doubted,* they whispered, and for the first time she heard what lived underneath the comfort: not confidence but terror, ancient and bottomless, the terror of men and women who had taken up absolute power precisely because they could not bear the alternative, who had worn the Crown because being uncertain in private was unbearable and being certain in public was the only cure anyone had ever sold them.

*We never doubted,* said the Crown, *because we could not afford to. Neither can you.*

"There it is," Ilse whispered. "There's the hook."

"Ilse?" Orsen's grip tightened.

"It's afraid." She turned to look at them, and her voice steadied as she named it, the way naming always steadied things. "All those kings and queens, all that certainty—it's fear, Orsen. Four thousand years of people who couldn't stand not knowing, and the Crown learned to hunt for anyone else who feels that way. It's not choosing me because I'm worthy. It's choosing me because I'm tired."

* * *

The Crown changed tactics. Of course it did. It had worn strategists the way other relics wore gilt.

The water around her ankles went warm. The gold light softened to candle-color, and suddenly she was not in Vael at all—she was nineteen, in the enforcers' vault beneath Cindral, the night of the theft, except the vault was lit kindly and the shard lay on a velvet cloth instead of a steel tray, and there was no blood, no alarms, no running. In this version she had simply been *given* it. Chosen. A young woman in a plain coat, and the most powerful object in the world saying: *yes, you, of course you, who else?*

She knew it was false. She knew the real night had smelled of oil and rust and her own terror-sweat, that she had left a guard bound and gagged who might have suffocated if the rotation hadn't changed, that she had not thought of that guard in ninety days and thought of him now with a shame that arrived like ice water. The Crown was editing her. Taking the ugliest night of her life and re-rendering it as destiny.

And gods help her, some part of her wanted to let it.

Because here was the arithmetic she had been doing silently for a hundred days, the sum she'd never shown anyone: if the theft had been destiny, then it hadn't been a crime. If the Crown had chosen her, then the guard she'd hurt, the enforcer she'd killed on the wall above the vault—he had died as part of a design, and designs absolve. Every ruler in her head was whispering the same lesson in different accents: *what you did was necessary, and necessary things are not sins.* It was the oldest sedition in the world, dressed in candlelight.

"No," Ilse said aloud.

The vision held a moment longer, tender as a mother's hand, and then she drove her heel down into the floodwater and the mosaic map beneath it and the pain of stone through boot-leather snapped the thread.

"No," she said again, louder, to the Crown, to the ghosts, to the girl in the velvet-lit vault. "I know what I did. I'm going to keep knowing it. That's the whole point of me."

* * *

The shards flared.

For a heartbeat the whole sunken hall went white, and in that white Ilse felt the Crown drop its gentleness entirely—felt, for one naked instant, the thing beneath the rulers, the hunger that had corrupted a tool built to preserve memory into a machine that hoarded it. It was not evil. That was the last horror, the one she would carry out of Vael and to her grave: it was not evil. It was a library that had learned to want readers, a reliquary that had learned to want worship, four thousand years of certainty compressed until it had become a gravity well, and it had been calling to her across a hundred leagues not because she was special but because she was *available*.

Then the white guttered, and the shards sank back to their slow turning above the empty throne, and the pull eased—not gone, never gone, but reduced to a tug, a tide, a suggestion.

Ilse found she was on her knees in the water. She had no memory of falling. Orsen was beside her, both hands on her arm now, and Tomas had come all the way into the hall despite the way his face said he'd rather be anywhere else, and Sefa was sketching fast in her waterproofed book—drawing Ilse's face, Ilse realized, drawing the gold fading out of her eyes, documenting it, because that was Sefa's courage: to record.

"I nearly took it." Ilse's voice came out scraped raw. "You should know that. All of you. I stood here and I wanted it, and if Bren hadn't—if I'd been alone—"

"You weren't alone," Orsen said. "That was the test, maybe. Not the wanting. Everyone would want it. Even me." A pause, and then, with the honesty that Ilse had come to trust more than any oath: "Especially me. I've spent my whole life invisible. Imagine what that thing could offer someone like me."

"So why aren't you on your knees?"

"Because I already met my temptation." Orsen glanced toward the doorway, toward the sound—distant but unmistakable—of boots on the causeway above. Many boots. Hollowmere militia boots, and behind them, the measured tread of enforcers. "It's walking down here right now, wearing Alderman Vess's face and Captain Reye's ledger. The Crown doesn't need to tempt me tonight. The world's doing it for free."

Ilse got to her feet. Her legs shook. She let them.

Above the empty throne, the three shards turned, patient as seasons. It would call again. It would call softer next time, or crueler, or it would wait until the hall was full of armed frightened people and offer itself to whichever of them broke first. She understood now that this was the true shape of the trap—not a single moment of choice but a siege, a long attrition against every doubt and every desire any of them carried.

But she also understood something else, something the Crown had shown her without meaning to, buried in the queen who wept once in sixty years: the rulers had all worn it *alone*. That had been the design and the disease both. One head, one crown, one certainty, alone in a hall full of subjects.

Ilse looked around the flooded hall—at Orsen with the stolen memories locked behind their eyes, at Bren who remembered being forgotten, at Sefa who drew truth she could not speak, at Tomas who had betrayed them out of love and would spend the rest of his life paying for it, at the doorway where torchlight was beginning to flicker down the drowned road.

Alone, the Crown was a hunger. Together, they might be something else.

"It called me heir," she said. "Let it keep calling. But hear me—all of you—if it takes one of us, it takes all of us, and we decide together. That's the only way any of us walks out of here as ourselves."

Boots on the causeway. Closer now. And beneath them, rising through the water, through the stones, through the soles of her feet, the Crown began—softly, sweetly, like a lullaby—to sing to everyone in the hall at once.
