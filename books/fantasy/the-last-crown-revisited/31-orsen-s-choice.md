## 31. Orsen's Choice

The antechamber of Vael had a ceiling like the inside of a drowned bell, and Orsen had been staring at it for an hour without seeing it.

Around them, the others slept in the ways people sleep when they know morning will bring a fight: Ilse curled tight as a fist, Sefa sitting upright with her charcoal stub still in her hand, a half-finished sketch of the sunken hall on her knee. Bren snored with the practiced economy of a man who had learned to rest between wars. Tomas was awake too—Orsen could tell by his breathing, too even, too deliberate—but neither of them spoke. There would be time enough for words tomorrow, and not enough of them.

Orsen sat apart, in the crook of a fallen archway, and turned the second shard over in their hands.

It was warm. It had been warm since the thirteenth stone, since Grandmother's message had burned itself into Orsen's mind in her own dry voice: *The Crown can only be unmade where it was made. And whoever unites all shards will wear every ruler at once.* They had carried that warning down the drowned road like a stone in the chest, and now, here at the threshold of Vael itself, Orsen finally made themselves look at what else the shard wanted to show them.

Not memories this time. Something older. A map—not drawn but *felt*, a lattice of power laid over the world the way a hand lays over a wound. The Crown had been forged whole once, in this citadel, by makers who feared forgetting more than they feared tyranny. And when it was broken—when the first rebellion shattered it into thirteen pieces—the rebels had done something clever and terrible with the fragments.

They had built walls with them.

Orsen's breath went shallow. The lattice resolved, and every node of it blazed with a light Orsen knew intimately: the pale, humming glow of ward-stones. Thirteen stones ringing a valley. Thirteen shards of the Last Crown, sunk into the earth of Hollowmere before Orsen's mother was born, singing their silent song of *not-here, nothing-here, look-away* — and the valley had believed itself safe ever since. Safe because it was invisible. Invisible because it was built out of the bones of the thing that hoarded every memory ever worn.

*Oh,* Orsen thought. *Oh, you clever, cruel woman.*

Grandmother had known. Had to have known. She had been the Crown's final bearer; she had helped break it; she had chosen the valley herself, or been chosen by it. She had spent her whole life in Hollowmere dropping warnings like seeds—*count the stones, child, count them every season*—and she had never once said why. Because saying why would mean admitting that the valley's peace was not peace at all. It was anesthesia. Every soul in Hollowmere lived inside a fragment of the Crown, wrapped in its forgetting, and called that safety.

Orsen looked down at the shard in their palm and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what destroying the Crown would actually cost.

To shatter it—to do it properly, to end the age of singular power the way Grandmother's message described—you could not just break the assembled thing in Vael. You had to unmake every fragment, everywhere. You had to pull the thirteen pieces out of the thirteen stones. You had to take the walls down.

And the valley—

The valley would be *seen*. For the first time in three generations, Hollowmere would exist to the world outside. Every debt, every grudge, every army and tax collector and remnant enforcer with a ledger would be able to find it. The wards kept magic out, everyone said. But that had only ever been half the truth. The wards kept the world out. And the world, once invited back in, did not knock.

Orsen thought of Alderman Vess, who had sold names to the crown for years out of fear dressed up as duty. Thought of Captain Reye, whose ledger held the name Zhou in careful ink. Thought of the widow from the first hard winter, whose grief Orsen had lifted for coin, and of every door in Hollowmere they had robbed in smaller, quieter ways. If the valley became visible, all of that came home. All of it, at once, with interest.

There was another way. Of course there was. There was always another way, and it glowed as gently as a hearth.

Leave the shards in the stones. Let the Crown stay broken but alive, its pieces scattered and sleeping, holding up the valley's invisibility forever. Take the found family and go somewhere else—Cindral, the coast, anywhere—and let Hollowmere keep its anesthesia. Let the widow keep her purchased peace. Let Orsen keep being invisible, comfortable, a thief among thieves in a town that didn't remember its own name.

No one would ever have to know. That was the whole architecture of the place. No one would ever have to know anything.

The shard pulsed against Orsen's skin, patient as a held breath.

"You've been sitting there long enough to grow moss."

Tomas. He crossed the antechamber quietly, stepping over Bren's outstretched boots, and lowered himself onto the archway beside Orsen with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. He didn't ask to see the shard. He'd learned that much on the road.

"Can't sleep either?" Orsen said.

"I can sleep fine. I just choose not to, so I don't dream about militia spears." He rubbed his face. "You've got your thinking face on. The bad one. The one you used to make before you picked someone's pocket at the harvest fair."

"That was one time."

"Three times." He was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "What is it?"

Orsen turned the shard so its inner light caught the underside of Tomas's jaw. "Do you remember what the wards felt like? When we were kids. Before they died."

"Like the air after rain. Like the whole valley was holding its breath for us." Tomas smiled faintly. "Why?"

"Because I know what they were made of now." Orsen heard their own voice come out flat, which was how they knew how much it cost. "The Crown. Thirteen fragments, one in each stone. The valley wasn't protected, Tomas. It was *anesthetized*. We've been living inside the thing we're supposed to destroy."

Tomas went very still. Orsen watched him arrive at the arithmetic—they were good at sums, Tomas, always had been, better than Orsen—and watched his face change as the total came in.

"So if we shatter it," he said slowly, "the stones die. The wards die."

"The valley stops being hidden. Forever. Everyone in Hollowmere becomes findable. Vess. The widow. Your mother." Orsen swallowed. "Me. Especially me."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the Crown stays alive. Sleeping, scattered, hungry. And someday—maybe not us, maybe our kids' kids—someone finds all the pieces again. Someone like Reye's superiors. Someone worse. And next time there won't be a grandmother hiding shards in ward-stones, because no one will remember there's anything to hide." Orsen closed their hand around the shard. "Safety now, bought with forgetting. Or truth now, paid for with everything."

Tomas exhaled through his teeth. "That's not a choice. That's a knife with two edges."

"It's the only honest thing anyone's offered me in years."

They sat with it. Somewhere deeper in the citadel, water moved through drowned corridors with a sound like slow applause. Above them, through cracks in the vaulted dark, the sky where stars should have been stayed black and starless—the Crown's hunger still eating the light—and Orsen found that detail decided something in them more firmly than any argument could have. A world with no stars overhead was not a world. It was a room. And Orsen had spent their entire life in rooms.

"Tomas," they said. "When I left payment on the doors. In Hollowmere. Did it help?"

He considered it honestly, which was one of the thousand reasons Orsen loved him. "I don't know. I think it helped *you*. I think that mattered."

"That's what I keep coming back to." Orsen looked at their own hands, thief's hands, memory-thief's hands. "I built my whole life on the idea that taking pain away was a kindness. That forgetting was mercy. Sell the widow her peace, pocket the coin, walk away clean. And it worked, right up until I saw my own childhood in a vendor's case with a price tag on it. Up until I understood what it feels like to have someone decide for you what you get to remember." They laughed, short and bitter. "I was the Crown, Tomas. Little version. One-woman Crown, working out of a cottage in Hollowmere."

"You were also the one who took me in when my father threw me out," Tomas said. "People are allowed to be both things. That's rather the point of everything we've walked through, isn't it?"

"Maybe." Orsen stood. Their legs ached; the drowned road had taken its toll. "But I'm done deciding for other people what they get to carry. If the valley wakes up—if everyone remembers what was taken from them, including what *I* took—then they get to hate me for it. Fairly. With full information." They looked down at Tomas, and let themselves say the true thing, plainly, the way Grandmother's message had been plain. "I choose the version where we're seen. Even if what they see is ugly. Even if some of them never forgive me. A valley that remembers me as a thief is worth more than a valley that doesn't remember at all."

Tomas looked up at them for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded over a finished piece of joinery. "All right," he said. "Then we'd better make sure you survive being seen. Because I'm not carrying this family alone."

From across the antechamber, a dry voice: "Neither am I." Bren hadn't moved; his eyes were open, had perhaps been open the whole time. "For what it's worth, soldier's opinion? Walls made of stolen things always fall. Better you pick the hour than the wall does."

Sefa said nothing, as always. But she had risen, and she crossed to Orsen, and she pressed a fresh sketch into their hands: the great hall of Vael, drawn from below, from the angle of someone standing at its heart—and at the center of the drawing, where the dais would be, she had drawn a small figure with its arms raised, and around the figure she had drawn thirteen lines radiating outward like rays, like roots, like cracks in glass.

She met Orsen's eyes. *I already drew your choice*, the picture said. *Now live up to it.*

Only Ilse was left. She had woken too—of course she had; mages slept like cats—and she stood at the edge of the firelight with the first shard cupped in her scarred hands, watching Orsen with an expression Orsen had learned to read on the road from Cindral: hope, held at arm's length so it couldn't be hurt.

"You understand what it means," she said. Not a question.

"I understand that my grandmother built a cage out of the monster's bones and called it home," Orsen said. "And I understand that I'm her grandchild, and I can do better."

Ilse came forward and held out the first shard. "Then we finish it together. And Orsen—" her voice caught, steadied, "—whatever the valley decides you are, afterward. You will not be alone in it. I have been hunted by every crown that ever claimed me. Being *known* is the first kind thing that has ever happened to me."

Orsen took the shard. The two fragments touched, and sang—one low note, deep as a bell struck underwater, and every drowned corridor of Vael answered it, and far above, through the broken roof, the empty sky flickered. Once. Like an eye trying to open.

And then, from the direction of the causeway, came a sound none of them needed explained: boots. Many boots. The ring of militia steel, and beneath it, thin and carrying across the flooded halls, a voice Orsen had known since childhood, calling out with the confidence of a man who had already sold his bargain:

"Hollowmere comes in peace! Bring out the thief Orsen Zhou, and the valley keeps its protections!"

Vess. Here. Already.

Orsen closed their fingers around both shards, felt their heat climb toward unbearable, and turned toward the great hall where the Crown waited to be whole.

"Well," they said. "Let's go be seen."

* * *

The doors of the sunken hall stood open, and the water lay across its floor like a sheet of black glass, and standing on that glass—as though it were marble, as though the drowned citadel had raised him a stage—was Alderman Vess, flanked by forty militiamen in mismatched armor, and behind them, filling the far archways like a tide coming in, the gray ranks of the enforcers.

Between the two forces, on a plinth of fused stone at the hall's heart, the Crown's empty frame hovered above the water, turning slowly, waiting for its last two pieces.

Waiting for Orsen.

Reye stood at the enforcers' front rank, and when her eyes found Orsen across the hall, she did not draw her sword. She gave the smallest nod—a nod that said *I burned the ledger, I brought the shard, the wager stands*—and then her gaze flicked, meaningfully, to the frame above the water, and to the narrow window of chaos between Vess's speech and the enforcers' advance.

One chance. One breath of a chance.

Orsen stepped out onto the black glass, the shards burning in both fists, and began to walk toward the throne that had eaten every ruler who ever wore it.

Behind them, softly, Sefa began to draw.
