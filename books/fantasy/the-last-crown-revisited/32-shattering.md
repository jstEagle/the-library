## 32. Shattering

The Crown hung in the air above the sunken hall, and it was singing.

Not with sound—Orsen felt it behind their sternum, a vibration like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The three shards had found each other the moment Reye set them down on the flooded flagstones, and now they turned slowly, gold threading itself to gold, seams of light knitting closed. Every ruler who had ever worn it was in there. Every conquest, every coronation, every midnight decision that had sent ten thousand people to their deaths for a border no one could see from a horse.

And it was calling Orsen's name.

*Come,* it said, in a voice made of every voice at once. *You were always meant to carry me. Your grandmother knew. She hid you here like a coin beneath a floorboard.*

"Orsen." Ilse's hand closed around their wrist, hard enough to bruise. Her face was pale in the Crown's glow, and water lapped at her knees—the citadel was drowning faster now, the sea pouring through breaches in the drowned road they'd come by, filling the great hall inch by inch. "Whatever it's telling you—it lies. That's what it does. It tells you exactly what you most want to be true."

"It isn't lying," Orsen said, and hated how steady their voice came out. "That's the worst part. It's telling the truth."

Because it was. They could feel it now, the way they'd felt their own stolen memories when they'd bought them back in Cindral—that vertiginous recognition, the sense of a door in themselves swinging open onto a room they'd always known was there. The wards of Hollowmere were built from this. Thirteen stones, thirteen fragments, and their grandmother had carried the last one into the valley and set it into the earth like a keystone, and the valley had vanished from every map and every memory beyond its borders because the Crown remembered how to make things forgotten.

Their whole life—the trade, the extraction, the careful invisible years—all of it had been built inside a lie their grandmother had paid for with everything she was.

"Orsen." Tomas, at the edge of the dais, water to his thighs, holding Sefa upright. The mapmaker was gray-lipped and shaking, her wrists raw from the enforcers' bindings, but her eyes were fixed on the Crown with an expression Orsen had learned to read: she was memorizing it. Even now. Even drowning. "The stairs are gone. Whatever you're going to do—do it soon."

Across the hall, the militia stood in a ragged line along the broken colonnade, Vess at their front with his alderman's chain glinting wetly, his face a mask of terror he couldn't hide anymore. Behind them, Captain Reye's enforcers had stopped advancing entirely. Reye herself stood with her sword still sheathed, watching the Crown turn, and Orsen saw the exact moment she understood what Orsen understood: that none of them were going to fight over it. That fighting over it was already losing.

"The valley," Orsen said. Not to anyone. To the Crown. "You want to know what I remember? I remember my grandmother's hands. I remember her teaching me to count the ward-stones. Thirteen, she said, and never told me why thirteen mattered." They waded forward, one step, two, the water dragging at their legs like hands. "She spent her whole life keeping you in pieces. And everyone called it peace."

*It was theft,* the Crown said gently. *I am memory. All of it. Every ruler took only what they needed—*

"Took." Orsen laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Yes. I know about taking. I made my living at it. Do you know what a widow pays to forget her husband dying? Everything she has. Do you know what she gets back, if she ever gets it back? Nothing. A hole shaped like him." Another step. The light of the Crown washed over them, warm as a hearth, and underneath the warmth Orsen felt the hunger, vast and patient, the hunger of something that had eaten centuries. "You're not memory. You're the *hoard*. Memory belongs to everyone or it belongs to no one, and you decided it belonged to you."

*Then take me,* the Crown said, and its voice went soft, went tender, went grandmother-warm. *Take me and end the taking. One bearer, one will, and no more wars over me. You would be kind with me. I have seen your kindness. It is the kindest hand I have ever offered myself to.*

And there it was. The trap with no walls. Because Orsen *would* be kind. They knew it the way they knew their own heartbeat—they would wear it gently, they would give memories back, they would spend a hundred years undoing the harm, and somewhere in year forty or sixty they would decide, reasonably, mercifully, that some things were better kept. That some people were better guided. That the world was heavy, and someone had to hold it.

Every ruler in the Crown had thought exactly that. Orsen could feel their certainty stacked like sediment, layer upon layer, all of it reasonable, all of it loving, all of it ending in ash.

"Ilse," Orsen said quietly. "When I was a child. Before the valley. Was there ever—"

"You're stalling," Ilse said. But her eyes were wet. "Ask me after. Ask me when you're still *you* to hear the answer."

Orsen nodded. That was answer enough.

They looked back once—at Tomas gripping Sefa's arm, at Bren standing between the militia and the dais with his old soldier's body planted like a fence post, at Reye, who met their eyes and gave the smallest nod, a captain surrendering a battle she'd finally decided wasn't hers—and then Orsen reached up and seized the Crown with both hands.

The world went white.

* * *

They were everywhere at once.

A coronation in a hall of black marble, a boy of nineteen kneeling while the Crown was lowered onto his head and feeling himself become wise. A queen standing on a wall watching her own army burn a city she had ordered burned, weeping, and knowing even as she wept that she would order it again. A king signing a decree of forgetting with a steady hand, erasing a province, eleven thousand names, because they had worshipped wrong. A bearer in the dark ages whispering *just a little longer, just until things are stable* across four hundred years of stability that never stabilized. A woman with Orsen's own jawline and their grandmother's stubborn mouth, carrying a wrapped shard through snow, past the bodies of everyone who had helped her, saying to herself: *not power, not again, never again*—

Orsen screamed, or meant to. There was no mouth. There was only the flood, centuries of it, every hoarded memory pressing inward, and the Crown's voice rising beneath it all, triumphant and gentle:

*Now you understand. Now you can hold it properly. Give me your grief and I will make it useful—*

"No," Orsen said, inside, everywhere. "You don't get to make it useful. Grief isn't *for* anything. That's the whole point. That's what she knew."

And they did the thing no bearer had ever done, the thing the Crown had been engineered across a thousand years to prevent.

They let go.

Not of the Crown—of themselves. Of the grip. Of the idea that they were the one who should hold it. They opened every door in their own mind at once—the extracted memories, the bought-back childhood, the widow's grief they'd carried out of Hollowmere in a glass vial and never returned—and they *poured*, offering the Crown not a bearer but a breach, a thief's trick, the oldest one they knew: you don't break a vault, you find where it leaks.

The Crown had one leak. It had always had one leak. It was built to hold the memories of rulers, and rulers were only ever one person at a time, and so it had never once had to withstand being *everywhere*.

Orsen shattered it into thirteen pieces—not with force, but with direction—and threw each piece outward, screaming through the flooding dark, toward the points their grandmother had taught them to count. *One. Two. Three.* The stones of Hollowmere, far away under hills and rivers, and the Crown's fragments answered them across the distance like iron filings snapping to a magnet, tearing free of Orsen's hands, of the air, of the assembling whole, streaking away through stone and water and miles of earth.

*No—* the Crown said, and for the first time in a thousand years, it sounded afraid. *We were meant to be *whole*—*

"We were never meant to be *one*," Orsen said. "*Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.*"

"*Thirteen.*"

The last fragment tore loose, and the Crown came apart in Orsen's hands like a star coming apart—silently, totally, with more light than light—and the release hit the world.

* * *

It went out in every direction at once.

Every memory the Crown had hoarded for a millennium poured into the minds of every living thing within reach of it—which was, now, everything. In the flooded hall of Vael, the militia dropped their weapons and clutched their heads; Vess went down onto his knees in the rising water with a sound like a man being unmade, because the first thing the returning tide brought him was his own ledger, his own years of sold names, delivered back to him with interest. Reye stood motionless while her enforcers staggered, and Orsen saw her lips moving—counting, maybe, or apologizing, or both.

Tomas caught Sefa. Bren caught Tomas. Ilse caught Orsen, who was falling, who had been hollowed out and refilled in the same instant, who could feel the Crown's fragments racing away beneath the earth toward thirteen stones and could feel, faintly, wonderfully, the stones beginning to sing back.

The water was at their chests now. The citadel was dying around them, its ancient halls giving way, and the drowned road was gone, and none of that mattered, because the light was still pouring out of everything—out of the water, out of the walls, out of Orsen's own skin—as the hoard became the world's again.

Ilse dragged them toward the colonnade, where Bren had found the old soldier's route up—a stair behind the fallen masonry, half-submerged, climbing toward air. "Move," she gasped. "You can grieve on higher ground."

"I'm not grieving," Orsen managed. And then, because it was true and they wanted one true thing said aloud before the water took the words: "I'm *empty*. Ilse—I'm empty and it's the best I've ever felt."

"That," Ilse said, hauling them up the stair, "is the single most mage thing you've ever said."

Behind them, Vael folded into the sea with a long, groaning sigh, like something finally allowed to sleep. The light rose off the drowning ruin in a column visible, Orsen would later learn, from the borders of every nation that had ever fought over the Crown—from Hollowmere's hills to Cindral's markets to the far capitals where kings and councils woke in the night remembering everything their thrones had paid to bury.

The stars were still gone. But under the black sky, as the six of them crawled out onto the rocks above the drowned causeway—soaked, bleeding, alive—Orsen felt the fragments settle into the ward-stones, all thirteen, felt the old lattice wake and spread, not hiding anything this time but *holding*, carrying the Crown's hoard out along lines their grandmother had laid down decades ago, waiting all these years for someone to finish the circuit.

The valley's invisibility was already failing. Orsen could feel it go—could feel Hollowmere stepping blinking into the world's memory, its name returning to maps its people had never seen.

Tomas sat down heavily on the rock beside them, water streaming off him, and started laughing—the shaky, disbelieving laugh of a man who has watched his town's protection dissolve and cannot summon a single regret. "Your grandmother is going to haunt us forever."

"She'd have wanted it done right," Orsen said.

Above them, in the starless dark, the first thin light of dawn was gathering in the east—and with it, far off across the water, Orsen heard something that made them go still.

Bells. Hundreds of bells, in towns that had no reason to ring them, ringing anyway—because somewhere in the flood of returned memory, someone had just remembered why they used to.

Ilse touched Orsen's shoulder. "The world's waking up," she said. "All of it. At once."

Orsen looked east, at the light coming, and understood that nothing—not the valley, not the trade, not any of them—was ever going to be invisible again.

"We should go home," they said. "Before it gets there without us."
