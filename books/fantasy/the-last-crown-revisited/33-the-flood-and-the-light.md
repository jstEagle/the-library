## 33. The Flood and the Light

The water came for them like a debt collector.

Tomas heard it before he saw it—a groan deep in the bones of Vael, older than any language he knew, as if the drowned citadel had finally decided to stop pretending it was a building and admit what it had always been: a shipwreck waiting to finish sinking. Then the sound of the sea arrived through every corridor at once, a white roar, and Orsen's voice cut through it from somewhere ahead.

"RUN!"

Tomas ran. He was not brave; he had never been brave. He was the one who had written to Hollowmere, who had handed their route to Alderman Vess with shaking hands and told himself it was protection, protection, *protection*, right up until Vess marched the militia through the gates behind him. So when the flood came, Tomas ran the way guilty men run—faster than everyone, ashamed of it, unable to stop.

The sunken hall was behind them now, and behind them too was the sight he would carry until he died: Orsen standing on the dais with the assembled Crown in both hands, light pouring out of its fractures like heat out of a cracked stove, while the sea poured in through the great doors. Ilse screaming something that might have been *don't* or might have been *do*. Reye holding the militia's line with nothing but her body and her voice. And Sefa—the mute mapmaker who had drawn them through Ashfield Crossing with ink and stubbornness—dragging Bren toward the stairs, looking back once, only once.

Tomas did not look back. He could not afford to. The causeway stairs were narrow, slick with centuries of brine, and lit now by a glow that came not from torches but from the air itself—from the water, from the walls, from everywhere. The Crown's memories were already loose. He could feel them pressing against his skull like weather.

*Keep moving,* he told himself. *Grieve later.*

* * *

They burst out onto the seaward terrace as the first wave broke over the lower galleries below them. Ilse was there, soaked to the ribs, coughing seawater, alive. Bren was there, half-carrying Sefa, whose hands were already moving—drawing shapes in the wet air, urgent, wordless. Tomas counted them twice, three times, his heart hammering out the arithmetic: himself, Ilse, Bren, Sefa. Four.

No Orsen.

No Reye.

"The stairs," Ilse gasped, pointing back into the dark mouth of the corridor. "There's a second stair—it goes up through the tower—if they took the main gallery—" She didn't finish. She turned and plunged back inside, and Tomas, cursing every god he'd stopped believing in, went after her.

The corridor was filling. Water to his knees, then his thighs, cold enough to make his legs stupid. The light was stronger here, gold-white, pulsing in time with something like a heartbeat, and where it touched the flooded stone, images bloomed—faces in the water, faces in the walls. A woman in a crown of river-pearls, laughing. A king burning a city, weeping while he did it. A child being taught to sign her name. Thousands of years of memory, freed all at once, drowning the citadel a second time—not with water but with everything the Crown had ever swallowed.

Tomas felt them start to seep into him.

It began gently, the way rain begins. A memory that wasn't his: standing in a wheat field at dusk, watching soldiers come over the hill, knowing—knowing with total certainty—that he would die there, and feeling no fear, only a terrible tenderness for the wheat. Then another: signing a treaty with a hand that shook. Another: burying a daughter and choosing, deliberately, to forget her face so he could keep ruling, and hating himself for the choice every day after, forever, because forgetting hadn't worked.

Tomas stopped in the flooding corridor and sobbed once, hard, for a dead girl whose name he would never know.

"Tomas!" Ilse grabbed his arm. Her eyes were wide, streaming—not just from the spray. "It's in the water. It's in everything. Don't fight it, don't drown in it—just *carry* it. Like a pack. One strap at a time."

"One strap at a time," he repeated, idiotic, and found that it helped. You couldn't hold ten thousand lifetimes. But you could hold this step, and then the next one.

They climbed.

* * *

The tower stair let them out onto the highest terrace of Vael, a broken crown of stone above the drowning halls, and from there Tomas saw the whole impossible thing.

The sea had taken the lower city. Black water churned through streets that had been dry for three hundred years, and above the water the light rose—threads of it, thousands upon thousands, lifting off the citadel like sparks off a bonfire, streaming outward across the night sky in every direction. Toward Hollowmere. Toward Cindral. Toward every town and valley and hidden place where the wards had kept memory out and the people in.

And on the central spire, small with distance but unmistakable, stood two figures. Orsen. And beside them, Captain Reye, her enforcer's coat plastered to her body, her sword gone, her hands raised—not in threat, Tomas realized, but bracing Orsen against the wind.

Then the Crown shattered.

Tomas had expected thunder. What he got was silence—a single vast moment of silence, as if the whole world had inhaled—and then the light *left*. All of it. It tore free of the spire in a ring that expanded faster than sight, and where it passed over the water the waves glowed, and where it passed over the sky the clouds glowed, and then it was gone, everywhere at once, delivered.

Tomas sat down hard on the wet stone.

He didn't decide to. His legs simply ended their employment. And in the sitting, the flood reached him—not the sea, which was still climbing the terraces below, but the other flood, the true one, the memories finding their addresses at last.

He saw Hollowmere. Not as it was—as it had been, forty years ago, sixty, a hundred. He saw the valley before the wards, open under an unhidden sky, and he saw the bargain his grandmother's generation had made: give us your painful years, your griefs, your guilts, and we will keep you safe and small and sleeping. He saw the ward-stones drinking it all in, generation after generation, and he understood suddenly why Hollowmere's people were so gentle and so hollow, why his mother could never tell him what she'd been like as a girl, why the town's history began conveniently at its own founding.

And then he saw Vess.

Alderman Vess, thirty years younger, kneeling in the ward-house with a crown officer's ledger open in front of him, writing names. Not names of enemies. Names of neighbors. The cooper's son who'd asked too many questions about the border. Widow Maren's husband, who remembered too much of the old roads. Writing them down in exchange for the officer's quiet promise: *your family will be protected when the protections fail.* And beneath the fear, underneath it like a stone under moss, the thing Vess had paid the crown to take out of him: the memory of the winter he'd voted, as a young councilman, to turn away refugees at the pass rather than risk the wards. Forty-one people. Forty-one, in the snow. He had sold their memory of it to the stones themselves, and the stones had kept it safe for him, all these years, the way a bank keeps the worst thing you own.

"Oh," Tomas said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, you coward. You miserable, ordinary coward."

He recognized the shape of it because it was the shape of his own letter to Hollowmere. Fear wearing the costume of care. Protection as a knife you held pointed at yourself and called a shield.

The wave hit the terrace then—real water this time, waist-high, tearing him from his thoughts—and Ilse hauled him up by the collar, and they ran again, up the outer rampart steps, Sefa ahead of them signing *this way, this way* with hands that shook and did not stop, Bren carrying the old soldier's weight of himself without complaint, all of them glowing faintly now, all of them full of strangers' lives, running toward the high ground where the boats waited.

Behind them, Vael settled into the sea with a sound like a door closing.

* * *

They reached the cliffs above the drowned road an hour past dawn, if dawn could be said to exist in a sky still raining light. The fishing boats Sefa had marked on her map—three of them, beached above the tideline—were exactly where she'd drawn them, and Tomas almost laughed at that, at the plain miracle of a map telling the truth.

That was when he saw the militia.

They straggled along the cliff path from the landward side, maybe two hundred of them, Hollowmere men and women in mismatched armor, mud to their thighs, led by Alderman Vess on a grey horse that wanted very badly to be anywhere else. They had run all night from the ambush at the ford, fleeing the same flood the rest of them had, and now they stood at the top of the beach with nowhere left to retreat and the light still falling on them like slow snow.

Vess saw Tomas and his face did something complicated—relief and hatred and terror fighting for the same ground.

"You," Vess said. His voice cracked in the middle. "You wrote to me. You told me where they'd be. And now look—" he gestured wildly at the sky, at the sea, at his own trembling militiamen, several of whom had sat down where they stood, weeping or laughing or both. "Look what they've done. Look what your friends have done to us."

"They didn't do anything to us," Tomas said. He was surprised how steady his voice came out. "We did it to ourselves, Alderman. For a hundred years. The Crown just kept the receipts."

Something moved through the militia then, a ripple, like wind through wheat. Tomas watched it travel—men and women stopping mid-step, staring at nothing, mouths opening. The memories were reaching them fully now, the ones the valley's stones had held, pouring home after decades in exile. He watched a farmwife clutch her sister and say a name Tomas didn't know, over and over. He watched a young soldier drop his spear and cover his face.

And he watched Vess.

The alderman swayed in the saddle. His hand went to his chest, then to his temple, then simply hung. His lips moved—*no, no, no*—and then, horribly, *forty-one*, and then nothing, because his body had begun to tip.

Tomas ran. He was not brave, but he was fast, and guilt had trained him well. He reached the horse as Vess slid from it, and caught him—caught all of him, the bony weight of the man, the smell of fear-sweat and wool—and lowered him onto the shingle as the alderman's eyes rolled back and the tears came, sudden and enormous, the tears of a man remembering forty-one people freezing in a pass he had closed.

"I forgot," Vess wept. His fists knotted in Tomas's shirt with shocking strength. "I *paid* to forget. I didn't know—I swear I didn't know what I'd done until just now—" He retched, dry-heaved, clutched harder. "Forty-one. I can see their faces. I can see all of them."

"Yes," Tomas said quietly. Around them the militia had gone silent, watching, and he understood that every one of them was seeing their own version of this—their own buried winters, their own sold sorrows—and that Vess was only the first to fall. "Now you remember. That's the price, Alderman. That's what it costs. We all pay it now."

"Take me to the stones," Vess whispered. "The ward-stones. Can I—can I give it back? Give it to them? Their families?"

Tomas looked up. Ilse had come down the beach and stood nearby, arms crossed, watching the alderman with an expression Tomas couldn't read—pity and judgment braided together. Beyond her, at the tideline, the sea was calm now, and far out, where Vael had gone under, the water glowed softly, like a lamp under a blanket.

"I don't think it works that way anymore," Ilse said. "I think the giving-back is the only way through. You carry it, or it carries you." She crouched next to Vess, and her voice gentled. "But you don't carry it alone. That was the whole point. That was always the whole point."

Vess looked at her, and then at Tomas, and whatever he saw in Tomas's face made him close his eyes.

"You should hate me," the alderman whispered. "Both of you. I sold your friend's route to the crown. I led armed men after children."

"You did," Tomas agreed. "And I helped you. We're going to have to live in the same town, you and I, knowing both those things. I haven't figured out how yet." He looked out at the glowing sea, and the tightness in his chest loosened by a single degree—not forgiveness, not yet, but the space where forgiveness might eventually be built. "Ask me again in a year."

* * *

Orsen came ashore near noon.

Tomas saw the boat first—a battered skiff, riding low—and then the figure in it, small against the bright water, and he was running down the beach before his mind caught up with his feet. Reye rowed. Of course Reye rowed; even defected, even soaked, she rowed like a machine. And in the bow sat Orsen, gray-faced, salt-crusted, empty-handed, and *alive*.

Tomas waded out to meet them and dragged the bow up the sand, and for a moment none of them spoke. The whole beach had gone quiet. Two hundred militiamen and four fugitives and one collapsed alderman, all of them watching Orsen Zhou climb out of the sea with no crown and no shard and no magic left in their hands.

"You did it," Tomas said. His voice failed on the second word and he tried again. "You actually—you're alive. How are you alive?"

"Reye knows the water," Orsen said hoarsely. There was something strange in their face—lightness and grief occupying the same features, the way dawn occupies a window. "And the Crown... it didn't want to hurt anyone at the end. It wanted to be *put down*. Three hundred years of being held, Tomas. Nobody ever asked it what it wanted." They looked past him, at the militia, at Vess crumpled on the shingle, at the glowing horizon. "Is he—"

"Remembering," Tomas said. "All of it. He asked if he could give it back to the families."

Orsen closed their eyes. When they opened them, they were wet. "Then it worked. Then it's working." They put a hand on Tomas's shoulder, and their grip was thin and strong and human. "I saw your letter. In the flood. I saw what you sent, and why." Tomas flinched, but the hand didn't move. "You were trying to build a wall around the people you loved. It's what the valley did. It's what the Crown did. It's what everyone does, until they learn better." A ghost of their old dry smile. "Welcome to learning better. It's awful. There's no one I'd rather be awful with."

Tomas laughed, and it came out as a sob, and he decided he didn't care who saw.

Ilse came down the beach then, and Bren, and Sefa, and the five of them stood in the shallows holding each other up like drunks, while behind them the light finished its long delivery across the world. Far inland, Tomas imagined it arriving in Hollowmere—sliding under doors, settling into teacups, waking grandmothers mid-dream. Somewhere in the town square, the thirteen ward-stones would be glowing like lanterns, giving back everything they'd been paid to keep.

Sefa tugged his sleeve. She held up her slate, wiped it clean, and drew with quick sure strokes: a road, winding east out of the drowned coast, toward the mountains, toward home. Under it she wrote—for the first time since Tomas had known her, she *wrote*—in a careful, unpracticed hand:

*I remember my name. It's Sefa. It was always Sefa.*

She showed it to all of them, one by one, like a gift.

And that was when Bren, squinting north along the cliff path, said quietly: "Riders. A lot of riders. Coming fast."

Tomas turned. Against the bright sky, dust was rising from the landward road—too much dust for merchants, too disciplined for refugees—and at its head, a banner he didn't recognize, flying a sigil he did: an open eye above a broken crown. Word traveled fast when the whole world woke up at once. Whatever was coming down that road, it had heard what happened in Vael.

Ilse's hand found Orsen's. "Friends?" she asked.

Orsen watched the dust a long moment, and Tomas saw them weigh it—truth against safety, one last time, the way they always would, the way everyone would now.

"No," Orsen said at last. "But I don't think they're enemies either. I think..." They exhaled. "I think the world wants to talk to us. All of us. Come on—we'd better decide what we're going to say back."
