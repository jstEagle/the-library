## 35. Reye's Testimony

The hall had been a granary once. Reye could tell by the iron hooks in the ceiling beams where sacks of grain had hung through the long winters, by the wide loading doors at either end that let the wind move through in cold, honest drafts. Now the hooks held lamps, and the loading doors framed crowds: farmers and ferry-keepers, weavers and ward-tenders, people who three months ago had not known their own names for longer than a season.

They had come to hear her confess.

She stood alone on the granary floor, her ledger bound shut with cord at her hip—the same ledger she had carried across half a continent, its pages soft as cloth from handling, its ink faded to the brown of dried blood. She had offered to burn it. The council had said no. *We need what's written,* they'd said, in the voice of people learning a hard new truth: that memory, freely given, was the only currency worth anything now.

*Freely given.* Reye almost smiled. She'd spent twenty years taking things that were never offered.

The presiding elder of the council—a broad woman named Hesta Oduya with grey threading her braids and the patient eyes of someone who had buried two husbands and one war—rose from her seat among the twelve. "Captain Sera Reye. You stand before the Free Council of the Riverlands. Before this body you have waived counsel, waived amnesty, and waived the protections of rank." Her voice carried without effort; the hall had been built for listening. "Do you understand what you waive?"

"I do."

"Do you come of your own will?"

Reye thought about the question honestly, because these people would smell a lie the way dogs smelled rain. Had she come of her own will? Or had she been driven—by the ledger's weight, by Bren's face when he'd recognized her name, by the look in Orsen Zhou's eyes the night she'd brought them the first shard and said, *this isn't surrender, it's a wager*?

"Yes," she said. And found it true.

"Then speak."

* * *

She began where all confessions should begin: with a name that wasn't hers.

"The crown I served was called the Remnant, but it had another name before the war ended. It was called the Office of Continuity. Its purpose was simple and it was stated plainly in its founding charter, which I have read, because I was made to read it on my first day of service. *No ruler shall be ruled by ignorance.* That was the ideal. The Crown itself—the relic—was built to serve that ideal. It would hold every ruler's memories so that no king or queen could ever be lied into ruin, could ever forget the cost of the last war while starting the next."

She paused. In the third row, an old man with burn-scarred hands watched her without blinking. She made herself keep going.

"What the charter did not say—and what I did not learn until years after I'd sworn my oaths—was that storing memory is not the same as keeping faith with it. The Crown did not merely remember. It *kept*. And what is kept can be withheld."

She unbound the cord from the ledger. Her fingers were steady; she had practiced this part in front of a river for three nights running, speaking the worst of it aloud to the water until the water stopped seeming like an audience and started seeming like nothing at all.

"This is the Ledger of Touched Names. Every person recorded who came into contact with Crown-magic in the last sixty years. It contains eleven thousand four hundred and six entries. I know the number because I counted them myself, twice, in a border fort, while deciding whether to burn it."

"Why didn't you?" Someone asked it from the crowd—not hostile, genuinely curious, the way children ask. A woman with a child asleep against her shoulder.

"Because burning it would have been mercy," Reye said, "and mercy was not mine to give. Eleven thousand names. Some were scholars. Some were thieves—Ilse Varn, for instance, whose entry I wrote myself the night she stole the shard from our vault at Cindral. But most—" and here her voice did something she hadn't planned, dropped half a tone, roughened, "—most were simply people who lived near the old vaults. Farmers whose wells ran over buried foundations. Children who played in ruins. We took them in the night. Not their lives—the Remnant had learned that murder leaves martyrs—but their *memories*. Whole years, sometimes. A wedding. A mother's face. The knowledge of a craft learned over decades. We took them and logged them and filed them under Continuity, and the people woke poorer than beggars and were told they'd always been poor."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the shoulders.

"And your role?" Elder Oduya asked gently. Too gently. Reye would have preferred cruelty.

"I was a taker," Reye said. "For nineteen years. I rose from field agent to Captain of the Third Circle. I designed the extraction protocols. I trained forty agents in their use. I personally supervised the erasure of—" she opened the ledger to the ribboned page she'd marked, because she refused to round the number down, refused even the small comfort of vagueness— "six hundred and twelve individuals."

She let the number sit in the air like smoke.

"One of them is sitting in this room."

* * *

Bren had told her he would be here. He'd sought her out the evening before, in the yard behind the granary where she'd been splitting wood for the kitchen fire—a penance the council hadn't assigned but that she'd taken up anyway, because her hands needed work her conscience couldn't finish.

"You'll say my name tomorrow," he'd said. Not a question.

"If the council permits."

"They'll permit." He'd leaned on the fence, this old soldier with his ruined knee and his careful way of standing, a man rebuilt around an absence the way a tree rebuilds around lightning. "You want to know the thing that eats me, Captain? It's not that you took it. War takes things. I've taken things." His jaw worked. "It's that you gave me back my life afterward and told me it was whole. You handed me a pension and a cottage and a letter of commendation, and I stood there holding them like a man holding his own ashes, thinking, *why does none of this feel like mine?* Twenty-two years, Reye. I had a wife in those years I can't remember marrying. There's a grave outside Harrowdale I visit every season because some part of me knows I loved whoever's in it. That's what you did. You didn't take a memory. You took the shape around which a whole life was built, and then you watched me try to live in the empty space."

He hadn't said it with hatred. That was the unbearable part. He'd said it the way a man reads a supply requisition—accurately, thoroughly, without rage, because accuracy was all he had left.

Tomorrow, in front of everyone, she would answer for it.

Now, on the granary floor, she turned and found him in the crowd. Third row, left side, exactly where he'd said he'd be. She nodded to him, once, the way soldiers acknowledge each other across impossible distances, and he inclined his head—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but *witness*. That was enough. That was more than she deserved.

"Bren of Harrowdale," she said, and her voice held. "Unit commander, Ninth Irregulars. On the fourth day of the Battle of Ashfield Crossing, his unit held a ford against three times their number for six hours. They saved a column of refugees. They also saw something the Remnant decided civilians should never see: enforcers extracting memories from prisoners on the field. Bren filed a complaint. The complaint reached the Office of Continuity within a day." She looked down at the page. "My order. My signature. I erased him and his entire surviving unit rather than risk the story spreading. Then I wrote his commendation myself, because I was good at my work, and being good at monstrous work is its own particular damnation."

* * *

The testimony went on through the morning and past midday. The council let the crowd come and go—there was work to do, children to feed, a world still reassembling itself out of the flood of returned memories—but a core of listeners stayed, shifting seats as others left, and by afternoon the hall held perhaps two hundred people who had chosen to stay and hear the whole of it.

She told them about Alderman Vess of Hollowmere, who had sold his neighbors' names to the crown for fifteen years in exchange for the valley's protection, and who now sat in a room in the same building where she slept, under the council's care, waking each morning into the full weight of everything the released memories had given back to him. She did not soften it. She did not exaggerate it either. She had learned, in the long walk from Vael, that precision was the only honesty available to people like her.

She told them about the interrogation rooms beneath Cindral's fort, and the mute mapmaker named Sefa who had answered every question with drawings—drawings that bought time, misled captors, and ultimately mapped the route that broke the Crown. She watched a young woman in the second row, charcoal-stained fingers folded in her lap, listen with her chin high, and understood that this too was testimony: the living proof that courage needs no voice.

She told them about Captain Reye's Offer—her own offer, made to Orsen Zhou on the road, amnesty for the valley in exchange for the shard. "I believed I was offering mercy," she said. "I was offering a bribe dressed in mercy's clothes. The difference matters. Mercy costs the giver something. A bribe only costs the mark."

And then she told them the part she had traveled furthest to say.

"On the night the Crown shattered, I was in the flooded causeway below Vael. When the memories released—when eleven thousand stolen lives poured back into the world like light through broken glass—I felt them pass through me. All of them. Every extraction I'd supervised, returning to its owner, and every owner's grief arriving in the same instant at the door of the person who caused it." Her hands, finally, were not quite steady. She let them be seen. "I have been asked, more than once since, whether that moment felt like punishment. It did not. It felt like arithmetic. Every debt arriving at once, correctly addressed. And I want this council to understand: I do not ask to be forgiven for what that moment settled. Being drowned in consequence is not the same as paying. Paying is slower. Paying is chosen. Paying is what I am asking permission to do."

* * *

Elder Oduya let the silence run long before she spoke again. Outside, the wind moved through the old loading doors and stirred the lamplight, and somewhere beyond the walls a child laughed, quick and bright, in a world where laughter was newly complicated by everything it now remembered.

"The council has heard your testimony," Oduya said. "Before we deliberate, there are questions from the floor. You will answer them all. No one will be turned away."

The questions came like weather.

A ferry-keeper wanted to know if other ledgers existed—regional copies, duplicates in forts she hadn't burned. *Three,* she said, and gave the locations, and watched scribes write them down. A young widow asked whether the agents she'd trained knew her face well enough to find her if they chose vengeance. *Yes,* Reye said. *I taught them to memorize their superiors' features as a security protocol.* An old ward-tender, one of the few who'd always known the valley's stones were hollow, asked why she hadn't defected sooner. Reye met her eyes and said, "Because I still believed the Remnant protected people. Belief is the longest siege there is. It fell, for me, the night Captain Reye looked in a mirror and couldn't remember why she'd joined." She did not explain the joke. Some of them laughed anyway, and the laugh hurt worse than anger would have.

Then a girl stood up near the back—fourteen, maybe fifteen, wearing a boy's coat two sizes too large, with the fierce stillness of someone who had already survived more than childhood should hold.

"You said six hundred and twelve," the girl said. "That you supervised. My da was taken when I was six. I don't know if he's one of your numbers. I don't know if he's alive. The memories came back to *us* three weeks ago—his, some of them, floating loose with nowhere to land—and I've been carrying them in a jar on my shelf because nobody knows how to put a person back together." Her voice cracked and steadied. "So my question is: what happens to the ones who aren't coming home?"

The hall went very quiet.

Reye walked toward her. She did it slowly, openly, hands visible, because trust between a crown captain and a crown orphan was not built in a single stride. She stopped an arm's length away.

"I don't know your father's fate," she said. "But I know the Remnant's files, and I know that extraction was ordered to preserve subjects alive—dead witnesses raised questions. If he was taken, there is a strong chance he survived it, and a chance—smaller, but real—that he is living somewhere with a hole in himself shaped exactly like the memories in your jar." She crouched, bringing her eyes level with the girl's. "When this session ends, I will help you search the ledger for his entry, and the entry will tell us where he was taken and when. After that, I give you my word: I will not stop looking until you know, one way or the other. Not because you demand it. Because it's mine to carry."

The girl studied her for a long moment with eyes too old for the face above them. "Everyone keeps saying that," she said. "'Mine to carry.' Like it's a pack you volunteered to haul." She lifted her chin. "Fine. Carry it. But don't you dare put it down."

"I won't," Reye said. And meant it the way she had rarely meant anything: absolutely, and forever.

* * *

Deliberation took until dusk. The council withdrew to the granary's loft; the crowd dispersed into the streets of the river-town, where lamps were being lit one by one along the water. Reye waited on the granary floor, alone except for the ghosts of the iron hooks overhead, and did not pray, because she had never learned how, but stood instead in the posture of attention she'd held through twenty years of inspections and judgments, spine straight, weight even, ready to receive whatever sentence arrived.

When the council returned, Oduya spoke for them.

"Sera Reye. This council finds that your crimes are beyond the reach of any punishment we could devise, and we decline to pretend otherwise. There is no cell deep enough, no term of labor long enough, to balance six hundred and twelve stolen lives. Any sentence we passed would be theater, and this world has had enough of performances." The old woman's voice softened by a degree. "Instead, we offer you work. The ledgers must be reconciled. The missing must be searched for. The returned memories—loose, ownerless, drifting like yours daughter-of-Harrowdale's—are multiplying faster than anyone can gather them, and someone must teach the freed communities how to hold what they've recovered without drowning. Someone who understands exactly how memory can be taken, stored, misused, and returned." Oduya's eyes held hers, steady as bedrock. "We do not offer you absolution. We offer you a desk, a road, and a lifetime. Will you take it?"

Reye had imagined many endings for herself. A cell. A rope. A blade in a dark corridor, delivered by one of her own trainees. She had imagined, in the worst nights, that she wanted those things—that punishment was the only clean garment left to her.

Standing on the granary floor, she understood at last that wanting punishment was just another form of wanting to be finished. And she was not finished. None of them were. The work was enormous, unglamorous, endless: a continent of people learning to live with everything they'd lost and regained, and every day of it would be chosen, and none of it would ever be done.

It was the only future she'd ever actually wanted.

"I'll take it," Reye said.

Oduya nodded once. "Then the council is adjourned, and the work begins at dawn—"

But the doors banged open before the words finished landing.

A rider stood silhouetted in the western doorway, lathered horse steaming behind her, a travel-worn figure with mud to the knees and urgency in every line of her. Reye knew the stride before she knew the face—had marched beside that stride through wards and battlefields and a drowning citadel.

Ilse Varn crossed the hall at a pace that made the crowd fall back, and thrust a folded paper into Reye's hands. Her breath came ragged.

"It's Orsen," Ilse said. "There's been word from Hollowmere. The thirteenth stone—" She swallowed. "The thirteenth stone is singing. And Orsen says it's singing *names*—every name the Crown ever kept—and one of them is a name nobody in that valley has spoken aloud in sixty years." Her eyes, dark and certain, fixed on Reye's. "Your grandmother's name, Captain. It's on the stone. And it's asking for you."
