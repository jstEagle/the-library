## 36. Ilse's Quiet Power

The letter arrived on the third morning of the thaw, carried by a rider whose horse had thrown a shoe somewhere on the Cindral road, so that he came into Hollowmere limping and apologetic and smelling faintly of livery stables. He wore no colors. That was the first sign of how seriously they were taking her. Anyone who wanted to flatter her with the trappings of office would have sent gold braid and a herald. Instead they had sent a tired man with a sealed packet and the look of someone who would very much like to sit down.

"Ilse Varn," he said, reading from the packet itself rather than from memory, which meant the words were not his own. "By request of the Council of Freed Communities, convened at Cindral under the terms of the Reckoning—"

"You can skip to the part where you tell me what they want," Ilse said.

She was standing in Orsen's doorway with a mug of barley tea going cold in her hand. Behind her she could hear Tomas arguing cheerfully with Sefa about whether the new maps should put rivers before roads or roads before rivers, and Bren's low laugh at something neither of them had said aloud. The house smelled of woodsmoke and bread. It was, Ilse thought, the most dangerous place she had ever been asked to leave.

The rider cleared his throat. "The Council requests your presence. As—" he turned the page, frowned, "—as *the Crownbreaker*. There is some discussion about the title. The alternative formulation is 'She Who Ended the Age of Singular Power,' but I'm told that one doesn't fit on the seal."

"They want to give me a chair," Ilse said.

"They want to give you *the* chair," the rider corrected gently. "First seat. They're calling it the Seat of Memory. And there's a sash."

"A sash."

"Cerulean. With silver thread." He said it the way a man describes a fish he has been asked to admire.

Ilse looked down into her tea. In its surface she caught the warped reflection of her own face—the scar through her left eyebrow where an enforcer's baton had split the skin at Ashfield Crossing, the grey coming in early at her temples, the eyes that had once made a magistrate in her homeland lean across a table and say, *you have the look of a woman who reads verdicts before they're written.* She had hated him for it. She had spent six years running from exactly that look, from the certainty it described, from the terrible ease with which certainty could be worn like armor.

"No," she said.

The rider blinked. "No?"

"No thank you," she amended, because her mother had raised her with manners even in the years when manners were all they had. "Tell them I'm honored. Tell them I'm flattered by the sash. Tell them—" She stopped, and thought about it, really thought, while the rider waited with the patience of a man paid by the mile. "Tell them I'll come to Cindral. But not for the chair."

* * *

The trouble, as Ilse explained it later that evening over the ruins of dinner, was that everyone kept mistaking refusal for humility, and humility for a kind of currency.

"They don't want me," she said, turning her cup in slow circles on the table. "They want what happened in Vael. They want a story with a shape. A woman steals a shard, a woman breaks a crown, a woman gets a throne. Beginning, middle, coronation. It's tidy. Tidy stories are worth more than true ones, and I would know—I've sold enough of them."

"You never sold stories," Tomas said around a mouthful of bread.

"I sold myself as a story. For six years I let people believe I was just a fugitive, just a thief who got lucky, because 'just' is the best disguise there is." She set the cup down. "And now the whole world wants to hand me the biggest *just* of all. Just accept the chair. Just wear the sash. Just be the symbol, Ilse, it's only a symbol, symbols don't bite."

"And do they?" Bren asked quietly. "Bite?"

"Symbols? Symbols are mostly teeth." Ilse looked at each of them in turn—Tomas with his flour-dusted forearms, Sefa with her stylus already moving across paper, capturing the conversation in quick lines because she trusted drawings more than minutes, Bren with his hands folded like a man at a funeral he approved of. "You all saw what the Crown did. It didn't start hungry. It started as a tool. Somebody made it to keep rulers honest—to make sure no king could burn his own past and rule from a clean slate. And then somebody wore it, and found it convenient, and handed it to their heir, and within three generations it wasn't keeping anyone honest. It was keeping everyone *full*. One person holding every memory of every ruler who ever lived, and every one of those memories whispering: *this is how it's done. This is how it's always done.*"

Sefa's stylus paused. Then, deliberately, she drew a single line: a crown, and beneath it, a mouth.

"Yes," Ilse said. "That's it exactly. Power talks. It talks constantly. And the first thing it says is *I'm different*, and the second thing it says is *just this once*, and if you listen long enough—and I listened to that shard for months, I know its voice—you start believing that taking the chair is a duty. That refusing it is vanity. That the world will fall apart without you specifically sitting in it." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it. "The Crown nearly had me at the end. You know that. Every ruler's certainty in my head, telling me I was the obvious choice, the natural heir, that my theft had marked me for it. I stood in that sunken hall with the thing singing to me and I wanted it. Not power. *Certainty.* The end of doubt. Do you understand what a temptation that is, for someone who has doubted everything since the day she ran?"

Tomas reached across and refilled her cup without asking. It was such a small domestic gesture, so ordinary, that it undid her more than any argument could have.

"So what do we tell them?" he asked.

"Not we," Ilse said. "Me. This is mine to answer." She looked at the window, where the first stars were coming out again—real stars now, returned since the shattering, though the old-timers swore they sat differently in the sky than before. "I'll go to Cindral. I'll stand in front of their council. And I won't refuse the work. Refusing the work is how cowards dress up cowardice as principle. I'll refuse the *chair*. And then I'll offer them something better, and they'll hate it, and half of them will take it anyway."

"What's the something better?"

Ilse grinned—a real grin this time, sharp at the edges, the one Orsen had once said made her look like a fox considering a henhouse.

"School."

* * *

Cindral in spring was a city trying on new clothes and not yet sure they fit.

The memory market still stood—it would always stand, Ilse suspected; the trade in forgetting was older than any crown—but its stalls had changed hands and its wares had changed character. Where vendors had once sold sweetened oblivion, they now offered *context*: a grandmother's recipe alongside the story of the famine that made it precious, a soldier's letter home beside the battle it was written before. The Returned Memories had done that. When the Crown broke and every hoarded recollection flooded back into the world, people woke to find griefs in their heads that weren't theirs and joys they'd forgotten owning, and the first mad weeks had nearly burned Cindral to the ground twice. What saved it—not law, not soldiers, but sheer human stubbornness—was neighbors sitting with neighbors, saying *tell me what you're seeing, and I'll tell you what I'm seeing, and maybe between us we'll know what's true.*

That, more than anything, was why the Council wanted a queen. Someone to hold the lid on the pot.

The council chamber had been the memory market's auction hall, and it still smelled faintly of tallow and old money. Forty seats in a crescent, and above them, newly installed, a forty-first: raised, canopied, draped in cerulean. Empty. Waiting.

Ilse stood before the crescent in her travel-stained coat, having declined, with great politeness, the ceremonial robe they'd offered at the door.

"Lady Varn," began the moderator, a broad woman named Chancellor Odele who had the air of someone who had run a granary through two famines and did not frighten easily, "the communities represented here have deliberated. The age of singular power ended at Vael. It must not begin again. To ensure that, the people ask for a guardian of memory—one voice empowered to weigh every claim, license every archive, judge every dispute over the Returned. We ask you to take the Seat of Memory."

"The people ask," Ilse repeated. "Or the forty of you ask?"

A ripple. Somewhere in the crescent, someone muttered *insolence*, and someone else shushed them.

"Because here's my problem with chairs," Ilse went on, before the muttering could gather force. "I've sat in one. Metaphorically. For about four hours in a drowned citadel, the Crown sat me in the chair of every ruler who ever lived, and I learned the lesson they all learned: from up there, everyone below looks small. It isn't corruption. It's geometry. Raise a person high enough and the faces blur. You want a guardian who will weigh every claim fairly? Don't raise anyone. Lower the work until everyone can reach it."

Odele folded her arms. "Poetry. The disputes are real, Lady Varn. Towns are fighting over whose memories of a border are correct. Merchants are selling false pasts to grieving families. Last month a man in Vess's old district—we don't call it that anymore—tried to sell his neighbor's childhood back to him at triple price. Who arbitrates?"

"*Everyone*," Ilse said, "badly, at first, and then better. That's what I've come to offer. Not a judge. A school."

Silence. Even the man with the sash, waiting hopefully by the wall, went still.

"Every community that sends a delegate to this council sends also a student. Two years. They learn what memory is—how it degrades, how it heals, how it lies. They learn the ethics of extraction: when taking a memory is mercy and when it's theft, and how to tell the difference, which is harder than anyone wants it to be. They learn the history the Crown hoarded—all of it, the burning cities and the broken treaties, nothing polished, nothing tidied. And then they go home, and they teach, and their students teach, and in twenty years there is no Seat of Memory because the memory sits everywhere. In kitchens. In market squares. In schools smaller than this room." She spread her hands. "You wanted a crown that couldn't be worn. Here it is: a crown made of ten thousand teachers. Try putting *that* on one head."

"It's slow," Odele said. It wasn't quite an objection. It was the tone of a granary-keeper testing grain between her teeth.

"Famine relief is slow. So is justice. So was breaking the Crown—two years of running, losing, burying friends' trust and digging it back up." Ilse's voice dropped, and the chamber leaned in despite itself. "Chancellor, I held the Crown's hunger in my own skull. I know exactly what it costs to have one person carry what many should share. I am not going to build a smaller version of the thing I broke and call it guardianship. Offer me the chair again in ten years, if the school fails. If it works, you won't need to."

The crescent argued for six hours. Ilse stayed for all of it, standing, answering every question—including the ugly ones, including *who died so you could be humble?*—and when the vote finally came, it was not unanimous. Thirty-one to nine. She counted the nine, and memorized their names, and resolved to send each of them a personal letter within the month, because the school would need its critics more than its cheering section.

The sash, she gave to Odele, suggesting it be cut into strips and used as bookmarks for the first library.

* * *

The school opened in autumn, in a decommissioned ward-house on Cindral's east side—a squat stone building that had once housed the enforcers who wrote the Ledger, which Ilse considered either irony or penance, depending on the day. Its first class numbered eleven students: a fisherman's daughter from the coast who could recite every tide-legend her village had lost and wanted to learn why legends degrade; a former enforcer, young, who had come with a sealed letter of recommendation from Captain Reye and a face like someone expecting to be spat on; twin archivists from Vael itself, who had grown up in the drowned citadel's shadow and spoke of the Crown the way farmers speak of weather.

Ilse taught the opening session herself, standing on a plain table because the podium hadn't arrived.

"Rule one," she said. "Nobody in this room has authority over anyone else's memories. Not me. Not the council. Not love, not guilt, not the common good. The moment you believe you do—no matter how merciful the reason—you are building the Crown again, shard by shard, and you won't notice, because you'll call it care. Rule two: consent is not a form. It's a conversation that can be revoked at any point, including afterward. Rule three—" she looked around the room, at their uncertain faces, at the enormous weight of what they were choosing to carry, "—rule three is the hard one. When you cannot tell whether you're helping or hoarding, ask the person whose memory it is. And then believe them. Even when it costs you. *Especially* when it costs you."

The former enforcer raised her hand. Her voice shook. "What if they're wrong? What if remembering destroys them?"

"Then you sit with them while they remember anyway," Ilse said, "because a person's truth belongs to them even when it burns. We learned that in the valley. Didn't we, Orsen?"

And there in the back row, having traveled three days without announcement, sat Orsen Zhou—arms crossed, expression carefully neutral, eyes bright with something they would deny under torture was pride.

* * *

Winter came early to Cindral, and Ilse spent the solstice writing letters by lamplight: to the nine dissenters, to the coastal towns disputing a harbor boundary, to a widow in Hollowmere who had written asking whether the school taught forgiveness, and if not, why not. Ilse answered honestly—that it didn't, that forgiveness couldn't be curriculum—and received, weeks later, a parcel containing the widow's returned memory wrapped in linen, along with a note: *Then teach them what it feels like to give something back.*

She pinned the note above her desk.

On the last night of the year, a knock came at the ward-house door long after classes ended. Ilse opened it to find a courier, snow in his hair, carrying a single envelope sealed not with any council's wax but with a pressed sprig of valley sage. Inside, in handwriting she knew almost as well as her own, was a single line:

*Hollowmere remembers you. Come home before the thirteenth moon turns. There's something we need to decide together—before the stars finish returning.*

Ilse stood a long moment in the doorway, snow gathering on her shoulders, the letter warm in her hand. Behind her, the school slept. Ahead, the road north lay white and waiting.

She reached for her coat.
