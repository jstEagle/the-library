## 37. Hollowmere, Unhidden

The valley announced itself long before Orsen could see it.

For thirty years, Hollowmere had hidden behind the thirteenth stone's veil—travelers forgot the road as they walked it, maps blurred at its borders, even the crows circled wide. Now, from the ridge above the pass, Orsen looked down and saw the valley plainly: the silver thread of the Mere, the slate roofs of the town stacked along its banks like spilled letters, smoke rising honest and unhidden from a hundred chimneys.

And on the road below, a merchant cart rolled toward Hollowmere without hesitation, its driver consulting a map that showed every house.

Orsen stood there a long time, letting the wind off the high country pull at their coat. Ilse waited beside them, arms crossed against the autumn cold, her shard-scarred hand tucked into her sleeve. Behind them on the road, Bren leaned on his spear like an old man leaning on a fence, though he was watching the treeline out of habit that would never leave him.

"You don't have to go down today," Ilse said. "There's no one waiting for you. That's rather the point."

"That's exactly why I have to." Orsen adjusted the pack on their shoulder—the same one they'd left with three months ago, lighter now by one stolen Crown and heavier by everything else. "I spent twenty years taking things from these people. If I come back quietly, sneaking in like always, then nothing's changed except the wards."

Sefa touched Orsen's elbow—a light tap, twice—and pointed down at the town, then drew a quick shape in the air with her finger. A door. Then another. Doors opening.

"She says half of them will open," Tomas translated from further up the path. He'd been quiet since Vael. Quieter than his betrayal deserved, some said; Orsen didn't think so. "She also says the other half will close."

"Sefa has always been an optimist," Orsen said, and started down.

* * *

Hollowmere looked smaller from inside it than it had in memory. That was the strange arithmetic of return: the streets Orsen had walked daily for decades had somehow shrunk, or Orsen had grown around them. The well at the crossroads still leaned east. Old Marta's shutters were still painted the blue she swore was fashionable forty years ago. The bakery smelled the same—rye and woodsmoke—but now the baker's boy stood outside handing loaves to travelers who would have walked past Hollowmere a season ago without ever knowing it existed.

People stopped when they saw Orsen.

It wasn't the hiding that was gone. It wasn't only that. The released memories had reached everyone in the valley weeks before Orsen returned, flooding through the shattered ward-stones like spring meltwater through a cracked dam. Every person in Hollowmere now remembered what the Crown had hoarded for centuries—and, more uncomfortably, they remembered each other. They remembered the widow's grief that Orsen had lifted for coin. They remembered their own sold sorrows, and who had bought them, and who had sold them.

They remembered Orsen.

A woman dropped her basket of apples at the sight of them. She didn't bend to pick them up. She just stood there with her hands open at her sides, staring, and Orsen recognized her—Talia, the cooper's daughter, whose fear of drowning Orsen had extracted when she was nineteen because she couldn't afford to keep it anymore. Talia had wept with gratitude afterward. Orsen remembered being paid six coppers and feeling generous about the price.

"Talia," Orsen began.

"Don't." Her voice cracked. Not with anger—with something worse, something Orsen couldn't name yet. "Don't say my name like you know me. You *did* know me. Better than anyone. And you charged me for it."

She left the apples where they fell and walked away, and the street closed behind her like water.

Ilse's hand found Orsen's shoulder briefly. "First door," she murmured. "It opened."

"It slammed."

"Same hinge."

* * *

Orsen's house sat where it had always sat, at the end of Lark Lane, with the crooked chimney and the garden gone wild. But someone had been inside. The door stood slightly ajar, and through the gap came the smell of someone else's life—bread, again, and lamp oil.

Inside, the widow Maren sat in Orsen's own chair.

She was older than Orsen remembered, which was unfair, since Orsen had seen her last only four months ago. Grief did that. It aged you in seasons, not years. Her husband's memory—that first extraction, the one that had started everything, the one Orsen still counted as their finest work and foulest sin—sat somewhere in the pack on Orsen's shoulder, wrapped in oilcloth like the relic it had become.

Maren looked up. Her eyes were dry. Whatever weeping she'd done, she'd done it already, and thoroughly.

"The door was unlocked," she said. "In thirty years, you never once left this door unlocked. I used to think you were afraid of thieves." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "Imagine my surprise."

"I've been told I'm a hypocrite of the highest order."

"I've been telling people that for weeks." She rose from the chair slowly, her joints announcing themselves. "Sit down, Orsen. This is your house. I'm just the trespasser."

"Maren—"

"*Sit.*"

Orsen sat. Maren lowered herself back into the chair opposite, and for a moment neither spoke. Outside, a cart passed on Lark Lane, and Orsen heard voices—strangers' voices, asking directions to the inn, marveling at the valley. Hollowmere, unhidden. The whole world could find them now.

"Do you remember what you took?" Maren asked.

"Every detail. His voice. The way he hummed when he shaved. The morning he died." Orsen's throat tightened. "I kept it separate from the others. I told myself it was because it was valuable. It was because I couldn't bear to sell it twice."

"He asked me, near the end, whether I'd ever forget him." Maren's hands folded in her lap, knuckles like river stones. "I said never. Then I woke up one morning and the bed beside me was just a bed. I knew I'd been married the way you know facts from books. And I felt *relieved*, Orsen. That was the worst part. For three years I lived relieved, and I hated myself for it, and I couldn't remember why."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not. Not yet." She said it without cruelty, which made it land harder. "Sorry is what you'll be after you've carried it yourself. That's the trade you never understood—you thought memory was weight, and forgetting was mercy. But the mercy was mine to give or refuse, not yours to sell." She reached across the table. "Give it back."

Orsen unwrapped the oilcloth. The memory sat in its vial, swirling faintly gold, restless as a trapped moth. Twenty years of careful preservation. Orsen's masterpiece.

Their hands shook as they passed it over.

Maren held it up to the lamplight, studying it the way a jeweler studies a stone. Then she pulled the stopper and drank it down in one motion, the way one takes medicine.

For a long moment she was utterly still. Then her breath caught, and her face changed—not younger, but *fuller*, like a landscape filling with rain. When she finally spoke, her voice was thick.

"He hummed *off-key*. All these years I'd have lived with an angel singing in my head, and the truth is he hummed like a strangled cat, and I loved him, and it was terrible, and it was ours." She set the empty vial down with great care. "Thank you."

Orsen stared. "You're thanking me?"

"I'm thanking you for keeping it safe. The taking—" she raised a finger— "the taking, we will discuss for the rest of both our lives, and I will not pretend it was anything but theft, however gently done. But you kept it. Through wards falling and stars dying and crowns shattering, you kept his humming safe in your pocket." She stood, joints cracking. "That counts for something. Not everything. *Something.*"

At the door she paused. "There's a meeting tonight. The whole town, at the meeting hall. About what's to be done with you."

"And what will you tell them?"

Maren smiled then, fully, and it transformed her face. "I'll tell them the truth. That you're a thief who kept better custody of our hearts than we did ourselves. Let them make of that what they will." She stepped out into the evening. "Wash your face. Wear something decent. You're going to be judged by people whose memories you've held in your hands."

* * *

The meeting hall had never been so full.

Orsen stood at the front, alone, while the town filled the benches and overflowed into the doorway and pressed against the windows from outside. Strangers from the new road mingled with neighbors Orsen had known all their life. Alderman Vess was absent—still in custody, awaiting the council of freed communities' judgment, his years of secret correspondence with the crown laid bare by the flood of returned memories. His chair at the head of the room sat empty, and everyone looked at it, and no one sat in it.

Old Marta spoke first. She'd been Orsen's first customer after Maren, thirty years ago, trading away the memory of a stillborn child.

"I got it back with all the rest," she said, her voice steady as a mason's level. "And do you know what I learned? That the day after you took it from me, I laughed at a joke. I danced at a wedding. I went on living like a woman who'd never lost anything, because you'd made sure I couldn't grieve." She fixed Orsen with eyes gone pale with age. "You stole my mourning, child. You stole the one thing that proved I'd loved. I want you to know I'm angry. And I want you to know I'm working on forgiving you, and that some days the anger wins, and that's how it's going to be for a while."

"Thank you," Orsen said, and meant it, and knew it wasn't enough, and knew nothing would be enough, and that this—this standing here, this being seen, this accounting—was the whole of what could be offered.

Others followed. A man whose courage Orsen had bought cheap in a bad winter. A girl—barely grown—whose first heartbreak Orsen had extracted for her mother, who hadn't wanted her daughter to suffer. Each one spoke, and Orsen listened, and the listening was the punishment and the penance both, and it went on for hours.

Some doors opened. Talia's stayed closed—she came, stood at the back, and left before Orsen could speak to her again. Some doors opened only partway: the blacksmith would take Orsen's coin-work repairing the forge roof, but nothing else, "and that's business, not forgiveness, don't confuse the two."

Near midnight, when the last speaker had finished, old Bren—sitting with the veterans at the side, having insisted on attending though Hollowmere wasn't his home—leaned over and muttered, "In the army, after a defeat, they make you write the report yourself. This is worse. This is better. I can't decide."

Then Maren stood, one last time.

"I move," she said, "that Orsen Zhou keeps their house, and stays in Hollowmere, and does the work of making amends for as long as it takes—which may be forever, and that's fair. And I move further"—her voice rose, and something in the room rose with it—"that their trade changes hands. No more selling. No more buying. But there are people in this world, now more than ever, carrying memories too heavy to hold alone. Someone should help them carry. Not take—*carry*. Side by side, awake, together." She looked at Orsen. "If you want a trade, thief, learn a new one."

The vote wasn't even close.

* * *

Afterward, Orsen stood outside the hall under a sky that still held no stars—the stars had not returned yet, though Sefa's maps promised they would, moon by moon, as the thirteen ward-stones settled into their new work of holding memory instead of hiding a valley.

Ilse found them there, breath steaming in the cold.

"Well?" she said. "Verdict?"

"Hard labor. Indefinitely." Orsen laughed, and it came out ragged. "She wants me to sit with people while they remember. To witness. To help them carry what they can't put down." Orsen shook their head. "Twenty years I made a living helping people forget. Now they want me to teach remembering."

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then: "My teacher in Vael's archives wrote that memory isn't a possession. It's a practice. Like music. You don't store a song—you play it, or it dies." She bumped Orsen's shoulder with hers. "You've got a lot of dead songs to help bring back, Zhou. Better get started."

Down the lane, a light burned in Orsen's kitchen window—Maren, probably, or Tomas, letting themselves in with the key Orsen had finally cut for friends, the door standing open behind them. From inside came the sound of laughter, several voices tangled together, and beneath it, faintly, someone humming badly off-key.

Orsen stood in the dark a moment longer, looking up at the starless sky, counting moons until the first one would rise.

Thirteen turns. One already past.

And tomorrow there would be hard labor, and grief to carry alongside strangers, and doors that opened and doors that stayed shut, and a home that was no longer invisible—which meant it could finally, truly, be seen.

Orsen turned toward the light, and the humming, and went in.
