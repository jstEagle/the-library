## 38. The Widow's Memory

The frost came early that autumn, silvering the rooftops of Hollowmere like a second skin of memory. Orsen noticed it first on the morning they swept their doorstep—the same doorstep where a dying mage had once collapsed with a shard of the Last Crown clutched to her chest—and thought, absurdly, of the widow Maren Kettle.

They had not seen her since the night the stars went out.

That was not entirely true. They had seen her at a distance, in the market square, in the queue outside the baker's, her back straight and her basket on her arm, always turning away before their eyes could meet. Orsen had understood. There were perhaps forty people in Hollowmere who had sold Orsen their worst hours over the years, and now every one of them carried those hours again, returned by the shattering of the Crown into the ward-stones—returned unasked, unwanted, in the middle of an ordinary evening while bread was rising and children were being called in from the fields. Forty people who remembered exactly what it felt like to hand their grief to a stranger for coin, and exactly what it felt like to have that stranger give it back without permission.

Orsen did not blame them for looking away.

What surprised them was how much they minded.

* * *

The knock came on the third morning of the frost, just past dawn, when the light through the eastern window lay across the floorboards in long amber bars. Orsen was still learning to sleep properly—learning what sleep was when you weren't afraid of your own dreams—and the knock pulled them up out of a dream about their grandmother's kitchen, the smell of cardamom bread, the sound of her voice saying *remember this, little thief, remember all of it.*

Maren Kettle stood on the step with snow in her grey hair and a small wooden box held in both hands, the way one carries a bird with a broken wing.

"Maren," Orsen said. Their voice came out rough. "Come in. It's freezing."

"I'll stand." She didn't say it unkindly. She said it the way she'd said everything in the years Orsen had known her: plainly, with no decoration, a woman who had buried a husband and a son and had run out of patience for anything that wasn't true. "I won't keep you long. I've been walking past this door for six weeks, if you want to know. Every morning I tell myself today, and every morning I turn around at the well." She looked down at the box. "This morning I made myself come before I could change my mind."

Orsen stepped back anyway, leaving the door open, an invitation that cost nothing to offer and might never be taken. Maren considered it, then crossed the threshold after all, sitting in the chair by the fire as though the sitting itself were a concession she hadn't planned to make.

"I owe you an accounting," she said.

"You don't—"

"Let me finish, boy. Girl. Whatever you are these days." A ghost of her old dry humor flickered there and was gone. "I practiced this speech for six weeks too, so you'll hear all of it."

She set the box on her knees but did not open it.

"When my Tam died—my son, not my husband, my husband I'd made my peace with years before—you came to my house with your little glass vials and your soft voice, and you took his death out of me. The night he drowned in the millrace. You took it, and you left me two coppers' worth of peace, and I paid you a month of egg money for it, and I thanked you." Her hands tightened on the box. "I thanked you, Orsen Zhou. Do you know what that was like, remembering it? After the Crown broke? Remembering that I had handed my own child's drowning to a stranger because carrying it hurt, and then thanking them for taking it?"

Orsen sat very still. The fire ticked. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, ordinary as anything.

"It was like waking up mid-drowning," Maren said. "That's what it was like. All of it came back at once—Tam, and the millrace, and the water in his lungs, and the look on his face when they pulled him out—and underneath it, this other thing, this shame, that I had paid to forget him. That I had let someone carry off the last piece of him I had, like he was a coat I'd grown tired of wearing." She finally looked up, and her eyes were wet but level. "I was angry at you for a long time. Six weeks of angry. I'm telling you that honestly, because you deserve honesty more than you deserve comfort."

"I know," Orsen said quietly. "I've been counting the doors that don't open when I knock. It's most of them."

"And you keep knocking anyway."

"Someone has to hear the apology eventually. If I stop knocking, I've decided for them."

Maren studied them for a long moment. Then something in her shoulders eased—not forgiveness yet, but the ground it could grow in.

"That's not what I came to say, though," she said. "I came to say I understand it now. What you were doing. Not—" she raised a hand before Orsen could speak, "—not excusing it. Understanding it. When the memories came back, everyone in this valley got their whole history at once, yes? Every stolen hour, returned. And do you know what I learned, sitting with Tam's drowning night after night?" She opened the box at last.

Inside, nested in wool gone grey with age, lay a glass vial no longer than a finger. It caught the firelight and held it, and even from across the room Orsen felt it—a faint pressure against the mind, like a word waiting to be spoken. One of theirs. One of the old extractions, from before, from the years when Orsen had believed the wards would hold forever and forgetting was a kindness you could sell.

It should have been empty. Every vial in Hollowmere had drained the night the Crown shattered, its contents flooding home to their owners along threads of light no one could explain. Orsen had checked. They had checked a hundred times, standing in their cellar among the racks of glass, finding nothing but clean, empty vessels.

"This one didn't go back," Maren said. "I woke up with everything else, but this stayed behind. I asked Sefa about it, when she was here drawing her maps. She said some memories root too deep to travel. She said maybe part of me refused to take it back." Her voice wavered, steadied. "But I think it's simpler than that. I think it's yours now. You carried it for eleven years, Orsen. You carried my son's death in your head alongside all the others, every day, whether you knew it or not. That makes it partly yours. And I find—" she laughed, short and startled, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, "—I find I don't want it anymore, but I can't throw it away either. So I'm bringing it to its keeper."

She held the box out.

Orsen did not move for a moment. Then they rose, crossed the room, and knelt beside her chair to receive it, the way one receives something consecrated. The vial was cold even near the fire. Through the glass, faint as breath on a window, Orsen saw the millrace at night, the churn of white water, a boy's boot catching on the sluice-gate—and looked away before it could open fully.

"I'm sorry," they said. It was inadequate and they both knew it. "For all of it. For taking it. For selling it. For making you thank me."

"I know you are." Maren's free hand settled briefly on their head, an awkward benediction, gone almost before it landed. "And here's the rest, so listen close. When I got Tam back—all of him, the drowning and the laughing and the way he whistled off-key—I thought it would kill me. Everyone thought it would kill us, that first week. Half the town walked around like ghosts with their eyes open." She smiled, and it was a tired smile but a real one. "We didn't die. We carried it. That's what people do, when there's no one to sell it to. We carry it. And some days the carrying is holy, Orsen. Some days remembering him is the only thing keeping me upright. I wouldn't trade it back now for anything."

She stood, smoothing her skirt, businesslike again.

"But there are people in this valley for whom the carrying is too heavy. You know who they are better than anyone—you took from them yourself. Old Piet, whose brother did whatever it was he did in the war. The Halloran girl. Half the folk on Mill Lane. They got their memories back and they're drowning in them, and there's no market for forgetting anymore, thanks to you, and good riddance—but that leaves them nowhere to put it down." She fixed Orsen with a look that had cowed aldermen. "So. Here is what I've decided you're going to do instead."

Orsen blinked. "Decided?"

"I'm seventy-one years old and I buried everyone I loved. I get to decide things." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "You're going to sit with them. That's all. No vials, no extraction, no coin changing hands. You're going to go to their houses and you're going to listen while they tell you the worst thing they ever lived through, and you're going to help them carry it—not by taking it away, but by holding one end of it. The way friends do. The way nobody in this valley ever did, because we were all too busy buying our way out of each other's lives."

"That's not a trade," Orsen said slowly. "There's nothing in it for me."

"No," Maren agreed. "That's rather the point, isn't it? You spent eleven years being paid to help people forget. Now you can spend the rest of your life unpaid, helping them remember. See which one leaves you lighter." She shrugged into her shawl. "Start with Piet. He hasn't left his house in three weeks. His sister's memory came back and it's eating him alive, and he's too proud to say so, and his pride will bury him next to his brother if someone doesn't get there first."

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, snow swirling in behind her.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the knocking worked. On me. Six weeks of you leaving bread on my step and notes under my door, apologizing to a woman who wouldn't look at you. Foolish boy. Stubborn as your grandmother." And then she was gone down the lane, straight-backed, her footprints filling with snow behind her.

Orsen stood in the doorway a long time, the box cradled against their chest, feeling the vial's small cold weight like a heartbeat.

* * *

Piet Aldous's house sat at the crooked end of Mill Lane, shutters drawn against the morning, smoke barely threading from the chimney—as if the fire inside burned out of habit rather than need. Orsen had robbed this house twice. Once for a debt Piet couldn't pay and once, later, for a memory he'd begged them to take: the last letter his brother Corwin had sent from the war, full of things a brother shouldn't know and couldn't unread.

They knocked. Nothing. Knocked again, and heard, faintly, the scrape of a chair.

"Piet," Orsen called through the wood. "It's Orsen Zhou. I'm not selling anything. I'm not taking anything. I just brought bread." A lie; they'd forgotten the bread. They'd bring some tomorrow. "Maren Kettle sent me. She says you haven't been eating."

A long silence. Then, muffled, bitter: "Send her my regards. And tell her I don't need a thief's pity."

"No," Orsen agreed, leaning their shoulder against the doorframe, settling in the way one settles in for weather. "You don't. But you might need company, and I happen to be the only person in this valley who already knows the worst thing that ever happened to you. You don't have to explain it. You don't have to perform it. You can just be sad in front of someone who was there."

Another silence, longer. Orsen counted the heartbeats of it, watching their breath fog in the cold.

The bolt slid back.

Piet Aldous looked like a man who had been underwater for three weeks. His eyes were rimmed red, his beard untrimmed, and when he saw who stood on his step his jaw tightened—but he stepped aside, and Orsen entered, and the door closed behind them against the snow.

Inside, the house smelled of dust and cold tea. Corwin's letter lay on the table where it had lain, Orsen suspected, for twenty-one days straight, its edges softening from handling.

"He wrote it the week before he died," Piet said hoarsely, not looking at it. "Did you know that? Twenty-two years I didn't know what was in that letter. Twenty-two years I mourned him clean, like a good brother should. And now I know, and I can't—" His voice cracked. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "How do you carry a thing like that?"

Orsen sat down across from him. Outside, the snow fell on Hollowmere without mercy and without malice, covering the lanes where forty closed doors waited, and beyond the valley the world went on remembering itself, vast and raw and new.

"One end at a time," they said, and reached for the letter. "Tell me about him. Start anywhere. I'll hold whatever you hand me."

And somewhere far to the north, past the mountains where the drowned citadel slept beneath its flood, past the border towns learning to live unhidden, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its final turn—and Orsen, for the first time in their life, was not afraid of what remembering would ask of them next.
