## 39. Found Family, Found Home

The house on Lantern Row had never held this many people.

Orsen stood in the doorway of their own kitchen and counted them the way they once counted ward-stones—out of habit, out of fear that one might be missing. Ilse at the table, sleeves rolled, arguing cheerfully with Tomas about the correct way to gut a river trout. Bren by the hearth, too large for any chair Orsen owned, sitting instead on the floor with his back against the stone like a soldier who had never fully trusted furniture. Sefa at the window seat, sketching, her charcoal moving in quick sure strokes even as she watched everything else.

And at the edges—the doorway, the fence line, the far side of the garden where the light from the kitchen couldn't quite reach—Captain Reye, standing like a woman waiting to be told she wasn't welcome.

Orsen supposed she was right to wait.

* * *

It was Tomas who had organized it, which surprised no one who knew him and would have astonished everyone in Hollowmere who didn't. He'd spent two weeks after their return going door to door—not apologizing for what he'd done, though he'd done that too, in the small painful way he did all hard things—but inviting. Not demanding attendance, not pleading. Just naming an evening, an address, and leaving before anyone could refuse him to his face.

"They'll come or they won't," he'd said, hauling Orsen's old table into the yard to scrub off years of dust. "That's not ours to decide anymore. That's the whole point, isn't it? Nothing's ours to decide for other people now."

The whole point. Orsen turned the phrase over sometimes like a coin they weren't sure was currency. The Crown shattered. Every memory it hoarded returned to the world in a single flood of light and grief. And now Hollowmere—a town built on forgetting, on being forgotten—sat unhidden under ordinary stars, full of neighbors who remembered things about each other that could not be unsaid.

Some of those things were about Orsen.

Mrs. Calloway, whose husband's last words Orsen had sold to a stranger for three silver marks, still crossed the street when she saw them coming. Old Piet wouldn't take their coin at market, wouldn't take anything from them at all, just looked through them the way you look through a window at weather. Orsen had expected rage. Rage would have been easier. What they got instead was arithmetic—the quiet daily summing-up of a thief's ledger, read aloud in glances.

But others came differently. A cooper whose childhood Orsen had extracted twenty years ago found them at the well and said only, "I have it back now. All of it. Even my mother's voice." He'd squeezed Orsen's shoulder hard enough to bruise and walked away, and Orsen had stood there holding the bucket, understanding that forgiveness was not a door that opened but a field you had to walk across, slowly, in all weathers.

Tomas understood that better than anyone. He had been the one who wrote to Vess. He had been the one whose letter led the militia to the ford, to the ambush, to Sefa's capture and the loss of the first shard and nearly all their lives. No one blamed him—everyone had said so, quickly, the way you say such things—and that was precisely why he couldn't bear it. So he scrubbed tables. He invited. He carried Bren's pack without asking. He rebuilt what he could reach with his hands because his hands were the only part of himself he trusted anymore.

The evening he'd planned was meant to be small. Six people. One supper.

By the time the light went amber over the western hills, word had spread the way warmth spreads, and half the street had left things on Orsen's doorstep: a loaf, a jar of honey, a bundle of late carrots, a bottle of plum wine with no note. Some of the givers were seen; most were not. Orsen counted the offerings and felt something crack open behind their ribs that was not entirely pain.

"Stop counting," Ilse said, appearing at their elbow with the particular grace of someone who had learned to move quietly around people carrying heavy things. "You do it with everything. Doors, coins, people."

"It's how I know nothing's missing."

Ilse looked out at the yard—at Bren laughing, actually laughing, at something Tomas had shouted from the fire pit; at Sefa's charcoal whispering across paper; at Reye, still at the fence, still outside the lamplight.

"Something is missing," Ilse said gently. "Go get it."

* * *

Reye heard footsteps on the grass and did not turn. Twenty years of habit kept her facing the road, cataloguing approaches, though the war was over and her ledger was ash and the only thing left to guard was her own discomfort.

"You're letting the food get cold," Orsen said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Liar." Orsen came to stand beside her at the fence. Below the lane, Hollowmere's windows were lighting up one by one, and above them—this was still new, this was still a small miracle every time—stars. Real stars, dozens of them, thickening as the dark deepened. For thirty years the valley had shown its people nothing but haze. Now the sky went on forever, and Orsen caught themselves looking up the way other people checked a wound.

"The council sits tomorrow," Reye said. "In Cindral. My testimony."

"I know. Tomas has been rehearsing objections to your testimony all week. He says if you soften anything he'll know you're lying."

A sound escaped Reye that might once have been a laugh, before duty filed it down. "He's right. I've drafted it eleven times. Every version makes me more forgivable than I was." She turned then, finally, and Orsen saw the exhaustion carved into her face—the face of a woman who had burned her own name along with her ledger. "I killed people, Zhou. Before the Crown fell. Good ones. People like Bren's unit, erased and discarded. I followed orders because the orders were written down and the writing made them feel clean." Her jaw tightened. "Your supper won't make that clean. Nothing will. That's rather the point of remembering, isn't it?"

"Yes," Orsen said. "And you come anyway. That's also the point."

They stood together while the stars multiplied.

"I don't know how to sit at a table," Reye said at last, very quietly, in the voice of someone confessing to a smaller crime than her real ones. "I haven't—I don't remember how."

"Then sit near Bren," Orsen said. "He doesn't remember either. You can learn together."

* * *

Sefa drew the evening as it happened, because that was how she understood evenings: one stroke at a time.

She drew Ilse's hands first, always the hands—they moved when she talked, carving shapes out of air, and tonight they were shaping the story of the drowned citadel for perhaps the hundredth time, water rising in gestures, the Crown assembling itself out of shadow. She drew Tomas's shoulders, hunched with laughter, and the exact angle of Bren's head thrown back. She drew the fire pit and the smoke going straight up in the windless dark, and she drew the moment Reye finally crossed the yard—drew it mid-stride, caught between fence and lamplight, neither enemy nor quite guest—and Bren looking up at her with an expression Sefa worked hardest of all to capture, because it contained both the order he remembered and the hand he chose to extend anyway.

He'd taken her wrist when she reached the fire. Not a handshake. A grip, soldier to soldier, the kind that said *I know what you did and I am deciding, nightly, what to do about it.* Reye had gone very still. Then she had gripped back.

Sefa drew that too.

Her voice had gone with the flood—when the memories poured out of the shattering Crown and into every living mind, hers had simply... stopped, drowned somewhere in the rush of ten thousand returned lifetimes. The healers said it might come back. They said it might not. Sefa had decided, in her silent methodical way, that it hardly mattered. Words had been the least honest thing about her anyway. Give her charcoal, give her paper, and she could say anything.

Tonight she said: *look.*

She turned her sketchbook around and passed it across the fire, page by page. The table. The food. Ilse mid-story. Tomas laughing. Bren and Reye, wrists locked. And on the last page, drawn small in the corner as if she hadn't quite dared to make it bigger: the whole group from above, from the point of view of the stars, six small figures around one small fire in one small yard at the edge of a valley that had spent thirty years pretending it wasn't there.

No one spoke for a moment. Then Tomas said, thickly, "That's us," in the tone of a man confirming a rumor he hadn't let himself believe.

Bren studied the drawing a long time. His unit had been nine men. He remembered all of them now—every face, every joke, the songs they'd sung on the march to Ashfield, and the morning after, when Captain Reye's clerks had come with their instruments and their forms, and Bren had woken up with holes where his brothers used to be. He had spent forty years grieving people he couldn't name. Now he named them nightly, aloud, like a prayer with teeth.

"This," he said, tapping the sketchbook, his voice gravel-rough. "This is what they took from me. Not the fighting. Not the war. *This.*" He looked around the fire, at each of them in turn. "Forty years thinking I was alone because some clerk decided loneliness was safer than loyalty." He raised his cup. "So. To the clerk's worst nightmare."

"To strays," Ilse said.

"To the mapmaker," Tomas added, nodding at Sefa, "who got us out of the ford with a route that shouldn't exist."

"To liars, thieves, deserters, and defectors," Reye said, and the surprise on her own face at hearing herself joke was worth more than the joke.

They drank. The fire cracked. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked and was answered, and the ordinary sounds of a town settling into night rose around them like water returning to a dry riverbed, filling every low place.

* * *

Later, when the wine was gone and Bren had fallen asleep sitting up and been lowered, protesting, onto Orsen's ancient couch, Ilse stepped out to the garden where Orsen was stacking dishes that no longer needed washing just to have something to do with their hands.

"You keep doing that," she observed.

"Habit."

"Mm." She leaned on the fence beside them. Above, the thirteen moons were rising—or rather, twelve moons and the space where the thirteenth would complete its cycle soon, the valley's strange calendar turning toward whatever came next. "Tomorrow I leave for Cindral," she said. "The teaching. Memory-ethics, they're calling it. As if ethics were a thing you could teach instead of a thing you choose every morning before breakfast."

"Will you take students?"

"Anyone who asks. Especially the ones who frighten me." She smiled faintly. "Especially the ones I resemble."

Orsen stacked another dish. "You could stay. There's work here. The town needs—" They stopped. Started again, honestly: "I'd like it if you stayed."

Ilse looked at them for a long moment, and in the starlight her scarred face was very open, very tired, very fond. "I know," she said. "And I'll come back. But I spent my whole life running from what the Crown made of power, and now I have the chance to stand in front of young people and tell them the truth about it. Declining the throne was one choice. Teaching others to decline theirs—that's the rest of my life, I think." She bumped Orsen's shoulder with hers. "Besides. You're not alone anymore. Look at your yard. You collected an entire ridiculous family while you weren't paying attention."

Orsen looked. At the fire burning low. At Bren's snore rolling out the open window. At Sefa still sketching by the last candle, drawing the sleeping house as tenderly as she'd draw a fortress. At Tomas, asleep in a chair with a dish towel over his face, having refused all evening to go home because, he claimed, someone had to guard the leftovers.

"I stole memories for fifteen years," Orsen said quietly. "I told myself it was mercy. Taking the worst moments from people who couldn't carry them. And some of it was mercy—don't look at me like that, some of it was. But mostly it was easier to hold other people's grief than my own. Easier to sell the past than live in it." They set down the last dish. "Now everyone remembers everything, including me, and it turns out the grief fits after all. It fits because it's shared."

"That's the whole lesson," Ilse said. "Took you forty chapters to learn it."

"What?"

"Nothing," Ilse said, and laughed at some private joke with the universe, and went inside to claim the good blanket before Tomas woke up and took it.

* * *

Near midnight, long after the fire had become embers, Orsen sat alone in the yard with Sefa's sketchbook open on their knees, studying the view from the stars.

That was when they noticed it.

At the bottom corner of the final page, beneath the six figures around the fire, Sefa had drawn something small and precise and unmistakable: the thirteenth moon, hanging over the western ridge, rendered exactly as it would appear on the night its cycle completed. And beneath it, in the neat silent handwriting she'd taught herself in place of speech, four words:

*the sky finishes then*

Orsen looked up. The moons wheeled overhead, patient as ward-stones, and somewhere beyond the ridge the cracked thirteenth stone stood in its hollow in the earth, empty now, holding nothing but grandmother's message and the shape of what had been kept there.

Soon, then. Whatever came next—whatever a world with no Crown and no hidden valleys and no singular power would become—it would begin on that night, under a finished sky.

Orsen closed the sketchbook, and went inside to sleep among their family, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of nothing stolen at all.
