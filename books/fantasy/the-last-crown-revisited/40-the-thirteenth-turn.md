## 40. The Thirteenth Turn

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as arrive.

Orsen felt it before they saw it—a pressure behind the sternum, like a door being unlatched somewhere in the blood. They were sitting on the roof of their own house, the shingles warm beneath them even after dark, because Hollowmere's autumn had gone strange and gentle since the world remembered itself. Below, the town glowed with lamplight that spilled honestly from windows instead of hiding behind shutters. Nobody hid anymore. That was the strangest miracle of all, and it had taken Orsen the whole season to stop flinching at it.

They counted the moons out of habit.

One through twelve hung above the valley in their slow procession, pale and patient, the way they had always hung—or the way everyone had *believed* they hung, before the night the stars went out and the wards collapsed and the truth came flooding through the broken glass of the sky. The thirteenth moon was different. It had appeared only weeks ago, low on the southern horizon, a sliver of silver-green that no almanac named and no elder could explain. It grew a little each night. Tonight, the last sliver would close.

Tonight, the circle would complete.

Orsen's grandmother's voice rose unbidden—no longer a ghost now, not since the flood of returned memories had given her back to Orsen whole: her laugh with its cracked edge, her hands smelling of stone-dust and lamp oil, her terrible singing. *Thirteen stones, thirteen moons, little thief,* she used to say when Orsen was small enough to sit on her knee and still small enough to believe safety was a permanent condition. *Everything in this valley comes in thirteens. Count them. The day you stop counting is the day you've stopped paying attention.*

She had hidden the Crown here. She had built the lie of the valley around it, ward by ward, moon by moon, a shell of forgetting thick enough to hide a relic that held every ruler who ever lived. And she had done it knowing—she must have known—that someday someone would have to finish what she started. She had left the message in the hollow stone like a hand on Orsen's shoulder, saying: *I'm sorry I gave you this instead of giving you nothing. Choose better than I did.*

Orsen had chosen. The Crown lay shattered in thirteen pieces, one sunk into each ward-stone, its hoarded centuries poured out into the world like water finding every crack in a dry field. Vael was drowned again, properly this time, its sunken hall silent under the river. And the valley—Hollowmere, invisible for three generations, safe as a secret—was visible forever. On every map Sefa drew now, there it sat: a green notch between the mountains, marked with no symbol at all, just her neat hand-lettered name.

Some nights Orsen still woke reaching for the old invisibility the way you reach for a blanket that's been taken away. Some mornings they stood at the window and watched a merchant caravan come up the road from Cindral—the road that hadn't existed, the road that was suddenly and irreversibly there—and felt the fear rise and then, slowly, fall. Because the caravans brought letters. Brought granddaughters reunited with grandmothers whose names had been sold away decades ago. Brought a widow named Maren with a jar of plum preserves and a smile that said *I remember, and I stayed anyway.*

The fear fell faster these days.

Below, a door opened. Light spilled across the yard, and Tomas's voice carried up the way it had carried across every rooftop of their childhood: "You're going to miss it sitting up there brooding like a gargoyle."

"I'm not brooding."

"You're doing the thing where you count things."

"Grandmother said to count."

"Your grandmother," Tomas called back, climbing the ladder they'd nailed to the side of the house two summers ago and never explained to anyone, "also said never trust a man who whistles while he works, and you married a man who whistles while he works, so her record is mixed." Ilse appeared behind him on the ladder, laughing, one hand pressed to her ribs where the enforcer's blade had caught her a lifetime and four months ago. She still favored that side in cold weather. She probably always would. Some scars were simply part of the architecture now.

Orsen scooted over on the shingles and made room. Three of them fit if nobody minded elbows, and nobody minded elbows anymore. That was another thing the year had taught: there was always room if you stopped pretending you wanted to be alone.

"It's close," Ilse said, tilting her head back. The twelve moons hung in a loose arc, and the thirteenth was nearly full now, its green-silver light lying across the valley like frost. "Can you feel it?"

"I've felt it all week. Like a held breath."

"Tomas says it's nothing. Tomas says the moons are just rocks and rocks don't keep appointments."

"Tomas," said Tomas, settling heavily against the chimney, "has been right about almost everything except the things that mattered, which he has gotten catastrophically wrong, and he'd like that noted for the historical record." But his voice was soft. He was watching the sky the way he'd watched the ford at Ambush—coiled, loyal, ready to throw himself between his people and whatever came next, even if what came next was only weather.

Orsen looked at their oldest friend and felt the old ache and the old gratitude tangle together, the way they always did. *What Tomas Saw.* They had forgiven him within a day of the drowning of Vael—had forgiven him almost before he finished confessing, standing soaked and shaking on the causeway with militia torchlight dying behind him—but forgiveness wasn't the same as forgetting, and forgetting wasn't possible anymore for anyone. That was the bargain. That was the whole bargain, the one Orsen had made with thirteen stones and a shattered crown: *you may have everything back, all of it, the betrayals and the coronations and the burning cities and your mother's face. Everything. Forever.*

Nobody had thanked them. Everybody had wept. It had seemed, at the time, an acceptable exchange rate.

"The others will be sorry they missed it," Ilse said.

"They're not missing it. They're just missing it from the ground." Bren was down at the tavern telling war stories to an audience that, for the first time in history, could verify them; the old soldier had discovered that remembering was a kind of labor and had thrown himself into it the way Reye threw herself into testimony—with grim, redemptive discipline. Sefa was in the undercity of Cindral still, or she had been yesterday; her maps arrived by courier now, each one a small act of cartographic rebellion, borders drawn in pencil so they could be erased. And Reye—

Reye was at the edge of the lamplight, as always. Orsen could see her silhouette by the gate, standing watch over a town that had every reason to hate her, holding a cup of Maren's cider she hadn't drunk. Former Captain Reye. Testifier Reye. The woman who had burned her own ledger page by page in the square at Cindral while the people whose names it held watched, some cheering, some spitting, one—an old woman whose son's erasure Reye had personally ordered—walking up and pressing the ashes back into her hands. *Carry it,* the woman had said. *That's your penance: carry it.*

Reye carried it. She would carry it until she died. Some debts weren't paid; they were inhabited.

"She'll come up if we wave," Tomas said.

"She won't."

"She might."

"She won't," Orsen said, "and that's hers to choose. That's the whole point of everything we did. Everyone gets to choose what they carry and how close they stand." They paused. "But leave the gate unlatched anyway."

Tomas reached down and unlatched the gate without looking. Some things didn't need choosing twice.

* * *

The thirteenth moon completed at the eleventh hour, and it did not complete quietly.

Orsen had expected silence—some vast celestial click, a lock turning, nothing more. Instead, when the final sliver filled, the moon rang. There was no other word for it. A tone rolled out across the valley, deep and silver, felt in the teeth and the stones and the water of the well, and every ward-stone in Hollowmere answered it—one, two, three, up to thirteen, each releasing a thread of light the color of dawn seen underwater. Orsen had put the Crown's shards into those stones. Orsen knew what slept in them. And now, on the completion of the thirteenth moon, the shards woke—not hungering, not calling, not assembling, never again assembling—but *singing*, briefly, like bells rung once at the end of a long funeral.

And then the stars came back.

They didn't fade in. They detonated—thousands upon thousands, the whole black dome igniting at once, constellations tumbling out of the dark like a chest of jewels overturned, the Milky Way pouring across the sky from horizon to horizon, so dense and bright that the moons paled and the valley below lit up in shades of blue. Orsen heard the town cry out—a single collective sound, wonder and terror braided together, the same sound they'd made the night the stars went out, but inverted now, transfigured, grief turned inside out into awe.

Ilse grabbed Orsen's arm hard enough to bruise. Tomas was laughing, actually laughing, big helpless gusts of it. Down at the gate, Reye set down her untouched cider, went to one knee in the dirt, and bowed her head to the sky, and Orsen chose not to decide whether that was worship or apology or both.

Because Orsen was looking at the stars and *remembering.*

That was the thing no one had warned them about—the last secret the Crown had kept, or perhaps the first secret the world had kept before the Crown ever existed. When the thirteen moons completed their cycle, something opened. Not in the sky. In the memory. Every person in the valley, in the world, felt it, Orsen would learn later: the sensation of a vast library unlocking, shelves swinging wide, every book available, every book demanding to be read.

For most people it would be gentle. A rush of their own lives, complete at last, the gaps the wards had eaten quietly refilled. For most people, tonight would be the night they got back the shape of their own hearts.

But Orsen had touched the shard. Orsen had carried the Crown's fragments, had read the makers' records in drowned Vael, had stood in the sunken hall when the assembled Crown sang its single hungry note across the valley. The Crown knew them. And so when the library unlocked, it opened *everything*—not only Orsen's stolen childhood, already bought back and grieved over in Cindral, but the Crown's own hoard. Every ruler. Every coronation. Every betrayal whispered behind throne-room doors. Every city burned, every famine ignored, every beautiful law and every monstrous one. Four hundred reigns, poured directly into one memory-thief's skull under a sky full of returning stars.

Orsen gripped the ridge of the roof and let it happen.

It was not pain, exactly. It was weight—the Weight of Forgetting inverted, the Weight of Remembering, arriving all at once. They felt themselves crowned and crowned and crowned again. They felt a queen weeping in a garden because she had signed a death warrant with a steady hand and couldn't make her hand shake. They felt a boy-king lied to so thoroughly that he died believing himself beloved. They felt the makers of the Crown, generations before any of it, arguing by lamplight about whether a ruler should be allowed to forget anything at all, and the terrible hope in their voices—they had meant it kindly. Gods, they had meant it kindly. The corruption hadn't been designed. It had grown, the way rot grows in a sealed room: slowly, quietly, fed by good intentions and bad air, until the thing built to preserve memory became the thing that devoured it.

They felt Ilse steal the shard—felt her terror, her trembling hands, the moment she decided a stranger's life was worth her own. They felt their grandmother, young and furious, carrying the Crown into a valley that didn't exist yet and beginning to build the lie that would keep it asleep. They felt her loneliness. Forty years of it. She had never once regretted it, and she had regretted it every single day, and both of those were true, and now Orsen could hold both, because that was what a complete memory was: room enough for contradictions.

They felt Maren the widow, the first client of their old life, and the memory Orsen had extracted from her—the last hour of her husband's voice—came home to its rightful place inside her, and somewhere across town Orsen knew she was feeling it too, holding it, choosing it, and they wept without shame for the version of themselves who had charged her eight silver marks for the privilege of losing him twice.

The flood crested. Held. Began, slowly, to settle—not draining but *settling*, the way sediment settles in clear water, layering itself into something navigable. Orsen knelt on the roof of their house under the blazing stars with four hundred rulers inside them and found, at the center of it all, astonishingly, themselves. Not erased by the hoard. Anchored by it. Every stolen life throwing their own small life into relief, the way the new stars threw the valley into visibility: not smaller for being seen. Defined.

"Ilse," they said. Their voice came out scraped raw. "Are you—"

"I have it too." Her eyes were streaming, but she was smiling, that fierce crooked smile that had survived a war, a theft, a battlefield, and a confession. "All of it. Every reign." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Do you know what I keep landing on? Not the kings. A cook. There was a cook in Vael, three hundred years ago, who memorized every meal the mad king ate so that afterward, when the historians argued about whether he'd been mad at all, someone could say *yes, he was, I fed him, I watched him forget his own daughter's face between breakfast and supper.* She kept the truth alive in recipes." Ilse shook her head. "History isn't thrones. It's cooks. It's whoever writes it down and refuses to let go."

"That's why it didn't take you," Orsen said slowly, understanding arriving like the moonlight, cool and total. "At the hall. When the Crown called a claimant. It chose you because you stole from it—but it couldn't hold you because you were never trying to *keep* anything. You were trying to give it back. From the very first night, at my threshold, bleeding on my doorstep—you were carrying it to give it back."

Ilse didn't answer. She leaned her head against Orsen's shoulder, and that was answer enough.

Below them, Hollowmere blazed with light. Doors were opening. People were coming out into their yards and the streets, faces turned upward, neighbors finding neighbors in the star-glare, some of them embracing, some standing apart with the careful posture of people relearning each other. Orsen saw Alderman Vess at the edge of the square, alone, both hands over his mouth—carrying it, all of it now, every name he'd sold, every letter he'd written to the crown, and no one forcing him to look, and no one letting him look away either. He would live with it. Perhaps he would grow around it, the way a tree grows around a fence post. Perhaps not. That was his road, and no one else's, and Orsen found they could wish him endurance without wishing him absolution. Both true. Room enough for contradictions.

Tomas broke the silence the way Tomas always did—gently, and from the side. "So," he said. "Four hundred rulers in your head. Any of them any good?"

"One." Orsen smiled despite everything. "A regent. Ruled eleven years for a niece who was too young. Refused the crown formally three times. Handed power over the moment the girl turned sixteen and retired to a farm and grew apples." They closed their eyes, savoring it, the way you savor the last note of a song. "Her whole reign fits in one sentence: *nothing happened, and everyone was fed.*"

"We should build her a statue."

"We should build her an orchard."

* * *

Much later—past midnight, when the twelve old moons had drifted west and the thirteenth hung full and luminous and finally, unmistakably, *named* (Sefa's courier had already sketched it onto the newest map, labeled in her precise hand: *the Moon of Return*)—the house emptied. Tomas went down to walk Maren home. Ilse curled up in the guest room that had stopped being a guest room sometime in the spring. Reye, passing below the window on her endless circuit, glanced up, and Orsen met her eyes, and nodded once, and she nodded back, and that was all either of them needed. The gate stayed unlatched. She came in and latched it behind herself, which was its own small treaty.

Orsen stayed on the roof.

This was theirs now, this hour, this perch. Below, their trade was dead—no one needed memories extracted anymore, no one needed forgetting purchased; the whole economy of their old life had dissolved overnight into something unprecedented and unprofitable and immeasurably better. What remained was stranger and harder: people came to Orsen now not to lose their pasts but to *hold* them. The widow had started it, handing back her husband's last hour and asking Orsen to help others carry what they could not forget. Now they came every market day—a farmer who remembered a brother he'd sold downriver and wanted help writing to him; a woman who'd recovered a memory of her own mother's cruelty and needed someone to sit with her while she decided what to do with it; children, endlessly curious children, asking Orsen what the stars had looked like when they were gone, and Orsen telling them the truth: *like a ceiling. Like being loved by something that wouldn't show its face.*

They were not invisible anymore. Their name was known in three towns and one capital and at least one council of freed communities, usually preceded by the words *the one who shattered*. Strangers waved at them in the street. Some forgave them. Some didn't. All of them knew exactly who had taken their memories and sold them, and exactly who had given everything back, and both of those people were the same person, and the valley had decided—together, imperfectly, in the way communities decide anything—that it could hold both truths in one pair of hands.

Orsen held both truths in one pair of hands.

They looked up. The stars were all there—every constellation the elders had half-remembered in songs, every pattern the mapmakers were racing to redraw, and beyond them the deep field of lights no one had names for yet, waiting, like blank pages. The thirteenth moon burned green-silver at the center of it, and from its light the thirteen ward-stones of the valley drank, and the valley itself—visible, vulnerable, mapped, remembered—glowed faintly in answer, a bowl of lamplight cradled in dark mountains, no longer hiding from anything.

Grandmother, Orsen thought, into the vast attentive dark. Look. We counted. All thirteen. We paid attention, and then we paid the price, and it was worth it, and I forgive you, and I am going to spend the rest of my life earning that sentence.

The wind moved through the valley, smelling of woodsmoke and rain and the far-off sea, and it felt less like an ending than like a page turning.

Because that was the other thing the stars had returned with, the thing Orsen had felt settling into their bones as the flood of memory receded: the open road. Not flight—Orsen was done running. Something else. Somewhere south, in the drowned ruins of Vael, divers were already reporting lights beneath the water, and the makers' records had mentioned other vaults, other relics, other experiments in the engineering of memory that the Crown's makers had abandoned—or buried. Somewhere east, Sefa's maps showed blank spaces that blank spaces had no right still to be, in a world where everything was supposed to be remembered. Somewhere out there, other valleys might be hiding under other lies, full of people who had forgotten on purpose, waiting for someone to climb the mountain and tell them the truth gently enough to survive it.

Orsen was, it turned out, uniquely qualified.

They stood up on the roof, under the whole restored sky, four hundred rulers and one apple-growing regent and one memory-thief from Hollowmere all looking out through the same eyes, and they spoke aloud to no one and everyone:

"All right. I remember everything." A breath. The stars burned. The moon held. "Now let's see what else is out there worth remembering."

Below, the unlatched gate creaked softly in the wind—open, the way it would stay, the way everything would stay from now on: doors, borders, skies, hands.

Orsen climbed down to pack.
