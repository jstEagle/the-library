## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The ledgers of the Cartographers' Guild were balanced to the width of a hair, and Noor Nystrom was the only one who knew it, because Noor Nystrom was the only one who read them all.

That was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement for eleven years, since a thin, quiet apprentice with ink-stained cuffs had demonstrated an uncanny gift for finding errors no one else could see, and Guildmaster Vess had smiled her slow smile and said, *You'll do.* Since then, Noor had lived in the counting room beneath the map vault—a room with no windows, no name on the door, and no expectation that anyone would ever come looking for its occupant. The guild's cartographers drew coastlines for kings. Noor made certain the numbers behind those coastlines agreed with one another.

Invisible work. Invisible hands. It suited Noor fine—or had, until tonight.

The trouble began at the ninth bell, in the margin of Ledger Seven, under the column headed *Ward Maintenance, Northern Reach*. There should have been an entry there. There was always an entry: quarterly payments to the ward-keepers' chapter, charcoal and silver for the anchor-stones, the standing tithe that kept the border wards fed like a hearth-fire is fed. Noor had copied that column a hundred times. They knew its rhythms the way other people knew the rhythm of their own breathing.

Tonight the column simply stopped. Not crossed out. Not corrected. *Stopped*—as though someone had lifted the words off the page and taken them elsewhere, leaving paper that had forgotten what it was supposed to say.

Noor ran a finger along the blank space. The vellum was smooth. No scrape marks, no ghost of ink beneath. An entry did not vanish from a guild ledger; entries were amended, struck through, initialed. Entries left scars when they went.

This one had left nothing at all.

They checked the neighboring columns. More gaps—small ones, easy to miss unless you had spent a decade learning what a ledger looked like when it was whole. A payment missing from the third of Frostmonth. A signature gone from the attestation line, though the attestation itself remained, unsigned by no one. And in the oldest ledger, the one chained shut in the iron cabinet that Vess alone was supposed to open, an entire page had thinned to translucence, as if the writing had soaked through the world instead of the paper.

Noor sat back on their stool. The lamp above the counting table hissed. Somewhere overhead, through three floors of stone and timber, the guild hall murmured with its usual evening noise—and then stopped murmuring.

Noor heard the silence spread like frost across glass. Then footsteps, many of them, hurrying. Then a sound they had never heard inside the guild's walls before: shouting, and beneath it, low and continuous, a groan like the earth turning over in its sleep.

They climbed the back stair two steps at a time and pushed through into the great map hall, where every cartographer in Highmoor Keep seemed to have gathered beneath the Vault of Heaven—the great painted ceiling, centuries old, where a thousand stars in gold leaf wheeled around a painted sun. Except half the stars were dark now. Not fallen. *Dark*, their gilt faces turned to shadow as though a hand had passed over them, and as Noor watched, another winked out, and another, the darkness crawling inward from the northern quarter of the ceiling like rot through fruit.

"The northern wards," someone was saying. "The whole Reach, gone dark—"

"Impossible. The wards are anchored—"

"Anchor-stones don't fail."

Noor pressed against the wall, unnoticed as always, and watched Guildmaster Vess descend the dais stairs with her ledger of state tucked under one arm and her face composed into something almost serene. She was a broad woman with a voice built for closing arguments, and she used it now.

"A tremor," she announced. "A natural subsidence in the old stones. The ward-keepers will restore order by week's end."

"But the stars—" a young mapmaker began.

"The *painting* has flaked," Vess said, "as paintings do. Return to your desks."

It was a lie so smooth that most of the hall swallowed it whole. But Noor had spent eleven years reading lies in ledgers, and they knew the grain of one when they saw it. Vess knew exactly why the stars had gone out. You could see it in the way she held the ledger of state—not carried, but *guarded*, pressed to her ribs like a wound she was keeping closed with both hands.

* * *

Vess summoned Noor to her study at midnight.

The study smelled of cedar oil and cold iron. Vess sat behind a desk older than the guild itself, and she did not offer a chair. She slid a single sheet of vellum across the desk instead, and Noor saw their own neat hand upon it—last month's audit summary, the one they'd submitted through the slot in the counting-room door, unsigned as always.

"You noticed," Vess said. It was not a question.

"The Ward Maintenance column. Yes."

"And you told no one." Vess leaned back. "Good. That discretion is precisely why I trust you with this next instruction." She folded her hands. "There are irregularities in the historical ledgers. Old ones. You will not audit them. You will not copy them. You will not so much as open the iron cabinet again. In fact—" the slow smile, the one Noor had once mistaken for kindness, "—I think the counting room needs a quieter occupant going forward. One who asks fewer questions. Do you understand me, Nystrom?"

Noor understood perfectly. They understood that Vess had just confessed, in the language of orders, that the vanished entries were not corruption but *excision*. That whatever had broken tonight had been broken long ago, deliberately, by the guild, and paid for out of a column Noor had balanced with loving care for a decade.

"No questions, Guildmaster," Noor said quietly.

Vess dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, already reaching for her wine.

Noor walked back down through the sleeping guild with their heart hammering a strange, bright rhythm. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, something else was kindling—something that felt disgracefully like *relief*. Eleven years invisible. Eleven years balancing the books of people who would walk past them in a corridor without seeing. And now, at last, the truth had put its hand on their shoulder and said: *you. You are the one who sees.*

If Vess wanted an invisible cartographer, she should not have taught one to read.

* * *

The iron cabinet yielded to Noor's picks sometime past the second bell—for eleven years Noor had picked everything in the guild worth picking, locks being merely ledgers made of brass—and inside, beneath a false bottom, lay the maps.

Covenant maps. Noor had heard the word *covenant* perhaps twice in a lifetime, always in whispers, always cut short. Now they unfolded the first sheet with shaking hands and understood why.

The border wards were not walls. They were a *promise*—a covenant sealed in dragon-fire centuries ago, anchored at three great stones across three realms, drawn here in inks that still glimmered faintly, red-gold and alive. And along the margins, in a clerk's cramped hand generations dead, ran the record of its unraveling: fire diverted, sold, siphoned into guild coffers. Entry by entry. Year by year. The same tidy figures Noor had been balancing all their life, revealed at last as the arithmetic of a betrayal.

The guild hadn't failed the wards. The guild had *unmade* them, coin by coin, and buried the receipt.

Noor worked fast, copying by lamplight—the three anchors, the covenant's terms, the route between realms marked in a dead cartographer's hand. When the last sheet was rolled and hidden inside their coat, they blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the guild breathe around them.

Then they went to find Sef.

They found the ward-keeper in the disused chart-room where the guild stored its failures—chained to a chair, actually, wrists bound with guild cord, sitting amid dusty globes with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to be furious or amused. Sef Ordo was grey-templed and broad-handed, a woman who had spent thirty years feeding silver to anchor-stones, and tonight the whole guild had decided the collapse was her negligence.

"They needed a keeper to blame," she said, when Noor had cut the cords. "And I'm the one who reported the stones running dry. Bad luck, reporting things here." Her eyes narrowed. "Why are you helping me? I don't even know your name."

"Noor Nystrom." It came out rusty; they hadn't said it aloud in months. "Nobody knows my name either. That's rather the point of me."

They pressed a copied map into her scarred hands. Sef unrolled it, read it by the light of the guttering lamp—and Noor watched thirty years of professional grief rearrange itself into fury, then into purpose.

"The Ember Threshold," Sef said slowly. "Three anchors. Three realms. If even half this is true—"

"It's true. I balance the ledgers. I would know."

Sef looked up at them, and something passed between them there in the room of failures—two invisible people recognizing each other by the light of stolen paper.

"Highmoor's gates lock at dawn," Sef said. "And Vess will burn these maps and anyone holding them. So we leave now, we travel hard, and we trust no one but each other and whoever's waiting at the first stone."

"Waiting?"

"The covenant maps show a dragon's sigil at Miravelle, still burning after all these years." Sef shouldered her pack and gave Noor a crooked, dangerous grin. "Either that's hope, cartographer, or it's the biggest lie on the page. Either way—we go see which."

Above them, through the high slit windows, the last gold stars over the painted north went dark, one by one, like eyes closing.

Noor pulled their coat tight around the stolen maps and followed Sef down the servants' stair, out of the only home they had ever been unseen in—walking, for the first time in eleven years, toward being seen.
