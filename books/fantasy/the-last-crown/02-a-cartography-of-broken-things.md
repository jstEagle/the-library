## 2. A Cartography of Broken Things

Noor had drawn Miravelle a hundred times, and every one of those maps was a lie.

The guild's charts showed the drowned realm as it had been before the sinking: terraced vineyards, bell-towers crowned with gulls, roads stitched neat as sutures between hilltowns. What lay beneath Noor's boots now was the drowned truth of it—a kingdom gone to shallow sea and salt-rotted stone, the bell-towers rising from green water like the ribs of some enormous, patient corpse.

They walked the spine of an old causeway, the only dry road left, and Noor kept stopping to redraw.

"Again?" Sef said, not unkindly. The ward-keeper walked like someone carrying a weight just behind the shoulders, hands always half-curled as if expecting chains. Three days since they'd fled the guildhall together, and Sef still flinched at every crow's call.

"The map's burning again." Noor held up the sheet. Where the ink showed the village of Tallowmere—prosperous, whole—the paper had scorched to a brown ghost of itself, edges curling inward like a hand closing. Beneath the burn, in fainter strokes that hadn't been there before, water lines were surfacing. The paper was telling the truth whether Noor wanted it or not.

Sef leaned close. In the low light of the drowned sun, their face was all hollows and stubbornness. "It did that in the guild archives?"

"No. It's doing it *here*." Noor turned the sheet over, checked the grain, checked for ash. Nothing. "These are covenant maps, Sef. Sealed in dragon-fire when the wards were raised. I think—" They stopped, hating how small the next word came out. "I think they're correcting themselves."

"Maps don't do that."

"No," Noor agreed quietly. "Honest ones don't have to."

They walked on. The water on either side of the causeway was the color of bottle glass, and things moved in it—not fish. Fish didn't move in straight, deliberate lines. Sef kept one hand on the ward-satchel at their hip, where the salvaged anchor-shards clicked together like teeth.

"You should know," Sef said, after a mile of silence, "that whatever we find down there—if we find the first stone—it may not be kind to us. Ward-keepers broke the covenant, Noor. My order. Whatever blame fell on me when the stars went out, some part of it was earned by people who died centuries ago."

"And cartographers drew the borders that made the trade possible." Noor folded the burned map away. "I've been doing the arithmetic since we left. Every ledger I ever balanced, there were entries I couldn't source. Fire-tithe. Ember-rent. I thought it was furnace fuel." Their laugh came out wrong. "We weren't buying coal."

Ahead, the causeway descended into a flooded plaza where a fountain stood dry above the waterline, its carved dragons worn smooth as river stones. And in the plaza's center, half-submerged, something dark caught the dying light and threw it back in colors no drowned thing should own: ember-orange, banked-coal red.

The first anchor-stone.

* * *

Sef had knelt before anchor-stones a thousand times. This was the work: palms flat on the glyph-face, breath slowed, voice pitched to the old cadences until the ward answered or didn't. It had never once answered with a voice.

"—who walks on Miravelle's bones? Who carries fire out of fire?"

Sef snatched their hands back. The glyphs along the stone's face had lit in sequence, and now they pulsed like a heartbeat—and beneath the pulse, from somewhere inside or below the stone, came breathing. Long, labored, wet-edged. A sound like a bellows full of seawater.

"Sef." Noor's voice had gone very careful. "There's something *in* the stone."

"Not in." The voice again, closer now, and Sef understood with a lurch that it wasn't coming through the stone but from behind it—from the shadow pooled against the plaza's far wall, a shadow much too large to be a shadow. "Under. Around. Through the cracks of everything your masters left broken. Come and see what a covenant looks like when it starves."

It dragged itself into the light.

Sef had seen dragon-bones in the guild museum, mounted and labeled and safe behind glass. Nothing had prepared them for a dragon made small by dying—a creature built for the span of mountain ridges folded down to the size of an ox, wings like torn sails, scales dull as cooled slag except along the throat, where a last thread of ember-light pulsed in time with the anchor-stone. One eye opened, molten gold going gray at the rim.

"Ashk," the dragon said, as if introducing itself to its own executioners. "Last heir of the Ember Line. And you are the thief and the pen. I can smell the guild on you both."

"We came to restore the wards," Sef said.

Ashk laughed. It was a terrible sound, cracked and rusting. "Of course you did. Now. When the price is paid by others. Tell me, keeper—do you know what the fire-tithe was?"

Sef's mouth went dry. Behind them, Noor said, very softly, "Tell them."

"Your guild," Ashk said, dragging itself nearer, each movement a negotiation with pain, "was sworn to feed the covenant. Dragon-fire anchored the wards; the wards needed tending; the tending needed flame. And three hundred years ago, your precious Guildmaster Corvane discovered the flame could be *sold*. Bottled. That a vial of covenant-fire would light a city for a winter, and kings would pay crowns for it." The gray-rimmed eye fixed on Sef. "So they skimmed. A little each year, always less than would be missed. Your ledgers called it fire-tithe because the tithe flowed *away*, not in. Generations of keepers feeding the wards thinner and thinner rations while the guild grew fat on the difference. Until the year the arithmetic finally failed."

The night the stars went out.

Sef sat down on the wet stone without deciding to. "I was taught the wards failed from neglect. From age."

"They failed from *theft*." Ashk's voice dropped to smoke. "And your cartographer's guild drew the trade routes that carried the stolen fire to market. Drew them beautifully."

Noor made a sound then—small, involuntary—and when Sef turned, they saw the cartographer holding the covenant map open in both hands. Half of it was burning. Not the drowned realm this time. The margins, the annotations, the neat guild stamps of authentication—curling black, peeling away like bark, and beneath them letters were surfacing in handwriting none of them recognized. Old handwriting. Covenant handwriting.

"It's true," Noor whispered. "Every lie I ever inherited—it's burning off the page."

* * *

They made camp in the shell of the fountain, above the waterline, with a driftwood fire that gave more smoke than heat. Ashk lay with her scarred length curled around the base of the anchor-stone like a moat of flesh, and the ember-thread at her throat pulsed slower now. Slower than it had that morning.

Noor worked by firelight, redrawing the map of Miravelle from scratch—no guild conventions, no inherited lies, just what their eyes could see and their hands could measure. Sef watched them work and thought about the weight behind their own shoulders, the one they'd carried since the guildhall doors closed behind them. Blame, they'd called it. But sitting here, watching a person discover their life's foundation was a fraud and keep drawing anyway—that wasn't blame anymore. That was grief with a compass.

"You could turn back," Sef said. "Take Ashk's testimony to the capital. Watch the guild burn properly, from outside. Be a hero instead of a fugitive."

Noor didn't look up. "Would the wards come back?"

"...No."

"Then I'm not interested in heroes tonight." They set down the pen and looked at Sef across the fire, and their eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "My whole life, the guild told me what I was. Invisible. Useful. A pair of hands that draws. I believed them because the alternative was believing my family built itself on theft." A breath. "Both things are true. But Ashk is dying keeping that stone company, and you carried shards out of a burning archive for strangers, and I—" their voice caught, steadied, "—I am done being invisible for people who lied to me. We go to the Threshold. All three."

From around the stone, Ashk's voice, dry as ash: "Sentimental. You'll both die at the Threshold. The covenant takes a crown-bearer's life, and neither of you is wearing a crown."

Noor went still. Sef saw it—the flinch, quickly buried, of someone who has just remembered something they spent a lifetime forgetting.

"Noor," Sef said slowly. "Why did the ledgers know your name before anyone else's did?"

Noor looked at the fire for a long moment. Then they picked up the pen, turned to a fresh page, and began to draw the road east—toward mountains that glowed faintly at their roots, toward a pass marked on no honest map.

"Ask me at the Threshold," they said. "If the fire doesn't tell you first."

Above the drowned towers of Miravelle, where no stars had shone for three days, a single point of light flickered—and held.
