## 1. The Quiet Country

The dead of Cinder Valley came to me in pieces.

Not their souls—those were gone before they ever reached my workbench, gone wherever souls go when a country has no magic to catch them. What came to me was quieter than that: a shinbone wrapped in linen, a skull cradled in a farmer's hat like something he'd plucked from his own garden, a child's finger bones laid out on a scrap of blue cloth because the mother remembered the child had been wearing blue the day the fever took her. I took them all. That was the arrangement, unspoken and older than any contract: the valley buried its dead, and I listened to them first.

I was carving the morning the story properly begins, though I didn't know it then. I was carving and the frost on my window had grown feathers overnight, and the light coming through it was the color of skimmed milk, and I remember thinking—this is enough. This is exactly enough. Remember thinking it is important, because it was the last time I would think it honestly for a very long while.

The bone on my bench belonged to Aldous Merrin, who had farmed the terraces above the millpond for sixty years and died in his sleep with his boots still by the door, laced, as if some part of him had been planning one more walk. His son had carried the femur down through the snow himself, refusing the sledge, refusing help, walking the four miles with the bone against his chest the way you carry an infant or a hot stone.

"He talked about you," the son had said, standing in my doorway two days prior, stamping snow off his boots without actually entering, because that was how people entered my workshop—they didn't. "Said you were the only one who'd know what to do with him."

What to do with him. As if Aldous had been a difficult knot, a stubborn hinge. In a way he was. Old bones hold old weather, and Aldous's held sixty years of the same valley, the same fields, the same woman's name said ten thousand times until the saying wore a groove in him deeper than any tool could cut.

I set my chisel against the femur and began.

Here is what I hear, since you'll want to know, and since no one in Cinder Valley ever asked me plainly enough for me to answer plainly: bone remembers. Not thinks, not dreams—remembers, the way a riverbed remembers water. Every life leaves its sediment. When I carve, the chisel's song changes under my hands, and in that song I can find the shape of what the bone lived through. A hard childhood sounds like flint. A long marriage sounds like two notes struck together and held. Grief sounds like a bell underwater. Joy—I have only found joy three times in twenty years of this work, and each time it sounded so much like ordinary breathing that I nearly missed it.

Aldous sounded like laced boots. Like a man who kept himself ready to go somewhere and never went.

"Where," I murmured to him, working the first groove of the memorial sigil into the shaft, "were you going?"

Bone doesn't answer questions. It only repeats itself. But I heard the groove of it again—the wife's name, the fields, the boots—and beneath it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something I had never heard from valley bone before. A kind of waiting. Bones are patient things; they're used to being underground. But this was different. This was the patience of a man standing at a door he never opened, hand raised, for sixty years.

I carved him a door into the femur. Small, no bigger than my thumbnail, with a lintel and a sill and a handle worn smooth by an imagined hand. It wasn't any sigil I'd been taught. It was just what the bone asked for.

When I finished, I wrapped him in linen dyed with alder bark, and I sat back, and I looked out my frosted window at the valley, and I thought again: *enough.*

Cinder Valley in winter looks like the inside of a closed fist. The mountains come down close on both sides, grey and patient, and the river runs through the middle of everything, flat and dark as poured iron, and the village huddles along its banks with its chimneys smoking like a congregation of small prayers. There are eleven hundred of us, give or take the season. We grow barley on the terraces and apples in the sheltered hollows and we keep sheep that grow wool thick enough to insult the weather. Nothing about us would fill a page in anyone's book of wonders. That is precisely the point. The valley does not appear in books. The roads that lead here are the kind travelers take by mistake, and the ones who arrive always say the same thing—that they can't quite remember deciding to come, only that the pass seemed open, and then they were here, and then they stayed.

I was born here. At least, that is what I told people, which is not the same thing, and which I will come to.

My workshop stands at the edge of the village where the road gives up pretending to go anywhere—a low stone building that used to be a smokehouse, with a yard full of apple trees too old to fruit well but too respected to cut down. There is a sign over the door that Maren painted for me years ago: *WINTERBOURNE — BONES CARVED, ECHOES KEPT.* She'd wanted to add something grander. I made her sand it off.

* * *

Maren Ostell keeps the Drowned Bell, the inn at the center of the village, and she has kept it the way a lighthouse keeps a coast—stubbornly, visibly, and with the deep suspicion that the sea is up to something. She is built like the doorframe of a church, broad and load-bearing, with forearms that could wring confession out of a laundry line. Her hair went white early and she wears it in a braid thick as a ship's rope, and she has never once, in the nineteen years I've known her, let me pay for a meal.

"You'll eat sitting down," she told me that evening, when I came in smelling of bone dust and alder bark, "or you'll eat standing in the doorway like a heron, and the herons get cold stew."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always not hungry. Sit."

The Drowned Bell at dusk in midwinter was the warmest place in the world, if you allowed yourself to believe such a place existed. The fire on the great hearth was built of apple wood, which pops and smells sweet, and above the mantel hung the inn's namesake—a bell fished out of the millpond forty years ago, green with age, that rang once by itself every year on the solstice and no other day. Nobody knew why. Nobody in Cinder Valley much cared why. Things happened here the way stones are round: simply, without explanation, and nobody lost sleep over it.

That is the valley's whole character, if you want it in a sentence. Magic cannot enter Cinder Valley. I did not learn this as a child; there was no one to teach it. I learned it the way everyone learns the shape of the room they live in—by bumping into the walls. Travelers sometimes arrived carrying charms that had gone dull as river pebbles. Hedge-folk passing through found their cards fell flat, their teas tasted like tea. And the dead—well. In the lands beyond the passes, I'm told, the dead linger, haunt, whisper, occasionally demand. Here they simply ended. The ground took them and gave nothing back except quiet, and quiet was the one thing the valley grew in abundance.

Except for me. Except that bones spoke to me, faintly, echo-ward, and no one—including me—ever thought to wonder whether that counted as magic. Echoes aren't magic. An echo is just the world remembering out loud. I had made my peace with the distinction the way you make peace with a limp: thoroughly, and by never running.

"Solstice tomorrow," Maren said, setting down a bowl of stew and a heel of bread with the authority of a magistrate. "Bell rings tomorrow night. You'll come."

"I always come."

"You didn't come the year your hands were bad." She fixed me with the look she used on drunkards and debtors. "You stood outside in the cold listening through the window like a cat, and don't think I didn't see you."

"My hands were bad."

"Your hands were fine and your heart was being stupid." She sat across from me, uninvited, which was also how she did most things. For a moment she just watched me eat, and her face did the thing it did when she thought I couldn't see it—the softening, the worry gathering at the corners of her eyes like frost at the corners of glass.

"Maren."

"Hm."

"I carved a door into Aldous Merrin today."

She blinked. "A door."

"A little one. In the bone. He—" I turned my spoon over. How to explain what even I couldn't fully explain? "He sounded like a man waiting at a threshold. Sixty years of it. So I gave him one."

Maren was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and squeezed my wrist, brief and hard, the way she did everything—briefly, hard, meaning it completely. "He'd have liked that. He was a man who liked his exits marked." She stood, gathered someone else's empty bowls, paused. "You do good work, Noor Winterbourne. Whatever else is true of you, that's true."

*Whatever else is true of you.* She said it lightly. She had said versions of it for nineteen years, ever since the ferryman brought a silent, half-frozen child of six up from the river landing in the bottom of his boat and handed her to the innkeeper with the words, "Found her at the threshold. You'll keep her." And Maren had. They both had. Neither of them had ever asked the question hanging under those words, the question I heard every time, the question I had spent my whole life making sure no one needed to ask aloud:

*Whatever else is true of you.*

* * *

Doss was at the river landing when I walked home the long way, along the bank, past the millpond frozen to a sheet of black glass. The old ferryman sat on his mooring post in the blue hour with a pipe going and his hands folded over the head of his cane, watching the ice floes come down from the high country like slow white ships.

He was the oldest thing in the valley that wasn't a mountain. Eighty-some years, no one counted anymore, and his face had gone the texture of driftwood, and his eyes were pale as shallow water and missed nothing. He had ferried the river for sixty years, back when the crossing mattered, back before the roads forgot us. Now the ferry mostly carried sheep and coffins and, once, me.

"Noor." He didn't turn. He never had to turn; he heard my step on the shingle the way I heard bone sing. "Cold for walking."

"Cold for sitting."

"Sitting's warmer than it looks." He patted the post beside him. I sat. The river muttered under its ice, a sound like someone talking in another room.

We watched the floes a while. With Doss, silence was not a gap in the conversation; it was the conversation, and everything spoken was just the banks of it.

"Brought a femur down from the Merrin farm today," he said at last.

"I know. I carved him."

"What'd he sound like?"

This was the thing about Doss. Everyone else in the valley treated my hearing the way they treated the bell above Maren's mantel—with a shrug, a fondness, a refusal to look directly at it. Doss alone asked what the dead sounded like, and waited for the answer, and never once crossed himself or changed the subject after.

"Like a man who kept his boots laced for sixty years," I said. "Ready to go somewhere. Never going."

Doss nodded slowly. Smoke curled off his pipe and unraveled in the wind off the water. "That's the valley," he said. "Whole valley's got its boots laced."

I looked at him. His face was turned toward the high passes, where the last light was dying on the snow, and there was something in it I had seen maybe five times in nineteen years—something old and guarded and awake, like a man hearing footsteps on a road he'd thought was abandoned.

"Doss. What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing, girl." He knocked out his pipe against the mooring post, and the old ease came back over his face like water closing over a dropped stone. "Old men talk nonsense. It's how we stay young." He levered himself up with the cane, joints cracking like green firewood, and paused. "You'll be at the solstice tonight? Bell rings."

"I'll be there. Inside, even."

"Inside." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and yet underneath it something stayed watchful, the way the river stays moving under the ice. "Good. Stay inside. Cold's coming down off the passes tomorrow, they say. Cold like we haven't had in years."

He walked me as far as the lane that climbed to my workshop, which he always did, which I had long ago stopped protesting, and stood at the bottom of it watching until I reached my door. From the doorstep I looked back. He was still there, a dark shape against the last grey light, and he was not looking at me. He was looking upriver. Toward the high country. Toward the passes.

Toward whatever was coming down them.

* * *

That night I lit my lamp and unwrapped the day's remaining work: a set of finger bones on blue cloth, the fever-child, waiting eleven months now for me to find the right sigil. Some bones resist carving. Hers resisted gently, the way a sleeping child resists waking, and I had learned to wait for her, working around her, doing other commissions, letting her lie in her blue cloth by the window where the frost made its feathers.

I picked up her smallest finger bone and held it to the lamplight, and for the thousandth time I listened.

And the bone sang.

Not echoed—sang. Clear and high and utterly unlike anything I had heard in twenty years of listening to the dead, a melody in a mode I had no name for, rising and falling like speech, like a lullaby, like a language—

I set it down. My hands were shaking. Outside, the wind had come around to the north, and the frost on my window was growing new feathers even as I watched, spreading inward from the edges of the glass, and far off in the direction of the passes I heard—or thought I heard, or told myself I only thought I heard—a sound beneath the wind.

Small. Bare feet on snow.

Knocking.
