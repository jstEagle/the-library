## 2. A Knock in the Frost

The knock came at the hour when even the dead were supposed to be asleep.

Noor heard it first as a wrongness in her hands. She was at the workbench, finishing a shinbone for old Tam Wicker—*rest him well, he earned it, forty years hauling slate and not one cruel word anyone could prove*—and the burin had just begun its long curl through the marrow channel when every carved bone on her shelves began to ring.

Not loudly. Bones don't do anything loudly; that's what she'd always loved about them. But they rang, all of them at once, a faint glassy hum like wet fingers circling the rims of bowls, and Noor sat back with the burin still poised over Tam's tibia and felt the hair rise on her forearms.

Twenty-five years in Cinder Valley, and she had never once heard magic. That was the valley's whole covenant: no magic crossed its borders, none seeped up from its soil, none clung to its rivers or hid in its stones. It was why people came here—the frightened ones, the hunted ones, the ones who wanted to disappear so thoroughly that even their own pasts couldn't find them. The valley was a held breath. A room with the door shut against everything uncanny.

And yet her bones were singing.

She set down the burin. Her workshop smelled of bone dust and beeswax and the pine smoke leaking under the door from Maren's inn two lanes over, and those smells were ordinary, blessedly ordinary, and she gripped them like handrails while she listened.

The hum faded. Then came the knock again—actual this time, flesh on wood, three small taps at the height of a person who wasn't very tall.

Noor looked at the window. Frost had grown ferns across the panes, thick as fur, and beyond the frost the night was the deep blue-black of the solstice, the longest dark of the year, when the valley folk shuttered their windows early and left offerings of bread and salt on their sills for reasons nobody could remember anymore. Nobody traveled on the solstice. Nobody walked to a bone-carver's house at the edge of the village, past the last lamplight, where the road gave up and became a suggestion.

Three more taps. Small. Patient.

"I'm closed," Noor said, because her voice worked better than her legs did. "Come back after the thaw."

Silence. Then, muffled but perfectly clear, a child's voice said something in no language she knew—and yet she understood it, the way you understand a door is locked without reading the sign. *Let me in. It's cold out here.*

Which was strange, because when she opened the door a moment later, the child standing on her step didn't look cold at all.

* * *

She was six years old, maybe seven—small enough that Noor's eye went instinctively to her feet first, checking for frostbite, and found bare toes resting on snow-crusted stone as if the stone were warm flagstone in high summer. Barefoot. In the deepest cold of the year. Not blue, not shivering, not even pink with chill. Just feet. A child's feet, slightly dusty, as though she'd walked a long way through somewhere dry.

She wore a smock of undyed wool, homespun, the weave tight and fine in a pattern Noor didn't recognize—not valley work, not lowland work either. Her hair was dark, cut blunt at the jaw with what must have been a knife. Her face was round and calm and utterly unafraid, which was the detail that finally made Noor's stomach drop, because children who knock on strangers' doors at midnight are afraid of something, always, even if only the dark or the scolding waiting behind the door. This child looked at Noor the way Noor looked at finished work: with quiet assessment, and then acceptance.

"You can't be here," Noor said. "Where are your parents? Where did you come from?"

The child tilted her head. Then she hummed.

It was four notes. Five. A descending phrase that folded back on itself like water finding its way downhill, simple and old-feeling, the kind of tune a mother makes up without knowing she's making it up. And Noor's bones—

Her bones *answered*.

Not the bones on the shelves. Her own. The deep ones, the femurs and the ribs and the long silent columns of her spine, and they answered the way tuning forks answer, shivering into resonance until the vibration climbed her vertebrae and buzzed behind her teeth and filled her skull with a sound like a struck bell heard from inside a bell. She put a hand on the doorframe to keep herself upright. The frost ferns on the window behind the child seemed to lean toward the sound.

"Stop," Noor whispered. "What are you—stop doing that."

The child stopped. She looked at Noor with an expression that was not quite surprise—more like confirmation, the look of someone who has thrown a stone into a well at last heard it strike bottom. Then she spoke again, that impossible language, liquid and consonant-rich, words that seemed shaped around meanings rather than attached to them, and Noor understood every word and could not have repeated one of them aloud to save her life.

*You hear me. Good. I was told there would be someone who hears.*

"Who told you?" Noor's voice cracked on the second word. "Who told you that? Where did you come from? What's your name?"

The child considered each question in turn, the way children do, giving each its own weight. Snow began to fall around them both—fine, dry flakes that settled on the child's shoulders and hair and did not melt, and Noor thought distantly, *that's wrong too, that should be melting,* and then thought nothing at all because the child reached out and took her hand.

Small fingers. Warm as fresh bread. And through the touch, faint as the memory of a smell, Noor felt the hum again—not in her bones this time but in the child's, a resonance running through that small body like a river under ice, vast and patient and impossibly deep, as if the child weren't humming the melody at all but merely standing where the melody passed through.

"You're freezing," Noor heard herself say, in the tone she used for grieving families and frightened apprentices, the tone that meant *I will handle this.* "Inside. Now. We'll sort the rest out after."

The child let herself be led in. At the threshold she paused—one heartbeat, no more—and glanced back at the empty road, the falling snow, the dark beyond the dark, with an alertness that made Noor think of rabbits in open meadow. Then she stepped over the sill, and Noor shut the door on the longest night of the year, and slid the bolt, and stood with her forehead against the wood for a long moment, listening to the valley hold its breath.

* * *

She fed the child bread and honey and watched her eat with the tidy thoroughness of someone who had learned hunger as a fact rather than a fear. The child sat cross-legged on the bench by the fire, bare feet tucked beneath her—Noor had offered wool socks and been refused with a small, polite shake of the head, as though socks were a custom from a foreign country, which, Noor supposed, they were—and she studied the workshop with wide, unblinking attention.

She looked at the memorial bones the way other children looked at sweets.

There were forty of them on the shelves, give or take: the year's commissions, each one a life compressed into carved whorls and knotwork, names and deeds and griefs cut into calcium by Noor's steady hand. The valley burned its dead in summer and buried their bones in winter, but between those acts the bones came to her, and she listened to them—that was the gift, the only gift she'd ever claimed—and carved what they remembered. A farmer's hands remembered rope and rain. A midwife's bones held forty years of first cries. Old Tam's tibia, half-finished on the bench, remembered slate dust and a wedding song sung off-key.

The child moved along the shelves and touched nothing, but her lips moved, silently, as if reading. And wherever her gaze rested, the bone would hum—softly, briefly, a plucked string settling—before going quiet again.

"That one's Tam," Noor said, mostly to break the silence, because silence had become a thing with teeth. "He hauled slate for the quarry his whole life. Never missed a shift. His daughter wants a lark carved at the shoulder, because he used to whistle at them."

The child turned. Said something in the old language, gentle, interrogative, and pointed at the tibia, then at Noor, then pressed her own small palm flat against her chest.

"No," Noor said slowly. "I don't—I carve them. They talk. Or—" She rubbed her eyes. "They echo. Everything a body lived stays in the bones a little while, if the body was loved enough. I don't know how else to explain it. It's not magic. Nothing here is magic."

The child frowned at that—at the word *magic*, specifically—with the particular frown of someone being lied to politely. She said something else, longer, and pointed out the window toward the north, toward the fells, toward whatever lay beyond the fells, and her voice took on a cadence that Noor recognized with a jolt: it was the cadence of homesickness. Of naming a place you cannot get back to.

"I don't understand," Noor said, which was a lie, which was becoming a habit within the space of a single hour, because she *did* understand, word for word, and the understanding sat in her chest like swallowed river-stone. *I come from beyond. Take me home. You're the one who hears.*

She understood because some buried part of her had always understood that language, the way a scar understands rain.

That thought arrived and Noor flinched away from it physically, stepping back from the firelight as though it had reached for her. She was Noor Winterbourne, bone-carver of Cinder Valley. She had been born here. Everyone said so. Doss the ferryman had pulled her from the shallows of the Greywash when she was an infant, or so the story went—Doss, who never told the story himself, who went quiet as a closed book whenever anyone asked about the year of the flood. Maren had raised her at the inn alongside her own boys. The valley had taken her in before she could walk, and the valley did not lie, and the valley especially did not forget.

Except that the valley forgot things constantly. That was its nature. People arrived with missing years and unaskable questions, and Cinder Valley asked nothing, remembered nothing, kept nothing but the bones of its dead. It was a place built on deliberate amnesia, and Noor had spent twenty-five years mistaking that amnesia for safety.

The child watched her across the fire with ancient patience.

"What's your name?" Noor asked again, kneeling so they were level, and this time she touched her own sternum, and then the child's, the way you ask a name without words.

The child covered Noor's hand with her own. She breathed in, and the fire leaned sideways in the grate, flames bending toward her like grass toward wind, and she said a word.

It sounded like a key turning. Like ice releasing a branch. Like the first syllable of a song Noor's mother—if she'd had a mother, if mothers were things she'd had—might once have hummed above a cradle. The bones on the shelves all hummed with it at once, forty lives echoing in harmony, and Noor felt tears on her cheeks and didn't know when they'd started.

*Ivet,* the word meant. *Ivet.* And underneath the name, carried in it like a stone in a streambed, another meaning entirely:

*The one who was sent.*

Outside, far off across the frozen fields, something howled. It might have been wind. It might have been a dog. It was neither, and all the bones in the workshop went silent at once, every shelf, every whorl, old Tam's unfinished tibia included, as though the dead themselves had stopped to listen.

Ivet's calm face changed for the first time. She looked at the door. Then at Noor. And she said, in the language older than any kingdom, in the voice of a six-year-old girl standing barefoot at the end of the world:

*It found the trail. We have until moonrise tomorrow.*

Noor understood every word.

And for the first time in twenty-five safe, small, carefully hidden years, Noor Winterbourne was afraid.
