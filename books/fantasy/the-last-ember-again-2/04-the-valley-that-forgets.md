## 4. The Valley That Forgets

Maren Osgood had kept the Hearthstone Inn for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one law of Cinder Valley more reliable than any scripture: trouble always arrived on foot, and it always knocked twice.

The child had not knocked at all.

She thought about that now, standing at the taproom window with a rag gone dry in her hand, watching Noor Winterbourne's workshop across the frozen lane. Smoke curled from the chimney the way it always did, thin and gray and ordinary. Inside that ordinary house, behind an ordinary door, an extraordinary thing was eating soup with a bone spoon—because Noor had told her so last night, and because Maren had seen the child's bare feet on her own threshold and they had been warm as bread.

Warm feet. In this cold. Nineteen years of innkeeping, and she had never once poured a drink for anything that walked barefoot through frost without leaving prints of suffering behind it.

"Maren." Tam Brase's voice from the kitchen doorway, low and urgent in the way he used when the council was already assembling itself in his mind. "They're coming. Half past nine, like I said."

"I heard you the first time."

"You didn't look like you'd heard me."

She turned from the window and looked at him properly—her husband of eleven years, broad as a barn door, wringing his apron the way other men wrung their hands. He was frightened. She could count on one hand the times she'd seen him truly frightened, and every one of them involved water over the ferry banks or fire near the hay. This was neither, and somehow worse.

"Set out the good cups," she said. "If they've come to ruin my morning, they can do it drinking from the glaze that doesn't chip."

"They've come to ruin more than your morning." Tam lowered his voice further, though the taproom was empty except for old Pell snoring in the corner chair where he'd slept off his ale since before Maren bought the place. "Alder Vetch wants the child gone by nightfall. He's got signatures."

"Signatures." She said the word like something she'd scraped off her boot. "It's a child, Tam. Seven years old, maybe. Eats like a sparrow. Sleeps like the dead."

"That's what frightens them."

*That's what frightens them.* Yes. She'd understood that part already. It was the same fear that emptied a taproom when a stranger coughed too long, the same fear that made farmers burn their fences after a bad winter and rebuild them exactly the same. Fear was the valley's second harvest, and it came in every season.

"Then let them be frightened in my taproom," Maren said, "where I can watch their faces while they say what they've come to say."

* * *

The council arrived at half past nine precisely, which told Maren everything about how long they'd spent rehearsing. Alder Vetch led them, as he led everything—the miller's son grown into the miller's power, a man built like his own grindstones, all wheel and weight and turning. Behind him came Widow Sorrel with her ledger face, and the Hedley brothers, who between them possessed one opinion, and young Corvin Ashe, whose family had held the eastern farms for six generations and who still blushed when a woman spoke to him plainly.

Maren set out seven cups of cider. Six of them drank. Vetch did not touch his, which was also rehearsed.

"We'll come to the point," Vetch said.

"Please don't," Maren said. "I enjoy the scenery first. Tell me about the weather. Tell me about the price of barley. Lie to me pleasantly for five minutes, Alder, the way people used to."

Someone shifted. Widow Sorrel's pen scratched against nothing.

"The child," Vetch said.

"What about her?"

"You know what about her. Barefoot in the solstice cold. Speaking words that made old Marta Kell drop her basket in the square—she swears the words moved the air, Maren. Moved it, like wind through wheat, and there wasn't any wind."

"Marta Kell swears she saw a ghost in her pantry every autumn until we stopped selling her the strong cider."

"This isn't Marta being old." Vetch leaned forward, and Maren saw that under the miller's authority he was genuinely afraid, and that the fear had been growing in him for days like mold in a dark corner. "You've heard it yourself. You've stood in that workshop and listened to whatever language that is. Don't tell me you haven't."

Maren kept her hands still around her cup. She had heard it. Three nights ago, delivering a crock of stew, she had stood in Noor's doorway and listened to the child sing—or speak, or pray, it was impossible to say which—and the sound had gone through her like cold water through cloth. Not the words. She hadn't understood a single word. But the *shape* of the language had done something to the room, to the candle flame, to the marrow behind her own ears. She had left the stew and walked home fast and told herself it was the dark.

"Noor says the girl is lost and hungry," Maren said. "Noor says she'll keep her until someone comes asking. That's the whole of my report, Alder, and it's worth more than yours, because at least mine is firsthand."

"Then here's mine." Vetch laid a paper on the table, and Maren saw names on it—a dozen, two dozen, inked in the careful hands of people she'd fed and housed and mourned alongside for two decades. "By council voice, the foundling is to be taken to the valley gate and turned out, with provisions, come first light tomorrow. Kindly done. Properly done. But done."

"And if Noor refuses?"

Vetch's jaw worked. "Noor Winterbourne has no standing in this valley. No land. No kin. She carves the dead's bones because no one else will touch the work, and we've all been glad enough to have her do it—but gladness isn't standing, Maren. You know that better than anyone."

There it was. The knife inside the argument, slipped in soft. Maren set down her cup with great care, because if she didn't, she was going to throw it.

"Say what you actually mean," she said. "You've danced around it for three days. Say it plain, and then get out of my inn."

Vetch looked at his fellows. Widow Sorrel closed her eyes as though praying, or counting. And then, quietly, in the voice of a man reciting something he'd been taught rather than something he believed, Alder Vetch said:

"There are places that were struck from the world, Maren. Struck clean. And things that did the striking don't forget what they've erased—not ever. A child speaking the old tongue is a lit torch in a dry field. Whatever unmade *that country* will follow the sound of it. It followed worse than that, once. Ask anyone older than seventy why there's no village past the Grey Ford anymore, why the maps in the temple have four pages sewn shut. Ask them what happened the winter the bells rang backward." He spread his hands. "We are small, Maren. We are magicless, which has kept us alive when every grand thing around us burned. We survive by forgetting, and by being forgettable. That child makes us memorable. I won't bury this valley to spare one orphan a cold night."

Silence. Even Pell's snoring had stopped; the old man's eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, listening the way old men listen when the conversation finally reaches the year they were born into.

"And Noor?" Maren asked.

"What about her?"

"She's the one keeping the child. Is she turned out too? Does your signature page send her to the gate as well?"

Vetch had the grace to look uncomfortable. Corvin Ashe, of all people, spoke up—red-faced, stammering, but speaking. "Nobody's saying turn out Noor. She's—" He faltered. "She's ours. She carved my father's fingerbones when the scythe took them, and she wouldn't take payment. She's ours."

"But the child goes," Maren said, "even if it breaks her heart. Even if she stands at the gate and refuses. Then what does the council do? Drag a bone-carver through the snow?" She rose, and her shadow fell long across the table, across the paper of names, across the untouched cup. "I'll tell you what happens, because I've watched this valley for forty years, longer than some of you have been alive. You'll talk yourselves righteous for a week. Then you'll wait for someone else to do the ugly thing, and when nobody does, you'll decide the danger was exaggerated, and in twenty years you'll swear you never wanted the child gone at all. That's how Cinder Valley survives. Not by courage. By waiting each other out."

"Maren." Widow Sorrel's voice was thin and tired. "My grandmother came from beyond the Grey Ford. From the village that isn't there anymore. She never spoke of it, but she woke screaming every winter solstice till the day she died, and in her sleep she said words in that same tongue—that same *shape*. Whatever took that place, our grandmothers remember it even if the maps don't. I won't hand it a reason to come down the valley road." Her eyes, wet and steady, met Maren's. "I love that girl like everyone does. But I loved my grandmother too, and I'm telling you: the fear is inherited for a reason."

For a moment—one long, cold moment—Maren felt the room tilt toward agreement, felt the weight of all those sleeping nightmares pressing down like snow load on a roof. She thought of Noor at eight years old, delivered to Doss's ferry with frostbitten fingers and no name anyone knew, and of Doss carrying her up the lane himself because Maren had been the only one with a spare bed and a spine. She thought of twenty-five years of stew sent across the lane, of boots bought two sizes large to grow into, of sitting with Noor through the fever that nearly took her at fourteen, fanning her and lying that everything would be fine.

Found family, her mother had called such things, with contempt. *People who choose you because nobody else would have you.* As if choosing were the lesser kind of love.

"Noor Winterbourne," Maren said slowly, "was brought to this valley as a foundling herself. Did none of you ever wonder where from? Did none of you ever look at that woman—at her coloring, at the shape of her cheekbones, at the way she hums when she works, tunes no lullaby in this valley matches—and wonder?" She let it land. Saw it land. Saw Vetch go still and Sorrel's pen stop scratching. "I wondered for twenty-five years. Last night, listening to that child sing, I stopped wondering and started knowing. So hear me, Alder, and carry it back to your signature page: if the council turns that child out at the gate, Noor Winterbourne will follow her into the snow. And I will follow Noor. And Tam—" she glanced at her husband, who had appeared in the doorway at some point and stood there with his arms crossed and his chin set in the manner of a man prepared to physically remove council members from premises, "—will follow me. Turn out one foundling, and this valley loses three of its best, plus an inn, plus a ferryman, plus whoever else has a spine. Count that cost on your ledger, Widow. Write it down."

The silence afterward had texture, like wool.

It was Vetch who broke it, and to his credit he broke it honestly. "You'd throw away your life's work. For her. For a woman who isn't even kin."

"She's the closest thing to kin I have," Maren said, "which means she's kin. That's how it works, Alder. That's how it's always worked, everywhere, in every country—including ones that aren't on maps anymore."

* * *

They filed out by midday, unresolved, which Maren counted as a victory. Unresolved meant another day. Another day meant time, and time was the only currency the poor ever had in plenty.

Tam washed the cups in silence, which was how he apologized for things and worried about things simultaneously. Maren dried. Through the window, she watched Noor cross the lane with the child wrapped in a coat clearly cut down from an adult's, the sleeves rolled five times, the girl's bare feet—boots now, thank the saints, Noor had gotten boots—leaving small neat prints in the snow.

"She knows," Tam said. "About the vote. Someone talked. They always talk."

"I know she knows. She came by at dawn, before the council even gathered, and asked me not to fight for her." Maren laughed, short and disbelieving. "Not for herself—for the *child*. Said if turning the girl out keeps the valley safe, she'd walk her to the gate herself. Twenty-five years building a life out of smallness, and the moment something matters, she offers it all up like it's nothing."

"That sounds like her."

"It sounds exactly like her, and it makes me want to shake her." Maren folded the rag, unfolded it, folded it again. "Tam. The pages in the temple. The sewn-shut ones. When I was a girl, Doss got drunk once—truly drunk, the only time I ever saw it—and started talking about a crossing. About a door. He said he'd carried things across that weren't supposed to cross. He said—" she hesitated, because saying it aloud gave it weight, and weight was dangerous, "—he said he'd carried a baby across. Twenty-five years ago. From the far side."

Tam set down the cup he was washing. Water dripped from his big hands onto the floor, and he didn't notice.

"You think—" he started.

"Noor's coloring. The humming. The fact that she understands that child's language when no one born this side of the Grey Ford should be able to parse a word of it." Maren turned from the window. "Fetch Doss. Tell him I've got a bottle of the plum brandy I've been saving for a funeral, and I'd rather open it tonight than let him make me open it at one."

Tam was already reaching for his coat. At the door he paused. "And if he tells us something we don't want to know?"

Maren looked out one last time at the small figures crossing the snow—the tall carver, the small stranger, the smoke rising straight from the chimney into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Somewhere south, past the Grey Ford, past four sewn-shut pages, lay the answer to every question Noor had never been allowed to ask.

"Then we'll know it together," Maren said. "That's what family is for."
