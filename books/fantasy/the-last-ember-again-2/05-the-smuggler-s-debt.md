## 5. The Smuggler's Debt

The river told Doss things the village never asked him to say aloud.

He had ferried the dead and the living across the Greywater for fifty-one years, and in all that time he had learned that water keeps accounts better than any ledger. It remembers weight. It remembers what was carried, and who wept, and what was thrown overboard so it would never be found. On nights like this one—windless, the ice forming at the edges in long white teeth—the river sat still as a held breath, and every debt it had ever recorded rose up through the hull of his flat-bottomed boat and settled on his chest like wet wool.

Doss sat in the boat anyway. He always sat in the boat when he couldn't sleep, which lately was every night since the child came.

* * *

He had watched her from the far bank two days ago, when Noor carried the little girl through the frost to Maren's inn. He'd seen the way the child walked—barefoot on ice-rimed ground, unhurried, as though cold were a rumor she hadn't bothered to verify. And he had seen Noor's face above her, that careful carved face she showed the world, cracked clean down the middle by something she didn't have a name for yet.

Doss had a name for it. He'd had the name for twenty-five years.

*Vessian*, he'd thought, and his hands had gone numb on the oars though he wasn't rowing anything but memory.

Now he climbed the path to Noor's workshop with a bottle of plum brandy under his arm—not because either of them drank much, but because an old man arriving empty-handed at midnight looked like what he was: a man coming to confess. The brandy gave them both somewhere to put their eyes.

She opened the door before he knocked. She'd heard his knees on the stairs, no doubt; the girl could hear a moth change its mind at forty paces. Behind her, in the warm lamplight of the workshop, the child sat cross-legged on the floor among bone shavings, humming.

Doss's heart stopped, started again, and stopped properly this time.

That song. That *damnable* song. He had heard it once before, through a dying woman's bloodless lips, on the far side of the worst night of his life—and he had spent twenty-five years pretending the river had washed it out of him. Rivers wash out everything except the things you need gone.

"Noor," he said. "I need to tell you about the night you were born."

* * *

She didn't ask how he knew she'd want to hear it tonight. That was Noor. She poured two cups of the brandy, set a third cup of warmed milk beside the child—Ivet, the girl had begun answering to it, or perhaps had always answered to it and was only now permitting the valley to catch up—and then sat across from him with her carving knife still in her belt, which Doss chose to find reassuring rather than alarming.

"I was younger than you are now," he began. "Thirty-one. I'd been running the ferry six years, and I'd already learned that there's folk who pay in coin and folk who pay in other ways, and you learn to take whichever they've got."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind found the eaves and worried at them like a dog with an old bone.

"It was midwinter. Solstice night, same as the night your little one turned up on your doorstep—there's a pattern there, if you're the sort who looks for patterns, and I'd advise against it, looking for patterns is how you end up knowing things." He took a swallow of brandy that burned all the way down and pooled in his stomach like a coal. "A woman came to the north bank. Not walking—*arriving*. One moment the bank was empty, the next she was there, kneeling in the snow with a bundle in her arms, and the snow around her wasn't disturbed. Like she'd been set down by something that didn't believe in footprints."

Noor's hands had gone very still in her lap. Doss made himself keep talking.

"She was dying. I don't mean hurt-dying, I mean *spent*-dying, the way a candle is dying when there's more smoke than flame. She'd given something enormous to get where she was, and it was still draining out of her, and she knew it, and she wasn't afraid. That's what I remember most. I've seen men die on this river—drownings, the fever year, old Tam with his heart—and every one of them fought it or bargained with it or went mad with it. This woman just looked at me like she was sorry to be taking up my time."

"What did she look like?" Noor asked. Her voice came out level. Too level. The voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside.

"Dark hair, grey eyes. Your chin." He said it gently. "Your exact chin, girl. I've been looking at it across this river for twenty-five years and telling myself I was a sentimental old man."

The child stopped humming.

It was such a small thing, but the silence that followed it had weight, the way silence does in a room where something important is being decided. Ivet looked from Doss to Noor and back again, and then—deliberately, gravely, the way a judge accepts evidence—she picked up a curl of bone shaving from the floor and placed it in Noor's palm, and closed Noor's fingers over it, and patted them twice.

Go on, said the gesture. Keep listening.

"The ferry was frozen in," Doss said. "Solstice night, the Greywater locks itself, everyone knows that. But she walked to my boat and the ice parted under her feet like it had been asked politely. And she said—" His voice caught. Twenty-five years of not saying it, and now it wouldn't come out smooth. "She said, *Waterman. I need the quiet country. My daughter must be born where nothing can find her.*"

"Nothing," Noor repeated.

"That word. Not *no one*. Nothing." He met her eyes. "And I laughed at her, girl, I'm ashamed to say it, I laughed and told her Cinder Valley doesn't take newcomers, hasn't in living memory, the council seals the passes and the boundary does the rest. And she reached into her coat and brought out this."

He fumbled at his neck and drew out a cord he'd worn hidden under his shirt for a quarter century. On it hung a small disc of pale material that might have been bone and might have been shell and might have been neither—a button, a coin, a token, worn smooth as a river stone.

"She pressed it into my hand and the whole world went quiet. Not silent—quiet. The difference matters. Silent is when nothing's making sound. Quiet is when everything that was making sound has agreed to stop. And into that quiet she said: *I cannot pay you in coin. I will pay you in what I carry. Take my remembering of her, and take her across, and forget that you ever forgot.*"

Noor's breath left her slowly. "Her memory. Of you. Of—" She stopped. Started again. "Of her own daughter."

"Of everything," Doss said. "Not just the baby. Everything. She paid with the whole of it—her mother, her country, her own name, I'd wager. That's why she was spent. You can't hand off a life's worth of remembering and stay standing unless you're already mostly gone." He turned the token over in his palm. It was warm. It had always been warm; he'd told himself for years it was his own body heat, but it was warm even on mornings he'd pulled it from beneath a frozen floorboard. "I took it. Gods forgive me, I took it, because the alternative was watching her die on the ice with a newborn screaming, and I am not a brave man, Noor. I'm a ferryman. I move things from one bank to the other and I don't ask what happens after."

"And then?"

"And then I rowed. Ice parting ahead of us, sealing behind. She held you the whole crossing and sang to you—that song, the one your little listener there won't stop humming—and by the time we touched the south bank she'd stopped singing. Stopped everything. I buried her above the waterline where the valley's dead go, and I carved no marker, because she asked me not to. *Let me be the last thing Vessia loses quietly,* she said. Those were nearly her last words." He looked down at the brandy he couldn't taste anymore. "Nearly. The actual last words were yours, funnily enough. You'd stopped crying the moment we crossed the boundary. First quiet you'd ever known. And she smiled and said, *There. She can't hear it anymore.*"

"Hear what?" Noor whispered.

Doss spread his hands. "Everything, girl. Whatever it is your bones have been echoing your whole life, whatever the dead say to you that nobody else can hear—out there, past the boundary, it isn't a whisper. It's a roar. She crossed half a dying country carrying that roar inside her skull while something hunted her, and she brought you here because this valley is deaf. Magicless. Silent." He shook his head slowly. "She didn't hide you in the quiet country because it was safe. She hid you here because it was *quiet*."

* * *

The fire had burned low. At some point Ivet had fallen asleep against Noor's side, one fist curled around a bone shaving, and Noor hadn't moved, hadn't dared move, as though the child were made of frost and breath.

Doss stood at the window, looking out at the dark shape of the northern hills, where the boundary ran—an invisible line the valley's founders had drawn so long ago that no one remembered drawing it, the line he had crossed exactly once, carrying the most dangerous cargo of his life.

"There's something else," he said. "You're going to want to throw me out for this part, so let me get it all out first."

Noor said nothing. Waiting. She was good at waiting; the dead had taught her that.

"The woman—the crossing—it wasn't free just because she paid. Boundaries like ours aren't doors, they're *wounds*. Scars in the world. And scars remember being opened." He turned from the window. "I know where the crossing happened. Where the boundary is thinnest. There's a hollow in the north hills, under a split stone older than the valley, and the old-timers called it the Door and wouldn't graze their sheep within a mile of it. I've kept its secret twenty-five years because keeping secrets is the only penance I know how to do."

"You're offering to take us there." Not a question.

"I'm telling you that you'll go whether I offer or not, because you've got her chin and her stubbornness and that child is singing her mother's song, and some cargoes a ferryman never finishes delivering." His laugh came out cracked. "But hear the price before you decide. That night, when I crossed back alone, the river spoke to me. First and only time. It said: *a soul was carried over unowed, and the threshold holds the debt.* Twenty-five years I've owed that crossing. And I've felt it, Noor—every solstice, every anniversary, something on the north bank, patient as winter, counting."

"Counting what?"

"Days. Or souls. I genuinely don't know which, and I've been too much of a coward to find out." He set the token on the table between them, where it seemed to drink the lamplight. "Here's the warning, and it's the only thing I'll ask you to believe without proof: that woman didn't flee Vessia because it fell. Countries don't fall quiet, girl—they're *made* quiet. Something erased that land from every map and every memory, folded it away like a letter no one should read, and it left hunters behind to make sure no page of it ever got unfolded. If that child is what I think she is—if she's a key, or a door, or a name the erasers missed—then opening the way home for her means calling the thing that did the erasing. To this valley. To Maren's inn. To every grave I've dug and every soul sleeping in them."

The wind rose. In the hills, faint as a memory of a memory, something that might have been wind and might not have been, sighed along the boundary line like a creditor walking the length of his ledger.

Noor looked down at the sleeping child. Then at the token. Then at Doss, and her eyes—he'd seen those eyes handle the grief of a hundred bereaved families—were wet and furious and certain, all three at once.

"Then we'd best be quick," she said, "before it finds out we're coming."

Doss closed his hand around the warm token, and outside, far up in the frozen north hills, something that had been counting for twenty-five years stopped counting—and began, softly, to knock.
