## 6. Naming the Child

The child had been with Noor for nine days, and in nine days she had learned exactly three things: the girl ate sparingly and preferred her bread burnt at the edges; she slept curled against the wall rather than against a pillow, as though beds were an indulgence she hadn't earned; and when she thought no one was watching, she hummed.

It was the humming that undid Noor every time.

She sat now at her workbench in the back of Maren's inn, lamplight pooling over a half-finished femur—old Aldric's, who had died at eighty-one with his boots on and everyone agreed it was how he'd have wanted it—and across the room, wrapped in a blanket that swallowed her like a cocoon, the child hummed. The same melody. Always the same melody, six notes rising, two falling, then a long held tone that seemed to go on longer than breath should allow.

And in the drawer beside Noor's elbow, in the box where she kept the relics too old to carve, something answered.

Noor had noticed it on the third day. A faint vibration, like a plucked string under cloth. She'd told herself it was the floorboards settling, or her own pulse, or wishful thinking—the mind did strange things when a barefoot child appeared out of a solstice frost speaking words that shouldn't exist. But tonight, with the inn quiet above and snow sealing the windows, she could no longer pretend. She set down her knife, crossed the room, and opened the box.

The relic inside was a knuckle bone, brown with age, worn smooth as a river stone. It had come to her years ago from Doss, dredged up from the shallows near the old ford, and she had never been able to carve it—it resisted her tools the way iron resists wood, and so she'd kept it whole. Now it trembled against the box's lining, ticking softly, keeping time with the child's song.

Noor carried it to the hearth and turned it over in her hands. *Listen,* it seemed to say. Not in words. In the pressure behind her sternum, the way bone always spoke to her—not language but echo, the residue of a life pressed into mineral. Except this was not residue. This bone wasn't remembering anything.

It was answering.

The child had stopped humming. Noor looked up. The girl was watching her with those grey eyes, unblinking, patient in the way of someone waiting for another person to catch up.

"You know what this is," Noor said quietly. "Don't you."

The child tilted her head. Then she slid off the bench, padded across the floorboards—still barefoot; Noor had given up trying to get shoes on her—and reached out one small finger to touch the knuckle bone.

She said a word.

It was the same word she'd said the first night, standing in Noor's doorway with frost blooming and dying on her skin: that liquid syllable Noor had assumed was a name for herself, or a plea, or nothing at all. But hearing it again, with the bone singing faintly under her palm, Noor felt the meaning arrive the way meaning arrived when she worked a fresh bone—not through her ears but through her marrow. A cold bright sense of *smallness*. Of *one small thing kept safe*.

"Vessel," Noor whispered. "Or... key." Her mouth shaped the old tongue clumsily, but the understanding came clean. "You're saying what you are."

The child watched her lips move and something flickered across her face—hunger, almost. She spoke again, faster now, a stream of those impossible syllables tumbling out, and Noor stood frozen in the middle of her own workshop while meanings broke over her like waves.

*Sent.* That was the first word she caught, and it hit her like a slap: not *lost*, not *wandered*, not *fled blindly into the frost*. *Sent.* Someone had put this child on a road and pointed her here, to Cinder Valley, to a valley where magic could not follow, and the reason came bundled with the word itself—a dark shape moving behind the meaning, something that *hunted*, something that found keys and—

Noor's knees went weak. She sat down hard on her stool.

"—and you ran because it follows you," she breathed. "Not followed you. *Follows* you. Present tense."

The child didn't understand the valley words, but she understood the tone. She nodded once, gravely, the way adults nod, and then she did something that cracked Noor open: she reached out and took Noor's hand—the hand still holding the ancient bone—and pressed the knuckle of it gently against her own small breastbone.

Then she said her name.

It was two syllables, soft-edged, the second one rising like a question even though it wasn't a question. *Ivet.* And this time there was no mistaking it, because the bone in Noor's palm rang like a struck bell, and the meaning poured in alongside the sound the way water fills a cup: *Ivet. Small kept thing. Sent.*

"Ivet," Noor repeated.

The child—Ivet—exhaled. Her whole body loosened at once, shoulders dropping, chin lifting, and Noor realized with a lurch that in nine days she had never once seen the girl relax. Every moment, awake or asleep, she had been braced, coiled, a creature waiting for the trap to spring. And all it had taken to loosen her was hearing her own name spoken aloud by someone who understood it.

Someone who understood *her*.

"Oh," Noor said, very quietly, to herself more than to the child. "Oh, you've been alone with this the whole time."

Ivet climbed onto the bench beside her without asking, the way children do when they have decided a person is theirs, and pulled the blanket around them both. Then she began to talk. Really talk—for the first time, a flood of it, words spilling over each other, and Noor could only catch fragments, but the fragments were enough to build a terrible picture:

A country whose name arrived with the taste of green things and the smell of rain on warm stone. *Sleeping,* that word meant—or maybe *kept*, the two meanings folded together like hands. People sleeping. A door. And threading through everything, recurring like a refrain, a word that made the old knuckle bone shudder in Noor's grip: *the hollow ones. The ones who empty.*

"They're coming for you," Noor said. Her voice sounded far away. "They sent you away to keep you from them, and they're coming anyway, and you came to the one place they can't—" She stopped. Started again. "Why here? Why me?"

But even as she asked it, she knew, because Ivet answered by touching Noor's chest this time, right over her heart, and saying one word in the old tongue. Noor felt its meaning settle into her like a stone dropped into deep water:

*Hearer.*

*The one who listens to what remains.*

Noor looked down at her hands—carver's hands, scarred and steady, hands that had spent twenty-five years listening to the dead of Cinder Valley because it was the only work she was good at and the only company that never asked questions about where she came from. Doss had told her once, drunk on festival wine, that she had the finest ear for bone he'd ever seen, uncanny, unnatural, and they'd both laughed and never spoken of it again.

Uncanny. Because she hadn't been born here. Because the gift that made her invisible and indispensable in a valley with no magic was not a valley gift at all.

"You weren't sent to me by accident," Noor said. "Whoever sent you knew someone would be here who could hear you. Knew about—" She gestured helplessly at herself. "Knew about *me*?"

Ivet shrugged, small and maddening, and yawned—a huge, jaw-cracking yawn that belonged entirely to a six-year-old and briefly demolished the gravity of everything. Then she leaned her head against Noor's shoulder, and within three breaths she was asleep.

Noor sat very still, afraid to move, holding a sleeping child and an ancient bone and the wreckage of everything she'd believed about her own life.

Above her, the floorboards creaked as Maren closed up the common room. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves. And somewhere beyond the valley's edge, past the frost line, past the boundary that had protected this place since before anyone could remember why—something patient was walking.

* *

Maren found them like that an hour later, when she brought down the supper neither of them had touched: Noor on the stool, Ivet asleep against her shoulder, both of them lit orange by the banked fire.

"Well," Maren said, setting the tray down. She was a broad woman with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles and a face that gave away nothing she didn't choose to give away. "You look like a woman who's been talking to ghosts."

"Worse." Noor kept her voice low so it wouldn't carry past the sleeping child. "I talked to her. Maren—her name is Ivet. She has a name, and a purpose, and people who *sent* her, and none of it is good."

Maren pulled up the second stool, unhurried, and poured herself the tea on the tray. "Tell me."

So Noor told her—the sent, the hunters, the sleeping country, the word *hearer* pressed against her heart. Maren listened the way she did everything: completely, without interruption, her eyes moving once to the child and staying there.

When Noor finished, Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Doss needs to hear this."

"Doss already knows some of it. I think he's known more than he's said since the night she arrived. He looked at her doorway-frost like he'd seen a ghost." Noor shifted carefully; her arm had gone numb under the child's weight, and she found she didn't care. "Maren, they're going to call a council meeting. I heard Toma Harrow telling people at market today that the child should be turned out before 'it' finds us. Before it follows her here."

"It may already be following her here."

"That's what I said."

"And?" Maren set down her cup. "What did you decide, while you were sitting here being brave about it?"

Noor looked down at Ivet—at the chapped lips parted in sleep, the lashes lying on cheeks still round with babyhood, the fingers curled loosely in Noor's sleeve as if even unconscious she was making sure Noor wouldn't leave. Nine days ago this child had been a problem, a dread, a crack in the careful small life Noor had built stone by deliberate stone. Tonight she was a weight against Noor's ribs that felt less like a burden than like something returning to a socket it had always fit.

"I decided," Noor said slowly, "that I spent my whole life carving bones for people who died safe inside these walls, and calling that a life. And I don't think I can sit in this valley and wait to find out what happens to her if I do nothing. Whatever comes through that frost line is coming for *her*, and it will have to—" She stopped, surprised at her own voice. "It will have to come through me."

Maren studied her for a long time. Something moved behind her practical face—not surprise, Noor realized. Recognition. Grief, maybe. The look of a woman watching someone walk a road she'd hoped they'd avoid.

"You know what that means," Maren said finally. "That means leaving. That means the council, and Toma Harrow, and every frightened soul in this village deciding you're the reason the cold feels colder this winter. You've spent twenty-five years being nobody, Noor. Nobody survives in valleys."

"I know."

"And you'll need Doss. Whatever's at the edge of this valley, whatever door she came through—he knows more about doors than any man alive, and he knows he owes a debt he's never named." Maren rose, gathered the untouched supper, and paused at the stair. For a moment she looked older than her years, lit from below like a woman standing at the mouth of a tomb. "Get some sleep, carver. Tomorrow we tell him everything. And tomorrow after that, we deal with the council."

"Maren." Noor's voice caught. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." But her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "Thank me when I've finished shouting at Toma Harrow."

She climbed the stairs. The inn settled around them, beams ticking as the fire cooled.

Noor sat in the emberlight with a sleeping child against her shoulder and the old bone warm in her free hand, humming faintly now, content, as if it too had been waiting a very long time to hear its name spoken.

*Ivet,* Noor thought. *Small kept thing. Sent.*

Well. She was kept now.

Outside, far off toward the valley's western edge, a dog began to bark—one sharp note, then another, then silence, the kind of silence that has a shape to it. Noor listened the way she listened to bones, and heard beneath the wind something that might have been footsteps, slow and unhurried, stopping just past the frost line.

Waiting.

She tightened her arm around the child and did not sleep until dawn.
