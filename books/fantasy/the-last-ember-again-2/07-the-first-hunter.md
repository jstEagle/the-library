## 7. The First Hunter

The cold does not touch Ivet.

She knows this is wrong the way she knows her own name—a knowing without words, a shape in the chest. The frost lies on the ground like spilled salt, silvering every fence post, every dead stalk in Maren's garden, and when she walks barefoot through it the white does not bite. It parts around her feet like water around a stone. The other children of the valley—she has seen them from the window, bundled in wool until only their eyes show—scream and stamp and blow on their fingers. She has never once needed to.

*Wrong,* says the shape in her chest. *You are a thing that should feel the cold.*

But there are worse wrongnesses coming.

She feels them before she understands them. That is how everything arrives to her now: first as feeling, then as image, then—if she is lucky—as words in Noor's strange soft tongue, which Ivet is learning the way you learn to swim, by drowning a little each day.

The feeling comes at dusk on the third day after the solstice. It comes as a *flattening*, as if the whole valley were a drumhead and something enormous had pressed one grey finger against it, far away, at the rim. The bread in Maren's kitchen goes stale between one breath and the next. The fire shrinks into itself. And beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar, beneath the roots of the valley itself, Ivet hears—the way she hears everything, through her bones, through the marrow that is not entirely her own—a long, thin note of fear rising from the sleeping dead.

They feel it too.

That is the worst part. In Vessia-before, in the country-that-was-folded, the dead had been warm things, drowsy things, a congregation murmuring in their sleep beneath the fields. Here they are cold and awake and pressed flat under a sky that will not let them rise, and when the flattening comes they all turn their faces toward the valley's edge at once, thousands of them, the way a flock of starlings turns.

*It is here,* the dead say, in the language with no valley name, the language that lives in Ivet's teeth. *It is here, it is here, little key, hide your hinge.*

Ivet drops the cup she is drying. It breaks on the stone floor, and even the shards seem quieter than shards should be, as if sound itself is holding its breath.

Maren comes running from the pantry, flour to her elbows. "What is it? What's wrong, love?" She uses the endearment easily now; Ivet has been three weeks in this house and Maren has never once asked where she came from, only fed her and fussed over her boots and muttered about finding a coat. Ivet loves her in the wordless way, the bone-way. She points at the floor. She points at the east, where the sun is going down red as an open wound.

"Noor," she manages. It is still nearly the only word she trusts herself to say. "Noor."

* * *

Noor comes at a run from her workshop across the lane, apron still dusted with bone-meal, and when Ivet grabs her hand and presses it flat against her own sternum—*listen, listen through me*—Noor's face goes through three changes in as many seconds: confusion, then concentration, then a horror so quiet it barely moves her mouth at all.

"Something's crossed the boundary," she says. Her voice is low, meant for Maren alone, but Ivet catches every word the way she catches everything, helplessly, the way a bowl catches rain. "At the edge. Near the old ford road. It shouldn't be possible—nothing crosses into Cinder Valley. Nothing *wants* to."

"Then what—" Maren begins.

And that is when the knock comes at Maren's door.

Three knocks. Even. Patient. The knock of a man who has all the time in the world because time is a ledger he keeps, and everyone in it owes him.

Noor blows out the lamp. They stand in the dark kitchen, the four of them—Ivet, Noor, Maren, and old Doss, who has appeared from the back room without anyone hearing him come, which for a ferryman of his years is its own small magic—and Ivet listens through the soles of her feet, through the flagstones, through the frozen earth, and hears what stands on the other side of the door.

It wears a body. That is the first horror: it has gone to the trouble of wearing a body, and the body is well-made. Boots. A coat. A satchel with a brass clasp. Hands folded politely. But inside the body there is nothing, no more than nothing inside a glove, and the face—

The face is borrowed. Ivet can taste the borrowing. It is a round, grey, forgettable face, the kind of face that belongs behind a desk, with ink-stained fingertips and a smile kept ready like coin. Somewhere a real man wore that face once. Somewhere he set it down—or had it taken—and now it hangs on the emptiness the way a sign hangs on a shopfront, saying *ordinary, ordinary, nothing to see here.*

*I know this thing.* The knowing rises in her from below her memory, from the folded place. *In the old country we called them the Quiet Clerks. They do not hate us. Hating would be something. They count us.*

"It'll be wanting information," Doss breathes, so quietly it is hardly sound at all. His hand finds Ivet's shoulder, light as a moth. "Down, girl. Behind the flour barrels. Whatever happens, whatever you hear—not a sound."

Ivet folds herself into the gap between the barrels and the wall. It smells of grain and mouse. Through a knothole in the wood she can see a sliver of the kitchen door, and through the window beyond it, the last red light dying on the snow.

The knock comes again. Three knocks. Even. Patient.

Maren looks at Noor. Noor nods once—*answer it, hiding makes it curious*—and Maren squares her shoulders like a woman going out to argue with a butcher over a bad quarter-pound, and opens the door.

* * *

The man on the doorstep is exactly what his face promises: a tax collector, or a census-taker, some grey functionary of some grey office. His coat is good wool, travel-dusted. His satchel bulges with papers. He smiles, and the smile is a masterpiece—it reaches his eyes, it crinkles them, it has warmth in it, and none of it is true, none of it is *in* anything, it is painted on the surface of him like gilding on a coffin.

"Good evening, mistress," he says. His voice is pleasant. It is the voice of a man reading aloud to himself. "Forgive the hour. I'm making the winter rounds—assessments, you understand. New toll schedules for the spring." He produces a paper, and even in the lamplight spilling past Maren's shoulder, the writing on it seems to slide, refusing to be read. "May I ask you a few questions?"

"You may ask," Maren says. Her voice is iron wrapped in wool. "Doesn't mean I'll answer."

The smile widens by one tooth. "Naturally." He unfolds a page. "Household of two. Yourself and—your sister? Your daughter?"

"My boarder. Carves bones for the dead. You'd have seen her work in the churchyard."

"Ah. Yes." Something flickers across the borrowed face—not interest, not quite. *Recognition of a category.* "A carver. How useful. And no children in the house?"

The silence lasts half a heartbeat too long. Ivet holds her breath among the flour barrels and wills her heart to stop hammering; it does not obey; hearts never obey.

"A valley full of children," Maren says, "and you come asking at my door about mine. Who sent you? Which office? I've lived in Cinder Valley fifty years and no assessor ever walked the winter rounds, not once, not in the deepest famine year when the tolls went unpaid. So you'll tell me who you are, sir, before you take one more liberty with my evening."

For a moment—one long, terrible moment—the smile stays exactly where it is, and the eyes above it go utterly still, still as a frozen pond, still as the space between stars, and Ivet understands with total clarity that beneath the face there is a *thing considering*, weighing Maren the way a clerk weighs whether a debt is worth collecting.

Then the pond thaws. The smile becomes human again, apologetic.

"Forgive me. Long road. I must have mistaken the house." He closes his satchel. "There was a report, you see, of a child traveling alone through the high passes. Six or seven years of age. Dark-haired. We make inquiries whenever such reports reach our office. Children are..." he pauses, and chooses the word the way a man chooses a coin of exact denomination, "...*liabilities*. Unregistered liabilities. The crown takes a dim view."

"Not my concern," Maren says.

"No." He steps back off the threshold. "Not yours. Good night, mistress. Keep warm."

He turns and walks down the lane, unhurried, and Ivet watches through the knothole as he passes beneath the window, and as he passes, he stops.

Just for a moment. Just a pause, a hitch in his stride, the way a man pauses when he remembers an errand.

His head tilts toward the house. Toward the kitchen. Toward the flour barrels.

The dead beneath the valley begin, very softly, to sing—to drown out her heartbeat, to fill the air with the murmur of ten thousand sleepers, and Ivet realizes with a jolt that they are doing it *for her*, throwing their voices over hers like a blanket over a cage. *Sing, little key. We will cover your sound.*

The man stands in the falling snow for the length of three heartbeats. Then he continues on, down the lane, past the smithy, past the well, a grey shape dissolving into greyer dusk, and he does not look back.

But he does not leave the valley either. Ivet can feel him at the valley's edge, near the old ford road, sitting down in an abandoned croft as though settling in for a long winter's accounting. Waiting. Because waiting costs him nothing. He is made of nothing. Nothing can wait forever.

* * *

They dig her out from behind the barrels with hands that shake—Maren's shaking with anger, Noor's with something colder.

"He knows," Noor says. There is bone-meal still on her cheek and she does not brush it away. "Maybe not your name. Maybe not which house. But a child arrived in this valley three weeks ago, and children don't arrive in Cinder Valley, Ivet. Nothing arrives here. He'll knock on every door between here and the churchyard, polite as Sunday, asking his questions, and someone—someone will answer wrong. Someone always answers wrong."

"Then she doesn't stay to be found," Doss says.

Everyone turns. The old ferryman sits by the banked fire with his ruined hands spread toward the coals, and his face is the face of a man looking at a bill that has finally come due.

"You heard him. He'll wait. They're patient as gravestones, his kind." Doss looks up, and his eyes find Ivet's, and there is something in them that makes her marrow hum—an old guilt, worn smooth as a river stone. "Twenty-five years ago I learned what these things cost. I swore I'd never ferry another soul across that boundary." His jaw works. "I'm about to break that oath, girl. For you. But not tonight. Tonight we think, and we plan, and nobody answers any doors."

Ivet does not fully understand the words. She understands the shape of them, the weight, the way she understands the weight of stones. She climbs into Noor's lap the way she climbed into her mother's lap in a country that no map admits to, and Noor's arms come around her, and Noor's heart beats above her ear—steady, steady, steady—but underneath it, in the blood, Ivet can hear the other rhythm, the one Noor doesn't know she carries:

A song. A lullaby. *Her mother's lullaby.*

The key turning softly in the lock of Noor Winterbourne, all these years, without her ever once hearing it.

Outside, the snow falls on the valley, filling footprints, erasing evidence, keeping its cold indifferent peace. And far away at the edge of the world, in a roofless croft by the ford road, a hollow man opens his ledger to a fresh page, dips his pen in nothing at all, and begins to write down the names of every child in Cinder Valley—one by one by one—until he finds the one that isn't there.
