## 9. Road of Small Mercies

Doss had crossed the Cinder Valley boundary four times in his long life, and every crossing had felt like passing through a wall of cold water. This time it was Noor who flinched. He heard her breath catch behind him, a small sharp sound, and he didn't need to turn to know what had struck her. The world outside the valley was loud. It was loud in ways that had nothing to do with sound.

He kept his eyes on the road. Dawn was coming up grey over the low hills, and the frost on the gorse burned off slow, and somewhere ahead a lark was making its idiot morning argument with the sky. Ordinary things. He clung to them. Behind him, the girl walked with Ivet on her hip the way she'd carry a sack of flour she couldn't afford to spill, and Doss thought: twenty-five years I've carried this, and now it walks beside me wearing bare feet.

They made the old drover's track by midmorning. The country here was changed—Doss had known it would be, but knowing is a thin coat against weather. When he'd been a young man running goods, this valley had held three villages, a shrine, a stone bridge with the names of bridge-builders carved into its parapet. Now there was one village, half-ruined, no shrine, and the bridge stood with its carvings worn smooth as if a century of thumbs had rubbed them out. As if something had come along and worried the memory out of the stone itself.

Noor stopped at the bridge and put her hand flat on the parapet.

"There were names here," she said.

"Aye." Doss shifted the bundle on his back. "There were."

She looked at him then, and he saw the thing in her face he'd seen in her mother's face once, briefly, across the water—the look of someone reading a page everyone else insists is blank. "You can feel where they were," she said. Not a question. Her hand moved along the stone like a blind woman reading. "Like scars under paint."

Ivet reached up and touched the parapet too, and said something in that old tongue, soft, two notes of it, and Noor went very still, and Doss looked away down the river because there are things an old man should not watch too closely or he'll start weeping on an empty stomach.

* * *

The first mercy came at noon.

It was a farmstead set back from the road, smoke from its chimney, a woman splitting kindling in the yard. Doss would have walked past—he'd spent fifty years perfecting the art of walking past—but the woman straightened up when she saw Ivet and dropped the axe.

Not in fear. That was the strange part, the part Doss turned over all afternoon like a stone looking for what was underneath. She dropped the axe and pressed both hands over her mouth, and when they drew level with her gate she said, in a voice cracked as old crockery: "You'll want bread. And the little one will want—" and then stopped, frowning, as if she'd begun a sentence whose end had been taken from her. "I don't know what she'll want. Why don't I know what she'll want?"

"She'll want bread same as anyone," Doss said carefully.

"No doubt. No doubt." But the woman was already moving, fetching a loaf and hard cheese and a jar of honey, piling them into Noor's arms, refusing coin so firmly that arguing would have been an insult. At the gate she caught Ivet's sleeve, gentle as handling a moth, and said, "My grandmother used to sing a song. About doors opening in hillsides. I never knew where she learned it. I always thought I dreamed it."

Then she stood there looking at Ivet with an expression Doss could only call homesick, which made no sense at all, since the woman had never left her farm and Ivet was a stranger, and yet there it was, plain as sunrise: homesickness, aimed at a child.

They ate in the lee of a drystone wall a mile on. Noor broke the loaf and gave Ivet the heel, honeyed, and watched the child eat with an attention Doss recognized. He'd worn it himself once, watching a bundle sleep in the bottom of his ferry while a dying woman's payment drained out of her into the water.

"They know her," Noor said quietly. "That woman. Not her face. Something deeper than faces."

"Folk remember what they're told they can't," Doss said. "It goes somewhere. Memory doesn't rot like meat, girl. It just gets buried, and buried things grow."

"Grow into what?"

Doss chewed. Considered. "Into kindness you can't account for," he said finally. "Into a tune you hum without knowing where you got it. Into why a farmer leaves the last field unploughed every seventh year though it costs him dear." He nodded at Ivet, who had honey at the corner of her mouth and was watching the crows with the solemn interest of a small queen reviewing troops. "Vessia wasn't just cut out of maps. It was cut out of everything. But a country is made of people, and people leak. They married out, fled out, were scattered before the folding. Their children's children carry pieces they can't name." He tapped his own chest. "Why d'you think I ferried your mother, girl? Fifty years on that river, and one night a woman comes to the bank speaking a tongue I never learned, and my hands knew what to do before my head did. Inherited reflex. Like flinching from fire."

Noor was quiet for a long time. "And the thing that erased Vessia. Does it have reflexes too?"

"It has patience," Doss said, and wished he hadn't, because her face did something complicated and brave and frightened all at once, and he'd promised himself on the far bank of the valley that he'd keep the fright off her as long as the road allowed.

* * *

They slept that night in a haybarn belonging to nobody—or belonging to somebody who had clearly not checked on it in years—and Doss woke in the small hours to voices.

He came awake the way old smugglers do, all at once and silently, knife already loose in his hand. But the voices were Noor's and a stranger's, low, coming from the barn door, where a shepherd had appeared out of the dark with a lantern and a face full of apology.

"—heard there was a woman and a child and an old man going north," the shepherd was saying. "Don't ask me how I heard. I heard it the way you hear rain coming. So I brought these." He set down a bundle: wool socks, a flask, dried apples wrapped in cloth. "And there's a ford two miles up where the water runs shallow. The other fords—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "The other fords are bad. Don't use the other fords."

"Why are they bad?" Noor asked.

"I don't know," the shepherd said, and looked genuinely distressed by it. "I just know I'd walk a long way round rather than cross at Miller's Ford, and I've never known why, and my father never knew why, and his mother before him wrote in her book that the water there remembers wrong." He tipped his cap to Ivet, who peered at him from behind Noor's leg, and the shepherd's whole face softened into something helpless. "Safe roads, little miss," he said, in a tone men usually save for shrines.

He was gone before Doss could offer thanks, and Doss stood in the doorway a long moment, listening to the lantern-light shrink up the hill.

"He knows," Noor said. "Somewhere in him, he knows."

"Everyone knows," Doss said. "That's the trouble. Nobody knows that they know." He looked up at the stars, at the familiar constellations, and found himself hunting among them for a gap, a space where something used to be. He'd done it before. There was a hole in the sky south of the Hunter that he'd noticed as a boy and never mentioned to anyone, because how do you tell a man the sky has a missing tooth? "Sleep, girl. We make the shallow ford at first light, and after that the country gets lonelier."

* * *

The lonelier country was moorland—black peat and standing stones and wind that came off it like a tide. They walked two days seeing no one, and Doss taught Noor the old smuggler's habits without calling them lessons: walk the sheep tracks, not the roads; camp high, where you can see folk coming; boil water even when you're certain it's clean, because certainty is a luxury that gets travelers killed.

Ivet thrived, which unsettled him more than anything. The child who had arrived barefoot in winter now walked the moors barefoot still, over frost and scree, unbothered, humming. Once he saw her stop at a standing stone older than any kingdom, press her palm to it, and laugh—one bright syllable of laughter, her first—and the stone seemed to lean toward her, very slightly, the way a plant leans at light. Noor saw it too. Neither of them spoke about it.

On the third day the mercies resumed, stranger than ever.

A tinker met them at a crossroads, took one look at Ivet, and mended Doss's boot unprompted, muttering that his own grandfather had been "a cobbler of a kind no one needed anymore," which he'd never understood until this moment, he said, and then looked confused about having said it. A widow in a turf-roofed cottage fed them stew and sent them off with a knitted blanket "for the child, because my mother knitted one just like it for a child once, and I never asked her for whom, and now I think I ought to have asked." An old soldier sitting by a milestone stood up as they passed and saluted—not Doss, not Noor—Ivet—with tears running freely down his ruined face, saying only, "I held the line. Tell them I held the line. I don't know who them is, but tell them."

By evening Noor was quiet in a way Doss distrusted deeply. He'd raised no children, but he'd ferried enough grief to know its silences.

"Say it," he prompted, when Ivet had gone asleep between them under the widow's blanket.

"Everyone out here has a splinter of her country stuck in them." Noor turned her hands palm-up in the firelight, the scarred, capable hands of a bone-carver. "A song, a superstition, a field nobody ploughs. And none of them know. They're all walking around with holes shaped like Vessia and calling the ache by other names." Her voice frayed. "I spent twenty-five years thinking I was the only hollow one. And the whole world is hollow in the same spot. All of us, carved by the same absence."

"That's not a wound, girl. That's a fingerprint."

She looked at him.

"Whoever folded that country away wanted it forgotten clean," Doss said. "But forgetting isn't cutting. Forgetting is covering. And coverings wear through. Every one of those strangers today—the widow, the soldier, the shepherd—that was the covering wearing through. Which means it can be worn through altogether. Which means—" he hesitated, because the next word was a dangerous one, and he said it anyway, "—which means it can be undone. If the right hands do the wearing."

"The jailers won't allow it."

"No. They won't." He fed a stick to the fire. "But I've known administrators all my life, girl, and here's what I know: they follow their own rules past all sense, because the rules are all they have. Give them a rule to obey and they'll march into the sea with it. Remember that, when the time comes. It may matter."

"When what time comes?"

Doss didn't answer. He was looking at the fire, but seeing another fire, twenty-five years gone, on the far bank of a black river, and a woman with emptying eyes pressing something warm and screaming into his arms, and the water whispering terms.

"Doss."

"Sleep," he said. "Long day tomorrow."

* * *

The hook came on the fifth morning, and it came in the shape of a child's drawing.

Ivet had taken to drawing in the dirt with a stick—pictures Noor half-understood now, the old language and its images braided together. That morning she drew as they walked, stopping every hundred yards to scratch lines into the mud of the track: a hillside. A door in the hillside. Lines radiating from the door like light, or roots, or fingers.

Then she stopped. Stood very straight. Drew one more figure, small, apart from the others: a stick-figure man with a smooth blank oval for a face, standing on the near side of the door. Watching it. Guarding it.

And then she drew the figure again, closer. And again, closer still. Each figure bigger than the last. Walking toward them down the page.

Down the road.

Noor went white. Doss followed the child's pointing finger north along the track, to where the moor dipped toward a river valley—and there, faint in the morning haze, maybe two miles off, something grey was moving along the bank of the water. Not fast. Not slow. With the unhurried certainty of paperwork being delivered.

"Miller's Ford is east," Doss said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "The shepherd said the water there remembers wrong. Well, girl—it seems to me that's exactly the sort of place a thing like that can't follow." He gripped his staff and started down toward the river at a pace just short of running. "Let's go find out what it remembers."
