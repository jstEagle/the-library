## 10. The Hollow Man Catches Up

The river announced itself before we saw it—a low rushing murmur beneath the birdsong, the kind of sound that made Doss straighten in his saddle like a man remembering a debt.

"Ford's a half-mile down," he said. "Water's high this time of year. We cross quick, no lingering."

Ivet rode pillion behind me on Doss's old mare, her arms locked around my waist, her cheek pressed between my shoulder blades. She hadn't hummed since morning. That was how I knew something was wrong. The child who had arrived barefoot in the frost, unbothered by cold that killed grown men, who sang songs older than kingdoms while she stirred porridge—she had gone quiet as a held breath.

"What is it?" I asked her, soft, in her own tongue. I was getting better at it. The words came to me now the way valley words never had, rising up whole from somewhere below memory, and every time one did, I felt the ground shift under the story I'd built my life on.

She answered with one word against my back: *Sulm.* The hollow one. The empty walker.

"He's close?"

Her arms tightened. That was answer enough.

* * *

We'd been three days out from Cinder Valley, moving through country I had only ever imagined—rolling pasture gone wild, orchards strangled by their own unpruned branches, villages where the houses leaned together like conspirators. Magic lived here. I felt it now the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of copper at the root of the tongue. And threaded through all of it, constant, unbearable, the dead.

They were everywhere outside the valley. That was the thing no one had warned me about—that magiclessness was also silence, and silence was the only mercy the dead ever got. Here, every field carried its harvest of voices. Men who'd died in the war Maren wouldn't name. Women who'd died in childbirth in houses that still stood. A child's small ghost repeating a game-song in the ruins of a well. I had learned, in three days, to walk through the world the way you walk through deep water: head down, breathing carefully, letting the current break around you.

Ivet cut through the noise like a blade through fog. Wherever she walked, the dead went quiet—not silenced but *soothed*, drawn to her the way cold hands draw to a hearth. She was a key, Doss had said. Keys are warm things. Everything lost wants to be found.

Which meant everything lost would lead the hunter straight to us.

Doss reined the mare off the track and down through alder scrub toward the river, and I saw the ford—and I saw the man standing in the middle of it.

He stood on a stone shelf where the water split around him, dry in the middle of the flood, his boots unstained, his coat grey as river-fog. He wore a tax collector's face—I'd seen the type in the market towns, mild and patient, the face of a man who only wants what is owed. He held a ledger under one arm. He was smiling.

My whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the spray off the water.

"That's not possible," Doss breathed. "That's the same face. From the letters—the neighbors' letters—he was asking after children in Harrowmere two days ago. That's forty miles east."

"He doesn't travel," I heard myself say. "He *arrives*. Like a bill come due." I didn't know how I knew it. The knowledge came up through my hands, my carving hands, the same place the old language lived.

Ivet slid off the mare before I could stop her. She walked to the water's edge and stood there, small and barefoot even now, refusing the shoes we'd bought her in the last village, staring at the hollow man across the rushing brown water. And he stared back, and his smile widened by exactly one tooth, and he opened his ledger.

"Noor," Doss said quietly. "Get her back on the horse."

"He can't cross running water," I said. "Can he?"

"I don't know. I know he doesn't have to. Look upstream."

I looked. On the far bank, maybe fifty yards up, a second figure stood among the alders. Same grey coat. Same patient face—or near enough; these things are mass-produced, I understood suddenly, like nails or coffins. And further up, half-lost in mist, a third.

"They're not chasing us," I whispered. "They're *herding* us. They want us at the ford because they want us tired when they take her."

The nearest one spoke then, and his voice carried over the water without effort, flat as paper:

"Noor Winterbourne. Born beyond, raised within. You are harboring an asset of the Hollow Court. This is not an accusation. It is an accounting. Return the asset and your account remains open. Refuse, and the balance comes due today, at this location, in full."

It was the most frightening thing I had ever heard, and it was frightening precisely because it wasn't cruel. There was nothing in it to hate. It was arithmetic.

Ivet turned and looked up at me, and her eyes were ancient and six years old at once, and she said—in the old tongue, but I heard it plain as plain—"You can give them what they want. No one would blame you. Even I would not blame you."

"Don't you dare," I said, and my voice cracked, and I knelt and took her by the shoulders. "Listen to me. I spent twenty-five years being nobody so that nobody could find me, and do you know what it bought me? A warm room and a quiet life and a hole in the middle shaped exactly like a question I was too afraid to ask. You are the question. Do you understand? You're the *answer*, and I am done being afraid."

The hollow man closed his ledger.

"Balance due," he said.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do—all the slow dread collapsing into a single terrible minute.

The one on our bank simply stepped out of the alders ten feet away, having been there the whole time, or having decided just then to exist there. Grey coat, ledger, smile. Doss didn't shout a warning. He moved. Twenty-five years of ferrying things across thresholds had made him quick in the way old men sometimes are, quick the way a snake is quick, and he put himself between the hollow man and Ivet with a boat-hook he'd carried since the valley and swung it like he was catching a drowning man.

The hook passed through the grey coat and caught—caught something, some scaffold under the shape—and the hollow man staggered, and for the first time his face did something other than smile. It *rearranged*. Features sliding like silt in a current, trying to reassemble patience and finding only a brief, bureaucratic surprise.

And then the other two were crossing the water. Dry-shod. Of course.

"Doss!" I screamed, and he was already down—one of them had simply touched him, laid two fingers on his forearm the way a clerk initials a page, and where the fingers landed the flesh went grey and cold and wrong, frost spreading under skin. Doss made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a scream. A gasp of *recognition*, like a man greeting an old creditor.

Noor. Think. You carve bones. You carve bones for the dead—

It came to me whole, the way the language had, the way the truth of my birth had: bone holds. Bone remembers. Bone is the oldest ward in the world because bone is what's left when everything negotiable has burned away. My keepsakes—my mother's little comb of yellowed bone that Doss had given me with my swaddling cloth. The carved button from Maren's coat, the one she'd pressed into my hand at the door saying *come home*. The fish-bone charm Doss whittled me when I was seven, ugly and perfect.

I had them all in the pouch at my belt, because I have always carried my people with me, and I had never once thought of them as anything but sentiment.

I dropped to my knees in the mud and drew my carving knife, and I began to cut.

Not gently. Not the careful, loving strokes of memorial work. I cut sigils into my mother's comb the way you score a branch to make it bleed, lines I had never learned and had always known—*the threshold sign, the keeping-sign, the nameless letter that means mine*—and as the knife bit, the pieces woke. The comb grew warm. The button flared white-hot in my palm and I didn't let go, because it was Maren's and Maren had told me to come home and I would carry her fire if it scorched me to ash.

"Hold the line!" I shouted, absurdly, a woman with a knife and some buttons against three clerks of erasure.

But it worked. God help me, it worked.

When the second hollow man reached for Ivet, I thrust the comb forward like a lantern, and the sigils blazed, and the thing recoiled as though the air itself had turned to teeth. Its face rippled. Its ledger smoked at the corner. "Warded," it said, in that flat paper voice, and there was something almost like respect in it, which was worse than hatred would have been. "Unlicensed craft. This will be noted."

"Noted this," I snarled, and pressed forward, and it gave ground, giving ground the way ice gives, reluctantly and with cracking sounds.

Doss was on his knees in the shallows, cradling his arm. The grey had climbed past his elbow. His lips were going blue-white. "Noor," he said, and his voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Don't waste time on me. The ford. Get her across. Water slows them even if it stops them not at all. Every second in the current costs them. Go, girl. I've been owing the river a crossing for twenty-five years anyway."

"You shut your mouth."

"That's the spirit." He grinned at me with grey-veined lips, and then he hauled himself upright with the boat-hook as a staff, and turned to face the hollow man whose touch had marked him, and planted himself in the shallows like a fence post driven into the bed of the world. "Well?" he said to it. "I'm told there's a balance outstanding. Come collect."

* * *

I got Ivet into the water. That much, at least, I did right.

The current grabbed us immediately—it was spring-fed snowmelt, brutal, chest-high on me and over her head, so I wrapped one arm around her and held the warded comb high in the other hand and we fought our way across while behind us Doss fought his own battle, and I heard it in fragments: the flat voice reciting terms, Doss cursing it in ferryman's slang, the crack of the boat-hook, once the wet heavy sound of a body hitting stone.

I did not look back. Looking back is how you drown. I put my face against Ivet's hair and drove forward stroke by stroke, and the sigils on the comb burned so bright they lit the water green beneath us, and the dead along both banks rose up—all of them, the war-dead and the child at the well and hundreds more, nameless farmers and forgotten soldiers—and for one impossible moment they stood along the shore watching us, silent, and then, all together, they *leaned*.

Toward the ford. Toward Doss. As if lending weight. As if the dead of this drowned country remembered what it was to stand in a line.

We hit the far bank. I dragged Ivet up the stones and collapsed beside her, coughing, shaking, alive.

Across the water, Doss was down. The hollow men stood over him in the shallows, unhurried, consulting their ledgers. Then the nearest one looked up—at me, across the whole width of the flood—and inclined its head, courteous as a banker.

"The account is not settled," it called. "Merely deferred. Assets remain outstanding." And all three of them folded sideways, the way a letter folds, and were gone, leaving only Doss lying broken in the shallows and the river running red-pink around him.

I was back in the water before I'd decided to be. The current tried to keep me; the dead helped push. I reached him and hauled his head above the waterline and his arm—his poor arm, grey to the shoulder now, cold as a grave-marker—hung wrong, and his eyes found mine and focused with effort.

"Told you," he whispered. "Owed the river a crossing."

"You're not dying, you stubborn old man, you're not allowed—"

"Noor." His good hand caught my wrist, grip shockingly strong. "Listen. It marked me. Court-touched. There's a place—east, past Harrowmere—a hedge-witch. Wren, they call her. Dreams of Vessia, the madwoman says, but madwomen know more than councils. She can hold the grey back. Maybe pull it out." His breath hitched. "Tell her... tell her the ferryman sent her a passenger at last. Tell her I finally carried someone *worth* the fare."

"Stop talking like a eulogy. You're going to walk there yourself."

"Am I." He smiled up at me, and behind the pain in his face was something I had never seen there before, something light. "Twenty-five years I've been waiting to find out if saving you was worth what it cost me. Standing here now—" his eyes flicked to Ivet, crouched at the water's edge, watching him with her ancient, grieving eyes— "standing here, I'd say the books are balanced. Better than balanced. Now get her up the hill, girl. They'll be back. Deferred isn't forgiven."

His eyes closed. His breathing stayed—shallow, rattling, but there—but the grey was still climbing, inch by patient inch, and I knelt in the shallows holding a dying smuggler in a foreign river, with a warded comb in my pocket and a hunted child at my back and fifty miles of hostile country between us and a witch I'd never met.

Ivet touched my shoulder. She pointed east, up the long slope of the hills, where storm-light was gathering over the road to Harrowmere.

Then she said a word I knew without knowing, and understood completely:

*Hurry.*
