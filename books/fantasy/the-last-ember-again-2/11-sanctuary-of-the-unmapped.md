## 11. Sanctuary of the Unmapped

Wren had dreamed of the green country again the night before the strangers came up her hill.

She always dreamed it the same way. Fields under a sky the color of unglazed porcelain, wheat that grew in rows too straight for any farmer she'd ever known, and bells—bells ringing from a tower that had no church beneath it, ringing a pattern that meant *come home, come home, all is well*. She had been dreaming that country since she was a girl, since before she could name what a dream was, and every hedge-wife and cunning-man who had trained her had told her the same thing: *don't speak of it, girl. Dreams like those are doors, and doors let things through.*

So she had kept it folded inside her like a letter never sent, sixty years of mornings waking with the smell of that country's bread still in her nose, saying nothing to anyone.

Then, on a grey morning in late winter, with frost still furring the brambles and her goats complaining at the cold, three figures came limping along the drover's path below her cottage—one old man half-carried between a woman and a small child, leaving a dark thread of blood on the frozen mud behind them.

Wren stood in her doorway with a bucket in her hand and knew them the way you know a song from your childhood.

*Vessians*, said the part of her that dreamed. Not the woman and not the child, perhaps, but the old man—there was something in the set of his shoulders, the way he bore his hurt without complaint, that rang the same bell-note as the tower in her sleep. And the child—

The child turned and looked directly up at her cottage, across half a mile of open field, as though Wren had called out loud.

The bucket slipped from her fingers. She didn't remember bending to pick it up. She was already down the hill.

* * *

"You're bleeding through," Wren said, hauling the ferryman onto her table by main strength while Noor held the lantern. "Through the bandage, through my good cloth, through the table, likely. What did this to him?"

"Man," Noor said. Her voice was scraped raw; Wren heard the valley in it, the flat careful vowels of Cinder Valley folk who learned early not to say anything worth repeating. "Grey man. Wearing a tax collector's face. He—" She stopped. Started again. "He wanted the child."

"And he cut you instead of taking her," Wren said, peeling back the soaked bandage. The wound beneath was ugly—a long diagonal slash across the old man's ribs, deep enough to show the white gleam of bone—but clean, cauterized somehow at the edges, as though the blade that made it had been more interested in marking than killing. "And then he let you go."

"He laughed," said the child.

It was the first thing Wren had heard Ivet say, and the words came out in that old tongue, the one Wren's grandmother had forbidden, the one that lived only in dreams—and Wren understood it. Understood it whole, the way you understand your mother's voice through a wall.

She sat down hard on her stool.

The woman—Noor, the ferryman had rasped on the walk up—caught her arm. "You understood that."

"Yes." Wren heard her own voice go strange. "Yes, I did. And so did you, I think, which is a far more interesting question." She looked down at the wound, at the ferryman's grey face, and made herself be practical, because practical was the only prayer she trusted. "Boil water. There's yarrow in the blue jar, and comfrey, and if you see anything with purple flowers in that jar it's for drinking, not for wounds, don't mix them up. I'm going to pack this gash with moss and honey and pray to whatever gods haven't been taxed out of existence."

Doss opened his eyes. They were sunken, fever-bright. "Hedge-witch," he said. "They said there was one over the ridge who remembered."

"Remembered what?" Wren asked, though her hands already knew.

"Vessia."

The word went through the cottage like wind through a shuttered house. Every hanging herb stirred on its string. The fire leaned sideways. In the corner, the child hummed two notes—the first two notes of the bell-song—and Wren's bones rang with them like a struck fence post.

"Out," Wren said to Noor, gently. "Water. Now. Before I fall over."

* * *

The fever took Doss properly by nightfall, and Wren worked him through it with willow bark and cold cloths and long low words in the old tongue that she hadn't known she remembered until they came out of her mouth—words her own hands seemed to recall from some life she had never lived. Toward midnight his breathing eased. Toward midnight, also, Noor appeared in the doorway of the sickroom with the child asleep against her shoulder, and Wren knew by her face that the woman had spent the hours since dusk sitting very still and listening to something no one else could hear.

"The dead," Wren said. It wasn't a question. "You hear the dead."

Noor's arms tightened around the sleeping child. "Since I was born. Only—it doesn't happen here. Inside, I mean. Cinder Valley is—" She searched for it. "Quiet. Magic can't get in. Nothing gets in. I thought that's why I couldn't hear them there. But we've been out four days now and it hasn't stopped since the boundary, and I need someone to tell me—" Her jaw worked. "Tell me what I am. Because I've spent twenty-five years thinking I was either cursed or mad, and both of those are very lonely countries, and I would like to emigrate."

Wren poured two cups of nettle tea and pushed one across the table. Then she took down the key that hung above her hearth—not a key to any lock in the cottage, an iron key worn smooth as a river stone, older than the cottage, older than her grandmother—and laid it on the boards between them.

"I'll tell you a story," she said. "My grandmother told it to me, and hers to her, all the way back to women who weren't storytellers at all but witnesses. Sit. This takes the length of a candle."

* * *

"There was a country," Wren began, "east of everywhere. You won't find it on maps because maps were burned, and you won't find it in songs because songs were bought or broken, and you won't find it in graves because—" she tapped the iron key— "the graves were the first thing they took. A country of carvers and bell-founders and bone-singers. A people whose gift wasn't fire or flight or any of the flashy magics kings collect. Their gift was listening. They heard their dead the way you hear a neighbor through a wall—not haunting, just... near. The dead of Vessia never left. They stayed close, in the way heat stays in a stone after sundown, and the living built their lives around that warmth. Named children with the dead's advice. Settled disputes by asking grandmothers three generations gone. It was the gentlest magic I've ever heard tell of, and that is exactly why it frightened the wrong people.

"Because you can tax a harvest. You can conscript an army. But how do you govern people whose ancestors stand at their shoulders and say *no, child, we tried that once, it ended badly*? How do you rewrite history when every family keeps its own historians buried in the dooryard, still talking?" Wren turned the key over. "There was a war nobody names because naming gives it shape. The other side lost the field and won the century. They couldn't kill Vessia, so they did worse. They folded it away. Out of the world like a letter into an envelope. The land, the cities, and everyone in them—sleeping, my grandmother said, mid-breath, mid-laughter, a whole nation caught between one heartbeat and the next. And the jailers swept the borders of the world ever after, hunting anyone who might remember the fold. Hunting keys."

Noor's face had gone the color of the moonlight through the window. "The child."

"The child. And you, bone-carver." Wren reached across the table and took Noor's hand—took it firmly, the way you hold someone about to faint. "You were carried out of that country as an infant. Across a threshold, by this ferryman, at a price I suspect neither of you has fully counted. And the listening—that isn't a curse that fell on you, girl. That's your inheritance. That's the sound of your own dead calling to you across the fold, trying to reach a daughter who was smuggled out of the house before the door shut."

The silence stretched. In the sickroom, Doss murmured in his fever—in the old tongue, Wren noted, words of apology, over and over.

"I hid it," Noor said at last. Her voice cracked down the middle. "My whole life, whenever I heard them outside the valley—I thought something was wrong with me. I learned to pretend deafness. I learned to flinch and cover. I built myself small so no one would notice the noise I carried." A breath that shuddered going in. "I spent twenty-five years apologizing for being born, when I was only—only answering my own family."

"Yes," Wren said simply.

"They're screaming, Wren. All of them. Fifty miles of graveyards and battlefields and I can hear every soul in them, and underneath it, farther away than distance, there's something else—a whole country, humming, like bees in a wall. Is that them? Is that Vessia?"

"That's them." Wren's eyes stung. Sixty years of dreams, and here was a woman who could hear the dream awake. "Asleep and dreaming, most like, the way I dream them. Waiting for a door."

Noor put her face in her hands. Wren let her weep, because some griefs are the honest kind—the grief of a woman discovering that the cage she hated was also the hiding place that saved her, and that the thing she mourned as a curse had been a birthright singing her name the whole time.

When Noor finally lifted her head, scrubbed and red-eyed and steadier, the child stood in the sickroom doorway.

Ivet crossed the room without a word, climbed onto the bench beside Noor, and pressed something into her palm: a small disc of pale wood, carved all over with fine spiraling lines. Wren leaned close and felt her heart stop and restart.

Bell-script. The writing of the bell-tower in her dreams. She had seen it perhaps five times in her life, always in sleep, always fading by morning—and here it was, daylight-stable, carved by a six-year-old with a confidence no six-year-old should own.

"What is it?" Noor asked.

Ivet pointed at the carving, then at Noor's chest. Then she spoke, slowly, in the old tongue, and Wren translated in a whisper because translation felt like a duty:

"*It says your true name. The one your mother gave you before the crossing.*"

Noor went utterly still. "What is it?"

But the child only shook her head and pointed toward the sickroom, where the ferryman lay burning, and then drew one finger across her throat—not a threat, Wren realized, but a warning, a picture of a debt unpaid—and climbed back into Noor's lap and fell asleep between one breath and the next, like a candle pinched out.

* * *

Wren slept poorly, as she had all her life, and woke before dawn to the sound of the goats in a panic.

She came out with her knife and her fire-pot and found nothing at the gate, nothing in the lane, nothing anywhere except—on the frost of her doorstep, dead center, placed with the precision of a clerk filing papers—a single grey feather, and beneath it, pressed into the ice as if stamped with a signet ring, a mark like a ledger-line. An entry. A record that a debt existed and a collector knew the address.

Behind her, in the cottage, Doss cried out in his fever. And in the old tongue, clear across the room's hush, the child answered him—words Wren understood and wished she didn't:

*"The jailers have found us. We must reach the door before the turning of the moon, or the sleeper wakes alone forever."*

Wren stood on her step with the feather in her fist, feeling sixty years of forbidden dreams rise in her throat like a tide, and made the decision she suspected she had been making all her life, in a green country east of everywhere.

There was a road, she thought. An unmapped road. And she was going to have to tell them everything she knew—including why she knew it.
