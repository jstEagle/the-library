## 12. Ivet's Testimony

Ivet knows the words for *river* and *bread* and *door* in the valley tongue now, and the word for *thank you*, which she says to Wren every morning until Wren laughs and says once is enough, little bird. But some things cannot be said in any tongue that has a word for *goodbye*. Some things must be drawn.

So on the third night at Wren's cottage, while Doss sleeps propped against the hearth with his bandaged arm laid out straight as a fallen branch, Ivet takes the charcoal stub from the crock by the fire and pulls Wren's good tablecloth toward her.

Wren watches from her chair, hands folded, saying nothing. Noor sits across from Ivet, elbows on her knees, the way she always sits when she is listening with more than her ears.

Ivet draws.

* * *

First she draws a house, because that is where all stories begin in Vessia. A house with a steep roof and a door painted blue — she colors it with a crushed bit of larkspur from Wren's jar, because charcoal has no blue and this matters, the door was blue, everyone's doors were blue, so that travelers could find their way home even drunk, even blind, even in snow. She draws the roof heavy with thatch. She draws smoke rising from the chimney in a fat curling rope.

Then she draws the fields around the house, and here her hand slows.

She draws wheat. Then she draws the wheat again beside it, taller, with heads like small bells. Then barley, then flax, then a row of beehives shaped like woven baskets, then an orchard whose trees she marks with dots because the fruit changed color depending on which song you sang to it. She draws all of it crowded together, field pressed against field, because that was how Vessia farmed — everything touching everything, nothing wasted, nothing alone.

Noor's breath changes. Ivet hears it catch the way it does when Noor holds a bone up to the light.

Ivet draws people. A woman carrying a basket. A man with a scythe. Children running between the rows with their arms out like birds. She draws them small and quick, dozens of them, filling the margins of the cloth, because you could not walk anywhere in Vessia without passing someone who would make you stop and talk until your bread went cold.

Then she draws the sky above it all, and in the sky she draws the thing that ended the world.

Not fire. Not soldiers. The valley people would understand fire; they have stories about fire. What came for Vessia was quieter than fire.

She draws a great grey hand.

It comes down out of the top of the cloth, fingers long and jointed like a spider's legs, and beneath each fingertip she draws what the finger did. Under one finger: a map of Vessia, and the finger folding it, folding it the way Noor folds a letter, smaller and smaller, until the country fits inside a crease. Under another finger: the mouths of the people, sewn shut with grey thread — not their lips, she shows with careful little stitches, but their names, the names pulled out first, because a nameless thing can be put away without anyone missing it. Under a third finger: the roads unwinding like thread off a spool. Under the fourth: the bells of the orchard trees going dark, one by one, little circles with lines through them.

Under the fifth finger, the smallest one, she draws the people themselves, lying down. Rows and rows of them, lying in their houses, in their fields, on their roads, eyes closed, faces peaceful.

Sleeping. Not dead. This is the most important thing and she will draw it as many times as it takes.

Noor reaches out and touches the row of sleeping figures, very lightly, the way she touches bones she is afraid of waking.

"They're alive," Noor says. It is not quite a question. Her voice sounds scraped.

Ivet nods, hard, so hard her whole body nods with her. Alive. Asleep. Folded. She taps the crease in the map where the folded country went, and then she draws the place where the crease was hidden: a hillside, green, unremarkable, with a door set into its face. She draws the door blue. Of course blue. And around the door's edges she draws the seal — thick bands of bone, carved over and over, locking the door into the hill like a splint locking a broken arm straight.

* * *

Wren makes tea while Ivet rests her hand. Charcoal work is tiring when the pictures are this true. Wren sets a cup in front of Noor and one near Ivet and does not ask questions yet, which Ivet is grateful for, because the next part is the part that took her longest to understand herself, back home, before she was sent.

While the tea steams, Ivet draws the jailers.

The hollow man. She draws him carefully, because he is not easy to draw — his face is borrowed, and borrowed things do not sit right on paper. She gives him the tax collector's face again, the flat polite face, the face that asked the ferry-keeper's wife about children, about strange children, about children who hum. Then, around the face, she draws what he really is: a coat with nothing inside it. A shape like a person made of the same grey as the hand. She gives him a ledger under his arm, because the jailers are not monsters. Monsters would be easier. Monsters you can run from. The jailers carry books, and in the books are rules, and the rules say that Vessia was put away lawfully, that the debt of forgetting has been paid in full, that no door may open, that no key may live.

She draws the key last.

A child. Standing. Singing.

She points at it, then at herself.

Noor goes very still. Stillness looks different on Noor than on other people — other people's stillness is empty, but Noor's stillness is full, packed tight, like a held breath, like a bone full of echo.

"You were sent," Noor says slowly, tasting each word. "Not lost. Sent."

Ivet nods. Sent. She draws it so there is no mistake: the sleeping country, and from inside it, one small figure walking out through a crack in the seal — a crack she draws as a hairline fracture in the bone bands, thin as a winter ice crack. The elders had found it. The elders had wept over it, because a crack means the fold is weakening, means the sleep is thinning, means either the country wakes soon on its own or the jailers come to mend the crack by folding it smaller, tighter, forever.

And a mended fold, the elders said, does not keep dreamers. A mended fold keeps nothing. The sleepers would fade like breath off glass.

So they woke one child — only one, because the seal lets almost nothing through, and a child is mostly door-shaped anyway, small and soft and able to slip where armies cannot — and they taught her the songs, and they kissed her forehead, and they sent her out to find the one thing the prophecy-bones had promised:

*A hearer. One born outside who carries the old blood in her ears. One who listens to the dead.*

Ivet looks up at Noor.

She had known on the first night. On the frost night, at the door of the bone-carver's hut, when she hummed the lullaby and heard — through the wood, through the cold, through twenty-five years of silence — a voice answer in the old tongue, halting and accented and *there*. She had cried then, though she could not explain why to the woman who found her. She cries a little now, remembering, and wipes her face with the heel of her hand and keeps drawing, because the story deserves a steady hand.

She draws Noor. It is not a good likeness — too tall, and she gives the hair too much black — but she draws the ears carefully, and around the ears she draws sound-lines, the wavy marks that mean *listening*, and she draws them reaching down into the ground, into the sleeping hill, into the folded country, connecting.

Noor stares at the drawing of herself for a long time.

"I'm not Vessian," she says finally. "I was born here. In the valley. Maren told me—"

But her voice runs out, because they both know that story has been unraveling since the frost night, thread by thread. Born outside. Doss ferried an infant across the threshold. A dying woman paid with her memory. Ivet does not know all those words yet, but she knows the shape of the truth underneath them, and the shape is simple: Noor was carried *out* of the crease before it closed. Noor is Vessian the way a seed is still the tree it fell from, even after the wind takes it over a wall.

Ivet draws one more thing. She draws the hairline crack in the seal, and then she draws it closing — the jailers' grey fingers pressing it shut, smoothing it flat — and then she draws what happens to the sleepers inside when it closes: she draws them lighter. Fainter. She presses so lightly with the charcoal that the figures barely show, ghosts of ghosts.

Then she draws the alternative. The same crack, widened. A door, open. Blue. Light coming out of it like water. The sleepers walking through, blinking, waking, into a world that has forgotten them — but a world that can remember. She draws Noor standing beside the door, holding it, and she draws the sound-lines again, going from Noor's ears down into the earth, and this time she draws what comes *up* the lines: song. Little notes, floating up out of the deep like bubbles.

The dead of Cinder Valley. All those trapped echoes Noor has listened to her whole life, all those bones that speak and never sing. Ivet doesn't have the valley words to say it, but she draws it plainly enough: the trapped dead of this cold magicless valley, and the sleeping living of folded Vessia, and a door between them, and one hearer who belongs to both.

That is why the elders sent her. Not just to find a key. To find a *bridge*.

* * *

The fire pops. Doss stirs in his sleep and murmurs something that might be a name, might be an apology — he murmurs apologies in his sleep, Ivet has noticed, night after night, a whole litany of them, as if his dreams were a ledger he is still trying to balance.

Wren leans forward and turns the tablecloth slowly, studying each drawing in turn, and her face does something complicated — grief and fury and a kind of fierce joy all fighting for the same ground.

"Folded away," Wren says softly. "All these years I dreamed of a country under glass, and I thought I was mad. We all thought—" She stops. Starts again. "There were songs. Broken songs, songs with holes in them, that nobody knew the words to. My grandmother sang one. Every village has one. That's what's left of Vessia out here — holes in songs." She looks at Ivet with wet bright eyes. "And you're telling me the singers are alive. They're *right there*, behind a hill, asleep, and the grey men with the ledgers would rather let them fade than admit what they did."

Ivet nods. Hope with teeth — that is what the elders called it, the feeling she is trying to give these women now. Not the soft hope of wishing. The hope that bites. The hope that says *the door exists, the crack exists, the hearer exists, therefore wake them*.

Noor has not spoken for a long time. She is looking at the drawing of the faint sleepers, the ones fading like breath off glass, and Ivet watches her jaw set in a way Ivet has seen before — on the frost night, when the neighbors whispered; at the ford, when the hollow man came out of the reeds; always before Noor does the thing everyone said couldn't be done.

"The jailers offered us a deal," Noor says quietly. "At the ford. Before the warding drove it off. It spoke in my head — leave the child, keep your lives, let the forgotten stay forgotten." Her hand closes around her teacup, knuckles white. "I thought it was threatening me. But it wasn't. It was *bureaucracy*. It was reading me the terms."

Ivet picks up the charcoal again. There is one more thing to draw, the hardest thing, the thing she has saved because drawing it makes her chest hurt.

She draws the crack in the seal again. And beside it she draws a line of small marks — a countdown, the way the elders scratched it on the temple wall, one mark worn away each dawn. Most of the marks are crossed out. Only three remain.

Three dawns. Then the crack seals itself, with or without the jailers' help, and the fold settles for good, and the sleepers sleep past all waking.

She turns the cloth so Noor can see, and points at the three marks, and holds up three fingers, and says it in the old tongue because some numbers should be said in the language of the thing they count down to:

"*Tri nahten.* Three nights."

Noor looks at the marks. At the blue door. At Ivet. Something moves behind her face like water under ice — and then, outside the cottage windows, far off across the dark fields, a light appears. Small. Grey-white. Unnatural. Then a second. Moving along the ridge in perfect, patient formation, the way things move when they are not walking but *processing*.

Wren snuffs the candles with her palm. "They've found us," she whispers.

Doss opens his eyes. He looks at the lights on the ridge, and then at the tablecloth with its sleeping country and its three remaining marks, and he swings his wounded legs off the bench and stands up, swaying, grimacing, gripping the table's edge.

"Well," he says. "Best not waste the two we've got left."
