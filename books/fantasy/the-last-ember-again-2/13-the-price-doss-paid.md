## 13. The Price Doss Paid

The wound in Doss's side had stopped bleeding sometime in the night, which he knew from long experience with rivermen and drowned men was not a good sign. Blood that stayed put was blood that had given up. He lay on a straw pallet in Wren's back room, under a roof of woven willow that smelled of dried yarrow and woodsmoke, and listened to the house breathe around him.

It was a strange thing, listening. All his life he had been the one who made noise—the slap of oars, the creak of the ferry rope, his own voice singing off-key across the black water of the Cinder. Now the noise was inside him: a wet rattle in the chest, a slow tick like ice settling in a thawing lake. The hollow man's blade had gone in just under his ribs, and though Wren had packed it with moss and sung over it until her voice went hoarse, Doss could feel the truth of it the way you feel weather coming through an old scar.

He was dying. Not today, maybe not tomorrow, but soon enough, and the knowing sat in him strangely light, like a coin he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten its weight.

The door opened without sound. Wren's house did that—doors and floorboards moved politely, as if the whole structure were holding its breath for the people inside it.

Noor came in carrying a basin of water and a strip of clean linen. She moved like she always moved, careful and quiet, a woman who had spent her life making herself small in rooms. But there was something different in her face now. Something unhidden. Twenty-five years he'd watched her keep her chin down and her eyes lowered, and now she looked at him straight on, and it was like being seen by someone for the first time all over again.

"You should be sleeping," she said.

"I'll sleep when I'm dead." He tried to grin. It came out crooked. "Which, if the ache in my side is any judge, won't be long."

"Don't joke."

"Somebody has to. You never do." He patted the edge of the pallet. "Sit, girl. Sit. I've got things to say and I don't trust myself to say them standing up, and I surely don't trust tomorrow to hold still while I find the words."

She set the basin down and sat. Her hands were stained faintly with bone-dust even now—he'd teased her about it for years, called her *chalk-hands* when she was young and foolish enough to laugh at an old man's jokes—and they folded in her lap the way they always did when she was bracing herself.

"Ivet's asleep," Noor said. "Wren's watching her. She drew pictures all evening. Boats." A small, wondering pause. "She drew your boat, Doss. From memory. She's only ridden it once."

"Good memory, then." His chest tightened around something that wasn't the wound. "That boat's carried more than passengers. Carried secrets. Carried—" He stopped. Looked at his own hands, broad and cracked and spotted with age, hands that had hauled rope for fifty years and lied for twenty-five. "Noor. I need to tell you how you came to this valley. All of it. Not the version I gave Maren, not the version I've told myself at night to make it sit easier in my gut. The true one. And I need you to hear it before the fever takes me, because after that I can't promise what comes out of my mouth will be mine."

"Doss—"

"Let me talk." His voice cracked like the ferry rope used to. "Please. I've rehearsed this drowning-man speech for a quarter century and I'll be damned if I die before delivering it."

* * *

"It was midwinter," he said. "Twenty-five years ago, near enough. The night of the deep frost, the kind where the river goes quiet and the stars come down close enough to bite. I was running the late crossing, which in those days meant I ferried smugglers and runaways and once a tax collector I pretended not to recognize, gods rest his corrupt little soul. And she came down to the bank just past midnight. A woman. Young. Walking like the cold didn't touch her, which should have been my first warning, because everything touches everyone in Cinder Valley—that's the whole point of the place.

"She said she needed to cross. I told her the ferry doesn't run for ghosts, because that's what she looked like—something already half-gone. And she smiled at me, and I have thought about that smile every day since, and I want you to understand what it was. It wasn't sad. It wasn't brave. It was *relieved*. Like a woman setting down a load she'd carried so far her shoulders had grown into it.

"She said, 'I'm not crossing for myself.' And she opened her coat, and there you were. A baby. Maybe three weeks old, wrapped in a blanket I have never been able to describe since—not wool, not linen, something that seemed to move slightly, like water remembering itself. You were asleep. You were the quietest infant I ever held, and I held four of my sister's, so I know. You didn't cry. You just looked up at me with these dark eyes, far too old for your face, and I swear on every god whose name got forgotten along with Vessia—you *knew* me. Knew what I was going to choose before I chose it.

"I asked who she was. She said a name I couldn't hold onto—it slid out of my memory like fish out of a net, and I've chased it for twenty-five years and never caught it once. But she said the word *Vessia*, and saying it cost her something visible. Her breath fogged and the fog shaped itself into letters before it faded. Old letters. Letters I'd seen scratched on the underside of the old bridge stone when I was a boy and never told anyone, because telling felt dangerous in a way I couldn't explain.

"She said the valley was safe. That nothing magical could follow you past the boundary, that here you would be invisible to the thing hunting you. She said she couldn't stay herself—said the threshold had already marked her, that she was 'spent coin' and the door took spent coin back. And then she said the part I've never forgiven myself for hearing and agreeing to anyway.

"She said, 'I will pay the ferryman.'

"I told her passage cost three coppers. I was trying to be kind. Trying to make it small, make it ordinary, make it a transaction between strangers instead of whatever it actually was. And she shook her head and said, 'Not to you. To the door.'

"You have to understand, Noor—I grew up on that river. My grandfather ran the crossing before me. There are rules older than kingdoms about thresholds and crossings, and every ferryman learns them drunk and half-believes them sober. One soul may pass the boundary unmarked. But the boundary keeps accounts. Nothing crosses free. Someone pays, or something is owed.

"She laid you in my arms. And she walked to the waterline—to the exact spot where the old door used to stand, before the valley forgot it, the spot where the stones still hum if you're fool enough to kneel and listen—and she spoke to the threshold in that same language Ivet speaks. And the river went still. Not frozen. *Still*. Like a held breath. And I understood, in the way you understand thunder, that she was paying.

"She traded her death for your passage. Do you hear me? Not her life—her death. She gave the threshold the only thing left that was hers to give: the manner of her ending. She bound herself to walk back through that door when it next opened, to be taken by whatever waits on the other side of it, forever, in exchange for one unmarked crossing for her daughter. And the threshold accepted. The river started moving again. And she turned to me and smiled that relieved smile and said, 'Keep her small. Keep her dull. Let no one tell her what she is, and she will live.'

"Then she kissed your forehead and walked upstream along the bank, and I watched her go until the frost haze took her, and I have never known whether she died that winter or whether she's been walking all this time, waiting for a door to open so the debt could collect her. And I have never decided which answer is worse."

* * *

The room was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, Wren's fire popped. Noor hadn't moved. Her folded hands had gone white-knuckled.

"The debt," she said finally. Her voice was steady in the way a frozen river is steady. "You said the boundary keeps accounts. If she paid—"

"She paid for *you*." Doss closed his eyes. Here it was. The last stone. "But I did something that night. After she left. I stood on the bank holding you, and I was frightened—properly frightened, first time since I was a boy—and I wanted insurance. So I spoke to the threshold too. Asked it to hide the crossing. To blur the account, so that if anything came looking, the ledger would show nothing. A smuggling trick. I'd used lesser versions of it for years—asked the river to forget a face, a name, a cargo.

"But this wasn't a face or a cargo. This was a soul's passage. And the threshold agreed—but agreement isn't gift. It said—I heard it, clear as my grandfather's voice—*then a soul stands surety.* Mine. It holds my death the way it held hers. And when the door opens, the account comes due. That's the bargain, girl. That's the price Doss paid. Not that night. Every night since. Every morning I woke up on this side of the grass, I woke up borrowed."

Noor's breath came out shaky. "The hunter. At the ford. Your wound—"

"—is the account knocking early, maybe. Or maybe I'm just an old man who caught a blade. Doesn't matter much which." He opened his eyes and found her face, and what he saw there nearly undid him—grief and fury and love all fighting for the same ground. "Don't you dare look like that. Don't you dare. I made a choice with open eyes and I've had twenty-five years of sunrises I didn't earn. That's not a tragedy, Noor. That's profit."

"You let me believe I was nobody. Born of nobody. You watched me carve bones for other people's dead and never once—"

"Yes." No flinch. He owed her that much, at least—no flinch. "Because her words were the only instruction I had, and I loved you too much to gamble with them. Keep her small. Keep her dull. I told myself it was mercy. Some nights I even believed it." His voice dropped to gravel. "And some nights I sat on the ferry with the rope in my hands and hated myself so thoroughly I could taste it. Because you'd look at me across the water with those eyes of yours—her eyes—and ask why you heard things in the bone that nobody else did, and I'd say *valley folk are plain, we don't hold with such* and watch you fold yourself smaller to fit the lie. I built your cage, girl. I just lined it soft."

Silence. Then Noor reached out and took his hand—those cracked old hands—and held it the way he'd once held her, three weeks old and impossibly light, on a frozen riverbank.

"I'm not angry," she said.

"You should be."

"I am. I'm furious. I'm furious at you and at her and at the threshold and at every grey-faced clerk in whatever court sent the hollow man." Her grip tightened. "But underneath the fury, Doss—underneath all of it—is this: you pulled a stranger's child out of the dark and kept her alive for twenty-five years with nothing but lies and love, and the lies were terrible and the love was not. I forgive you. Not because the debt excuses itself. Because forgiveness is the only coin I have that's worth anything, and I'd rather spend it on you than hoard it."

Doss wept. He was not ashamed of it. An old ferryman crying into a straw pallet, griping his daughter's hand—there were worse ways to be seen, and he'd seen most of them.

"There's one more thing," he managed. "The reason I made you sit down. When the door opens—and it will, girl, I felt it humming in my teeth the moment we crossed the boundary—the account comes due. Mine. The threshold will call, and I mean to answer, and I mean to do it *on my feet*, buying time, the way a ferryman buys time against a flood. But I needed you to know it isn't a price anyone's forcing on you. It's mine. I signed for it. And I'd sign again tonight, fever and all, because the alternative was a world without you in it, and I have seen that world, every night for twenty-five years, and it has no light in it anywhere."

"Doss—"

"Hush now." He squeezed her hand, weak as a bird's. "A man should get to finish his drowning-man speech. I practiced the ending special."

He looked past her, toward the window, where the frost was climbing the glass in patterns like old letters.

"And here's the ending: I spent my whole life thinking I'd crossed that river ten thousand times. Back and forth, back and forth, nothing but water and rope. But it turns out I only ever made one crossing that mattered. Everything else was just... keeping the boat warm." His eyes came back to her, and they were clear, clearer than they'd been in days. "You were the fare, girl. Best fare I ever carried. Now go on. Go sit with your daughter. Learn her language. Learn *your* language. Leave an old man to rest—he's got a door to meet, and he intends to be punctual."

Noor bent and pressed her forehead to his, the way valley folk bid goodnight to their dead, and he laughed—a real laugh, wet and ragged—at the joke of it, being mourned in advance by the only family he'd ever had.

She rose and went to the door. Stopped with her hand on the frame.

"Doss. The woman on the bank. Did she say anything else? Anything at all?"

He searched the frost-fogged vault of twenty-five years, and there—surfacing at last, the way drowned things surface when the ice breaks—one small thing, one thing he'd never been able to keep hold of until this exact hour, when the account was coming due and the ledger lay open:

"She sang," he whispered. "While she walked away. A lullaby. Four notes, rising, then falling like a bow drawn slow. I could never remember it after. But Ivet hums it. I've heard her. Haven't you?"

Noor had gone very still.

"Noor? Girl?"

"That's the song," she said softly, to herself more than to him. "The song Ivet came here humming. The song my bones have been answering my whole life." She turned, and her eyes were bright and terrible and awake. "Doss—my mother's lullaby is the key to the door."

Outside, far off across the frozen fields, something began to sing back.
