## 14. Wren's Bargain

The wound in Doss was worse than Wren let on, and she let it on more than she should have.

She knelt beside his pallet in the back room of her cottage, hands hovering over the gash the hollow man had opened along his ribs, feeling the heat of it like a held breath. Hedge-craft could knit flesh, given time and willow bark and a stubborn patient. What it could not do was give them time. The door in the hillside would close within one turning of the moon, and the road there by ordinary ways ran nine days through country where grey men in tax collectors' faces asked questions at every ford and ferry. They had already spent four of those days hiding in her root cellar while the hunter circled the village like a slow dog scenting blood.

Wren pressed her palms flat over the wound, murmured the small words that were older than any kingdom but younger than Vessia, and felt the flesh reluctantly begin its work. Doss groaned in his sleep. His hand lay open on the blanket, palm up, as if still offering something to someone who wasn't there anymore.

*Twenty-five years of debt,* she thought. *And he never once told her.*

She had known, of course. That was the trouble with hedge-witches who dreamed true: they knew things they wished they didn't. She'd watched Noor Winterbourne grow up across the valley for two decades—a bone-carver who heard echoes no one else could hear—and known exactly what she was looking at, and said nothing, because saying something meant becoming part of it, and part of *it*, and Wren had spent forty years being very careful about what parts of what things she became.

Then a mute child had walked barefoot out of the frost, and careful had stopped mattering.

She rose, joints cracking, and went to find Noor.

* * *

Noor was in the workshop, carving.

That in itself told Wren how bad things were. The woman carved when she was frightened the way other people prayed—steadily, obsessively, chips of bone falling like snow around her feet. Tonight's work was a memorial plaque for Ivet to carry: a strip of sheep bone thin as a wafer, worked with the sigils of safekeeping that Noor had discovered, three days ago at a river ford, could bite a hollow man hard enough to make him flinch.

Ivet sat cross-legged at Noor's feet, watching the blade move. The child had not spoken since the ford—not in the old tongue, not at all—but her eyes followed the knife the way other children watched fire. When Noor set down the finished piece and held it up to the lamplight, Ivet reached up and touched it, then touched Noor's wrist, twice, deliberately.

"She says thank you," Wren said from the doorway.

Noor didn't startle. She'd stopped startling days ago; there was no room left in her for it. "You understand all of it now? Everything she signs?"

"Everything she's shown me." Wren came into the light and lowered herself onto the workbench stool with the honesty of old knees. "Though I'll be honest with you, carver—I understand perhaps a tenth of what I dream. The rest is water."

"Then tell me the tenth that matters." Noor turned the little plaque over in her fingers. "We can't keep running. Doss is dying by inches, the hunter knows roughly where we are, and the moon is eating itself toward new. Tell me there's another way to the door."

"There is." Wren met her eyes. "But it isn't free."

Nothing was. Wren had learned that lesson so long ago she'd forgotten learning it, the way you forget learning your own name. She looked at this woman—this quiet, deliberate woman built entirely out of smallness and craft and grief worn smooth as riverstone—and hated what she was about to say.

"The Folded Road," Wren said. "It runs underneath. Under the valley, under the rivers, under the places the jailers watch. It was made when Vessia was folded away—the seam the tailors left behind, if you like. It comes up a half-day's walk from the door itself. Two days' travel instead of nine, and no grey faces anywhere along it."

Ivet made a sound. Small, sharp, involuntary. Both women looked at her, and the child pressed both hands flat against her own chest and shook her head—not *no, don't go*—Wren read the gesture carefully—not *no*. Something else. Fear, yes, but fear shaped like memory.

"She knows the Road," Wren translated quietly. "Or knows of it. It frightens her."

"It frightens everyone who's used it," Wren went on before Noor could ask. "Because it takes payment."

"Money?"

"Memory."

The word dropped into the workshop like a stone into a well, and the silence after it went down a long way.

* * *

Wren led them down into the cellar, because some things had to be shown rather than told.

At the far end, behind the shelves of preserves and dried herbs, her grandmother had bricked up an archway. It was old brick, mortared with something dark, and even now—even sealed—it breathed cold. Not the cold of frost. The cold of a place where warmth had been *removed*, deliberately, like a tooth.

"This is a mouth," Wren said. "One of hundreds. The Road runs beneath all of it—all of us, everything. My grandmother kept this door. Her grandmother before her. We've always known about the Road, and we've always known its price, because the price is written on the bricks. Look."

She held the lamp close. In the mortar, faint as watermark, letters swam:

**WHAT YOU CARRY IN, YOU LEAVE A PIECE OF.**

"The Road doesn't take coin," Wren said. "Coin is nothing; coin is a story people agreed to tell. It takes weight off the soul. You walk in carrying everything you are, and somewhere between mouth and mouth, the Road chooses—or lets you choose—one thing to set down. And you come out the other side without it. Forever. Not hidden. *Gone.* You won't know what you lost. You won't know there's a hole where it was. You'll just—" she searched for it— "be lighter. Wrong-shaped. Everyone who uses the Folded Road comes out wrong-shaped, and most of them never learn why."

Doss had come down the stairs behind them, gray-faced, one arm pressed to his bandaged side, refusing the bed the way he refused everything now. He stood listening at the bottom step, and Wren saw him do the arithmetic on his face—the same arithmetic she'd done alone in the night.

"Nine days," he rasped. "I don't have nine days. Whatever's in my blood from that cut, it's moving faster than willow bark." He looked at Noor. "Two days, I might live to see the door."

"Doss—" Noor began.

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't spend your softness on me yet, girl. Save it." He turned to Wren. "How does the choosing work? You said the Road *lets* you choose."

"That's the mercy of it, and the cruelty," Wren said. "If you enter blind, it picks for you—takes whatever it judges heaviest. Most people's heaviest thing is their grief, or their love, and they come out the other side hollowed and don't know why. But if you know the price going in—if you name your payment at the threshold—the Road honors the naming. You choose what to leave. Choose something small. Choose something old. Choose something you can afford to be wrong-shaped without."

She looked at Noor and did not look away, because the girl deserved better than a hedged truth.

"But understand this, carver. You're going to a door that opens only for the old blood. You're going to carve the seal off a country with your own hands. Every scrap of who you are is tooling for that work. So choose carefully what you put down—because you may need the piece you leave behind, and you'll never know you needed it."

* * *

They argued about it until the candle burned to a puddle.

Doss wanted to go first, alone, to test the Road—wanted it badly enough that Wren saw the deathwish curled inside the wanting like a worm in fruit. Noor refused him with a flat finality that surprised them both. Ivet sat apart, drawing on the floorboards with a charred stick: the archway, the bricks, and above them a figure walking, and above the figure a line of small shapes spilling out of her like drops—memories leaving a body like blood.

Then, near dawn, Noor went quiet in the way Wren had learned meant the woman was done arguing and beginning to decide.

"I've been taking inventory," Noor said. Her voice was level, her carver's voice, the one she used over delicate work. "Everything I have. Everything I am. Maren teaching me letters on flour sacks. Doss teaching me the river. The first bone I ever carved, and whose it was. The smell of my workshop. The lullaby—"

She stopped.

Wren felt it happen—the whole room felt it happen—a small implosion of attention, like air rushing into a crack.

"What lullaby?" Doss asked gently.

"The one my mother sang." Noor's hands were very still in her lap. "I don't remember her face. I don't remember her voice. I was an infant when she died. But the song stayed. It's the earliest thing I own—before names, before words. Just melody. And when Ivet hummed it on my doorstep the first night, I knew—before I understood anything else—I knew that song came from wherever she came from. From *her*. From my mother's country." Her jaw tightened. "It's proof. It's the only proof I have of her. Of what I am."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "And it's the heaviest thing you carry. Which means if you enter the Road unchosen, it takes exactly that. Or—" and here her heart broke a little, doing what she had to do— "you can name it yourself, and walk through knowing, and come out lighter and wrong-shaped and unable to mourn what you lost, because mourning requires remembering."

Silence again. Ivet crawled across the floorboards to Noor and took her hand, and held it in both of hers, the way you hold something that might be carried away by wind.

"You could pay with something smaller," Doss said hoarsely. "A day. Some gray afternoon. There must be dozens—"

"There aren't." Noor almost smiled. "That's the thing, Doss. I built my life small on purpose. Every day in it is load-bearing. Take one out and the whole roof comes down differently." She looked at the bricked-up archway, breathing its removed cold. "But take the lullaby, and I lose the proof of her. Keep the lullaby, and the Road takes something I haven't chosen—maybe the feel of Ivet's hand, maybe the reason I'm walking at all."

She was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in the tone of a woman setting chisel to grain:

"Ivet was sent to find someone who could hear the door. Someone of the old blood. The song proves the blood—but I've already proven the blood. To myself. To the door, soon enough. I don't need proof anymore. I need passage." She looked at each of them. "My mother gave her life to get me out of a closing country. I think—" her voice caught, steadied— "I think she'd rather I spend the song getting Ivet back into an opening one."

"Noor." Doss's voice cracked like ice on the river.

"It's decided." She stood. "When do we walk?"

Wren closed her eyes briefly. *Grief requires remembering,* she thought. *So this girl is choosing a wound she'll never find to dress.*

"At dusk," Wren said aloud. "The Road likes thresholds, and dusk is the threshold of the day. Rest now. All of you. Eat. Say what needs saying while the saying's still yours." She paused at the stairs. "And Noor—sing it once more before you go. For the house. Houses remember longer than Roads do."

* * *

At dusk they stood before the archway, and Wren pulled the bricks down one by one with her bare hands, as her grandmother had taught her, murmuring the courtesies. Cold rolled out like a tide. Beyond the arch there was no tunnel visible—only a darkness with depth to it, a darkness that seemed politely to wait.

Doss went first, jaw set, naming his toll in a low rough voice that Wren pretended not to hear and would never repeat. Then Ivet, small and straight-backed, signing something swift and fierce at the darkness before stepping through—as if daring it.

Noor stood last at the threshold. She turned back once, and Wren saw her lips moving—singing, silent, the melody Wren had heard through the floorboards the night before, the song older than kingdoms, given freely to the house, to the dark, to whatever remembers.

Then Noor spoke her payment aloud, clearly, like a woman signing a ledger, and stepped through, and the darkness folded shut behind them like a book closing.

Wren stood alone in the cellar with the lamp guttering, and began, brick by brick, to seal the mouth again.

Halfway through, she froze—chisel in hand—because the Road had sent something back up through the seam. Not a sound. Not a word. Just a sensation, the way dreams send weather: the unmistakable feeling of a heavy thing being set down, gently, somewhere far below.

And threaded through it, fading fast, a snatch of melody she didn't recognize—couldn't recognize, would never be able to recognize—

—which was, she understood too late, exactly the point.

The bricks went back in. The cottage settled. And somewhere under the world, on a road folded out of a stolen country, three travelers walked north toward a door in a hillside—one of them lighter now, wrong-shaped, and carrying nothing of the song that had called her home.
