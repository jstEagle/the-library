## 15. What Noor Gave Up

The toll was not collected at the mouth of the Folded Road. Wren had been clear about that, in the way of hedge-witches who have learned that precision is a kindness. The road itself would take its payment somewhere along the way—where, exactly, no one could say, because the road was folded like cloth folded over cloth, and a thing taken from you in one crease might be missed only three creases later, when it was too late to turn back.

"You'll know what it wants," Wren had said, pressing a stub of tallow candle into Noor's hand. "It doesn't take whatever you offer. It takes what you flinch from offering. So don't flinch."

Noor had spent two days trying not to flinch.

She sat now on the cold lip of the descent, where the world above ended and the world below began—a seam in the hillside behind Wren's cottage, a crack in the earth wide enough for one traveler with a pack, narrow enough to make Doss swear under his breath about his shoulders. Ivet stood beside her, small hands wrapped around the bone whistle Noor had carved her on the third night out, the one shaped like a sleeping wren. Doss leaned against the stone, his bandaged arm cradled against his chest, his face grey but his eyes steady. He had insisted on coming this far, and Wren had stopped arguing with him around dawn.

Below them, the Folded Road went down into a dark that was not the dark of any cave. It was the dark of a place turned inside out. Light fell into it and did not scatter; it simply stopped, the way a sentence stops mid-word. Noor could feel the road breathing. Not wind—breathing. A slow tidal pull, in and out, patient as a debt collector who knows he will be paid.

"Say it aloud," Wren had told her. "Whatever you choose. Say it before you go down, so the choice is yours and not the road's."

So Noor said it.

She said it quietly, because some things break if you speak them loudly.

"I give up my mother's lullaby."

* * *

She had known for days. Since the river ford, since the hollow man's grey face had come apart like wet paper against her carved sigils, she had been turning the question over the way she turned a femur in her hands—looking for the flaw, the grain, the place where it would split clean. What could she afford to lose?

Her memories of Maren: the smell of bread at four in the morning, the innkeeper's rough laugh, the night Maren had stood in front of the village council and lied to their faces with such calm that Noor had almost believed the lie herself. No. That was load-bearing. Take that out and the whole house of her came down.

Doss: the ferry, the rope burns on his palms, the way he whistled through the gap in his teeth when he thought no one listened. He was dying by inches already; she would not spend him twice.

The valley itself: the frost patterns on her workshop window, the sound of the river under ice, the particular silence of Cinder Valley at dusk, which was not an absence of sound but a presence of quiet, a thing you could lean against. She reached for that memory and found she could not imagine being Noor Winterbourne without it. It was the wall she had built herself out of.

And then there was the lullaby.

She had never known where it came from. That was the truth she had carried all her life without examining, the way you carry a scar without tracing it. Her earliest memory—the first one, the foundation stone—was of warmth and darkness and a voice humming six notes. Six notes, rising and falling, rising and falling, in a pattern that did not belong to any song sung in Cinder Valley. She had hummed it to herself for twenty-five years without knowing why. She had hummed it while carving bones for the dead, and the dead had seemed to settle easier for it.

And then a barefoot child had appeared at her door on the winter solstice, unafraid of the cold, and hummed those same six notes back to her, and Noor's bones had rung like bells.

That was the cruelty of it. The lullaby was the thread that connected everything—the mother she never knew, the child she had come to love, the country erased from every map, the door she was walking toward. It was the most important thing she owned.

Which meant, she understood finally, that it was exactly what the road would want. And Wren had told her: *it takes what you flinch from offering.* If she offered something small—if she tried to cheat the Folded Road with a memory of a bad winter or a stranger's face—it would reach past her open hand and take the lullaby anyway, and it would take it messily, tearing, the way a dog takes meat. But if she held it out herself, named it, gave it whole—

Then it would be a payment. Clean. Chosen. Hers.

There was a difference between losing a thing and giving it away, and Noor had built her entire life on that difference. She had chosen smallness. She had chosen invisibility. Every year of quiet in Cinder Valley had been a decision, renewed daily, and decisions were the only currency she had ever trusted.

So she would decide this too.

* * *

They went down single file: Ivet first, because the child moved through the fold-places like water finding its level; then Noor; then Doss, refusing all help, muttering that he'd ferried worse cargo than himself across worse crossings. Wren stayed above. Hedge-witches, she said, were rooted things, and the Folded Road ate roots.

The dark closed over them like a hand closing over a coin.

It was not black, exactly. It was *folded*. Noor's eyes reported walls that were sometimes near and sometimes impossibly far, stone that curved in directions stone should not curve, stair-steps that descended and also, somehow, arrived. Sound behaved strangely. Their footsteps arrived before they made them, or after. Once, Ivet laughed at something ahead of them, and Noor heard the laugh twice—once bright and near, once faint and old, as though someone had laughed it years ago and the road had kept it.

"How deep does it go?" she asked Doss, more to hear a voice than for the answer.

"Deeper than the last time," Doss said. "Last time I walked it, I was carrying you." His voice caught on the words, and he steadied it. "Twenty-five years ago. Your mother paid at the third fold. I remember—" He stopped. "I remember watching her forget. It wasn't like sleep. It was like watching snow cover a field. One moment the field was there. Then it was just... white. She looked at me at the end and didn't know who I was, but she smiled anyway, because smiling was the last thing left in her."

Noor said nothing. There was nothing to say that the road was not already listening to.

The first fold took nothing. They knew because Wren had taught them the sign: a stillness, a held breath in the air, a sensation like a page being turned just at the edge of hearing—and then nothing missing. Ivet counted on her fingers afterward, holding up six, then folding one down, then looking up at Noor with a frown, checking. Still six. Still whole.

The second fold passed them by entirely. They felt it shudder somewhere to their left, in a direction that did not exist, and move on, sated or indifferent.

Between the second fold and the third, Noor began to sing.

She did not decide to. Her feet found the rhythm of the descent and her lungs found the rhythm of her feet, and the six notes rose out of her the way they always had—unbidden, older than her, hers and not hers. Rising and falling. Rising and falling. In the folded dark, the notes did strange beautiful things: they echoed forward and backward, they harmonized with themselves across years, and once—for a single suspended moment—Noor heard them in a voice that was not her own. Warmer. Lower. A woman's voice, singing along from somewhere just behind her shoulder.

Doss went very still. "That's her," he whispered. "That's your mother. I'd know—"

He didn't finish. The road exhaled.

The third fold came for them like weather. Noor felt it as a pressure behind her eyes, a softness in the edges of the world, and she understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment—not because the road announced it, but because the lullaby had risen in her throat unasked, and the road had noticed. Things that are noticed get taken.

*I give up my mother's lullaby,* she said again, inside herself this time, the way you sign your name to a document. *I give it freely. Whole. Chosen.*

She sang the six notes one last time—all the way through, slowly, deliberately, the way you read a letter before burning it. She sang them for the woman behind her shoulder. She sang them for the child walking ahead of her, who turned at the sound with her whole face, the way she had on the solstice night, barefoot in the frost.

Then she opened her hands, which held nothing, and let go.

* * *

What does forgetting feel like from the inside?

It feels like nothing. That is the horror of it. There is no pain, no tearing, no moment of loss. There is a lullaby, and then there is a shape where a lullaby used to be, and the shape is so smooth and so perfectly fitted to the space that you cannot tell it is a shape. You cannot miss what is gone, because missing requires remembering, and the road takes the remembering too.

Noor knew this, intellectually, even as it happened. Some observer-part of her—the part that had spent a lifetime listening to bones, cataloguing echoes, noting what was absent—took notes as the snow came down over the field.

*Six notes. Rising and falling. Gone.*

*The humming I do while I work. I will notice it stop, and I will not know why.*

*A woman's voice behind my shoulder. I will not know whose.*

*The child's song at my door. I will remember the child. I will remember the frost. I will remember that the melody meant something. I will not remember what.*

The snow covered the field. The field went white.

And then it was done, and Noor was walking down a folded road in the dark, humming—she caught herself doing it, six notes, rising and falling—and stopped, bewildered, her own throat a stranger's. Why am I humming? she thought. Where did that tune come from? It's pretty. It sounds old. It sounds like something I should know.

She did not know it. She would never know it again. The tune lived in her muscles now, orphaned, a ghost that did not know it was a ghost, haunting a body that no longer recognized the house.

Ivet stopped walking. The child looked up at Noor—at her face, searching it—and Noor saw the exact moment Ivet understood. The child's lips parted. Her small hands came up, hovering, wanting to touch, afraid to touch. And then Ivet began to sing.

Six notes. Rising and falling. The same song, offered back, the way you call to someone across a dark valley so they know they're not alone.

Noor heard it and felt—something. A resonance in her sternum, a grief with no address, tears that arrived without explanation. She knelt on the folded stone and held out her arms and Ivet came into them, and Noor held the child and wept for a loss she could not name, in a place she could not map, for a song she could not remember, and the weeping was the truest thing she had ever done, because it was the only part the road could not take. Grief, it turned out, was stored somewhere deeper than memory. The road could empty the house. It could not dig up the foundations.

"That's the toll paid," Doss said hoarsely, from behind them. He had watched the whole thing with his good hand pressed over his mouth. "Third fold. Same as your mother. Same fold." His voice cracked. "God forgive me, Noor. I walked you down this road once before and I swore I'd never—"

"It's paid," Noor said. Her voice came out flat and strange, emptied of something she couldn't identify. "Don't carry it. You've carried enough."

But Doss's eyes were wet, and he did not answer, and Noor understood dimly, distantly, that she had just said something kind to a man about a wound she could no longer see.

* * *

They walked on. The folds came faster now, or perhaps time folded with the road; there was no telling. Noor noticed absences the way she had once noticed presences—a skill repurposed, a carver reading the grain of her own mind and finding gaps where the knife had passed.

She remembered Maren. She remembered Doss. She remembered the valley, the workshop, the chisels, the frost. She remembered the child arriving, the language, the door, the hunters, the ford, Wren's kitchen smelling of ash and thyme. All of it intact. Only one thread pulled out, and the fabric held—but the fabric knew. She kept reaching for something at the center of herself and finding a smooth white shape instead, and each time, a small animal panic rose in her chest and subsided, rose and subsided, like a tide against a seawall built by someone who no longer remembered building it.

At some point—Ivet's hand in hers, the dark thinning from folded-black to folded-grey—Noor realized she was humming again. Six notes. Rising and falling.

She stopped. Frowned. Started again without meaning to.

"Why do I keep doing that?" she asked the dark.

Ivet squeezed her hand. The child did not answer, because Ivet did not use words unless words were the last door left. Instead she lifted the bone whistle to her lips—the little sleeping wren—and played the six notes back to Noor, softly, the way you keep a fire alive by feeding it one stick at a time.

*Someone has to remember it,* the gesture said. *You gave it away. I can keep it.*

And Noor—who could not remember why the song mattered, who felt only an ache like a tooth pulled wrong, a hollow tuned to a frequency just below hearing—understood the shape of the gift she had given. Somewhere in the waking world, a country slept under glass, and its people slept inside it, and the song that had crossed from a dying mother's throat into a carver's bones and out again into a mute child's whistle was the same song, perhaps, that Vessia had sung to itself before the jailers came. A lullaby for a nation. And she had paid it as a toll, and the road had taken it, and the child had caught it before it hit the ground.

Some things, Noor thought, following the small warm hand down through the folds, are not lost when you give them away. They are only handed on. She had spent her life believing that carving bones for the dead was keeping them. Now she knew better. You keep nothing by holding it. You keep it by passing it through you—out of your hands, into someone else's, still warm.

The road narrowed. The air changed. Below them, impossibly far down and yet somehow close, Noor began to feel a vibration in her teeth, in the bones of her feet, in the long bones of her arms—a deep, slow pulse, like a heart beating under a floor.

Ivet felt it too. The child stopped, went rigid, and pointed down with one small finger, and her eyes were enormous.

The pulse quickened. Or they were getting closer. With folded roads, Noor suspected, those were the same thing.

Ahead, where the grey dark gathered itself into something almost like architecture, there was a light. Not torchlight. Not daylight. An ember-light, red-gold, breathing in and out in time with the pulse—and beneath it, cut into stone that had no business existing this far under the world, a shape.

A lintel. A frame. The suggestion of hinges.

A door.

Doss made a sound behind her—half prayer, half warning—and put his good hand on her shoulder.

"Noor," he said. "Whatever happens next—remember that I—"

But the road took the rest of his sentence the way it took everything, folding it away into the dark behind them, and the ember-light grew, and the door waited.
