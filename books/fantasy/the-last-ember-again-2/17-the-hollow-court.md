## 17. The Hollow Court

They came without horses.

Noor saw them first from the door in the hillside, where she knelt with her chisel against the bone seal, and for a moment she thought the morning had simply grown a stain. Three figures walking up the long meadow slope through the frost-burned grass, unhurried, their coats the grey of ledger paper, their shoes leaving no prints at all. Not monsters. That was the worst of it. She had spent the whole road expecting monsters—teeth, smoke, the shriek of something wrong—and instead they moved like men on their way to collect a debt politely owed, and one of them carried an umbrella against a sky that held no rain.

"They're here," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

Behind her, Wren rose from where she'd been grinding chalk and ash into paste. The hedge-witch's face went carefully, deliberately blank—the look of someone closing shutters before a storm.

"Greycoats," Wren said. "I dreamed them twice this year. In the dreams they were filing papers." She wiped her hands on her apron. "That's not a figure of speech, Noor. That's what they do. They file things away."

Ivet pressed herself against Noor's side, small fingers finding the hem of her coat, and Noor put a hand on the child's head without thinking about it anymore—it had become reflex in these weeks, this hand-on-head, this claiming—and felt the girl's skull humming like a struck bell. The old language poured off her in a whisper too fast to follow, but Noor caught the shape of it: *not-them, not-the-sleepers, the ones-who-count.*

The ones who count.

Doss was propped against the hillside's shoulder where they'd dragged him after the ford, his leg bound in Wren's poultices, his face the grey-white of tallow. He had been fading since the hollow man's blade found him, and everyone knew it, and no one said it. But when he saw the three figures climbing, he laughed—a dry, cracked sound like a gate swinging on rusted hinges.

"Look at that," he said. "They've come all this way. Somebody must owe somebody money."

* * *

Doss

He knew them.

Not their faces—he wasn't sure they had faces so much as arrangements of features, like furniture arranged by someone who had read about faces in a book. But he knew the *manner* of them, because twenty-five years ago, on the night he ferried a dying woman and a squalling infant across the threshold, one of them had stood on the far bank with a lantern and a ledger and asked him, in a voice like a clerk reading terms aloud, whether he understood the price.

He had said yes. He hadn't understood anything. That was the whole trick of it.

Now the three of them arrived at the mouth of the dell and stopped at a precise, courteous distance, close enough to speak, far enough to suggest they would not be staying long. The middle one opened the umbrella anyway. Rain began to fall beneath it, thin and silver, touching no one else.

"Doss Thackeray," the leftmost one said, consulting nothing. There was no book in its hands, but Noor heard the whisper of turning pages somewhere behind the world. "Ferryman. Debtor. You are noted."

"I'm flattered," Doss said. "You remembered me."

"We remember everything," the rightmost one said, and it was not a boast. It was a job description. "That is the difficulty."

Noor stepped forward then, putting herself between the grey ones and the hillside door, and Doss's heart did something painful in his chest, because she stood the way her mother must have stood—chin level, hands loose, the posture of a woman who has already decided what she will refuse. He had seen that woman only once, across black water, holding a child made of noise and hunger, and she had looked at him exactly the way this girl looked at the jailers now.

"You've come about the child," Noor said.

"We have come about the account," said the middle one, folding the umbrella now that its private rain had made its point, whatever the point was. "The child is a discrepancy. Discrepancies are our concern."

"And Vessia?"

"Vessia is a closed file."

Doss watched Noor take that in. Watched her jaw set. He had spent twenty-five years paying interest on the night he saved this girl, and he would have paid it gladly even if it ended here—but he was beginning to suspect, in the way of dying men who get sudden clarity like weather, that it was going to end here, and that there might be a use for that.

* * *

Wren

Wren had faced down landlords, constables, a bishop, and one genuinely alarming goose, and she had learned that power wears many costumes, but she had never seen power dressed as *patience.*

The three grey ones stood in the frost and did not fidget. Did not shift weight. Did not breathe, that she could see. Their eyes—if eyes were what those pale surfaces were—rested on Noor with the mild attention of an assessor valuing a house.

"You are Noor Winterbourne," the leftmost one said. "Carver of bones. Keeper of echoes. Born outside the valley you hid inside. We note your resourcefulness."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It is an entry."

Wren almost laughed. Almost. It was horrible how easy it would be to be charmed by them—to be *managed* by them. They spoke the language of ledgers and fair dealing, and every word was a soft hand on your elbow steering you toward a door you didn't remember agreeing to walk through. She had counseled frightened women in back rooms for thirty years; she knew coercion when it wore a smile, and she knew it better when it wore none at all.

"Here's my entry," she said loudly, stepping up beside Noor. "Three clerks walked into a country that doesn't exist to threaten a bone-carver and a six-year-old. Write that down."

The middle one turned its pale regard on her. "Wren Ashlow. Hedge practitioner. Dreamer of the folded country. You are also noted. You are also forgiven."

"Forgiven?" Wren's spine prickled. "For what?"

"For remembering. Memory is heavy. Carrying it has cost you sleep, standing, and the company of people who think dreams are nonsense. We are prepared to relieve you of it." The creature's voice remained perfectly level, perfectly reasonable, the voice of a man offering to carry your trunk upstairs. "Forget with us, and be light."

And there it was. Wren felt the offer slide into her like a splinter, because gods help her, some tired cellar-room part of her *wanted* it—wanted the dreams gone, the weight gone, the thirty years of half-mad certainty gone. She thought of all the mornings she'd woken weeping from fields under glass she'd never seen.

"No," she said, and the word came out steadier than she felt. "My dreams are the only map I've got. I'll keep my heaviness."

"As you wish. It is noted." A pause, no longer than a heartbeat, and yet somehow the pause contained an entire afternoon of bureaucratic patience. "We will return to you last."

* * *

Ivet

The tall grey ones smelled like the space inside a locked drawer.

Ivet pressed her face against Noor's coat and listened with the listening place behind her sternum, the place Grandmother Sefa had told her was older than her name. The grey ones were speaking in the counting-tongue, the tongue with no songs in it, and underneath their words ran numbers the way a riverbed runs under water. Numbers about doors. Numbers about days. One number kept surfacing, over and over, cold as a coin pulled from snow:

*nine. nine. nine.*

Nine days. The number of days the door would hold once opened, or perhaps the number of days until it sealed forever—Ivet couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like swallowed ice.

Then the middle one crouched.

It crouched the way adults crouch for children, knees folding neatly, face coming level with hers, and its face rearranged itself into kindness the way a shopkeeper arranges fruit. Up close she could see it had no breath. Its kindness had no breath either.

"Little key," it said—in the old tongue, fluent, perfect, and hearing Vessian in that grey mouth was like seeing her grandmother's shawl draped over a fence post. "You are very far from home. Your people are sleeping and cannot miss you, which means no one is grieving, which means no harm has been done. Do you understand? Nothing sad has happened. It has only been *tidied.*"

"Tidied," Ivet whispered.

"A country is a large thing to keep clean. We keep it clean by keeping it closed." The creature extended a hand, palm up, patient as a feeding trough. "Come with us and you can sleep too. Everyone sleeps eventually. You would be with your grandmother. You would dream the green fields. You would feel nothing ever again, and feeling nothing is a kind of rest, isn't it? Isn't it, little key?"

*I am not little,* Ivet thought with a fury that surprised her, that filled her up like a struck match. *I am sent.*

She looked up—not at the grey one, but past it, up at Noor, whose hand still rested on her head, whose fingers had gone rigid as carved bone. And Ivet did the thing Grandmother Sefa had drilled into her in the last bright week before the sending, the one instruction wrapped around her like a second blanket:

*When they offer you rest, say your name. Names are anchors. Say it until your feet grow roots.*

"Ivet," she said. Loud. Into the grey one's face. "Ivet Sefa's-daughter. I am not resting. I am *sent.*"

Something flickered across the arranged features—not anger. Recalculation. The creature rose, smoothing its coat.

"Noted," it said.

* * *

Noor

They made their offer to her at noon, when the frost had gone to wet grass and the door behind her hummed against her spine like a second heartbeat.

"Here is the shape of things," the leftmost one said, and Noor understood suddenly why people called them hollow: not empty, but *shaped around* something missing, the way a mold is shaped around the thing it will never contain. "The door opens. It requires an anchor—a speaker of the blood who remains outside while the passage holds. That anchor will stand between worlds for as long as the door stands, which is to say forever, which is to say you will never again be a woman of any single world. You will hear both sides always. You will belong to neither. You will age slowly and die alone at a hinge."

"We know this frightens you," the rightmost one added, gently.

"It does," Noor admitted.

"Good. Honesty is efficient. Now hear the alternative." The middle one produced, from nowhere, a document. Actual paper. Actual ink, drying even as it appeared, and Noor thought absurdly: *they've printed it fresh; they've done this so many times they print it fresh.* "Leave the child. Walk away. Return to Cinder Valley, to your innkeeper, to your ferryman, to the small warm life you built out of silence. The door seals. The file closes. Vessia stays forgotten—which costs its sleepers nothing, they dream on regardless—and costs you nothing but a memory you've only just acquired. Keep your life, Noor Winterbourne. It is a good life. We have read the entries. Maren's kitchen. Doss's ferry. Bones that echo. You made something rare out of hiding. Why trade it for a doorway?"

And that was the trap, she realized—not the threat, there wasn't even a threat, they didn't need one. The trap was that they were *right* about everything except the conclusion. Her life *was* good. She loved it. Every stone of it she had laid herself, in a valley where magic couldn't reach her, and they were asking her to weigh that warm, hard-won, deliberate smallness against a country of strangers asleep under glass, and the scales were not obviously tipped.

They had designed the scales. That was the point of them.

"You said the sleepers dream on regardless," Noor said slowly. "Dream what?"

"The fields," the leftmost one said. "The harvests. Their families. A complete and pleasant dream of continuity."

"So they dream they are alive."

"They are not suffering."

"That's not what I asked." Her voice came out flat and cold, and she heard in it the tone she used over bones—the tone for the dead, the respectful, unflinching tone. "Do they dream they are alive?"

A pause. The first true pause any of them had taken.

"Yes."

"Then you haven't tidied a country," Noor said. "You've buried it and taught it to dream of daylight. I'm a carver of memorial bones, sir or madam or whichever applies. I have spent my whole life listening to the dead who were *allowed* to be dead—who got mourning and names and graves. What you're describing isn't peace. It's the cruelest grave I've ever heard of, and I won't sign my name to it."

The three grey ones regarded her. Somewhere behind the world, pages turned.

"The offer expires at moonrise," the middle one said. "After that, the account moves to collection."

"Which means?"

The rightmost one smiled. It was the first expression any of them had worn, and it fit them like a borrowed glove. "It means we stop being reasonable."

* * *

Doss

They turned to go. That was the moment.

Doss had been doing arithmetic since dawn, the grim quiet arithmetic of a man whose ledger had one line left in it. Twenty-five years ago the threshold had marked him: *one soul, owed.* He'd assumed it meant his own, someday, taken in his sleep, and he'd made his peace with that across a quarter century of mornings. But debts, he'd learned on the river, were flexible things. A debt was a weight, and weight could be *spent.*

He hauled himself upright against the hillside, and his ruined leg screamed, and he let it scream.

"Oi," he said.

The three grey ones paused. All three, together, like one creature thinking.

"You mentioned my account," Doss said. "Twenty-five years outstanding. One soul, owed to the threshold." He grinned at them, gap-toothed, the grin that had talked tollkeepers out of fees and widows out of despair for fifty years on the water. "I'd like to make a payment plan."

"Doss—" Noor started, and her voice broke clean in half on his name, and he loved her for it, gods, he loved her like the daughter the dying woman had handed him across black water and asked him, with her last spent memory, to *keep safe.*

"Hush now, chick. Grown men are negotiating." He pushed off the hillside and stood, swaying, upright, terrible. "One soul, owed. I'm offering it *now*, in advance, at the door—against whatever you're planning to do at moonrise. Call it a retainer. Call it good faith. You lot love good faith."

The leftmost one tilted its head. "You would spend your soul to buy minutes."

"I'd spend it to buy a *chance*, and minutes are what chances are made of." His legs were shaking. He locked them. "But I want terms in writing, and I want them generous. When the door opens—and it *will* open, I've watched that girl carve, she's got her mother's hands—nobody touches the child. Nobody touches Noor. Nobody touches the witch or the innkeeper coming up that road behind you, because yes, I can see the dust of her cart, and Maren Winterbourne drives like a woman avenging something. Those are my terms."

"You understand," the middle one said, "that a soul spent at a threshold does not go to rest. It goes *into* the hinge. It becomes part of the mechanism. Forever."

Doss considered the meadow, the wet grass, the far hills, the small warm valley he'd poled his ferry across for fifty years. He considered the inn kitchen where Maren would put a cup of something strong in his hands tonight if he lived, and the look on her face if he didn't.

"Fifty years on the water," he said, "and I finally get to be the boat. Where do I sign?"

"No!" Noor was moving toward him, Ivet clinging to her hand, and her face was the face he'd feared and hoped for all at once. "Doss, no—you don't get to—"

"Chick." He caught her hands, his own cold as river stones, and held on. "Twenty-five years ago I rowed a woman and a baby across a black river and I took money for it and I have regretted taking the money every day since, because some things you should do free. Let me do this one free." He bent his forehead to hers, old man to young woman, ferryman to the cargo he'd secretly, hopelessly, permanently adopted. "You hold that door open. You hold it like you mean it. That's all the payment I ever needed."

The grey ones waited, patient as winter.

And behind them, up the long slope, the rattle and jingle of a cart at speed, and a voice Noor knew better than her own heartbeat shouting her name into the wind—

Maren had come.

The middle one unfolded its umbrella against the rain that fell for no one else, and said, with the mild satisfaction of a clerk balancing a column at last:

"The account is accepted."
