## 22. Emberlight

The door in the hillside had been open for one hundred and forty days when the first bread arrived.

Noor counted. She had always counted—carvings finished, winters survived, nights spent listening to bones that could only echo. But now she counted mornings of birdsong that came through the threshold from a country that should not exist, and she counted them the way other people counted blessings: carefully, so as not to lose any.

The bread was dark and dense, studded with seeds Noor did not have names for, and it came wrapped in cloth the color of late frost, carried by a woman named Halva who had walked out of Vessia three weeks after the door opened, blinking at a sun she had last seen as a child before her country was folded away like a letter no one would send.

"This is *lemba*," Halva said, pressing the loaf into Noor's hands. "My grandmother's recipe. She is awake now. She remembers me." Her voice cracked on the word *remembers*, and Noor understood that in Vessia, memory was not a private thing but a communal fire, and for twenty-five years Halva's grandmother had slept without knowing anyone tended hers.

"Then eat," Noor said, tearing the loaf and handing half back. "And tell me everything."

The workshop stood on the threshold itself—not inside the valley, not inside Vessia, but in the seam between, where the air tasted faintly of both woodsmoke and wild thyme. Noor had built it from the ribs of the old ferry shed Doss had kept for forty years, salvaged plank by plank after his funeral pyre on the riverbank. The floor was stone from the Folded Road. The window looked east into Cinder Valley; the door looked west into a country of waking fields.

She carved there every day. And the bones sang.

That was the change no one had predicted, not even Wren with all her hedge-cunning and her dreams full of folded hills. In the valley, bone had only ever echoed—whispers of the dead, repeated and fading, like voices down a well. But here, on the threshold, when Noor set her knife to femur or rib or the small delicate bones of a sparrow, the dead spoke forward instead of back. They said new things. They asked questions. Once, an old shepherd's knucklebone had hummed a tune Noor didn't know until Halva identified it as a Vessian harvest song that hadn't been sung aloud in a generation—and then the whole settlement learned it, because the dead, it turned out, were excellent teachers.

The valley folk called her workshop the Singing House, though they were shy about saying so to her face. Maren called it *that drafty place you live now* and brought stew twice a week regardless, because Maren's love had always been expressed in ladles and disapproval, and neither had diminished.

"You're too thin," Maren said this morning, setting down a pot heavy enough to anchor a boat. "Threshold-keeping is not a diet."

"I eat."

"Eating bread someone hands you at the door is not eating. Sit. Eat. That's an order from your landlady, your almost-mother, and the woman who lied to a village council for you, in ascending order of authority."

Noor sat. The stew was lamb and barley and something green she couldn't name, and it tasted like every winter Maren had ever fed her through, which was to say it tasted like being claimed.

Outside the window, the market was assembling itself in the manner markets do when no one has officially permitted them: blankets on the ground, a boy selling honey, two Vessian exiles arguing cheerfully over the price of wool with a valley shepherd who understood perhaps one word in five and was enjoying himself immensely. A month ago the council had formally debated whether the door should be barred. Last week the same council had voted to build a proper road to it. Fear, Noor had learned, did not die so much as get outbid—by bread, by songs, by the simple gravitational fact that people are more interesting than their rumors.

Doss would have loved it. That thought arrived every day now, less a wound than a warmth, the way a scar stops hurting and starts meaning. He had spent his owed soul at the door of the Hollow Court, buying minutes with the only coin he'd ever held, and those minutes had been enough. She had carved his memorial herself—a ferry oar's blade of pale bone, hung above the threshold where everyone who passed must touch it, because that was how he would have wanted it: useful, touched, slightly in the way.

Sometimes, when the wind came off the Folded Road, the oar creaked in a rhythm suspiciously like his laugh.

* * *

Ivet arrived on the afternoon of the hundred-and-fortieth day, running down the hillside path with the particular velocity of a child who has news and no patience whatsoever.

She was seven now by valley reckoning, eight by her own, and she had grown half a head taller since winter, which Noor found vaguely offensive. Her hair was still that impossible white-gold, her feet still refused shoes on principle, and her language—the old tongue, the tongue older than kingdoms—had become the second language of the threshold, spoken in fragments by traders and children and at least one sheepdog who responded to commands in Vessian and pretended not to understand valley speech.

"*Emma!*" Ivet skidded into the workshop, breathless, holding a bundle of papers against her chest like contraband. "*Emma*, you will not believe—"

"No running in the Singing House."

"You can't hear running. Bones don't have ears." She dumped the papers on the workbench anyway, scattering bone dust, and Noor let it go because some crimes were committed out of joy and deserved pardons.

*Ivet* meant *ember* in the old tongue. Noor had learned that in the first terrible week, before she knew anything else—that the child's name was a small fire carried through darkness, and that whoever named her had known exactly what they were doing. Now the name fit differently. Not a spark fleeing the dark, but a coal carried home to wake the hearth.

"Look," Ivet said, unrolling the papers. Drawings covered them—Ivet drew the way she spoke, in bursts, all elbows and certainty. "The eastern provinces woke first. All of them, all at once, like—" she made a sound like a wave, mouth and hands together, "—like breathing in. Grandmother Halva's mother says the sleeping wasn't sleep, it was *holding*. Everyone holding the country closed from the inside so it wouldn't come apart. And now they've stopped holding, and—" she flipped to a fresh sheet, "—the glass fields melted. The ones under the glass. Crops grew in a single week. Wheat that's been mid-breath for twenty-five years just... finished exhaling."

Noor studied the drawings. Fields. A river with fish drawn far too large. A crowd of small stick figures with their arms up, which Ivet insisted was not celebration but *the stretching*, the whole nation stretching after a long sleep, and Noor felt her throat tighten at the image despite its simplicity—or because of it.

"And the jailers?" she asked quietly.

Ivet's face did the thing it did when she was deciding how much truth a grown person could carry. She was very good at that calculation. It was, Noor suspected, the inheritance of a child sent alone across a folded world.

"Gone," Ivet said finally. "Or—no. Not gone. *Unemployed.* Wren's word. Their law said a keeper outranks a court, and you—" she pointed at Noor with the fierce directness of a child stating physics, "—you are the keeper. So they went away to file complaints somewhere. Wren saw it in a dream. She says they complained for nine days."

Noor laughed. It still surprised her, laughing—how much room there was for it now, how it didn't have to be small.

"There's more," Ivet said, and her voice changed, dropped into the register she used for important things, the one that made the hanging bones on the walls chime softly in sympathy. "The Council of Waking—they're the ones who sent me, remember? Before. When I was the key?" She waited for Noor's nod. "They want to meet you. Formally. In Vessia. There's a ceremony. There are *robes*." She wrinkled her nose at the robes. "But Emma—you don't have to go. I told them keepers don't leave their doors. I told them the door IS you now, sort of, and if you went too far the hinge would..." she wobbled her hand, "...wobble."

"Did they accept that?"

"They're *deliberating.*" Ivet pronounced it with the contempt of youth for bureaucracy. "So I said I'd come ask you myself, because deliberating is what people do when they haven't met you. If they met you they'd stop." She said it without flattery, as fact, and Noor had to turn to the window and pretend to check the weather so her face could do what it needed to do privately.

The child who had once been a burden-shaped duty, then a daughter-shaped duty, and then simply a daughter—she had crossed a folded country, testified before a waking nation, negotiated with a council, and walked back through the door to bring the question home. To *this* home. Because whatever Vessia was becoming, Ivet had decided where her center of gravity lay, and it was a workshop on a threshold, next to a carver who smelled of bone dust and Maren's stew.

"When?" Noor managed.

"Midsummer. But I told them you'd probably say yes to the meeting part and no to the robes."

"The robes are non-negotiable?"

"Everything's negotiable. That's what you taught me."

Noor had taught her that during the Hollow Court, actually, standing in front of grey administrators with reasonable voices, refusing to be reasonable. She hadn't known she was teaching. Apparently the best lessons were the ones you gave while terrified.

* * *

Evening came slowly in spring, and the threshold held the light longer than either side of it, as if the seam between worlds kept its own hours. Noor lit the lamps and worked while Ivet sprawled on the bench amid shavings, telling stories in the loose associative way of children and prophets: the man in Vessia who remembered being a door himself, the library where all the erased maps had ended up filed under *weather*, the dog.

"The dog again?"

"The dog is IMPORTANT. He guarded the western door for twenty-five years. He never slept. Dogs don't hold countries closed, Emma, he just *chose* to stay awake, and now there's a statue—"

"A statue of a dog."

"A statue of a GOOD dog. There's a difference." Ivet rolled onto her back. "Wren's coming tomorrow. She's bringing her apprentice. Did I tell you? She took an apprentice. A valley girl who hears echoes. Wren says echoes are just songs waiting for a threshold."

Noor's knife paused over the rib bone in her vise. An apprentice. A valley-born child who heard the dead—except now, with the door open, maybe the hearing would grow toward singing the way Noor's had, the way everything touched by the threshold seemed to. Lineage. She had spent twenty-five years believing she was an accident, a smuggled exception, a mistake the valley tolerated. Now there were apprentices. There was a school forming around her workbench without her permission, the way markets formed, the way families formed.

"What's her name?" Noor asked.

"Bell. She's scared of it. Of the hearing. Like you were." Ivet said it plainly, watching Noor with those too-old eyes that had gotten, over a hundred and forty days, appropriately childish again. "I told her you'd know what to say."

"Did you?"

"You always do. You said the dead aren't a weight, they're a chorus, and you just have to learn your part." Ivet yawned enormously. "Did you make that up?"

"On the spot. Standing in a frozen doorway with a barefoot child humming a song I didn't know I knew."

"It worked."

It had. Everything had worked, eventually, in the way that rivers work—not straight, not clean, but arriving. Doss's debt paid. Maren's lie forgiven by time. Wren's toll taken and grieved—the lullaby was still gone, a smooth absence in Noor's memory like a tooth lost long ago, and some evenings she caught herself reaching for it out of habit. But Ivet hummed it sometimes without knowing, a melody with no words, and Noor had decided that this was better than remembering: the song had escaped her keeping and gone free, sung by a child who was herself an ember carried through the dark. You could not lose a thing that had become everyone's.

The lamp flickered. Outside, the evening market was packing up, and someone—Halva, probably—was teaching the honey boy's little sister a round song, the melody threading through the doorway from the valley side and meeting, halfway across the threshold, a harmony drifting in from the Vessian fields. The two melodies shouldn't have fit. They fit anyway. That was the whole discovery of the age, Noor thought: things fit anyway, if you stood in the middle and held the space open.

"Emma?" Ivet's voice was going sleepy, thick at the edges. "What happens to doors that aren't needed anymore?"

Noor set down her knife. On the bench, the rib bone she'd been carving—a commission from a valley family, their grandfather's voice preserved forward now instead of backward—waited half-finished, its first sigil already glowing faintly, warming like a banked coal.

"Doors?" she said. "Doors become homes. Ask any cat."

Ivet laughed, and the sound rang the hanging bones, and the bones sang back, and somewhere beyond the western door a whole country breathed easy in its first true spring in twenty-five years.

But Noor stayed at the workbench a while after the child slept, knife moving, listening. Because the bones had brought her something tonight—new words, forward-speaking, from a direction she didn't recognize. Not the valley's dead. Not Vessia's sleeping. Something farther, older, patient, gathering at the edge of what she could hear, like a knock.

Like a knock in the frost, once, a lifetime ago.

Noor smiled, reached for a fresh bone, and began to carve.
