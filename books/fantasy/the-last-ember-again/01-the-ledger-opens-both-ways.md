## 1. The Ledger Opens Both Ways

The bottled thunderstorm had been restless all morning.

Paloma Ashgrove noticed it the way a shepherd notices weather—without looking up from his work. The jar sat on its velvet cradle at the back of the Gilded Ledger's vault, third shelf from the floor, between a saint's left kneecap (provenance disputed) and a crate of forty-nine apologies owed to a dead man. Inside the glass, cloud churned. Lightning flicked against the cork like something knocking politely to be let out.

"You'll have your turn," he told it, and turned the page.

The vault of the Gilded Ledger was not a place for gold. Gold was vulgar, portable, and easily stolen by anyone with a crowbar and poor judgment. What the Ledger kept was stranger: things that could not be owned outright, only held in trust. A debt of one honest tear, still accruing interest. A shadow that had outlived its owner and paid rent in warmth. A promise, folded eight times, that no one dared unfold because the last person who unfolded a promise had spent three days speaking only in questions.

Paloma kept the books on all of them. He had done so for eleven years, since the night Master Corvane found him half-frozen in the guild's doorway, seventeen years old and nameless as wet paper, and asked him one question: *"What do you have that cannot be taken?"*

"Nothing," Paloma had said.

"Good," said Corvane. "Then nothing can be stolen from you."

It had seemed like wisdom then. It had seemed like wisdom for eleven years.

He worked by candlelight because the vault did not trust lamplight—oil came from somewhere else, somewhere outside, and the inventory preferred light that was born where it would die. His pen scratched. The entries marched down the page in his small, exact hand:

*Item 212. One bottled thunderstorm, minor deity's temper tantrum, vintage unknown. Status: agitated. Do not shake. Do not sing near.*

*Item 213. Debt of one honest tear, owed by the Duchy of Marrow to a widow who has refused to cry. Interest accrues seasonally. Widow is winning.*

*Item 214. Forty-nine apologies, unsent, addressed to "Tomas." Owner deceased; apologies now self-aware and muttering. Recommend continued storage away from mirrors.*

Item 215 was new. It had come in yesterday, and Paloma had been avoiding it the way he avoided all things that arrived without paperwork.

A key. Small, brass, warm to the touch. No tag. No chain of custody. No signature in the receiving book. It had simply appeared on his desk overnight, which should have been impossible—the vault locked like a jealous heart—and when he picked it up, the metal had felt less like an object than like an invitation.

He wrote: *Item 215. Key, brass, unclaimed. Origin:* —and stopped.

His pen hovered over the line. Then, without any decision on his part, the word *home* appeared beneath his nib, in ink that dried blacker than the rest.

Paloma set the pen down carefully, the way one sets down a snake that has not yet decided to bite.

* * *

"Wren's been at the till again."

Sable's voice came through the vault door along with the smell of frying onions, which meant dinner, which meant the day was ending whether Paloma's columns balanced or not. He capped the ink, slid Item 215 into the drawer where he kept problems he wasn't ready to name, and went up into the guildhouse proper.

The Gilded Ledger guildhouse had been a counting house once, then a chapel, then a counting house again, and it wore all three histories like layers of paint. Candelabras hung where altars had been. The confessionals were now private booths for clients who wished to discuss their debts with dignity. And everywhere, everywhere, were shelves—shelves of ledgers going back two hundred years, each spine stamped with the guild's seal: a hand holding a coin, and above the coin, a single star.

Wren was behind the front desk, feet up, shelling pistachios into a customer's coin purse.

"That's not yours," Paloma said.

"It's nobody's. That's the beauty of it." She was nineteen and quick as a struck match, all elbows and grin, dark hair cut short in defiance of every fashion the city had offered her. "Man left it here Tuesday. I'm just keeping it warm."

"You're seasoning his coins with pistachio salt."

"He'll thank me. Salt preserves value." She tipped the last shells onto the desk and swung her legs down. "Also, someone paid for a retrieval job today and I couldn't make change."

"Paid how?"

Wren hesitated. Wren never hesitated. She was the fastest tongue in the guild, faster even than Sable, and hesitation on her face was like a crack in porcelain—you noticed it precisely because it didn't belong.

"He wouldn't say what it was. Just wrapped it up." She reached under the desk and brought out a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord. "Said it was for the keeper of the books. Said—" she stopped, and this time the hesitation had teeth, "—said you'd know when to open it."

"Did he give a name?"

"No. But he paid the door-warden too, so he got past Sable's questions somehow." Wren turned the parcel over in her hands before giving it up, which for Wren was practically a eulogy. "It's heavy for its size."

Everything about this was wrong. Clients paid in coin, in favors, occasionally in goods appraised and entered into inventory. They did not pay in unlabeled parcels addressed to Paloma personally, because Paloma did not exist publicly. In twenty-two years he had never appeared in a guild registry, never signed a contract under his own hand, never let his face be sketched by a portraitist or his name be spoken above a whisper. Master Corvane had taught him that too: *The man who keeps the books must not appear in them.*

He took the parcel upstairs to his room, and unwrapped it there, alone.

Inside was a book.

It was old—not antique-old but *weathered*-old, the way stone gets old, worn smooth by hands and time together. Leather gone soft as skin. No title on the cover. When he opened it, the pages smelled of cold ash, and the handwriting inside was fine, precise, and a century out of date by the style of its lettering.

It was a book of prophecy. He recognized the form; the guild had handled three of them over the years, and all three had been frauds, cleverly aged, sold to frightened rich people. But frauds were built to impress. This book was built to *last*, and it did not try to impress him at all. The early pages were mundane—harvest predictions, the fall of a bridge, the price of wool in years whose kings were dust. Small, verified, dull truths. The kind of record a real prophecy leaves behind, the way a real river leaves silt.

He turned pages. Winter records. A fire in the tanneries. Someone named Odile marrying twice.

Then, near the end, the handwriting changed. Larger now. Hurried. As though the writer had seen something that made patience impossible.

*When the season turns and the Court below hungers,* the entry began, *the soul pawned in trust shall be called due, and the Court shall consume what was sold a hundred years ago.*

Paloma read it twice. Souls pawning was old business; the Ember Court beneath Mount Cendre traded in them, if half the stories were true, and the guild had strict policy never to broker with the mountain. Nothing here concerned him.

Then he saw the date at the head of the entry.

Tonight's date. Not approximately. Exactly. The day, the month—even the year, written a century ago in faded iron-gall ink.

His first thought, the careful thought, the thought that had kept him alive and invisible for eleven years, was: *coincidence of calendar reform.* Calendars changed. Kings reset years. There were a dozen innocent explanations and he began cataloguing them the way he catalogued everything, neatly, in columns—

And then he saw the second line.

*The keeper of the ledger, who believes himself unseen, will come for her. Let him. His name was always ours to write.*

Beneath it, in the same century-old hand, a single word:

***Paloma.***

* * *

He did not remember sitting down. He was on the floor with his back against the bed, the book open in his lap, and the candle had guttered low, and outside the window Vesper's district bell rang the hour—nine o'clock—and the sound of it passed through him like cold water through cloth.

His name. Inked a hundred years ago. In a book that had been wrapped and delivered by a stranger who knew exactly which shelf of exactly which vault to leave a brass key that wrote its own provenance.

Paloma Ashgrove had spent his whole adult life being no one. It was not modesty; it was architecture. Every wall of his existence had been built deliberately—the borrowed surname, the unremarkable face, the refusal of titles and credit and witness. When clients asked who kept the Ledgers, Sable said *a servant of the guild* and left it there. When the tax assessors came, they assessed a guild with staff and no staff roster worth reading. He had made himself into a blank page, and he had believed—he had *needed to believe*—that the blankness was his own work.

But blank pages get written on. That was the first law of his trade.

And someone, a century before he was born, had already written on his.

He thought of the silent god. Everyone in the district thought of Vesper eventually; his shrine stood at the crossroads by the guildhouse, a plain stone post worn glossy by touching hands, and his silence was famous. Other gods answered prayers with portents and demands. Vesper answered nothing. Priests of louder gods called him dead, or cowardly, or a metaphor. The guild's oldest members touched the stone anyway, out of habit or hope, and Paloma—who had no habit and claimed no hope—touched it too, every morning, without knowing why. It was the one ritual he'd never audited.

*A god who does not speak,* Corvane had said once, drunk enough to be honest, *is either absent or listening. Pray you never learn which.*

Paloma looked at the prophecy book. Looked at the word *Paloma* sitting in century-dead ink like a trap already sprung.

*Who believes himself unseen.*

His invisibility had always felt like a skill. Now it felt like a cage built by someone else, sized exactly to him, and the chill that moved through him was not fear of the Court below the mountain. It was the colder fear of a careful man discovering that his care had been anticipated—that every wall he built might have been drawn on a blueprint he'd never been shown.

He was still on the floor when the screaming started downstairs.

Not Wren's scream. Older. Rougher. Sable's voice, cracked wide open, shouting something he'd never heard the level-headed door-warden shout in eleven years:

*"Where did she learn that song? WHO TAUGHT HER THAT SONG?"*

Paloma closed the prophecy book, tucked it into his coat against his heart, where it lay warm as a coal, and went down to find out what his family had just remembered.

Because he already knew, in the way the vault had known before him, in the way the ink had known: whatever came next had started a hundred years ago tonight.

And tonight was almost over.
