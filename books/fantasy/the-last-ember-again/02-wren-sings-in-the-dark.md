## 2. Wren Sings in the Dark

The thing about being raised by thieves is that nobody ever teaches you the rules, because the first rule is that there are no rules. Wren had learned this at six years old, standing on a chair in the Gilded Ledger's kitchen, stealing honey cakes off a cooling rack while three grown criminals watched her and pretended not to.

Now she was seventeen, and she was still on the chair. Metaphorically. Mostly.

"Pass me the wax," she said, mouth full of pins. She was restringing a lockpick set into the lining of her sleeve, which was technically against guild policy and practically a Tuesday.

Marisol, who was mending armor by the fire and pretending she wasn't listening to everything in the room the way knights always did even after they'd stopped being knights, slid the tin across the table without looking up. "You know Paloma counts those."

"Paloma counts everything. That's his whole personality." Wren bit the thread, tied it off, flexed her wrist. The picks sat flush against her skin like they'd grown there. "He counted my sneezes once. I'm not joking. Fourteen. He wrote it down."

"He wrote down *fourteen sneezes*?"

"In the ledger. Under 'miscellaneous liabilities.'" Wren grinned around another pin. "I asked him what column depreciation went in and he actually thought about it. For like ten seconds. I saw him do math about my nose."

That got a laugh out of Marisol—short, rusty, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled since her oath broke—but a real one. Wren collected Marisol's laughs the way other people collected coins. They were rare currency.

Tetch was sprawled across two chairs by the window with her boots up, sketching. Tetch was always sketching. Maps mostly, though lately her maps had started including things like *here be a door that hates you* and *this street moves on Thursdays*, which everyone agreed was either brilliant or a cry for help. "If he hears you talking about him," Tetch said without looking up from her inkwork, "he'll add it to your file."

"I don't have a file."

"You have four files. I've seen them. One of them is just titled *Wren: Escalating*."

"*Escalating?*"

"It's a very thick folder," Tetch said solemnly. "There's a subfolder called *Incidents Involving Roofs*."

"There's a subfolder—" Wren pointed the needle at her like an accusation. "The roof incident was ONE time and the gutter was LOAD-BEARING—"

"The gutter was not load-bearing. The gutter was decorative. You are lucky to be alive, is what you are."

"I'm lucky in general," Wren said, and hopped off the chair, and did a little spin to show off the sleeve. "Look. Look at this. Invisible. Silent. A whisper in fabric form. If I wanted I could steal the teeth out of your head right now and you'd never know until you smiled."

"I'd know," Marisol said mildly. "I'd hear you breathing."

"You would NOT—"

"Wren." It was Paloma's voice from the doorway, and both women went quiet in that particular way people go quiet when the auditor walks in. He stood there with a lamp in one hand and a ledger under his arm, because of course he did, because Paloma Ashgrove slept with a ledger under his pillow the way other children slept with knives. His eyes moved over the room the way they always did: inventory first, people second, Wren third but longest. "What are we escalating tonight?"

"Nothing," said Wren.

"Theft of dental equipment," said Tetch.

"A threat to my person," said Marisol.

Paloma wrote all three of these things down. He actually did. He had a little book for this—a pocket ledger, a ledger of ledgers—and Wren watched him make three neat entries and felt something in her chest go warm and stupid, because that was the thing about Paloma nobody said out loud: he counted everything because counting was how he loved people. Every sneeze, every crime, every joke at his expense, entered and preserved. Nobody else in the world had ever bothered to keep a record of her. The guild had found her in a doorway at four years old with empty pockets and no name anyone could pronounce, and Paloma had given her a name by writing it down. *Wren.* Small bird. Loud song. Asset, not liability.

Mostly asset.

"Actually," Wren said, and here she should have stopped, and she knew even as she opened her mouth that she should have stopped, "actually, I learned something today. Well—not learned. Remembered? Discovered?" She frowned at her own sentence. "There's a song."

Paloma's pen stopped moving.

That was the thing about Paloma's pen. It never stopped moving. It was a famous fact of the guild, a law of nature, like gravity or Tetch's inability to draw a straight line. The pen stopping was an event. People marked time by it. *The night the pen stopped,* old Sefa called it, whenever anything strange happened, and then she'd knock wood twice and spit over her shoulder, because Sefa was Vesperan and Vesperans were weird about omens.

"There's a song," Wren repeated, and hummed it.

She didn't think about it. That was the whole point of what she was trying to explain—that she didn't have to think about it, that the melody lived somewhere below memory, in the basement of her, in the part of her that had existed before the guild, before the doorway, before the name. It came out of her the way breathing came out of her. Six notes up, four notes down, a long held tone at the end that felt less like singing and more like *tending*, like keeping a fire fed.

She hummed maybe eight bars before she noticed the room had changed.

Marisol had gone white. Not pale—white, the specific bloodless white of someone seeing a ghost through a window. Her hand had gone to where her sword would be if she still carried one, which she didn't, which meant her hand closed on nothing, which somehow made it worse.

Tetch had dropped her pen. Tetch, who once mapped a city that ate its own streets and had come back with nothing worse than a nervous twitch in her left eye, had dropped her pen, and was looking at Wren like Wren had just spoken in a voice not her own.

And Paloma—

Paloma had set down the ledger. Both hands flat on the table. He was very still, the way he got still when numbers refused to balance, and his face was doing something Wren had never seen it do, which was nothing at all. Paloma's face was always doing something. Concern, mostly, permanently, professionally. This was different. This was a man watching a door open onto a room he'd hoped didn't exist.

"What," Wren said. "What's wrong. What did I—it's just a song. It's a nice song. It's a lullaby kind of song. Where did I even learn it, that's the weird part, I've never—"

"Where did you hear it?" Paloma asked. His voice was careful. Controlled. The voice of a man carrying something heavy across ice.

"That's the thing! Nowhere. I've never heard it anywhere. It's just *in* me. It's been in me forever. I used to hum it when I couldn't sleep and nobody ever said anything, so I figured it was mine, you know? Like a thing you make up as a kid and keep. Except—" she paused, and the pause itself frightened her, "—except I was humming it in the market yesterday, near the tinker stalls, and this old woman grabbed my arm. Hard. And she looked at me and she said, *child, where did you hear the Court's anthem*, and when I said what court, she let go of me like I was hot iron and left her own purchases sitting on the table. Full basket. Just walked away. So I figured she was mad. Old people get mad. But then tonight I hummed it here and—"

She turned to look at them, all three of them, and the warmth curdled in her chest and went cold.

"—and you're all looking at me like the old woman looked at me."

Nobody answered for a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, Sefa was pacing, which Sefa did when the god was restless, except the god wasn't supposed to be restless, the god was supposed to be silent, the god had been silent for a hundred years, that was the whole arrangement.

"Sing it again," Paloma said quietly.

"No."

"Wren—"

"No, because now I know it's not mine, and it feels different in my mouth knowing that. It feels like wearing someone else's coat." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Tell me what it is."

Paloma looked at Marisol. Marisol looked at the floor. It was Tetch who spoke, in the flat careful voice she used for terrain that could kill you.

"When I was younger," she said, "before the guild bought my debts, I took a commission. A mapping commission. Underground. There's a mountain south of here—Mount Cendre—and beneath it there's a place. A court. A whole court, underground, lit by banked fires, and they trade in things that shouldn't be tradable. Names. Memories. Years. Souls." She said the last word lightly, the way you say a word you're trying not to give weight to, and failed. "They have an anthem. Everyone who's ever been down there comes back knowing it. It gets into you. Into your bones. Some people say it's how the Court marks what belongs to it."

"And I know it," Wren said. "By heart. Without ever going anywhere."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Tetch opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Paloma, and what passed between them was one of those conversations that happens entirely in eyebrow movements, and Wren hated it, hated being on the outside of it, hated most of all the growing suspicion that whatever they weren't saying was about *her*.

"Because," Paloma said finally, and his voice cracked slightly on the word, which it never did, which it had maybe done twice in the eleven years Wren had known him, "there's a client. Came in yesterday. Paid his debt in an unusual currency—an old book. A prophecy book. Printed a century ago, hand-annotated, provenance unclear. I've been auditing it tonight. Cross-referencing dates. And there's a passage, Wren, and I need you to listen very carefully, and I need you to understand that I have read this passage forty times tonight trying to find a way that it means something else."

The room got very small. Wren became aware of the fire, of the ticking of it, of Marisol's hand still hovering at her absent sword, of her own heartbeat, which had picked up like a pickpocket sensing a watchman.

"Read it," she said.

"At the turning of the season," Paloma recited, and his voice was steady now, terrible and steady, the way it was steady when he read out debts people couldn't pay, "the Court beneath Cendre shall consume that soul long pawned and held in trust, and the consumption shall fall upon the hundredth year, and the mark of the stolen one shall be this: she shall carry the anthem untaught, and sing it in the dark, and know not whence it came."

Silence. Total, ringing silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your eardrums, that makes the fire sound loud.

"My name isn't in it," Wren said. "It doesn't say Wren."

"No."

"So it might not be me. Lots of people hum songs. Statistically—"

"It also says," Paloma continued, and now his composure was visibly costing him, visibly, like watching a man hold a door shut against wind, "that the soul was pawned by its birth parents in the year the book was printed. One hundred years ago. Held in trust. Maturing at season's turn." He swallowed. "Wren, the guild's oldest ledger—the founding ledger, the one nobody opens, the one in the vault under Sefa's floor—has a page from that same year. An entry. A pawn transaction. I checked the date against your arrival at our door. Against the age you were. Against—"

"Against what," Wren whispered. "Against what, Paloma."

"Against the fact that you have no birth record. No family name. Nothing before the doorway. Which every orphan has *something* of, Wren, some scrap, some paper, some story, and you have never had any, and I told myself for eleven years that was poverty, that was war, that was the world being cruel in its ordinary way." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and Wren realized with a distant shock that she had never once seen Paloma Ashgrove cry, not at funerals, not at anything, and that she was seeing it now. "But it wasn't ordinary cruelty. It was arithmetic. Someone did sums, and the sums came due tonight, and the answer is you."

The fire popped. Wren flinched.

"That's not funny," she said.

"It's not a joke."

"It's not FUNNY, Paloma, you don't get to—you don't get to stand there with your little books and tell me I'm—I'm a *line item*. That I'm *inventory*. That some court under a mountain owns—" her voice climbed, cracked, climbed higher, "—owns *me*. That I've been theirs since before I could walk. That the song isn't mine, it's a *leash*. That everything—" and here her voice did break, fully, and she hated it, hated crying in front of them, hated the way Marisol stood up like she was going to cross the room, "—everything I am is collateral. Is that it? Is that what the ledger says? That I'm *maturing*? Like interest? Like I'm a wine or a bond or a—"

"Wren."

"Don't." She backed up a step. "Don't you *Wren* me in that voice. Don't look at me like I'm already gone. I'm standing RIGHT HERE. I'm seventeen years old and I can pick a lock in the dark and I stole the Lord Provost's signet ring off his HAND at his own daughter's wedding and nobody, NOBODY, owns me, do you hear me? Not a court, not a queen, not a prophecy, not a *song*—"

She stopped. Because Paloma had crossed the room—fast, for him, faster than she'd ever seen him move—and taken her by the shoulders, gently, the way you handle something you're afraid will shatter, and pulled her against his chest, and she realized he was shaking.

Paloma never shook. Paloma was the steadiest person alive. Paloma counted sneezes.

"They can't have you," he said into her hair, and it wasn't loud, it wasn't dramatic, it was quiet and absolute, like a number that had finally balanced. "Do you hear me? Whatever the book says. Whatever the ledger says. Whatever a century-old contract says. Ledgers can be amended. Debts can be disputed. Contracts can be broken. I have spent my whole life learning every way a record can lie and every way it can be made true again, and I am telling you, little sister, I am telling you as the keeper of the Gilded Ledger: they made an error. They left us the receipt. And I am going to buy you back."

"You can't buy back a soul," Wren said into his chest, muffled, furious, terrified.

"Then I'll steal it," Paloma said. "We're thieves. It's what we're for."

And Wren laughed—wet, cracked, ugly, real—because that was the most Paloma thing anyone had ever said, and because underneath the terror, underneath the cold weight of the anthem sitting in her lungs like borrowed smoke, some stubborn coal in her had caught light. *We're thieves. It's what we're for.*

Marisol was already buckling on armor she'd sworn off. Tetch was pulling maps out of her satchel, muttering about roads that move. Somewhere above them, Sefa had stopped pacing, and the silence from upstairs had the quality of a god leaning closer to listen.

"We leave when?" Wren asked.

"Tomorrow," Paloma said. "Tonight we plan. Tonight you teach me the song."

"Why?"

"Because," Paloma said, and his face had gone carefully blank again, auditor-blank, the blankness he wore when he'd found something in the numbers he wasn't ready to say out loud, "if it's a leash, I want to know exactly how long the chain is. And because—" he hesitated, and the hesitation lasted three full seconds, and Wren would spend years wondering what he'd decided not to say next, "—because I think I've heard it too. Once. A long time ago. From someone who isn't here anymore."

Wren looked up at him. "Who?"

"Tomorrow," Paloma said. "Tonight, we plan."

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, past the hour when even thieves sleep—Wren lay in her bunk in the loft above the kitchen and hummed the anthem softly into her pillow, testing it, tasting it, trying to feel the shape of the leash.

Six notes up. Four notes down. The long held tone at the end, like tending a fire.

And in the dark, so faint she almost missed it, so faint it might have been the settling of the house or the tick of cooling embers or her own heart playing tricks, something hummed back.

It hummed the harmony.

Wren sat bolt upright in the dark, heart slamming, and did not scream, because screaming was for amateurs. She listened for a full minute. The house was silent. The house was only a house.

But when she finally lay back down, she did not hum again.

And downstairs, in the office, Paloma sat alone with the prophecy book open under lamplight, and beside it the founding ledger, and between them a single sheet of fresh paper on which he had written one line in his smallest, neatest hand:

*Debtors' muster at dawn. Call in everything. We go beneath the mountain.*

He underlined *everything* twice, blew out the lamp, and in the dark said—to no one, to the silence, to whoever had once taught a little girl a song she was never supposed to remember—

"I'm coming to collect."
