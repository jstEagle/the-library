## 3. Debtors of the Gilded Ledger

The Gilded Ledger kept its debts the way other institutions kept their dead: carefully, privately, and with a certain reverence for the ones who hadn't stayed buried.

Paloma spent the hour before dawn in the vault under the guildhall, pulling ledgers by candlelight. Not the great founding ledger chained to the desk upstairs—this was the *second* book, the one no client ever saw, the one with a spine worn soft as an old dog's ear. The Ledger of Owed Things. Every favor the guild had ever granted, every mercy extended on credit, every life quietly rearranged so that some desperate soul could keep walking. Each entry ended the same way, in his predecessor's hand and then his own:

*To be called at need.*

He had never once called one in. Neither had old Master Corvane, in forty years of keeping the books. That was the point of them, Paloma had always believed. A debt uncollected was a promise kept in advance—a way of saying *the world owes you nothing, but we chose to anyway.* Calling them in felt like burning furniture for firewood.

But Wren's name was inked into a prophecy a century old, and the season turned in eleven days, and Paloma Ashgrove had never been a man who let sentiment outrank arithmetic.

Three entries. He'd chosen three out of two hundred, and he hated how easy the choosing had been.

* * *

Marisol Vane lived above a farrier's shop in Copperlane, in a room with one window and no curtains, because she said curtains were for people hiding from something. She opened the door before he knocked twice.

"I heard your boots on the stairs," she said. "Nobody walks like that unless they're collecting."

"Good morning to you too."

"It isn't." She stepped back to let him in anyway, which was as close to hospitality as Marisol came these days. The room was bare except for a bed, a whetstone, and a sword wrapped in oilcloth that lay across the bed like a patient nobody visited. "You've come about the debt."

"You know why I'm here?"

"No." She crossed her arms. "But it's dawn, and you're here, and I've owed the Ledger for six years. So."

Six years ago, Marisol Vane had been Ser Marisol Vane, oathkeeper of the Order of the Brazen Dawn, until the day she broke her knighting vow to carry a dying child out of a plague-locked district instead of holding her post. The Order had struck her name from their rolls. The guild had gotten her past three checkpoints and forged her passage out of the city, and when she'd tried to pay, Corvane had waved her off with the words *to be called at need.*

Paloma set the second book open on her table. "There's a girl," he said. "She was raised in this guild. Her soul is held beneath Mount Cendre, and it comes due at the turning of the season. I intend to take it back."

Marisol looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, ugly sound with no humor in it.

"A soul. You want me to march into the Ember Court over a *soul*."

"Yes."

"For a pickpocket."

"For my little sister," Paloma said, and watched the words land somewhere behind her ribs, because they weren't quite true and were truer than anything else he could have said.

She turned away from him. For a while the only sound was the farrier below, hammering iron into obedience.

"Do you know what they offered me?" she said finally, not turning around. "After I broke the vow. The Order sent a herald. Said the breach could be forgiven. One act of restoration, and Ser Marisol Vane would live again." Her hand drifted toward the oilcloth bundle on the bed and stopped short of touching it. "I said no. Do you want to know why? Because forgiveness that costs you the reason you sinned isn't forgiveness. It's just a cleaner cage."

"I'm not asking you to be a knight again."

"No," she said. "You're asking me to break something properly this time." She faced him. There were new lines around her eyes that hadn't been there six years ago, cut deep by six years of one-window rooms. "The debt's yours. But hear me, Ashgrove—if we go beneath that mountain, I'm not going for your girl. I'm going because there are vows I still owe and nowhere left to pay them. Maybe hell's the only place that takes my kind of coin."

She pulled the oilcloth off the sword. Beneath it the blade was bright, kept sharp against all hope of use.

"When do we leave?"

* * *

Tetch was harder to find, which was the nature of Tetch. The guild's records listed her address as *the corner where the map stops being true,* and for once the record was literal.

Paloma found her at midmorning in a market square that appeared on no chart of the city, selling sketches to travelers who would never find the square again. She sat cross-legged on a blanket covered in maps of places that moved—the Wandering Steps, the Tide Road, the alley in Old Harbor that only existed on Thursdays—and when she saw him coming she began rolling up her wares without waiting for him to speak.

"Tetch."

"Ledger man." She didn't look up. "I felt it. Like weather. Somebody's calling in a debt somewhere and my teeth ache."

"You could have run."

"Ran from the guild once." She tied the last roll. "Ended up in a valley that wasn't there the day before and wasn't there after. Took me four months to walk out. Never again." She stood, brushing dust from her trousers, and finally met his eyes. She was small, wiry, sun-browned, with ink stains on her fingers to the second knuckle and a scar through one eyebrow that she'd drawn onto the map of her own face, she liked to say, for symmetry. "So. Where is it this time?"

"Under Mount Cendre."

Her face did something complicated and brief, like a shutter slamming. "No."

"Tetch—"

"*No.*" Louder than she meant; a fruit-seller glanced over. Tetch lowered her voice. "You don't understand what you're asking. The passes under Cendre don't hold still. Roads rearrange to keep secrets. I could take you to the gate, maybe, if the mountain lets us—but the mountain *remembers*, Ledger man, and I have reasons—"

"I know about the memory."

That stopped her cold.

Paloma gentled his voice. "It's in the old files. Twenty years ago, Tetch Ferrow mapped the approaches to the Ember Court. Sold the memory afterward to cover her guild debts—her own choice, witnessed and sealed. The guild holds the receipt. We always assumed you sold it because you wanted it gone."

"I did want it gone."

"And now I'm asking if you want it back."

For a long time she said nothing. Around them the impossible market went on its impossible business, vendors crying wares from towns that had burned down decades ago.

"You think a memory's a thing you can just buy back and wear again," she said at last. "Like a coat. It's not. Whatever's in that jar, it's twenty years stale. It's the me who walked down there, and I don't know her anymore. Maybe she knew things I can't afford to know."

"Maybe she knew the way in."

"That's what frightens me." Tetch laughed, hollow. Then she crouched, pulled a stub of charcoal from her pocket, and drew a single line on the cobblestones—a road, starting nowhere, ending nowhere. "You know why I map places that move, Ashgrove? Because everything else I loved held still long enough to lie to me. My family. My order—I trained with the Wayfinders' Guild, did you know that? Before. They disowned me for drawing the moving roads. Said I was making the guild complicit in chaos." She smeared the line with her thumb. "Everything that loved me turned out to be standing still. So I stopped trusting anything that didn't shift."

"The mountain shifts," Paloma said.

"The mountain shifts," she agreed.

"So does the guild. We've moved three times in fifty years. Burned our own archives twice rather than let them be read by the wrong hands. Nothing about us holds still either—except the people." He crouched beside her, level with her eyes. "Wren is nine years older than the day we found her, and she has never once stopped being ours. Come draw us a road, Tetch. Hold still for eleven days. Just eleven."

Tetch stared at the smeared charcoal line. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and rough.

"If I do this—if I get you to that gate—you owe me more than the debt. You owe me the jar. Whatever's in it, I read it first, alone, before anyone sees."

"Done."

She spat in her palm and held it out, and they shook on it, which in Copperlane market squares was as binding as any seal.

* * *

Brother Quill received him in the chapel of Saint Ambiguous—which was not a real saint, which was the point, and which Quill maintained with the fierce devotion of a man who had decided that if God would not specify, then specificity itself was blasphemy.

He was dusting the altar when Paloma arrived. Small round man, ink-stained like Tetch but in black instead of brown, with the look of someone perpetually surprised to be allowed indoors.

"Ah," said Quill, seeing him. "The silent god's bookkeeper."

"He's your god too."

"Oh, I know." Quill set down the duster. "That's the tragedy of me."

Brother Quill had been ordained in the Church of Vesper at nineteen, defrocked at twenty-six, and taken in by the guild at twenty-seven, all of which was recorded in the second book under a single entry: *sheltered during winter of the schism; fed; permitted to argue.* His heresy was simple and unforgivable: Quill worshipped Vesper *loudly*. He sang. He preached. He rang a bell he'd bought himself and addressed prayers aloud to a god whose entire theology was silence, and the Church had cast him out for profaning the quiet.

"They'll excommunicate me again if they learn I helped you," Quill said, pouring tea that was mostly hot water with ambitions. "Well. Excommunicate me *further.* There's a word for it. There's probably a whole rite. They love their rites."

"I haven't told you what I need yet."

"You need someone who knows the liturgies of the deep places. Someone who can read covenant-script and bargain-formal and the old tongue of surety. You need a priest, and you came to the only priest alive who loves Vesper wrong enough to go where he's been." Quill smiled, gentle and sad. "Also you need someone who talks, because the three of you combined couldn't fill a pew with conversation. Marisol grunts. Tetch draws. And you—" he pointed the teapot at Paloma, "—you've made an art form of answering questions with inventory numbers. No offense."

"Some taken."

"Good. Honesty." Quill sat, cradling his cup. "Tell me about the girl."

So Paloma told him. About the prophecy book, the date, the name. About the song Wren hummed that no one taught her. About the entry in the century-old ledger, though he kept the details close—Quill would find them soon enough, and Paloma wanted one more night before the truth put its weight on the girl's shoulders.

Quill listened without interrupting, which for Brother Quill was a form of asceticism. When Paloma finished, the little priest was quiet for a full minute, staring into his ambitious tea.

"You know what breaks my heart," he said finally. "Everyone assumes the silent god abandoned us. Silence reads as absence. It's the obvious sin to pin on him." He shook his head. "But I was a loud man in a silent church, Ashgrove, and I'll tell you what I learned: sometimes the ones who go quiet aren't gone. Sometimes they're listening so hard it looks like leaving." He looked up. "What if your god didn't abandon that girl? What if somebody *sold* her, and he's been holding the note ever since, waiting for someone with a ledger to come collect?"

The question landed in Paloma's chest like a dropped coin, ringing.

"That," he managed, "is heresy."

"Obviously." Quill stood and fetched a traveling cloak from behind the altar, along with a bell that he tucked into his pack with the tenderness of a father packing a child's lunch. "Where's my heresy without a mountain under it?"

* * *

They gathered at the guildhall as the light went amber and long—four of them around the audit table, surrounded by shelves of bottled impossibilities. Marisol sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen. Tetch spreading charts that argued with each other. Quill arranging his bells and script-slates like a man setting an altar.

Paloma stood at the head of the table with the second book open before him, and struck through three entries in red ink.

"Debts cleared," he said.

Nobody cheered. Nobody thanked him. Marisol said, "About time," and Tetch snorted, and Quill rang his small bell once, softly, for the occasion.

And that was the thing Paloma understood, watching them, that he would carry beneath the mountain like a lamp: none of them had come for the debt. He'd known it since Copperlane. Debts were the excuse, the door they could walk through with their pride intact. Marisol came because a broken vow still ached like a tooth. Tetch came because the mountain had stolen a piece of her and she needed to see its face. Quill came because he'd loved a silent god loudly for thirty years and never once been answered—and tonight, somehow, the answer might be waiting underground.

They came, every one of them, because something they loved had abandoned them, and they were done letting it stand.

"Wren doesn't know yet," Paloma said. "By design. She stays here, she stays safe, and—"

From the doorway behind him, a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat said, sweet and dangerous:

"She stays safe doing *what*, exactly?"

Paloma turned. Wren stood in the office threshold in her thieving clothes, arms crossed, the lamplight catching the ledger dust in her hair—and in her hand, held up between two fingers like a stolen coin, was the fresh sheet of paper from his desk.

*Debtors' muster at dawn. Call in everything. We go beneath the mountain.*

"You should really lock your office," Wren Ashgrove said, "when you're planning to erase people from your own crew."
