## 4. What the Mountain Remembers

Tetch had mapped the world twice over—once in ink and once in her head—and she trusted neither copy anymore.

The pass beneath Mount Cendre began as an ordinary road, which was the first lie it told. Ordinary roads do not begin with a milestone that reads *HERE* on one side and *NOT YET* on the other. Tetch crouched beside the stone in the grey pre-dawn light and ran her thumb along the carved letters. The chisel-work was old, older than the guild, older than any kingdom whose coins she'd ever counted. Someone had wanted travelers to know, right from the start, that this place kept its own books.

"Is that a warning or a welcome?" Marisol asked behind her. The knight's voice was flat as ever, but she'd positioned herself at Paloma's shoulder without being asked, the way soldiers do when they've decided a man is worth guarding.

"It's a doorbell," Tetch said.

Paloma came up beside her. He carried the ledger against his chest like a breastplate, and his eyes had the particular shine they got when he was frightened and pretending to be interested instead. "Explain."

"Roads under Cendre rearrange." She stood, dusted her knees. "Not randomly. They rearrange to keep secrets. The mountain decides what you're allowed to find, and it moves everything else out of reach. You can't map it. I know because—" Her mouth did the thing it always did around this part of the story, closing like a shop shutter. She made herself push through it. "Because I tried. Once."

Wren, who had been told to stay home and had followed them anyway for eleven miles before anyone noticed, popped up between two supply crates. "You tried to map the Ember Court?"

"I tried to map the road *to* the Ember Court," Tetch said. "The Court itself is beyond cartography. That's not poetry. That's a technical assessment."

"And?" Wren's eyes were enormous and hungry. She was seventeen and thought danger was a flavor she hadn't tasted enough of yet.

Tetch looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the sky, which was doing nothing useful. Finally he sighed and gave the small nod that meant *your story, your call*.

"And I found it," Tetch said. "And then I sold the finding, because a memory of the Court's location was worth more coin than I'd seen in my whole life, and I was twenty-two and stupid in the specific way that twenty-two-year-olds with empty pockets are stupid." She tapped her temple. "I don't remember mapping it. I remember *that* I mapped it, the way you remember a room you've slept in but can't picture anymore. There's a hole where three weeks of my life used to be. The buyer took the weeks and left me the receipt."

Nobody laughed. Good. It wasn't a joke.

Brother Quill adjusted the strap of his satchel—he'd brought more books than food, which told Tetch everything about how seriously he expected them to eat—and said gently, "Then why come at all? If the mountain already has what's yours."

Tetch had asked herself that every night since Paloma had called in her debt. She'd expected to feel resentful about it. What she felt instead was something worse: relief. A debt meant someone still wanted her somewhere.

"Because holes itch," she said. "And because if we're going to walk unmappable ground, you want the one person who knows what losing the map feels like from the inside."

* * *

The mountain swallowed them by midmorning.

It didn't feel like entering a cave. It felt like being *filed*. The pass narrowed into corridors of black stone veined with something that glowed faintly orange, like embers banked under ash, and the air grew warm and dry and smelled faintly of struck matches. Their footsteps stopped echoing almost immediately, as though the walls were drinking sound.

Tetch walked point with a compass she'd owned for thirty years and a piece of chalk she'd bought yesterday. Every fifty paces she marked the wall. It was ritual, not navigation; she already knew the marks wouldn't help. But rituals are how cartographers pray.

At the first fork—a Y-split where both branches curved identically into dark—she stopped and held up a fist.

"What's wrong?" Marisol whispered.

"Nothing. That's what's wrong." Tetch turned slowly. "There should be a draft. Underground passages breathe—one side pulls air, one side pushes. This one doesn't. Both branches are breathing *in*." She knelt and scattered a pinch of gravel at the junction, noting how it settled. "The mountain's holding its breath. It knows we're here."

Paloma crouched beside her. Up close she could see the fine tremor in his hands that he probably thought he hid. "Can it change the road while we're standing on it?"

"Yes."

"Will it?"

"That depends." She brushed chalk dust off her fingers. "On whether it thinks we're worth keeping secrets from. Small secrets get small defenses. If all it's hiding from us today is a shortcut, we'll walk a long boring way and be fine. If it's hiding something big—" She shrugged. "Big roads move fast."

They chose the left branch. Twenty minutes later it delivered them, without a single turn, back to the same Y-junction, where Tetch's chalk mark waited on the wall like a smug little accusation.

Wren swore with impressive fluency for someone raised by accountants.

* * *

They tried the right branch. It looped them past the milestone—the one reading *HERE / NOT YET*—which should have been impossible, because the milestone sat outside the mountain's mouth, in open air, under a sky they had left hours ago. They stood in daylight looking at a stone they had read at dawn, and behind them the pass exhaled warm air that smelled of matches, and Quill quietly sat down on a rock and began, very calmly, to recite something that might have been scripture or might have been a shopping list, his way of praying when prayer failed.

"We're not walking in circles," Tetch said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too steady, the voice of a woman describing weather in someone else's country. "Circles would be kind. Circles mean the mountain wants us tired. This means the mountain wants us *lost*, and there's a difference. Tired ends. Lost doesn't."

"So what do we do?" Wren asked. For the first time since Tetch had known her, the girl looked genuinely young. Scared-young. It made her look exactly like what she was: somebody's daughter, somebody's sister, somebody's soul with a due date.

Tetch looked at the crew. An oathbreaker knight who hadn't drawn her sword once, as if afraid of what using it would cost. A priest of a silent god carrying books nobody could hear. A ledger-keeper with a dead man's calm and a live man's fear. A pickpocket who should have been home.

She knew what to do. She'd known since the junction. She just hated saying it, because saying it would make it real, and real things could fail.

"Hold hands," she said.

Marisol's eyebrow climbed. "*Pardon?*"

"The mountain moves roads to separate secrets from seekers. Its whole method is isolation—you turn a corner alone and the corner comes with you, and suddenly you're a party of one in a corridor that only exists for you. It can't do that if we don't let go of each other." Tetch flexed her fingers, hating the vulnerability of it, hating more that she'd learned this from a memory she no longer had, some ghost-lesson scratched into her by a three-week absence. "A chain of people is a single object. Harder to file separately. We walk roped together, hand to hand, and nobody lets go. Not to fight. Not to run. Not for anything. Whatever you see, whatever calls your name, whoever screams from a side passage—*you hold on*."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind mixed together.

"I'll take point," Tetch said. "Quill behind me, then Wren—no arguments, girl, you're the smallest and the most stealable—then Paloma, then Marisol at the rear, because if anything grabs us it'll grab from behind and I want its teeth meeting steel first."

Marisol drew her sword an inch, considered it, and slid it home. "Understood."

They linked up. Wren's hand in Tetch's was small and calloused in the pads, a pickpocket's hand, warm and quick even at rest. Quill's palm was damp and he apologized for it twice. Paloma gripped like a man holding onto a ledge. Marisol's grip at the end of the line was the anchor-stone the whole chain hung from.

"Rules," Tetch said. "One: nobody speaks to voices. Two: nobody steps anywhere their feet aren't already pulled. Three—if we start to stretch apart, everyone stops and we sing."

"*Sing?*" said three voices at once.

"Rhythm keeps a chain honest. And the mountain hates songs. Don't ask me how I know. I don't—that's rather the point." She faced the dark mouth of the pass, which had, at some unmarked moment, stopped being the same pass they'd entered. The orange veins in the rock pulsed now, slow, patient, like something breathing in its sleep. Or counting.

"Onward," she said, and stepped in, and pulled her family in after her.

* * *

The ground lied to them for six hours.

Tetch counted paces because counting was the last tool she had. At pace four hundred the floor tilted uphill though they descended. At pace nine hundred the walls narrowed enough that Marisol had to walk sideways, sword-hand scraping stone, and Wren's breath went quick and shallow against Tetch's back. At pace thirteen hundred they passed a window—a genuine glass window, curtained, set into solid rock—through which they saw a kitchen where a woman washed dishes and never once looked up, though Quill knocked until his knuckles bled.

At pace two thousand, the voices started.

They came from the side passages, soft and reasonable. Marisol's name, spoken in a commander's tone. Quill's name in a choir's harmony. Wren's name in a mother's voice, sweet and pleading, and Tetch felt the girl's hand seize hers hard enough to grind bone and whispered, "Rule one," and Wren bit down on a sob and held.

Paloma's name came last, and it came differently. Not from a side passage. From everywhere. From the stone itself, low and vast, the way a mountain would say a name if a mountain had loved someone once and lost the ability to say so.

*Tetch.* No—it said *Paloma*. But underneath it, so faint she might have invented it, it said her name too, and the sound of it opened the hole inside her like a key opening a lock that had rusted shut, and for one vertiginous second she *almost remembered*—

She sang instead. Loud, tuneless, obscene verses of a marching song sailors had taught her in a port whose name she'd also sold. The others joined—Wren first, then Quill in a surprisingly decent baritone, then Marisol, tone-deaf and committed, and finally Paloma, whose singing voice was exactly as careful as the rest of him. The voices in the walls thinned. The orange veins dimmed, sulking.

The mountain hated songs. She'd been right about that. She wished she knew *why* she was right. She wished she knew anything about herself except the shape of what was missing.

* * *

They camped in a chamber that allowed it—which is to say, a chamber that decided to allow it, because Tetch found no logic in its choosing. The ceiling glowed ember-orange, dim as a dying hearth. They ate cold rations in a circle, hands still loosely linked because nobody wanted to be the first to break the rule, and the silence had the texture of a confession waiting to happen.

Tetch went first, because it was her debt that had dragged them all here and debts deserve interest.

"You should know what we're walking toward," she said, staring at the glowing ceiling. "Not the Court. I can't tell you about the Court. But the road. When I was twenty-two I spent three weeks mapping this pass. I had a system—chalk, string, a compass I bribed a witch to enchant. It worked. For three weeks I beat the mountain. I charted forty miles of shifting road and found the pattern underneath the movement, and the pattern led down, and at the bottom of down there was a gate, and past the gate—" Her throat closed. She drank water that tasted of matches. "Past the gate, something. I don't know what. That's the part I sold. I sold the *finding*, and the sale took the journey with it, because a memory isn't a page you can tear out clean. It's a thread. Pull it and the whole weave comes loose."

"What did you buy with the money?" Wren asked softly.

"A house," Tetch said. "A real one. With a door that stayed where I put it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I lived in it for a year before I understood why I couldn't sleep there. Rooms that don't move felt like rooms that were dead. I'd spent three weeks learning to trust a living place, and then I paid to forget the lesson and kept the reflexes. Do you understand? I have a cartographer's instincts for a maze I cannot remember entering. My hands know roads my head has never seen. Everything I am is a receipt for something that was taken."

Quill set down his bread. "Vesper teaches that what is given away is never truly lost."

"This wasn't given away. It was *sold*, Brother, and I was the seller, and I'd sell it again, because I was hungry and the Court pays well and hunger makes theologians of us all." She looked around the circle, at each face in the ember-light. "So understand what I'm telling you. Down there, the Court will offer each of you something. It offered me money, and I took it, and I've been paying for the purchase ever since. It will offer you better. It will offer you *true* things—memories you actually lost, people you actually miss, names you actually earned. That's the horror of it. The Court never lies. It just sells you back your own life at interest."

Paloma spoke for the first time in an hour. "And Wren's soul. Is that true too? Is that really hers, down there?"

Tetch met his eyes. He was asking her to lie, or hoping she would, or hoping she wouldn't—she genuinely couldn't tell, and that frightened her more than the moving roads.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know the Court keeps what it's paid for. And I know that whatever deal was made for that girl, a century ago, the paper trail leads down." She squeezed Wren's hand, which had found hers again without either of them noticing. "And I know the mountain remembers what I've forgotten. So tomorrow, when the roads shift, watch what they *avoid*. Mountains don't hide everything equally. They guard the true path hardest. We follow the resistance."

* * *

Sleep came in shifts, and Tetch took the last one, and dreamed—not of the Court, but of a hand drawing a map. Her hand. Younger, steadier, chalk-white at the fingertips. In the dream the map covered a table entire, and at its center was a symbol she almost recognized, a circle with a line through it, and beneath the symbol, in her own handwriting, three words she woke up reaching for and lost before her eyes opened:

*It forgives nothing.*

She sat up in the ember-dark, heart slamming, and found Paloma awake across the dead fire, watching her.

"You remembered something," he said. Not a question.

"A fragment. Three words and a symbol." She rubbed her face. "Paloma, I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear it as a cartographer and not as a friend, because the friend part is going to hate it."

"Tell me."

"The dream wasn't random. Dreams aren't random underground—the mountain feeds them. Which means either the mountain is showing me my own past..." She paused, choosing the truth over the kinder option, the way you choose the harder road because the easier one is lying to you. "Or the Court knows I'm coming, and it's *reminding* me. Softening me up. Showing me the shape of what it still holds, so that when it offers to sell my three weeks back, I'll already be homesick for them."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. The ember-light moved on his face like water.

"Would you take it?" he asked. "If it offered. Your memory. Your three weeks. Would you buy yourself back?"

Tetch thought of the house with the door that stayed put. Thought of the hole shaped like a person where her twenties used to be. Thought of Wren's hand in hers, and Marisol's grip at the end of the chain, and a priest who prayed wrong and a keeper who feared right.

"No," she said, surprising herself completely. "I'd ask it a question instead. I'd ask what the symbol meant. Circle with a line through it. Because I drew it at the center of the only complete map of the Court that has ever existed, and I marked it important, and I think—" her voice dropped, "—I think it might be the reason I sold the memory at all. Not greed. Fear. I saw something down there, Paloma, and I ran, and I made myself forget the running. And now we're walking straight back toward it."

Above them, the ceiling's glow pulsed once—slow, deliberate, like a nod.

And far down the pass, in the direction they had not yet traveled, something answered it. A sound like a great door unsealing, deep in the mountain's heart, and beneath it, faint and terrible and unmistakable:

Singing. The same melody Wren hummed without knowing why.

Tetch got to her feet and reached for the others. "Wake up," she called, her voice cracking on the words. "All of you—wake up. It knows we're coming. And it's started the music early."
