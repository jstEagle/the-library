## 5. The Toll of Names

The mountain did not look like a place that ate people.

That was the first lie of it, Paloma decided as the five of them stood in the shadow of Mount Cendre's lower slopes at dusk. It looked almost gentle — a broad-shouldered giant of grey stone wearing a mantle of pine, its peak wrapped in cloud like a scarf against the cold. Smoke rose from vents along its flanks in thin, unhurried threads, the way smoke rises from a house where someone is baking bread.

Tetch spat into the gravel. "It's smiling," she said. "Don't let it smile at you."

"It's a mountain," Marisol said. "Mountains don't smile."

"This one does." Tetch unrolled a corner of one of her maps on a flat stone, weighted it with pebbles, and stared at it with the expression of a woman reading a letter from an enemy. The parchment showed the pass beneath Cendre as a tangle of inked lines, some drawn over so many times they'd gone black, some crossed out entirely, some simply trailing off the edge of the page into nothing. "I mapped this ground once. Before." She didn't say *before what*. Nobody asked. "The roads down there move. Not like rockslides move. Like they're *deciding*."

"Then how do we get through?" Wren asked.

Paloma had argued for three days about whether to bring her this far. He'd lost the argument to Wren herself, who had pointed out — with the flat logic of someone raised by mercenaries and accountants — that if her soul was the prize, she had the best claim to be present when it was recovered. He'd compromised: she came as far as the gate, no further. He was already regretting every compromise he'd ever made in his life.

"We get through," Tetch said, "by holding hands like children crossing a market square, and by not letting go no matter what we see. And we get in by paying the toll."

"The ferryman," Brother Quill murmured. He'd been quiet since the pass began, his old priest's hands folded around a prayer book he never opened. "Every account agrees. There's a ferryman at the gate of the Court, and he takes names."

"I've got three," Wren said. "Wren Ashgrove. Wren Quickfingers — don't laugh, Marisol, I earned that one. And whatever name my mother screamed at me before she gave me up, which nobody's ever told me."

Something went through the group then, quick and cold as wind through a keyhole. Quill's hands tightened on his book. Paloma kept his face still, because keeping his face still was the thing he did best, but inside him an old ledger page turned over — the prophecy, the date, the soul maturing at season's turn — and he thought: *she doesn't know why the toll matters more for her than any of us.*

He would tell her. Later. When he knew how.

* * *

They reached the gate an hour past full dark.

There was no gate, exactly. There was a lake — black water lying in a bowl of stone where no lake should be, fed by nothing, reflecting stars that Paloma, checking instinctively against the sky, found were not the stars overhead. Above the water hung a single lantern on a crooked iron pole, and beneath the lantern lay a flat-bottomed ferry, and standing at the ferry's stern was the ferryman.

He was tall and thin as a winter branch, robed in something between burlap and ash, and his face was smooth as the back of a spoon. No eyes. No mouth. Where features should have been there was only a faint suggestion of them, the way a memory of a face is fainter than the face. When he moved — and he did move, turning toward them with terrible courtesy — the lantern flame bent toward him like a listener leaning in.

"Well," Tetch said. "He's uglier than I remembered."

"You remembered him?" Marisol asked.

"No. That's rather the point."

The ferryman raised one long hand, palm up. Waiting.

Paloma stepped forward. In the Gilded Ledger, he negotiated everything — debts, prices, threats, the occasional hostage situation involving a bottled thunderstorm — and his method was always the same: know the terms before the other party states them. "One name per passenger," he said. "Freely given. That's the toll."

The smooth face inclined. A nod. Or perhaps just the wind.

"And freely given means truly given," Paloma continued. "You can taste a lie."

The ferryman's hand turned over, palm down now. *Hurry.*

Marisol went first.

She walked to the water's edge the way she did everything — like a soldier approaching a superior officer she despised but could not disobey. She stopped at the brink of the black water and stood very straight, and Paloma watched her gather something up from inside herself, something heavy, the way you'd gather a coat you'd worn so long you'd forgotten it wasn't your skin.

"My name," Marisol said, and her voice cracked on the second word, which was how Paloma knew this was real, because Marisol's voice never cracked, "is Serah Vael Dornemarche. Knight-Captain of the Order of the Unbroken Oath. Sworn at seventeen. Stripped at thirty-one." She swallowed. "Take it. Take all of it. The title, the order, the day they pinned the silver sun on my chest. Take the whole name."

The air around her shivered.

Paloma saw it happen — saw the name leave her. It came off her like steam off hot metal, a pale ribbon of light unwinding from her mouth, her chest, her scarred hands, drifting across the water into the ferryman's open palm. He closed his fingers around it and the light went out like a snuffed candle.

Marisol swayed. Wren caught her elbow.

"What—" Marisol blinked, looked at her own hands, flexed them. "What was my name? Before? My mother used to call me—"

"Serah," Paloma said quietly. "Serah Vael Dornemarche. You just gave it up. You'll get it back or you won't, but right now you're Marisol, and Marisol got us this far."

"Marisol." She tested it. Nodded slowly. "Feels smaller." A pause. "Lighter, though." And she walked onto the ferry without looking back, which took more courage than anything Paloma had seen her do, including the duel he hadn't yet witnessed and couldn't imagine.

* * *

Tetch paid next. She gave up the name her cartographers' guild had granted her — *Tetch of the Moving Lines, First Surveyor of Unstable Ground* — and afterward she stared at her own maps with mild confusion, as though unsure who had drawn them, until Wren squeezed her hand and said, "You're still Tetch. That one's ours. We made it." And Tetch nodded and held on to that name visibly, the way you hold a rope over a drop.

Brother Quill surprised everyone. He offered his birth name — *Aldous Merrin*, farm boy, third son, the one who left — and when it was taken he laughed, actually laughed, a wet and wondering sound. "Fifty years carrying that," he said. "Fifty years thinking I'd failed everyone back home. And now I can't even remember their faces properly. Just that I loved them." He wiped his eyes. "The god asks strange things of us. But cheap, compared to some."

Then it was Wren.

And Paloma moved without deciding to, stepping between her and the water. "No."

"Paloma—"

"Not yours. Not tonight. If the toll takes a name from you, and your name is already written in their books, already owed—" He heard himself and stopped, because he'd nearly said too much, and Wren's eyes had gone sharp as a blade finding the gap in armor.

"Already owed," she repeated. "What does that mean?"

"It means I'm paying for you," Paloma said. "Both of us. One name each, and mine covers your passage. That's the negotiation. I've done harder ones."

"That's not—" Wren started, and then stopped, because she was guild-raised too, and she knew when a negotiator was spending capital he couldn't afford, and she knew better than to argue in front of the counterparty. Her jaw set. "We're talking about this later."

"We are."

The ferryman's hand turned over again. *Hurry.*

Paloma walked to the edge of the black water alone.

Here was the problem, and he'd known it since the pass, turning it over in his mind like a coin he suspected was clipped: he did not want to give his true name. Not because it was precious to him — he'd spent forty years being nobody, invisible, unremarkable, the man clients forgot within the hour. But the prophecy had his name in it. Inked a century ago. Which meant his name was already in the Court's economy somewhere, already circulating, already priced — and a man walking into a bank does not hand the teller his last coin to find out what it's worth.

So he lied.

"I am called Ash," he said. "Ash Corvane. A ledger-keeper from the low country. Nothing else."

The moment the words left his mouth, fire took him.

Not around him — *in* him. A white-hot line drew itself down his throat and through his chest, and Paloma doubled over, gasping, as the false name burned its way through him like swallowed lightning. It didn't burn flesh. It burned deeper than flesh, burning the *lie itself*, searing it out of him, and the pain was absolute and clarifying and it lasted three seconds or three years, he couldn't have said. He dropped to one knee on the stony shore. Somewhere far away Wren was shouting his name — his real one, the one the guild gave him, *Paloma, Paloma* — and the sound of it cut through the fire like water.

When he could see again, the ferryman was crouched before him. Close. Too close. The smooth face hovered inches from his own, and although it had no eyes, Paloma felt himself being *read* — felt something turn over inside him, page by page, patient and thorough, the way he himself audited a suspicious inventory.

Then the ferryman did something no account had ever recorded.

He hesitated.

His open palm — the palm waiting for payment — trembled. And from somewhere inside that featureless head came a sound, thin and rusted, like a hinge unused for a hundred years: the ghost of a voice trying to speak and finding it had sold the ability. The sound rose and fell twice. It might have been a word. It might have been half of one.

Then it stopped, and the ferryman withdrew, and gestured: *board.*

"He didn't take anything," Wren whispered, hauling Paloma up. She had his arm over her shoulders; when had she gotten there? "You didn't pay. Why didn't he make you pay?"

"I don't know," Paloma said. It was even mostly true.

But he knew something else, something that sat in his burned chest like a coal that refused to cool: the ferryman had recognized him. Not Ash Corvane. Him. And whatever name the Court had for Paloma Ashgrove, keeper of impossible things, it was worth more than a knighthood, more than a surveyorship, more than a farm boy's fifty years of guilt.

His true name wasn't currency here.

It was treasure.

* * *

The ferry crossed the starless water in silence. Paloma sat in the stern with Wren pressed against his side — she'd appointed herself his keeper and would not be dissuaded — and watched the shore they'd left recede, and the shore they approached emerge from the dark: a beach of fine grey cinders, and beyond it, carved into the mountain's roots, gates tall as cathedral doors, glowing from within with a deep banked orange, like coals breathing under ash.

The others slept or pretended to. Quill sat beside him instead.

"Ash Corvane," Quill said softly. "You lied to a servant of the Ember Court, and it burned you, and then it *let you aboard anyway*. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the rules bend for some people."

"That the rules *bend toward* you." Quill's voice dropped further. "I have read every fragment ever written about Vesper, Paloma. Every heresy, every apocrypha. And in the oldest of them — older than the silence — there's a line the church struck from the canon. *When the Silent One returns to claim his own, the gates will know him, and the toll will not apply.*"

The coal in Paloma's chest pulsed.

"I'm not a god," he said.

"No," Quill agreed mildly. "You're a man who keeps a god's belongings on a shelf and hasn't wondered why none of them ever seem to lose their warmth." He patted Paloma's knee with one small dry hand and shuffled off to sleep, leaving Paloma alone with the glow of the gates growing larger, and the terrible arithmetic assembling itself in his mind:

*A god who went silent. A court that buys voices. A ferryman who tried to speak and couldn't. A name worth more than the toll. And a prophecy, written a century ago, that ends with a soul consumed at the turning of the season —*

The ferry grounded on the cinder beach with a soft crunch.

The gates began, slowly, to open.
