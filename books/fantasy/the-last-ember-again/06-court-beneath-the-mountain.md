## 6. Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not swallow them so much as invite them in.

That was the worst part, Paloma decided. He had expected teeth—a throat of stone, a descent like being eaten. Instead the passage widened by degrees, the walls smoothing from raw granite to something cut and polished, veined with threads of ember-light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat. The air grew warm without ever becoming hot, the way a hearth is warm to a child called home for supper.

"Stop looking at it," Marisol murmured at his shoulder. She had not surrendered her sword at the gate—only her name—and she held the blade now the way a man holds a crutch he's ashamed of. "Looking is how they get you."

"I'm an appraiser," Paloma said. "Appraising is what I do."

"You're a liar with excellent posture. There's a difference."

Ahead of them, Tetch walked with her eyes half-closed, one hand trailing along the wall as if reading braille. Brother Quill brought up the rear, clutching his ledger against his chest like a shield that had failed other men before him. And Wren—

Wren was not there. That was the point. She was somewhere behind them in the dark, disguised in scullery gray, having sworn to Paloma in a whisper that she would follow only if he stopped looking at her like she was made of glass. He had looked at her exactly that way anyway. She had seen him do it. She had gone anyway.

The passage opened.

Paloma had catalogued impossible things for eleven years. A bottled thunderstorm that rattled its glass on stormy nights. A debt of one honest tear, still accruing interest. A key that unlocked nothing but opened every door it was shown to. He had believed himself past wonder, the way a surgeon believes himself past squeamishness.

The Ember Court corrected him.

It was a cathedral built of banked fires—not flames but the memory of flames, coals the size of carriages glowing a deep patient orange, stacked into pillars and arches and a vaulted ceiling that breathed heat down onto the floor like the exhalation of some vast contented animal. The light never flickered. Nothing here was allowed to flicker; flickering implied change, and change implied time, and time was the one currency this place refused to honor.

Courtiers moved through the glow in robes the color of cooling ash. They did not walk so much as drift, and when they passed one another they paused, touched fingertips, and exchanged—Paloma saw it clearly, saw it and felt his stomach drop—the exchange was visible. A small light passing between hands. One courtier would hold out a mote of brightness cupped in her palm like water, and another would take it, and press something back in return, and both would close their fingers over what they'd received and wear, for a moment, expressions of terrible private satisfaction or terrible private grief.

"They're trading memories," Quill whispered. His voice cracked on *memories*. "Look at their faces. Look at—oh, Vesper keep us, look at what they're smiling about."

"Don't say his name here," Marisol said sharply.

"Why not?"

"Because names are currency." Tetch said it flatly, without turning. Her hand still trailed the wall. "I told you at the gate. Everything you carry that can be owned, they can price. Your name. your mother's face. The smell of bread from your childhood. Especially the things you think are priceless. Those go for the most."

A herald drifted toward them—or what served as a herald: a tall figure whose robe was stitched all over with small brass tags, each one stamped with a word Paloma couldn't read from this distance. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way sound behaves in dreams.

*Appraisers. You are expected. The Court honors those who can name a thing's worth.*

"We're honored," Paloma said, and hated how smoothly the lie left him.

*Follow. Touch nothing. Want nothing.*

The last instruction hung in the warm air longer than the others, and Paloma understood it was not a rule but a warning. In this place, wanting was the same as reaching, and reaching was the same as buying, and buying was the same as selling, and everyone who sold anything here eventually found out what they'd been paying with.

* * *

They were led through galleries of fire.

In the first, courtiers browsed shelves of glass jars, each holding a scene rather than an object: a wedding dance suspended mid-turn, a funeral's last amen, a summer afternoon in which nothing happened at all and which, judging by the crowd around it, was worth a fortune. An elderly courtier wept silently before a jar containing only rain on a window, and no one thought this strange.

In the second gallery, names were kept. Paloma heard them before he saw them—rows of small silver bells, each chiming faintly with its own sound, and the sound of each bell was a person's name spoken by someone who loved them. A broker moved along the rows with a tuning fork, checking each bell's pitch, and where a bell had gone silent the broker simply removed it and set it aside in a bin marked, in elegant script, SPENT.

Marisol stopped walking. Paloma watched her look at the bin of spent names, and watched her understand that somewhere in this cathedral her knighthood's name was already ringing in someone else's pocket, and watched her decide—with a visible effort, like setting down something heavy—not to burn the place down then and there.

"Keep moving," Paloma said quietly.

"You keep saying that."

"It keeps working."

In the third gallery, Paloma made his mistake.

He had been careful. Eleven years of keeping the Gilded Ledger's inventory had taught him to look without lingering, to want without showing it, to treat every marvel as a line item with a provenance and a risk assessment. He had appraised a god's fingerbone and felt nothing. He had catalogued a mirror that showed the viewer's death and slept fine afterward.

But in the third gallery, on a shelf of plain black lacquer, among jars of ordinary memories priced modestly—a first snow, a forgiven debt, a dog's whole faithful life—he saw a jar labeled in handwriting he knew.

He knew it because he had spent years trying to read it. It appeared in the margins of old guild ledgers, in annotations no scribe would admit to making, in the flyleaf of the prophecy book itself: a slanted, hurried script, the writing of someone who had learned to write in a hurry and never slowed down. Vesper's script. The silent god's handwriting, which Paloma had studied the way other men study the faces of absent fathers.

The jar contained a memory. He could see it even through the glass: a hillside at dusk, a boy of perhaps nine sitting cross-legged in dry grass, and above the boy—not a figure, not a shape, but a presence, a warmth in the air taking the barest suggestion of form, the way heat shimmers above a road in summer. And the boy was laughing. Not afraid. Laughing, the way you laugh when something enormous and gentle has just said something kind.

The label beneath the jar read:

*One memory, gently used: the last time the god Vesper spoke aloud. Buyer receives the hearing of it. Price: one true name, freely given.*

Paloma stood very still.

He had been nine once. He had sat on a hillside once. He had spent twenty years telling himself the memory was a fever dream, a child's invention, grief wearing a costume—the story of the day the warmth spoke to him, the day before the warmth went silent forever, the day he had never told anyone because who would believe that a mercenary's whole faith rested on four words spoken into dry grass by something with no mouth?

Four words. He could almost hear them. He had built his entire adult life on almost hearing them.

"That one's not for sale to you," said a voice beside him, silk-soft.

The broker was small and neat, with the face of someone who had sold their own boredom centuries ago and now experienced everything fresh, constantly, horribly. Its eyes were the orange of banked coals.

"Everything has a price," Paloma said. Appraiser's voice. Steady hands.

"Everything has a price," the broker agreed pleasantly. "But not every buyer can pay it. This lot requires a true name freely given. Yours, I think, would be... insufficient." It smiled. "Insufficient things make poor coin. The Court does not accept counterfeit."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," the broker said, with terrible gentleness. "You don't. That's the tragedy of you."

It drifted away. Paloma stood before the jar a moment longer, watching the boy on the hillside laugh at something he could no longer hear, and felt the seduction of it settle into his bones like cold: *one true name, freely given.* He knew names had power here—he'd watched Marisol pay hers at the gate. But the broker had said his own name was *insufficient*, and that meant either the Court lied, or his name was worth more than he knew, and both possibilities frightened him equally.

"Paloma." Tetch's hand on his arm. Her grip was iron. "Whatever's in that jar, it isn't yours anymore. Whatever's in any jar here isn't anyone's anymore. That's the whole trick. They let you see yourself on the shelf so you'll negotiate against your own heart."

"I'm fine."

"You're white as tallow and your hand is shaking."

"My hand is always shaking."

"It isn't."

He stepped back from the shelf. Behind the glass, the boy laughed at a joke twenty years old, and the warmth above him shimmered, and Paloma turned away and did not look again, and the not-looking cost him more than the toll at the gate had.

* * *

They were granted audience at the far end of the cathedral, where the pillars of banked fire drew together into a throne of embers, and upon the throne sat the Queen of Cinders.

She was beautiful the way a burned forest is beautiful—vast and blackened and shot through with living color. Her gown was ash that moved like silk. Her skin held the deep red of coals under their gray crust, and her crown was made of vows: Paloma could see them, small rings of pale fire circling her brow, each one a promise someone had made and broken, harvested and worn. There were thousands. Some of them, he realized with a lurch, were still faintly trying to be kept, flickering with the ghost of intentions their original speakers no longer remembered making.

Her voice, when she spoke, was the sound of a fire settling.

*Appraisers. The Court is pleased. We have so much, and so few who can tell us what it is worth.*

"Your Majesty honors us," Paloma said, and bowed the bow of a guild man greeting a difficult client.

*Rise. Tell us—what is the worth of a soul?*

The question dropped into the warm air like a stone into a well. Beside him, Quill's breath caught. Marisol's hand found her sword hilt and stayed there, resting, a promise to herself.

"A soul," Paloma said carefully, "is worth whatever someone will give for it. That's not appraisal. That's just arithmetic."

*And if no one gives?*

"Then it was never truly owned. Only held."

Something moved behind the Queen's coal-dark eyes—amusement, or appetite, or the memory of an appetite. She leaned forward on her throne of embers, and the crown of broken vows flickered, and for one disorienting moment Paloma thought he saw, in the deepest ring of that crown, a vow shaped differently from the others. Older. Brighter. Still burning after a century, refusing to go out, inscribed in a slanted hurried hand he knew better than his own.

The Queen followed his gaze to her own crown, and smiled, and covered the oldest vow with her hand.

*You have good eyes, appraiser. We will enjoy pricing them.*

She waved them away with fingers like cooling iron, and the herald reappeared to lead them back through the galleries, and Paloma walked with his crew through the cathedral of everything people had given up, and told himself the mission was on course, the vault mapped, the plan intact.

He was lying, and the Court could taste lies—it had burned him once already at the gate—but no flame rose to catch this one, and that frightened him most of all. Because it meant the Court didn't consider this particular lie worth punishing.

It considered it worth waiting for.

Behind them, on its shelf of black lacquer, a boy on a hillside went on laughing at a joke Paloma could no longer remember hearing—and in the Queen's crown, the oldest vow burned on, patient as a banked fire, waiting for the season to turn.
