## 7. The Girl Who Was Sold

The archive beneath the Gilded Ledger smelled of dust and old tallow and, if you believed hard enough, of patience itself.

Brother Quill had come to believe it. Three years he'd been allowed down here—first as a debtor scrubbing catalog cards for a blasphemy committed in another diocese, then as a volunteer, then, without anyone quite deciding it, as the keeper of the deep stacks no one else wanted. The other clerks called the lower vaults *the quiet shelves*, and they said it the way sailors say the doldrums.

Quill loved the quiet shelves. He was, after all, a man who had devoted his life to a god who did not answer, so he had long ago made his peace with rooms that kept their own counsel.

He worked by lamplight among the oldest holdings, re-binding ledgers whose spines had gone soft as bread. These were the guild's founding records, predating the Gilded Ledger's current charter by a hundred years and more—back when the guild had been something meaner and smaller, a counting-house above a pawnbroker's in the river district, run by a woman with ink-stained fingers and, according to the marginalia, a temper like a struck flint.

Quill read everything. That was his heresy in miniature. His order, the Vigilant Quiet, taught that Vesper was best honored by silence and service, never by scrutiny—that to dig into the god's affairs was to treat the divine like gossip. Quill had been defrocked twice for reading things he shouldn't. He wore the shame of it like a second cassock, comfortable and worn-in.

He was halfway through a water-damaged folio from the guild's ninety-first year when he found the page that would ruin him.

It was tucked into the binding, not sewn—a single leaf pressed flat between the boards where some clerk a century dead had hidden it, or perhaps protected it, or perhaps simply run out of courage. The paper was good stock, older than the folio around it. The hand that had written on it was careful, formal, the script of someone trained to record transactions that mattered.

Quill smoothed it flat under the lamp and began to read.

*Received into trust, this third day of the Waning Season: one soul entire, unmarked, female child, age two years. Pledged by the birth parents, names surrendered per custom, in consideration of debt forgiveness totaling four hundred crowns and the continued health of the pledgors' remaining children.*

*Term of pledge: one hundred years, or until the turning of the season falling nearest the centenary, whichever concludes first.*

*Held against: the Court Beneath Mount Cendre.*

*Condition at intake: lightly worn. The child has not yet learned to want anything she cannot name.*

*Note appended by the undersigned: I have taken the liberty of entering this lot under the guild's protection clause. Whatever the Court believes it holds, the Ledger holds the truth. Let the girl grow up among us. Let her be ours in every way but the one they think matters. When the term ends, we will find a way. —M.A., Keeper*

Below the note, in fresher ink—fresher by decades, but still old, still browned at the edges—a second hand had written:

*No way found. Passing the burden on. Whoever reads this: she sings a song none of us taught her. Now you know why. Do better than we did.*

Quill sat very still.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above him, muffled through stone and timber, the guild went about its evening—the clink of coin trays, Marisol arguing with a porter about axle grease, the ordinary music of people who believed they understood their own inventory.

He read the page again. Then a third time, because his mind kept sliding off it the way a boot slides off wet stone, refusing purchase.

One soul, entire, pledged a century ago. Held in trust until the season turns.

The season turned in nine days.

And there was only one person in the Gilded Ledger young enough, only one person the guild had raised from cradle-take rather than debt-indenture, only one girl who—

Who sang a song none of them had taught her.

Quill's hands began to shake, and he set the lamp down carefully, the way you set down a sleeping child, before he let himself feel what he was feeling.

* * *

He knew the song. Of course he knew it; everyone at the guild knew it, the way you know a tune you've heard hummed in stairwells and over dishwater for years without ever learning its name. Wren sang it when she was concentrating, picking a lock or palming a purse, her lips moving around a melody that had no words—or seemed to have none.

Three nights ago, Paloma had heard it too, and Quill had watched the color leave his face like water out of a cracked jug. Paloma had grabbed Quill's sleeve in the corridor after, hard enough to bruise, and asked him in a voice stripped down to wire: *"You're the one who reads the old books. Find out what that song is."*

Quill had searched. He'd gone through hymnals and court registers, through the confiscated songbooks of three banned sects, through a chapbook of mountain folklore that made his skin crawl with its illustrations of smiling figures standing too close to firelight.

He hadn't found the song. But he'd found references to it. A traveling merchant's diary, eighty years old: *In Cendre's shadow they sing an anthem with no words, and the courtiers below the mountain are said to know it as their king's lullaby.* A bishop's letter condemning "the wordless hymn of the Ember Court, which is sung, they say, by souls already claimed."

The anthem of the Ember Court. And Wren sang it the way other children hummed nursery rhymes—not learned, but *remembered*. The way you remember your own heartbeat.

Now the page in his hands explained it, and explanation was so much worse than mystery. She hadn't learned the anthem. She'd been *near* it. Two years old, held in the antechambers of a cathedral of banked fires, soaking in its music the way a child soaks in milk and warmth—before her soul was drawn out and pawned like a silver spoon, before the guild bought the space she used to occupy with a promise of protection no one had ever managed to keep.

*Lightly worn,* the old keeper had written. As if a soul were a coat.

Quill pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw sparks.

Here was the thing about Brother Quill that his order had never forgiven and never quite managed to burn out of him: he prayed constantly, and he believed completely, and neither of those facts had ever once been rewarded. Vesper did not speak. Vesper did not sign miracles across the sky. For twenty years Quill had knelt in the dark and offered up his voice to a god who took it and gave back nothing—not silence even, really, because silence implied presence, implied an ear. Just absence, vast and upholstered, like kneeling inside a closed piano.

And Quill had stayed anyway. That was the whole of his faith, and he knew it was a strange one: he loved a god who could not love him back, and he had decided, somewhere in his thirties, that this was actually the purest form of love available to a mortal—to pour yourself out into a vessel with no bottom, and keep pouring, and call the pouring itself the reward.

So he knew something about loving someone who couldn't answer. And he knew, with the terrible expertise of the abandoned, exactly what it did to a person to learn they had been given away.

* * *

He climbed the archive stairs slowly, the page folded into his cassock against his chest, and stood a while in the doorway of the common room without going in.

Wren was there.

She was upside down, draped backward over a chair with her legs hooked over the backrest and her head hanging off the seat, practicing coin rolls across her knuckles while she argued with Tetch about whether a moving city needed a map or a schedule. Her braid hung nearly to the floorboards. The firelight caught the coins as they walked her fingers, little gold moons rising and setting.

"—all I'm saying," she was saying, "is that if a street can decide to be somewhere else, then the street has opinions, and you should be negotiating with it, not charting it—"

"Streets don't negotiate," Tetch said.

"These streets do. You told me yourself the pass under the mountain rearranges to keep secrets. That's not geology, that's *policy*."

"You," Tetch said with grudging wonder, "are the worst apprentice cartographer in the history of the discipline."

"I'm not your apprentice, I'm your accomplice. There's a difference and it's mostly the pay."

Marisol laughed from the corner where she was oiling a sword that had no business being oiled in a counting house. And at the big table, bent over tonight's ledgers with his sleeves rolled and his pen moving in that precise, patient hand, Paloma looked up—looked at Wren, the way he always looked at her, which was the way a man looks at the one candle he trusts in a house full of drafts—and Quill saw it plain as scripture:

Paloma knew. Or he half-knew, or he feared it the way you fear a diagnosis before the physician speaks. He had found his own name in a prophecy book a week ago, dated to tonight, and he had not told anyone what the rest of the page said, and now he watched this upside-down girl roll stolen gold across her knuckles with an expression like a man watching the tide come in over something he loves.

They were all of them keeping pieces of the truth in separate pockets, Quill realized. Like thieves dividing a take. And the girl they were robbing was humming the anthem of the court that owned her.

Wren flipped upright, spotted him, grinned. "Brother Quill! Tell Tetch that maps are just opinions with borders."

"I will tell you," Quill said, and was proud of how steady his voice came out, "that both of you should eat something before the kitchen closes."

He did not go to dinner. He went to the chapel instead—the guild's chapel, which was really a converted storeroom with a cold hearth and no icon, because you cannot paint a god who declined to have a face. Quill had furnished it himself, minimally: one bench, one candle, one empty alcove where the altar should be. The emptiness was the point. He'd told the guild members who asked that Vesper required no image. Privately, he suspected the god required nothing at all, and that the empty alcove was less a shrine than a wound he'd decided to keep open on purpose, the way you keep a window open for someone who left.

He lit the candle and knelt, and the page crackled faintly against his chest when he bowed his head.

*Talk to me,* he prayed, as he had prayed ten thousand times before. *Just once. Not for me—I've made my peace with me. Tell me what to do about her.*

The flame stood straight. The alcove stayed empty. The god kept his century-old silence, and Quill knelt in it, and grieved, and raged, and noticed—as he always noticed, and always suppressed—that his rage had a specific shape. It wasn't aimed at the birth parents who'd sold her; hunger makes theologians of us all, and he could imagine the arithmetic of desperation that ended in a signature. It wasn't even aimed at the Court, not primarily, though there was a furnace of anger reserved for them that he intended to feed for years.

It was aimed upward. At the empty alcove. At the god who had let a two-year-old's soul be entered into a ledger of debts and had said nothing—had, as far as any record showed, *done* nothing—for a hundred years while the guild raised her, fed her, taught her to pick locks and fold bandages and laugh with her mouth wide open.

If Vesper saw all things, then Vesper had seen Wren. Had seen her every morning for seventeen years, singing his enemy's anthem without knowing it, wearing a soul with someone else's lien stamped on it. And had said nothing.

*Unless,* whispered a thought Quill had never permitted himself before, and which arrived now with the weight of a key turning in a lock, *unless the silence isn't absence.*

*Unless the silence is a debt of its own.*

He thought of Paloma's impossible inventory. The bottled thunderstorm. The honest tear in its vial. The clock that ran on regret. Things that had no business existing, catalogued and shelved by a man who served a silent god and had never once wondered aloud why his guild attracted wonders the way a wound attracts flies.

He thought: *what if the god didn't leave? What if the god hid? What if a god fleeing a court that trades in names and voices would do exactly what a frightened man does—pour himself into vessels, scatter his substance into safekeeping, and go quiet so thoroughly that even his priests mistook it for death?*

What if the silence Quill had worshipped for twenty years wasn't neglect?

What if it was concealment? What if every prayer he'd poured into the dark had been received by something crouched in the dark, hiding, listening, *unable to answer because answering would make a sound, and sound could be traced, and traced could be seized*—

Quill put his hand over his mouth. An old habit. In the Vigilant Quiet, novices covered their mouths during prayer to remind themselves that speech belonged to God. He hadn't done it in years. He did it now because he was afraid of what might come out of him—some heresy too large for his chest, some terrible hope.

Because if the god had hidden, then the god had chosen the guild. Had chosen *these* people, these debtors and oathbreakers and pickpockets, to be his scattered reliquary. And had chosen, perhaps, one soul in particular to stand closest to—one soul pawned to his enemies, planted among his treasures like a seed behind enemy lines.

Or—and here the thought curdled—had the god simply failed her too? Was the silence just silence after all, and the pattern Quill kept seeing just the human talent for finding faces in fire?

He didn't know. That was the truth of it, the whole truth, and it sat in him like a swallowed stone: *he did not know whether his god had abandoned this girl or entrusted her, and the difference between those two words was the difference between everything and nothing.*

But he knew what the page in his cassock said. He knew the term ended in nine days. He knew that a century of guildkeepers had passed this secret down like a hot coal, each one writing *do better than we did*, and each one failing, and now the coal was in his hands.

* * *

He found Wren an hour later, alone, in the guild's roof garden—which was a generous term for six planters of hardy herbs and one stubborn fig tree growing out of a rain barrel—but it was her place, the highest point of the highest roof, where she went to count stars the way other people counted sheep.

She was lying on her back on the warm tiles with her hands laced behind her head. She didn't turn when his boots scuffed the gravel. She always heard him coming; she pretended not to, as a courtesy, the way he pretended not to notice her lifting his keys.

"The kitchen saved you a bowl," he said. "It's the good stew. Marisol threatened the cook, so it's extra good."

"I'll get it later." A pause. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you do when you've read something sad in the basement and you're deciding who gets to know." She rolled her head sideways and looked up at him, and her eyes in the starlight were quick and dark and utterly without fear, and that was the worst part, that was the part that made his throat close: she trusted them. Completely. Without terms. The one thing she owned that no court could lien.

"Wren," he said. "If I told you something heavy—you know I'd help you carry it."

"Obviously." She said it the way you'd confirm the sun. "That's what family's for. Why d'you think I haven't picked your pocket in months?" A grin, crooked. "Mostly."

He sat down beside her on the tiles. Below them the city glittered, ordinary and oblivious. Nine days until the season turned. Somewhere south, a mountain full of banked fires waited for its property to mature.

Tell her, said one voice in him, the voice that had gotten him defrocked twice: she deserves the truth; a lie of kindness is still a cage, and she has spent her whole life proving she can carry anything you hand her.

Shield her, said the other voice, quieter, older, the voice of every keeper who had written *do better than we did* and then done worse: she gets nine days of not knowing; give her that; grief keeps; childhood doesn't.

And underneath both voices, a third, which was perhaps the only honest one: *you want to tell her because carrying it alone is killing you, and you are dressing your need as her right.*

Wren reached over and tugged his sleeve. "Hey. Whatever it is—" she shrugged, casual as a card sharp, "—you'll figure out how to say it. You always do. You're the worst liar in the building, Brother Quill. It's honestly inspiring."

He laughed. It came out broken in the middle.

And that was when the door to the roof stairs banged open, and Paloma came through it fast, coat still on, a book clamped under one arm and a look on his face that Quill recognized from the mirror an hour ago—the face of a man holding a page that burned.

Paloma stopped when he saw them. Looked at Wren. Looked at Quill. And Quill watched him arrive, in real time, at the same conclusion Quill had been circling all night: *he knows something too.*

"We need to talk," Paloma said. "All three of us. And Quill—" his voice dropped, roughened, "—whatever you found in the basement today. I need you to show me. Because I found something too, and I think our pages are from the same book."

Above them, the stars wheeled on, indifferent. In nine days, the season would turn.

And Wren, looking between the two of them with her grin fading into something older and sharper—the look of a pickpocket who has just felt the mark's hand close on her wrist—said quietly:

"Then start talking. Because I've been stealing information since before either of you could spell it, and I *know* when a room is hiding something from me."
