## 8. Wren Refuses to Be Rescued

The trouble with being raised by thieves is that nobody ever teaches you how to be found.

Wren had learned the guild's lessons too well. She knew how to move through a crowd like water finding cracks, how to make herself a rumor instead of a girl, how to read the weight of a man's purse by the way he sat. So when Paloma started whispering in corners and Marisol started oiling her sword at midnight, Wren did what any self-respecting pickpocket would do.

She followed them.

Which was how she came to be standing in the Ember Court wearing a scullery maid's rough gray smock, three sizes too big, with ash to her elbows and a copper basin balanced on one hip like she'd been born carrying other people's filth.

*You absolute idiots*, she thought, watching Paloma cross the great gallery below with his appraiser's satchel and his careful face. *You beautiful, impossible idiots.*

* * *

She'd stowed away in the coal cart on the second night of their journey, folded between sacks of black rock that whispered to each other when they thought no one was listening. Everything under Mount Cendre whispered. Tetch had warned them about the roads that rearranged themselves; nobody had warned Wren that the *darkness* rearranged itself too, breathing around you like something with lungs.

She'd nearly been caught twice. Once by a ferryman whose face was all shadow except for two coins of light where eyes should have been—he'd looked straight through her disguise and said nothing, which was worse than saying something—and once by a courtier made almost entirely of candle smoke who had paused beside her cart and inhaled deeply, as if tasting the air for uninvited souls.

Wren had held her breath and thought about the guild kitchen: Marisol burning the porridge, Tetch stealing salt out of pure habit, Quill saying grace over food no god could hear. She thought about Paloma teaching her to count cards with a deck missing three queens because he'd lost them to her fair and square.

*You are not alone*, she told herself. *Even here. Especially here.*

And then she'd arrived, and seen the Court, and understood why Paloma's voice went flat whenever he talked about it.

It was a cathedral built of banked fires—fires that didn't burn so much as *wait*, coiled in braziers and hearths and hollowed-out hearts of stone, all of them dim red, all of them patient. The courtiers moved among them in robes the color of cooling embers, and they traded as they walked: a memory of first snow for an hour of true courage, the name of a childhood dog for the ability to forgive. Coins were for tourists down here. The real currency was pieces of yourself.

Wren had spent her first day hauling ash and slops through corridors that rearranged when no one watched, memorizing everything, cataloguing exits the way Paloma had taught her. *Always know two ways out. Always know what you're willing to leave behind.*

She'd found six ways out of the Court. None of them worked for someone whose soul was kept in a vault.

Because that was the thing—the thing that made her hands shake every time she let herself think about it directly, so she mostly didn't. Her soul was *here*. Had been here since before she was born, apparently, pawned by parents she couldn't remember for debts she'd never agreed to, sitting in some vault like a winter coat left at an inn. And in a few days, when the season turned, the Court intended to burn it for fuel.

Unless she stayed.

* * *

The offer had come on her second evening, delivered by a courtier whose face was kind the way a warm bath is kind—enveloping, thoughtless, slightly drowning.

"You're the Ashgrove girl," the courtier had said, appearing beside her in the scullery corridor while she scrubbed a cauldron that seemed to grow dirt back faster than she could remove it. Not a question. "The maturing lot. We don't usually speak to inventory, but you've been *observing* us, little thief. It's charming. Very few souls take notes."

"I don't know what you mean," Wren said, in the flat accent of a hundred scullery girls before her.

"Of course not." The courtier smiled with teeth like banked coals. "Here is what we mean regardless. Stay. Walk willingly into the season's turning, and the Court will give you anything within our power. Every skill in this place. A century of borrowed time. A name that opens doors in nine kingdoms. Your parents sold you cheaply—we will pay you *lavishly*. All you must do is stop running from what you already are."

"And if I don't stay?"

"Then in three days we consume what we hold, as is written, as was always written." The courtier tilted its head. "We are not cruel, child. Cruelty is wasteful. But contracts are contracts, and yours is very old, and very clear."

"What did they get?" Wren heard herself ask. "My parents. What did they sell me *for*?"

The courtier's smile softened into something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. "A good harvest. Three healthy winters. And the ability to forget they had ever had a daughter." It drifted backward into the corridor's shadows. "Think about our offer, little thief. You of all people should appreciate a well-structured deal."

Wren had stood there with her hands in cold dishwater and realized, with terrible clarity, that she was going to say yes to *something*—just not anything the courtier would recognize as agreement.

* * *

She found Paloma on the third day, in a side gallery where the fires burned low enough to cast more shadow than light. He was crouched before a display case with his ledger open on his knee, doing the thing he did—that slow, total absorption, the world narrowing to ink and appraisal. Even disguised as a stranger, even in enemy territory, Paloma Ashgrove was exactly himself, and the sight of it hit Wren somewhere behind her ribs like a fist wrapped in velvet.

She stepped out of the alcove where she'd been waiting for forty minutes and said, "If you're going to rob this place, you're casing the wrong cabinet. The lock's decorative. The case is the ward."

Paloma didn't startle—Paloma never startled—but every line of him went still in a new way, the way a man goes still when the floor turns out to be thinner than he believed. He turned. His eyes found the smock, the ash, the basin she was still stupidly carrying, and then found her face beneath it, and she watched three years of brotherhood pass across his expression in about a second and a half: disbelief, horror, fury, and underneath all of it, so deep he probably didn't know it was there, relief.

"Wren." His voice came out strangled. "What in Vesper's silent name—"

"Shh." She glanced up the gallery. "Courtiers everywhere. I'm a maid. You're an appraiser. We've never met." She set down the basin, walked past him, and pretended to scrub the base of a brazier while he knelt frozen by his case. From a distance they were just two servants attending their work.

"How." His whisper was barely air. "How are you *here*. How did you even know—"

"I live with you people. Marisol polishes her sword when she's scared. Quill copies scripture when he's lying to himself. Tetch starts drawing maps of places she's never been. You—" she glanced at him sideways, "—you re-ink your ledger entries until they shine. I've known this was coming for a month. I packed before you did."

"That is not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, with the careful control of a man handling something explosive. "Wren, we came here to save you."

"I know." She scrubbed harder than the brazier required. "That's the problem."

"It's the *problem*?"

"Yes, Paloma, it's the problem, because you decided. All of you. You read your spooky book and called in your debts and marched off under a mountain without once—" her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, forced it steady through sheer spite, "—without once asking me. My soul. My name in your prophecy. My funeral you're all planning. And nobody thought, hmm, perhaps the person whose soul is at stake might have an opinion."

"We were protecting you."

"You were *selling* me." She turned to face him fully now, consequences be damned. "Don't you see it? That's exactly what my parents did. They decided what my life was worth and made the deal without me. And now you've done the same thing, just in the other direction—you've priced my rescue without asking what I want to pay for it. Rescue without consent is just another kind of selling, Paloma. You taught me that. First lesson. Nothing belongs to anyone until they claim it."

He stared at her, and she watched the truth land—not all at once, but in pieces, the way heavy things land. His jaw worked. His hand went to the ledger at his belt, that reflexive touch, and then stopped halfway, as if he'd caught himself reaching for scripture in the middle of a sin.

"When did you get wise?" he said finally, hoarse.

"The same day you got blind. Somewhere around the time everyone started treating me like a thing to be saved instead of a person to be asked."

* * *

They talked in the language of servants, heads bent over work, voices pitched under the fire-hum. Wren told him about the offer. All of it. The skills, the time, the lavish payment, the courtier's soft cruelty. Paloma listened the way he listened to clients describing impossible inventory—completely, without blinking, filing every word.

"They'll give me anything," she said. "That's what frightens me. Not the burning. The *shopping list*. They know exactly what I am—they know I'm a thief, they know what I'd want, and they're offering it all on a silver tray because staying is cheaper for them than losing me."

"And what do you want?" Paloma asked. "Honestly. Not bravado. What does Wren want?"

It was such a Paloma question—precise, unflinching, genuinely asking—that it undid her a little. She looked at her hands, gray with ash, scarred at the knuckles from ten years of other people's pockets.

"I want to choose," she said. "That's all. I've never gotten to choose anything about myself. Not my name—guild gave me that. Not my trade—guild gave me that. Not even my own soul—it was sold before I had teeth. This is the one decision in my whole life that's actually mine, and everyone I love keeps trying to make it for me because they love me." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know how insulting it is to be loved like that? Being loved shouldn't feel like being handled."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, rawer, stripped of the appraiser's smoothness.

"If you walk out of here with us," he said, "it's because you chose to walk. If you stay—if you take their offer—I'll hate it until I die, but I won't drag you. I'd rather lose you to your own choice than win you against your will."

"That," Wren said, blinking hard, "is the first correct thing anyone's said to me since I got here."

"Don't tell the others. Marisol will cry and blame herself for a decade."

"She already cries when she thinks no one's watching. I steal her handkerchiefs back afterward so she doesn't run out." Wren paused. "Also, Quill knows I'm here."

Paloma closed his eyes. "*Of course* he does."

"He saw right through the disguise in about four seconds. Said the gods hide better than that. Then he gave me extra bread from his pack and asked me to please not tell you he knew, because and I quote, 'the boy needs to believe he can still protect someone.'"

"He's a heretic and a menace."

"He's family," Wren said simply. "So are you. That's the whole point, Paloma. Whatever I choose—it's not choosing between the Court and the guild. It's choosing whether I walk forward as myself or as somebody else's asset. Either way, I know exactly where home is." She jabbed a finger at him. "But if you try to smuggle me out in a barrel, I will pickpocket you in your sleep for a year."

"Noted."

* * *

They might have managed a longer conversation—a plan, even, a real one—but the fires chose that moment to change.

Every banked flame in the gallery flared at once, all down the corridor and beyond it, hundreds of waiting fires suddenly *awake*, burning tall and gold and loud. The hum became a chord. The chord became a voice layered from a thousand embers speaking together:

**"THE AUCTION BEGINS AT MOONRISE. ALL LOTS WILL BE PRESENTED. ALL DEBTS WILL BE SETTLED. THE CENTURY CLOSES AS THE CENTURY OPENED: WITH A SALE."**

Courtiers poured into the galleries from every direction, robes streaming, faces lit ravenous. Somewhere a bell tolled with a sound like a coin dropped down an endless well. Two scullery hands grabbed Wren's arms and hauled her toward the kitchens—inventory had to be presented too, and maids who lingered near valuable display cases attracted exactly the wrong kind of attention.

She twisted free long enough to grab Paloma's sleeve and press something into his palm: a scrap of paper, stolen from the Queen's own antechamber three nights ago, covered in cramped ember-gilt script.

"Auction order," she breathed. "Lot numbers. Yours is number nine. And Paloma—" the scullery hands were pulling her away now, and she shouted the last part low and fierce across the gathering roar of the Court, "—whatever you're planning, whatever you wrote in that book a hundred years before I was born—*I'm* the one who decides how this ends. Not you. Not the Queen. Me."

Then the crowd swallowed her, small and gray and absolutely unafraid, and Paloma stood alone in the flare of a thousand waking fires, holding a stolen page, reading the auction listing for lot nine:

*One soul, lightly worn, maturing at season's turn.*

And beneath it, in smaller script, added recently, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh as though it had been written minutes ago:

*Reserve price: one divine ember.*

Paloma's blood went cold. The Queen knew. The Queen had always known what slept inside him.

Moonrise was coming. And Wren had just promised to decide everything—which meant he had maybe four hours to make sure that when she decided, there would still be something left to decide *with*.
