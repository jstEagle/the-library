## 9. Auction of Impossible Things

The Ember Court held its auctions in the Nave of Banked Fires, a cathedral so vast that the ceiling was less architecture than weather—a slow drift of embers that had been rising for centuries and would, presumably, go on rising until the mountain forgot it had ever been cold. Paloma stood at the edge of the crowd in his appraiser's coat, hands folded behind him like a man with nothing to want, and let the heat of the place write its slow arithmetic on his skin.

He had counted the exits twice. There were three that the Court wanted people to know about, and one that only existed when you stopped looking for it. Tetch had drawn him a map of the first three and a shrug for the fourth. *You'll feel it,* she'd said. *Like a door left open in a house you thought was empty.*

The courtiers gathered in tiers of benches carved from coal-black stone, their faces lit from below by channels of fire that ran between the rows like irrigation ditches. Some wore masks of hammered copper. Some wore no faces at all, just smooth stretches of skin where features should have been—courtiers who had traded away their expressions one at a time and now had nothing left to smile with. They murmured to each other in a language made entirely of prices.

At the front, on a dais of fused glass, stood the auctioneer: a tall figure in robes the color of banked coals, whose voice arrived slightly before his lips moved, as though sound were eager to get away from him.

"Honored bidders," the auctioneer said, and the word *honored* came out tasting like ash. "Tonight marks the hundredth turning since our last great sale. The Court has emptied its vaults of wonders. We have memories of places that no longer exist. We have the last honest lie ever told in the northern kingdoms. We have—" here he paused, savoring it, letting the firelight gutter along the hem of his robe, "—one soul. Lightly worn. Maturing at season's turn."

Paloma's hands did not move. He had trained them not to move for twenty years, through audits and ambushes and the long silent years of praying to a god who never answered. But something under his sternum pulled tight as a ledger line.

*Lightly worn.* As if Wren's soul were a coat someone else had grown out of. As if the girl who hummed the Court's anthem without knowing why, who could lift a purse off a duke in a crowded market and leave a flower in its place because she thought it was funny—as if all of that were *wear.*

"Provenance," said a courtier near the front, in a voice like coins being counted.

"The soul was pawned," the auctioneer said. "A century past. Held in trust against the season's turning. The original holders are... no longer in a position to redeem it." A ripple went through the tiers—not laughter exactly, but the sound a fire makes when it finds something new to eat. "Condition is excellent. The soul has been kept unclaimed, which is to say untouched, which is to say *hungry.* Souls kept hungry mature beautifully."

Paloma breathed out slowly through his nose. Around him, the bidding began on lesser lots—a vial containing the memory of a summer that had happened to someone else; a jar of thunderstorm, which drew a lazy murmur until the auctioneer clarified that this was *not* the famous one, merely a cousin; a box of borrowed time, sold by the decade.

He barely heard any of it. He was watching the vault doors.

They stood behind and above the dais, set into the mountain's living rock: two slabs of black iron taller than Marisol, worked over with reliefs of hands—hundreds of hands, open palms, grasping palms, hands releasing things. Between them burned a seal shaped like a flame inside a lock, and the flame moved wrong, burning downward, eating toward its own root. Two wardens flanked the doors, figures in ash-grey livery whose faces were smooth as eggs. They hadn't blinked since Paloma started counting.

*Tetch needs the mechanism,* he reminded himself. *Marisol needs the warden's schedule. You need to be forgettable.*

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. The bidding was theater—theatrics cost nothing if you knew they were theatrics. Let the Court think a guild appraiser wanted the lot. Let them dismiss him. Dismissal was a door, and Paloma Ashgrove had spent his whole life walking through doors people didn't bother to guard, because nobody guards the way out for a man they've already decided doesn't matter.

His invisibility, Tetch had once told him, was a kind of superpower. He'd never had the heart to tell her it felt more like a symptom.

* * *

The lesser lots went fast. A courtier bought the memory of a dead city and wept while paying for it, which Paloma understood was considered good manners. Another purchased an hour of sunlight from a country that had been conquered two hundred years ago, and the whole tier applauded when the auctioneer opened the box and let the light spill out—one hour of some vanished afternoon, gold as honey, moving across the stone floor while everyone watched it go.

Paloma found himself thinking of the Gilded Ledger's back room, of the shelf where impossible things waited for owners who never came. The bottled thunderstorm sat there between the debt of one honest tear and a key that opened a door in a house that had burned down. Vesper's inventory. Though he hadn't known it yet then—though he still wasn't sure what he knew now—he had dusted those shelves the way other men tended graves.

Then the auctioneer's voice changed pitch, and the cathedral went quiet the way a forest goes quiet.

"And now," the auctioneer said, "the lot you have waited a century for."

Two wardens carried it up the steps: a lantern of green glass, and inside it hung a small light, no bigger than a candleflame, that moved the way a sleeping animal breathes. It wasn't bright. It was the opposite of bright—it drew the eye the way a wound draws the eye, and once you saw it you couldn't stop seeing how much room it took up in the world for something so small.

Wren's soul.

It turned slowly in its glass, and Paloma felt it turn in him too, some sympathetic rotation, the way his own chest had learned her breathing patterns over ten years of hearing her sleep in the loft above the counting room.

"Lot ninety-nine," the auctioneer said softly. "One soul. Lightly worn. Maturing at season's turn. The opening bid is one cherished memory, freely surrendered."

The tiers leaned forward. Somewhere in the cathedral, a fire channel crackled.

Paloma stepped out of the crowd.

He did it deliberately, unhurriedly, the way he approached every negotiation: a man who knew exactly what he could afford and had already decided what he couldn't. The courtiers turned to look at him—masks and blank faces and eyes like spent coals—and he felt their attention slide over him, snag briefly on the appraiser's coat, and begin to dismiss him. Good. Right on schedule.

"A bid from the floor," the auctioneer said, amused. "Name your offer, appraiser."

"I offer," Paloma said, and reached into his coat, "a bottled thunderstorm."

He set it on the bidding table: a squat jar of dark glass, seething faintly, lightning coiling inside it like a worm of white light. It had sat on the Ledger's shelves for sixty years. No one had ever claimed it. It was, according to the oldest inventory page, *priceless, do not open, do not sell, ask Paloma why.*

He had asked himself why many times. He still didn't have an answer. That was rather the point of it.

The cathedral's attention sharpened. Even the fires seemed to lean in.

"A storm," the auctioneer repeated. "Captured whole?"

"Whole," Paloma said. "Rain, wind, and the particular silence that comes just before the first strike. It has been aging for six decades. Storms improve, like grief."

Someone in the upper tiers laughed—a dry, papery sound—and then stopped laughing, because the auctioneer raised one hand, and the whole cathedral remembered itself.

"A worthy object," the auctioneer conceded. "Do I hear a counter?"

And that was when the courtier rose.

She sat in the third tier, in a box draped with grey silk, and she was old in the way that mountains are old—not wrinkled but *worn*, her face bearing the same patient erosion as the walls. She wore a gown stitched with what looked like handwriting, thousands of tiny lines of inked script flowing across the fabric, and when she moved, the words shifted, rearranging themselves into new sentences no one had written yet.

"I counter," she said, "with a century of borrowed time."

The words dropped into the cathedral like a stone into a well, and the well swallowed them without a splash.

Paloma kept his face still. Inside, something cold began walking up his spine.

Borrowed time was the worst currency in the Court—or the best, depending on which side of the transaction you stood on. It meant years taken from someone else, held in trust, spendable. A century of it meant a hundred lives' worth of afternoons, of mornings, of ordinary Tuesdays, all stripped from their owners and bundled into a single offer. It was wealth the way a plague was health.

The auctioneer's smile widened by a degree. "A century of borrowed time," he echoed. "From the collection of Lady Vesperine Sallow. Verified." He glanced down at Paloma, and his voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the cruelest thing Paloma had heard all night. "Appraiser. Would you care to raise?"

Every eye in the cathedral was on him. The blank-faced wardens hadn't moved. The green lantern turned its slow, sleeping turn.

Say yes, said the part of him that was a mercenary. Raise again. Find something. The Ledger had more impossible things; the inventory was deep; somewhere on those shelves there had to be—

No, said the part of him that was a careful man. Because a careful man knew that the moment you fought for a thing in public, you told the whole room what it was worth to you. And the Court traded in worth the way other markets traded in grain.

He had come to lose. He had known, walking in, that he would probably lose. Losing was the plan.

But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different rooms, and this room was very hot.

"I withdraw," Paloma said.

The word came out steady. He was proud of that, later. Steady, flat, the voice of a man closing a book he'd decided not to buy.

The courtier in the script-stitched gown inclined her head to him—a small, courteous, devastating gesture, the nod one gives a child who has brought a handful of pebbles to trade for a horse. The auctioneer's gavel, a rod of black iron tipped with a coal that never quite went out, came down once.

"Sold," he said, "to Lady Sallow. One soul, lightly worn, maturing at season's turn."

The green lantern was carried down the steps and handed up into the grey box, and Paloma watched it go with his hands folded behind his back and his heartbeat doing something quiet and enormous behind his ribs. The little light turned in its glass. He would have sworn—later, telling the others, he swore it—that as it passed him, it flickered. Once. Like recognition. Like a girl tapping a code on a wall.

*Hold on,* he thought at it, with everything he had, with the whole silent vocabulary of a man who had prayed for years to a god who never answered. *Hold on. I'm coming. I'm sorry. Hold on.*

The lantern disappeared into the grey silk, and the auction moved on to the final lot of the evening: a memory of Vesper speaking aloud, priced at one friend's true name. Paloma did not bid on that either. He had already spent everything he had tonight, and it hadn't cost him a single coin.

* * *

Afterward, in the colonnade outside the Nave, where the fire channels cooled to a dull orange and the courtiers drifted past in their masks and their blankness, Paloma stood very still and committed the vault doors to memory.

He counted the wardens' rotations: four minutes, eleven seconds, and a pause at the seal that lasted exactly long enough for a woman with a knight's training to cross thirty feet of open floor. He noted the hinge-side relief of the hands—all of them open-palmed except one, low on the right door, a single hand closed around something small. A handle, maybe. Or a warning. He noted the way the downward-burning flame in the seal guttered whenever the auctioneer's gavel struck, as if the whole mechanism listened to the sound of sales concluding.

Tetch would need all of it. Every second, every seam.

"You bid a storm," said a voice beside him.

Lady Sallow stood there, the green lantern nowhere visible beneath her script-stitched gown, her face wearing the pleasant erosion of a woman who had won. Up close, Paloma could see that the writing on her dress was legible, if you tilted your head. Debts. Hundreds of years of debts, recorded in thread.

"A fine storm," she said. "I nearly let you have the lot for it. But time beats weather, appraiser. Time beats everything. That's rather the point of my collection."

"Congratulations," Paloma said.

"Mm." She studied him with eyes the color of cooling ash. "You withdrew very gracefully. Too gracefully. Men who lose gracefully have usually already decided how they're going to win." She smiled, and the smile was old and tired and, in its way, honest. "I'll tell you a secret, since you amuse me. The soul I bought tonight—I don't intend to consume it. I intend to keep it. On a shelf. In a jar. The way your guild keeps its storms." Her gaze drifted toward the vault doors, toward the single closed hand low on the right. "Some things are worth more unspent. Surely a man of the Gilded Ledger understands that better than anyone."

She patted his arm with one papery hand and drifted away into the colonnade, her gown rewriting itself sentence by sentence, and Paloma stood alone among the cooling fires with the wardens' rotation running in his head—four minutes, eleven seconds—and the shape of a plan assembling itself out of his own humiliation, piece by piece, like a storm deciding whether to break.

Four minutes and eleven seconds.

Marisol would need three.
