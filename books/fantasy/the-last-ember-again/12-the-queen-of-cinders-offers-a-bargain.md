## 12. The Queen of Cinders Offers a Bargain

They came for him in the hour between the auction's end and the Court's midnight, which in a place with no sky was no time at all—only a deepening of the orange gloom, the banked fires along the cathedral walls guttering low as if the mountain itself were holding its breath.

Paloma heard them before he saw them: courtiers whose footsteps made no sound on the ash-strewn floor, because they had traded away their weight years ago and never bought it back. Six of them, tall and graceful and wrong, their faces beautiful the way an unpaid debt is beautiful—smooth, polished, and promising nothing good.

He did not run. Running through the Ember Court would be like running through a ledger you'd already signed; every corridor had his name in it now. He'd learned that much from the auction, from the way the herald's voice had curled around *Paloma Ashgrove* like it was tasting an old vintage.

"His Radiance requests the appraiser," said the foremost courtier. Her smile had too many teeth in it, all of them patient.

"Does His Radiance know I'm not an appraiser?"

"His Radiance knows everything the fire knows." The smile widened a fraction. "And the fire has been watching you since you paid your toll at the gate."

*The false name that burned.* He'd known then that the Court could taste lies. He hadn't known they'd been savoring it ever since, like a spice left on the tongue.

Marisol started forward from where she stood by the auction hall's great doors—one hand already moving to the sword she'd surrendered at the gate, out of habit rather than hope—and Paloma caught her eye and shook his head, small and sharp. *Not yet. Not here. Not with six of them and half the Court watching.*

Her jaw worked. But she'd broken one oath already this week; apparently she could break another, smaller vow, the vow of a friend not to let a friend walk into the dark alone. She let him go. Tetch pressed something into his palm as he passed—a folded scrap of map-paper, blank, because every place in the Court was unmappable, but the crease-lines of it were familiar, and holding it was like holding her hand without holding her hand. Brother Quill murmured something under his breath that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse on the whole burning establishment. With Quill it was usually both.

Then the courtiers closed around him like warm water, and Paloma Ashgrove walked down into the heart of the mountain to meet its queen.

* * *

The throne room was not what he expected, though by then he should have expected nothing else.

He had imagined a forge-hall, perhaps—the popular paintings showed the Queen of Cinders enthroned amid roaring flame, terrible and magnificent. What he found instead was quiet. A long gallery of dark stone, cool enough that his breath almost fogged, lit by a single hearth at the far end so old and so banked that its coals had gone the color of dusk—purple-grey, patient, the fire of something that had been burning down for a very long time and intended to go on doing so.

The Queen sat beside the hearth, not upon any throne, on a plain stone bench like a woman waiting for bread to rise.

She was smaller than the paintings. Older, too—not in face, which was ageless the way all the courtiers were ageless, but in the set of her shoulders, in the way her hands lay folded in her lap like tools put away after a long day's work. Her crown was exactly as described: spent vows, hundreds of them, little rings of dull metal strung on a circlet of iron, each one the color ash goes when the heat is gone out of it forever. They clicked softly when she turned her head.

"You're shorter than the paintings too," she said. Her voice was ordinary. That was somehow the worst thing yet—that the Queen of Cinders sounded like someone's mother, tired at the end of market day.

"I'm not an appraiser," Paloma said.

"No." She gestured to the bench across from her. "Sit. You've been standing in other people's rooms all night pretending to belong to them. It's exhausting. I remember."

He sat. The stone was warm beneath him, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. Everything here stayed warm hours after the fire died. He understood suddenly that the entire Court was built on that principle.

"You wrote the prophecy," he said. "The book my client sold me. A century old. My name in it. Tonight's date in it. Wren's soul in it."

"Yes."

"The ink was a hundred years old. The paper aged true. I checked."

"I would have been disappointed if you hadn't."

"Then how—"

"Ask the question you actually came here asking," the Queen said gently. "You've been carrying it up the mountain like a stone in your boot. Ask it, and we can both stop pretending this is an appraisal."

The hearth ticked. Somewhere far above, the cathedral of fires roared and whispered, ten thousand transactions happening at once—memories for names, names for years, years for warmth—and none of it touched this quiet room.

Paloma looked at the Queen of Cinders, at her crown of dead promises, and asked the stone out of his boot.

"Why does a god write prophecies about a pickpocket girl?"

The silence that followed was long enough that he counted his own heartbeats against it. Eleven. Twelve.

Then the Queen laughed—not cruelly, which startled him most of all. It was a tired laugh, the laugh of someone who has just been reminded of a joke she told herself so long ago she'd forgotten the punchline.

"There it is," she said. "You knew. Some part of you has known since you read your own name in that book, because you are a careful man, Paloma Ashgrove, and careful men do their arithmetic even when they hate the sum." She leaned toward him, and the dusk-colored coals behind her seemed to lean too. "Vesper wrote that prophecy. Vesper, the silent god, the god who abandoned you—the god whose silence you have carried like a wound since you were old enough to pray and receive nothing back."

"Don't," Paloma said quietly.

"He didn't abandon you."

"*Don't.*"

"He couldn't speak," the Queen went on, merciless in her gentleness, "because he sold his voice. To me. To this Court. One hundred years ago, give or take a season—he was never precise about time, gods rarely are. It was the largest transaction this Court has ever recorded. The voice of a god, pawned entire. Do you know what a god's voice buys?" She touched her crown, and the spent vows clicked. "It buys this. Every fire in this mountain. Every memory traded in those halls upstairs. All of it runs on the interest from that single deposit. We have been living off Vesper's voice for a century, the way a ruined family lives off the last good harvest."

Paloma's hands had gone cold in a room full of fire. "Why would he sell it? Gods don't need money. Gods don't need—"

"Gods need to be heard." For the first time, something moved behind the Queen's ageless face—something quick and pained, there and gone like a spark jumping a gap. "A god who cannot be heard stops being a god. Becomes a story, then a rumor, then nothing. He came here desperate. He bargained hard—I'll grant him that; he got better terms than anyone before or since. And then he walked out of my mountain mute, and he spent the rest of his power doing the only thing a voiceless god can do."

"Writing," Paloma said. His mouth had gone dry.

"Writing." The Queen nodded. "Prophecies. Ledgers. Little notes tucked into the world's margins. He could not say *beware*, so he wrote *beware*. He could not say *I'm sorry*, so he wrote dates, and names, and let the people who loved him find their own grief in the margins." She paused. "He wrote yours a hundred years before you were born. That's not abandonment, mercenary. That's a man leaving instructions."

The floor dropped.

Not literally—the stone held, the hearth burned, the Queen sat watching him with those tired, ordinary eyes. But Paloma felt the drop anyway, the vertigo of a man who has built his whole house on a certainty and just heard the foundation crack. Because he had built everything on the silence. His whole life. The guild took him in because he was unclaimed; the Ledger trusted him because he wanted for nothing beyond inventory and order; he had made a philosophy out of being unforsaken-by-attention, a man so thoroughly abandoned by his god that no god could ask anything of him. He had prayed as a boy and gotten silence, and he had decided—carefully, deliberately, the way he catalogued everything—that silence was the answer. *You are on your own. Plan accordingly.*

And now the Queen of Cinders was telling him the silence was a receipt. Proof of purchase. His god hadn't stopped speaking to him; his god had *paid* his voice into this vault of embers, and every prayer Paloma had ever sent up into the dark had been arriving, all these years, at a teller's window with no funds to answer it.

"He wrote Wren's soul into the book," Paloma managed. "Why? Why her?"

"Because souls are the one currency I cannot mint," the Queen said. "Everything else burns and returns—memories can be re-remembered, names can be re-sworn, even years can be borrowed back. But a soul given up freely stays given. Unless." She held up one finger, and the gesture was so much like a guild clerk flagging a clause that Paloma nearly laughed, hysteria rising in him like floodwater. "Unless the contract was written with an escape written into it. Which Vesper did. Of course he did. He was always better at contracts than I was."

"What escape?"

"That brings us to the bargain." The Queen rose at last, and her crown chimed, and for a moment she looked every inch the monster from the paintings—until she folded her hands again, and was once more a tired woman by a dying fire. "Your client who sold you the prophecy book. You assumed the Court sent him. We didn't. Vesper arranged that sale a century ago, timed to the turning of the season, timed to the maturing of the girl's soul-debt. He has been moving pieces on this board since before your grandmother drew breath, and every piece converges on tonight. On you."

"On me," Paloma repeated. "I'm a mercenary. I keep books. I'm nobody's piece."

"You are the keeper of the Gilded Ledger's impossible inventory." The Queen's eyes fixed on him, and the banked coals flared once, briefly, the color of dawn. "Did you never wonder why your guild collects things no one can use? A bottled thunderstorm. A debt of one honest tear. A key that opens a door that hasn't been built. A hundred items of pure impossibility, gathering dust on your shelves, and no client has ever claimed one?" She smiled, and it was the saddest thing he had seen since Marisol talked about the order she'd lost. "Those aren't inventory, Paloma. Those are *ashes*. When a god sells his voice, he doesn't sell himself all at once—he scatters. He hides the burning parts of himself inside ordinary objects, in the care of ordinary people, and he trusts the world to lose them slowly enough that no one notices a god going out."

The hearth-room tilted. Or Paloma did. He gripped the edge of the bench and thought—absurdly, desperately—of the warehouse above the guild hall, of the long shelves, of dust motes hanging in the lamplight while he ran his fingers down spines and jars and boxes, checking seals, noting condition, keeping everything safe. Twenty years of it. Twenty years of tending a collection everyone mocked, guarding junk no one wanted, and it had never been junk. It had been a dying god, shelved in the dark, and Paloma had been his caretaker the whole time without ever knowing he was kneeling at an altar.

"You think I don't know what that's like," the Queen said softly. "Living beside something holy and calling it clutter."

"Why are you telling me this?" His voice came out rough. "You're the one who holds his voice. You win if he stays silent. So why hand me the truth like a knife?"

"Because I am going to offer you a trade, and I want you to make it knowing everything." She reached into the folds of her gown and produced something small, cupped in her palm like an egg of coals: a mote of light, deep gold shot through with red, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. Even at a distance Paloma felt it—the same warmth he felt sometimes from the strangest items on his shelves, the bottled storm, the honest tear. Bigger, though. Older. *The original stock.*

"This is an ember of Vesper. Not his voice—that sleeps in my deepest vault, and even I dare not wake it—but a piece of his own fire, the self he hid best of all, because he hid it in the last place I would ever look." The Queen's eyes did not leave his. "Inside a man. Inside a keeper of ledgers. Inside you, Paloma Ashgrove. It has been sleeping in you since the night the guild found you on their step, unclaimed, unburned, unremarkable. He poured it into an orphan because orphans are never missed, and he trusted the Ledger to keep you the way you kept everything else."

"I don't feel like a god's hiding place."

"You feel like a man who has never once been cold," the Queen said, "and told yourself it was good circulation."

He opened his mouth to deny it and found, searching back through twenty years of memories, that he couldn't. Fires had always leaned toward him. Candle flames steadied when he passed. Wren used to joke about it—*you're the guild's lucky charm, Pommie, nothing ever quite goes out near you*—and he had laughed and filed it away under coincidence, because filing things away was what he did.

"Now," said the Queen of Cinders, and the weariness fell away from her like a cloak, and what remained was a thousand years of hunger wearing a mother's voice, "the bargain. The girl's soul matures at the turning of the season. In hours. When it ripens, I consume it, and the Court survives another century, and everything proceeds as written—including the parts Vesper wrote, which I have read more times than I can count and still do not fully understand. Or—" she lifted the ember higher, and its light painted her ageless face gold, "—you give me what sleeps in you. Freely. Willingly. A god's own fire, returned to the Court that holds his voice. In exchange, the girl walks out of my mountain with her soul in her chest, the debt annulled, the prophecy fulfilled in the only way it was ever going to be fulfilled. One burning thing for one burning thing. Fair terms. I'll even throw in your friends' safe passage, and my personal word—which is worth more than my crown—that no one follows you home."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then at season's turn I eat a child's soul, and you spend the rest of your life knowing you had the price in your pocket the whole time."

The words landed in the quiet room like stones in still water, and Paloma watched the ripples spread and thought: *she means it. She'd prefer yes, but she'll take no, because either way she feeds.*

"You want his fire," he said slowly. "Why? You have his voice. You have a century of interest. Why does the Queen of Cinders need one more ember?"

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—the mask slipped entirely, and he saw down through the ageless face into something so old and so lonely that he forgot to breathe. Then it was gone, sealed over, and the Queen was only a monarch again, folding her hands.

"A woman likes to hold the whole of what she's owed," she said. "Consider it. Consider it quickly. The season turns tonight, and the fires are already hungry."

Paloma rose on legs that felt borrowed. At the door of the quiet room he stopped, because there was one question left, the one under all the others, the one that had been in his boot longer than the rest.

"When he wrote the prophecy," he said, without turning around, "did he know I'd choose right?"

Behind him, the coals breathed. The crown clicked. And the Queen of Cinders said, in a voice gone soft and strange:

"He hoped. That's what embers are, keeper of ledgers. Hope that refuses to go out." A pause. "Go and decide which fire you are."

He walked back up toward the noise and the light and the auction halls full of traded lives, and in his chest, beneath twenty years of careful bookkeeping, something turned over in its sleep—and warmed him from the inside, like a hearth that had been lit all along.
