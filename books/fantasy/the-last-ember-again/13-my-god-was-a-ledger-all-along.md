## 13. My God Was a Ledger All Along

The corridor behind the Queen's audience chamber smelled of cold iron and old smoke, and Paloma walked it the way he walked every aisle of the Gilded Ledger—slowly, with his hands clasped behind his back, as though the walls might owe him money.

They had thrown him out of the vault. Not violently. The Queen's chamberlain, a tall courtier whose face was a smear of banked orange light, had simply appeared beside him and said, *The vault is closed to appraisers after dark,* and Paloma had understood that this was not a rule but a mercy, and that he had seen exactly what he was meant to see and one thing more.

One thing more.

He had seen the shelf.

It had been at the back of the vault, past the lanterns of hanging souls, past the jars of sold summers and pawned first kisses—a plain wooden shelf, unremarkable, holding a row of objects that made his breath go strange in his throat. A bottled thunderstorm, its glass sweating lightning. A debt of one honest tear, sealed in wax. A key that opened no door but every silence. A candle stub that refused, against all law, to burn down.

He knew these things. He had dusted them for eleven years.

*Impossible inventory,* the guild called them. Items that came in through the front door of the Gilded Ledger with no provenance, no seller, no price anyone could name. They sat in the deep catalog, section nine, subcategory *unclassifiable*, and every auditor before him had tried to appraise them and failed, and every client who asked to buy them was turned away with a polite cough and a lie about pending valuation.

Paloma had always assumed they were curiosities. Oddments. The residue of a hundred years of strange trade drifting downstream and pooling in his cellar.

Standing in the Ember Court's vault, seeing those same objects—his objects, his dust, his careful handwritten tags—arranged on a shelf in a cathedral of fire, he felt the floor of his understanding give way like rotted decking.

Because the Court did not keep curiosities.

The Court kept *pieces of gods.*

* * *

He found an alcove off the corridor, a niche where a brazier had gone out and no one had bothered to relight it, and he sat down on the cold stone and let himself remember.

Flashback: he is nineteen, newly bound to Vesper, kneeling in the guild's chapel-of-convenience—which is to say, a broom closet with a chair in it—and the god says nothing. Vesper never says anything. That is the whole of the faith: you speak, and something vast and warm listens, and nothing comes back, and you learn to call the listening an answer.

His mentor, old Aldous Vane, keeper of the ledger before him, had explained it with the patience of a man describing tide pools to a child. *The silent god hears everything, boy. He simply stopped talking. Some say he never started. Some say he lost the words. Aldous had smiled at that, a dry papery smile. Lost them where, eh? Gods don't misplace their voices like housekeys.*

Paloma had accepted it. He was good at accepting things. It was why the guild trusted him with the impossible inventory—he didn't need things to make sense. He needed them to be counted correctly, tagged, shelved, insured against loss. Meaning was someone else's department.

Eleven years of dusting a god's silence. Eleven years of speaking into it and feeling, most nights, the particular warmth of being heard—the warmth he had privately, embarrassingly, come to think of as the closest thing to love his faith offered.

And now, sitting in a dead brazier's niche beneath Mount Cendre, he did the arithmetic he had spent his whole careful life avoiding.

The Court traded in souls, memories, names, vows. Fire was their currency; fire was their flesh. And Vesper—Vesper, whose name meant evening, whose silence had begun a century ago, whose last recorded act in any scripture was a prophecy book inked in a hand that trembled—Vesper had gone silent at precisely the moment the Ember Court rose to power beneath Mount Cendre.

Aldous had been wrong. Gods do misplace their voices. But they don't lose them.

*They hide them.*

* * *

Flashback, deeper: he is seven years old, the year the fever took his mother, and he is alone in the ash-market outside Cendre's shadow, holding a copper he cannot spend because everything costs silver, and an old woman with a ledger under her arm stops beside him.

She does not offer him food or coin. She opens the ledger to a blank page and says, *Tell me your name, child, and I will write it somewhere it can't be taken from you.*

He tells her. She writes it. And then she writes, beneath it, in a different hand, smaller, almost shy: *This one is mine. —V.*

He had thought, even then, that the second line was odd. A note in the margin of a guild registry, signed with a single letter, as if the ledger itself were keeping accounts of its own.

The old woman had pressed her thumb to the page and left a smudge of warmth that faded in seconds, and she had said, *You'll work for us someday. You'll keep our strangest things. You won't know why they matter. That's all right. Keeping is enough.*

Keeping is enough.

Oh, he had been such a careful fool. Such a devoted, meticulous, blind fool. He had spent his life believing he served a silent god by keeping a strange inventory, when the truth was so much simpler and so much worse:

There was no difference between the god and the inventory.

* * *

He took out the small notebook he carried everywhere, the working copy of the deep catalog, and he began, with hands that would not quite steady, to list what he remembered of section nine.

The bottled thunderstorm. Acquired ninety-three years ago from a seller who gave no name and paid no fee, who set it on the counter and wept and left. Tagged: *unclassifiable. Do not open. Do not sell.* He had shaken it once, early in his tenure, just to hear the thunder roll inside the glass, and the sound had been less like weather than like a voice trying very hard to be weather.

The debt of one honest tear. No creditor listed. No debtor listed. Only the words, in Aldous's hand: *payment received in full, source unknown.* A tear that someone had wept honestly and then *paid*, the way you pay a toll. Whose toll? What gate?

The candle stub that would not burn down. Fifty years in the cellar. He had lit it once, during a blackout, and it had given light without diminishing, and he had written in the margin of its tag: *violates conservation. Investigate.* He had never investigated. There had always been something else to count.

The key that opened no door but every silence. He had held it up to his ear as a joke, once, alone in the cellar at midnight, and heard—not wind, not settling stone, but a low steady warmth, like a hearth in a distant room, like breathing.

Every impossible thing. Every item too strange to price, too stubborn to sell, too persistent to discard. He had catalogued them the way a sexton catalogues bones.

He had been keeping his god's ashes on a shelf for eleven years.

No. Not ashes. Ashes are what's left after burning. These were embers. Banked, hidden, scattered—one spark in a storm bottle, one in a tear, one in a candle, one in a key, one in the honest weight of a ledger page, one, God help him, one in *him*. In the warmth he'd felt listening all those nights in the broom-closet chapel. In the way Wren hummed a song she'd never been taught and old members went pale. In the way the prophecy book had opened to his own name without his asking.

Vesper hadn't abandoned his voice. He had *distributed* it. Hidden himself in the only place the Court could never search: the mundane inventory of a mercenary guild above the mountain, in the cellars, in the margins, in the hands of a boy who liked counting.

The god had not gone silent.

The god had gone into hiding, and hired an accountant.

* * *

Paloma sat in the dark niche until his knees ached, and then he sat longer, because there are some realizations you have to finish grieving before you're allowed to stand up.

He thought about what it meant. Not the theology of it—Quill could have the theology, Quill would *insist* on having the theology—but the human shape of it.

For eleven years he had spoken into silence and believed himself unanswered. And the whole time, the answer had been the dust on his sleeves. The answer had been the way the cellar stayed exactly cold enough to preserve wax seals. The answer had been the improbable fact that no thief, in a century of thieves, had ever successfully stolen from section nine—as if the inventory itself declined to be parted from him.

He had prayed to a god who lived in his filing system.

He had poured out his loneliness night after night into a broom closet, and the god had received every word, because the god was the shelf, and the wall, and the draft under the door, and the small stubborn warmth of the lamp he'd never needed to relight.

*You were never silent,* Paloma said aloud, to the dead brazier, to the mountain, to whatever fragment of evening still drifted in the air of this place. *You were just very, very well organized.*

And the darkness around him warmed. Slightly. The way a room warms when someone who loves you has just come home and lit the stove without telling you.

He laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and he let it, because there was no one down here to see a grown man laugh-weeping in a niche, and because Marisol had taught him on the road that a man who cannot weep in front of his crew will eventually weep in front of his enemies.

* * *

Flashback, the last one, the one that hurt most: three years ago. Wren is fourteen, caught stealing from the till for the third time, and instead of turning her out Aldous's successor—that was Paloma himself, already keeper—makes her sit across from him in the cellar among the impossible things and copy inventory tags until her fingers cramp.

*Why here?* she'd asked. *Why not the office?*

*Because the things down here behave better when someone's watching,* he'd said, which was a joke, except it wasn't.

And she'd picked up the candle stub to move it, and it had flared—just slightly, just for her, a little bloom of light like a nod—and she'd laughed and said, *Ooh, it likes me.*

He'd written *fluctuating luminescence, cause unknown* in the margin and moved on.

Wren. The girl whose soul was pawned a century ago, before she was born, held in trust until the season turns. The girl who sang the Ember Court's anthem without ever learning it. The girl the candle liked.

If Vesper had scattered himself into the inventory—if each impossible thing carried a spark of him—then the god had known, a hundred years ago, exactly what was coming. Had known a soul would be pawned. Had known whose. Had arranged to be nearby when the debt came due. Had spent a century hiding pieces of himself in a cellar in the path of a little girl's inheritance.

Not watching over her.

*Waiting* for her. Banking warmth against the winter he knew was coming, the way any careful father banks wood.

Paloma pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations. Devastating tenderness—that was the phrase Tetch used for maps of places that loved you too much to let you leave. It applied here. It applied to everything. His whole faith, his whole careful invisible life, had been a father's coat laid over a sleeping child, and he had never once noticed the weight of it.

* * *

When he finally stood, his legs were stiff and his face was wet and his notebook held four pages of new entries, written in a hand steadier than he expected, because grief, in Paloma's experience, improved penmanship.

Entry one: *Section nine reclassified. Former designation: unclassifiable. New designation: VESPER, SCATTERED. Count: incomplete. Est. fragments: unknown. Condition: banked, warm, patient.*

Entry two: *The keeper is also inventory. See: prophecy book, tonight's date, my name.*

Entry three, written slower, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages: *The ember sleeps in me. The Queen believes this. The Queen may be right. If she is right, then I am carrying enough of my god to buy a soul, and I do not yet know what I will do, and I am writing this down so that whichever choice I make, I make it with the ledger open, both ways, as it has always been.*

He tucked the notebook away and climbed back toward the light, toward the borrowed rooms the Court grudgingly allowed its appraisers, toward Marisol sharpening a knife that hummed with her old oath, toward Tetch sketching corridors that moved when she blinked, toward Quill asleep with a scripture open on his chest like a bird with folded wings.

Toward Wren, somewhere in this mountain, wearing a scullery maid's apron and a soul with a maturation date.

He passed a courtier in the corridor—a minor functionary with coins for eyes—and the courtier looked at him strangely, sniffed the air, and murmured to a companion, *That one smells of hearthsmoke. Unclaimed hearthsmoke. How curious.*

Let them smell it. Let the whole mountain smell it. He was done pretending his warmth was ordinary.

In the common room, Quill was awake after all, sitting up, reading by candlelight—or by the candle stub, Paloma realized, the candle stub, which Quill must have brought from the cellar, smuggled in his pack like the relic it was. It burned without shortening. Of course it did.

Quill looked up, and his eyes went wide, and then wider, because Paloma was glowing. Faintly. Imperceptibly to anyone who wasn't a priest who worshipped Vesper wrong—which is to say, worshipped him honestly, without doctrine, the way you love a stray dog that follows you home.

*Brother Paloma,* Quill whispered, and the word *brother* had never been in his mouth before. *You're—you're alight. Underneath. I can see it. What happened to you?*

Paloma sat down across from him and opened his notebook to entry one and slid it across the table, and said, with the terrible calm of a man who has finished his accounting:

*I found my god. He was in the cellar the whole time. And now I have to decide whether to give him back.*

Quill read the page. Read it again. And when he raised his head, his face was the face of a man standing at a fork in his own soul, and Paloma knew—knew with the certainty of a keeper who has learned to read people the way he reads ledgers—that whatever Quill decided in the next hour, half this crew would follow him, and the season turned tonight.
