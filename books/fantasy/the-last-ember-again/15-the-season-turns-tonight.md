## 15. The Season Turns Tonight

The cell they gave her was made of gold the way a cage is made of bars—beautifully, and on purpose.

Wren had counted everything in it by the second night, because counting was what you did when you couldn't pick a lock yet. Four walls of hammered amber-gold, warm to the touch, like standing inside a banked hearth. One bed with sheets that felt like wearing someone else's dream. One window that looked out onto nothing—not sky, not stone, just a soft orange glow that never changed, the way the Court's light never changed, because down here there was no morning to wait for.

And one door that had no lock at all.

That was the part she'd hated most, the first day. No lock meant no challenge, but it also meant no door was ever really closed. The courtiers came and went as they pleased, drifting in on feet that made no sound, bringing trays of food that tasted like remembering something wonderful. They smiled at her the way merchants smile at coin.

"Little ember," they called her. "Almost ripe."

She'd thrown a tray at the first one who said it. The tray had passed through him like a thrown tray passing through smoke, and he'd kept smiling, and she'd learned something important: down here, you didn't fight with your hands. You fought with what they wanted from you.

They wanted her edges. So she kept them tucked close, like a knife against the forearm under a sleeve.

* * *

On the fourth night—or what she decided was night, because Paloma would have called it night—the singing started.

It came up through the floor first, a low hum in the gold beneath her bare feet, and then the whole cell caught it, the walls resonating like the belly of an instrument. Wren knew the song. Of course she knew it. She'd been humming it since before she could talk, a melody with no words that old guild members went pale hearing, that Marisol had once gripped her wrist over and asked, too gently, *where did you learn that?*

Now, in the heart of the Court Beneath the Mountain, the song finally had its context, and its context was terrible.

It was a harvest hymn. She understood that without being told, the way you understand a hook is for catching fish. It was the sound the Court made when it prepared to eat, and every wall around her was singing along.

Wren sat down on the edge of the impossible bed and put her hands flat on the floor and listened to herself being seasoned.

*All right,* she thought. *So we're doing this early.*

* * *

They came for her at what the Court probably still called the proper time, except the proper time had moved up, because the season was turning above them and things up there were already changing—she could feel it even through the mountain, a faint coolness in the warm glow, like a draft under a door. Autumn leaning in. Her deadline arriving ahead of schedule, the way debts always did.

Three courtiers this time, dressed in robes the color of cooling coals, their faces pleasant and slightly unfinished, like portraits someone had stopped painting halfway. The tall one held a garment folded over its arms: a dress of woven light, all embers and gold thread.

"For the rite," it said. Its voice sounded kind. Everything down here sounded kind; kindness was the Court's native language and also its favorite lie. "You'll want to look beautiful, little ember."

"I want," Wren said, "to not be eaten."

"That isn't one of the options." The courtier drifted closer. Up close, she could see that its eyes were banked fires, patient ones, the kind that could wait a hundred years because they had. "But we are generous, you know. We offered you anything. A life in comfort. A name in our rolls. You chose to sulk."

"I chose my family."

"Mm." It laid the dress across the bed, smoothing folds that glowed faintly where its fingers touched. "Family. The word the surface folk use for people who haven't sold them yet."

Wren's hands curled. She thought about Paloma's ledger, about the way he wrote numbers like they were promises. About Tetch teaching her to read maps of places that moved, laughing when Wren got lost. About Quill's terrible tea. About Marisol showing her how to fall without breaking anything important.

*Not for sale,* she thought, though nobody had written it down yet. *Not at any price.*

"The rite begins at the turning," the courtier said, moving toward the door. "Rest, little ember. Eat. You'll want your strength. Being consumed takes longer than you'd think, if you fight it."

They left. The door stayed open behind them, because locks were for things that might escape, and the Court believed she wouldn't dare.

Wren looked at the dress of woven light.

Then she looked at the open door, and did what any sensible member of the Gilded Ledger would do with a door that had no lock.

She started planning.

* * *

Here was the thing about thieving that the Court had never understood, because the Court had never stolen anything in its long, glittering life—it only bought and traded and consumed, which was different, which was the difference between a hand and a mouth:

Thieving wasn't about locks. Locks were theater. Thieving was about attention. Every treasure in the world sat inside a ring of watching eyes, and your job was to be somewhere the eyes weren't looking, and the best place for that was always the same place.

Right under them.

So Wren didn't sneak out of her cell like a prisoner escaping. She put on the dress of woven light—because it was gorgeous, and because clothes that glowed made people assume you belonged—and walked out the unlocked door like a girl who had somewhere to be, chin up, stride easy, the walk Marisol had drilled into her: *you're not sneaking, you're late for something important.*

The corridors of the Ember Court unspooled around her like veins of fire in dark stone. Courtiers drifted past, carrying memories in little glass vials the way surface folk carried coins. Nobody stopped her. One bowed.

She nearly laughed. A hundred years of perfect security, and it had never once occurred to them that the merchandise might know how to wear the merchandise.

*Paloma,* she thought, *is going to be so insufferable about this.* And then, immediately, the other thought, colder: *if he's even here. If they haven't already caught him. If the prophecy book didn't just get everyone I love killed.*

She pushed that thought down where she kept all the others, in the pocket-space behind her ribs where she stored things she couldn't afford to feel yet.

* * *

Finding Paloma was the plan, or the shape of a plan. Find the crew, find the vault, find her soul, get out. Simple. The kind of plan Tetch would draw with four lines and then spend an hour explaining why each line was a death trap.

But the Court was vast, and it was rearranging itself—or maybe she was getting turned around; down here, with no sky, direction was a rumor—and after what felt like hours she found herself somewhere she definitely shouldn't be, at the end of a corridor that narrowed into a stair of black glass descending into deeper red.

And at the bottom of the stair was a door.

Not gilded. Not grand. A plain iron door, scarred and old, older than everything around it, set into rock that predated the Court's pretty architecture entirely. And unlike every other threshold in this place, it had a lock—an actual, physical, honest-to-the-silent-god lock, iron and rust and age.

Which meant, in the grammar of thieves, one thing: *something worth keeping in here.*

Wren crouched in front of it and listened with her fingertips, the way Tetch had taught her to listen to maps. Two tumblers. A wardspring, faintly humming with borrowed heat. Nothing clever, really—just old, and confident, and forgotten. The Court guarded its treasures with ceremony and left its true secrets behind a lock nobody remembered existed.

Her picks were gone—they'd taken everything, even her bootlaces—but the dress of woven light had a hairpin holding its collar shut. Of course it did. Rich people's clothes always had hairpins. That was practically a law of nature, and Wren had built a career on laws of nature.

The wardspring bit her once, a snap of heat like a stove-top kiss, and she hissed and sucked her finger and tried again slower, coaxing instead of forcing, *patience, patience*, until something deep inside the door sighed and let go.

The lock clicked like a full stop.

Inside was a small round room, and in the middle of the small round room, on a pedestal of raw stone, stood a single jar.

Every other memory in the Court lived in vials—little glass things, trade-sized, currency. This was a jar. Big as her head, thick-walled, sealed with wax stamped by a hand she somehow recognized without knowing why: a simple mark like a flame drawn by someone who loved flames. Inside, the memory didn't flicker like the others. It *burned*. A steady, golden, stubborn light, and looking at it, Wren felt something she hadn't felt since the guild hall: warmth that wanted nothing back.

There was no placard. No label. No guard outside the door.

Because no one guards the thing no one knows matters.

But Wren had grown up in a guild of ledger-keepers, and the first rule of reading ledgers is: *follow what's missing.* No guard meant either worthless or priceless. No label meant the owner didn't need reminding. A plain iron door at the bottom of the deepest stair, in a palace of gold?

This was the Queen's.

And whatever burned in that jar, the Queen had hidden it better than her crown, her vault, her whole economy of names and fire—which meant it was the one thing in this entire mountain that could hurt her.

Wren's hands were shaking. Not fear. Something adjacent to awe, the feeling she got sometimes picking a nobleman's pocket and finding, instead of a purse, a locket with a dead wife's portrait. The moment when theft became trespass into somebody's actual heart.

*Sorry,* she thought, not meaning it much. *You're eating my soul tonight. We're past polite.*

She took the jar.

The moment her fingers closed on it, the whole Court screamed.

Not loudly—that was the awful part. Every banked fire in every corridor guttered at once, and every courtier everywhere turned its unfinished face toward the deepest stair, and the harvest hymn stopped mid-note for the first time in a hundred years.

Above her, far away, bells began to ring in reverse.

"Oh," Wren said, hugging the burning jar to her chest, its warmth soaking through the glowing dress like a second heartbeat. "Oh, that's going to be a problem."

She ran.

* * *

She ran up stairs that hadn't been there on the way down, through corridors that were actively trying to become walls, the Court convulsing around her like a body discovering a splinter. Courtiers poured into the halls, faces finally finished now—all of them furious, all of them suddenly very detailed—and the dress of woven light, her clever disguise, blazed against the dark like a signal fire.

"There!" someone shrieked. "The little ember has the First—"

Wren rounded a corner and nearly ran into a wall of cinder-robed bodies, spun, ducked through an archway that tried to close on her like a jaw, and came out onto—

A gallery. High and wide, overlooking a vast chamber below, and below was the vault.

She knew it instantly, the way you know home windows from the street. Rows and rows of lanterns hanging in the dark, hundreds of them, thousands, each one lit with a soul-light of a different color, swaying gently in a wind that had no source. Somewhere down there, among those lanterns, hung one particular flame with her name on it. Maturing at season's turn.

And below, spilling into the vault's antechamber, torchlight and shouting—familiar shouting, one voice bellowing oaths in old knightly cadence and another answering in a nervous priestly wail.

Marisol. Quill. *They're alive. They're here.*

Wren leaned over the gallery rail, lungs burning, jar clutched tight, and laughed—a short, wild, disbelieving laugh that echoed off stones that were trying very hard to catch her.

Then the temperature dropped.

Not cooled—*dropped*, wrongness spreading through the warm air like ink through water, and the courtiers in the gallery fell silent mid-shout, and even the lanterns below stilled. Wren turned.

The Queen of Cinders stood at the far end of the gallery.

She wore her crown of spent vows, and her face was the face from every whispered story—beautiful the way ruins are beautiful—and her eyes were fixed on the jar in Wren's arms with an expression that Wren had seen exactly once before in her life: on Paloma's face, the night she'd almost picked *his* pocket and he'd caught her wrist, and instead of anger there had been something older and sadder.

Recognition. Grief. The look of someone seeing a grave opened.

"Little thief," the Queen said, and her voice was quiet enough that Wren had to strain to hear it over the ringing bells. "Do you know what you're holding?"

"No," Wren said honestly, backing toward the rail, calculating the drop, the lanterns, whether souls broke your fall or broke *with* you. "But I'm guessing it's the reason your whole scary kingdom just wet itself."

Something moved through the Queen's face—rage, yes, but underneath it, horribly, something that looked like relief. Like a woman who had been waiting a century for someone brave enough or stupid enough to steal the truth off its shelf.

"Keep it, then," the Queen of Cinders said, and began to walk forward, and the fires of the entire Court bent toward her like wheat. "Carry it to the boy who carries my god's ember. Let him see what Vesper loved first—and what she did to him. And then, little ember, when the season turns—"

She raised one burning hand, and every lantern in the vault below flared at once, and Wren felt her own edges begin to fray like cloth in a strong wind.

"—we will see what any of you are willing to burn."
