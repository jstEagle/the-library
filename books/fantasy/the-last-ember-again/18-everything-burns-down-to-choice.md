## 18. Everything Burns Down to Choice

The Court began to die the way a fire dies: not all at once, but in pieces.

Paloma felt it first through his boots—a tremor in the cathedral floor, faint as a heartbeat skipping. Then the banked fires along the walls guttered, and for one terrible instant he saw what they had been hiding all along: not flames at all, but memories burning in slow motion, thousands of them, whole lifetimes rendered down to light and heat and held there by the Queen's will alone.

Now her will was weeping.

The courtiers scattered like sparks off a struck flint. Some fled toward the gates, clutching jars of borrowed time to their chests. Some simply stood in the aisles of that vast ash-carved nave and let themselves come apart, unraveling into smoke, because whatever held them together—the Court's economy, its hunger, its hundred years of traded names—was coming loose at the seams.

And in the center of it all, on a dais of cooling cinder, sat the Queen of Cinders with her crown of spent vows, and she did not move at all.

* * *

Paloma stood where Wren had left him, at the foot of the dais, holding two things.

In his left hand: the Queen's first memory, sealed in a vial of black glass that Wren had lifted from beneath the Queen's own throne while half the Court watched her do it and none of them could stop her. It was warm. It hummed against his palm like a sleeping bird.

In his right hand—no. Not his hand. In his chest, behind his ribs, where it had been hiding for years without his knowing: Vesper's ember. The god's last unspent portion, poured into the guild's inventory shelf by shelf, ledger line by ledger line, until the whole impossible collection of the Gilded Ledger was nothing more than a god's ashes kept safe by a man who thought he was only doing his job.

The bottled thunderstorm on the third shelf. The debt of one honest tear. The door that opened onto winter. He had catalogued them all with dry ink and steady hands, and every single one had been his god, folded small, waiting.

The Queen had told him as much an hour ago—or a lifetime ago; time down here moved like honey off a cold spoon—and then Wren had stolen the memory that broke her, and now the Queen sat amid the ruin of her own oldest grief, and the Court was falling, and Paloma Ashgrove stood in the middle of it holding enough divinity to remake a god.

Or to save a girl.

That was the arithmetic. That was the whole of it, and it would not simplify no matter how many times he ran the sums. One ember. Two claims. A god who had gone silent rather than be taken, and a girl who had been taken because no one had gone silent for her—because her parents had sold her soul a century before she was born, and the guild had raised her anyway, fed her anyway, taught her to pick locks and pockets and hearts, and never once told her she was collateral.

"Paloma."

Marisol's voice, from somewhere behind him. He didn't turn. There wasn't time to turn. The ceiling of the cathedral was shedding cinders like snow, and each one that touched the floor became a small dying scream—a memory burning out forever, some stranger's wedding day or first betrayal or the smell of bread in a house that no longer existed.

"Paloma." Closer now. Her hand on his shoulder, armor still smoking from the bridge. "The vault's open. Tetch has Wren's soul-lantern. We have minutes, maybe less."

"Then take it and go."

"You know I won't."

He knew. That was the trouble with people who owed you debts—they always insisted on repaying them at the worst possible moment.

"The ember," Marisol said quietly. She had seen it too, then. Everyone had seen it, in the end; when the Queen had named it, the whole Court had turned to look at him the way creditors look at a man who has just been revealed to be carrying the entire guild's fortune sewn into his coat. "You can't do both."

"I know the arithmetic, Marisol."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. She was a woman who had broken one oath already and spent the rest of her life paying interest on it. "Because I've watched you audit ledgers for three days, and you're very good at making hard numbers look simple. So look at me and say it plainly. Say which one you're choosing."

He couldn't. That was the truth of it, plain as anything. He looked at the ember behind his ribs—he could feel it now, really feel it, a warmth like a hand pressed flat against the inside of his sternum—and he looked at the vial in his fist, and he understood that both of them were the same thing, in the end. Both were love, banked. Both were someone saying *I will burn myself down so that something else can keep going.*

Vesper had done it for the world, or for the guild, or maybe just for him. The Queen had done it for a memory of being loved. And Paloma—

Paloma had been keeping accounts his whole life because keeping accounts meant you never had to choose. Every entry balanced. Every debt tracked. As long as the books were clean, he never had to decide what mattered more than what.

The floor shuddered again. A pillar of fused glass went down thirty feet away, and the sound it made was almost gentle, like a book closing.

* * *

Quill found him next. Of course Quill found him next. The priest moved through collapsing cathedrals the way other men moved through gardens.

"You're standing very still," Quill observed, "in a building that is actively attempting to kill us."

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit. Vesper never indulged in it." Quill came around to face him, and up close Paloma could see the soot in the lines of his face, the tear tracks cutting clean channels through it. His church had just taught him that his god had sold his own voice to a jealous queen, and Quill had wept, and then Quill had picked himself up and started looking for the exit, because faith, in Quill's practice, had always been less about answers than about showing up. "Have you decided?"

"No."

"Good." Paloma blinked. Quill smiled, tired and crooked. "Don't decide yet. Decide is a word for men with options. You have a choice. They're not the same thing. An option is something the world gives you. A choice is something you give the world." He tapped Paloma's chest, right over the ember, and the warmth flared like a struck match. "He's still in there, you know. Listening. He's been listening your whole life. Whatever you choose, make sure it's something worth having heard."

Then the roof came down between them, and Quill was gone, shouting something about the east colonnade, and Paloma was alone in the fire.

* * *

Alone, except for the prophecy.

It had appeared in his hand—there was no better way to say it. One moment his right hand held only the vial; the next, a page lay across his palm like a fallen leaf, thin and brown and a century old, torn from the book that had started everything. The client's payment. The impossible inventory item he'd carried since the beginning without understanding it.

His own name was still there, inked a hundred years ago. Tonight's date. *A soul sold to the Ember Court will be consumed at the turning of the season.*

But below it, in the same ancient hand, in ink that shimmered now with heat-light, words he had never read before—words that had not been there when he'd first opened the page, because they had not yet been true:

*the last ember chooses.*

Not *chose*. Not *will choose*. *Chooses.* Present tense. Ongoing. As if the sentence had been waiting a century for him to catch up to it.

Paloma laughed. It came out wrong, ragged, half a sob. A century-old prophecy written in the present tense, because Vesper had known—had *hoped*—that whoever read it would stand exactly here, exactly now, with the ending still wet.

His god had written him a blank line and called it destiny.

* * *

He walked toward the Queen.

There was no reason to. Every rational thread in him screamed to run—to find Tetch and Wren and the lantern with Wren's soul hanging pale inside it, and get out before the mountain finished swallowing its own heart. But the Queen sat unmoving on her dais as her kingdom burned down around her ears, and Paloma had spent twenty years as keeper of an inventory, and there was one thing every keeper knows:

When everything else is lost, you save the record. You save the reason it happened. Otherwise the burning was for nothing.

She didn't look up until he stood before her. Up close, without the crown's shadow, she was smaller than she'd seemed—not a monster, not a queen, just a woman old past reckoning, with cinders in her hair and an expression he recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone who has made one unforgivable choice and built an entire life on top of it so they'd never have to look underneath.

"You should flee," she said. Her voice was ash and embers. "Or take it. Take my throne, my hoard, my name. It's all kindling now."

"I don't want any of it."

"Everyone wants something. That's why the Court worked. Wanting is the only currency that never devalues." She finally raised her eyes. They were the color of a hearth going out. "You want your god back. Or you want the girl free. You cannot want both; the ember is one flame, and a flame goes where it's poured."

"I know."

"Then pour it, appraiser. Stop standing in my ruins doing sums."

"It's not sums," Paloma said. And he heard his own voice go quiet and strange, the way it did only once or twice a decade. "Sums are how I hide. From choices. From—" He stopped. Started again. "You loved him. That's what the memory says. Before you sold his voice. Before you built all this. You loved him, and he left—or you believed he left—and so you made a place where nothing leaves, where everything gets bought and held and owned, because owned things can't abandon you."

The Queen's hands closed slowly on the arms of her cinder throne.

"And Vesper," Paloma went on, "couldn't bear to fight you. So instead of taking his voice back, he went silent. He scattered himself into small things—into storms and tears and doors, into a guild's ledger, into a clerk who thought he was invisible. He hid from you for a hundred years by becoming everything you couldn't buy." He held up the vial. Black glass. Her first memory: a young god laughing in a kitchen, before thrones, before silence. "This is the only thing in your entire court that was freely given. He gave you this day. You've kept it locked under your throne for a century because it hurt too much to look at."

"Stop."

"He never stopped loving you either. That's the part you got wrong. Silence isn't absence. Ask anyone who prays."

"*Stop.*"

But she didn't command him. Her voice broke on the word, and the last of the Court's fires dipped low, and the Queen of Cinders put her face in her hands and wept, and each tear that fell hissed into steam on the burning floor, and Paloma understood that he was watching the end of an empire that had been, all along, just one person's grief wearing a crown.

He set the vial gently in her hands.

Her head came up. For the first time, she looked truly at him—not as an appraiser, not as a thief, not as a vessel of stolen divinity. Just as a man.

"What are you doing?"

"Returning an item to its owner." He managed something that was almost a smile. "It's what I do. Everything in the ledger opens both ways. I learned that the hard way this week."

"This is—you could have ransomed it. Traded it. It's worth kingdoms."

"It's worth one honest grief," Paloma said. "Yours. Take it back. Remember being loved. And then—" he knelt, so they were eye to eye, queen and clerk, "—let my family walk out of here."

The Court shuddered. Somewhere far above, stone ground against stone, the mountain deciding whether to close its throat forever.

The Queen looked at the vial for a long, long moment. Then she pressed it to her lips like a kiss, and broke the seal.

* * *

What came out was not fire. It was morning.

Paloma saw it even through closed eyes: a kitchen, sunlight, a laugh. A hundred years of darkness in the Court, and its foundation stone had been a sunny room where someone had once been happy, and the Queen made a sound he would carry to his grave—a sound like a bell cracking, like winter ending—and every remaining flame in the cathedral stood up straight and burned gold.

When he opened his eyes, she was looking at him with clear eyes, and the crown of spent vows had slipped from her head and shattered on the floor, and she was, briefly, just a woman holding a good memory in both hands.

"Go," she said. "All of you. The gates will hold long enough. And clerk—" her voice caught, steadied, "—the ember. Spend it well. He always did."

* * *

He found them at the vault doors: Tetch with Wren's soul-lantern cradled like a newborn, Marisol bleeding but upright, Quill singing something tuneless and defiant, and Wren—Wren alive, Wren furious, Wren fraying at the edges where the season-turn gnawed at her, her outline flickering like a candle in wind.

"There you are," she said, in the tone she used for brothers who were late to dinner. "We're leaving. Try to keep up."

"Not yet." Paloma stepped into the middle of them, into the stillness at the center of all that fire, and reached into his chest.

It didn't hurt. That surprised him most. He had expected tearing, and instead it was like opening a ledger to its final page—like setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. The ember rose out of him into his cupped hands, small and gold and impossibly bright, a god the size of a seed.

Four voices said his name at once. He ignored all of them.

"One flame," he said aloud, to the ember, to Vesper, to the silence that had never actually been empty. "Two claims. You wrote the prophecy, so you tell me—no. No, you wrote the present tense. *Chooses.* You left the line blank on purpose, you stubborn, quiet, impossible god. You wanted it to be mine."

The ember pulsed. Warm. Patient. Listening, the way it had always listened.

Behind him, the Queen's freed memory burned through the dying Court like dawn through fog, and the gates stood open, and his family stood around him with their hands out and their faces full of everything they were too proud to say.

Paloma looked at the ember one last time.

Then he turned toward Wren, and the final line of the prophecy blazed across his skin like a brand, and he made his choice—

* * *

—and the mountain, feeling the season turn, began to close.
