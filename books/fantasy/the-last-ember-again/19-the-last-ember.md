## 19. The Last Ember

The mountain began to close, and Paloma opened his hands.

The ember sat in his palms the way a sleeping bird sits in a cupped nest—lighter than it had any right to be, warmer than anything in this cathedral of banked fires had ever managed to be. It did not struggle. It did not flicker. It waited, the way Vesper had always waited: through every unanswered prayer of Paloma's boyhood, through every silent vigil, through thirty years of talking to a god who never once talked back.

*Except he did,* Paloma thought. *He talked back constantly. I just didn't know how to read.*

Around him, the Court was dying beautifully. The Queen's first memory—her own young face looking up at Vesper with something that was not yet jealousy and not yet love but was already both—burned through the vaulted dark like dawn poured into water. Courtiers who had spent centuries trading pieces of themselves stood frozen mid-transaction, their borrowed time running backward through their fingers, their purchased memories rising out of them like smoke finding its way home. Somewhere far above, stone groaned against stone as the mountain remembered it was a mountain.

"Paloma." Wren's voice. Small, steady, furious with hope. "Whatever you're about to do—do it fast."

He looked at her.

She stood at the center of everything, this girl the guild had fished out of a gutter nineteen years ago with stolen silver in one fist and nothing else to her name. The consumption rite had already begun at her edges; he could see it now that he knew what to look for. Her outline shimmered faintly, like ink held too long in water. The Court had been patient for a century. It would not be patient much longer.

"I need you to hold still," he said.

"You need me to *what?*" Marisol demanded from somewhere behind him. "You need her to stand still while the ceiling comes down?"

"I need everyone to trust me," Paloma said, "for approximately ten more seconds."

"That's asking a lot," said Tetch.

"It's asking everything," said Brother Quill, and there was no argument in his voice at all. Only awe. Quill had spent his whole life worshipping a silence, and here, at the end of all things, he could see what the silence had been saving up for.

Paloma knelt.

It seemed right, somehow. He had spent his life kneeling before altars that gave back nothing but the smell of cold incense, and now he knelt before a pickpocket girl in a scullery maid's dress with soot on her cheek, and she was the holiest thing left standing in this place.

"Wren Ashgrove," he said—and used the name deliberately, the full name, the one the guild had given her when her birth parents' ledger page had burned away everything else—"I'm going to give you something."

Her eyes went wide. "No. No, Paloma, that's *your*—that's him, that's your *god*, you can't just—"

"He was never mine," Paloma said. "That's the whole point. That's what I finally understand. He was never anyone's. He belonged to whoever needed him. He scattered himself across an entire inventory of impossible things just so someone, someday, could gather enough of him together to do exactly this." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I've been his courier for fifteen years, Wren. I just didn't know the delivery address until tonight."

"The ceiling," Tetch said again, helpfully.

"Ten seconds," Paloma said.

Wren's hands came up—not to push him away, but to frame his face, the way she'd seen Marisol do once to a dying soldier, the way people do when they want the last thing someone sees to be a face that loves them. Hers was smudged and tear-streaked and absolutely certain.

"If this kills you," she whispered, "I will steal your ghost and haunt you right back."

"That," said Paloma, "is the most comforting threat I've ever received."

He tipped the ember into her.

* * *

There are no words in any ledger for what it feels like to pour out a god.

Paloma had expected fire. What came instead was warmth—the specific, domestic warmth of a hearth on a winter night, of bread pulled from an oven, of a blanket dragged over a sleeping sibling who'd fallen asleep by the door waiting for you to come home. It moved through his palms and down through his arms and out of him in a long, slow exhale, and with it went everything: the weight he'd carried since the day he'd found his name in a century-old book, the loneliness of serving a silent altar, the terrible suspicion that faith was just love with nowhere to land.

It landed now.

It landed in Wren.

Light filled her the way water fills a dry channel—finding every crack, every hollow the Court had carved, every fraying edge of her soul, and pressing itself into each one like a craftsman fitting joinery. Where the light touched, the fraying stopped. Where it pooled, the fraying reversed. Paloma watched the shimmering outline of her solidify, watched the century-old debt written into her bones get paid off line by line, watched the ember do what embers do best:

It kept things warm instead of letting them go out.

And then—briefly, so briefly that later he would spend years trying to decide whether he'd dreamed it—a voice spoke.

Not in the air. Not in the vaulted dark. In the warmth itself, in the last thread of heat passing through Paloma's hands, pitched low and close and familiar as his own heartbeat:

***Thank you.***

Two words. A god's entire final sermon, delivered in the space between one breath and the next. And underneath the words, woven into them the way a signature is woven into ink, something else—something that felt like a hand resting briefly on the crown of Paloma's head, the way his mother had done once, the way no one had done since.

Then the warmth passed fully out of him, and Paloma Ashgrove was empty.

* * *

Empty is not the same as dead. He learned that immediately, because the first thing he felt in his new emptiness was Wren catching him before he hit the floor.

"—stupid, *stupid*, you absolute ledger-brained—" she was saying, hauling his arm over her shoulders with a strength that had nothing to do with gods and everything to do with panic. "Breathe. Breathe, you idiot, breathe or I'll—"

"I'm breathing," he managed.

"You're breathing *wrong.*"

"He's fine," Quill said, dropping to his knees beside them, and the old priest's hands were shaking as they hovered over Paloma's chest. "Look—look at his skin. Look."

Paloma looked down at himself.

The brand was gone. The final line of prophecy, which had blazed across his forearms like a tattoo of fire since the Queen's memory had freed it, had faded to nothing. He turned his hands over. No marks. No sigils. No god's claim written anywhere on him.

For the first time since he was nine years old, Paloma Ashgrove belonged to no one.

"It worked," Tetch breathed. She was staring past them all, at Wren, and her cartographer's eyes were wide with a wonder that had nothing to do with maps. "Look at her."

Wren glowed.

Not metaphorically. Not the faint shimmer of a soul mid-consumption. She *glowed*—a low, banked, hearth-light radiance coming up through her skin like lamplight through paper, warm gold shot through with threads of deep red, and where her feet touched the vault floor, the ancient stone was softening, warming, waking up after a century of cold.

"She's not just free," Quill whispered. His voice broke clean in half. "She's *carrying him*. She's carrying—oh. Oh, merciful silence. She's carrying the god."

"I'm carrying *me*," Wren said—but her voice wobbled on the word, because even she could feel it: the second heartbeat under her own, slow and vast and gentle, beating in time with nothing but itself. She pressed a hand flat against her sternum. "He's... quiet. He's quiet, but he's *there*. Like someone fell asleep by my fire."

"He always did like hearths," Paloma said.

And then the mountain screamed.

* * *

It was not a sound so much as a decision—the whole mass of Mount Cendre remembering, all at once, that courts built on stolen hearts cannot survive their own honesty. The Queen's freed memory had done its work. Every courtier in the cathedral of banked fires was weeping or laughing or clutching at memories that were suddenly, violently, their own again, and without their transactions the Court had nothing left to stand on. Its economy had been grief. Its currency had been loss. And the Queen of Cinders, crowned in spent vows, stood at the center of it all with her first love's face burning in the air before her, and she was crying.

Paloma had imagined this moment a hundred ways. In none of them did the monster weep.

"Go," the Queen said.

Her voice was ruined. Stripped of the resonance that had made centuries of souls flinch, it was simply an old woman's voice, tired and cracked and human in a way that hurt worse than any threat.

"The gates are open. The mountain is closing. Take your girl and your debts and your impossible inventory and *go*, before I remember how to be what I was." She looked at Wren—at the glow of her, at the sleeping god beneath her ribs—and something in the Queen's face collapsed inward like a roof giving way. "He chose you," she said, to Wren, though her eyes found Paloma's. "A hundred years I told myself he'd gone silent because he was cruel, because he'd abandoned me, because silence was his punishment. And the whole time he was hiding. Hiding in ledgers. Hiding in *bottled thunderstorms*." A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh if laughter knew how to grieve. "He hid in the most boring places imaginable, because he knew I'd never look there."

"He knew you'd look everywhere beautiful," Paloma said quietly.

"Yes." The Queen closed her eyes. "Yes, he did. That was always the trouble with him."

Stone dust sifted from the vaulted dark. The bridge of ash behind them—where Marisol stood bleeding and victorious and nameless—was crumbling edge to edge, cinders peeling away into a dark that had stopped pretending to be a floor.

"GO," the Queen roared, and for one instant the old fire flashed through her, the terror of ages—and then it guttered, and she was small again, and she waved them away with a hand like a woman shooing children out of a kitchen. "Before I change my mind. Before the mountain does it for me. *Run.*"

They ran.

* * *

Later, Paloma would piece the escape together from fragments, the way Tetch pieced together shifting coastlines: Marisol hauling Quill by the collar while the bridge of ash disintegrated behind them; Tetch navigating collapsing corridors by instinct alone, shouting turns that made no sense and were always right; Wren glowing like a lantern at the front of the pack, and the corridors *leaning toward her*, stone warming at her passage, the dying Court making way for the god inside the girl the way a crowd makes way for a king.

And Paloma in the middle of it, empty-handed, unmarked, mortal, running on nothing but borrowed breath and the grip of Wren's fingers around his wrist.

The gates of the Ember Court were open. Of course they were. The Queen had opened them herself, and stood silhouetted between them as they passed—smaller now, dimmer, her crown of spent vows slipping down her brow like a circlet that no longer fit—and as Wren ran by, the Queen reached out and, with two fingers, touched the glow at the girl's shoulder.

Just touched it. Just once.

"Oh," the Queen said softly. "Oh, there you are."

And the mountain closed behind them like a book whose story was finished.

* * *

Dawn was a rumor when they stumbled out of the pass, and a fact by the time they collapsed at the treeline.

Paloma lay on his back in wet grass and watched the sky go from ash-grey to rose, and felt—nothing where the god had been. Not pain. Not grief, exactly. An absence shaped like warmth, the way a hand leaves a shape in a bed. He catalogued the feeling automatically, professionally, the way he'd catalogue any item entering the Gilded Ledger's inventory:

*One absence, divine, lightly worn. Provenance: everything. Value: not for sale.*

"You're doing the thing," Wren said. She had flopped down beside him in the grass, still glowing faintly, still radiating heat like a stove wrapped in a scullery dress. "The ledger thing. You're writing entries in your head. I can *see* it."

"Can you?"

"You have a face for it. You've always had a face for it." She rolled onto her side to look at him. Up close, the glow under her skin moved when she breathed, and he thought: *he's comfortable in there. He's finally comfortable somewhere.* "Does it hurt? Being empty?"

Paloma considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and always had.

"No," he said. "It feels like..." He searched for it. Fifteen years of inventory, thousands of entries, and the words came slowly. "It feels like putting down a load I forgot I was carrying. It feels like the moment after you set down something heavy and your arms don't know what to do." He flexed his bare, unmarked hands against the grass. "It feels like morning."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she reached over and took one of those empty hands and pressed it flat against her sternum, over the second heartbeat, and held it there.

Warmth seeped up through his palm. Faint. Familiar. Patient.

"He wanted you to know," Wren said, "that you're not getting rid of him that easily. He says—" she tilted her head, listening to something no one else could hear, and her mouth curved— "he says a ledger isn't finished when the books balance. It's finished when someone inherits it."

Paloma laughed. It hurt, and it healed, and he let it do both.

Behind them, Marisol sat sharpening a sword she no longer had a name to fight under, and looked lighter than any of them had ever seen her. Quill was on his knees in the dew, praying—not to the silence anymore, but *to* the girl glowing ten feet away, and grinning like a heretic the whole time. Tetch was already sketching the sunrise, because some things deserved to be mapped even if they never moved.

The season had turned. The prophecy was fulfilled. The god was spent.

And Paloma Ashgrove, keeper of the Gilded Ledger, lay in the wet grass holding his little sister's hand over a sleeping god's heartbeat, and understood at last what he had become:

Not a servant. Not a keeper. Not a sworn man.

An inheritance.

Somewhere below them, deep in the closing dark, the last of the Ember Court settled into ash—and somewhere above, the sun cleared the ridge, and struck the whole mountainside gold, and every drop of dew caught fire with borrowed light.

Wren squeezed his hand.

"So," she said. "What do we tell the guild?"

Paloma smiled at the sky, at the light, at the blank page waiting for him in a ledger a hundred miles away.

"The truth," he said. "We tell them the truth. It's worth more than anything we stole."

He got up. They all did. And they walked home.
