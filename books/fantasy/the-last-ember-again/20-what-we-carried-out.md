## 20. What We Carried Out

Tetch had mapped her own death twice, once in ink and once in terror, and neither version had included walking out of a collapsing mountain at dawn with ash in her teeth and a jar of bottled thunder cradled against her chest like a newborn.

The tunnel behind them groaned. Not the ordinary groan of settling stone—Tetch knew that sound the way sailors know weather—but something deeper, a sound like a ledger being closed by hands too large for it. The Ember Court was coming down. A century of borrowed time, spent all at once, and the interest was the whole mountain.

"Keep moving," Marisol said, from the rear. Her voice had changed since the bridge. Tetch didn't have a map for what it had changed *into*. Lighter, she supposed. The way a sword is lighter after you've snapped off the hilt. "Don't look back."

"I'm not looking back," Quill said, who was absolutely looking back. His robes were scorched to ribbons and his face wore an expression Tetch had only ever seen on people standing inside a miracle: reverent, terrified, and grinning like an idiot. "I'm bearing witness. There's a difference. It's a theological difference."

"It's a difference that'll get you brained by falling rock."

"Then I'll die having witnessed."

"You're both being very loud about dying," Wren said, ahead of them, and her voice did the thing it had been doing since they'd found her on the last bridge—the thing where it sounded like one voice and then, underneath, faint as a coal wrapped in cloth, like many. She carried a god's spark now. Everyone could hear it if they listened. Tetch tried not to listen. Listening felt like reading someone's mail.

The passage narrowed. Tetch took the lead because taking the lead was the only prayer she knew. Her hands remembered the way even when her maps couldn't follow—she'd drawn this mountain four times from memory and every drawing contradicted the others, because Mount Cendre rearranged itself around its secrets. But secrets were cheap today. The Court was spilling everything it had ever kept, doors flung open down every corridor, vaults yawning, memories evaporating off their shelves like breath off glass. For the first time in a hundred years, the mountain had nothing left to hide.

Which meant, for the first time, the roads ran straight.

* * *

They came out through a fissure in the eastern scree, into air so clean it hurt.

Dawn was doing its work over the valley below—gold laid across the terraces, smoke rising honest and domestic from chimneys, a dog barking somewhere with the idiotic confidence of a creature that had never heard of the Ember Court. Tetch sat down hard on a boulder and let the shaking start. It had been waiting politely for hours, the way her shaking always did. Professional courtesy.

Marisol lowered herself onto the rock beside her without asking. That was new too. The old Marisol had stood apart, always half a pace outside the circle, guarding a name she'd already lost. This one sat down next to a cartographer covered in cave dust and said nothing, which was the most eloquent thing anyone had said all morning.

Quill collapsed flat on his back in the scree and addressed the sky. "I am officially a heretic," he announced, to no one and everyone. "The Concordance will strip my orders, burn my writings, and strike my name from the rolls of the faithful." He beamed. "And I have never been more content in my life. Do you understand? I argued theology with my own god's chosen servant and *won*, and the prize is excommunication, and it is the finest thing I have ever earned."

"You argued that we should spend a god to save a girl," Marisol said.

"I argued that worship demands restoration. I lost. Paloma argued that love demands sacrifice." Quill folded his burnt hands over his chest. "And then, when the choice came, he chose—and I found that my faith had followed him instead of my church. So I sided with a man against my own doctrine, and the doctrine burned, and here I am." He sighed, deeply satisfied. "A heretic. Content. I recommend it."

"You would," said Marisol, but she almost smiled.

Tetch looked past them both, down the slope, to where Paloma and Wren walked side by side, some distance away, speaking low. Or rather—Wren spoke, and Paloma listened, and occasionally he laughed, and the laugh came out of him strange, like a man testing whether his voice still worked. He wasn't sworn to anything anymore. Tetch had watched him pour Vesper out of himself and into his little sister, and she had watched what it cost, and she had watched him stand up afterward anyway, unmarked, unclaimed, mortal as bread.

He looked lighter than Marisol. He looked like a man whose debts were finally paid, except Tetch knew better. She'd seen his face in the vault-light when he made the choice. Nobody spends a god without keeping the receipt.

Wren turned, caught Tetch staring, and waved. And there—flickering under her skin, visible only when the dawn hit at the right angle—a glow, warm as a hearth seen through a window on a cold night. She carried a spark of Vesper in her chest now, and she carried it the way she'd once carried stolen purses: casually, proudly, with a pickpocket's certainty that nobody would ever catch her at it.

* * *

"We should inventory," Tetch said, later, when they'd limped as far as the old shepherd's road and Paloma had called a halt in the lee of a drystone wall. "Before we lose anything. Crews always lose things on the walk home. Boots. Fingers. Memories." She patted her satchel, where her recovered memory sat in its jar alongside everything else she'd pulled from the vault. "Trust me. I've read enough ledgers to know you count your goods while they're still breathing."

Paloma nodded, and it was the old nod, the auditor's nod, and something in Tetch's chest loosened to see it survive everything else.

So they counted themselves out, sitting against the wall in the climbing light.

"One oathbreaker knight," Paloma said. "Currently nameless."

"Lighter than I've been in twenty years," Marisol said. She flexed her sword hand, which still trembled from the duel. "The Court gave me back my knighthood and I spent it in an hour. Best purchase I ever made. Turns out I never wanted the title. I wanted permission to do the right thing badly."

"One heretic priest."

"Content," Quill confirmed.

"One cartographer." Tetch raised her hand. "Whole. Which is—" She stopped. Started again, because some things deserved saying properly. "I had a memory sold out of me once. I've been mapping places that move for six years because I couldn't map the place inside myself. Last night I opened a jar and remembered who I was before the Court got its fingers in me." She looked at each of them in turn. "Her name was also Tetch. She was worse at directions and better at trusting people. I've decided to keep both versions."

Nobody laughed. Nobody needed to. Wren leaned over and bumped her shoulder, and that was the whole ceremony.

"And one pickpocket," Paloma said quietly, "carrying a god."

"Carrying a *piece* of a god," Wren corrected. "He'd want to be accurate about it. He was very accurate." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone to her sternum, pressing, the way you press a bruise or a keepsake. "He talks sometimes. Not words. More like... warmth with opinions. When I'm frightened he gets brighter. Last night, in the dark part of the tunnel, he was so bright I could've read by him." She glanced at Paloma, and Tetch saw the question pass between them, the same question they'd been passing back and forth since the collapse: *was it worth it, was it worth it, are we allowed to say yes.*

Paloma just nodded, slow, and Wren breathed out, and the coal under her skin settled.

* * *

It was Quill, naturally, who raised the matter of salvage.

"The ruins," he said, jerking a thumb back toward the smoking fissure. "The Court's whole treasury was memories, but the physical holdings—the vault proper—it doesn't all burn. Some things are too stubborn."

"Nothing down there is worth going back for," Marisol said.

"One thing is." Quill looked at Paloma. "You know what I mean. You carried it in. Lot forty-one. *One thunderstorm, bottled, provenance unknown, temper uncertain.*"

Tetch felt her eyebrows go up. "You're joking. We went through all of that and you're proposing we loot a *weather system*?"

"It's an impossible thing," Paloma said. He'd gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet before making an entry. "I catalogued it eleven years ago. First week I held the guild's inventory. A client defaulted on a debt and paid us in a storm, and I put it on a shelf, and I have dusted that shelf every month since, wondering what kind of world produces a debt that can only be settled with weather." He stood. "The Court collected impossible things because impossible things are power. But the guild collects them because somebody has to keep them safe until the world's ready to know why they exist." He shouldered his pack. "Some things should survive. Even if we don't yet know what for."

"I'll come," Wren said immediately.

"No," said four voices.

"Yes," said Wren. "Look—I'm the fastest, I'm the smallest, and I'm carrying a god who spent a century hiding in that mountain. If anything in those ruins is going to recognize a friend, it's me." She paused. "Also you'll all argue about the route and get lost, and I won't."

Nobody had an answer to that. Tetch suspected nobody ever would again. The apprentice had out-thieved the masters; arguing jurisdiction with her now was like arguing with the tide.

* * *

They went back in pairs, and Tetch went with Wren because Tetch's legs were shortest and Wren set the pace anyway.

The lower vaults were drowning in their own light. Every lantern-soul had been released or consumed or simply gone out, and what remained was the architecture of fire without any fire in it—arches of glassy slag, shelves fused into sculpture, the Queen's auction hall open to the sky through a wound in the mountain's crown. Ash fell upward in the heat-drafts, snowing in reverse. Somewhere far below, stone was learning how to be lava again.

Wren moved through it like she owned it, ducking under a fallen beam, skipping across a crack that glowed dull orange, and stopped at last before a shelf that had somehow survived intact—a single alcove of black basalt, cool to the touch, untouched by the ruin around it.

The jar sat exactly where it had sat for a hundred years, because the Court had never known what it was and so had never dared touch it.

Inside the glass, the storm turned. Clouds the size of fingernails, lightning no longer than a hair, rain falling a quarter-inch and striking bottom and falling again. It had rained in that jar since before any of them were born, and it showed no sign of stopping, and when Tetch leaned close she could hear it—faint, furious, alive—thunder small as a beetle's heartbeat.

"Careful," she whispered, as Wren lifted it. "Bottled storms don't like—"

The storm noticed Wren. Tetch saw it happen: the clouds swirled, the lightning flickered twice, quick and bright, and the whole tiny tempest pressed itself against the glass toward her like a cat greeting its person.

"—huh," said Tetch.

"It likes me," Wren said, delighted.

"Everything impossible likes you lately."

"Well." Wren tucked the jar into her coat, snug against the spark in her chest, and Tetch watched the two lights greet each other—god-spark and storm-heart, ember and thunder, glowing softly through the wool. "Somebody has to carry them."

* * *

They climbed out for the second time, and the mountain let them go without a fight, because it had nothing left to keep.

By full morning they were on the valley road, five figures walking north with ash in their boots and their shadows stretched long behind them. Tetch brought up the rear, because rearguard was where she'd assigned herself years ago and old habits were the only maps that never changed.

She looked at each of them in turn, the way you check a compass against true north.

Marisol walked without armor, having left it in the ruins along with her restored name, and she moved easier than Tetch had ever seen her—as though she'd been marching in ill-fitting boots for two decades and had only now thought to take them off. Nameless and lighter. Whatever vow she'd choose next, she'd choose it herself, and that was worth more than any knighthood the Court could counterfeit.

Quill walked with his burnt robes hitched up and his prayer beads—he'd kept the beads, only the beads—clicking against his belt, and every few steps his lips moved. Not the old liturgies. Something newer. Tetch caught fragments: *thank you, thank you, I was wrong about the shape of you and right about the heart of you, thank you.* A heretic's grace. Contentment looked strange on a priest, like snow on a forge, but it suited him.

Wren walked in the middle, because the middle was where families put the ones they'd fought mountains to retrieve. The jar rode in her coat. The spark rode in her chest. Two impossible things, carried by a girl who'd started life as a line item in a pawnshop contract and had refused, loudly and repeatedly, to end as one.

And Paloma walked at the front, alone at the front the way he'd always walked, except that now and then—one step in twenty, no pattern to it, Tetch had checked—he drifted sideways, toward Wren, until their shoulders touched, and then drifted back. A man learning a new habit. A man who had been bound to silence and service since before she'd met him, walking unsworn for the first time in his adult life, and finding that his feet still pointed at the same people.

*Tetch, whole,* she counted for herself, and touched the jar in her satchel where her own name waited in suspension, hers again, all of it, even the parts that hurt. Especially the parts that hurt. Wholeness wasn't the absence of cracks; wholeness was carrying every piece you'd ever been, in one container, in the same direction.

The road bent around a hillside, and the guildhall's rooftops appeared in the middle distance—slate and chimney-smoke and the weathervane Marisol kept threatening to fix, turning lazily in a wind that smelled of ordinary bread.

"There it is," Wren said. "Home."

"Not yet," Paloma said.

Everyone stopped. He hadn't broken stride, hadn't turned around, but the word carried down the road the way his words always did—quietly, and landing like a stamp on wax.

"The ledger opens both ways," he said. "That's the first thing it ever taught me. Every entry gets answered. We walked out of that mountain carrying more than we carried in, and the guild's books need to know it." He glanced back, and Tetch saw it in his face—not the old careful blankness, but something warmer and less finished, a page not yet written. "Five names crossed that threshold going down. Five come back, but none of them the same. Before we walk through that door, somebody has to decide what we're worth now."

Wren grinned, and the spark under her skin flickered bright enough that Tetch saw it reflected in the road dust.

"Lucky for you," she said, "I know somebody who keeps excellent books."
