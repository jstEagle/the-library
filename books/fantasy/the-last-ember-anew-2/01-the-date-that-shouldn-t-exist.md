## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The bone came to Freya wrapped in oilcloth and lies.

That was normal. Most things worth carving arrived that way in Thornfell — the good ivory out of the drowned coast, the wyrm-rib from the deep passes, all of it moving through smugglers' hands like water finding cracks in stone. Freya had long since stopped asking where a piece came from. Bones didn't care about borders. They cared about being heard.

They set the rib on the workbench and pinned it down with leather straps, and the lamplight caught along its length the way light catches on a frozen river — pale, veined, faintly warm even though it had been cold in the ground for who knew how long. A woman's bone. Freya knew it before they touched it, the way you know rain is coming by the smell of the air. The rib was a woman's, and she had been old, and she had died afraid of something that wasn't death.

"Right," Freya said aloud, to the workshop, to the shavings on the floor, to no one. "Let's see what you've got."

The first pass of the graver went smooth as breath. Bone always told you how it wanted to be worked — this one wanted curves, wanted spirals, wanted the pattern of smoke rising. Freya followed it, chips curling away beneath their thumb, and let the rhythm take them the way it always did: the small music of steel on calcium, the smell of dust like chalk and something older underneath, something like rain on a grave.

The voice came at the third hour, when the lamp had burned low and Freya's shoulders ached in the good way.

*Fire,* said the bone.

Freya's hand stilled. They didn't flinch — you learned not to flinch, in this trade. The dead spoke through carved bone the way wind spoke through a cracked window, and most of what they said was small and sad and repetitive: names, regrets, the temperature of the moment they died. Freya had spent fifteen years learning to listen without drowning. They kept working.

*Fire,* the voice said again, and this time it had a shape to it — an old woman, low and dry as autumn reeds. *Fire coming down the mountain. Fire with hands. Fire that writes.*

"Writes," Freya murmured. "That's a new one."

*It knows your name.*

Chips fell away. The spiral grew. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters the way it always did in the weeks before solstice, when the mountain seemed to hold its breath. Freya worked until the candle guttered, and the voice stayed with them the whole time, patient, repeating itself like a lullaby gone wrong: *fire, fire, fire that writes.*

When they finally set down the graver and flexed their stiff fingers, the rib bore a finished border of rising smoke, waiting for the client's chosen inscription. And Freya sat alone in the dark with their heart going too fast for no reason they could name.

Dead women said strange things. That was the whole of the craft. You carved them a place to speak, and they said what they'd carried, and you gave it to whoever paid, and the world went on turning on its rails of roads and routes and debts. That was how it worked.

So why did the word *name* sit in Freya's chest like a swallowed coal?

They banked the lamp and went to bed, and dreamed of smoke that spelled things out against the stars.

* * *

The cargo arrived three days later, and with it, everything broke.

Rurik Ostrov's runners brought it up the switchback trail at dusk — six crates, tarped against the drizzle, hauled by two mules and one exhausted-looking boy who wouldn't meet Freya's eye. Rurik himself hadn't come. That was also normal; Rurik only appeared in person when he wanted something or owed something, and he'd been conspicuously absent since the blood-debt between them had settled last spring. The manifest was in his cramped, slanted hand: bone, salt-cured hides, ironmongery, *one item for inspection, client request.* Payment in advance. No return address.

Freya pried open the crates by lamplight while the rain thickened against the roof. Salt hides, as promised. Iron pots. And in the fifth crate, wedged between false bottom and straw, wrapped in waxed canvas gone brittle with age —

A book.

Not a rare one, at first glance. Freya had seen enough of them to know: vellum gone the color of old teeth, boards swollen with damp, the spine stitched in the old Thornfell style that predated the clans' shattering by a century at least. Someone had tried to disguise it as a ledger. The disguise was insultingly bad. Ledgers didn't get bound in consecrated hide, and they didn't smell of shrine-smoke when you opened them.

The first pages were indeed a ledger — grain tallies, toll payments, entries in faded brown ink. Then, a third of the way through, the handwriting changed, and so did the language, sliding into the high ceremonial script that the Order used and no one else dared.

Freya read standing up, because sitting suddenly felt impossible.

*And in the hundredth year after the breaking,* the text ran, *the Ember shall be taken up again by one whose hands remember the dead. Born under the north-fall star. A carver of bone, unlettered in the Order's ways, who hears the voices the Order silences.*

Below it, in ink darker than the rest, as if added later by a different, more certain hand:

*The Emberbearer's name is written here, and shall not be known until the night of its writing.*

And below that, in fresh ink — fresh, the black still faintly glossy, days old at most:

**Freya Ishikawa.**

And beside the name, a date.

Tonight.

The lamp hissed. Rain walked its fingers across the roof. Freya stood very still and felt the floor tilt under them — not literally, never literally, but the other way, the worse way, the way the world tilts when the thing you were standing on turns out to have been moving all along.

"No," they said. Their voice sounded small in the workshop, smaller than any dead voice they'd ever channeled. "No, that's not — someone wrote that. Someone's having a joke. Someone wants me to look at this and—"

But their eyes went back to the date, and the date was today, and their own name sat under it in ink that hadn't dried when Rurik's mules were climbing the trail, and the handwriting — Freya knew handwriting, knew it the way they knew bone, by grain and pressure and the tremor of the living hand — the handwriting was nobody's they'd ever seen. It was careful. It was certain. It was *waiting*, the way a trap waits.

They turned pages with fingers that had stopped feeling like their own. The prophecy continued, dense and terrible, speaking of an ember stolen from the old gods' hearth, of a war that had shattered the mountain clans into the scattered, feuding remnants Freya had grown up among. Speaking of the Ashen Order, who had carried the ember away and called it holy theft. Speaking of the solstice — eleven nights hence — and of a choice, though the page here was damaged, water-eaten, the crucial lines dissolved into gray ghosts of letters.

*Fire,* whispered the rib on the workbench, quiet as breath. *Fire with hands. I told you.*

"You told me nothing useful," Freya said, and hated how their voice shook.

They thought about roads then — the way they always thought about roads when the world went wrong. The salt road down to Verren. The high passes through the Teeth. The river route Rurik favored, all bribes and blind eyes. Fifteen years of knowing exactly which paths were safe, which innkeeps could be trusted, which checkpoints took silver and which took blood. A map drawn inside their skull, worn smooth with use, as reliable as the sunrise.

And now every line of it looked wrong. Because if this book was real, then the world had a second map laid over the first, a map of names and fates and hundred-year plans, and Freya had just discovered they were marked on it — not as a traveler but as a piece on the board. *Emberbearer.* The word sat in their mouth like a stone. They hadn't asked for it. They hadn't agreed to it. Some dead scribe with certain ink had reached across a century and picked them up like a tool off a bench.

"I'm not yours," they told the book.

It said nothing, of course. Books were worse than bones that way. Bones at least answered.

Freya should burn it. That was the sensible move — feed it to the stove, tell Rurik the fifth crate was empty, take the payment and forget the whole mad night. Anyone with sense would. But their hands wouldn't move toward the stove. Their hands held the book the way they held a rib mid-carving, gently, listening, and somewhere beneath the terror a colder thought was forming, sharp as a graver's edge:

Someone had gone to enormous trouble to put this book in a crate on this trail to this door. Which meant either the prophecy was true, or someone wanted Freya to believe it was — and both possibilities ended with Freya standing exactly where they stood now, holding a fire that wasn't lit yet.

*It knows your name,* the dead woman had said.

"It doesn't get to," Freya answered.

Outside, far down the valley, a light flickered where no light should be — not a farmhouse glow, not a lantern on the trail. It moved. It moved *upward*, against the roads, against the switchbacks, a slow orange thread climbing through the rain-dark toward the high country. Toward the old collapsed shrines that dotted the passes like knucklebones scattered by a careless god.

Toward, Freya realized with a vertigo deeper than any the book had given them, the place the dead woman's voice had been circling all week, the place her words kept bending toward no matter how Freya tried to straighten them out.

The lamp guttered. On the workbench, the carved rib lay in its half-finished spiral of smoke, and for the first time in fifteen years of listening, Freya was afraid of what a voice might say next.

*Fire,* the bone whispered, almost tenderly. *Go and find it before it finds you.*

Freya closed the book, took down their traveling cloak, and blew out the lamp.
