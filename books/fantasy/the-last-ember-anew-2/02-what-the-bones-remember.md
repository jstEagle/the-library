## 2. What the Bones Remember

The voice did not stop at dawn.

Freya had expected it to fade the way the dead usually did — a slow thinning, like smoke through a shutter's gap, until only the shape of the woman remained in the bone and not her breath. But this one clung. All through the night Freya had sat with the commission piece on their bench, a rib of mountain elk destined for some lowland lord's mantelpiece, and all through the night the dead woman had spoken through it, patient as water wearing at stone.

*Follow the cold,* she said. *Follow the cold where I was warm.*

By morning light, gray and thin through the workshop's single window, Freya set down the carving knife and pressed their palms flat against the elk rib as if to still a heartbeat that wasn't there.

"Who are you?" they asked aloud, because speaking made the voices clearer, gave them something to push against.

*You know my name already,* the woman said, and there was something almost amused in it, almost tender. *You have been saying it for three days without knowing. Say it again. Slower.*

Freya closed their eyes. The name rose the way names always rose when they worked bone — unbidden, arriving whole, the way a stone comes up out of river mud already polished. "Maren," they said. And then, because the syllables opened something: "Maren of the High Passes."

The workshop went very quiet. Even the wind outside seemed to hold its tongue.

*Yes,* Maren said. *Now bring me home.*

---

Freya should have refused. They knew every reason why — the commission due in nine days, the client who paid in silver that fed them through winter, the simple animal wisdom of never following a dead woman anywhere. Bone-carvers who listened too long went strange. Everyone in Thornfell knew it. Old Sefa had followed a voice into the white country thirty years back and come home with frostbitten feet and nothing she would say about what she'd found.

But the voice wasn't pulling. That was the thing Freya couldn't explain, even to themselves, as they packed rope and bread and the good knife and left a note pinned under a stone for any client who came asking. A voice that pulls is a trap. This one *waited*. It waited the way a friend waits at a crossroads, hands empty, trusting you to choose.

They climbed out of the valley by the old shepherd tracks, the ones the maps had stopped marking two generations ago. Snow lay in the shadows like spilled salt. The air thinned and sharpened until each breath felt minted fresh from metal. And always beneath the wind, steady as a pulse: *follow the cold.*

Near midday, the ridge opened onto a bowl of stone, and Freya stopped so suddenly their boots skidded on scree.

It had been a shrine once. You could still read its bones the way Freya read anything — by what remained standing and what had been made to fall. Pillars of black granite, snapped at the waist. A roof of slate slabs slumped inward like a collapsed lung. And everywhere, worked into every surviving surface, carvings: spirals, hands, flames rendered in the old deep-cut style that no living mason practiced anymore. Someone had loved this place enough to fill it with art. Someone else had hated it enough to break it.

*Here,* Maren said, and for the first time her voice trembled. *Under the altar stone. Where they could not dig deep enough.*

The altar had been toppled face-down, which was the only reason it survived. Freya dug for an hour, fingers bleeding through their gloves, the cold climbing up their arms like a tide. The ground beneath the shrine was wrong — warmer than it should be, though everything above was frozen. Warmth rising from below, cold pressing down from above, and Freya caught between them like a fish in ice.

Their gloves touched something smooth.

They pulled it free and nearly dropped it.

It was a shard of what looked like glass, if glass could hold a sunset — a splinter of ember-colored light no longer than a finger, edges worn soft, weightless in the palm and yet somehow heavier than the whole mountain. It did not glow. It *remembered* glowing, the way a scar remembers the wound. And it was cold. Not winter-cold. Cold the way the space between stars must be cold, a cold that had nothing to do with temperature and everything to do with absence.

Then it spoke.

Not in Maren's voice. In *all* of them.

Every soul Freya had ever carved for — the drowned ferryman whose knucklebone now hung in a merchant's purse, the child lost to fever whose finger-bones the mother kept in a cedar box, the old smuggler, the young bride, the nameless ones sold in lots to travelers who wanted luck or memory or proof that love outlasted the body — all of them spoke at once, a chorus layered like strata in stone, and every one of them said the same word:

*Bear.*

Freya fell to their knees in the ruined shrine. The shard sat in their bare hand, burning cold, and the chorus swelled and shaped itself into something almost like language, almost like a sentence:

*We waited. We chose. Carry us home or carry us nowhere, but carry us.*

"I didn't ask for this." Their own voice sounded small and cracked in the vast silence. "I carve names. That's all. I keep people remembered. I don't—"

*You do more than remember,* said Maren, rising clear above the others for one moment, one voice among the flood. *You listen. No one listens anymore, little carver. That is why we chose you.*

"Chose me for *what?*"

But the shard had gone quiet again, humming that starless hum against their skin, and the mountain offered no answer except wind.

---

They heard the horses before they saw them — iron shoes on frozen stone, a sound like teeth.

Freya had grown up on stories of the Ashen Order the way children grow up on stories of wolves: half warning, half wonder. Gray cloaks. White ash sigils. Men and women who burned what the old clans built and called it cleansing. But stories stayed in stories. Stories did not ride over the ridge in ranks of six, torches lit at noon, faces grim beneath steel caps.

"There!" The shout cracked across the bowl of the shrine. "The thief has found it!"

Freya did not think. Thinking was a luxury of people whose worlds still held their shape. They shoved the shard into the inner pocket of their coat, against their chest, where its cold sank through wool and linen and settled against their heart like a second, stranger heartbeat — and ran.

The scree took them down the far side of the ridge in a controlled avalanche of their own making, boots skating, hands out, the world reduced to slope and sky and the drumming behind them. An arrow hissed past close enough to part the hair near their ear. Another struck stone and shattered into sparks.

"The Emberbearer runs!" someone bellowed, and the title landed on Freya's back like a brand. *Emberbearer.* A word from a book they'd read exactly once, inked beside their own name and a date that had not yet finished becoming today. They had told themselves it was coincidence. Coincidence did not send six riders and a quiver of arrows.

The pass swallowed them.

It was a crack in the mountain's shoulder, barely shoulder-wide, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall — the kind of place only smugglers and goats ever learned. Freya had learned it at fourteen, hauling contraband bone for a man who'd since died owing them money. Now they wedged themselves into the dark and cold and wet and pressed their spine to stone while the riders wheeled and shouted somewhere above, their voices bouncing off the cliffs, losing each other, losing Freya.

Hours passed. Or years. The light in the crack of the falls went from noon-gray to dusk-violet to nothing. Freya's legs cramped. Their breath frosted and hung around their head like a shroud. And through all of it, the shard pulsed against their chest, patient, unhurried, cold as a promise.

*They will not stop,* Maren said quietly, from somewhere deeper than the pocket, deeper than the bone. *They have hunted this fire for a hundred years. They burned my shrine. They burned me. They will burn whatever stands between them and what you carry.*

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

*What you were always going to do,* she said. *Carry us. There is a place the ember must return to before the solstice. You know the roads better than any living soul. That is the other reason we chose you, Freya Ishikawa. Not only your listening. Your walking.*

Freya laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it, muffled against their own knees in the dark behind a frozen waterfall while men with torches combed the mountain for them. "I'm a bone-carver. I make trinkets for rich lords. I don't save fires. I don't end wars. I don't—"

The shard warmed. Just slightly. Just enough to feel, against the breastbone, like a hand laid flat.

Somewhere in the chorus, faint beneath a hundred dead voices, Freya heard their own grandmother — whose wrist-bone hung on a cord around their neck, carved with her name in Freya's own hand — say, gently: *You were never just keeping names, little one. You were practicing.*

The torchlight swept past the waterfall curtain and moved on. The shouts grew distant, then scattered, then ceased.

Freya stood, stiff and shaking, and looked up the dark throat of the pass toward the high country — toward the white country, the roof of the world, the place where maps gave up and the solstice lived like a blade on the calendar, nineteen days away.

Nineteen days. Six hunters. One shard full of the dead.

"Well," Freya said to no one, to everyone, to the cold thing beating against their heart. "I suppose you're coming with me."

The shard pulsed once, like a nod.

They began to climb.

Behind them, far below in the gathering dark, a horn sounded from the direction of the valley — long, low, and answered by another, and another, a chain of horns running along the whole spine of Thornfell like fire catching a fuse. The Order was calling in its hunt.

And ahead, at the mouth of the high pass where the last light died against the snow, a figure stood waiting in the shadows — a man in a gray cloak with the ash sigil torn from it, sword drawn, watching Freya come.
