## 3. A Defector's Debt

The rain had followed Cassian Vane for three days, and he had begun to think of it as a judgment.

It came down in the lowlands the way it never did in the mountains — a gray, patient soaking that found every seam of leather, every crack in a man's resolve. He sat beneath a leaning alder at the edge of the Verren road with his hood drawn and his sword across his knees, watching the mud swallow the wheel-ruts whole, and he tried to remember when he had last slept somewhere dry. Somewhere honest.

He knew the road because he had patrolled it. Six years in the Ashen Order's gray-and-cinder livery, six years of escorting wagons through Thornfell's passes, and he could read traffic in the mud the way priests read scripture. Which was how he knew, three hours before he saw her, that someone small and burdened had come down off the high trail alone — the stride too long for comfort, the left footprint deeper than the right, favoring an old injury or a heavy pack carried wrong.

And he knew why she would come down at all. Word moved faster than weather through the Order's network. *A bone-carver has taken the shard. The Emberbearer walks.* Serath Hale had said it in the chapter-house with that terrible gentleness of his, the way he said everything, as if the whole world were a child who needed explaining to. *Find them before the solstice. Bring back what our grandfathers bled for.*

Cassian had been gone from the chapter-house by dawn.

Not defected. Not yet. He refused the word even now, sitting in the rain with a stolen horse tethered behind him and a hole where his oaths used to live. Defection implied you had chosen something else. He had only chosen *away* — away from the smoke of Hollowmere still clinging to his cloak no matter how many rivers he'd washed it in, away from the memory of a village elder kneeling in the ash asking, not for mercy, but *why*, and Serath answering with scripture while the fires ate the roofs.

He had not raised his hand at Hollowmere. That was the thing that kept him awake. He had stood there in his fine armor and done nothing, which the Order called discipline and which Cassian's bones, in the long dark hours, called something else entirely.

So: he would intercept the carver. Take the shard. Return it himself, unasked, and let the Order believe their lost knight had come home. And then — then he would be clean again. Then the ash would wash out.

That was the story he told himself as the rider finally appeared through the rain, and it lasted exactly as long as it took him to see her clearly.

*Them,* rather. Small figure, hood back despite the wet, dark hair plastered flat, and riding like a person who had learned horses late and resented every hour of it. Freya Ishikawa. He had seen their name on the cargo manifests he once escorted — bone-carver, Thornfell side, reliable, discreet. Reliable people did not run from knights. Discreet people did not carry gods' fire in their saddlebags.

Cassian stepped out of the alder's shadow into the road.

The horse shied; Freya hauled its head around with more anger than skill, and under the hood their eyes went from his face to the sword at his hip to the gray wool of his traveling cloak — plain enough, but a soldier's cut, and they saw it. Their hand dropped to something at their belt. A knife, probably. A carving tool. It didn't matter which; he watched them decide to die reaching for it rather than sit still, and something in his chest turned over.

"Freya Ishikawa." His voice came out rougher than intended. "I'm not going to hurt—"

"You're Order." Flat. Certain. Rain ran off their chin. "I can smell the incense they burn into your cloaks. Cinder-sage. It doesn't wash out."

No. It didn't. He had three days of proof of that.

"I've left the Order," he said, and heard immediately how it sounded — a lie wrapped in a truth, the oldest armor there is.

Freya laughed. There was no warmth in it at all. "Then walk past me."

*Walk past me.* Two words and a command, and Cassian stood in the road with the rain coming down and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the moment. That all the miles since Hollowmere had been leading here, to this narrow muddy place, where a stranger offered him the simplest test in the world and every version of his future hung on which way he walked.

He opened his mouth —

— and the treeline answered first.

* * *

They came out of the pines without sound, which should have been impossible for men in plate, and Cassian knew their silhouettes the way he knew his own hands. Brother Aldric on the left, shield up. Brother Tomas on the right, crossbow already leveled. And behind them, unhurried, gray cloak beaded silver with rain, Serath Hale.

"Cassian." Serath's voice was gentle. It was always gentle. It had been gentle at Hollowmere. "We grieved you, son. Come home."

Every hair on Cassian's arms rose. Because Serath had not sent two brothers after a deserter. Two brothers was what you sent to *retrieve*. This was a hunting party of three, spread wide, crossbow strung — this was what you brought when the order was *no witnesses*.

"Tomas," Cassian said quietly, not moving his eyes from Serath. "Put it down."

"The shard, carver," Serath said to Freya, sweet as a psalm. "And we'll leave your body for the crows with its sins forgiven. You've been lied to, child. What you carry belongs to the faithful."

"It belongs to the dead," Freya said. Their voice shook, but they held the horse steady, and their other hand had closed around something in their pack, and Cassian saw light — cold, white-gold, wrong-colored — leaking between their fingers like water through a fist.

Tomas's crossbow tracked from Cassian to Freya.

There was no decision in what Cassian did next. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the thing that finally undid him: not courage, not redemption, just a body moving faster than its shame. Three strides. His shoulder hit Freya and the horse both, hauling them sideways off the road as Tomas loosed — the bolt cracked off the alder where Freya's chest had been — and then Cassian was between them and his brothers with his sword out and rain in his eyes and six years of brotherhood standing on the other side of it.

"Aldric," he said. "Please."

Aldric's jaw worked under his helm. "You don't have to die for a stranger, Cass."

"No," Cassian agreed. "I died at Hollowmere. This is just the burial."

Serath sighed — actually sighed, sorrowful as a father — and drew his sword, and it caught what little light the day allowed and threw it back white. "Take the carver alive. The traitor, do as you like."

What followed took eleven seconds and Cassian counted them all his life. Aldric came in honest, shieldsman's angles, and Cassian gave ground, gave ground, gave ground — let the big man overcommit on the wet clay, hooked his ankle, put him down hard enough to ring his helm on a stone. Tomas dropped the empty crossbow for his mace and got one swing, a good one, glancing off Cassian's pauldron with a blow that numbed his arm to the elbow. Then Freya's knife — their *carving tool*, he'd learn later, honed finer than any soldier's blade — opened the back of Tomas's knee, and the brother went down screaming a word that wasn't a word.

Which left Serath.

Serath, who had taught Cassian the first forms. Who had held his shoulders when his mother died. Who looked at him now over the crossed swords with an expression of such tender disappointment that it was worse than hatred, worse than anything.

"You were the best of us," Serath said.

"I know," Cassian said. "That's why I left."

Steel met steel twice, three times — and then Freya made a sound behind him, short and startled and afraid, and the cold light flared, and Serath staggered as if struck, as if something in the air itself had turned against him. Cassian didn't waste the miracle. He broke his mentor's guard and laid the flat of his blade — the *flat*, God help him, some part of him still refusing — against the side of Serath's helm, and the chapter-master of the Ashen Order folded into the mud among his fallen brothers.

Silence, then. Just rain, and Tomas moaning, and the horse blowing nervously, and the shard's strange light fading back down to nothing inside Freya's clenched fist.

Cassian stood over Serath and shook, and could not have said whether it was fear or grief or the last of the ash finally leaving his lungs.

* * *

They rode east until the light failed, double-mounted because Freya's horse had bolted during the fight and Cassian's stolen gelding bore them both, badly. They made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, old clan-work, its stones furred with moss, and neither of them spoke until Freya had a small fire going — small, screened, the fire of people who expected to be hunted — and thrust a tin cup of something hot and bitter into his hands.

"Why?" they asked.

Cassian looked at the fire. At his own reflection warped in the tea. At the bandage Freya had insisted on wrapping around his forearm where Aldric's blade had kissed him, their fingers quick and impersonal and strangely careful, a craftsman handling material they hadn't decided what to make of yet.

"There was a village," he said. "Hollowmere. The Order burned it because an old woman there remembered a song the founders wanted forgotten. Forty-one people." He swallowed. "I stood in my armor and I did nothing. And then I told myself if I could just bring them the shard, undo my desertion, I'd be clean." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Then I saw them spread out in the trees and I understood they weren't sent to take you back. They were sent so there'd be no one left to tell the tale. And I understood there's no version of me that goes home. There's only the version that decides what it stands in front of."

Freya was quiet a long time. Rain dripped through the broken tower roof, soft as counting.

"The voices in my bones say you're telling the truth," they said at last. "Or that you believe it. With the dead, those aren't always the same thing." They fed a stick to the fire. "You could still take it, you know. I can't stop you. One knight with a sword, one carver with a knife — you'd win."

"I know," Cassian said.

"So why haven't you?"

Because, he almost said, *because the shard saved us, because the light knocked Serath sideways like a wrathful saint, because I felt it choose* — but none of that was the true answer, and lying to this person suddenly felt like a sin worse than desertion.

"Because I spent six years carrying things for men who burned villages," he said. "I'd like to spend whatever's left guarding something instead."

Freya studied him across the fire with those unreadable dark eyes, and outside, far back along the way they'd come, a horn sounded — one long note, thin and cold, the Order's recall, or its summons, or its promise. Then a second horn answered it from the north. From the direction they were heading.

Freya closed their eyes. "They're boxing the passes."

"Yes," Cassian said quietly, gripping his cup, feeling the fragile new thing in his chest — hope, maybe, or just purpose — crack and hold, crack and hold. "And I know a way through that isn't on any of their maps. But we'll need help. There's a smuggler down in the lowlands who owes me nothing and owes *you* everything, and he's the only man alive who can get us across the Verren flats before solstice."

Freya opened their eyes. "Rurik Ostrov."

"You know him?"

"He owes me a blood-debt," Freya said, with a ghost of a smile that didn't reach anywhere near their eyes, "and he'll hate paying it. Let's go collect."
