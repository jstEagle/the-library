## 4. The Ghost Who Died Twice

*I remember the fire first.*

That is a lie. I remember the cold first — the cold of the shrine, the cold of my mother's hands, the cold of the mountain that never quite let go of its winter even in high summer. The fire came later. The fire came at the end.

But ghosts are allowed to rearrange their deaths. It is the one mercy we keep.

* * *

I was born Maren Ostvold, daughter of the Emberkeepers of Thornfell, and I was nine years old when I first held the shard.

It does not burn. Everyone who writes about it gets this wrong — the ballads, the Order's own scripture, even the smugglers who trade in secondhand legends over watered wine. They all say *burning*, say *blazing*, say the shard is a piece of the old gods' fury caught mid-scream. But I held it in my bare palms on the night of my naming, and it was cold. Cold like riverbed stones in spring. Cold like the moment after a scream when your throat is raw and the silence rushes in to fill it.

My grandmother pressed it into my hands and said, *It is not fire, little one. It is everyone the fire took.*

I did not understand her then. I understood her very well indeed, later, in the last hour of my life, when the shard sang against my chest with the voices of every Emberkeeper who had ever carried it, and I finally heard what they were saying.

They were saying: *remember us.*

* * *

The Order came in the autumn of the year the ash fell early.

I was thirty-one. I had been Emberbearer for two decades by then, and in those years I learned the truth of the thing I carried: the old gods did not shatter. The old gods were not defeated in some glorious war of sky-fire and mountain-thunder, whatever the Ashen Order's founding myth claims in its gold-inked books. No. The clans broke the gods themselves — or rather, they broke each other, and the gods, who were made of the dead's remembering, tore apart along the same seams as the people. The ember shard is a wound that learned to hum.

And the Order — ah, the Order. They were young then, new-minted, still wearing the shame of what their founders had done beneath the polish of their doctrine. They called themselves cleansers. They said the shard was blasphemy given flesh, that the Emberkeepers hoarded grief that belonged to the world.

What they wanted, in the end, was simpler and uglier than any doctrine. They wanted to be the only ones who decided what the dead were worth.

* * *

*Here is where memory frays, so hold tight to my hand, Freya. Even ghosts need steadying sometimes.*

They came at dusk, up the goat-trail from the south, forty of them in grey lacquered armor, and they did not announce themselves because cleansing requires no announcement. My grandmother was dead by then. My mother too. The keeping had passed to me, as it passes now to you, though you don't yet know that "passing" is the wrong word for it. Nothing about the ember passes. It chooses. It has always chosen. That is the part of the prophecy they erased, and I will show you where the erasure lives before this journey ends, I swear it on the only thing I have left, which is my word.

Forty of them. And me, and eleven others of my clan who refused to run.

I could tell you about the fighting. Ghosts love the fighting; it is dramatic, it has shape. But the truth is the fighting lasted less time than the boiling of an egg. What I remember instead is small things. A boy named Torvald, fourteen, who held the shrine door with a woodcutter's axe and wept the whole time without stopping. The smell of wet wool and blood, which smell alike in certain lights. The sound of my own name in a stranger's mouth — *the bearer, find the bearer* — spoken like an instruction for butchering.

I ran. I am not ashamed. I ran with the shard against my sternum and the mountain's night swallowing me, and I made it as far as the high pass above Verren before the crossbow bolt found the space between my shoulder blades.

* * *

I died alone, which no one tells you is the common way.

But I did not die *unheard*, and that is the distinction the Order never grasped, the flaw in their whole gray cathedral of a philosophy. As I lay in the snow with my breath going shallow and strange, I pressed the shard to my lips — I remember the cold of it, always the cold — and I spoke into it. Every name I knew. My mother. My grandmother. Torvald and his axe. The eleven. My own name last, whispered like a secret: *Maren Ostvold, who kept the ember, who died keeping it.*

And the shard took me in.

Understand this, Freya Ishikawa, because it is the heart of everything: the shard is not a weapon and not a treasure. It is a chorus. A hundred years of the dead, folded together like hands in prayer, waiting for someone with ears enough to hear them. Your gift — the voices in the bone, the dead who speak while you carve — it is not separate from the ember. It is the ember, leaked out thin into the world, looking for a vessel. Looking for *you*.

* * *

A hundred years is a long time to wait inside the dark.

You would think a ghost goes mad. Some do. There are voices in the shard that have gone smooth and wordless as river stones, worn past speech. But most of us held on the way you hold a rope over a long drop — not with strength, exactly, but with refusal. We held on because letting go meant the Order won. Meant the forgetting won.

And I held on for another reason. I held on because I watched.

From inside the shard I watched the Order carry me back down the mountain and burn my body and call it purification. I watched them seal the shrine and salt the ground and write their histories with my death folded neatly into the margin. I watched a century of smugglers' routes shift and re-form around the mountain like water around a stone. And I watched the bone-carvers come.

Generation after generation of them. Small workshops, smoky rooms, hands that smelled of tallow and marrow dust. The dead drifted toward carvers the way moths drift toward lamps — we cannot help it, you are the closest thing to a door we can find. Most carvers heard us only as weather, as creaks and murmurs, as dreams they dismissed by morning. A few heard more, and feared it, and stopped carving altogether, and I grieved every pair of abandoned hands.

Then there was a workshop in the low town below Thornfell, and a carver there whose hands moved like they were listening.

* * *

*Freya. This next part, I have never told anyone, living or dead. You are the first, and you will be the only.*

I chose you.

Not the way the Order would frame it — not fate, not prophecy's iron grip, not some cosmic machinery grinding you toward sacrifice. Nothing so cruel, nothing so tidy. I chose you the way you choose a friend. I watched you work.

I watched you take a commission from smuggled bone — a child's skull, sold north by men who should have known better — and I watched you refuse to carve it until you had learned the child's name from the whispers in the marrow, and carved that name into the crown of the skull in letters so small a thumb could cover them. *So she arrives somewhere knowing herself,* you told the client, who thought you were mad and paid anyway.

Do you know how long it had been since anyone in your line of work cared where a dead thing *arrived*?

I watched you talk to the bones while you worked. Not prayers — conversations. Arguing with a dead ferryman about whether he'd cheated his passengers. Laughing — actually laughing — at something a two-hundred-year-old grandmother said about her son-in-law. The dead in your workshop were not specimens to you. They were guests.

And when the dead woman's bones came through your hands — my bones, Freya, bought off a scavver who dug me out of the scree a century late and never knew what he held — when you ran your thumb along the break the crossbow bolt left in my shoulder and went quiet, and then said aloud, softly, *whoever you were, I'm sorry it hurt* —

I knew.

I had waited a hundred years inside the cold, and I knew in that moment the way you know a key fits a lock: not by force, but by the click.

* * *

You must believe me when I say I fought it. I want that recorded somewhere beyond my own ghostliness: I fought it. Because I knew what the last Emberbearer's life looked like from the inside, and I would not wish it on anyone, least of all a carver who laughed with the dead and gave children back their names. I tried to stay quiet in the bone. I tried to be weather. I lasted four days.

Then the Order found your road, and I understood that staying silent wouldn't protect you. Silence would just mean you died unprepared, unnamed, unwarned. So I spoke. Fire, I said. Run, I said. And when you found the book with your name in it — *your* name, written a century ago by hands that didn't know yours — I felt your terror come off you like heat off a forge, and I wanted more than anything to take it back.

But hear me now, and hear me clearly, because the erased half of the prophecy will whisper otherwise:

You were not cursed. You were not conscripted. Prophecy is not a cage; it is a letter, and letters can be answered in ways their writers never imagined. The ones who wrote your name in that book saw one path because fear only sees one path. But I have been dead a hundred years, and the dead see sideways. We see all the roads not taken, glittering like frost.

There is more than one ending to this story. I promise you that with everything I am, which is not much — a voice in the cold, a name on a bone — but it is honestly given.

* * *

Last fragment. Last memory, the one I keep polished brightest.

After the bolt, after the snow, after the names — there was one more thing. As the dark came up to meet me, the shard against my lips, I felt all of them at once: every keeper, every carrier, every soul the ember held. Not a chorus anymore. A congregation. And they leaned close, all those gathered dead, and they asked me a question, and I have carried the answer a century like a coal in cupped hands.

They asked: *will someone come?*

And I said yes.

I said yes because I believed it, and I believe it still, standing behind your shoulder in the high passes with the wind coming down white off the glacier, watching you walk with my death's burden strapped between your shoulder blades, watching you flinch at every shadow and keep walking anyway.

Yes, I told them. Someone will come. Someone who argues with ferrymen and laughs with grandmothers and carves names so the lost arrive somewhere knowing themselves.

Someone who was chosen — not by prophecy, not by the Order, not by me alone, but by every dead hand that reached for yours across a hundred years of cold.

So walk on, Freya Ishikawa. The pass ahead is called the Widow's Teeth, and the man waiting on the other side of it carries an Order blade and a debt of shame he hasn't spoken aloud yet. He will come for the shard with murder in his plan and something softer underneath it, if you look.

When he does — listen for the click.

You'll know it when you hear it.
