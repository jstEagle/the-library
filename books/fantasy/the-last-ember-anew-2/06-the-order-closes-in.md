## 6. The Order Closes In

The smoke reached them before the news did.

Freya smelled it at dawn — not woodsmoke, which was honest and familiar, the smell of hearths and bread and Rurik's terrible attempts at coffee, but something sweeter and wrong beneath it. Pitch. Lamp oil. The particular stink of thatch burning wet, the way a building screams when it dies.

They stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Way, where the trail bent around a shoulder of granite and dropped toward the valley, and looked down at the waystation of Hollowmere. Even from this distance Freya could see the roof had gone. The building that had stood for three hundred years — that had fed every smuggler, pilgrim, and lost child who ever crossed the Thornfells — was a black ribcage against the pale morning sky.

"Three," Maren said quietly. She didn't need to breathe anymore, but sometimes she sighed anyway, out of habit or kindness. "That's the third one. Grey Ford burned two nights ago. Stonecross yesterday."

"I know." Freya's voice came out flat. Their hands, wrapped around the strap of their pack, were steady in the way hands get when they've stopped pretending anything is fine. "I counted."

The ember shard rode against their sternum, wrapped in wool and oilcloth and then, because Freya was superstitious in ways they'd never admit, wrapped again. It was cold. It was always cold now, colder than the mountain air, as if it were drinking warmth from everything near it. Some nights Freya woke certain it had grown heavier, though it never had when they weighed it in their palms.

Below them, ash lifted off the ruin of Hollowmere in slow grey spirals and drifted up the mountainside like snow falling the wrong direction.

"They're not chasing us anymore," Rurik said from behind. He'd come up silent as always; for a man his size he moved like spilled water. His eyes were on the smoke. "Chasing implies you might run somewhere. This is—" He made a gesture with one gloved hand, encompassing the pass, the valley, the distant white teeth of the high peaks. "This is closing a book. Burn the stations, burn the caches, burn the bridges. No beds, no food, no shelter between here and the lowlands. They're going to make the mountain itself do their killing."

"Cassian said the Order doesn't fight in winter," Freya said. "Said they wait people out. Starve them into surrender."

"Cassian said a lot of things about the Order." Rurik's tone wasn't cruel, exactly. It was worse than cruel — it was careful. "He also used to say the Order doesn't burn what it means to keep."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, a smuggler's cache Rurik knew from years past — a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, half-sheltered, with an old iron ring bolted into the rock where travelers had once strung tarps. There was no tarp now. There was barely enough deadfall to make a fire small enough to hide, and Rurik argued they shouldn't have one at all until Cassian pointed out, reasonably, that they would all freeze to death long before the solstice, and freezing to death would be a very stupid way to fail a prophecy.

So: a fire the size of two cupped hands, and four figures huddled around it — three living, one not, though Maren sat as close to the flames as any of them, her ghost-hands open toward heat she could no longer feel. She did it, Freya suspected, because it made the rest of them feel less alone. That was the kind of person she had been, a century ago, when she'd been a person at all. The kind who warmed her hands at fires she couldn't touch so others wouldn't sit shivering by themselves.

Cassian took first watch, as he always did. He climbed to the lip of the hollow where the rocks gave a view down-valley, settled himself against the granite with his sword across his knees, and became the thing he'd been trained to be: still, patient, watchful. A stone with eyes.

Freya tried to sleep. They failed.

It was the ember, they told themselves. The shard hummed against their chest at night, a frequency more felt than heard, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Lately the chorus inside it had been getting louder — fragments of voices, names, the last words of strangers. *Tell my daughter the loom is hers. The river was higher than this. I never meant—* It was like sleeping in a house full of murmuring guests who would not introduce themselves.

But it wasn't only the ember keeping them awake. It was the way Cassian hadn't slept in two days. It was the way he'd gone quiet after Hollowmere, quieter than his usual guarded quiet, and the way Rurik watched him when he thought no one was looking — the old smuggler's face folded into an expression that said, plainly, *I've seen men come home from the other side before, and I know how it ends.*

Freya rose without waking anyone — or so they thought — and climbed the hollow's rim to bring Cassian something warm. It felt like the smallest possible act of faith. They needed to perform some small act of faith tonight, or lose their grip entirely.

Cassian heard them coming, of course. He turned, and for a moment his face in the starlight looked almost young — surprised, unguarded, the mask slipping before he caught it and set it back in place.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"One of us has to watch the road."

"Then trade me," Freya said. "Half the night each. You're no use to anyone if you fall asleep standing up tomorrow and walk us off a cliff."

Something moved through his expression — gratitude, maybe, or guilt, hard to tell the difference in a man built like him. He accepted the tin cup Freya handed over. Their fingers brushed. His were ice.

And that was when Freya heard the voice on the wind.

At first they took it for the mountain — the Thornfells spoke constantly, groaning rock and whistling passes, a language of creak and sigh. But this voice was shaped. Human. Carried up the valley on the night air the way sound carries over snow, thin and clear across impossible distances.

"—amnesty, Vane. Full restoration. Your name cleared, your oath reinstated. The Grandmaster himself signed the writ."

Freya went still. Cassian had gone stiller.

Down the valley, perhaps a quarter mile, a lantern swung — a single point of light moving along the old drover's road, unhurried. And behind the light, faint but unmistakable, footsteps crunched on frozen ground. Two sets. Maybe three.

"Come down, boy." The voice was older, gravelled, worn smooth in the way stone gets worn by decades of the same hand touching it. It carried authority the way a blade carries an edge. "You've made your point. Run, hide, play at virtue in the hills. No one will even call it desertion anymore. All you have to do is bring me the shard and the bone-carver, and everything you were comes back to you. Your rank. Your brothers. Your *name.*"

Freya's heart slammed once, hard, and then went strangely quiet.

They looked at Cassian.

Cassian was looking down the valley, at the lantern, and his face was doing something terrible and private — the look of a man watching his own history climb the mountain toward him wearing a familiar voice. Freya saw his knuckles whiten on the cup. Saw his jaw work. Saw, for one long suspended heartbeat, something in his eyes that was not refusal. Something that was hunger. Homesickness with teeth. The look of someone being offered the door back into the only house he'd ever known.

*Everything you were comes back to you.*

Freya thought: *he could do it. He could kill me right here, take the shard, walk down there, and be whole again. And who could blame him? Who among us wouldn't want to go home?*

Maren appeared beside them — she moved like smoke when she wanted to, and she'd been listening too, her translucent face tight. She said nothing. Ghosts, Freya had learned, understood better than anyone that the dead cannot choose for the living, no matter how much they ache to.

Down in the dark, the mentor's voice again, softer now, almost gentle, which was somehow worse: "I trained you, Vane. I know what you are underneath all this. You think you abandoned us. You didn't. You just got lost. Let me bring you home."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked softly through the branches above. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang one long descending note.

"No," Cassian said.

One word. But he said it aloud, into the valley, letting it carry — and Freya realized that was deliberate. He wasn't refusing privately. He was refusing *witnessed.*

"I know what you offered the village at Ashenmoor," Cassian called down, his voice roughening, gathering force like a rolling stone. "I carried the torches myself. So keep your amnesty. Keep your name. If the price of mine is another Ashenmoor, I'd rather be nameless the rest of my life." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "You trained me well enough to know the difference between orders and oaths. I finally learned which one I swore."

The lantern below swung once, twice, and began to withdraw down the valley — unhurried still, which frightened Freya more than haste would have. Men who hurry have lost. Men who retreat slowly are counting.

Cassian sat back down against the rock. He drained the cup Freya had brought, though it must have gone cold in his hands. For a long moment neither of them spoke.

"He'll come back with a hundred," Cassian said at last. "Not to negotiate. To bury us."

Freya nodded. But the nod was a lie, because something in their chest had cracked along a fault line they hadn't known was there, and the crack was widening. They believed him. That was the worst part — watching him refuse, hearing the truth ring in it like a struck bell, believing him *completely* —

—and still seeing, every time they closed their eyes, that flicker in his gaze when the word *home* crossed the snow. The hunger. The heartbeat of hesitation. Trust, Freya was learning, was not a wall that held or broke. It was bone: it bore weight, and it remembered every fracture, and it healed crooked or not at all.

They descended into the hollow and lay down beside the dying fire, and Maren settled near them, and the ember hummed its crowd of whispers against their ribs.

*He chose,* Maren said softly, only for them. *You heard him choose.*

*I know,* Freya said. *I heard.*

But sleep, when it finally took them, was shallow and full of smoke, and near morning they dreamed of black water under grey ice, of a lake holding a drowned archive in its cold fist, of shelves of books breathing bubbles, and of Maren's voice saying words they couldn't yet understand:

*There's a second half to the prophecy, little carver. And you need to see it before you trust anyone again — including me.*
