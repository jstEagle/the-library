## 7. Prophecy's Fine Print

*The dead do not sleep. I have told you this before, and it remains true: we drift. We hover at the edges of the living like frost on a window, watching warmth we cannot hold.*

*I had not wanted Freya to see this place.*

---

The lake called Verren sits in a bowl of granite high above the tree line, so cold that even in midsummer the shallows wear a skin of ice at dawn. The old clans built their archive beneath it — sunk on purpose, sealed with prayers, because paper burns and stone cracks but water keeps its secrets patient and whole. A century ago, when the Order came for everything written in the old hands, they missed this. Or they believed they'd already destroyed what mattered.

They were wrong about the second thing. They were right about the first, once.

I led Freya down through the drowned stairwell on the third night after the waystations burned, when smoke still hung in the high passes like a held breath. The entrance was a fissure in the lakeshore rock, half-hidden by a curtain of frozen overflow, and the water inside ran black and utterly still. Not stagnant — *waiting*. Old magic has a texture to it, and this was the texture of a room no one had entered since I was flesh.

Freya stood at the lip of the flooded passage with their lantern held high, breath steaming, and said nothing for a long time. That is one of the things I came to love about them: they know when silence is the correct response.

"It's under there," they said finally. Not a question. They could hear it too, by then. Through the shard, through me, through whatever strange thread had knotted itself between us since the shrine — they could feel the archive the way a tooth feels weather coming.

"Under there," I confirmed. "And Freya — what's down there will hurt you. I need you to know that before we go."

Their jaw did the thing it does, the stubborn set that Cassian had started calling *the carver's face*. "Everything hurts lately, Maren. At least this time I'll know why."

So we went down.

* * *

The water parted for us. I will not pretend otherwise; I spent what remained of my strength holding a corridor of air open through that black depth, a tunnel of trembling light, while Freya waded chest-deep with the lantern cupped against their body like something newborn. The shard rode against their sternum where they'd bound it, and it sang the entire way down — softly, a chorus hummed through clenched teeth, every voice I'd ever given them layered into one low note of anticipation.

The dead want to be remembered. The dead *ache* for it. And we were descending into the largest grave of names in the northern clans.

The archive opened around us like the ribcage of some enormous patient animal. Stone shelves rose from the floodwater in ranks, scrolls sealed in wax and oilcloth, tablets stacked in niches, and everywhere — everywhere — writing. The lantern light moved across it and the letters seemed to move back, the way letters do when they've been waiting a hundred years for eyes.

Freya found the prophecy book on the central dais, which rose just clear of the waterline, as though the archive itself had kept it dry for us. As though it had been *left out*, the way you leave a bowl of milk for something feral.

It was the same book. The same volume that had come smuggled through Thornfell, that had carried my warning and their name and tonight's date in its ink. But here, beneath the lake, it lay open to its true length — pages the smuggled copy had never contained, or had contained once, before someone scraped them blank.

"They erased it," Freya whispered. Their fingers hovered over the scraped parchment without touching. "The Order?"

"The Order's founders." I drifted close, and I felt my own death stir in me the way it always does near this book, the memory of Ashen knives and the smell of burning vellum. "But scraping doesn't always work, little carver. Ink goes deeper than the blade. Hold your lantern low. Let the light come *sideways* across the page."

They did. And the erased words rose up out of the parchment like bruises rising through skin — ghost-ink, the impression left where the pen had pressed, legible only to raking light and to anyone desperate enough to read it.

Freya read aloud. Their voice started steady and did not stay that way.

*"...and the Emberbearer shall carry the fire home, and the mountain shall drink deep..."*

That much matched the smuggled text. Then:

*"...for the ember is not given freely, nor returned alive. The vessel must break upon the resting place, that the fire may pour from the breaking. There is no other door. The bearer dies. The bearer has always died. This is the price written before the first bearer drew breath, and no craft, no love, no cunning shall unwrite it."*

Silence in the drowned hall. Water lapping at stone. Somewhere far above, wind worrying the ice on the lake.

Freya read it three times. I watched them do it — watched hope die by increments, each pass stripping another layer of maybe away. On the third reading their hand began to shake, and the lantern light shivered with it, and the ghost-letters seemed to writhe.

"No," they said.

*Freya—*

"*No.*" Louder now, cracking off the stone shelves, waking echoes in the dark water. "This is — someone wrote this. Someone *decided* this. Before me, before you, before any of it — some dead-handed priest sat in this hole and wrote *the bearer dies* like it was a line item, like it was *fine print*—" They snatched up the book and hurled it. It struck a shelf and slid into the black water, and sank, and Freya stood there heaving breath, fists clenched, tears freezing in the cold. "You knew. You *knew*, Maren. You've known since the shrine."

*I have known for a century,* I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. Ghosts are not made for shouting; we are made for the space between words. *Yes. I knew.*

"You chose me." The accusation landed like a dropped chisel. "You didn't warn me. You *selected* me. Out of every bone-carver on every road, you put your voice in my hands and led me to that collapsed shrine and let me dig the thing out of the ice, knowing — knowing *this*—" They struck the dais with the flat of their palm, once, twice, hard enough to split skin. Blood beaded black in the lantern light. "Was that all I was? A pair of living hands? A *vessel*? You needed someone who could hear the dead, so you went shopping among the living, and I — I trusted you. I carved your bones. I gave you back your *voice*. And the whole time you were walking me to my own altar."

The shard against their chest flared cold — I felt it, that deep chill it carries, the accumulated weight of everyone it holds — and the chorus rose, dozens of voices murmuring at once, and Freya tore at the bindings like they meant to rip it out and hurl it into the flood.

"Don't," I said sharply. "*Freya. Don't.*"

"Why not? What difference does it make? It's going to kill me either way! Either the Order takes it and finishes their war, or I carry it to the mountain and *break* — those are the choices? Those are the fine print?" They were crying openly now, furious at the tears themselves. "Cassian burned his whole life to defect. Rurik bled for us. And none of you — not you, not the Order, not whoever scratched these words into stone a thousand years ago — none of you thought the living deserved to *read the contract first*!"

*No,* I admitted. *We didn't. That is the oldest sin of the dead, Freya. We use the living to finish what we couldn't. Every generation of bearers was chosen by a dead predecessor who told herself she had no choice. My teacher did it to me. I swore I would never do it to anyone else — and then the Order burned my village and I died with the ember unfound, and a century later I heard a carver's knife moving through my bones, gentle as anything, and I thought: oh. Oh, there you are. Forgive me. Grief makes liars of us all.*

Freya stood in the flooded aisle, bleeding, shaking, and did not answer for a long moment. When they finally spoke, their voice had gone quiet in the dangerous way — the quiet of a blade being turned edge-up.

"I'm not doing it."

*Maren—*

"I'm not dying because a dead priest needed a plot device. If the ember ends, it ends *my* way or it doesn't end. Do you understand me?" They wiped their face with the back of their wrist, leaving a smear of blood. "Find me another door. Find me a crack in the fine print. You have until the solstice, Maren, and if there isn't one — then the prophecy can starve."

And there — in that moment, in that drowned hall full of erased truths — something in me that had been braced for a century finally unclenched. Because they were right, and because rage like theirs is not despair. Despair lies down. Rage picks up tools.

*Then we'd better start looking,* I said. *Because there's one more thing the erasers missed. It's not in the book.*

"What?"

*The ember doesn't hold fire, Freya. It holds voices. Hundreds of years of them — the dead the old gods poured into it when they fell. Which means the prophecy's authors made an assumption they never tested:* that the vessel had to break. *They assumed fire can only be released by destruction.*

Freya stared at me, blood drying on their knuckles, the shard pulsing slow and cold against their heart. Behind us, in the black water, the prophecy book settled into the silt, unread and irrelevant.

"But if someone could take the voices *out*," they said slowly, "the way I take voices out of bone—"

*Without breaking the vessel,* I agreed. *No bearer has ever tried. No bearer has ever been a carver.*

For the first time since the lake, Freya almost smiled. Almost.

We were still standing there, hatching heresy in the drowned dark, when the water above us shuddered — a long, rolling tremor, dust sifting down through my corridor of air, and the chorus in the shard went abruptly silent, listening.

Ashen drums. Far off, but closing. They had found the lake.

"They're early," Freya breathed. "Solstice is four days out."

*Then the mountain comes to us,* I said, *whether we're ready or not. Come, little carver. We have a family to warn — and I think, this time, you'll want Cassian awake when the Order arrives.*

We climbed toward the thin grey light of the surface, and behind us the archive closed its patient eye, keeping the last secret it would ever need to keep.
