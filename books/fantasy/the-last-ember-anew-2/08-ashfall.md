## 8. Ashfall

The mountain warned us before the Order did.

I had learned to read it the way Freya read bone — the way snow hissed off the high crags a half-breath before the wind changed, the way the crows lifted off the scree in silence rather than complaint. Crows that go quiet are not fleeing weather. They are fleeing men.

"Off the trail," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, the old command cadence rising in me like water into a well. "Now."

Freya looked up from the shard wrapped against their chest — they carried it swaddled like a colicky infant now, and I think none of us had remarked aloud on how natural that had become — but Rurik moved first. Of course he did. Rurik Ostrov had spent thirty years surviving things that announced themselves too late, and he did not debate. He caught Freya's elbow and hauled them both off the switchback and into the shadow of an overhang where the rock had shed its face centuries ago, and Maren — invisible to everyone but Freya, present to me only as a cold spot at the edge of hearing, a pressure like a word about to be spoken — drifted after them.

I stayed on the trail. Someone had to be seen.

Below us, around the shoulder of the pass, the Ashen Order came up the mountain the way ash falls: steadily, without malice, without hurry, certain of what they would cover. Grey cloaks. Grey faces beneath grey helms. And at their head, riding a horse he had no business riding this high, Ser Alaric Voss.

My mentor. The man who taught me which end of a blade was mercy and which was duty, back when I still believed there was a difference worth learning.

"Cassian." He did not shout. He never needed to. His voice carried the way frost carries, creeping through everything. "You're standing on the wrong side of history again. It seems to be a habit with you."

"It's not history yet," I called down. "It's four people on a mountain. You could still turn around."

"You know I can't." For one moment — one treacherous, human moment — something crossed his face that might have been grief. "Bring me the emberbearer and the shard, Cassian. Walk away. There is amnesty in this for you. There always was. The Order does not abandon its own."

*The Order does not abandon its own.* I thought of Hallowmere then, the village we burned because three of its households sheltered a defector's mother. I thought of the way the fires had sounded, how the screaming had stopped being distinguishable from the wind, how Alaric had stood beside me afterward and put his hand on my shoulder and said *this is what belonging costs* — as though belonging were something purchased, once, in blood, forever.

"No," I said. "But it sells them."

He sighed, and drew his sword, and forty grey shapes fanned out across the scree below us like spilled ink finding its level.

* * *

The fight began the way fights do when one side has planned it: badly, for us.

They did not charge. They sent arrows first, a soft whispering rain of them, pinning me behind a boulder while two squads climbed the flanks through deadfall. Rurik appeared at my shoulder — I never heard him arrive; the man moved through mountains the way rumors move through towns — with a knife in each hand and his usual expression of profound personal offense at the universe.

"They've got crossbows on the ridge," he said conversationally. "Two. Maybe three. Which means someone important wants this done quiet."

"Voss wants it done *clean*. Quiet is just what clean looks like to him."

"Mm." He turned a knife over. "There's a chute. Back of the overhang, drops into the ravine. Narrow enough that ten men can't come down it abreast. Your ghost-girl and the carver can make it if someone keeps these gentlemen interested in the front door."

"Rurik—"

"Don't," he said pleasantly, "make a speech. I'm a smuggler, Vane. Speeches cost extra, and nobody's paid me for one in my life."

I wanted to argue. I want it noted, wherever such things are written, that I wanted to argue, and that arguing with Rurik Ostrov was a sport I had gotten better at every night around the fire since Lake Verren — trading insults across the flames while Freya pretended to sharpen their tools and Maren, Freya said, laughed in her silent way, delighted by us, by the strange accidental shape of the family none of us had chosen and all of us kept choosing anyway.

But arrows were falling, and the flankers were close, and there was no time to be anything but what each of us was.

"I'll take the left squad," I said. "Draw them toward me. You get them to the chute."

"See?" He grinned, quick and crooked. "That wasn't a speech. That was logistics. You *are* learning."

Then he kissed his knife-hilts — an old Ostrov superstition, one kiss for luck and one for whoever he owed money to — and went up the rocks like smoke going the other way.

* * *

I fought the left squad among the pines, and I will not romanticize it. There is nothing lyrical about killing men whose faces you once ate beside. I knew three of them by name. Brannoc, who snored. Tey, who wrote letters home he never sent because he couldn't afford the postage. They came at me with the certainty of men who believe God is on their side, or worse, that God and the Order have an arrangement, and I broke that certainty in them one body at a time and felt nothing I will describe here except the old shame, the deep seam of it, running through me like a vein of ore.

Behind me, higher up the slope, I heard Rurik working — shouts, a scream cut short, the ring of steel on stone — keeping them interested in the front door, exactly as promised.

And then I heard the thing no one promises. A wet, punched-out sound. A body hitting rock the way bodies hit rock when they stop being people.

"RURIK!"

I killed Tey without seeing him. I climbed without feeling the climb. The chute was above me, narrow and dark, and Rurik was in it — sitting against the wall of it, actually, propped with his back to the stone as though resting, which Rurik never did, Rurik stood even to eat — and there was a crossbow bolt under his collarbone and another in his belly and blood everywhere, black in the shadow, spreading with terrible patience across the rock beneath him like ink finding its level.

Freya was already there. On their knees in the blood. Their hands hovering over the wounds, shaking, useless, hands made for bone and memory and not for this.

"You—" Freya's voice cracked apart. "You absolute *bastard*, you were supposed to—"

"Get them to the chute?" Rurik smiled. It cost him. Everything cost him now; I could see the arithmetic of it in his face, each breath a coin spent. "Did. Didn't I. Chute's right there. Go on. All downhill from here. My favorite kind."

Maren stood at the mouth of the chute, and for the first time I saw her clearly — not as cold air, not as rumor, but as a woman, young and grave-eyed, looking down at Rurik with an expression I recognized from funerals. She said something to Freya. Freya shook their head violently, twice, three times, a child refusing a truth.

"Hey." Rurik's hand found Freya's wrist. His grip was still strong. Smuggler's hands; rope and lockpick and ledger. "Hey. Bone-carver. Look at me."

Freya looked.

"You carve names." Each word came slower than the last, and he was spending them anyway, deliberately, a man paying out his purse to the very last coin. "That's your trade, isn't it? Names. So they keep." A breath, ragged. "Nobody knows my name. Not really. Rurik Ostrov — it's a mask. Wore it so long I forgot what was under. Mother called me something else. Something soft. Can't— can't find it anymore."

"Rurik—"

"Carve me anyway." His eyes were bright and feverish and absolutely clear. "Carve the mask. Carve the debts and the lies and the thirty years of running. Carve all of it. Because if you carve it—" his grip tightened, "—then it was real. Do you understand? All of it. Even the parts I hated. If it's carved, it *happened*, and I happened, and—"

His voice failed. Blood at his lips now, bright as a seal.

"—and somebody should remember me," he whispered, "who isn't a creditor."

Freya bent forward and pressed their forehead to his, the way you'd greet a brother, the way you'd say goodbye to one, and I heard them say — low, fierce, a vow with a knife's edge on it — "I'll carve you deeper than any god ever got carved. I promise you."

Rurik smiled. Really smiled, the crooked one, the one from the campfires.

"Worth every copper," he said.

And then the light went out of him between one breath and the next, quietly, without ceremony, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is spent — and the mountain wind came through the chute and touched him and found nothing left to move but cloth and hair, and Maren knelt beside him and laid her ghost-hand over his mortal one and stayed there, keeping vigil, the dead honoring the newly dead.

Above us, faintly, the fighting on the ridge stopped. Below, it hadn't. There was no time. There is never time. We covered his face with his own cloak — his good cloak, the one he'd been saving for selling something expensive to someone gullible — and we went down the chute.

I went last. I looked back once. I am not ashamed of that.

* * *

The lower pass was where Voss had saved his best for us.

He stood alone in the throat of the defile with six knights behind him, and the snow had begun to fall — fat, slow flakes, ash-colored, the mountain mourning in the only language it had. Behind me, Freya leaned against the rock wall, grey-faced, hollowed, the shard clutched against their chest. Through their coat I could feel it — feel *it*, the thing itself — pulsing. Not warm. Never warm. Cold the way a struck bell is loud: a cold that rang.

And it was speaking. I could hear it now, faintly, the way you hear surf through a closed door. A chorus. Hundreds of voices braided into one long sustained note of wanting.

*Hear us. Hear us. Hear us.*

"Ser Cassian." Voss walked forward, unhurried, sword loose in his hand. He looked past me at Freya the way a merchant looks at freight. "Last offer. It stands because I wish it to stand. Give me the ember and walk away whole. You were the finest student I ever trained. Don't make me spend you."

"Do you hear it?" I asked him.

"Hear what?"

"The ember. The shard. Listen." I stepped aside, deliberately, letting the sound reach him. "Every voice it's ever swallowed. Every person the Order burned to feed that founding lie of yours. Hallowmere. The shrine clans. The Emberbearers, century after century, led up mountains to die on schedule. They're all still in there, Alaric. Still talking. Still asking."

Something flickered across his face. Doubt, maybe. Or the place where doubt used to live before he bricked it over.

"They're not asking," he said. "They're *waiting*. There's a difference. Waiting is order. Asking is chaos. When we restore the flame to the old throne, all of it — every voice, every ghost, every unfinished grief — gets gathered into purpose. That is not cruelty, Cassian. That is *peace*."

"And who decides the purpose?"

"We do." Simple. Honest, even. That was always the horror of Alaric Voss: he told you the truth of himself plainly, and expected the plainness to be mistaken for virtue. "Someone must. Now — step aside, boy. I buried your father. I will not enjoy burying you."

"You won't bury me at all," I said, and raised my sword. "But you'll remember me. That's how it works. Somebody remembers."

He came at me, and I met him.

I will tell you honestly: he was better than me. He had been better than me for twenty years, and age had slowed his legs but not his judgment, and within a dozen exchanges he had opened my arm and my cheek and driven me back against the rock with the inevitability of a tide. But I had something he didn't. I had a family bleeding behind me, a dead man's promise ringing in my ears, and nowhere left to retreat to that wasn't *them* — and so I did not retreat. I gave ground the way you give ground in a flood: slowly, expensively, buying every inch twice.

Behind me I heard Freya cry out — not fear. Recognition. The shard had gone silent, suddenly, utterly silent, the way a room goes silent when someone finally says the true thing.

"*Oh,*" I heard Freya breathe, in a voice full of terrible understanding. "*Oh, that's what you are. That's what you've been all along.*"

And the shard began, softly, to sing.

Voss faltered — one half-second, his blade wavering as the sound reached him, hundreds upon hundreds of voices rising through the snow, and every knight in the defile froze as though the mountain itself had drawn breath —

I did not waste the half-second.

My blade stopped at his throat. His stopped at mine. Snow fell between us, settling on our shoulders like ashfall, like forgiveness neither of us knew how to ask for.

"Surrender the ember," he rasped.

"Come take it," I said, "and meet the people you'd be taking it *from*."

And down the defile, through the falling snow, the dead sang louder — and Freya Ishikawa stood in the middle of it with their eyes closed and their hands open, listening, as though hearing, at last, instructions they had been waiting their whole life to receive.
