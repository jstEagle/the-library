## 9. The Solstice Choice

The mountain opened like a mouth.

Freya had expected a door, a gate, something built by hands — but the resting place of the old gods' fire was older than building. The pass narrowed to a throat of black stone, and the stone simply *ended*, sheared away in an ancient wound that breathed cold air upward, scented with iron and snow and something beneath both: a warmth so deep it was almost memory. Maren hovered at their shoulder, her edges frayed silver in the pre-dawn dark, and even she seemed to hesitate at the threshold.

"This is where I died," the ghost said quietly. "The first time."

"First time?" Cassian's voice came tight from behind. He had not slept. None of them had, since the pass below — since Rurik's blood had gone dark on the stones and his last request had lodged in Freya's chest like a splinter of bone. *Carve my name. So I won't be forgotten.* They had buried him under a cairn of slate because there was no time and no earth, and Freya had carved nothing yet, because their hands were still shaking.

"The Order killed me here," Maren said. "And then the ember took me back — or what was left of me. I've been dying slowly for a hundred years, knight. You get used to it." Her voice cracked along its length like river ice. "You don't get used to being alone in it."

Freya stepped forward.

The shard had grown heavier with every mile. Not in weight — in *voice*. It pressed against the inside of their pack now like a crowd against a door, hundreds of murmurs layered over each other, the dead who had been folded into the fire across a century of the Order's harvesting. Every name they'd ever carved, every whisper that had come up through bone and antler and ivory, all of it braided into this one cold-burning thing. Freya had stopped sleeping partly from fear of the hunters, and partly because when they closed their eyes, the chorus sang lullabies in voices that had no lungs left to sing them.

At the bottom of the wound in the mountain lay the hearth.

It was smaller than Freya had imagined. A basin of fused glass, ancient beyond reckoning, shaped by no chisel any living smith would claim — and empty. Just emptiness, and around its rim, carved in a script older than the clans, words that Maren translated in a whisper:

*HERE THE FIRE RESTS, AND THE DEAD WITH IT.*

"They lied," Freya said.

"What?" Cassian crouched at the rim, sword drawn, watching the throat of the pass behind them.

"The book. The prophecy. *Return the ember or the war reignites.* Restore it or destroy it. Those were the only two futures it gave me." Freya knelt beside the hearth and set the pack down, and the chorus rose to a keening that made their teeth ache. "But look at it. Look at what it actually is. It's not a weapon. It's not a god's heart. It's a *prison*. Someone stuffed every dead soul the fire touched into this shard and called it holy, and then wrote a prophecy saying whoever carried it back had to die delivering it — so no one would ever live long enough to ask what was inside."

Maren's light guttered. "I knew," she said. "Part of me knew. That's why I chose you, Freya. Not because you could carry it. Because you could *hear* it."

Footsteps rang on stone behind them.

Freya turned, and the Ashen Order filled the throat of the mountain — two dozen figures in grey ash-cloaks, torches guttering in the high-altitude wind, and at their head, tall and terrible in scorched white, the Grand Igniter herself. Serah Vole. Cassian went rigid.

"You made it," Serah said, and her voice was almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "Every Emberbearer before you fought this. Screamed at the end. You walked it here like a good child. There's mercy in that, Ishikawa. Kneel at the hearth. Give us the shard, and your death will be quick, and the fire will be whole again, and the clans will have their war and we will have our purpose, as it has always been written."

"As *you* wrote it," Freya said.

Serah smiled. "Scripture writes itself, eventually."

"No." Freya stood, and pulled the shard from the pack, and held it up between both hands. Cold light bled through the cracks in it, and out of the cracks the voices spilled — audible now to everyone, filling the mountain throat, dozens of dead mouths speaking over one another. The front rank of Ashen knights faltered. Some of them flinched as if struck. Freya understood why a heartbeat later, as a woman's voice rose clear above the chorus: *"Tomas? Tomas, is that you—"* — and one of the knights made a sound like a wounded animal and dropped his torch.

"You've been feeding your ancestors' lie for a hundred years," Freya said, walking forward, slow, the shard raised like a lantern. "That the ember is divine. That returning it requires a sacrifice, so the sacrifice always falls on someone poor, someone small, someone whose bones end up in workshops like mine getting carved into trinkets for people like you. But I've heard what's inside it. All of it. And none of them are holy. They're just *dead*, and they want their names back."

"Ishikawa," Serah said, and drew her blade, white-hot along its edge, "put it down."

"No," Freya said. "I'm done putting things down. And I'm done picking them up. You gave me two fates — restore the fire and start a war, or die carrying it and make a martyr for your order to feed on for another century. Both of those futures belong to *you*. Both of them are just your founding lie wearing different clothes." Their voice did not shake. That surprised them most of all. "So I refuse. Both. I choose a third thing, and the third thing is mine."

They turned to the hearth — and instead of placing the shard in it, they drew their carving knife.

"What are you doing?" Serah's composure cracked for the first time. "That's sacrilege—"

"That's *carving*," Freya said. "It's the only thing I've ever been good at."

And they began.

The mountain's inner wall was basalt, hard as sin, and their knife was bone-handled steel, and it should have taken years. But the moment the blade touched stone, the shard flared in their other hand and poured itself down their arm — every dead voice funneling into the cut, guiding it, widening it, the dead carving through their living hands because they had waited a century for exactly this. Names came up through Freya's fingers like water finding a crack. *Maren Ostreva, who died twice and loved once.* *Rurik Ostrov, smuggler, debtor, friend.* *Tomas Bell, who only wanted to go home.* On and on, hundreds of names, the knife singing, the stone splitting open in letters that glowed faintly with borrowed fire, and Freya laughed — actually laughed — because it didn't hurt. It felt like the best work of their life flowing out of them all at once.

"Stop them!" Serah screamed. "Stop them NOW—"

Cassian moved first. He met the front rank alone, sword ringing against ash-grey blades, holding the narrow throat of the pass the way he'd held it for Rurik, shouting something Freya couldn't hear over the chorus. Maren blazed up like a bonfire of pale light and threw herself into the Order's line, and wherever she passed, knights staggered — because they *recognized* her, some of them, faces of grandmothers and great-uncles surfacing in her ghost-light, a hundred years of the Order's own stolen dead coming home to haunt them.

And in the hearth, the shard grew lighter with every name Freya carved. Lighter and warmer. By the fiftieth name it was humming. By the hundredth it was singing. And when Freya carved the final name — their own hand cramping, their own eyes streaming, the last syllable of the last forgotten soul rising out of them like a breath held for a century —

— the shard dissolved.

Not shattered. *Released.* It came apart into motes of warm gold that drifted up through the mountain's wound like sparks off a forge, and every mote was a voice saying *thank you*, and every voice was going somewhere Freya could not follow, and the hearth stood empty at last — truly empty, a hearth and nothing more, no god in it, no war in it, no prophecy in it.

Serah Vole screamed. Not in pain — in *collapse*. Freya watched the white robes sag, watched the Grand Igniter fall to her knees in the snow, because whatever the Order *was* — the fire in it, the authority, the century of certainty — had been the shard's reflected light, and now the light was gone and she was just a woman kneeling in the cold with a sword she suddenly had no right to swing. Around her, the ash-cloaks dropped their weapons one by one. Some wept. One tore off his cloak and burned it in his own torch flame, and no one stopped him.

Cassian lowered his sword. His cheek was bleeding; his hands were steady.

"It's over?" he asked.

Freya looked at the wall of names glowing softly in the dark — Rurik's among them, close to the top where the firelight reached. They put their palm flat against the stone, against the carved letters, and felt nothing but cool rock and, underneath it, very faintly, like a hearth in another room — peace.

"It was never mine to return," Freya said. "It was theirs to take back. I just held the door."

Maren stood beside the hearth, thinner now, translucent as morning fog. She was smiling. "You rewrote it," she whispered. "A hundred years of dead people's plans, and you just — rewrote it."

"Somebody had to," Freya said. "The dead weren't going to do it themselves. Too busy waiting."

The ghost's laughter was the sound ice makes when spring comes to a river. Then she began to fade — not tragically, not torn away, just *finished*, like the last page of a book closing. "I'll rest now," Maren said. "Thank you, carver. For listening."

And she was gone, gently, the way snow stops falling.

Freya stood a long time in the empty hearth-light with Cassian beside them, two survivors of a prophecy that no longer existed, and far below in the lowlands the first solstice dawn came up gold over the Thornfells — over a world with no Emberbearer in it, and no war coming, and no script left to follow.

Which meant, Freya thought, flexing their aching, ink-stained, bone-dust-crusted hands — that everything from this morning forward was unwritten.

And they had a workshop to rebuild, and a cairn of slate on the high pass that needed a proper carving, and a life that belonged to no one's plans but their own.

They picked up their knife, and started down the mountain.
