## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had a way of smoothing things. Rain fell softer here, or seemed to; the river ran without appetite; even grief, Hazel had noticed, arrived dulled at the edges, like a knife wrapped in wool. The old folk said the valley forgot, and that was a mercy. Hazel had built his whole life on being forgotten.

He was in the herb garden when the light began to go strange—gold thickening to amber, amber to something older—pulling weeds from between the rows of feverfew with his bare hands because Maren said iron tools bruised the roots of anything that meant to be medicine. His fingers were dark with soil to the second knuckle. It was honest work. It was *invisible* work, which was better.

Behind him, the cottage sat low against the hillside like something grown rather than built: turf roof going mossy at the seams, chimney breathing its thin gray thread into the evening. Smoke from Maren's hearth never smelled of ordinary wood. It smelled of juniper and burnt sugar and, faintly, of lightning. No one from the village came up the path anymore except to leave payment for cures on the flat stone by the gate, and they never stayed to talk. They remembered coming, afterward, the way you remember a dream—certain it happened, unable to say what was said.

Hazel swept the gatestone clean each morning. He kept the fences mended and the bees appeased and the cellar stocked with dried nettle and comfrey and the black honey Maren traded for in secret. He did not do magic. He was very careful, twenty-two years careful, never to do magic.

Except that magic, in the valley, could not follow. That was the whole point of the place. Whatever lived in Hazel—in his blood, behind his sternum, coiled somewhere deeper than his heart—went quiet here. It slept like a banked fire under wet ash. He had been carried across the boundary as an infant, Maren told him, and he had never once crossed back out. Twenty-two years of numbness. Twenty-two years of pretending the numbness was simply who he was: Hazel Nystrom, hedge-witch's ward, dull as ditchwater, safe as stones.

He felt it stir sometimes anyway. On nights when the aurora came down close over the northern ridge, green and restless, something behind his ribs would turn over in its sleep and press against the inside of him like a hand against a door. Those nights he lay awake counting his own heartbeats until dawn, telling himself the pressure meant nothing.

That evening, the light went strange, and then the stranger came.

Hazel heard him before he saw him—a crashing in the treeline where nothing should crash, where the pines stood so dense and old that even deer moved through them like whispers. Then a man staggered out of the shadows at the valley's edge, and Hazel understood at once that he was looking at something the valley had not smoothed. The man's coat was scorched. Not burned—*scorched*, all along one side, as if he'd been standing too near a bonfire made of something that didn't want him. His face was gray with fever, lips cracked, eyes rolling white-rimmed in sockets gone hollow. He took three steps into the garden and collapsed among the feverfew.

Hazel ran to him. Later, he would try to remember why he hadn't called for Maren first, and he would decide it was because some part of him—the part that slept—had already recognized the man, the way one flame knows another across a dark room.

"Here—here, drink." He got an arm under the stranger's shoulders and pressed his own flask to cracked lips. Water spilled down the gray beard, but some of it went in. The man's skin was hot as a hearthstone. Under the scorch marks, Hazel saw the coat had once been fine—dyed wool, silver clasps, the cut of a courtier or a courier. A man who belonged to the world beyond the ridge. A man the world beyond the ridge wanted dead.

"You're—" The stranger's hand shot up and seized Hazel's wrist with shocking strength. His eyes found Hazel's and fixed there, fever-bright, and Hazel felt something in the man's gaze *weigh* him, the way a merchant weighs coin he suspects is false and finds, to his horror, that it isn't. "You're warm," the man whispered. "Under all that ash on you, you're—gods. Gods, I found one."

"You've found a gardener," Hazel said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Inside, everything had begun to ring like a struck bell. "You need rest, and my mistress can—"

"No time." The man's other hand dove inside his coat and came out clutching a roll of leather, blackened at one end, still smelling faintly of char. He pressed it into Hazel's dirt-caked hands and folded Hazel's fingers closed over it with both of his own, trembling. "The map. Take it. Don't let them read it—don't let them *touch* it. It shows the way in. It's the only way in."

"The way into where?"

"Throne room." The word came out shredded. "There's a thing wearing the king. You understand? Wearing him. Like a glove. Like a *coat*." A shudder went through the man's whole body, and where it passed, the scorch marks spread another inch up his sleeve, and Hazel watched it happen and felt his stomach drop through the earth. "It eats remembering. It ate him. Twenty years it's worn his face and fed on everything he ever was, and now it's hungry for—"

The man stopped. His gaze dropped to Hazel's hands, folded around the map, and something like awe and something like pity crossed the ruined face together.

"It's hungry for embers," he finished softly. "And you're glowing, boy. Right through the valley's fog. Right through everything. How have they not found you?"

"I don't know what you're talking about." But Hazel's hands were shaking now, and the man felt it, and smiled—a terrible, tender smile.

"Nobody hides that well forever." He sagged back against the fence post. Sweat stood on his brow like beads of wax. "Listen. Listen. The crowned thing sends riders. Ash-bound. Men with no shadows and no fear. When they come—and they will come—they'll ask about a courier. Give them me. Say you buried me. Say anything. But the map goes nowhere they can find it, and neither do you." His grip tightened. "Swear it."

"I swear," Hazel heard himself say, and the words rang in him like the bell again, like a vow spoken over something far larger than a leather roll in a garden.

The stranger exhaled, long and rattling, and some of the terrible heat went out of him. "Good lad. Good. There's a name on the seal—you'll know it when—no. No, she'll know it. Show her. She'll tell you to burn it." His mouth twisted. "Don't. Whatever she says—don't burn it. Burn *anything* else. Burn the cottage first." He laughed, which became coughing, which became silence for a moment too long. When he spoke again his voice had gone thin as paper. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry to be the one who makes you real."

Then his eyes rolled back, and he slid down the fence post, and Hazel knelt in the trampled feverfew holding a scorched map and a dying man's weight, listening to the evening come down over the valley like a lid.

* * *

Maren found them there. Of course she did; Maren always knew. She came down the path with her shawl pulled tight and her staff in hand, and Hazel watched her take in the scene—the stranger, the scorch marks, the map—and watched seventy years of practiced calm hold, barely, like a dam holding a single wave.

"Inside," she said. "Both of you. Now."

They got the stranger onto the cot by the hearth. Maren worked over him with tinctures and murmured words that were not quite prayers, and the fever broke and returned and broke again, and through all of it the man slept like someone running in a dream. And Hazel sat on the bench by the door with the map in his lap, not opening it, feeling it against his thighs like a sleeping animal. Warm. It was warm. Leather shouldn't be warm.

Near midnight, Maren came and stood over him. She held out her hand.

"Hazel."

He gave her the map. He watched her unroll it a hand's width, no more, just enough to see the corner—to see the seal pressed into the vellum there, a mark in faded gold wax. He had never seen his guardian's face go white before. He had seen her set broken bones and draw splinters from fevers and stare down a wolf at the goat pen without raising her voice. He had never seen her afraid.

She rolled it shut so fast it cracked.

"Where did he touch you?" she demanded. "Did he bleed on you? Did he speak your name?"

"He gave it to me. He said—" Hazel stopped. The stranger had told him not to listen to her. The stranger had told him to lie. Twenty-two years of lying, and here was the strangest moment yet: the first time he'd ever considered lying to *her*. "He said to burn it."

Something flickered in Maren's eyes—relief, and beneath the relief, grief.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Burn it. Tonight. In the north hearth, where the smoke goes out over the ridge. And Hazel—" she gripped his shoulder, and her hand was not steady, and that frightened him more than the ash-bound men the stranger had named, more than the crowned thing, more than everything. "Whatever he told you, whatever you think you saw in yourself tonight, you bury it deeper. You are a gardener. You are nobody. That has kept you alive every day of your life, and it will keep you alive tomorrow. Do you understand me?"

He understood her. He understood that she loved him, in her flint-and-ash way, and that love and lies had grown together in her like roots in a wall.

"Yes," he said.

But that night, alone in his loft under the eaves, Hazel did not feed the map to the north hearth. He unwound the leather by candlelight instead, and the ink shifted under his eyes—not smearing, *moving*, roads rearranging themselves like veins deciding their course—and at the center of the vellum, drawn in gold that seemed to pulse very faintly, very slowly, like a heartbeat, like his heartbeat, was a tower. And beneath the tower, in a spidery antique hand, two words:

*The ember wakes.*

Downstairs, far off down the valley road, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then hooves—many hooves, moving fast, making no sound a shadow should make.

Hazel blew out the candle and held the map against his chest, and for the first time in his life, he was terrified of being seen—and thrilled, deep in the sleeping coal of him, to be seen at all.
