## 2. Ash on the Road

The riders came at dusk, and they did not trouble to hide.

Hazel saw them first as a smudge against the western sky, where the sun was drowning in its own color—three shapes on the ridge road, moving with the wrong stillness, the way smoke moves when it has decided to be a horse. The valley had taught him every ordinary thing: the creak of a cart, the whistle of a shepherd, the particular silence of a fox considering a henhouse. These were none of them. These were absence wearing the shape of pursuit.

He was on the witch's roof, mending thatch he did not need to mend, and his hands went still before his mind caught up. Below him, through the open door, came the smell of rosemary and hot iron—the witch was working a warding brew, and she only worked iron into her brews when she was afraid.

*Burn it,* she had said, three nights ago, standing over the scorched map with her knuckles white around her broom handle. *Whatever seal that is, whatever name it carries, burn it and let the valley forget you ever held it.* But Hazel had not burned it. He had told himself he would. He had sat with it in his lap by candlelight, turning it over, feeling the strange warmth of the vellum like a bird's breastbone, and each night he had said *tomorrow*, and each morning the map had been there, patient as a debt.

Now the debt was riding down the ridge road toward him.

"Hazel." The witch's voice, low, from the doorway. She had seen them too. Of course she had; she saw everything in this valley except the one thing she refused to see in him. "Back door. The goat track. Don't stop until the water."

"The water's the boundary," he said stupidly. "Magic can't cross the water."

"I know what it is." Her face was unreadable in the way of old stone, but her hands, hanging at her sides, were trembling. Twenty years she had kept him. Fed him, schooled him in dullness, drilled invisibility into him deeper than any lesson of letters. And now her mouth worked around something she could not say, and finally all she managed was: "You were never meant to die in this valley, boy. I only hoped you'd live long enough elsewhere."

She pressed a bundle into his arms—bread, a knife, a cloak that smelled of camphor and decades—and then she shoved him, hard, toward the back door, and the last Hazel saw of her was her silhouette framed against the hearthlight, straightening her spine, going out to meet the riders as though they were tax collectors and not the end of everything he knew.

* * *

The goat track was steep and dark and smelled of crushed juniper, and Hazel ran it with the map burning cold against his chest, wrapped now in oilcloth inside his shirt. Behind him, faintly, he heard voices at the cottage—not shouting, nothing so human as shouting. A sound like ash settling. Like a fire being asked a question and answering wrongly.

He did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people who intended to return.

The river announced itself before he saw it: a widening hush, a change in the air's taste, green and mineral. Then the trees broke open and there it was, the Greywater, broad and slow and silvered with the last light, and beyond it the land he had never seen—meadow rising into hills, hills into mountains with snow still clinging to their shoulders like old men reluctant to work.

Hazel stood at the bank, chest heaving, and understood that the moment had come that twenty years of careful smallness had been built to avoid.

The valley behind him was quiet. Magic could not follow across the water—that was the whole reason the witch had raised him here, the whole architecture of his hidden life. Whatever he was, whatever gift he refused to name, it stopped at this river like a dog at an invisible fence. He had always imagined crossing would feel like leaving a limb behind.

It felt like the opposite.

The moment his boots left the far bank—two strides into the shallows, water biting cold at his ankles—it came back. Not gradually. All at once, like blood flooding into a limb that had slept for years, so fierce and sudden that he cried out and dropped to his knees in the river. Every nerve lit. The world roared open. He could feel the current under the water, feel the trout turning in the pools, feel the slow patient hunger of the mountains drinking snowmelt, feel—God help him—feel the map against his chest *waking*, the ink shifting beneath the oilcloth like something stretching after a long sleep.

This was what he had starved himself of. This was the nameless thing. It poured into him and knew him, and worse, it *recognized* him, the way fire recognizes a wick.

He crawled out onto the far bank and lay in the wet grass, shaking, laughing, weeping, all three at once and none of them winning. Above him the first stars were coming out over foreign country, and for the first time in his life Hazel Nystrom felt entirely, terrifyingly awake.

* * *

The map led him east along the river for two days, and the ink moved.

That was the part he could not have explained to anyone in the valley. The roads on the vellum redrew themselves as he walked, faint silver lines crawling like frost forming in reverse, and when he took a wrong fork the correct path would darken, patient, insistent, until he obeyed. By the second day he had stopped fighting it. There was a strange comfort in being led—a life with instructions, after twenty years of instructions designed only to keep him invisible.

The town appeared on the third evening, folded into a bend of the river where the water slowed and deepened: low stone houses, lanterns already lit, and above every door a sign he did not understand until he understood it all at once—sigils, glyphs, stitched sigils of thread and hammered tin and painted wood, dozens of them, a whole town wearing its magic openly like embroidery.

A bell rang as he crossed the bridge. Not an alarm—a greeting, a single clear note that seemed to taste him as it passed through, and Hazel felt it pass through him too, felt it find the ember-heat in his chest and hum against it like a struck glass.

They were waiting for him at the near end of the main street. Not soldiers. Old people, mostly, and young people with old eyes—spellwrights, he would learn, exiled from three different courts, keeping their craft alive in exile the way embers are carried in a covered pot. A woman stepped forward, grey-braided, with ink stains up both forearms like a scribe's sleeves rolled past the wrist permanently.

"You crossed the Greywater alone," she said. Not a question. "With that on you."

"On me?"

"Under your shirt." Her eyes were kind and appalled in equal measure. "We felt you coming for half a day. Like hearing someone hum a song you thought was lost." She paused. "What family sends a child down the river untaught?"

"No family sent me."

"Then whose are you?"

And because he was tired, and fed on bread and honesty, and because no one in twenty years had ever asked him the question as though the answer mattered, Hazel said: "Nystrom. My mother was Nystrom. I think. I don't remember her."

The street went very quiet. Lanterns guttered, all of them at once, as if the town itself had drawn breath. The grey-braided woman put her hand over her mouth, and an old man behind her whispered a word in a language Hazel didn't know but felt in his teeth, a word that sounded like *ember* and like *heir* and like *oh no*.

"Nystrom," the woman repeated carefully. "Child, do you know what that name was, before the crown made it contraband?"

"No," Hazel said. "But I'm starting to be afraid I'm about to find out."

She looked at him a long moment—at the map-shaped bulge beneath his shirt, at the exhaustion and the stubbornness and the fear—and then she stepped aside and gestured up the street, where warm windows glowed and doors stood open in a way no door in the valley ever had.

"Then come in out of the road, Hazel Nystrom," she said. "And bar the shutters well tonight. Because if you crossed the Greywater, then the ash-bound crossed it too—and things that wear dead men's shapes don't stop at rivers."

Far off, downstream, where the water bent away into the dark, Hazel thought he saw the stars go out—one, then another, then another, like footprints of something walking toward them through the night.
