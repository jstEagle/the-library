## 3. The Hollow Crown

The capital announced itself by smell before sight: coal smoke and hot iron, tallow and crushed lilies, and beneath it all a faint sweetness like sugar left too long over a flame. Hazel had never imagined so many chimneys, so many lives stacked one atop another, each window a small square of gold against the bruised evening sky. In the valley, night simply fell, whole and undivided.

Here it fell in pieces.

He moved with the crowd pouring through the Gate of Bells, his hood up, the map folded small against his ribs where it had grown warm again—warmer every mile, as though it knew what waited at its end. Tomorrow was the Renewal, when the crown would be set upon the king's head before ten thousand witnesses, and the bells would ring until their throats cracked. Tonight the city rehearsed its joy: garlands going up, ovens roaring, children chalk-drawing crowns on cobbles that rain would erase by morning.

*He is not your king,* the stranger had rasped, dying into Hazel's arms at the treeline. *The thing that wears him is hungry, boy. And you—* The hand had tightened then, scorched paper pressed to scorched palm. *You are what it fears.*

Hazel had spent twenty years learning to be nothing. Unremarkable. A valley boy with no gift, no name worth speaking twice. He had been so good at it that even the hedge-witch's scrying pools showed him only as a smudge, a blur where a person should be.

But the map did not show him as nothing. The map pulled at him like a hook behind the breastbone.

And now, standing in a capital full of strangers, he felt eyes on him anyway—the particular attention of ash.

***

The spirit woke in its borrowed body and stretched fingers that remembered being smoke.

It had worn this king for eleven years now, and the flesh still fit poorly, like a coat tailored for another man. That was the trouble with stolen vessels: they resisted. Every morning the king's face fought to arrange itself into grief, into horror, into the expression he had worn when they first poured the ember down his throat—and every morning the spirit smoothed him flat again, spending precious heat to do it.

There were compensations. There was always the feeding.

Below the throne room, in the vaulted dark the court called the Treasury of Years, the spirit descended stairs that had not existed when the masons drew them. Its footsteps made no sound. Fire makes no sound unless it chooses to.

The shelves rose around it, floor to unseen ceiling: jars of smoked glass, thousands of them, each holding a curl of light that might have been mistaken for a firefly if one were kind, or a soul if one were honest. Memories. The realm's memories, harvested gently or brutally according to need—the harvesters had never been able to tell the difference, which the spirit found quietly funny.

It selected tonight's ration with the care of a sommelier. Not the king's own; those were nearly spent, burned down to wick. No—it chose from the newer stock, a jar labeled in harvester's script: *A woman, river-town, spinning thread. Age thirty. Remembers her mother teaching her the pattern.*

The spirit uncorked it and drank.

For three heartbeats it was a woman at a wheel, lamplight on her hands, her mother's voice saying *over, under, draw it home*—and oh, the sweetness of it, the terrible borrowed sweetness of a life that had loved someone once.

Then it faded, as everything faded, and the spirit stood alone among its shelves in a body that was not its own, hungering already.

Somewhere far away, it sensed a flicker it could not name. An ember it almost recognized. The spirit turned its stolen face toward the north and felt, for the first time in eleven years, something very much like fear.

***

Hazel learned the shape of the palace from a laundress.

She was one of the spellwrights' people—river-town folk who'd recognized his surname Nystrom and gone quiet in the way people go quiet around a lit fuse. They fed him, clothed him, taught him the servants' corridors, and asked no questions except one, asked by an old woman with ink-stained knuckles: "Your mother. Was she called Wren?"

"I don't know my mother," Hazel had said.

"Then she kept you safe," the woman said, "the only way left."

Now Hazel carried linen through the west galleries with his head bowed and his heart hammering out a rhythm the map answered beat for beat. Down. Everything led down. The map's ink had rearranged itself overnight into a single spiraling line ending at a word that hadn't been there before:

*Wren.*

He followed it past guards who looked through him—his old talent, his invisibility, still holding, though it cost more each day here where magic ran thick as weather. Past doors of black oak carved with flames that seemed to move when unobserved. Down stairs that grew warmer with every step, though no forge burned below.

At the bottom, the corridor opened into a hall lined with shelves, and the shelves were lined with glass, and the glass was filled with light.

Hazel stopped breathing.

Thousands of jars. Thousands of curls of glow, each one pulsing faintly, patient as eggs. And one shelf—one jar—burned different. Red-gold instead of pale silver, fierce instead of tame, and when Hazel came within arm's reach of it, the ember inside flared so hard the glass sang.

The warmth hit him like recognition. Like a hand laid on his cheek. Like a voice he had no memory of but his body knew, some chord struck in him before thought began: *my son.*

"Wren," Hazel whispered, and the jar blazed.

Behind him, soft as ash settling, someone said: "Well. The harvest comes home on its own feet after all."

***

They took the map first. Then his knife, his coins, the spellwright's token sewn into his collar. Six of them, ash-bound, their skin grey as old coals, their eyes banked fires behind shutters. They did not bind his hands. They walked him up, and up, and up, to a throne room hung with banners and empty of everyone but the king.

The king sat slumped on a throne of black iron, and he was flickering.

That was the only word Hazel could find for it. One moment the man's face was regal, whole, bearded; the next it guttered like a candle in wind and showed something beneath—young, terrified, mouth shaping a scream that eleven years had worn smooth. The crown upon his head glowed dull orange, and Hazel understood with sick certainty that it was not decoration. It was a lid.

"So." The voice came from the king's throat but used it carelessly, the way a tenant uses a rented room. The ember-spirit leaned forward, and its eyes were the exact color of the jar below. "Wren's whelp. She hid you in a dead valley and died of the hiding. Sentimental woman. It bought her nothing—here you are."

"What are you?" Hazel asked. His voice shook. He let it shake. Fear was honest; fear might buy him a moment.

"I am what keeps this kingdom warm," the spirit said. "Literally. Their little Renewal rite tomorrow isn't pageantry, boy—it's refueling. Ten thousand faithful pouring their love into a vessel, and I skim the cream off the top. Your mother's line guarded the last true ember. Her flame can unmake me. So I have spent eleven years making sure her flame was scattered, jarred, forgotten." It smiled with the king's handsome mouth. "And yet. Here you stand, glowing like a signal fire. Do you know how loud you are? You've been audible to me since the river-town."

Hazel's knees wanted to fold. He made them hold. *You are what it fears,* the dead stranger had said, and the stranger had died believing it, and belief like that was a kind of inheritance too.

"You're afraid of me," Hazel said.

"I am *hungry*," the spirit snapped, and for one instant the king's face vanished entirely and something vast looked out—a furnace seen down a long dark throat. Then it mastered itself. "I will consume you slowly, after the Renewal. You'll keep. But first—" the stolen eyes narrowed—"first we will discuss how you found the vault. Who helped you. Names, boy, or I start feeding early."

And that was when the throne spoke.

Not the spirit. The *king*—a rasp from somewhere deeper than the throat, a voice worn to threads, surfacing like a drowning man breaking water for one gasp. His flickering face turned toward Hazel with an effort that visibly cost him, eyes finding the boy's with desperate clarity.

"You carry her flame," the king whispered. "I can feel it. Gods—I can *feel* it, like spring through a prison wall." His gaze flicked to the crown's glow, to the guards, back. "Listen. Listen quickly. It must feed at dawn or begin starving—it will be weakest at the moment of Renewal, when all its strength flows outward through the crown. That is when you strike. That is when—"

"*Silence*," the spirit snarled, seizing its vessel, and the king's face slammed shut like a door.

But the words were in Hazel now, lodged under his ribs beside the map's pull and his mother's blazing jar: *dawn, weakest, strike.*

The spirit studied him a long moment, head tilted, fire banking itself down to embers of suspicion. Then, softly, almost kindly: "Chain him below. Next to the shelves. Let him spend his last night listening to everything he's about to lose."

As the ash-bound took his arms, the spirit paused, and added, almost as an afterthought:

"And bring me the red-gold jar. If the whelp won't tell me who helped him, perhaps his mother's memory will prove... persuasive."

Hazel was dragged toward the dark, toward the shelves of stolen light, and he did not fight them. He let himself go limp, let them think him broken, because his heart had gone suddenly, terrifyingly clear.

Tomorrow at dawn, the whole kingdom would watch a coronation.

And Hazel Nystrom—who had spent his life learning to be invisible, untalented, nothing—would give them something to look at.

He only had to survive the night, and decide how much of himself he was willing to burn.
