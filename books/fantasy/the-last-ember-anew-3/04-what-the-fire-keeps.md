## 4. What the Fire Keeps

The throne room smelled of cold ash and old honey.

Hazel stood at the foot of the dais with his wrists still raw from the ash-binders' cords, and he looked up at the thing wearing the king's face, and he understood at last why the valley had been built the way it was—why magic could not follow past its borders, why the hedge-witch had kept him there like a coal banked in a hearth of earth. He was not hiding from the world. The world had been hiding him.

"You came all this way," the ember-spirit said, and it wore King Aldous's voice the way a glove wears a hand. It sat slumped on the throne, magnificent and wrong, its crown flickering at the edges like paper catching. "To give me one more memory. One more flame to keep me warm through another century."

"I came to take them back."

The spirit smiled, and behind the smile Hazel saw the hunger—the vast, patient appetite that had eaten a kingdom's remembering one mouthful at a time. "They are not yours to take, little heir. Your mother burned her flame into the old king's keeping. I have held it since before you drew breath. And what is held long enough becomes owned."

Hazel reached into his chest—not with his hands but with the thing beneath his hands, the gift he had spent twenty-two years refusing to name—and found the ember where it had always waited. Small. Stubborn. A seed of heat he had mistaken for ordinary warmth, for the flush of the stove and the sting of scalded fingers and the strange comfort of standing near the cottage fire on winter nights when Maren thought he slept.

It woke at his attention like a bird unclenching its feet from a branch.

The throne room changed. Every candle leaned toward him. The banners along the walls stirred though no wind moved, and the ash-bound servants at the doors took one step back, then another, their gray faces cracking with something that might have been fear.

"Sit," the spirit said, and its voice frayed at the edges. "You do not know what you carry. You do not know what it will cost."

"I know exactly what it will cost." Hazel began to climb the dais steps. His legs shook. He let them. "Someone told me. Someone who is still in there."

* * *

He had heard the king three nights ago, in the dark of his cell beneath the palace—a voice like a guttering lamp, thin and flickering and stubbornly alive. *The spirit feeds on memory,* Aldous had whispered through the stone. *That is how it holds my shape. Give it yours freely and it will gorge itself. Gorged, it forgets to hold me shut. That is the only door, boy. You must let it eat you.*

"And you?" Hazel had asked the darkness.

A long silence. Then, almost gently: *I have been eaten for thirty years. There is very little left of me to lose. But what remains is mine, and I would like it back before I go.*

Now Hazel knelt beside the throne, close enough to see that the king's face was a mask over emptiness, close enough to feel the heat pouring off the crown like breath off an open oven door. He cupped his hands around the ember in his chest and did the thing he had never done, the thing every ward and every lie and every careful talentless day had been built to prevent.

He opened.

The fire went up through him like dawn through a valley. It did not hurt, or it hurt the way birth hurts, the way grief hurts—too large to be called pain. Light poured out of his skin. The shadows of the throne room fled to the corners and crouched there, and the ash-bound servants fell to their knees in the doorway, and the spirit rose from the throne with its borrowed face streaming like smoke.

"No," it said.

"Yes," said Hazel, and offered himself.

* * *

The first thing it took was the smell of bread.

Not the memory whole—just the smell, rising off Maren's kitchen table on a morning when rain ticked against the shutters and the dough had risen high as his fists. Hazel felt it lift out of him gently, the way a leaf lifts off still water, and he watched it go into the light of the spirit's open mouth and become nothing.

Then the rest came faster.

Maren's hands, cracked and clever, teaching him which mushrooms lied. The treeline in autumn, gold going to rust. The fevered stranger collapsing against him, the map's scorched edge biting his palm. The river-town lanterns strung across black water. The spellwrights saying his surname like a key turning in a lock: *Nystrom. Nystrom, we thought the line was ended.* The first time he crossed the boundary and felt magic flood back into him like blood into a numb limb, so fierce and sweet he had fallen to his knees in the road and laughed until he wept.

Each memory left a shape behind it, a hollow pressed into him like a footprint in snow. He had expected agony. What he had not expected was the tenderness of it—that losing should feel so much like being read aloud to, like being sung to sleep.

*Let them go,* the king had said. *What the fire keeps, it keeps forever. Nothing is lost. Only yours.*

The cottage went. The garden rows. The particular gray-green of the valley sky before snow. The witch's voice calling him in at dusk—*Hazel! Wash your hands!*—and then, later, quieter, the night she recognized the seal on the scorched map and went white as tallow and told him to burn it, burn it now, and looked at him with such naked terror-love that he understood she had always known what he was.

He let that one go last, and hardest, and he held onto it a moment longer than the others, the way you hold a doorframe before stepping into deep water.

Then he let it go too.

* * *

When it was over, Hazel lay on the cold stones of the dais and did not know where he was.

He knew facts, the way a man knows the words of a song whose tune has gone: that he had crossed a valley border; that he had a name; that the name meant something. But the meaning itself was a room with the furniture removed. He felt strangely light. He felt like a letter after the message has been read.

Above him, the spirit was screaming.

It had gorged itself past holding—thirty years of stolen kingdom plus one heir's entire childhood, crammed into a shape that had only ever been a mask—and the mask was failing. The king's face ran like wax. The crown flared white, then blue, then the color of a coal at the exact moment before it dies. Through the blaze Hazel saw the other thing inside it, the true thing, small and ravenous and suddenly, catastrophically full.

"Too much," it whispered, in a voice that was no one's. "Too much, too much—"

And it guttered. Like a candle in a doorway. Like a breath at the end of a long story. The light folded inward, and inward, and was gone, and the crown rang once against the floor like a dropped bell.

In the sudden dark, a man sat up on the throne.

He was old. He was thin as winter. His eyes were clouded and then clearing, clearing, like ice melting off a window, and when they found Hazel they were wet.

"Thirty years," King Aldous said. His voice was ruinous and real. "I dreamed I was a hearth with no fire in it. And then—" He pressed a hand to his hollow chest. "Warmth. Boy. Whose warmth was that?"

Hazel opened his mouth and found he did not know the answer. Somewhere in him, a valley was missing. He knew it the way you know a tooth has gone—by the space it left, by the tongue returning again and again to the gap.

"I don't remember," he said, and discovered he was crying, and discovered the crying did not hurt. "But I think it was given gladly."

* * *

They planted the hedge in spring.

Hazel chose the spot himself: along the western wall of the palace grounds, where the afternoon sun came low and gold through the new leaves of the city trees. The gardeners thought it an odd request—a hedge, of all things, common hawthorn and blackthorn, the kind farmers set to mark a boundary—but the king had granted him anything he asked, and this was what he asked.

He knelt in the turned earth with the saplings arrayed beside him and dug with his bare hands, and when the roots were settled he laid his palms flat against the soil and let a little warmth pass out of him, just a thread of it, just enough. Not magic, exactly. Or not only. The kind of blessing any person might put into a growing thing if they loved it enough and were patient.

The hedge would grow thick. In a few years it would be tall enough to walk behind, to disappear into, and children would dare each other to crawl through it, and someone—some fevered stranger, some runaway, some child carrying a secret too heavy to name—would find shelter in its green interior without ever knowing who had planted it or why.

Hazel sat back on his heels and wiped the earth from his hands and looked at the row of small trees, each one a promise of shade he would not live to sit in, and felt the hollow where the valley had been, and found that it did not ache. It was simply a room awaiting furniture. He would fill it himself, year by year, with things he chose.

"Hazel!" someone called from the wall—Tamsin, the young spellwright, come to fetch him for supper, because he belonged to people now, chosen ones, not inherited ones. "Are you coming, or are you going to talk to those sticks all evening?"

"Coming," he called back.

But he stayed a moment longer, one hand resting on the smallest sapling, feeling its thin pulse of life against his palm. Somewhere far away, in a valley he could not picture, a woman he could not remember might still be tending a cottage fire. If she was, he hoped it was warm. He hoped the bread smelled good.

He stood, brushed off his knees, and walked toward the sound of his own name—light as ash, rooted as hawthorn, belonging at last to no place but himself.
