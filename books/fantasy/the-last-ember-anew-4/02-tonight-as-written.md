## 2. Tonight, As Written

The returned ledgers always smelled of other people's decisions.

Jude Ashgrove had learned to read that smell the way other men read weather. This one — wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with the Guild of Impossible Things' triple knot, and delivered by a courier who had not blinked once during the entire handoff — smelled of dust and iron and something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a locked room.

Prophecy ledger. Category Seven. Returned from private hands after ninety-one years.

He set it on his desk beside the candle and did not open it immediately. That was procedure. Procedure was the spine of his life, and he clung to it the way drowning men were said to cling to driftwood, except that Jude sometimes suspected he was not so much drowning as simply built without a floor.

First: the intake form. He filled it out in his small, exact hand. *One (1) ledger, prophetic class, provenance: House Merrow, deceased. Condition upon return: unassessed. Appraiser: J. Ashgrove, Keeper of Small Debts.*

Keeper of Small Debts. It was the humblest title in the guild and the only one he'd been permitted to hold. Other appraisers handled years of life, traded by the decade like bolts of cloth. Others handled names — whole identities, bought and resold, worn by strangers until they fit. Jude handled the residue. The griefs sold twice. The river's name currently pawned upriver, which was why the maps had started calling it *the water*. The small, sad, unsellable remainder of human transactions.

He was good at it. He suspected this was because there was a hole in him shaped exactly like the work — a hollow where something should have been, and no record anywhere of what had been kept there.

His own file, which he had read eleven times over nine years, was thin as a hymnal. *Jude Ashgrove. Exiled. Covenant-breaking, particulars withheld per settlement terms. Memory of offense sold to the Guild for survival consideration. Sold by: self.*

*Sold by: self.* Three words, and behind them, nothing. Not darkness, exactly. Darkness would have been something. It was more like reaching into your own pocket and finding the pocket sewn shut, and knowing — the way you know your own hands — that you had been the one holding the needle.

He lit a second candle. Prophecy ledgers required two; everyone knew that, though nobody remembered why, and the guild's own manual on the subject consisted of a single line: *Two candles, lest it read you in the dark.*

Jude had always assumed that was a joke. Guild humor ran dry and strange.

He cut the triple knot, unwrapped the oilcloth, and opened the cover.

* * *

The first hundred pages were ordinary prophecy — which is to say, they were mad, but mad in the familiar grammar. Predictions of kings with too many shadows. A famine that would arrive by sea, wearing the shape of a wedding feast. Rain over the Ashfall Marches on a date forty years past, confirmed accurate, initialed by three separate appraisers across two generations.

Jude worked through them the way he worked through everything: line by line, entry by entry, verifying dates against the master calendar, checking for tampering, noting marginalia. A child had scribbled a horse on page sixty-three sometime around the reign of the second Merrow. He logged it. Even vandalism was data.

It was on page one hundred and twelve that the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. All at once, mid-page, the cramped merchant's script gave way to something older and stranger — letters that leaned like grass in wind, ink gone brown with age but somehow still wet-looking at the edges. Jude sat back. Checked the candles. Both burning steady.

He read the new hand's first full entry:

*In the year the ember gutters, a keeper of small debts will balance a book that balances him.*

He frowned. Coincidence. Prophecy loved coincidence the way rivers loved downhill — it was simply what they did. He made a note in the margin: *generic phrasing; "keeper" common title; see cross-reference appendix D.*

Then he read the next line.

*He will find tonight written here, and reading it, will already be inside it.*

Jude's pen stopped.

The candle flames did not flicker. That was the wrong detail to notice first, he would think later — not the words themselves, not yet, but the terrible steadiness of the light, as if the room had stopped breathing around him and the fire alone refused to pretend otherwise.

He looked at the date heading the entry.

He read it twice. Three times. Then he pulled the master calendar toward him with a hand that felt borrowed from someone else, and checked, because checking was what he did, checking was the floor he didn't have, checking was —

Tonight's date. Exactly. To the hour.

* * *

There was a procedure for this. Of course there was. Article Nine of the appraisal code: *Any ledger containing verifiable present-tense entries shall be flagged, quarantined, and reported to the Guildmaster within the hour.* He even had the flag ribbon in the third drawer, red, used twice in his entire tenure.

He reached for the drawer.

And the ink moved.

Not smeared. Not bled. *Moved* — the way a sleeper moves, a slow shifting under blankets, the brown letters rearranging their weight on the page as though settling in for a long stay. Jude watched the word *tonight* darken, deepen, go glossy and black as a beetle's back. Watched the sentence beneath it grow a clause it had not held a moment before:

*He will find tonight written here, and reading it, will already be inside it — and he will reach for the red ribbon, and hesitate, and read on.*

His hand hung in the air above the drawer handle.

Hesitating.

"Oh," Jude said quietly, to the empty room, to the candles, to whatever was writing him. "Oh, that's cheating."

He should close the book. Every instinct of nine years' careful trade screamed it — you do not keep reading an instrument that is reading you back; that is the first law of every market in impossible things, older than the guild, older than its ledgers. You do not look too long into inventory that looks into you.

But the next line had already formed, and it was in a different hand now. Not the old leaning script. His own. His small, exact, unmistakable hand, the one that balanced widows' griefs and pawned rivers, crawling onto the page letter by letter as though some invisible Jude were transcribing him from somewhere just over his shoulder:

*Entry: one (1) keeper of small debts, acquired this hour by means prophetic. Provenance: himself. Condition upon acquisition: unassessed.*

"That's my format," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "That's my — you're using my intake form."

The ledger did not answer. Ledgers never answered. They only recorded, and the recording went on, patient as interest accruing:

*He arrives at the threshold as written.*

*What threshold,* Jude almost wrote in the margin, and then understood, with a vertigo so complete it was less like falling than like discovering he had never been standing on anything at all — the question was already answered, three lines down, in ink that hadn't finished drying because it hadn't finished happening.

*The Ember Court's. Ruined. At winter's turn minus forty-one days.*

*He arrives afraid. The fear becomes purpose. The purpose becomes the whole rest of the book.*

Below that, the page went dark — not blank, dark, a spreading pool of black that swallowed the remaining lines like deep water swallowing stones. And in the middle of the darkness, one last line surfaced, pale as bone:

*He was written here before he was born. The exile was the writing.*

Jude Ashgrove, who had spent nine years cataloging other people's impossible trades, who had priced grief by the ounce and years by the season, who had read his own file eleven times looking for the shape of the thing sewn shut inside him — Jude put both hands flat on the desk to stop the world from tilting, and found that the desk was tilting anyway, or he was, or the hour was, because time had begun to stutter like a candle in a draft.

The clock on the mantel struck the hour. Then struck it again. Then began striking hours it had no business striking — midnight, then dawn, then midnight again, all in the space of six chimes.

The candle flames stood perfectly still.

The seam opened in the air above the desk — a thinness, a place where the room had been mended badly, glowing at its edges like heat off a forge.

Jude's last coherent thought, before the hour seized him like a creditor seizing collateral, was absurdly professional. He thought: *unlogged acquisition. I'll have to make an entry.*

Then the candle went out, and the seam opened wide, and the ledger went with him — clutched to his chest like the one document in the world that had ever known his name.

Behind him, on the abandoned desk, the red ribbon lay untouched in its drawer.

And on page one hundred and thirteen, in a hand that was learning, even now, to be his, fresh ink dried over the final line of the entry:

*Received in good order.*
