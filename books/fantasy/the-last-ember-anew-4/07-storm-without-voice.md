## 7. Storm Without Voice

The road out of the toll-wards climbed into country that had forgotten how to be gentle. Jude felt it in his teeth before he saw it — a pressure like a held breath, the air thick with something that wanted to move and couldn't remember how.

"Storm country," Sera said, not looking up from the map she'd bought with three years of her own childhood — a fair price, she'd said, for those particular years. "Used to be storm-caller territory. Before."

"Before what?"

She gave him the look she'd been giving him since the threshold, the one that said *you know exactly what I mean, traitor*, and said nothing.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shelf as the light went copper and then bruise-dark. Jude built the fire while Sera checked the wards she'd scratched into the dirt around their bedrolls — ember-warden work, quick and ugly, but functional. He'd watched her do it three nights running now and was beginning to read the grammar of it: *keep out* written four different ways, each one more desperate than the last.

"You're tense," she said.

"I'm always tense."

"You're *more* tense." She nodded east, where the horizon had begun to churn. "That's not weather. That's weather with an opinion."

Jude looked. The clouds were wrong. He'd spent a century among ledgers, but he'd grown up under clan skies, and some knowledge lived below memory, in the marrow where the guild's knives couldn't reach. These clouds moved without pattern. They wheeled and stalled and wheeled again, like birds circling a carcass, or like men searching a battlefield for bodies they'd already buried.

"They're looking for something," he heard himself say.

"They're looking for *us*. Hollow winds." Sera fed another line into the ward-ring. "When the covenant split, every storm lost its caller. A hundred years of gales with no hands on them. They don't think anymore. They just *want*."

"Want what?"

"Everything they used to have. Voices. Names. Someone to answer to." She glanced at him sideways, and there was something new in her face — not hostility, exactly. Appraisal. "The old stories say hollow winds can smell a caller's blood even when the caller can't remember being one."

The first wind hit at dusk.

It came down off the ridge without warning, a fist of cold air that scattered their fire into orange ghosts and tore one of the wards apart like wet paper. Sera shouted something in warden-cant and drove her palm against the ground; a second ward flared blue-white and held, barely. The wind broke against it and recoiled — and then, horribly, it *circled*. Came back. Pressed again, patient now, testing the way water tests a dam.

"That shouldn't happen," Sera breathed. "Wards don't read twice. It's learning—"

"It's remembering," Jude said. His mouth had gone dry. "Or trying to."

The second wind arrived with the dark, and the third behind it, and then the whole sky came down the mountain at once.

There was no strategy to surviving it. There was no time for one. The hollow winds fell on the camp like a mob falling on a house — shrieking through the granite shelf, shredding Sera's wards one by one, each collapse sounding less like magic failing and more like a voice giving out. Jude was thrown flat. Grit filled his mouth. Above him the storm massed, a single vast attention, and he felt it *looking* at him the way the prophecy ledger had looked at him two nights ago: recognition without mercy, the sense of being already known.

And something in him rose to meet it.

He didn't decide it. Later, turning the moment over the way he turned over entries that refused to balance, he would swear there had been no decision at all — only reflex, deep as breathing, older than exile. His hand came up. His fingers spread. And the words left him in the storm-tongue he had no right to know:

"*Veth-karai. Veth-karai, sov enna.*"

*Be still. Be still, and listen.*

The mountain held its breath.

Every wind stopped at once. Not slowed — *stopped*, mid-shriek, the whole roaring weight of the sky hanging above them like a curtain frozen mid-fall. In the sudden silence Jude could hear his own heart, and Sera's ragged breathing, and beneath both, faint as a pulse through a wall: the winds themselves, listening.

They were listening to *him*.

"*Sov enna,*" he said again, softer, and felt the truth of the language settle into his chest like a key settling into a lock it had been cut for. His hand knew these shapes. His throat knew these sounds. Whatever the guild had taken from him — whatever he had sold to survive — it had not taken this. This lived too deep to pawn.

The nearest wind drifted close, and up close it wasn't a wind at all. It was a absence shaped like weather, a storm-shaped hole full of reaching. And inside the reaching he felt — clearly, unbearably — its want. Not to destroy. To *answer*. To be called by name by someone whose name meant something back. A hundred years of howling at a world that had stopped speaking its language, and here, impossibly, was a voice.

His voice.

"*Enna karai,*" Jude whispered — *listen to me* — and the wind shivered with something that might have been joy.

For one long minute he stood in the center of the storm like a man standing in the center of his own forgotten life, and it was *magnificent*. Power moved through him without friction, without cost, the way it must have moved through him once, every day, before. He could feel every gust within ten miles as a thread in his hand. He could feel the pressure systems stacking up over the eastern peaks and knew, without knowing how he knew, that if he spoke the right word they would break gently, rain on the dry farms below instead of drowning them. He could feel the whole wounded sky waiting for instruction.

This was what he had been. This was what exile had amputated. Exhilaration went through him like lightning going to ground—

—and then curdled, all at once, into a question he couldn't put down.

If the gift still lived in him, whole and obedient, then it had lived in him a century ago too. On the night the covenant broke. On the night his clansmen had cast him out with a mark burned into his hand and a hole burned into his head. He had stood under some sky like this one, with this same power pooled in his palms, and done *something* with it. Something so terrible that the Ember Court itself had accepted his exile as payment. Something so terrible that his own memory had been worth more than his life.

The winds were still waiting, still trusting, and he was standing in the wreckage of their trust with a traitor's hands.

"*Go,*" he said hoarsely, in trade-tongue, because he couldn't bear to give them anything more beautiful in the storm-tongue. "Go east. Go to the high passes. Leave us be."

The winds obeyed. Of course they obeyed. That was the worst of it — the perfect, instant obedience, the way the whole storm turned as one creature and poured away over the ridge like water finding its level. Even stripped of their callers, even hollowed and mad with wanting, they answered him *completely*. As if he were owed that. As if somewhere in the missing hour of his life he had earned it, or worse — *spent* it.

The silence after was enormous.

Sera was sitting up in the ruins of the camp, staring at him. Her wards lay dead around her, scorched circles in the dirt. Her face had gone through fear and come out the other side into something colder and more careful.

"A century," she said quietly. "A century of storms with no master, and you quiet them in a minute. With the old cant. With the *high* cant, the kind only hearth-born callers ever learned."

"I don't—" Jude started, and found his voice shaking. "I don't know how I know that language."

"No," Sera agreed. "You don't. That's rather the point, isn't it?" She got to her feet slowly, brushing ash from her coat, and looked at him the way an auditor looks at a ledger that finally won't balance. "My order taught me that the traitor Ashgrove broke the covenant from the inside. That he used his calling to do it — that no ordinary betrayal could have split the realms, only a storm-caller's word spoken at the First Hearth itself." She stepped closer. Her eyes dropped to the mark on his hand, then rose to his face. "I told myself you were a fraud wearing a dead man's name. But the winds don't lie about blood, Jude. And neither did that."

He looked down at his own hand. It was trembling. Not from the power — from the memory of the power, the sweetness of it, how naturally his fingers had shaped the high cant, how *right* it had felt to command. A man didn't forget his way into fluency. Fluency meant practice. Practice meant years. Years meant that the thing he'd done at the hearth hadn't been a stumble or an accident.

It had been done by someone skilled. Someone trusted. Someone the storms had loved.

Someone like the man standing in this circle of dead wards, who had just quieted a hundred years of grief with six words he couldn't remember learning.

"The road ahead," Sera said at last, turning away to salvage what she could of the fire, "runs along the Griefline — through towns that live on resold sorrow. We'll pass through tomorrow. After tonight, I believe you'll find it instructive." She paused, her back to him. "But Jude. If we reach the First Hearth, and you touch it, and your hands remember what they did there —"

"Then I'll know."

"Then we'll *all* know." She coaxed a flame alight, and the small warmth of it looked fragile as a secret. "Get some sleep, storm-caller. The sky's given us a reprieve. I doubt the road will."

Jude lay awake long after her breathing steadied, watching the clean stars where the storm should have been. Somewhere east, the winds huddled in the high passes, obedient, orphaned, waiting for a voice that had promised them nothing and delivered everything.

He flexed his marked hand in the dark and tried to imagine it doing murder.

He couldn't. That was the horror — not that he could picture the crime, but that he couldn't. His hands felt like good hands. They had felt like good hands for a century of honest arithmetic. And yet somewhere in the gap between the exile date and the breaking of the covenant — a gap he was beginning to understand was not a gap at all but a locked door — those same hands had done something the world had never forgiven.

*Tomorrow,* he thought. *Griefline. And after that, the archive at the wayhouse. Ledgers don't lie, even when memories do.*

Above the eastern ridgeline, far off, a single hollow wind had broken from the others and circled back — not to attack, but to hover at the edge of the firelight, patient as a dog outside a door, keeping watch over the man who had spoken its language.

Watching him sleep.

Waiting, perhaps, to see what his hands would do next.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, and with it, far to the south, a rider on the road behind them — a figure in guild-gray who made no effort to hide, moving at exactly the pace of someone who did not need to catch up.

Because she already knew where they were going.

Because she had always known.

In her saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth beside a ledger bound in ember-red leather, Guildmaster Corvess carried the deed to a memory she had purchased a century ago and had never once needed to open —

until now.
