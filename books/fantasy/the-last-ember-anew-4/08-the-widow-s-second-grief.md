## 8. The Widow's Second Grief

The town announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and lye, the particular sourness of a place that had been crying for a long time.

Sera smelled it from the ridge road and stopped walking. Jude, three paces ahead, turned back. The wind pulled at his coat, and she watched his hand drift toward the storm-mark on his palm — that unconscious gesture he made whenever weather moved, as if checking a wound.

"Griefhaven," she said. "Or what the maps call it. The locals have another name."

"What do they call it?"

"Nothing. They stopped naming things here. Naming costs."

They came down into the town at dusk. It was built wrong, Sera thought, in the way of places rebuilt too many times on their own foundations. Houses leaned against each other like mourners holding one another up. The streets were clean — obsessively, terribly clean, swept every hour by people who needed something to do with their hands. And everywhere, on every doorframe and lintel and fence post, hung small cloth bundles. She knew what was inside them without looking. Grief, bundled and dried and ready for market. A whole town's sorrow, hanging like herbs.

Jude stopped at the first bundle. He touched it gently, the way he'd touched the ledgers back at the guild house — that same reverent, accountant's care. "These are tagged."

"Everything here is tagged." She nodded toward a door where an old woman sat shelling peas with mechanical patience, her eyes fixed on nothing. "That's Maren Ostwick. She sold her grief twice."

"Twice?" His voice caught on the word. "I process those trades. I know the forms. You can't sell the same grief twice. The second sale requires—"

"Requires you to grieve something new so there's fresh stock." Sera kept her tone flat, ledger-flat, because if she let anything else into it she'd never get through this town. "Her husband died when she was forty. She sold the mourning. Then her son drowned ten years later, and the guild paid a premium because her capacity was proven. Then her daughter left, and she sold that parting-grief to cover the winter. Now when anything happens to her — anything at all — the brokers come through like crows. They call it harvesting season." She looked at Maren's empty eyes, her busy hands. "She doesn't cry anymore. She can't afford to. Crying is waste product."

Jude stood very still. In the lamplight his face had gone the color of paper.

"I've seen these entries," he said finally. "Hundreds of them. Columns of figures. *Widow, grief, second sale, fair value.*" He swallowed. "I never once wondered what fair value meant to the person selling."

"That's not cruelty, Ashgrove. That's training."

"It should be both."

Something in her chest shifted at that — a small, unwelcome movement, like furniture dragged across a floor she'd thought was empty. She started walking again before he could see it land.

* * *

The inn was called the Full Measure, which Sera found either grimly honest or obscene, depending on the hour. The common room was warm but quiet in the way rooms are quiet when everyone in them has sold something essential and is listening for the echo.

The innkeeper took their coin without counting it — a bad sign; people who don't count money anymore have stopped believing in transactions — and gave them soup and bread and a room upstairs. Then, as Sera signed the guest book, the woman's gaze snagged on Jude's hand resting beside hers.

"You'll want the back room," the innkeeper said quietly. "Not the front-facing one."

Sera frowned. "Why?"

"Because the harvesters come at night." The woman said it without drama, the way you report weather. "They don't take from marked folk — guild property, even exiled — but they'll sit outside your door all night, and the ones facing the street wake up feeling... thinned. Like someone read them. The back room's got old wards. My grandmother put them up when the trade first came through. Back when we still fought."

"When did you stop fighting?" Sera asked.

The innkeeper considered the question honestly, which Sera respected. "When fighting started costing more than selling," she said, and went back to her taproom.

* * *

The back room had one bed, one chair, one window shuttered against the dark. Sera took the chair. Jude took the floor without being asked, and something about the unasked-ness of it annoyed her more than the arrangement itself would have.

She didn't sleep. She lay in the dark cataloguing the sounds of Griefhaven: the sweep of brooms on stone at midnight, the soft wet sound of someone weeping behind a wall who clearly believed no one could hear, and beneath everything, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the hum of the ember-road running south toward the First Hearth. It had gotten louder since the wayhouse. Louder since *him*, though she hadn't told him that yet either.

Near dawn, footsteps stopped outside their door. Not harvesters. Too deliberate. A knock, two knuckles, patient.

Sera was up with a knife before the second knock finished. Jude stirred but didn't rise; she noticed he trusted her to handle doors now, and filed that away among the other small terrifying accumulations of trust between them.

The woman in the hallway was old, wrapped in grey wool, carrying a lamp. Sera recognized her distantly — a face from the guild's own records, which meant a face from a hundred transaction slips. Widow Ostwick. Maren. Except up close, in lamplight, her eyes weren't empty after all. They were full — overfull, brimming, held back by sheer muscular discipline.

"You're going to the Hearth," the old woman said. Not a question.

"We're traveling south," Sera said carefully.

"The marked one is going to the Hearth." Maren's gaze went past her to Jude, sitting up on the floor, the storm-mark visible at his wrist. "I watched the prophecy-ledgers come through here sixty years ago, when I still worked the weighing-house. There was an entry about a man who'd carry the last light home. Everyone laughed. Weighing-house women don't laugh anymore, but we did then." She set the lamp down. "I'm not here about him. I'm here about you, warden."

Sera's grip tightened on the knife. "You know me?"

"I know your sister."

The room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with drafts.

* * *

They sat in the taproom after, the three of them, while the innkeeper pretended furiously to polish glasses that were already clean. Maren wrapped her hands around a cup of tea she didn't drink.

"She came through eleven years ago," the old woman said. "Young ember-warden, disgraced, half-starved. Selling whatever the brokers would take. They bought her memories in lots — small ones, cheap ones, the way you'd sell off a dead man's tools. Her tenth year. Her mother's voice. The name of her favorite hill." Maren's mouth twisted. "And then her face."

"My sister's face," Sera said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Yes."

"You say it like a price quote."

"Isn't it?"

"No." Maren leaned forward. "Child, I weighed grief for forty years. I know the difference between a thing sold and a thing surrendered. Your sister didn't surrender that memory. She held onto it until holding it was the only thing keeping her upright, and then she traded it — deliberately, specifically — to buy you passage out of the wardens' judgment. I checked the entry myself. The buyer's fee was routed to a warden's tribunal fine. Yours."

Sera had known the shape of this for eleven years. She had carried it folded up small, the way you carry a knife you're afraid to look at directly. But hearing it spoken aloud, in a town where grief hung on every door like laundry, cracked the fold open.

"I told myself she sold it because she hated me," Sera said. "That was easier. That was a story I could survive. Disgraced warden, traitor sister, cut ties, move on." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eleven years I've been angry at a woman who can't remember why she sacrificed for me. Do you understand how insane that is? I've been grieving a person who is still alive, walking around somewhere with my face in her head and no idea what it means."

"She knows it means love," Maren said simply. "Even sold, some things keep their meaning. It's the price tag that falls off, not the worth."

Jude hadn't spoken. When Sera finally looked at him, he was watching her with an expression she couldn't name — recognition, maybe. Or envy. At least she knew what she'd lost. At least her loss had a name and a seller and a story.

"You want to ask," she said to him.

He shook his head slowly. "I was going to say — I'm sorry. And that I've spent years balancing books where trades like your sister's were line items. *Memory, familial, one unit, fair value.*" His hands curled closed around the storm-mark. "Fair value. I hate that phrase. I've written it ten thousand times and I think tonight is the first time I've heard it scream."

Outside, dawn was coming up grey over the bundled doorframes. Somewhere down the street, a broom scraped stone, sweeping and sweeping and sweeping at nothing.

Maren rose, joints cracking. At the doorway she paused. "One more thing, since you're going south anyway. The hearth-road has tolls — you'll have found that. Warded gates, old covenant magic, hungry. They take payment in kind." She looked at Sera with terrible gentleness. "Payment in memory. So before you go offering pieces of yourself to those gates, girl, ask yourself what your sister's example is worth. Ask yourself what's left of you at the end of the road if you spend yourself buying passage."

"And what's the alternative?" Sera asked. "We walk around?"

"There is no around." The old woman smiled, and it was the saddest thing Sera had seen in a town full of sad things. "There's only what you pay, and what you keep. Choose like someone's watching. Someone always is."

She left. The lamp-light faded down the hall. Sera sat with her cold tea and her hot shame, and across the table Jude Ashgrove — exile, blank spot, the man whose missing memory had started all of this — reached over and, awkwardly, briefly, covered her hand with his.

Neither of them said anything. There wasn't a form for it. No column in any ledger.

By midmorning they were on the south road, and the first toll-gate was already visible on the horizon: an archway of black iron humming with hunger, waiting to be paid.

Sera rolled up her sleeve as she walked, and began deciding what she could live without.
