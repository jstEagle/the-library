## 12. What I Did That Night

The ember let him touch it.

That was the first wrong thing. Sera had warned him — *the wards will take your hand off at the wrist if the hearth doesn't know you* — but when Jude reached out across the stone basin, past the heat-shimmer and the last hundred years of ash, the fire leaned toward his fingers like a dog hearing its name.

It wasn't warm. That was the second wrong thing. It was cold in the way a wound is cold, in the way a room is cold after everyone has left it. The last ember of the covenant between the mortal realm and the Ember Court sat in its cradle of blackened granite no bigger than a robin's egg, and it was dying, and it knew him.

"Don't," Sera said behind him. Not a command. A plea with her hand already halfway to her sword hilt, which meant she didn't trust herself either. "Jude. Don't touch it until we know what—"

"I think," Jude said, and heard his own voice come out flat and strange, "it already knows what."

He touched it.

* * *

The world went white, then went back.

Not to the hearth chamber. To a night he did not recognize and recognized completely: rain hammering the eaves of a house on the edge of Ashfall Moor, the kind of house that existed because someone had once been paid to forget it was there. He stood in the doorway of it. His hands were younger. His knuckles were whole. There was a storm overhead with his name tangled in it, because that was what storm-callers did — they braided their names into the wind so the wind would answer when called, and every caller could feel their own braid like a second heartbeat.

The house was on fire.

No. Not yet. But it was going to be, and he knew that with the terrible certainty of a man who had already lived this night and paid for living it. The rain fell and did nothing. That was the first detail his mind snagged on like a nail: rain that would not put out a fire, because the fire wasn't a mortal fire. It burned upward from somewhere beneath the floorboards, orange-white and patient, the color of the Ember Court's own halls.

"Get out of the way." A voice made of cinders and courtesy. They came through the wall without troubling the wall — three figures in gray-and-gold, faces like coins left too long in a fire, and the middle one held an open ledger, and the ledger's pages were blank, and it wanted filling.

"The tithe is due," said the coin-faced one. "A mortal life, freely given, or the covenant closes and everything your storms have ever watered goes dry. Those are terms your grandmothers agreed to, storm-caller. You've known since childhood."

"I've known," young Jude said, and his voice cracked on it, twenty years old and already carrying a century's arithmetic. Behind him, upstairs, a child was screaming. Not in pain. In the particular terror of a child who can smell smoke and hears adults arguing about something worse than smoke.

*Whose child?*

The question rose in present-Jude like a bubble through deep water, and the memory didn't answer, and the not-answering felt deliberate — a door held shut by hands on the other side. His hands, maybe. The hands of the man who'd sold this night away.

"Then give us a life," the coin-face said. "Yours, if you're feeling generous. Or hers." A gesture toward the stairs. Casual. Bookkeeping.

"No," said young Jude.

"You don't get to refuse a tithe, Ashgrove. You only get to choose who pays it."

And here was the thing that made present-Jude's breath stop in the hearth chamber while his body stood in a burning doorway a century ago: he watched himself — younger, terrified, soaked to the bone in rain that wouldn't help anyone — watch himself *think*. He saw the thought move through his own face like weather. He saw the moment the idea arrived, and he saw that it was not a good idea. It was not a plan. It was a gamble dressed up as a sacrifice, made by a boy with a storm braided into his name and maybe ninety seconds before the Ember Court's patience ran out.

"There's another currency," young Jude said.

The coin-faces went still. Even the fire under the floorboards seemed to lean in.

"Memory," he said. "You trade in it. Your guild does. Your court does. I've seen the ledgers — don't look surprised, half my clan owes you money. A life is a bundle of memories wearing meat. So take the bundle. Take all of it. Every year, every face, every reason. Leave the meat walking. That's a fair price for a mortal life, and you know it's fair, because it's worth more than a life and you'll profit on the exchange."

Silence. Rain. The child upstairs had stopped screaming and started coughing, small and wet, and that sound went through Jude like a blade going through paper — easy, total, irreversible.

"That's not how the covenant works," said the middle coin-face, but the ledger in its arms had begun to flutter, pages turning themselves, hungry.

"Rewrite it," said young Jude. "You wrote it the first time. Write it again. Split it — keep the ember burning on my payment instead of a death. You're traders. Trade."

And the coin-faces looked at each other, and present-Jude, watching from inside his own skull across a hundred years, understood with a horror that had tenderness growing through it like grass through a grave: *they were tempted.* Not by mercy. By margin. A whole life of memory was worth more than one brief mortal span, and these creatures could no more resist arbitrage than the tide could resist the moon.

"It will break the covenant as written," the first coin-face warned. "The bond between realms runs through the tithe. Reroute it and the line frays. Frayed lines fail. When the ember gutters — and it will gutter, boy, everything burns down to its last coal eventually — there will be no clean renewal. Only reforging. And reforging requires fuel of its own."

"Fine," said young Jude, and the word came out steady, and present-Jude hated him a little for how steady, and loved him more. "When it gutters, I'll pay for that too. Put it in the book. All of it. Everything I am, everything I remember, against the debt — and the girl walks out of this house alive."

"*The girl,*" the middle coin-face repeated, savoring it, opening the ledger to a fresh page. "Say her name for the record."

And here the memory flinched. Here the whole vision bucked like a horse refusing a jump, and present-Jude felt the hearth's heat against his palm and Sera shouting his name from very far away and none of it mattered, because the memory was closing around the name like a fist, hiding it from him even as it showed him everything else—

But he saw enough. He saw a small figure carried down through smoke by a man who moved like the roof beams were already falling and didn't care. He saw a face smudged with soot, eyes squeezed shut, fists full of his coat. He saw himself press that child against his shoulder where the storm-braid beat under his own pulse, and say — into hair that smelled of woodsmoke and milk —

*Hold on to me. Nothing gets past me tonight.*

And the child held on. And the coin-faces wrote. And the covenant split with a sound like ice cracking on a lake at spring thaw, a sound that every warden in the Ember Court heard at once, a sound that ended the age of clean tithes and began the slow hundred-year guttering of the last ember — and young Jude stood in the doorway of a burning house holding a rescued child and felt, for exactly one heartbeat, triumphant.

Then they took the payment.

Present-Jude had sold memories before. Everyone at the Guild had, or bought them, or brokered them; it was work, it was commerce, it was other people's lives moving through his hands like coin. He had imagined — insofar as he'd let himself imagine — that losing his own would feel like the descriptions clients gave: a smoothness, a gap, a missing tooth the tongue worries at.

It did not feel like that.

It felt like being unmade in order. The rain went first — he felt the storm-braid tear out of his name like a stitch pulled from cloth, and with it went every hour he'd ever spent learning the sky. Then the moor, the clan-halls, his mother's voice singing the ledger-songs. Then smaller things, crueler things: the taste of honey bread at some festival whose name dissolved as he reached for it. The scar on his knee and the fall that made it. A friend's laugh. A friend's face. A friend's *name*, gone, gone, gone. He stood in the burning doorway holding the child and forgot the child in his arms, forgot why he was standing there, forgot the fire, forgot the fear, forgot the bargain, and the last thing the memory showed him — the last thing, before the whiteness took it all — was his own face going calm and empty as a slate wiped clean, and the coin-faces closing the ledger with a sound like a coffin lid.

*Paid in full,* one of them said, almost gently. *Go and live, whoever you turn out to be.*

* * *

Jude came back to himself on the hearth-chamber floor with his palm blistered and Sera kneeling over him with her sword drawn and tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face, though whether the tears were for him or for what she'd seen happening behind his eyes, he couldn't tell. Wardens could see a touched memory from outside, sometimes. He hoped not. He hoped to every god whose name hadn't been traded away that she hadn't watched him forget a child.

"Ashgrove." Her voice was wrecked. "Your eyes — they went ember-orange, the wards lit up like dawn, the whole hearth *sang* — what did it show you?"

He lay on the cold stone and stared at the dying flame above him, small now, guttering, a hundred years of rerouted debt burning down to its final hours. Winter's turn was close. He could feel it in the ember's stutter, a pulse skipping beats.

He remembered everything now.

He remembered the rain that wouldn't help. The coin-faces and their fluttering ledger. The child on his shoulder. The choice, made in seconds, that had cost him a century of not knowing why the world called him traitor. He had broken the covenant deliberately. He had split it open with his own two hands and his own one wild gamble, to buy a few minutes of a little girl's life, and he would do it again, and that was the horror and the tenderness tangled so tightly together he couldn't tell them apart: he had damned the realms to save one person, and saving one person might be the only thing that made the realms worth damning for.

Except.

His hand closed slowly over the blistered palm. Because there was a hole in the returned memory, and the hole was shaped like a name. Everything else had come back — the bargain, the fire, the cost, even the exact weight of the child against his chest. But the Ember Court had taken the payment, and the payment included her, and somewhere in the ledger of the guild that owned his past there was a single entry still sealed: the name of the girl he'd saved.

Sera was watching him. "What did you do?" she asked quietly. "That night. A hundred years ago. What actually happened?"

Jude sat up. Above them the last ember flickered, and flickered, and held.

"She lived," he said. "I need to find out who she is."

Because the ember wasn't just showing him the past. As his vision cleared he could see it plain now, woven into the hearth-light like a watermark: the reforging coming, the fuel it demanded, and — written in the old covenant-hand along the basin's rim, in letters that hadn't been legible until tonight — a clause he had personally dictated a century ago, with a child crying into his coat and no time to read what he was signing.

*The debt renews at winter's turn. Payment: a whole remembered life, freely given.*

His whole remembered life.

Now that he finally had it back.
