## 14. Reforging Requires Fuel

The hearth had spoken once, and Sera wished to God it hadn't.

*Equivalent payment,* it said — not in words, because the First Hearth was older than words, older than the Ember Court itself. It said it the way a wound says *ouch*, the way a dying fire says *wood*. The meaning arrived inside her ribs before it arrived in her mind: **a whole remembered life, freely given, or no covenant.**

Not a fragment. Not a toll's worth of childhood or a pawned year of grief. A life. Every face, every name, every scar, surrendered whole and burning, until nothing of who you were remained but the warmth you'd bought for everyone else.

Sera stood at the hearthstone with her hand still raised from where she'd touched it, and the ember beneath the ash pulsed like something starving.

"Absolutely not," Jude said.

She turned. He was standing back from the hearth, storm-mark dark on his palm, his ledger-keeper's face already doing arithmetic he didn't want to show her.

"It wasn't asking me," Sera said.

"It doesn't matter what it was asking." He came forward two steps, stopped, as if the heat itself were a wall between them. "You don't get to volunteer."

"You did."

"That was different."

"How?" She heard her own voice crack on it, hated that it cracked. "How exactly is that different, Jude? You sold your memory to survive. I've been selling pieces of mine since before you were exiled. We are both people made of installments. The only difference tonight is which one of us gets to be noble about it."

The ember flared. Not angry — *listening*. That was somehow worse. The First Hearth had been listening to them since they walked through the wards, and everything they said here became part of some account she couldn't read.

Jude crouched by the hearthstone, careful, deliberate, the way he balanced books. "There has to be another way. Corvess took the key-stone, the ember's failing faster now, but there's always an entry error somewhere. There's always—"

"There isn't." She said it flatly, because kindness would only make him argue longer. "I was a warden, Jude. I trained under the last woman who studied this place before the Court sealed it. Equivalent payment means equivalent. The covenant was forged on a mortal life given whole. It can only be reforged the same way. That's why your memory almost worked — it was *almost* whole. Almost isn't enough anymore."

He looked up at her, and the candlelight of the dying ember moved across his face, and she saw him arrive at the same conclusion she'd arrived at ten minutes ago, alone, while he'd been checking the wards.

"No," he said again.

"Yes."

"Sera—"

"Somebody has to," she said. "That's the whole truth of it. Somebody has to, and I have been preparing for this since the day they stripped my warden's sash and told me I was nothing. Do you know what I own, Jude Ashgrove? I own a sister's face I sold to eat. I own a rank I disgraced. I own a decade of toll-road fragments and a confession I gave you on a road built out of other people's grief. My life is *already collateral*. It's the one thing in this world that's already priced."

"That's not—" His voice caught. "That is not how I see it."

"Then how do you see it?"

And there it was. The question hanging in the hot dark of the ruined threshold, with the ember guttering below them like a pulse counting down, and Sera realized too late that she had cornered him the way she used to corner smugglers at the Court's gates — no exit left, nowhere honest to go except through.

Jude stood. Slowly. The way a man stands when he has decided to stop being careful.

"I see a woman," he said, "who bartered her past away piece by piece so that other people wouldn't have to do worse, and who thinks that makes her cheap. I see someone who confessed the worst trade of her life on a cold road because she thought I deserved honesty even when it cost her. I see—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I spent a hundred years hollow and I didn't know what the shape of the missing thing was until I met you. Now I know. It's you-shaped. And you're standing there offering to pour yourself into a fire."

Sera's eyes were burning. She blamed the hearth smoke. She would blame the hearth smoke for the rest of her life if she got one.

"That's not an argument," she managed. "That's a confession."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Confessions are arguments. They just come in a different format." He stepped closer, over the old ward-lines that should have burned him and didn't, and took her hand — the one still warm from touching the stone. "You want to know why I won't let you? Because I finally remember what it was like to give something up whole. I gave my memory to save a child, and it was right, and it was terrible, and I have spent a century feeling the hole it left without knowing its edges. I will not stand here and watch you choose that. Not when I'm the reason the payment's due. This debt is mine. It was always mine."

"The debt is the *covenant's*," she said. "And the covenant doesn't care whose heart it spends. That's the whole horror of it. That's what Corvess understood and built an empire on. Warmth wants fuel. It doesn't ask whose."

"Then we find fuel that isn't a person."

"With what time?" She gestured at the ember, and as if in answer it sank, dimmed, threw shadows up the ruined pillars like fingers grasping. "Winter turns in three days. Corvess has the key-stone. The hollow winds are circling the mountain looking for a door home. There is no clever entry, Jude. I've read every book in the wayhouse archive twice. There is one payment and two of us and—" her voice failed and she forced it back, "—and I am faster than you. I've decided. I touched the stone first. The hearth knows. It's been waiting for me."

"It's been waiting for whoever would say yes," Jude said. "It's a hearth, not a judge. And I am telling you—" and now his voice did break, fully, and he didn't try to hide it, "—I am telling you that if you burn, I will spend whatever's left of me making sure it was for nothing. I'll let the ember die. I'll let winter win. Because a world warmed by you isn't a world I want to live in either, Sera. You want to save everyone. Fine. But I only need to save one person tonight, and I found her on a road of paid tolls, and I am *not done knowing her.*"

The silence after that was very long, and very hot, and full of the small sound of an ember deciding things.

Sera pulled her hand free — gently, because she meant no cruelty by it — and turned to face the hearth directly.

"Then we have a problem," she said softly, "because I'm not done knowing you either. And one of us is lying about being able to live with the loss."

"I'm not lying."

"Everyone lies at the threshold of a fire." She thought of the child in his restored memory — the one he'd saved, the reason for everything — and something in her chest went quiet and certain. "You lied to yourself for a hundred years. I lied to my sister's ghost every night I survived without her face. We are both experts. Don't pretend you can't recognize the craft."

Behind them, at the far end of the threshold hall, stone scraped against stone.

They both turned.

Corvess stood in the shattered doorway, the key-stone cradled in both arms like a sleeping infant, and behind her the dark was moving — hollow winds pouring through the breach like smoke with intent, coiling along the floor toward the hearth's fading light. Her coat was scorched. Her composure was not.

"Well," Guildmaster Corvess said pleasantly. "Don't stop on my account. I do so love watching assets negotiate their own depreciation."

"You're supposed to be sealing the pass," Sera said, hand going to her blade.

"I was. Then I realized I didn't need to seal it. I needed witnesses." Corvess walked forward, unhurried, the winds parting around her like courtiers. "The hearth requires a whole remembered life. I hold the key-stone, which means I hold the terms. And I've come to make you both a final offer — one last trade, guild-standard rates, no tricks." Her eyes settled on Jude, and there was something underneath the cold ambition that Sera recognized, reluctantly, as fear. "Give me the memory, Jude. All of it, freely surrendered, and I'll let the ember die clean. No battle. No blood. The covenant ends, scarcity begins, and I become the richest woman in a world that needs warmth badly enough to pay anything. Everyone lives. Isn't that what heroes want? Everyone living?"

"And everyone cold," Sera said.

"Cold is survivable," Corvess said. "I've checked the ledgers."

Jude didn't look at her. He was looking at the ember — at the tiny, stubborn gold of it, down to its last breaths — and Sera watched his expression change into something she'd only seen once before, on a road at night, when the hollow winds came and he called them home.

"No deal," he said quietly.

"A pity." Corvess set the key-stone down on the flagstones with a click that echoed like a lock turning. "Then I suppose we do this the expensive way." She stepped back, and the winds rose around her like a tide. "Warden. Ledger-keeper. Last chance to walk away."

Sera drew her blade and put herself between the hearth and the dark.

"Jude," she said, without turning, without taking her eyes off Corvess. "Whatever you're about to do — do it fast."

Behind her, she heard him kneel at the hearthstone, and place both hands on the ancient ash, and begin to speak to it in a language older than ledgers.

The ember answered.
