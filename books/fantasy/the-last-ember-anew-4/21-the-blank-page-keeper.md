## 21. The Blank Page Keeper

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of his thumb, and Jude Ashgrove did not remember lighting it.

That was not unusual. That was, in fact, the whole shape of his life now — a series of small absences arranged neatly around a desk. He knew he had lit it. The wax told him so; the hour told him so; the faint smell of tallow and the singed wick told him so. He simply could not *remember* it, the way a man remembers reaching for a match, the small decision of flame against wick. There was a hole where that moment should have been, and the hole was shaped exactly like everything else.

He had learned to work in the hole.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts on the third floor of a building that leaned slightly east, as if perpetually listening for something. Jude's desk sat beneath the one window that faced the harbor, and every night he balanced the ledgers by candlelight: griefs sold twice, years pawned against bad harvests, a river's name still sitting in escrow after eleven months because no one in the valley could agree what to call the water anymore. He was good at this work. He had been good at it before, he supposed, though "before" was a country whose borders he could no longer find on any map he owned.

His hand moved down the column of figures. Steady. Certain. A hand that knew what it was doing even when the man attached to it did not.

*Entry 4,412. One memory, minor (the color of a specific door). Sold by a cooper of Vell Street to cover a debt of honor. Condition on return: excellent.*

Jude paused over the entry. He always paused over the memories. Years and names were arithmetic; you could weigh them, price them, watch them appreciate or rot like any other commodity. But memories were stranger stock. They came back changed sometimes, or they came back unchanged while the seller had changed around them, so that a man would buy back his own wedding day and find it fit worse than it had when he sold it, like a coat tailored for someone who used to be him.

He wondered — not for the first time, and with the particular flatness of a question asked ten thousand times — what his own memory would fetch at market. What condition it would be listed in. Whether there was an entry somewhere in these very books with his name on it, some trade he'd made in a year he couldn't account for, a sale so old the ink had gone brown.

He had looked. Of course he had looked. In his first weeks back at the guild — *back*, as if he'd ever been anywhere else, though Sera Vane insisted otherwise with a look on her face he had never learned to read — he had pulled every ledger on the shelves and run his finger down every index. *Ashgrove, Jude.* Nothing. Not a purchase, not a pawn, not a lien. It was as if the guild had never traded in him at all.

Which was impossible. Everything was possible here; that was rather the point of the institution. But *this* impossibility had a different texture than the others. The widow's twice-sold grief had a paper trail. The river's name had receipts. His own absence had nothing — no entry, no margin note, not even the ghost of a correction. Just clean paper where a man should have been.

He had asked Corvess about it once. The guildmaster had been diminished since the winter — everyone said so, though no one said why; there had been some trouble at the First Hearth, people whispered, some crisis among the ember-folk, and afterward the guildmaster walked slower and signed things she used to delegate — and she had looked at him across her desk for a long moment with an expression he could only describe as *accounting*. As if she were tallying something about him and the sum troubled her.

"Some pages," she had said finally, "are left blank on purpose."

"And mine?"

"Yours," she said, "is paid in full."

She had turned back to her correspondence then, and Jude had understood, in the wordless way he understood most things about himself, that the conversation was over and would never be reopened.

So he worked. He balanced the books. He cataloged the impossible trades of a city that sold its own past to get through the winter, and every night he went home to a room above a chandler's shop where the bed was made on the left side only, because he had apparently decided at some point that the right side belonged to someone, and he could not bring himself to unmake that decision even though he could not remember making it.

Tonight the ledger lay open to a fresh page, and tonight — as every night — the page bothered him.

It wasn't the entries. The entries were fine. It was the blank space beneath them, the white remainder at the bottom of the folio, waiting. Most clerks hated blank space; it meant unfinished work. Jude had come to feel something else about it, something he had no better word for than *recognition*. Blank pages didn't frighten him. They felt like home. And that frightened him, a little, because a man ought to be made of entries — ought to be a sum of recorded days — and instead he was mostly margin.

He dipped his pen. Wrote: *Entry 4,413.*

And stopped.

Because his hand had written something else first — a single stroke, a small curling mark at the top of the line, half-formed, already abandoned. It might have been the beginning of a letter. It might have been the beginning of a name. He stared at it for a long time, the candle guttering, the harbor fog pressing gray against the window, and felt the shape of something missing press back against him from the other side. Not a memory. Not exactly. More like the dent a heavy book leaves in a shelf long after the book is taken away. You could measure the absence precisely. You just couldn't read it.

"Paid in full," he said aloud, to no one, and crossed out the stray mark, and finished the entry, and found — as he always eventually found — a kind of peace in the work. The numbers balanced. The world, whatever had been done to it or by it, balanced. Somewhere out there the hearth burned steady through the turning season; he didn't know how he knew that, but he knew it the way he knew his own handwriting, without evidence, without record, without why. Some knowledge lived below the ledger line. He had stopped fighting it.

He was blotting the final figure when the stairs creaked.

Sera Vane came up the way she always came up, without knocking, carrying tea the way other people carried weapons — two-handed, braced, ready for the cold. She was dressed for the weather in a gray traveling coat, frost melting in the seams of her shoulders, and she set the pot down on the corner of his desk with the authority of someone claiming territory.

"You're out of coal," she said.

"I'm out of everything. It's the end of the month."

"It's the end of the month," she agreed, pouring, "and you are the man who balances the books of the Guild of Impossible Things, and you cannot afford coal." She handed him a cup. "There's a poem in that. I'd write it myself but I'm a warden, not a poet."

"You're not a warden anymore either."

"No," she said, and drank her tea, and looked out the window at the fog, and let the word sit between them the way she let most words sit — unargued, unhurried, permanent.

He had tried, in the early weeks, to reconstruct her. She clearly knew him. She looked at him the way you look at a house you grew up in — with ownership, and grievance, and something underneath both that she never once let surface all the way. She had appeared at his door eleven days after the trouble at the Hearth, according to the landlady, and had been appearing ever since, at intervals too regular to be chance and too irregular to be duty. She brought tea. She corrected his posture. Once, finding him hunched over a ledger with a headache, she had put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you'd steady a lantern in wind, and then taken it away as if the gesture had cost her something.

He had asked her, directly, more than once: *How do we know each other?*

And she had answered, every time, with variations on the same theme: *You wouldn't believe me if I told you.* Or: *Badly, at first. Better now.* Or, once, looking at him with an expression that made his chest hurt in a place he couldn't locate: *You know how. You just can't carry it. So I carry it for both of us.*

He had decided, somewhere along the way, to stop asking. Not because he didn't want to know. Because he had begun to suspect that the knowing was heavier than a man should have to hold, and that she was holding it deliberately, the way you'd hold a door shut against a fire, and that prying at her fingers would only burn them both.

"So," she said now, settling into the chair across from him — his guest chair, which had acquired, over the months, a worn spot in the cushion shaped exactly like her. "Show me the books."

"You don't want to see the books."

"I love the books. I love watching you do the thing with your pen when an entry won't balance. Your jaw does something."

"My jaw does something."

"Like it's personally offended by arithmetic." She reached over and turned the ledger toward herself, scanning the night's entries with a warden's efficiency — quick, thorough, missing nothing. Her eyes slowed at Entry 4,412, the cooper's door-colored memory. Slowed again at the crossed-out stray mark above Entry 4,413. Something moved through her face, fast, like a fish under ice, and was gone.

"What is it?" Jude asked.

"Nothing. Good entry. Clean hand." She turned the ledger back. "You crossed out a mark here. At the top of the new page."

"A slip of the pen."

"Mm," said Sera, in a tone that contained entire libraries of disagreement, and drank her tea.

They sat in the quiet a while. The candle burned. Down in the street a cart passed, wheels hissing on wet stone, and somewhere farther off the harbor bell counted the hour with its usual indifference. Jude found, as he always found in these evenings, that the silence with Sera was different from other silences. Other silences were holes. This one was full. It was the silence of two people who had, apparently, survived something together — he didn't know what, would never know what — and had come out the other side into a room with tea in it, and found that sufficient.

"Do you ever miss it?" he asked. "The road. The Hearth. Whatever it was we—"

"Yes," she said, before he could finish, and then, softer: "Every day. And no. It cost more than it bought. That's all I'll say about it, and I'll say it the same way next time you ask, so save us both the breath."

"That's not cryptic at all."

"I'm a very straightforward person," Sera said, "who is keeping one secret, and the secret is that I'm keeping it. That's as honest as anyone gets."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising him, coming up from somewhere below the ledger line — and she smiled into her cup, and for a moment the room was warm in a way that had nothing to do with coal.

Then she set the cup down and looked at him, and the look was the one he could never read, the one from the first day, the one that held ownership and grievance and the third thing underneath.

"There's a reason I came tonight," she said. "Besides the tea."

"There's always a reason besides the tea."

"The guild's spring inventory is tomorrow. Full audit. Every ledger on the shelves, every entry read aloud, every asset confirmed." She paused. "Corvess is opening the sealed archive."

Jude felt the words land somewhere under his ribs. "The sealed archive doesn't exist. Everyone says that. The sealed archive is a story clerks tell each other."

"The sealed archive exists," Sera said. "I've seen the door. And tomorrow, for the first time in a hundred years, it opens." She picked up her cup again, and her hand — the steady hand, the warden's hand, the hand that had held a line against something terrible enough to leave scars he'd noticed but never asked about — was not quite steady. "And Jude. The first ledger coming out of it has your name on the spine."

The candle flickered. The fog pressed at the glass. Somewhere below the ledger line, in the country of the missing, the dent in the shelf deepened, and Jude Ashgrove heard himself say, quietly:

"*Ashgrove?*"

"No," said Sera, and met his eyes, and did not look away. "Not Ashgrove. The spine says one word. It says *blank*."
