## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The widow Maren—no relation to any scholar, just a miller's widow with flour in the seams of her knuckles—sat on her porch step and watched the road where her husband used to walk home, and Wendell Quintana sat beside her because that was how it always began. Not with a touch. With a sitting.

"You'll catch cold," she said.

"I run warm," he said, which was true in a way he never explained. Lifting a memory was work, and work made heat. He'd learned that young, lifting his first thing at eleven—a baker's boy's pride in his own bread—and had come home sweating like he'd carried sacks of grain up a hill that didn't exist.

The Hollow held no magic. Everyone said so. The wards saw to it, though nobody could say what the wards were or who had set them, only that they'd stood longer than the oldest gravestone and that nothing inside them sparked or shimmered or whispered. No charms. No hexes. The valley floor stayed flat under your feet and the sky stayed honest above your head, and if you were born with something strange in you, the Hollow pressed it flat as river stone.

What Wendell did wasn't magic. That was the story he told himself, and he told it often enough that it had worn smooth as a prayer bead. What he did was *take*. A memory, lifted whole from one head and settled into another, or sealed into glass for keeping. It didn't sparkle when he did it. It didn't sing. It felt like reaching into cold water for a coin—you held your breath, you closed your hand, and when you came up, something was missing from the world and something extra was in your fist.

Three rules in the Hollow: no magic, no questions, no leaving. Wendell broke none of them, so far as anyone could prove. He asked no questions. He never left. And what he took wasn't magic—it was just memory, and memory belonged to whoever could hold it, same as anything else.

That was the lie. He knew it was the lie. He kept it anyway, the way a man keeps a coat with a burnt lining because it still keeps off the rain.

* * *

Maren talked about the snow.

She'd done this every evening for nine days now, since the funeral, since the ground had taken Tam Voss and given back nothing but a wooden marker and a debt. She talked about the first snowfall of their marriage, forty-one years gone, and she told it the same way every time, word for word almost, like a woman reading from a book nailed shut.

"The eaves came down low over the door that winter," she said. "Tam went out before dawn to see if the well had frozen, and I watched him from the window, and the snow came down so soft it looked like the sky was apologizing."

Wendell nodded. He'd heard it four times. He could have recited it.

"That's a good one to keep," he said gently.

Her hands stilled in her lap. She looked at him the way people look at a well they've decided to trust—leaning forward over their own darkness. "You can do that? Truly?"

"Truly." He turned his palm up. "It won't hurt. You'll keep knowing it happened. You'll know it like a story someone told you—true, but not yours anymore. And I'll pay you for it. Fair rate."

"How much?"

"Twelve coppers. Or—" and here was the part he hated, the part where his voice did the broker's trick without meaning to, "—or I can put something in its place. A quiet. Some folk prefer the quiet."

Maren was silent a long moment. Down the lane, a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The light was going amber behind the western ridge, and the first lamp in the village of Lowmark lit itself the way lamps do, by hand, by some neighbor's boy on a ladder, because the Hollow had no other kind of light.

"The quiet," she said finally. Then, quickly, before she could change her mind: "No. Keep it. Keep it all. If I keep it, I'll sit here every night until I die watching a road he isn't coming down. Take it out and I can plant the autumn beds. Take it out and I can sleep."

Wendell had heard every version of this speech. They always dressed it up, but underneath it was always the same sentence: *I cannot carry this.*

"All right," he said. "Look at me. Breathe slow. Think about the snow—the real one, the first one. The eaves. Tam at the window."

Her eyes went soft and fixed somewhere past his shoulder, past the ridge, past forty-one years.

He reached in.

There was no ritual to it, whatever the stories said. He laid two fingers against her temple, found the shape of the memory—it had a weight and a temperature, everything did; this one was cold and bright-edged, like a pane of frost glass—and he drew it out the way you draw a splinter: steady, gentle, all in one motion so the flesh doesn't tear.

The widow blinked. The amber light came back into her face ordinary and unhaunted. She looked at the road and it was just a road.

"Oh," she said, and touched her own cheek, surprised to find it dry. "Oh, that's better. That's—yes." She laughed, small and startled, a woman stepping out of a wind she'd forgotten she'd been standing in. "Is it gone? It's gone. Wendell, it's *gone*, I feel ten pounds lighter."

"Twelve coppers," he said, pressing the coins into her hand, because he always paid, because the paying was the last plank of the raft he floated on, the thing that let him call it trade instead of theft.

She walked him to her gate. At the gate she stopped and frowned, the way they sometimes did, groping after a shape in the fog. "Did we talk about something? Before?"

"Snow coming this winter, maybe," Wendell said. "Early, by the look of the hills."

"Mm." She patted his arm. "You're a good boy, Wendell Quintana. Your mother raised you polite."

His mother had raised him hungry, mostly, and dead by his fourteenth year, but he smiled and said thank you and walked away with a fistful of cold bright glass in his pocket, humming like a struck bell.

* * *

Broker Hale kept her shop at the crook of the millrace, behind a dyer's premises, up stairs that smelled of tallow and wet wool. There was no sign. There didn't need to be. In the Hollow, everyone knew what Hale bought, and everyone agreed not to know it out loud, which was its own kind of ward.

She was sorting letters when he came in—actual letters, paper and wax, because Hale liked dealing in things that could be burned. She was sixty-some years old, built like a barrel with opinions, and she looked up at him with the flat gray appraisal of a woman counting coins in her head before he'd even opened his mouth.

"Well," she said. "The ghost of Lowmark. Sit."

He sat. He set the memory-glass on her desk between a ledger and a letter knife. It caught the lamplight and threw a small cold star on the ceiling.

Hale picked it up. She didn't have his gift—or if she did, she'd never once admitted it—but she had thirty years of reading the goods, and she turned the glass slowly, watching the way the light moved inside. Frost-bright. Steady. Clean edges.

"First snowfall," Wendell said. "Married forty-one years. Widow took it clean, paid her twelve, she was glad of it."

"Glad." Hale snorted. "They're always glad. That's the terrible part, isn't it. Half my stock is gladness." She set the glass down and pulled her ledger toward her. "Eighteen silver. It's a good piece. Grief-adjacent, first-of-kind, forty years' patina. The southern buyers pay stupid money for patina."

"Twenty."

"Eighteen, and I don't ask where you got the second one." Her eyes flicked up, gray and quick. "The one in your left pocket. The one you've been carrying for three weeks and haven't offered me, which means it's either worthless or it's trouble, and you don't keep worthless."

Wendell kept his face still. It was a skill older than his gift. "Left pocket's personal."

"Everything's personal. That's what makes it sellable." She wrote the number in the ledger, dipped the pen again, paused. "Word of advice, Quintana, free of charge, which should worry you. There's been questions lately. From outside."

"There's no outside."

"There's an outside, there's always been an outside, and lately the outside has been asking about the Hollow. About the wards. About—" she waved the pen in a small circle that took in his pockets, his face, his whole careful invisible life, "—goods like ours. So. Sell me what you have, take your silver, and go home by the long way. And Wendell." She looked at him then, properly, and for half a heartbeat there was something in her face that wasn't the broker at all, something old and tired and almost kind. "Whatever's in that left pocket—if it hums wrong, bury it. Don't open it. Don't sell it. Some doors are locked for a reason."

"It doesn't hum wrong," Wendell said. "It hums."

"Hums." Hale grunted and counted out eighteen silver, one by one, onto the wood, each coin placed like a small verdict. "Everything hums. Question is what tune."

* * *

He walked home the long way, because she'd told him to and because the long way went along the ridge, and the ridge was where the sky was biggest.

This was the thing he never said aloud, the thing he barely said to himself: the sky was the only thing in the Hollow he hadn't figured out how to want less of. The valley was enough—that was the catechism. The valley was safe, the valley was warm, the valley asked nothing and gave porridge and gossip and a bed that smelled of home. The valley was enough. He had the little house behind the chandler's, three rooms, a stove that drew well. He had a reputation that was no reputation at all, which in the Hollow was the best kind. He had a trade. He was, by any honest accounting, content.

He was content the way a locked room is warm.

On the ridge he stopped, as he always stopped, and looked up. The stars were out hard and countless, the way they only were over the Hollow—no haze, no wandering, a sky like a ledger with every line entered and balanced. He'd heard travelers' tales, back when travelers still came through, of skies elsewhere that shifted and streamed, stars that fell, lights that danced green along the northern dark. The Hollow's sky did none of that. The Hollow's sky was a held breath.

He found himself doing the arithmetic he always did. Forty-one years, that widow's snow. He was twenty-six. If he lived to seventy, that was forty-four more winters of this sky, this valley, these three rooms. He tried to feel the comfort of it and got instead a sensation like standing in a doorway too narrow for his shoulders.

In his left pocket, the sealed memory hummed.

Seven years he'd carried it. Seven years since the night at the valley's edge—the blood, the stranger's hands shaking around a glass like this one, the words *keep it, don't look, don't let them find it*—and then the weight going out of the man all at once, the way weight does, and Wendell running because that was what you did when death found you at a border, you ran, and he'd been running ever since, in place, in the smallest valley in the world.

He'd never opened it. He'd never sold it. Every broker south of the mountains would gut themselves for a piece that old and that sealed, and Hale was right, it hummed—not wrong, exactly, but deep, like a note played on an instrument too big for the room. Some nights he took it out and held it to his ear like a shell and imagined oceans.

Tonight, on the ridge, under the balanced and breathless sky, it hummed louder than usual. Or he was listening harder. Hard to say which.

Below him, Lowmark put itself to bed lamp by lamp. Somewhere a shutter banged. The millrace kept its one dull conversation with the stones. Everything in its place, everything accounted for, the whole valley a ledger with every line entered.

Wendell Quintana stood on the ridge with eighteen silver and a stolen snowfall and a seven-year secret, and told himself, as he told himself every night, that the valley was enough.

The stars overhead burned steady as nails.

And then—one of them flickered.

Just one. Just for a heartbeat, at the very crown of the sky, a star stuttered like a lamp in a draft, and steadied, and burned on.

Wendell told himself he'd imagined it.

He was, for the first time in seven years, not entirely sure he believed himself.
