## 2. What the Dying Man Gave Me

There were two kinds of nights in the Hollow: the kind where Wendell slept like a stone, and the kind where the thing in his floorboards hummed.

Tonight was the humming kind.

He lay in his narrow bed above the cobbler's shop—rented rooms, rented life, everything in it chosen to be forgettable—and listened to the sound that was not a sound. It came up through the planks like a pulse under ice. Not magic, exactly. The Hollow didn't allow for magic. But whatever slept beneath his floor had been born somewhere else, and it had never learned to be quiet.

Seven years now.

He'd told himself a thousand versions of the story, and in most of them he'd done nothing wrong. He'd been walking home along the valley's eastern rim, past the last of the ward-stones, minding his own business in the way that kept him alive. The stranger had simply been there, folded into the bracken like something the wind had given up on.

Wendell knew dead men when he saw them. He'd seen enough. But this one wasn't dead yet—not quite—and when Wendell knelt beside him, meaning only to check the pockets, because habit was habit and a man's purse could feed him for a week, the stranger had opened his eyes.

They were ember-colored. That was the detail Wendell had spent seven years trying to unsee. Not brown, not amber, but lit from behind, as if someone had struck a match inside the man's skull and left it burning low.

"You're a thief," the stranger said. Not an accusation. Almost fond.

"I'm nobody," Wendell said.

"No," the man breathed. Blood on his teeth, blood black in the starlight. "Nobody is exactly what you are. That's why it has to be you."

And then he'd pressed something into Wendell's hands—a memory, but not like any Wendell had ever lifted. He took memories the way he took purses: gently, with a touch he'd refined over years, sliding them out of their owners' heads and into his own keeping, where they sat like coins in a hidden cache until Broker Hale weighed them and paid. Every memory he'd ever stolen had a texture. Grief was heavy and wet. First love was warm and slightly embarrassing, like holding someone's hand too long. Joy was the lightest of all, barely there, gone before you noticed you'd held it.

This one had weight without substance. It was sealed—wrapped in something like wax, like scar tissue, like a knot tied by someone who never intended it to come undone. And it hummed. Even then, dying in the dark at the edge of the wards, it hummed against his theft-sense the way a locked door hums against the shoulder of a man who knows locks.

"Take it," the stranger said. "Don't open it. Whatever comes—whatever comes looking—*don't open it.* Give it to no one. Sell it to no one." A wet, rattling laugh. "Though I suppose you'll try."

"I don't want it," Wendell said, which was true, and which changed nothing.

"It isn't yours," the man agreed. His ember-eyes were dimming by then, guttering like the last candle in a house where everyone's asleep. "That's rather the point. Keep what isn't yours. Isn't that your whole trade?" His hand found Wendell's wrist, grip shockingly strong. "Listen. When the sky goes wrong—that's your sign. When the stars—" and here the words broke apart, dissolved into a cough that sounded like it was coming up from the bottom of a well, "—when they go out, take it to the Hearth. You'll know the way. It's in the memory. It's all in the—"

He died mid-sentence, which Wendell had always considered deeply rude.

The stars hadn't gone out. Seven years, and the sky over the Hollow hung exactly as it always had—the same patient constellations, the same slow wheel of seasons, the one constant in a valley built on rules. *No magic. No questions. No leaving.* So Wendell had buried the memory under his floorboards, told himself the man was mad, told himself the fever-dream glow of those eyes was a trick of dying, and got on with the business of being nobody.

Mostly he managed it.

*

The trouble with being a thief of memories was that you couldn't stop remembering your own.

Wendell climbed out of bed, bare feet cold on the boards, and knelt at the loose plank in the corner where the hearth-stone met the wall. He didn't lift it. He just put his palm flat against the wood, the way you might rest a hand on the flank of a horse you weren't sure wouldn't bite.

The hum answered him. It always did. It pressed up against his theft-sense like a cat against a closed door—patient, insistent, aware of him in a way no stolen memory had ever been aware of anything. Memories didn't know they'd been taken. They were just moments, peeled free of their owners, inert. This one *knew*. Somewhere under the floor, wrapped in its seal, it waited for him with the specific patience of a debt.

"Shut up," he whispered.

It hummed louder.

He sat back on his heels. Outside, the Hollow slept the way it always slept: deep, dreamless, warded. The old-timers said the wards had stood since before anyone remembered, that the valley had been set aside long ago for people who wanted nothing to do with what lived beyond the rim. Wendell had grown up inside those rules the way a fish grows up inside water. Magic was a story told to children to keep them from climbing the eastern paths. The world ended politely at the ward-stones, and everything worth wanting fit inside a valley you could walk across in a day.

Except.

Except that a man with fire in his eyes had crawled through those wards to die at their feet, carrying something that hummed like it had opinions. Except that whatever had wounded him had done it *outside*, and had either lost him or let him go, and Wendell had spent seven years deciding which possibility frightened him less. Except that the Hollow's perfect sleep had always felt, to Wendell alone, less like peace and more like held breath.

He went back to bed. He did not sleep.

*

In the morning he did what he always did after a bad night: he went to work. Work made the humming easy to ignore, or easier, which in the Hollow passed for the same thing.

His mark that day was a carter named Pell, who'd come through the market with a wagonload of late apples and a fresh grief riding him like a second passenger. Wendell clocked it from thirty paces—the particular slump of a man newly bereaved, the way his eyes snagged on things and slid off. Fresh grief was delicate work. Too soon and the memory came away ragged, bleeding; too late and it calcified into something you couldn't pry loose without breaking the man around it. Three weeks was ideal. He'd have to wait.

So instead he bought apples he didn't need, made pleasant conversation about the weather, and felt the familiar shame settle over him like a coat. That was the part nobody understood about his trade. People imagined theft was thrilling. Mostly it was this: buying fruit from a sad man so he'd remember your face kindly, filing the moment away for later use, going home to rooms chosen specifically because no one would ever ask about them.

He was a pickpocket whose pockets were other people's pasts. That was all. That was the whole of him.

By afternoon the light had gone strange—a thin, bruised gold, the kind of light that makes even honest towns look like paintings of themselves—and the market was emptying early. Wendell noticed, distantly, that the birds had gone quiet. In the Hollow the birds never went quiet. There was nothing to frighten them.

He was weighing whether that meant anything when the boy found him.

Tamsin, the cooper's daughter, eleven years old and fearless in the way of children raised inside walls—they believe the walls make them safe, so they behave as if nothing can touch them. She skidded up to his stall with her braid half-unraveled and her face lit with the ugly excitement of bad news.

"Wendell. Wendell, there's a man up past Miller's Rise. Lying in the road."

"People lie in roads all the time. Drink does that."

"He's *burning*," she said. "Not on fire. Just—hot. Like a coal. And there's men coming up the valley road after him, fast ones, on horses that don't look right."

Wendell's hands went still on the scale weights. Something old and cold turned over in his chest, seven years deep.

"Tamsin," he said carefully. "Go home."

"But—"

"*Home.* Now. Lock the door."

She went, grumbling, mercifully obedient. Wendell stood a moment longer among his apples and his honest little stall, listening. Far off, up the valley road, he could hear hooves—or something like hooves, a rhythm just slightly wrong, like a heartbeat played backwards. And underneath it, fainter than faint, rising through the soles of his boots and the bones of his feet and the floor of the world itself:

the hum.

Louder than it had ever been. Insistent. Almost—there was no kinder word for it—*afraid.*

*

He should have run. Later, in all the versions of this night he would replay, this was the moment he'd point to, the fork in the road where a smarter man takes the left path. Instead Wendell closed his stall, walked home at a pace just short of running, and knelt at the loose plank with his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

He lifted the board.

The memory lay in its wrappings exactly as he'd left it, a knot of darkness in the shallow space between the joists, and up close—this close, closer than he'd allowed himself in seven years—it was worse than humming. It was straining. The seal around it had thinned; he could see light moving inside it now, ember-light, the same color as a dying man's eyes. It pushed against his theft-sense and for the first time in his career Wendell understood what it must feel like to be the memory—to be taken, held, buried under a stranger's floor for seven years while the world outside forgot you.

"Don't open it," he told it, and heard how insane he sounded. "You heard the man. Don't open it."

Hooves on the lane below. Wrong hooves. Stopping.

Wendell put the board back and pressed his palm flat over it, and in the silence that followed—no knock at his door, no voice, only the terrible courtesy of men who needn't announce themselves—he finally admitted the thing he'd known since Tamsin's braid came flying around his stall:

The stranger hadn't been mad. The stars were fine, yes, the wards were standing, the Hollow was sleeping its perfect sleep. But something had crossed the valley road today with fire in it, hunting, and the keystone—because he'd known it was called that, hadn't he? Some word the dying man had pressed into him deeper than language, some word he'd spent seven years pretending not to know—was under his floor, and it was awake, and it was afraid.

He looked at his hands. Thief's hands. Empty hands, Hale always joked, though she didn't know how true it was—he'd sold or traded nearly every memory he'd ever lifted, kept almost nothing, hoarded nothing but coin and caution. Except this. The one thing he'd never dared to sell. The one thing that wasn't his, that had never been his, that hummed under his floor like a debt collecting interest.

Outside, unhurried footsteps crossed his courtyard. They stopped beneath his window.

Wendell held very still, one hand flat against the floorboards, feeling the ember-light pulse beneath his palm like a second heartbeat.

When the sky goes wrong. When the stars—

The footsteps moved on. But far above the valley, high over the rim of the world, the first star of evening flickered once—twice—

and Wendell Quintana watched it die.
