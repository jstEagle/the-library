## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with living your whole life under one sky is that you stop looking at it.

Wendell Quintana had lived in the Hollow for thirty-one years, and in all that time the stars had been exactly where stars were supposed to be. That was the point of them, as far as he was concerned. The Hollow was a valley with rules—no magic, no questions, no leaving—and the stars were the one rule that never needed enforcing. You could steal a widow's memory of first snowfall and sell it before supper. You could lie to Broker Hale about how fresh a memory was. But you couldn't lie to the stars. They hung up there every night like witnesses who'd taken an oath of silence.

He'd stopped noticing them around the time he turned twenty. Tonight he noticed.

It started as unease, though he didn't have a word for it yet. He was walking home from the market square with his hood up and his hands in his pockets, the way he always walked, invisible on purpose. The evening had gone strange somewhere around dusk—not wrong, just *thin*, like a blanket pulled too tight over a bed. The lamps in the square had guttered twice without wind. Old Marta's dog had refused to come out from under her cart, whining at nothing. And Wendell had felt something he hadn't felt in seven years: a hum against the back of his teeth, faint and rhythmic, like someone plucking a string buried in his skull.

He knew that feeling. It was what a sealed memory felt like when you stood too close to it. Like a locked door humming with whatever was behind it.

But the keystone—the stranger's memory, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden beneath the loose hearthstone in his cottage—was three streets away and had never hummed before. Not once in seven years. He kept it because selling it felt impossible and destroying it felt worse, the way you keep a dead man's letter because throwing it away seems like a second murder.

The hum grew louder as he walked toward home. That was the thing he noticed next, the thing that made him stop in the middle of Cobbler's Lane and take his hands out of his pockets.

It wasn't coming from his house.

It was coming from everywhere.

* * *

By the time he reached his door, the whole valley was buzzing. Not audibly—it wasn't sound, exactly—but Wendell's theft-sense, the sense that let him feel memories the way other people felt weather, was ringing like a struck bell. Every soul in the Hollow carried a faint signature, a warmth he'd learned to read across a crowded market: grief here, a fondness there, the bright hard candy of a child's first triumph. Tonight all those signatures were flickering. Flickering like lamps in a draft.

Inside his cottage, he knelt and pried up the hearthstone. The oilcloth bundle sat where it always sat. When his fingers closed around it, the hum climbed so high his vision blurred.

The seal on the memory—the knot of light the dying stranger had pressed into his hands seven years ago—was glowing. Dimly, desperately, like an ember gasping.

"No," Wendell told it, the way you talk to a sick animal. "No, don't you do anything."

He set it on the table. He lit a candle because his hands wanted something to do, then immediately regretted it, because the candle flame leaned sideways though the room was still. Then it leaned the other way. Then it stood straight up, unnaturally rigid, like a soldier at attention.

Outside, someone screamed.

* * *

The market square at midnight looked like a festival that had gone rotten. Half the Hollow was there—farmers in their nightshirts, the baker still dusted white, old men clutching coats they'd grabbed without buttoning. Torches threw jittering light on faces, and every face was tilted upward.

So Wendell tilted his upward too, and the world dropped out from under him.

The stars were going out.

Not fading. Not clouding over. Going *out*—one by one, or rather not one by one, but in patches, like candles pinched by an unseen hand moving across the ceiling of the world. A star above the eastern ridge vanished. Then three more near it. Then a whole constellation he'd known since childhood, its shape erased, leaving a hole in the sky so black it looked less like absence and more like something painted over the night.

A sound went through the crowd—not a scream, not words, something lower, a moan that came up from the belly of two hundred people at once.

"They're coming back," said the baker's son, gripping his father's arm. "Right? They're—they'll come back?"

No one answered him. Another patch of stars died. The dark spread like ink in water, and where it spread, the night wasn't just starless—it was *wrong*. Wendell's eyes kept trying to find shapes in it and failing, sliding off, as if that part of the sky had been removed and something else hung there instead, something his mind refused to describe.

His theft-sense was shrieking now. Every memory-signature in the crowd had gone ragged, trembling, and beneath it all ran a deep vibration he recognized from the bundle under his arm—he'd brought it, God help him, he'd run out of his house with a dead man's secret clutched to his chest—and it was beating faster, urgent, like a heart trying to warn its body of poison.

"The wards," said a voice, and Wendell's stomach turned over, because he knew it. Broker Hale stood at the edge of the square, and for once she wasn't watching the crowd. She was watching the sky with an expression he'd never seen on her: fear, naked and unhidden. "The wards are failing. Do you all understand what I'm telling you? The wards are *failing*."

Murmurs. Then old Tam Wicker pushed forward, ninety years of the Hollow in his face, leaning on a hoe he'd probably never used. His voice cracked across the square like a whip.

"Quiet. Quiet, all of you." He waited until the moaning died down. "My grandmother told me stories. Called them nonsense, most of us did." He pointed his hoe at the black holes in the sky with a hand that shook. "She said the valley wasn't warded by walls or stones or any such thing. Said it was warded by a *covenant*. An agreement, older than the valley itself, between our founders and—" he swallowed— "and others. Powers beyond. She said the covenant had a keystone. One piece, held safe, that all the rest depended on."

"Keystone of what?" someone shouted.

"Of the bargain," Tam said simply. "You keep the flame's promise, the flame keeps your peace. That was the saying. *The flame keeps your peace.*"

"And if the keystone's lost?" It was Hale who asked it, and her voice was very quiet, and Wendell felt the question land on him personally, like a hand on the back of his neck.

Tam Wicker looked up at the devouring dark. "Then the peace ends," he said. "And the ones we made the bargain with come looking for what's owed."

Nobody spoke after that. There was only the sound of the crowd breathing, and above it, faint and rhythmic and getting faster, the hum of the sealed memory against Wendell's chest, beating like a second heart.

* * *

He slipped away from the square. Invisibility was his craft; even now, especially now, the skill held. He moved along the backs of buildings, through the gap behind the tannery, up the lane toward his cottage, telling himself he was going to bar his door and sit with the bundle and think.

Halfway home he realized he was crying.

It embarrassed him more than frightened him, which was saying something, because he was terrified. But the tears weren't about hunters or covenants or powers coming to collect debts. He stood in the dark lane with his sleeves pressed to his eyes and understood, finally, what was actually breaking in him.

It was the sky.

Thirty-one years, and the stars had been the one thing he'd never tried to steal, never sold, never lied to. Everyone in the Hollow had their comforts—the baker had his bread, Marta had her dog, Tam had his grandmother's stories. Wendell had the stars. They asked nothing. They witnessed everything and forgave nothing and forgave everything, both at once, the way only distant things can. On nights when the loneliness got so heavy he could taste it, copper at the back of his throat, he would stand in his yard and look up and think: *at least that doesn't change. At least that's real.*

And now it was being taken from him, patch by patch, and he hadn't even stolen it. For the first time in his life, something had been taken *from* him instead of by him, and he discovered he had no craft for bearing it.

The last cluster of stars above the northern ridge went dark while he watched. After that, the sky over the Hollow was a single unbroken black, smooth as slate, and the valley below it felt suddenly small—not sheltered anymore, but *held*, the way a fist holds something it intends to crush.

The hum in his arms spiked into a single sharp note.

Then the wards broke.

There was no sound. That was the worst of it. Two hundred years of protection ending should have been thunder, should have been trumpets. Instead it was a sensation—a pressure releasing, like an ear popping after a long descent, except it happened to his whole self at once. The constant faint resistance he'd felt at the edges of the valley his entire life, the invisible boundary every Hollow child learned to feel without naming it—gone. Just gone. In its place, openness, vast and cold and utterly unprotected, rolling in from the ridgelines like fog.

And on the back of that openness, faint but unmistakable, came something new. Something moving. Far off beyond the eastern ridge, at the exact spot where the first stars had died, Wendell's theft-sense registered signatures. Many of them. Cold ones, bright ones, hunting-shaped.

Coming down toward the valley.

Behind him, in the market square, the screaming started in earnest. Ahead of him, in his cottage, the sealed memory glowed like a coal refusing to die, and beat, and beat, and beat.

Wendell Quintana stood alone in the lane between the two sounds, clutching the last ember of a broken covenant, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever he did in the next hour—whoever he ran to, whatever he confessed, whatever he surrendered—his life of careful invisibility was over.

The first hunter crested the eastern ridge as he turned and ran.
