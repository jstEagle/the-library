## 4. Hale's Ledger

The stars were still gone when Wendell went to see Hale.

He had spent the day doing what he always did after a bad night: moving through the Hollow like smoke, saying nothing, watching everything. The market was a wound that wouldn't close. Old Tam's stall stood shuttered. Three families had loaded carts at dawn, though everyone knew there was nowhere to go—the wards kept people out as surely as they kept them in, and no one in living memory had tested which way they cut.

Wendell knew how they cut. He'd carried a man to the edge once, seven years ago, and felt the wards hum against his skin like a struck bell. In only. That was the bargain nobody had written down.

By dusk he had made himself walk past the chapel where the wardens were burning candles and arguing scripture, past the well where a woman wept into her apron because her son had been born under a sky with no stars and she couldn't stop saying *he'll never know what he missed*, past all of it, until he reached the crooked lane behind the tannery where Broker Hale kept her office above a shop that sold nothing.

The sign still read FINE GOODS, though the window hadn't held fine goods in twenty years. Wendell climbed the stairs the way he always did—quietly, out of habit—and stopped at the top because the door was open.

Hale never left her door open.

She sat behind her desk with a ledger open before her, a fat thing bound in green leather gone soft as cloth with age. She was writing in it by lamplight, unhurried, her pen scratching like a beetle in a wall. She was a small woman, Hale, dry and neat, with white hair pinned so tight it looked painted on. People underestimated her right up until she named a price.

"You're standing in my doorway," she said without looking up, "which means either the sky falling has made you brave or stupid. Sit down, Quintana. I haven't got all night."

"Hale." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "What happened last night?"

"Stars went out." Pen scratch. "Sky's empty. World's ending, if you believe the chapel folk, who believe everything."

"The wards came down with them."

That stopped the pen.

Hale looked up. Her eyes were pale gray, the color of dishwater, and they had a way of making you feel counted. "Now that," she said, "is an interesting thing for you to know."

"Everyone knows. Half the valley watched the light die on the boundary stones."

"Half the valley saw stars vanish. You're the one talking about wards." She closed the ledger slowly, one hand flat on its cover, the way a person quiets a dog. "And here I thought you were just another thief with clean fingernails."

"I'm not a thief."

"No?" Something moved through her face—not quite a smile. "You lifted a first snowfall off Widow Marsh two days ago and traded it to me for eleven silver and a jar of pickled plums. Don't look like that. I know what walks through my door, Wendell. I've always known. It's why you keep coming back."

His mouth had gone dry. Seven years of careful, invisible work, and she said it like she'd been keeping score in front of him the whole time. Maybe she had. "You told me what I do isn't magic. That it doesn't count against the rules."

"It doesn't. Memory-work is older than the covenant and quieter than dust. The wardens can't smell it." She leaned back. "But you're wrong about one thing, boy. Nothing in this valley has ever been invisible to me. Not you. Not your little hoard. Not—" she tapped the ledger once, lightly, "—the sealed memory you've been sitting on since the winter a dying man crawled to the border and put it in your hands."

The room tilted.

Wendell heard his own breathing, too loud. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it were listening too. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." Hale opened a drawer and took out a bottle and two cups, poured without asking, and pushed one across the desk. He didn't touch it. "Sit down. Truly. What I have to tell you goes better seated."

He sat. The chair creaked. Somewhere below, in the empty shop, a floorboard settled, and the sound seemed enormous.

"Seven years ago," Hale said, "a man came over the western ridge half-dead. Wards let him in—in only, same as ever—and he made it as far as the shepherd's hut before his blood gave out. You found him. And instead of doing the decent thing and running for the wardens, you did what you do. You took from him. Only this time something came loose that wasn't meant to come loose. A sealed memory. A keystone." She said the word the way other people said *plague*. "You've carried it seven years and never opened it, because even your thief-sense knows better than to pick that lock."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because the man talked before he died. To me." She drank, small and precise. "He came looking for a broker. That's what brokers are for, boy—doors between worlds. He wanted passage out again, someone to carry word to his daughter. He didn't make it. But he made an impression. And I have spent seven years wondering what he pressed into your hands."

Wendell's hands were flat on his knees. He noticed he was gripping them hard enough to whiten the knuckles. "It's not mine to give anyone. Not even you."

"No. It's not yours at all. That's rather the point." Hale set down her cup. "Let me tell you a story, since stories are cheap tonight. Once upon a time there was a broker in a warded valley who did good business. Quiet business. Memories in, silver out, no questions asked—the three rules, remember? But quiet business buys a quiet life, and I have never wanted a quiet life. So twelve years ago I started selling outward. There are markets beyond the wards, Wendell. Cities where memories trade like grain. And every few months, a parcel of borrowed years crosses the boundary in a trader's pack, and no one in the Hollow is the wiser."

The floor seemed to drop a slow inch beneath his chair. "You've been smuggling. Out of the valley. Through the wards."

"Through gaps in the wards. Gaps I paid a great deal to learn about." She said it plainly, without shame, and somehow that was worse than gloating. "You think the covenant keeps this place safe? The covenant keeps this place *asleep*. Everything worth having is out there, and I have been selling slivers of it to myself for twelve years while the rest of you sang hymns to a dark sky."

"And now the sky's dark anyway."

"Now the sky's dark anyway," she agreed. "Because the wards ran on the covenant, and the covenant ran on a flame, and the flame—" she spread her small dry hands, "—went out. Or was taken. Or was carried off by a dying man who hid its spark inside a memory and gave it to a thief in a valley full of sleepwalkers."

"That's not—" Wendell stood up. His legs felt borrowed. "I never asked for any of this. I stole a snowfall and a wedding day and a dead woman's lullaby. Small things. Things people wanted to be rid of. I never stole anything that mattered."

"That's the joke, isn't it." Hale wasn't smiling. "You never got to choose what mattered. Thieves never do. You picked a lock you didn't know was a door, and now everyone who wants what's behind it is going to come through *you*."

"Who?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and Wendell understood, with a sick slow certainty, that the answer had been in the room since he climbed the stairs. That the open door, the poured drink, the ledger laid out—all of it had been ceremony. Hale liked ceremony. Hale had probably rehearsed this.

"There are courts beyond the valley," she said. "Old ones. One of them tended the First Hearth—kept the flame, kept the covenant, kept the warmth flowing between the realms. The Ember Court. When the flame died, they started hunting. Not for the fire itself—they know the fire is ash. For the spark. The last ember. And their hunters have ways of knowing things." She tapped the ledger again. "Ways like me."

The cold went through him all at once, head to heel. "No."

"They sent inquiries. Months ago. Before the wards fell—I thought it was scholarship, then. Curiosity about old covenants. I answered politely. I'm polite." She rose, straightened her skirts, and began pinning something to the notice board behind her—a paper, a list, a name. "Then three nights ago a hunter walked into my back room wearing a man's face and asked me, very courteously, who in the Hollow had handled a sealed memory in the last decade. And I told him."

"*Hale.*"

"I told him," she repeated, "because he asked me what would happen to me if I didn't. And because I am not a brave woman, Wendell, whatever else I am. I am a survivor with excellent bookkeeping." She turned around. On the paper, in her small exact hand, was his name. *Wendell Quintana.* Below it: *holds keystone. unopened. carries it on his person.*

"You wrote down where I keep it."

"I write down everything. It's what I'm for." She almost sounded tired. "They'll come tonight or tomorrow night. Hunters move fast once they have a name. They'll take the memory and they'll take you, because a witness who's seen a hunter's face doesn't get to stay a witness. And then they'll burn this valley down looking for anyone else who might have touched it, because the Court does not leave loose threads, and this whole valley is a loose thread now that the wards are down."

"Why are you telling me?" His voice cracked on it. "If you've already sold me—why warn me?"

Hale looked at him, and for one unguarded moment she looked old. Older than the valley. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Because twelve years ago I chose the door over the wall," she said, "and I have regretted it every single day since, and I have never once said so out loud. Because you're a thief, Quintana, but you're a small one, a decent one, and I built my trade on people like you and called it commerce." She picked up his untouched cup and drank it herself. "And because the man who died at the border asked me to look after his spark if it ever surfaced. I looked after myself instead. Consider this the closest I will ever come to an apology."

She tore the page from the board—but she didn't tear it small, didn't crumple it. She folded it once and set it on the desk between them, face up, his own name staring at him in her tidy script.

"A copy goes to the hunter at midnight," she said. "The west road is unwatched for maybe six hours. After that, nothing in this valley will protect you, because there is no valley anymore—just a hole in the world with frightened people in it." She sat back down and reopened her ledger, pen poised, already becoming again the dry neat woman who bought grief by the ounce. "Get out of my shop, Wendell."

He stood in the doorway a moment longer. The lamp flame bent toward him, just slightly, like a thing leaning after a departing guest.

"Hale," he said. "The man who died. Did he say anything else? Anything about—why me?"

"He said," Hale replied, not looking up, "that the spark chooses empty hands. Then he apologized for the trouble." The pen scratched. "I assumed he was delirious. Go."

Wendell went. Down the stairs, past the empty shelves, out into the starless street where the whole Hollow lay dark and breathless under a sky like a closed lid. Behind him, faint through the open window, he heard the scratch of her pen, writing him down one last time.

Six hours. The west road. *Empty hands.*

He had no idea what any of it meant. But he knew the weight against his ribs, the sealed memory humming its locked-door hum, and he knew—for the first time in seven years—that he was not invisible, had never been invisible, and that somewhere beyond the ridge, something with a man's face and a hunter's patience was already reading his name.

Wendell Quintana turned west and started running.

* * *

He made the shepherd's hut by moonrise—or by where moonrise should have been; the sky offered nothing but black. The hut was exactly as he'd left it seven years ago, which was to say falling down, the roof holed, the hearth cold. He had buried the stranger's body behind it, in ground that had taken the shovel badly. He didn't stop. There was no time for graves.

The wards, when he reached them, were simply *gone*.

He had braced for a wall, a shimmer, some last flicker of the old covenant fighting to hold. Instead the night just—continued. The air at the boundary was the same air as the air inside it. The pines went on growing on both sides of a line that no longer existed. It was like stepping through a doorway and finding the house demolished around it.

Wendell stood with one foot in the Hollow and one foot out, and realized he was shaking.

All his life the valley had been the whole of the world, and the world had been a rumor. No questions. No leaving. He had hated the rules and obeyed them and told himself the valley was enough, and now the rules were dead and the enough-ness with them, and ahead of him the land dropped away into darkness that smelled of rain and far-off woodsmoke and things he had no names for.

Behind him, faint, a bell began to ring in the village. Then another. The wardens, waking to the loss. Or the hunters, announcing themselves. He couldn't tell, and knew he never would.

He stepped through.

The moment both feet crossed the line, the sealed memory against his ribs flared hot—not painfully, but urgently, like a hand gripping his wrist. And somewhere behind him, far back along the dark western road, a light kindled that was not lamplight and not starlight: a low, ember-red glow, moving. Fast.

Coming for him.

Wendell ran.
