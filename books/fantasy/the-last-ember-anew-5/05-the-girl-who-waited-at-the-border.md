## 5. The Girl Who Waited at the Border

Wendell ran.

He had never run in his life—not truly. In the Hollow you walked at a careful pace, eyes down, hands loose, because running meant guilt and guilt meant questions and questions were the first crack through which the valley swallowed a man whole. But the wards had died screaming at midnight and Broker Hale had sold his name to something out of the dark, and so now he ran, boots hammering the frost-stiff road toward the western gap, where the hills pinched together like a closing fist.

The stars were still gone. That was the worst of it. Seven years he had told himself the sky was the one thing that couldn't be taken from him—cheap constancy, free light—and now when he tilted his head back there was nothing above but a smooth black ceiling, featureless as slate, pressing down on the valley like a lid on a pot.

*She sold me,* he thought, and the thought had teeth. *Hale looked me in the eye over twenty years of trades and sold me.*

He should have felt betrayed. What he felt instead was something colder and more honest: she'd never been anything but what she was. A broker brokers. He was the one who'd mistaken a transaction for a sanctuary. Twenty years of stolen memories in other people's heads and he'd somehow convinced himself his own name was safe.

Behind him, down the length of the market road, torches bloomed. Not the orange of pitch or lantern-oil—a color he didn't have a word for, a burning like coals seen through amber, wrong for the Hollow, wrong for anywhere he knew. The hunters had come through fast.

The sealed memory shifted against his ribs.

Seven years it had lived there, sewn into the lining of every coat he owned, transferred from garment to garment with the same dull ritual as his knife and his coin purse. A dying man's last possession, pressed into his hands at the valley's edge while the snow came down: *Take this. Don't open it. Carry it.* And then blood on the frost, and breath going ragged, and nothing left to bargain with. Wendell had taken it because taking things was what he did, and he had never opened it because—for once in his miserable career—something in him had understood that this theft wasn't his.

It hummed against his theft-sense even now, even mid-flight, that sixth sense he used for finding memories worth lifting. Every memory he'd ever touched had a texture, a temperature. Grief was cold ash. Joy was warm bread. This thing was none of those. It was like putting your hand against a locked door and feeling, faintly, on the far side, a fire.

The gap between the hills opened before him. Beyond it the land dropped away into country he'd never seen—the Hollow's wards had always ended at that saddle of stone, and no one crossed, and no one asked why, because asking why got you a visit from the elders and a long conversation about gratitude.

Wendell crested the rise, lungs full of knives—

—and stopped, because the black sky ahead of him tore open with light.

Three figures stood in the road below, spaced wide, unhurried, the way men stand who have already won. They wore armor the color of banked fire, and their faces were hidden behind masks of hammered bronze, and where they stepped, the frost did not melt so much as *flinch*. The nearest one raised a hand, and something unspooled from its fingers—a thin cord of ember-light, drifting across the road like a spider's line, patient as a trap.

"Wendell Quintana," said the middle figure, and the voice came out of the mask like wind through a chimney. "You carry what is not yours."

His whole life, that sentence had been true and harmless. Spoken by these things, it sounded like a death warrant read aloud.

"I don't know what you—" he started, because lying was also what he did, and then the ember-cord snapped taut across the road and he understood that the time for lying was finished. He turned to bolt back the way he came.

The way he came was already burning.

Flame climbed the hillside behind him—not wildfire, but ordered flame, walls of it, herding. The hunters hadn't rushed because they never needed to. They had simply closed the box.

Wendell Quintana stood in the middle of a road at the edge of everything he knew, holding a dead man's secret against his heart, and discovered that he did not want to die. It seemed an absurd thing to learn about oneself at the end, and he almost laughed.

Then someone laughed for real—a short, hard sound, furious—and a girl dropped out of the dark like a verdict.

* * *

Isolde had waited seven years for this night, and it was not going the way seven years of planning promised.

In the plan, she tracked the keystone to whoever held it, and the holder was either a cunning enemy she could outfight or a frightened innocent she could outtalk. In the plan, the Court's hunters arrived afterward, if at all, and she was gone before they came.

In reality, she had watched three ember-hunters corner a hollow-cheeked man in a dead valley's only pass, and she had perhaps four seconds to choose between patience and violence, and she chose violence, because she had spent seven years learning exactly one lesson over and over again: waiting gets people killed. Waiting had gotten her father killed. She was done with waiting.

She came off the ridge above the pass with her mother's knife in one hand and a coil of salt-iron wire in the other, and she hit the nearest hunter from behind the way an avalanche hits a chapel.

The armor was worse than the stories said. Her blade skated off the shoulder plate in a spray of white sparks, but the impact staggered him, and staggering was enough, because the second hunter turned to face her and for half a heartbeat the ember-cord across the road went slack. The thief—Quintana, the trackers called him, though she'd learned that name only three days ago from a drunk caravan guard—dove under it like a man diving under a fence, and rolled, and came up running.

Good, Isolde thought grimly. Run. I'll need you alive.

Then the third hunter stopped pretending to be a person.

Masks came off when the Court wanted you to know what was killing you, her father's letters said, and this one took its mask off, and beneath it there was no face. There was a hearth seen from inside—a cavern of slow red light, and in the center of it, a single coal-shaped darkness that might have been an eye. The heat coming off it hit Isolde like a hand pressed flat against her chest.

"Little Vane," it said, and its voice was smoke. "Your father's blood smells on you. We wondered which of you would come."

Her father's name in that mouth. Something in Isolde went very quiet and very simple, the way it did right before she did something stupid. She set her feet in the frost, spun the salt-iron wire once around each fist, and said, "Come find out how it smells up close."

The hunter moved faster than anything that size had a right to move.

What saved her was not skill, though she'd bought skill dearly over seven years—from a hedge-witch in the Ashwood, from a disgraced guardian in a border town whose debts she'd sworn to remember. What saved her was geography. She had spent two days in this pass learning it the way her father taught her to learn ground: where the stone was rotten, where the slope broke, where water ran under the frost and made the footing treacherous. She gave ground deliberately, backward and leftward, drawing the hunter onto the spring-fed stretch of road, and when its burning foot came down on ice masquerading as stone, the world did what worlds do to things that presume.

The hunter slipped. Only for an instant. An instant was all she needed. Isolde wrapped the salt-iron wire around its trailing arm and hauled with her whole weight and her whole grief behind it, and the wire bit through whatever the armor really was, and the thing made a sound like a doused fire—hiss, shudder, shriek—and folded to one knee.

Across the road, the other two hunters disentangled themselves from the burning hillside and started toward her with the terrible calm of things that could not hurry because they would never tire.

Isolde backed away, knife up, calculating odds she did not like. Behind her, downslope, the thief crouched in the ditch watching her with the wide eyes of a man watching his own rescue and unable to account for it.

"Get up," she snarled at him without turning her head. "Whatever you are, get up."

"Why?" he shouted back. "Who are you?"

"The daughter of the man you robbed!" It came out of her like a thrown stone, seven years hot. "Now move!"

Something happened then that she would think about for a long time afterward. The thief—this gray little man built out of caution and shadow—did not run. He scrambled up the opposite embankment, yes, away from the fight, but not *fleeing*; he was doing something with his hands, something quick and practiced, the way a pickpocket works a crowd. He pulled something from inside his coat. Even at a distance, in the ember-light, Isolde saw it: a sliver of memory-light, torn free and held between finger and thumb like a coin.

And he threw it.

Not at the hunters. At the ground between them, and the memory shattered like a dropped glass, and what spilled out of it was—snow. First snowfall, some child's first snowfall, wonder and cold and a mother's laugh, a whole small warm life scattered across thirty feet of road. And the hunters stopped. Both of them. Stopped mid-stride and turned their eyeless burning faces down toward the spilled memory the way starving men turn toward bread, and for six full seconds the pass was silent except for frost creaking and Isolde's own ragged breathing.

Six seconds. She used four of them to cut the kneeling hunter's throat with her father's knife—it didn't die properly, but it collapsed into a drift of cooling embers and slag—and the last two to grab the thief by the collar and haul him off the road and down into the dark of the western slopes.

They ran. Behind them, the two surviving hunters waded out of the spilled memory, slower now, savoring it, and did not pursue past the old wardline. The Court, her father's letters had warned, does not chase. The Court waits. The covenant is broken; the ember is lost; time is on their side.

Let them wait, Isolde thought, dragging a gasping stranger down into the hills. Tonight, for once, waiting had been hers.

* * *

They stopped running when the pass was a gray smudge behind them and the stars' absence overhead had become, horribly, normal. Dawn came anyway—some pale sourceless seepage of light that belonged to a sky with nothing in it—and by that light Isolde finally allowed herself to look at the man she had saved.

He looked exactly like trouble. Narrow, quick-eyed, dressed in clothes chosen to be forgettable, with the callused fingertips of a craftsman and the restless gaze of a man perpetually pricing the room. His hands wouldn't stay still.

"So," she said.

"So," he agreed.

"You're Wendell Quintana. Memory-thief of the Hollow. You've held my father's keystone for seven years." She let each fact land like a coin on a table. "I've crossed four hundred miles and spent every coin I had and buried two friends to find you. So before you say one clever word, understand that I am deciding right now whether you're the heir of the last ember—or just the latest thief in a chain of thieves, and the only difference between you and the Ember Court is that they have better armor."

He didn't flinch. That surprised her. Most people flinched.

"You're Aldric's daughter," he said quietly. "He talked about you. At the end."

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. "He *talked*? You were there. You watched him die. And you took—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "What did you take from him?"

"Nothing." For the first time, the thief's voice had no craft in it. "I swear it by whatever's left worth swearing by. I found him at the valley's edge, bleeding out, and he pressed a sealed memory into my hands and told me to carry it and never open it, and I have carried it for seven years and I have never opened it. Ask it yourself." He pulled open his coat and drew out the thing she had chased across half the map: a small sealed shape of memory-light, dense as a star seed, humming faintly against her own trained senses like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

It was real. She'd known it would be—she'd tracked its resonance for three years—but seeing it in a stranger's coat lining, after all of it, undid something in her chest.

"It hums," he said. "Every day. Like a door with someone knocking on the other side. I'm a thief, lady—I know what stolen things feel like. This never felt like mine." He looked at her, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night's running. "I don't know why he gave it to me. I've asked myself that for seven years. Maybe he was delirious. Maybe I was just the only pair of hands there."

"He chose you," Isolde said, and heard how bitter it came out.

"Or I stole the choosing too. I honestly can't tell anymore." He tucked the keystone away, and then, because he apparently couldn't help himself, added: "You said the Court wants it. Why? What happens if they get it?"

"What happens," Isolde said, "is that the last spark of the First Flame goes into Lord Cassian's vault, the covenant dies forever, and both realms freeze—one slowly, one all at once. My father died carrying it toward the Hearth. He didn't make it." She looked west, where the sunless dawn was failing to properly begin. "So we finish the walk. You and me. And I'll be honest with you, thief: I don't trust you. You're exactly the kind of man my father would have given his life's work to, because he believed in second chances, and it got him killed. But you're what I have."

"And if I run?"

She smiled, and it was not a friendly smile. "I waited seven years. I'm very good at waiting. And I'm better at finding."

Wendell Quintana studied her for a long moment—pricing the room, pricing her—and then he did something she did not expect. He laughed, short and bleak, and rubbed a hand over his face.

"Everyone keeps selling me lately," he said. "It's nice to meet someone who'd rather hunt me." He nodded west, toward the drowned dawn and the long road beyond the dead valley. "First Hearth. Fine. But we should move before the Court stops savoring its snack. And Isolde—" he pronounced her name carefully, like something breakable, "—your father's last words weren't in that seal. Whatever else is in there, I promise you that much. He made sure of it. He said, *tell her herself.*"

The frost, the wind, the ruined sky—all of it went distant. "Tell me what?"

"I don't know." The thief shrugged, helpless, honest. "I never opened it. But he was very clear." He started walking, then paused. "Which means at some point, we're going to have to trust each other enough to open it. And neither of us likes the sound of that."

Far behind them, back at the lip of the Hollow, a column of ember-light rose against the black sky—signal-fire, summons, or celebration. Isolde didn't wait to learn which.

"Hunters will report the keystone survived," she said. "Cassian will stop sending three. He'll send an army with a legal claim." She shouldered her pack and fell into step beside the thief, knife-hand loose, heart a closed fist. "So we don't take the roads. There's a woman in the Ashwood who owes me, and a waystation past that where travelers ask no questions. We gather what help we can."

"And after that?"

"After that," Isolde said, "we walk into the Court's own country and steal a miracle. Consider it a trade. You're the expert."

Ahead of them the dead sky stretched to the horizon, starless and vast—and somewhere under it, a week's hard travel away, the First Hearth waited, cold as a dead heart, for someone brave enough and empty enough to fill it.

Neither of them looked back.
