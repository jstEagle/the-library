## 6. First Steps Past the Wards

The wards broke like a fever.

One moment I was walking through the border mist with Isolde's hand fisted in my sleeve, and the next the world opened its throat and swallowed me whole. That's the only way I can describe it now, though at the time I had no words for it—only sensation, arriving all at once, like a debt collector kicking down every door of my body simultaneously.

In the Hollow, the world is quiet. You don't notice how quiet until you leave. The valley doesn't just ward against magic; it mutes everything else by comparison, flattens taste into salt and bread, narrows color to the honest range of dirt and wheat and slate sky. I'd spent thirty-one years believing that was simply what the world was. A gray room with good light management.

Past the border stones, the gray room caught fire.

The grass sang. Not rustled—*sang*, each blade holding a note so thin and green I could feel it behind my teeth. When I looked up, the sky was doing something obscene with blue, layering it like a merchant stacking silks, and when I looked away the afterimage stayed burned on the inside of my eyelids, humming.

"Wendell." Isolde's voice came from very far away, or maybe I was the one far away. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." My own voice sounded wrong too, rounder than usual, as if the air were giving it back to me with interest. "It's too much. Everything's—it remembers."

That was the part that undid me. Not the colors, not the singing grass. It was that the air itself seemed to *remember*. Every breath carried something—a hearthfire someone else had lit, rain on a roof I'd never stood under, the ghost of a lullaby sung in a language I didn't know but understood anyway. In the Hollow I made my living pulling memories out of other people's heads and selling them off like cut flowers. Out here, the world was handing them over freely, bundled into the wind, asking nothing in return.

I sat down hard in the grass and laughed, because if I didn't laugh I was going to cry, and I had a reputation to maintain even with an audience of one.

"First-timers always sit," Isolde said. She crouched beside me, arms on her knees, watching me with that flat gray patience she wore like armor. "My father said the wards don't keep magic out of the Hollow. They keep the Hollow numb. Like a poultice over an open wound."

"Your father sounds like he talked a lot."

"He did." Something moved across her face—fast, gone before I could name it. Grief has a hundred disguises, and I know most of them, because I've stolen enough memories to recognize grief wearing a smile, wearing anger, wearing nothing at all. "Drink."

She pressed a waterskin into my hands. Even the water tasted like a story. I nearly wept into it.

We waited while the world finished shouting at me. Slowly the noise settled—not gone, never gone, but sorted into something I could carry. The grass went back to merely whispering. The blue settled into blue. And underneath it all, that low remembering hum remained, like a city heard from across a river.

"Better?" she asked.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'll manage."

"You'd better. We have three days' walk to the crossroads shrine, and you'll be crossing country where everything wants to look at you. Some of it will want more than looking." She stood and shouldered her pack. Her sword rode her hip the way my guilt rode mine: constantly, comfortably, like it belonged there. "Stay behind me. Don't eat anything offered to you unless I've eaten first. And Wendell—"

"Don't steal anything." I got up, brushing wet grass from my knees. "I know. You've told me four times."

"And you've looked at things the way a thief looks four times since we crossed. So I'll say it a fifth."

I wanted to argue. The trouble was, she was right, and we both knew it. Because here is what nobody tells you about leaving a warded valley after a lifetime inside: your craft comes with you. The theft-sense—that itch behind the eyes, that sense of *loose seams* I'd honed over twenty years of lifting memories from grieving widows and drunken farmers and dying strangers—out here, it was screaming. Everything past the wards was seamed. Loose. Full of memories not locked in skulls but hanging off trees, pooled in streams, coiled in birdsong like loose thread waiting for a pull.

A thief in the Hollow was a pickpocket. A thief out here could strip the world bare.

I kept my hands in my pockets and followed her north.

* * *

By midday we reached the old covenant road, and Isolde began to talk—or rather, she finally answered questions, which for Isolde amounted to the same thing.

She pointed with her chin at a moss-covered stone post leaning out of the ferns. "Waymarkers. They used to run from the First Hearth to every settlement in both realms. Four thousand years ago, give or take."

"What did they mark?"

"The warmth." She said it plainly, the way you'd say *the rain* or *the road*. "Before the Hearth went dark, the flame didn't just burn in one place. It *shared*. Every hearthfire in every house in both realms was a child of the First Flame. You could stand at your own fire on the coldest night of winter and feel the Hearth answering back. Warmth wasn't something you owned. It was something you were lent, and lending it onward didn't diminish it. That was the whole covenant. Fire shared is fire multiplied."

I walked a few paces in silence, listening to the ferns gossip about our passing.

"And then someone stole it," I said.

"Then someone stole it." She glanced at me sideways. "You already knew that."

"Hale talks when she's paid enough. Fragments. The Ember Court. A dark age. She called it 'the Dimming' and charged me double for the word."

"The Court didn't exist yet when it happened. They're what grew in the cold afterward—keepers turned jailers, guardians who decided guarding meant hoarding." Her jaw tightened. "As for the thief—the histories disagree. Some say a lord. Some say a scholar. Some say a spirit who wanted warmth all to itself because it had never been warm."

"But they agree it was theft."

"They agree it was theft." She stopped walking. The road bent ahead around a stand of silver-barked trees, and the light through their leaves fell on her face in coins. "Why does that matter to you?"

Because I'm a thief, I almost said. Because I've spent my life proving that taking a thing and having a thing are not the same, that a stolen memory goes cold in your chest within a season, that everything I've ever lifted has weighed less and less the longer I've held it, until selling it felt less like loss and more like setting down something that never fit my hands. Because somewhere under the pragmatism I've always known that the widow's first snowfall was worth more to her than any coin Hale ever paid me, and I took it anyway.

What I said was: "Just seems like relevant history."

Isolde studied me for a long moment. She has a way of looking at a person like she's reading the last page first, checking whether the ending justifies the book. Then she turned and walked on, and I exhaled without realizing I'd been holding it.

"One more thing about the covenant," she said, without turning around. "The flame couldn't be taken by force. Everyone agrees on that too. The First Flame could only be *given*—that was its nature. Chosen warmth, freely handed over. Whoever stole it didn't overpower the Hearth." A pause, the length of three heartbeats. "They were invited in first. Trusted. Someone opened the door for them."

The wind moved through the silver trees, and for a moment I could have sworn it carried the smell of a hearthfire in a house I'd never entered, and the sound of a family laughing behind a door that someone had once left unlocked for a stranger.

I thought about the sealed memory riding against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, humming its locked-door hum. Seven years I'd carried it. Seven years I'd told myself I hadn't opened it out of caution.

Standing on that road, in air thick with other people's warmth, I admitted for the first time that maybe I hadn't opened it because I was afraid of what kind of man it would prove I'd been.

* * *

We camped that night in the shell of a waystation—an old rest-house for covenant travelers, Isolde said, though the roof had mostly become sky and the hearthstone was cold as riverbottom. She built a fire anyway, striking sparks into a nest of tinder with the grim competence of someone who has slept outdoors for years, and I sat close to the flames because sitting close to flames suddenly mattered to me in a way it never had in the Hollow.

Firelight out here was different too. It leaned toward you. It listened.

"Ask," Isolde said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become its own conversation.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever question you've been chewing since noon. You chew loudly."

I fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. "The keystone memory. The one my father—" No. Not my father. The stranger. "The one the dying man gave me. If the flame can only be given, why would he seal his memory of it and press it into a stranger's hands? Why not just—carry it himself? Give it himself?"

"Because he was dying." She said it without softness, which is its own kind of mercy. "And because carrying it wasn't enough. The scholar I found in Vespertine's records—the fragments I could afford to copy—say the reforging requires a bearer with empty hands. Not innocent hands. Empty ones. Someone who has nothing left to give but the choice itself."

"Empty hands," I repeated. My own hands, calloused from twenty years of picking other people's pockets in the dark, lay open on my knees. They looked, in the firelight, like exactly what they were. "That's a strange requirement for a miracle."

"It's not a strange requirement. It's the only honest one." She pulled her knife and began sharpening it, the whisper of steel a metronome under her words. "Anyone can guard a treasure. Guarding is easy—you just hold tight and hit people. But the covenant was never about holding. It was about opening. Giving warmth away and trusting it to come back multiplied." The knife paused. "Whoever stole the flame couldn't do that. Couldn't let go. Maybe they told themselves they were keeping it safe. They always do."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark beyond our little circle, something large moved through the ferns, considered us, and moved on. The air remembered woodsmoke, and a woman's voice, and the particular silence of a house where everyone is asleep and loved.

"Do you think I'm him?" I asked quietly. "The heir. The bearer. Whatever you've been calling me in your head."

Isolde didn't stop sharpening the knife. "I think you're a memory-thief who happened to be standing at the right place when my father died, and that either the gods have a sense of humor or my father saw something in you that I haven't seen yet." She looked up. The firelight did complicated things to her face. "I'm hoping it's the second one, Wendell. I've been hoping it for seven years. Hoping is exhausting. Don't make me do it longer than I have to."

"I'll try to be efficient."

"That's the first joke you've made. It wasn't funny."

"It's been a long day."

Something that was almost, in poor light, at a great distance, a smile touched her mouth and died there. She rolled into her bedroll with her back to the fire, sword-hand out, the posture of a woman who had learned that the world collects debts while you sleep.

I stayed up. The stars here were wrong—more of them than over the Hollow, brighter, arranged in constellations that shifted subtly if you watched too long, like a crowd that knows it's being observed. Above the valley, the night had gone black and empty when the wards fell. Out here the stars still burned, and somehow that was worse. It meant the darkness over the Hollow wasn't the absence of stars. It was a curtain drawn over them. Someone had closed the door on my whole life and I'd lived thirty-one years without once noticing the draft.

The sealed memory hummed against my ribs. Closer to the border it had been a murmur. Now, out in the living world, it was louder—a second heartbeat, patient, waiting. I put my palm flat against it through my shirt.

*Soon,* I promised it, and hated myself for promising anything, because promises are just debts you volunteer for, and I had spent my entire life volunteering for nothing.

Across the dead fire, Isolde spoke without turning over. "Sleep, thief. Tomorrow we reach the roadhouse at Miller's Bend. There are people there I need you to meet."

"People?"

"People. Plural." A pause. "Try not to rob them."

I lay back against my pack, in a world that remembered everything, and stared up at stars I had no right to, and tried very hard not to think about what kind of man opens a door for a stranger carrying fire.

Somewhere south of us, I knew, riders were moving. I could feel them the way I felt loose seams everywhere now—a faint wrongness on the edge of the theft-sense, cold and purposeful, closing like a hand.

Three days, I told myself. We had three days.

The stars overhead rearranged themselves slightly, and did not answer.
