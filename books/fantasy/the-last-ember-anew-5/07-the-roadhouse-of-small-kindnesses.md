## 7. The Roadhouse of Small Kindnesses

Isolde smelled the roadhouse before she saw it—woodsmoke and onions, the particular sweetness of bread left to rise too long in a warm room. After three days of hard travel past the wards, with her boots worn through at the heel and Wendell flinching at every birdsong like it might be a hunter's whistle, the smell hit her like a hand catching her by the collar.

"Food," she said.

Wendell looked up from the road. He'd been walking with his head down for hours, which she'd learned was how he moved through the world—apologizing to the ground for existing on it. "You can smell food?"

"I can smell *stew*." She pointed down the slope, where a squat stone building hunched beside a crossroads, its chimney bleeding honest gray smoke into an evening sky that still made him stare. The stars here were real and numerous, and he kept looking at them the way other men looked at women they'd wronged. "Two days' walk from the Ashwood, if the map's right. We should eat something that isn't dried plums and regret."

"We don't have money."

She patted the pouch at her hip. Inside: her mother's silver hairpin, half a wheel of wax-sealed cheese, and the last of the traveler's tokens her father had sewn into her cloak lining years ago, back when he'd still believed she might need to run someday. She hadn't understood then why a man would prepare his daughter for flight. Now she carried his preparations like a second spine.

"He'd have liked this place," she said, before she could stop herself.

Wendell glanced at her. He had this habit of going very still when she mentioned her father—not out of discomfort, she was learning, but out of some strange attentiveness, as though her grief were a fire he was trying to learn to stand near without getting burned. It should have made her hate him less. It mostly just made her tired.

"The sign," he said quietly. "Look at the sign."

She looked. Above the door hung a wooden board carved with a single flame inside a ring of seven stones, painted in faded gold. Her breath caught.

"That's hearth-marking," she said. "Old covenant iconography. Nobody uses that anymore except—"

"—except places that want travelers to know they're safe." He said it flatly, but she heard what lived under it: wonder, and the reflexive suspicion of a man who had grown up somewhere safety was a wall rather than a door. "In the Hollow we had wards. Out here you have... signs?"

"Signs mean something," Isolde said. "Out here, a mark is a promise someone has to keep with their own two hands. No magic to hide behind." She started down the slope. "Come on. And Wendell—"

"Yes?"

"Don't steal anything."

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

* * *

The common room of the Waystation of Small Kindnesses—for that was its true name, lettered small beneath the flame—held perhaps a dozen travelers, a fire the size of a sleeping dog, and a smell so rich it made Isolde's knees consider mutiny. A broad woman with flour to her elbows took one look at them and jerked her thumb toward two stools at the long table.

"Stew's a copper or a kindness," she called over the noise. "Your choice."

"A kindness?" Wendell asked.

"You do something useful, you eat free. Chop wood, mend a fence, hold a baby, listen to old Marta there tell her story about the fish that got away—" the woman shrugged, ladle already moving, "—the fish gets bigger every telling, mind, but she tells it well. This is a house of exchange, not charity. Charity makes people small. Exchange makes them kin."

Isolde watched Wendell absorb that. She was beginning to be able to read his face now, which unsettled her more than she admitted—the way the thief's expressions flickered through calculations she couldn't see, arriving finally at something almost like awe.

"What counts as listening?" he asked.

"Sit. Nod. Don't correct her."

They ate. The stew was lamb and barley and root vegetables whose names Isolde didn't know, and it was so good that for several minutes neither of them spoke, which was itself becoming a kind of language between them—the silence of people too busy surviving to perform.

It was broken by the woman at the fire, who said loudly, to no one in particular, "If that hedge-witch hexes my kettle one more time, I'm feeding her soup to the pigs and letting her watch."

The hedge-witch in question sat at the corner of the hearth: a small, round woman wrapped in a coat that appeared to be made of at least four other coats, with iron-gray hair cropped short and eyes the green of pond water. Before her, on the floor, sat a dented copper kettle that was very slightly, unmistakably, humming.

"It wasn't a hex," the witch said with dignity. "It was a *blessing*. There's a difference. Hexes are faster."

"It was boiling my kettle without my leave."

"It was *encouraging* your kettle. There's a difference there too."

Isolde felt it before she understood it—that prickle along the arms, the sense of power banked low like coals under ash. Magic. Faint, domestic, stubborn magic, the kind her father had described as the hearth-magic of ordinary folk, the kind the Court called *untidy*. She set down her spoon.

Beside her, Wendell had gone rigid. She realized he was feeling it too—or feeling its absence, rather; a man raised in a warded valley, sitting three feet from live magic for the first time in his life, and trying very hard to look like he did this every day.

The witch noticed them noticing. Her eyes narrowed, traveled from Isolde's travel-ruined cloak to the pouch at her hip to Wendell's face—and stopped there, sharpening.

"Well now," she said. "You've got hollow hands, boy."

Wendell went white. "I don't—"

"Not empty. *Hollow.* Hands that take." She leaned forward, and the kettle's hum rose a note, protective or warning, hard to say. "I knew a memory-thief once. Back before my exile. They all have that look—like a man counting coins in another man's pocket."

"Bramble," the innkeeper warned. "House rules. No accusations before the second bowl."

"This is an observation, not an accusation. There's a difference—"

"There is *not* a difference and sit *down*."

To Isolde's surprise, the witch sat. But her eyes stayed on Wendell, bright and unblinking, and Isolde shifted on her stool so that she sat between them—a small movement, deliberate, and she saw the witch register it, and see her, really see her, for the first time.

"And who are you, girl? You've got the look of someone carrying something heavy and calling it luggage."

Isolde held her gaze. "My father died seven years ago at the edge of a warded valley. I'm finishing his errand."

Something changed in the witch's face. The sharpness didn't leave, but it made room for something else—recognition, maybe, or the memory of her own losses. "An errand," she repeated softly. "Those are always bigger than they sound." She extended a hand across the table, rings on four fingers, none of them matching. "Bramble Thornhollow. Hedge-witch, formerly of the Weald. Exiled, if the innkeeper hasn't made that clear. My crime was healing people the Court had decided should stay sick."

"That's a crime here?"

"That's the *best kind* of crime. The kind where you'd do it again." Bramble's grip was surprisingly strong. Then those pond-green eyes cut sideways to Wendell. "And him?"

"Wendell," Isolde said. "Of the Hollow. And..." She paused. She had spent seven years imagining this journey, and in none of her imaginings had she been honest about what she thought of her father's chosen carrier. "And he's the one who has to get us to the First Hearth alive. So try not to scare him off."

"Too late," Wendell murmured into his stew.

* * *

The fourth member of their table arrived the way weather arrives: gradually, then all at once.

He came in near dark, ducking through the door—a big man, big as a doorway himself, with a beard gone to salt and a burn scar covering the whole of his left forearm, old and smooth. Travelers made room for him without being asked. He nodded to the innkeeper, held up one finger, and settled onto the bench at the end of their table because it was the only seat left, which meant that Odo Vane—as Bramble would soon inform them, loudly—sat down among them by pure accident of arithmetic.

He ate in silence. He ate the way men eat who have eaten alone a long time: efficiently, gratefully, without ceremony. His cloak bore no sigil, but Isolde's eye caught the shape of something removed—a paler rectangle on the chest where a badge had once lived, sun-faded around the ghost of its absence.

"You were Order," she said.

The big man stopped chewing.

"Hearth-guardian," she pressed gently. "The pale patch. That's where the flame-badge sat."

He looked at her for a long moment. Up close his face was all weathered patience, like a wall that had decided against falling down. "Observant," he said at last. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"She's observant," Bramble agreed cheerfully. "I'm intrusive. Between us we'll have your whole sad story out by midnight."

Odo sighed—a sound like a millstone turning—and looked into his bowl. "Ten years I kept a beacon-shrine on the Graywatch pass," he said, apparently to the stew. "Kept the flame fed through two bad winters and one worse summer. Then a party of Court hunters came through chasing a family of ember-refugees. Children." His jaw worked. "The Order's law says the flame serves the Covenant, and the Covenant serves the peace, and the peace says don't interfere with lawful hunters." He flexed his scarred arm. "I interfered."

"They stripped your badge," Isolde said.

"They tried. It burned their hands. Flame remembers who feeds it." For the first time, the ghost of a smile crossed the ruined landscape of his face. "So they broke the shrine instead, and cast me out, and told me the flame would forget me within a year." He turned his wrist over; beneath the burn scar, faint as a brand seen through skin, glimmered a tiny mark like a spark. "Going on nine years now. Still waiting to be forgotten."

Nobody spoke. At the far end of the room, Marta began telling her fish story again, and somebody laughed, and the fire cracked, and Isolde felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched since the border.

"My father was hearth-sworn too," she said. "Not Order—older than that. Keeper-line. He used to say the flame doesn't care about badges or laws. Only about hands." She looked at her own hands, then, unavoidably, at Wendell's. "Only about what we choose to carry."

Odo studied her. Then Wendell. Then, with the unhurried attention of a man reading a battlefield, the whole table—Bramble's exile-rings, Isolde's worn cloak, the thief's careful emptiness.

"Where are you four going that you're sitting together looking like that?" he asked.

"The First Hearth," Isolde said.

The word landed on the room like a dropped plate. Conversations stuttered. Marta stopped mid-fish. The innkeeper's ladle went quiet in the pot.

Odo set down his spoon with great deliberation. "No," he said. "No, you're not."

"The wards fell a week ago. The stars went out above the Hollow. My father spent his life getting the keystone to the Hearth, and I intend to finish it."

"The Hearth is a ruin and a legend and a graveyard," Odo said flatly. "The Court hunts anything that walks toward it. There's a lord—Cassian—whose riders burn waystations that so much as shelter strangers heading east. I've seen the ash. I've walked through it." His voice didn't rise; that was the terrible thing about it. It just got heavier. "Whatever your father started, girl, it killed him. It'll kill you slower and worse."

"Then it's fortunate," said Bramble, refilling her bowl with the confidence of a woman who had never once been refused, "that he'll have a hedge-witch, a disgraced guardian, and a hollow-handed thief traveling with him."

Silence.

Everyone looked at Wendell.

Wendell, who had spent the entire evening folded small on his stool, watching them the way a starving man watches a lit window—she had seen it, had been unable *not* to see it, this hunger he thought he was hiding, this awful naked wanting to belong to something—looked up like a man caught stealing.

"I'm not part of anything," he said. "I'm cargo. I'm the thing being delivered."

"You're the keystone-bearer," Isolde said. "That's not cargo. That's a post."

"I stole the keystone."

"You *carried* it seven years without opening it, without selling it, without losing it. In the Hollow. Where you could have traded it for a life ten times better than the one you had." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had been sitting in her chest for days, this inconvenient fact, this splinter in her anger. "Thieves don't do that, Wendell. Thieves don't guard what they steal."

His mouth opened. Closed. He looked down at his hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

Odo grunted—the millstone again—and picked his spoon back up. "The Hollow," he said. "That's the warded valley. Wards need feeding. Old covenant work." He chewed. Swallowed. "If the wards fell, the keystone's gone cold. Which means the Hearth's the only forge left that can wake it."

"So you know the old rites," Isolde said, heart starting to pound.

"I know enough to know you'll die without someone who does." Another sigh, longer, the sound of a wall deciding, after nine years, to stop pretending it wasn't a door. "Graywatch taught me one thing. A flame you don't feed goes out. A flame you feed owns you forever." He looked around the table, at each of them in turn, and his eyes lingered longest on Wendell—with something that wasn't quite trust, but was perhaps its first cousin. "I'll walk as far as the Ashwood. After that we'll see who's still alive to argue about it."

"And I'm coming," Bramble announced, "because the Court wants me in irons, the Weald wants me in exile, and my knees want a quest. Also, someone has to protect that kettle-boiler of yours from doing something stupid with the keystone."

"It's sealed," Wendell said weakly.

"Sealed things are the most tempting things in the world, dear. Ask any cat."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to its patient red heart and the travelers had drifted up to their bunks, Isolde stood at the window of the room she shared with Bramble and watched the road.

Wendell was outside. She'd seen him slip out an hour ago, and some instinct she didn't like had drawn her to the glass. He sat on the chopping block by the woodpile, alone in the blue moonlight, turning his hands over and over—looking at his palms as though expecting to find the theft written there, some mark, some stain. Looking, she thought, the way Odo had looked at his wrist. Checking whether he'd been forgotten yet.

He hadn't touched anyone's memories since the border. She'd been watching for it, ready to hate him for it. He just... kept his hands closed, like a man holding water in a drought.

Behind her, Bramble stirred in her bunk. "You're brooding at the thief again."

"I'm *observing* the thief."

"Mm. Observing. There's a difference, I suppose."

Isolde smiled despite herself. Below, Wendell pulled his coat tighter and stared east, toward the Ashwood, toward whatever was coming for them out of its black trees.

Then he stiffened. Sat bolt upright. And Isolde followed his gaze to the ridgeline beyond the crossroads—where, thin and wrong against the stars, a smudge of light moved that was not a lantern, was not a campfire, was not anything a traveler should carry.

Three points of orange, moving in formation. Hunting-lights. Ember Court lanterns, the kind that burned only for a quarry already named.

Down in the yard, Wendell was on his feet, and even at this distance she could read his lips as he whispered the word they'd both been dreading:

"*Hunters.*"
