## 8. Hunters in the Ashwood

The Ashwood announced itself a mile before we reached it, by smell.

Char and old smoke, soaked into everything—the soil, the dead trunks, the air itself. Bramble wrinkled her nose and said it had burned eleven years back, in one of the border wars nobody in the Hollow ever heard about, and that nothing taller than bramble (she said this with some pride, as if the forest had named itself after her) had grown since. What stood now was a graveyard of black spires, thousands of them, stripped of bark, polished by weather into something almost beautiful. Bone-trees. That's what Odo called them, and he should know; he'd been walking with his head down since morning, and I was beginning to suspect it wasn't the road that bothered him.

We made camp early because Isolde wanted daylight to spare when we crossed the flats beyond. Small fire, screened by a ring of stones. Odo built it like a man performing surgery—no flame higher than his knuckles, no smoke worth naming.

I noticed because noticing is my trade. You can't lift a memory without reading the room first: where the fear sits, where the want sits, where the exits are. For seven years I'd read rooms in a valley of four hundred people who all knew my face and none of them knew my name for certain. I read this one too.

Odo kept his back to the fire. He faced the way we'd come.

"You expecting company?" I asked.

He didn't turn. "No."

"Good," I said. "Because you're watching the road like a man expecting company."

Bramble laughed around a mouthful of stew. Isolde didn't laugh at all. She looked at me across the flames, and I understood that she'd seen it hours ago and had been waiting to see if I would say anything. Testing me, maybe. Or testing him.

"The Court doesn't leave debts unpaid," Odo said finally. His voice was flat, the way stone is flat. "That's all you need to know."

"That's not all I need to know," I said, and immediately regretted it, because it sounded brave, and I am not brave, I am careful, those are different animals entirely.

Isolde stirred the pot. "Then tell us what we do need to know, Wendell."

And there it was again—that thing she did, saying my name like it was evidence in a trial. I was starting to think she enjoyed it.

But Odo answered before I could push. Maybe he wanted to spare me. Maybe he just wanted to stop pretending. Either way, he set down his spoon, and the firelight moved on his scarred face, and he told us.

"I was a hearth-guardian," he said. "Third rank, Ember Court, sworn to the First Flame before it died. My post was the western approach to the Hearth. Eleven years I stood it." He turned his hands over, palms up, and I saw the burns—not the clean kind you get from a cooking pot, but the kind you get from holding on to something that's trying to kill you. "The night the flame guttered, we were ordered to fall back. To let it die. There were orders from above, from Cassian's faction, sealed orders, and we were told the covenant was already broken, that guarding an empty hearth was sentiment, not duty." His jaw worked. "I didn't fall back."

"You held the door," Isolde said quietly.

"I held the door until they dragged me out of it. And then they held a tribunal, and the tribunal said I'd disobeyed a lawful order, and the lawful order was right, and my defiance cost the Court three good guardians burned for nothing." He looked up. "They didn't execute me. They did worse. They gave me my freedom. Struck my name from the rolls. Told me I was welcome to spend my life however I liked, knowing what I'd failed to save." A pause. "So now you know why I walk with my head down."

Nobody spoke. Bramble had stopped chewing. The stew went cold in the pot, and out in the dark the bone-trees creaked against each other like teeth.

It was me, of all people, who broke the silence.

"They'll come for you," I said. "Not for us. For you. Deserters go first, always—it keeps the rest in line." I knew how institutions hunted; I'd spent my life being invisible to one. "How long do we have?"

Odo's answer was interrupted by the dog.

*

I heard it before I saw it—a sound like a bell struck underwater, coming from the treeline to the north. Then the trees themselves began to move, or something between the trees did, low and fast and wrong, and Bramble was on her feet saying a word I didn't catch, and Isolde had a knife out that I hadn't known she carried.

"Hound," Odo barked. "Court hound. It tracks memory-scent. Down, all of you, douse the—"

The fire went out. Not doused—*taken*, snuffed like a candle between wet fingers, and standing where the light had been were six figures in ash-grey cloaks, and I knew their make even if I'd never seen it. The Hollow had rules about magic because the Hollow feared exactly this. Hunters. Ember Court hunters, come through the wards' ruin like water through a cracked dam.

Their leader stepped forward. Tall, grey-gloved, face hidden behind a mask of beaten copper shaped like a flame.

"Wendell Quintana." The voice behind the copper was dry and pleasant, which was somehow worse than shouting. "You've been sold, I'm afraid. Thoroughly and profitably. Hale does good work." A pause I could feel him smiling through. "Come quietly, give us the keystone, and the deserter keeps his head. The hedge-witch's mark will be honored. The girl walks free. Everyone profits. That's the Court's whole religion, really—everyone profits, so long as the ledger balances."

My legs were already calculating. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I have left. Behind me, downslope, there was a game trail—I'd marked it at dusk, thief's habit, always know your exits—and the hunters were facing north, toward the hound-sound, and I could be gone before anyone finished turning around. Gone, and alive, and alone, which had been my entire operating model for thirty-one years and had worked *fine*, thank you, right up until it hadn't.

For one terrible moment, I wanted it. Wanted it with my whole body, the way you want anything that costs someone else. Run, said the oldest part of me, the part raised in a valley that taught no questions and no leaving and therefore taught me both in secret. They're not your people. Four days. Four days is nothing. You've stolen years from strangers; you can steal these four days back and never owe anyone—

Then Isolde shifted, just slightly, putting herself between me and the copper mask. Without looking at me. Without hesitating. The girl whose father's last words I had taken, standing in front of the thief, ready to bleed.

And the wanting curdled into shame, and the shame into something harder.

"No," I said.

The masked leader tilted his head. "No?"

"He means no," Bramble snapped, and threw something—a pinch of something—from her sleeve, and the darkness bloomed white. Not light exactly; more like the *memory* of light, a flash of midsummer noon dumped into midnight, and the hunters staggered, and Odo moved.

I had seen Odo carry water. I had seen Odo pitch tents and mend straps and eat stew with the patience of an ox. What I saw then was another man wearing Odo's body. He came off the ground like a landslide, took the first hunter's spear-hand and folded it the wrong way, put a second into a tree hard enough to crack the trunk, and all of it silent, all of it economical, a big man spending violence the way misers spend gold. Third rank, Ember Court. I finally understood what that meant.

But six is six, and the hound was circling in from the north, and I could hear Bramble chanting through her teeth as her little stock of tricks ran dry, and Isolde fighting with that knife like she'd been taught by someone who expected her to survive.

Which left me, unremarkable Wendell Quintana, holding nothing but a satchel full of other people's lives.

Here is what no broker tells you about stolen memories: they keep. They keep the way fat keeps on a body through winter. I carried forty-odd of them—harvested carefully, ethically, I'd told myself, from people who mostly wouldn't miss one bright hour among hundreds. First snowfall on a widow's roof. A child's first taste of honey. A soldier's last morning before deployment. A wedding. A funeral. A hundred small human fires.

And the hound tracked memory-scent.

I fumbled the satchel open, chose blind, tore a vial free—one of Hale's glass phials, cold as a coin—and hurled it downslope away from us, into the bracken, and smashed it with a rock.

The memory went off like a bomb of pure *yesterday*. I don't know whose it was. It hit the night air and expanded—a summer kitchen, bread and woodsmoke, a woman laughing somewhere, rain starting on a tin roof—and the hound slammed to a halt as if it had run into a wall made of somebody's childhood. It began to circle the scent, whining, then digging, then howling at a ghost, because to a creature that eats memory, I had just dropped an entire banquet in the dirt.

The hunters faltered. Their beast was their compass, and their compass was pointing at bread and rain.

"NOW—" Odo roared, and it wasn't a word, it was a command in the old guardian-tongue, and the ash-grey cloaks flinched like schoolboys caught out after curfew, and that half-second was everything. Isolde cut a spear-line. Bramble threw her last flash and grabbed my collar. Odo swept the leader's legs and put a boot on the copper mask, gently, almost kindly, the way you'd pin a beetle you weren't going to crush.

"We're leaving," he said.

We left.

*

We ran until the bone-trees thinned and the ground turned to grey scrubland and the first pale suggestion of dawn sat on the horizon like an unpaid bill. Then we stopped, all of us bent double, breathing like bellows.

Bramble slid down a rock and started laughing—the unhinged, relieved laughter of someone who has very recently expected to die. "A *kitchen*," she wheezed. "Your hound-food was a *kitchen*. Six Court hunters and a tracking beast, routed by bread and a tin roof."

"It was effective," I said, with as much dignity as a man can muster while vomiting adrenaline.

Odo was checking the treeline. When he turned back, his face had changed—or rather, it had settled into something I hadn't seen on him before. Not softness. Acknowledgment.

"You could have run," he said to me.

Everyone went quiet. Even the wind seemed to wait.

"There was a trail," he went on. "Behind you, downslope. I watched you look at it. I've stood tribunals, boy; I know what a man looks like when he's measuring the cost of staying." He crossed his arms. "You stayed."

I thought about lying. It would have been easy. Lying is just theft with better manners.

"For about three seconds," I said. "Then I stopped."

"Why?"

And I looked at Isolde, because the true answer was standing right there with a knife and her father's grief, and I couldn't say any of it. So I said the second-truest thing instead.

"Because I'm tired," I said, "of being the only person in every room."

Odo grunted—the guardian equivalent of a speech, I was learning—and turned away to stand watch, and that was that. But Bramble caught my eye and grinned, and Isolde, wringing out her sleeve where a spear had grazed it, looked at me for a long moment and said nothing at all, which from her was nearly a blessing.

We walked east into the greying light, toward the flats and whatever waited past them. My satchel felt lighter by one memory. I found I didn't miss it.

I found, in fact, that I was counting what remained—and for the first time in my life, not to price it.

We made maybe two miles before Odo stopped so abruptly that Bramble walked into his back.

He was staring at the horizon, at a smudge rising off the eastern flats that might have been dust and might have been smoke. His hand went to the axe he'd taken off a hunter, and his voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"That's not riders," he said. "Riders don't travel under a standard. Somebody's brought the Court's *banner*."
