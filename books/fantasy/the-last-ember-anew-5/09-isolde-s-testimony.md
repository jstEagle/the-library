## 9. Isolde's Testimony

The fire Bramble built was small and mean, a fistful of flame crouched between stones, because the Ashwood was still watching us from the dark. Burned trees stood all around like the ribs of something enormous that had died standing up, and the ash came down soft as snow when the wind moved. Odo took first watch without being asked. He sat with his back to a fallen trunk, his old guardian's axe across his knees, and he did not look at us. He had a way of not looking at people that made you feel more seen than if he stared.

Isolde waited until we'd eaten. That was deliberate. I knew it was deliberate the way you know a storm is coming—not by any single sign but by the whole pressure of the air.

She sat across the fire from me, turning her bowl in her hands. She'd barely touched the stew. "My father left home on the eve of the harvest moon," she said.

Bramble looked up. Odo's shoulders went still.

"He didn't tell my mother where he was going. He told her there was debt to settle—old family debt—and that he'd be back before the frost. She believed him. She always believed him." Isolde turned the bowl again. "He kissed her forehead. He kissed mine. I was eleven. I remember thinking his beard smelled like woodsmoke and that I wanted him to hurry up and go so I could get back to my game." Her voice didn't shake. Somehow that was worse than shaking. "That was the last time I saw him."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the black, a branch settled with a sound like a sigh.

"He traveled south," she went on. "We know because of the waystation ledgers—he paid for passage at three crossings, always under a false name, always moving alone. Whatever he carried, he didn't trust anyone with it. Not even the guardians." A glance at Odo here, quick and hard. "Not even the order sworn to protect the Hearth."

"I've told you what I know of that," Odo said quietly. "The Court sealed the records. We were told the covenant was stable. We were told the ember needed no tending."

"You were told lies, and you repeated them for twenty years."

"Yes," Odo said. "I did." He said it without flinching, and Isolde looked away first.

She set the bowl down. "By the time he reached your valley, Wendell, he was already wounded. The Court's hunters caught up with him at the border. He'd been running for days. And he did the only thing left—he pressed what he carried into the hands of a stranger and died at the edge of a ward-line that wouldn't let him bring it home."

Every eye at the fire was on me now. Even Odo's, finally.

"I want to hear it from you," Isolde said. "Not the version where you're clever. The true one. What happened that night."

And because there was no way out of it—because I had run out of roads that didn't lead through this—I told them.

* * *

"It was raining," I said. "It rains a lot in the Hollow in autumn, cold rain that comes sideways off the ridge. I was out past the boundary stones because that's where the desperate ones go—the travelers who get turned around by the wards and end up circling the valley like moths at a lamp. They carry good memories, travelers. First flights. Weddings. The last conversation they ever had with someone they loved."

Bramble made a small sound. I kept going, because stopping would have been worse.

"I found him against an oak near the eastern stones. He was gut-wounded—hunter's blade, I knew the cut even then, though I didn't know whose hunters. He was trying to say something and couldn't get it out, blood in his throat. So he stopped trying to talk and he did something else instead."

I looked at my hands. They looked like ordinary hands. They weren't.

"He took my wrist. And he *gave*. I'd never felt anything like it—a memory handed over freely, no theft in it at all, just... a door opening. Sealed. Heavy. It hummed against my sense like nothing I'd ever lifted. Like holding a bell that hadn't been rung yet." I swallowed. "Then he said one word I could make out through the blood. *Keep.* And he died."

"Did you try to save him?" Isolde asked. Flat. Precise. A surgeon's question.

"No."

The word fell into the fire and burned.

"I checked him for coin. I took the memory. I went home and slept." My voice came out steady, which shamed me more than weeping would have. "In the morning I told myself he'd been dying anyway. I tell myself things like that. It's how I sleep."

"Do you?" she asked. "Sleep?"

"No," I said. "Not really."

* * *

Isolde rose. She crossed the fire-circle slowly, ash drifting through the light around her, and stood over me, and I understood that whatever came next she had carried for seven years and rehearsed in the dark of a hundred nights, and none of that rehearsal would make it easier for either of us.

"My father's memories," she said. "You have them. All of them. Every hour of his life that fit inside his head."

"The keystone is sealed. I never opened it. I swear that."

"I don't doubt the seal." She crouched so we were level, her eyes catching the firelight like coins at the bottom of a well. "Here is what you don't understand, thief. When a person dies, their memories die with them—unless they're taken. You took his. Which means every memory of me that existed in this world after that night lived inside your skull. His voice. His face. The way he said my name. The words he spoke at our table, the jokes, the scoldings, the songs—I have spent seven years forgetting him, Wendell, forgetting a little more every season, the way the living do. And the entire time, the complete and perfect record of who he was has been sitting in the head of a stranger who uses other people's lives as *coin.*"

"I never sold him. I never touched it—"

"You stole him!" It tore out of her, raw, and the fire leapt. "You stole my father twice. Once when you took the memory and once when you let the man who gave it to you bleed into the mud while you counted what it might fetch. Do you know what his last words to me were? Because I'll tell you: I don't. Nobody does. They died with him—except they didn't, did they? They're in *you.* You are walking around wearing my family's last evening like a coat you haven't decided to sell yet."

The camp had gone absolutely silent. Odo had risen halfway; Bramble had a hand on his arm. Out beyond the firelight, the Ashwood held its breath.

And I— I sat there with shame filling me the way water fills a boot, slow and total and impossible to walk in.

Because she was right. Not mostly right. Entirely right. I had built a whole life on the arithmetic of taking—take small, take clean, take from those who could spare it—and I had never once, not one time in all my years, asked what the taking cost on the other side of the transaction. Hale paid me in silence. The broker paid me in scrip. Nobody ever paid me in the thing these people were paying each other right now, freely, over a shared fire, and nobody ever showed me the bill.

"There's something else," I heard myself say.

Isolde stilled.

"When he gave it to me—it wasn't just the memory. There was—" My throat closed. I forced it. "There was a feeling underneath it. Underneath the seal. Something he wrapped around it before he died. I've never opened the door, but I can feel what's painted on it. Seven years, and I've never been able to unfeel it." I looked up at her. "It wasn't fear. It wasn't desperation. It was *hope*, Isolde. He trusted me. A stranger in the rain, and he trusted me with the most important thing in two worlds, and I have spent seven years being unworthy of it."

The fire popped. Ash drifted down between us like gray snow.

"So confess," she said. "If confession is what you have. Give it properly."

So I did. All of it, or as much as language could hold. The widow's first snowfall, lifted two days ago, sold before dawn. The bride's wedding joy. The soldier's homecoming. The mother's lullaby, taken from a woman too grief-drunk to notice, sold to a man who played it for amusement at parties. Years of them. Hundreds. I named the craft for what it was—not survival, not cleverness, just theft with better manners—and I watched Isolde listen with a face like carved stone and I understood that no punishment she could devise would weigh as much as the listening itself.

When I finished, the silence went on so long I thought she'd simply leave.

Then she said, quietly: "The scholar Maren will know whether the seal can be opened without destroying what's inside. If my father's words can be saved, you will give them to me. Not sell. *Give.*"

"Yes."

"And if the keystone requires a keeper on the road ahead—if carrying it means facing what the Court sends—you will carry it. Not because you owe a debt. Debts are just prices with extra steps. Because you *choose* it."

I thought about running. I thought about it honestly, the way I'd thought about it in the Ashwood that afternoon—the treeline, the darkness, the old habit of invisibility pulling at me like a tide. Then I looked at Odo, who had defended people who lied to him. At Bramble, who had traded her exile-mark for strangers' passage. At Isolde, who had spent seven years walking toward a thief because hope was the only inheritance her father left her.

They stayed. That was the miracle I kept failing to comprehend. They stayed.

"I choose it," I said.

Isolde studied me a long moment, hunting for the lie, finding none—or finding it and deciding it didn't matter anymore. Then she returned to her side of the fire and picked up her untouched stew, and the circle exhaled, and Bramble started talking too fast about wards and watch-rotations to fill the terrible quiet.

I lay back against my pack and looked up through the burned branches at the starless sky, and I did not sleep, but for the first time in seven years it wasn't the keystone humming against my sense that kept me awake.

Near dawn, Odo shook me gently by the shoulder. He pointed east, down the slope, where the ash-thinned trees opened onto a long pale vista.

Far off, on the horizon, a city glowed—towers lit gold and silver against the dark, beautiful and wrong, like a lantern hung over a trap.

"Vespertine," Odo said. "City of yesterday. Two days' walk, if the road allows it."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable on the wind, came the baying of hounds.
