## 10. The City That Sells Yesterday

We smelled Vespertine before we saw it.

Not smoke, not sewage—though the city had plenty of both. It was sweeter than that, cloying as overripe fruit. Isolde called it *leakage*, the scent of memories seeping out of a hundred thousand skulls and into the air. "The rich buy fresh ones," she said. "Fresh ones sweat."

The road crested a hill and there it was: a city built in tiers like an amphitheater, every level looking down on the one below, all of it lit from within by lamps that burned amber and violet and a green I had no name for. Beyond the Hollow's wards, I'd spent weeks learning how much light the world could hold. Vespertine held too much. It held light the way a miser holds coin—not to use, but to prove it could.

At the gate, a customs officer waved us through after glancing at Bramble's papers. No one checked our heads. Why would they? In Vespertine, what was inside your skull was nobody's business but yours—and whoever you sold it to.

I had been inside the trade my whole adult life. I thought I knew it.

I didn't know it at all.

* * *

Hale's shop in the Hollow was a back room with a ledger and a lamp. Memory-brokers here had storefronts. Marble facades, brass doors, doormen in livery. The biggest of them—Vance & Sons, Established Four Centuries Past—had a window display, and I stopped dead in front of it because I couldn't make myself walk.

Glass cases. Inside them, little vials of silvered liquid, each one labeled in a fine hand.

*A first kiss, 1789 vintage.*

*The smell of your mother's kitchen.*

*Your wedding day, complete.*

They were arranged like perfume. Like jewelry. A clerk caught me staring and smiled with professional warmth. "Looking for something specific, sir? We have a remarkable acquisition this week—a soldier's memory of coming home, unopened, provenance verified."

"Where does he get them?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded far away.

"The soldier? Oh, consignment, sir. Families sell, sometimes. Debts get settled." He said it the way you'd say *the tide comes in*. "Though if you're selling, I should tell you our rates are the best on the Amber Tier. Childhood runs especially high this season. Nostalgia's up among the gentry."

Childhood runs high. Nostalgia's up.

I walked away without answering. Behind me, Isolde watched me with that flat gray gaze of hers, the one that missed nothing and forgave less. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Everything she suspected about me was standing in a glass case with a price tag.

We were looking for Scholar Maren—a covenant-scholar, Odo said, one of three left in the world who could read the old seals. Maren kept rooms somewhere in the Low City, which was the polite name for the bottom tier, where the city's runoff collected. To get there we had to cross the middle, and the middle was where I started to understand what I had been, all these years.

* * *

The middle tier was called the Exchange, and the Exchange ran on debt.

Not coin-debt. Coin was almost quaint there. The currency was time inside your own head, and there were men at corners—clean men, quiet men, with ledgers chained to their wrists—who would advance you a loan against memories you hadn't decided to sell yet.

A woman sat outside one such office with her shopping basket tipped over, onions rolling into the gutter. She wasn't crying. That was the worst part. She was just sitting, moving her lips slightly, like someone searching a house for keys they'd already sold.

Bramble knelt beside her. "What's wrong, love?"

"I came to pay," the woman said. "I always pay on the ninth." A pause, long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. "What was I paying?"

"She's a two-time," the lender said behind us, not unkindly. He was maybe thirty and had the eyes of a much older man—eyes with nothing behind them, windows into a swept room. "Second default. The clause lets us collect early." He adjusted his cuff. "She'll stabilize. Most of them stabilize. First week's rough."

"What did you take?" Isolde asked him, and her voice had a blade in it.

He looked at her with mild surprise, the way you'd look at someone asking what a butcher took from the pig. "Whatever clears the balance. Her mother, mostly. Some of her daughter—the girl emigrated years back, so the sentiment had depreciated." He said *depreciated* about a woman's daughter. Then he frowned, faintly, like a man trying to remember why he'd come upstairs. "Was there something else? I have appointments."

He walked away into his office, and I understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that he probably didn't remember his appointments either. Or his mother. Or whether he'd ever had a daughter. The lenders ate their own inventory. Somewhere down the line of that office, back through the years, there had been a boy with a whole head full of tomorrow, and the trade had stripped it for parts until nothing remained but the arithmetic.

In the Hollow, I told myself, it was different. In the Hollow I took grief off people who wanted rid of it. A widow's first snowfall—she'd begged me, practically. *Take it, take it, I can't carry it anymore.* And Hale paid fair, and nobody got hollowed out. Small kindnesses. Careful work.

Standing on the ninth-tier stairs watching that woman gather her onions, I did the math I'd been avoiding for seven years.

Someone bought the widow's snowfall. Someone bought it because it was beautiful, because it was cheap, because it made their empty evening feel full for ten minutes before it faded. And when it faded they bought another, and another, and the widow forgot winter and the buyer never learned it, and between the two of them stood a thief in a warded valley telling himself he was doing charity.

I wasn't a small cog. Cogs don't get to pretend. I was a cog that lied to itself, which is somehow worse.

"Wendell." Isolde's hand closed on my arm. "Keep walking. Staring gets us noticed."

"I used to think—" My throat wouldn't finish the sentence. I tried again. "I only ever took what people offered. I told myself that made it clean."

"And here?"

"Here I'm just further down the river," I said, "finding out where it goes."

* * *

The Low City was darker and louder and more alive than anything above it, and it was also where the blanks lived.

You saw them in doorways and along the canal walls. People whose ledgers had finally cleared everything—every memory above a certain weight, sold or seized, leaving only the dregs. They weren't violent or strange. That was the horror of it. They were placid. They sat in the sun. When you passed, some of them smiled, because smiling was one of the last things they still owned.

One of them was a child.

Maybe nine years old, sitting on a step, sorting pebbles by color with total absorption. An older woman—her grandmother, I guessed, from the fierce way she planted herself between the child and the street—watched everyone who passed with the expression of a dog guarding a bone.

Bramble slowed. "That little one's blank."

"Debt inherited," Odo said quietly. His big hands were fists at his sides. "Father defaulted. Court law says the estate settles, and the estate includes dependents' holdings." He spat. "I served twenty years guarding hearths and I never once guarded *this*."

The child looked up at us and smiled, sweet and vacant, and offered me a pebble. A blue one. The best one, clearly, by whatever system governed pebbles.

For a moment I couldn't move. Because I knew—I *knew*—what it was to look at the world with holes in yourself where things should be. I'd spent my life filling other people's holes and ignoring my own. This child had no holes. Holes imply edges, a shape of what was lost. This child was smooth all the way through, a river stone of a person, and the family that had loved her was a debt someone else had settled.

I took the pebble. I don't know why. It seemed important not to refuse.

"There are scholars in the Low City," Isolde said, pulling me onward. Her voice was controlled, but her jaw was white. "Maren chose the Low City on purpose. Remember that. Whatever else this place is, remember somebody chose the bottom."

* * *

We found the scholar's door at dusk: a narrow townhouse wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered memory-house, its windows dark except one, its door bearing a symbol I recognized from the keystone's seal—a flame inside a circle, broken by a single vertical line.

Odo knocked the pattern he'd described: two, pause, three. The door opened on a chain. One eye, sharp and brown, surveyed us.

"We're closed," said a woman's voice. Dry as paper. "Permanently. Go bother the University."

"We need a seal read," Isolde said. "A covenant seal. First Flame era."

Silence. The eye moved from face to face: Isolde's iron calm, Bramble's exile-mark half-hidden under her collar, Odo's guardian's badge turned backward so the sigil faced his chest—he hadn't been able to stop wearing it, even disgraced. Then the eye found mine, and stayed.

"You're carrying something," the woman said slowly. "Something sealed. I can feel it from here. Gods below, I haven't felt that pressure since—" The chain rattled off. The door swung wide. "Inside. Quickly. And if anyone followed you, lie to them convincingly; I've no taste for dying this decade."

Scholar Maren was sixty-ish and built like a question mark, all elbows and stoop, with ink stains up both forearms to the elbow. Her front room was floor-to-ceiling books, most of them damaged, some of them visibly burned. She lit three lamps, looked us over again, and said:

"Now. Show me what you stole."

"Not stole," Isolde began.

"Everything in this city is stolen," Maren said, waving at the walls. "The question is only whether it was stolen *well*." Her gaze came back to me. "Well, boy? Which are you?"

And there it was—the question I'd been walking toward since the gate. Every horror of the day stacked up behind it: the vials in the window, the two-time woman with her onions, the lender's swept-room eyes, the child with the pebble. Seven years of careful invisibility, of *small trades*, of telling myself the valley was a mercy and the work was a kindness.

I set the wrapped keystone on her table. My hands were shaking, and for once I let them.

"I stole it from a dying man," I said. "Seven years ago. I didn't know what it was. I never opened it. And today I finally understood that not knowing was the last comfort I had any right to."

Maren unwrapped the cloth with fingers that trembled less than mine. When the seal showed—flame in circle, line through it—she went very still.

Then she laughed, once, without any humor in it at all.

"Do you know what you've been carrying, boy? Do you know what this is worth?" She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Half this city would burn the rest of it down for this. And I mean that literally—it's happened before."

"That's why we brought it to you," Isolde said. "Can you read it?"

"I can read it." Maren drew the lamps closer. "But reading is the easy part, and it's going to cost you more than coin." She glanced around at all of us, and her voice dropped. "Because I'm not the only one who can feel this thing. And something tells me that by morning, we're going to have company."

Outside, far above us in the amber-lit tiers, a bell began to ring—an alarm cadence, three and three and three.

Odo was on his feet before the second peal. "Court riders," he said grimly. "That's a sanction bell. Someone's claimed legal authority in Vespertine tonight."

Isolde looked at me, and I already knew what her eyes were saying: *Hale. It was always going to be Hale.*

Maren snuffed two of the lamps. "Right," she said. "Let's find out how much time you have left—and then, boy, you and I are going to talk about exactly what your hands have touched. Because if the seal is what I think it is, the only person in this room who can open it safely is someone with *empty hands*—and I very much doubt that describes a memory-thief."

The bell rang on. Somewhere above us, doors were being kicked in.

I put my palms flat on the table and felt the keystone hum against my skin like a locked door—and for the first time, I was afraid not of what was inside it, but of what opening it might demand of me.
