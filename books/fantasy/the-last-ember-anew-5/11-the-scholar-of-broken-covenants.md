## 11. The Scholar of Broken Covenants

Scholar Maren's tower smelled of burnt rosemary and old paper, and Isolde decided within the first minute that she did not trust the woman who lived in it.

Not because Maren had done anything wrong. Because she was *happy* to see them. In Isolde's experience, people who were happy to see strangers wanted something, and people who wanted something usually wanted what you were carrying.

"You brought it sealed," Maren said. She hadn't asked their names yet. She was circling Wendell like a hawk deciding whether a rabbit was worth the dive, her eyes fixed on his satchel. "Unopened. Seven years in a thief's keeping and you never once—"

"I don't open things I can't put back," Wendell said.

Isolde watched him say it. He stood near the door with his shoulders drawn up around his ears, the way he always stood in rooms that belonged to other people, as if he expected to be thrown out of any room he entered. The keystone sat in its oilcloth wrapping between them on the table, humming that low, wrong note it hummed when anyone got too close—a sound like a struck bell heard through water.

Maren stopped circling. "May I?"

"Ask him," Isolde said. "It's his."

She meant it as a small cruelty. She wasn't sure anymore whether she meant it to sting or simply to be true. It had been seven days since the fire at their camp, seven days since she'd told Wendell exactly what she thought he'd taken from her father—the last words, the last breath, the whole small country of a dying man's final hours—and he hadn't argued. He'd just sat there with his hands open on his knees and said, *You're right. I took more than the memory. I took your goodbye.* She had wanted him to argue. Arguing would have made it easier to hate him.

He hadn't argued, and now she kept catching herself watching him the way you watch weather, trying to decide if it was turning.

Wendell unwrapped the keystone without touching it, folding back the cloth until the thing lay bare on the table: a memory, but not shaped like one. Memories, when they were pulled loose, looked like drops of light, like beads of colored glass. This was a sphere the size of an apple, dark at the center and burning at every edge, and the seal wrapped around it was not wax or thread but script—characters written so fine they might have been etched by a needle made of starlight, spiraling from pole to pole without beginning or end.

Maren leaned over it and went very still.

"Where did you get this?" she whispered.

"A dying man gave it to me," Wendell said. "At the edge of the Hollow."

"No." Maren's head came up. Her eyes had gone bright and frightened. "No one *gave* you this. This is a covenant-seal. Do you know how many of these exist in all the records of both realms?" She held up one finger. "One. This one. The First Flame's own spark, bound by the Hearth-wardens themselves, before the theft, before the sundering. Every scholar for three hundred years has believed it destroyed with the old Hearth. And you—" she laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, "—you've been using it as a paperweight in a valley with no magic."

"It doesn't work in the Hollow," Wendell said. "Nothing does."

"That is the only reason it survived," Maren said. "Do you understand? The Court has been searching for this since the night the stars went out. If it had ever crossed the wards lit—if anyone with a drop of talent had touched it unsealed—it would have burned a signal across the sky that Cassian could read from Vespertine." She wiped her hands on her robe, suddenly, compulsively, as though she'd been handling something hot. "You may be the luckiest man alive, thief. Or the most cursed. I haven't decided."

Isolde pulled out a chair and sat down heavily. Hope was a dangerous thing to feel in front of a stranger; it showed on your face like a wound. But it was rising in her anyway, hot and stupid, because *the First Flame's spark*, here, real, in the hands of the one person in two realms stubborn enough and graceless enough to carry it where it needed to go.

"Can you open it?" she asked.

Maren didn't answer right away. She fetched a lens on a brass arm, a book chained to the desk, a second lens, and spent a long quarter hour reading the spiral script through the glass while the three of them stood in a silence broken only by the tick of the lens-arm and the distant bells of Vespertine counting out the hours below.

Finally she straightened, and Isolde saw the expression on her face, and hope curdled.

"Yes and no," Maren said.

"I hate yes-and-no," Bramble said from the corner, where she'd been pretending not to listen while ostensibly cataloguing jars. Odo stood beside her, arms folded, saying nothing, which was how Odo listened best.

"The seal is a covenant-lock," Maren said. "It isn't a lock the way your door is a lock—it's a lock the way a vow is a lock. It will only open under specific conditions, and it reads intent, not mechanism. You cannot pick it. You cannot force it. Believe me, the Ember Court tried for a century after the sundering—they found fragments of the ritual texts, they built engines, they sacrificed—well. They tried." Her mouth thinned. "Every forced attempt shatters what's inside. Not breaks the container. Destroys the spark itself. The flame doesn't survive coercion. That's rather the entire moral of the covenant, if you ask me, though nobody at the Court ever asks me anything."

"So there's a key," Isolde said. "There's always a key. A word, a bloodline, a place—my father carried this thing across half a map to deliver it somewhere. Tell me where and we'll take it there."

"There is no key," Maren said gently. "There is a condition. The seal opens for empty hands and no others."

The words dropped into the room and sat there.

"Empty hands," Odo repeated. His voice was gravel over something older.

"It's in the script itself." Maren turned the lens toward them. "Look. The spiral isn't decoration—it's a question, asked over and over, tightening inward. *What do you carry? What do you carry? What do you carry?* At the center, the answer it will accept: *nothing.* Bring it everything, and it stays shut until you die and it passes on unopened—that's why it could sit in a valley for seven years. Bring it anything held by greed, by duty, by debt, by fear—and the moment you try, it burns to ash. Only someone carrying nothing—not a stolen coin, not an owed favor, not a single memory that isn't theirs—can carry the First Flame home."

Isolde felt the floor tilt under her, very slightly, the way it does when a ship changes course.

Because she knew what Wendell carried.

Everyone at the table knew. He'd told them himself, around fires, in the flat toneless voice he used for confessions: years of taken memories hoarded in his mind like a miser's coins. First snowfalls and wedding days and a widow's grief, dozens of them, hundreds maybe—he'd never given a full count, and Isolde suspected he didn't know it himself anymore, that somewhere along the way the hoard had grown past counting. He carried other people's lives the way a pack mule carries salt. It was the whole shape of him. It was what he *was*.

And the flame required a man with nothing.

She looked at him. He was looking at the keystone, and his face was doing something complicated and quiet that she couldn't read—or that she could read too well. It was the face of a man hearing that the door he'd walked seven hundred miles toward opened only onto the one thing he could not do.

"Then it can't be him," Isolde said.

Everyone turned.

"He's a thief," she said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and pushed through it, because if she didn't say it now it would fester. "That's not an insult, it's a fact. His hands are the least empty hands in either realm. He's been filling them since he was a boy. If he tries to open that seal, it dies, and then the stars stay dead forever, and Cassian wins, and everything my father bled for—" She stopped. Breathed. "We need someone else. Someone clean. There must be someone."

"There is no someone else," Maren said. "That's the other thing the seal says. Read the outer ring." She traced it with a stylus, not quite touching. "*Given, not taken. Chosen, not sent.* The spark goes home the way it left—carried by a hand that gives instead of takes, by choice, freely. It doesn't want purity. Purity isn't the point. It wants surrender." She looked at Wendell. "You are the only person alive who knows what these things cost to take. Which means you're the only person alive who understands what it means to give them back."

"That's poetry," Wendell said. His voice was steady. Isolde noticed his hands weren't. "Poetry doesn't open locks."

"Covenant-locks aren't locks," Maren said. "They're poems that bite."

Silence again. Down in the city, the evening bells rang the third hour past dusk. Somewhere below, ten thousand people were buying and selling pieces of yesterday, and Isolde thought about the children she'd seen in the memory-markets, blank-eyed and rented out, and thought: *this is what a world looks like when everyone's hands are full.*

Wendell reached toward the keystone. Stopped. His fingers hovered an inch above the burning edge of the seal, close enough that the light gilded his knuckles.

"Don't," Isolde said sharply.

"I'm not going to open it." He withdrew his hand. He almost smiled; it didn't hold. "I wanted to see if it flinched."

"It's not alive."

"Everything in this world is alive," Wendell said. "The air remembers. The roads sing. I've spent my life around dead things pretending to be objects. I know the difference now." He wrapped the keystone back in its oilcloth with slow, careful movements, and Isolde realized he was handling it differently than he had a week ago. Before, he'd carried it like contraband. Now he carried it like something entrusted.

That terrified her more than Cassian did.

* * *

They didn't leave the tower that night. Maren fed them soup that tasted mostly of rosemary and spread her charts across the floor, and the conversation turned practical—routes to the First Hearth, the mountain passes, the week of travel, Cassian's riders, Bramble's still-healing shoulder. Isolde let the talk wash over her and watched Wendell watch the keystone.

Near midnight, Maren cornered her on the stair landing with two cups of something bitter and steaming.

"Your father," Maren said. "Aldric. Tall, grey before his time, argued with everyone about everything."

Isolde's chest tightened. "You knew him."

"Forty years ago, when we were both young and the Court still pretended to tolerate scholars. He was the one who convinced me the covenant could be reforged at all. Everyone else said the Flame was gone-gone, myth-gone. Aldric said, *it's not gone, it's waiting, and waiting is what flames do.*" Maren sipped her drink. "He came to me the night before he crossed into your valley, you know. Asked me to decipher a seal I'd only seen in drawings. I told him what I've told you tonight. Empty hands. He laughed and said, *then I'll have to find someone whose pockets I wouldn't trust and make them honest.*"

Isolde stared at her. "He knew. He knew he'd have to—"

"He knew whoever ended up carrying it would have to give up everything they were carrying. He thought that was funny." Maren's eyes were wet, and she blinked the wetness away with the brisk irritation of a woman who resented crying. "Your father planned his death better than most men plan their weddings, child. He chose your valley because the wards would hide the spark. He chose a stranger because a stranger owes him nothing and wants nothing from him. And whatever he pressed into that thief's hands besides the keystone—I suspect I know what it was, and I suspect it wasn't treasure."

Isolde's throat worked. "His last words."

"If I'm right, then those words weren't taken from you." Maren set down her cup. "They were *sent*. To the one person who could carry them somewhere they'd matter. Your father didn't lose his goodbye, Isolde. He posted it. Seven years in transit." She patted Isolde's arm once, hard, the way she probably patted books she liked, and went back up the stairs.

Isolde stayed on the landing a long time, holding a cup she didn't remember taking, feeling the ground of her anger shift beneath her like sand at low tide.

* * *

She found Wendell on the roof.

Of course he was on the roof. He was always on the roof, or the highest ledge, or the window—somewhere with sky. She'd noticed it weeks ago and filed it away: a man raised under wards, under a fake painted ceiling of a sky, who now treated open air like a religion. Tonight the stars were out, thick and cold, and he sat against the chimney with the keystone in his lap, not touching it, just sitting companionably beside it like two travelers sharing a bench.

She sat down next to him. Neither of them spoke for a while. That was another thing she'd learned about him: he didn't fill silence. Most thieves talked constantly, chatter as misdirection. Wendell treated silence like shelter.

"Maren talks in her sleep," Wendell said finally. "Did you know that? Something about the outer ring. She's been muttering equations at it for six hours through the floor."

"She told me about my father."

Wendell went still in a new way. "Yeah?"

"He knew what the seal required. He chose you on purpose." Isolde kept her eyes on the stars because it was easier than his face. "He thought it was funny."

Wendell exhaled slowly. "Of course he did." A pause. "Isolde. If it comes to it—if we get there and it turns out I'm the only one who can even *try*—the seal will kill the spark the moment I touch it. My hands aren't empty. They've never been empty. I don't think they *can* be." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. It sounded like a man reporting the weather at the end of the world.

"Then we find a way to empty them," Isolde said.

"You've seen what's in my head. You know what giving them back would mean. Some of those people are dead. Some of those memories exist nowhere else in the world except in me. If I pour them out—" He shook his head. "That's not emptying pockets. That's killing parts of myself that are the only copies."

"Then don't do it for the seal," Isolde said. "Do it because it's yours to do. That's what the script says, isn't it? Given, not taken. Chosen, not sent." She finally looked at him. Starlight put hollows under his cheekbones and made him look younger and more frightened than he ever allowed himself to look indoors. "Nobody's asking you to be pure, Wendell. Maren said it herself. They're asking you to let go."

"And if I can't?"

Down in Vespertine, a memory-debtor's bell rang somewhere in the dark streets, thin and desperate. Above them, the stars burned on, borrowed light from dead things, holding the sky together out of habit.

"Then we lose everything," Isolde said. "But you won't. I've watched you for seven hundred miles. You keep saying your hands are full, but every mile, you've been putting things down. You just haven't noticed yet."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, startled breath of it, gone as fast as it came, like a man surprised by his own reflection.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said. "For about four seconds on that landing tonight, I wanted to try it anyway. Just reach out and see. Prove I'm not—" He stopped. Started over. "There's a version of me that grabs first and grieves later. He's been driving my whole life. And he really, genuinely thought he could steal from a covenant."

"What changed his mind?"

Wendell glanced down at the keystone glowing faintly through its wrappings, then at her.

"He met people who'd notice if he ran."

They sat with that until the cold drove them inside. And neither of them said the other thing—the thing hanging unspoken over the roof, over the tower, over the whole road east:

Three days later, a rider in ember-livery would arrive at the gates of Vespertine bearing Lord Cassian's seal and a legal writ naming four fugitives and one stolen flame—and the hunt would begin in earnest.
