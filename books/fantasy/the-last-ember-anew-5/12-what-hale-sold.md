## 12. What Hale Sold

The trouble with Vespertine, Wendell decided on their third morning there, was that it looked like home if you squinted.

That was the horror of it. He'd spent the first two days telling himself the city was alien—memory-debtors chained to ledgers, rich men with eyes like scraped plates, children in the pawn-shops with that terrible blankness behind their faces. Alien. Other. Nothing to do with him. But on the third morning, standing outside a memory-broker's stall in the Lanternmarket while Isolde argued with a map-seller, he watched a woman lift a memory from her own mother—the last time they'd laughed together—and trade it for bread money, and he felt nothing but recognition. The practiced hands. The careful invisibility. The little lie at the end: *this is enough, this is fair, no one is hurt.*

He had told himself that lie for fifteen years.

"Stop making that face," Bramble said, appearing at his elbow with a paper cone of roasted chestnuts. She was disguised today as a respectable herbwife, gray shawl and all, which fooled no one because she kept winking at strangers. "You look like a man doing sums he doesn't like."

"I am."

"How much did you ever make? Selling them?"

Wendell took a chestnut just to have something to burn his fingers on. "Depends on the memory. First snowfall went for three marks. A wedding day, maybe ten. Once I got forty for—" He stopped.

"For what?" Bramble's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she was actually listening instead of performing listening.

"A man's memory of his son's face. He was dying. He said he couldn't carry it where he was going." Wendell turned the chestnut over in his palm. "I told myself I was doing him a kindness. Taking the weight."

"And now?"

"Now I think about the buyer. Some fat merchant in the capital, wearing another man's grief to dinner parties. And I think about the son, alive somewhere, wondering why his father stopped writing." He crushed the chestnut shell. "Forty marks, Bramble. That's what a father's love cost."

Bramble didn't say anything clever for once. She just bumped her shoulder against his, briefly, and let the market noise fill the silence.

It was Isolde who broke the silence—Isolde, who came back through the crowd too fast, who moved wrong, whose hand was already inside her coat.

"We need to leave. Now."

"What—"

"There are criers at the Exchange." Her voice was flat, which Wendell had learned meant frightened. "They're posting writs. Official Court writs." She thrust a torn scrap of parchment at him.

He read it twice before the words made sense, because the words refused to.

*By order of Lord Cassian of the Ember Court, sanctioned claimant to the Keystone of the First Flame: the bearer of the stolen covenant-memory is hereby declared a thief of the highest order. Reward offered for capture alive. All loyal subjects are enjoined to report sightings.*

Below the text was a sketch. It was a good likeness. It was a *very* good likeness, down to the way his hair fell over his left eye, and Wendell felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world, because there were only two people alive who knew that detail well enough to render it, and one of them was standing next to him looking equally pale, and the other—

"Hale," he said.

* * *

They found her at the Gilded Ember, the most expensive memory-house in the city, sitting in a private booth with a pot of black tea and an expression of profound self-satisfaction. She looked exactly as she had in the Hollow: small, silver-haired, wrapped in gray wool, a grandmother's face on a shark's accounting. The only difference was the quality of her boots.

"You're late," she said, pouring a fourth cup without being asked. "I expected you yesterday. You always were slow to read a ledger."

"You sold me." Wendell kept his voice low. Around them, the memory-house hummed with quiet commerce—a clerk appraising vials, a couple weeping over some purchased joy. "You sold my *face*, Hale. You sat across a table from me for fifteen years and you sold me."

"I sold your *location*. Your face you provided yourself, free of charge, by walking into the largest city in the realm and standing around looking guilty." She sipped her tea. "Sit down. You're making a scene, and scenes are bad for business."

Odo filled the booth's entrance like a door deciding to be angry. Bramble slid in beside Hale with the cheerful menace of a cat cornering something. Isolde stayed standing, one hand on her knife, staring at the old woman with an expression Wendell couldn't parse—something colder than hatred.

"You knew," Isolde said. "All those years. You knew what he was carrying."

"I suspected. Then I confirmed. Then I monetized." Hale set down her cup. "Girl, do you know how many brokers there are between here and the Ember Court's inner circle? Do you know what information about the keystone fetched this season? I could retire. I *have* retired. This tea costs more than Wendell earned in a year."

"The counterfeit claim," Wendell said. "Cassian's writ says he's the *sanctioned claimant*. Sanctioned by what? The flame doesn't answer to paperwork."

Hale smiled, and it was the smile Wendell knew best in all the world—the one she wore when a client signed away something they hadn't finished understanding. "No. But courts do. I sold Cassian a provenance, boy. A lovely forged genealogy tracing his line to the Hearth-tenders of old, complete with seals, testimonies, and one authenticated fragment of the original rite—which I may have assembled from scraps of memories I bought off drunk scholars over the years. As far as the Ember Court's lawyers can determine, Lord Cassian has every legal right to reforge the covenant under his own authority." She spread her small, soft hands. "And you? You have a stolen memory and no papers at all."

"Why?" It came out of Wendell rougher than he intended. "Fifteen years. You kept my secrets fifteen years. Why sell me now?"

Something flickered across Hale's face—there and gone, fast, but Wendell had spent a lifetime reading faces in a valley where reading faces was survival. For half a heartbeat she looked tired. Old. Like a woman carrying something heavy up a long hill.

"Because the wards fell, dear," she said lightly. "And when the wards fall, the Hollow stops being a sanctuary and starts being a cage with a broken lock. Everyone inside gets out eventually. Better to choose the moment than have it chosen for you." She refilled her cup. "Besides. Your past was going to catch up with you regardless. I merely arranged for it to catch up profitably."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Here's your real one: I've seen what happens to people who hold things the Court wants. I watched them take apart a hearth-guardian once, piece by piece, asking questions. It took eleven days." Her eyes flicked to Odo, who had gone very still. "So I made a choice. I chose the winning side, I chose the paying side, and I gave them you. If it helps—and clearly it doesn't—I set the price high enough that they'll want you alive. Dead men don't testify, and dead keystones don't open."

"You're a monster," Isolde said.

"I'm a broker." Hale's voice cracked like a whip, and for a moment the grandmother vanished entirely. "Monsters do what they do because they enjoy it. I do what I do because the world runs on ledgers, girl, and someone has to keep them honest. Every coin I've ever taken, I took openly. Can your thief say the same? Can any of you?" She looked around the booth. "You're all here hunting your own debts. I'm simply the only one who admits mine."

Silence. Somewhere across the room, a clerk announced a price for a childhood, and Wendell thought: *she's right, that's the worst part, she's right the way a blade is right.*

"What does Cassian know?" Isolde asked finally. "Exactly."

"He knows you passed through the Ashwood—he has the tracker-beast's account of your scent. He knows the scholar Maren examined your seal. And he knows you'll go to the First Hearth, because there's nowhere else to go." Hale stood, smoothed her wool dress, and left coins on the table with a precise little click. "He rides within the week. With legal sanction, forty riders, and a writ that lets him conscript every garrison between here and the mountains."

"Then we ride sooner," Odo said.

"Yes. I imagine you will." Hale paused at the edge of the booth, and for one strange moment she looked back at Wendell with something almost like the old familiarity—the face across the counter, the keeper of his secrets, the closest thing the Hollow had given him to a confidant. "One piece of free advice, boy, since I've already been paid for everything else. Cassian will offer you a deal. He always offers a deal; it's cheaper than fighting. Whatever he offers you—look closely at what he's asking you to forget, not what he's offering to pay. The price is never in the gold."

She walked away into the hum of the memory-house, small and silver and utterly unrepentant, and Wendell realized his hands were shaking.

"She could have warned us," Bramble said quietly. "Back in the Hollow. Before we ever left. One word, and we'd have had weeks."

"Maybe." Isolde sheathed her knife. "Or maybe warning us wasn't profitable."

Wendell barely heard them. He was watching the door Hale had gone through, and thinking about fifteen years of Tuesday meetings, of tea and tallies and the careful fiction that what existed between them was trust. And underneath the fury—hot, genuinely hot, hotter than he'd felt since the stars went out—was the colder thing settling into place beneath it.

His past wasn't done with him. His past was a ledger too, and every name in it was still open. The widow whose snowfall he'd sold. The dying man whose forgiveness he carried like stolen goods. Hale, who had sold him and called it honesty. Somewhere out there, every transaction was still collecting interest, and the debt had come due in the form of forty riders and a lord with forged rights and a writ.

"Vesper Crane would call this foreshadowing," Bramble muttered, gathering chestnut wrappers. "I call it Tuesday."

"Bramble." Wendell stood. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "How fast can you get us out of a city that legally wants to arrest us?"

"How fast do you need?"

"Faster than forty riders."

Bramble grinned, and for the first time all day the grin reached all the way to her eyes. "Then we leave through the corpse-gate, we bribe the ferryman with Odo's boots, and we pray the salt flats are kinder than lawyers." She paused. "Also we should probably steal a horse. Metaphorically. Mostly metaphorically."

But as they filed out of the Gilded Ember into the lantern-light of Vespertine, Wendell glanced back once—at the booth, at the untouched fourth teacup, at the shape of the life he'd thought was invisible and had never been invisible at all.

Hale's advice rang in his ears, unwelcome and true: *the price is never in the gold.*

Behind them, somewhere in the upper city, a bell began to ring—an alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one. The sound of a hunt beginning.

Odo put a hand on his shoulder. "Run first," the old guardian said. "Understand later."

They ran.
