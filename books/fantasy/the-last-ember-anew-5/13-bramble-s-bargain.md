## 13. Bramble's Bargain

Odo had stood watch over hearths for forty years, and he had learned that you could tell everything about a household by how it argued.

A family that argued cold—doors shut, words rationed, silences stacked like cordwood—was a family already burning down from the inside. A family that argued hot, loud and stupid and forgiving by morning, was a family that intended to survive the winter.

The four of them, crammed into the rented back room above the Gilded Wick, argued hot.

"You're not doing it." Isolde had her arms crossed and her jaw set in the way Odo privately called the siege posture. "Bramble, I mean it."

"It's my mark to spend." Bramble sat on the rickety table with her boots on a chair, turning something over in her fingers—a small iron disc, dull as an old coin, stamped with the sigil of the Hedge Warden's Circle and scored through with a single diagonal line. Her exile-mark. She'd worn it on a cord around her neck for eleven years, ever since the Circle cast her out for practicing root-craft they'd decided was heresy. "It's the only thing I own that anyone in this city wants. And we need passage tonight, or Cassian's writ catches up with us by dawn."

"There are other ways out of Vespertine."

"Name one that doesn't cost coin we don't have or blood we can't spare."

Isolde opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "The river barges—"

"Are controlled by the Ferrier's Guild, who are in the Ember Court's pocket, same as everyone else with a title and a fear of losing it." Bramble said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Girl, I've been running from institutions since before your father grew his first gray hair. I know how they work. They don't leave doors open out of kindness."

Odo leaned against the wall by the window, arms folded, saying nothing yet. That was his role in these arguments—the stone in the river. He'd been a hearth-guardian for most of his life; he knew when to bank a fire and when to let it roar. Right now he was watching Wendell, because Wendell was the one being quiet in the wrong way.

The thief sat on the floor with his back against Isolde's chair, knees drawn up, watching Bramble turn the exile-mark over and over like a man worrying a loose tooth. There was a look on his face Odo recognized from the Ashwood, from the salt road, from every moment the boy thought no one important was looking at him. Hunger. Not for food. For something he didn't have a word for yet.

"Bramble," Odo said finally, "walk me through it. What does selling the mark actually buy?"

She brightened the way she always did when someone asked her to explain a scheme. "The mark is proof of banishment. Officially recorded, sealed by the Circle, registered with every port authority in three realms. To a fence, it's worth a fortune—exiles' identities get bought by smugglers who need a clean-ish face with a dirty paper trail. To a certain kind of buyer—" she tapped the scored line through the sigil— "it's worth more than that. There are collectors who pay for Court-sanctioned suffering. Don't ask me why. Rich people are strange."

"And the passage?"

"The Ferrier's Guild runs night-barges up the Salt Canal to the flats beyond the city wall. The harbormaster owes me—or rather, owes *Marrowfen Bramblehedge*, hedge-witch of the Third Circle, exiled under seal. Show him the mark as surety, he lets us aboard, sells the mark on to his collector friends, everyone's happy. We're on the water before midnight and across the flats before Cassian's riders even saddle their horses."

"And if the harbormaster decides an exile's mark is worth more than an exile's silence?" Isolde asked. "He could sell *you* along with it. The Court pays bounties on unregistered practitioners, Bramble. You'd be walking into a cage with your own neck as the payment."

"I've been in cages." Bramble shrugged, but Odo saw her thumb press hard against the iron disc, once, like a heartbeat. "They're drafty."

"This isn't funny."

"I'm not laughing. Look at my face. Am I laughing?" She wasn't. Under all the brass, her eyes were scared, and she was doing what scared brave people did: making jokes faster than the fear could catch them.

Odo pushed off the wall. "There's another option nobody's said out loud, so I'll say it. I go to the harbormaster instead. My name still carries weight in certain circles. Disgraced or not, I served the Hearth for thirty years. Some of the old guildmasters remember when hearth-guardians could walk into any port in the realm and be fed first and questioned never."

"They also remember why you were disgraced," Isolde said quietly.

"They do." He let that sit. It needed to sit. "And some of them hated the Court for making an example of me. There are cracks in everything, girl. Even loyalty has seams."

"No." Wendell spoke for the first time, and his voice cracked on it. Everyone looked at him. He looked startled himself, like the word had gotten out of the pen without permission. "No. You've—all of you keep—you keep trying to be the one who pays." He stood up, awkward, hands half-raised like he wasn't sure what to do with them. "Isolde wants to trade herself to Cassian for time. Odo wants to trade his name. Bramble wants to trade the only thing she owns. Does anyone else see the pattern here, or is it just me?"

"We see it," Bramble said. "We just don't agree it's a problem."

"It's a problem because—" He stopped. Started again, slower, like a man crossing ice. "I spent seven years trading things. Memories. Little pieces of people's lives, lifted and sold, and I told myself it was fine because nobody missed what they couldn't remember missing. And then I met you lot, and I learned what it looks like when someone gives something instead of taking it. And now every one of you is lining up to *give* until there's nothing left, and I'm supposed to just stand here and watch?"

"So what are you offering?" Isolde asked, not unkindly.

"I don't *have* anything!" It burst out of him, too loud for the small room. Somewhere below, the tavern crowd murmured on, oblivious. He lowered his voice, and it came out raw. "That's the whole point. I've got a bag of stolen years and a dead man's memory I don't understand, and neither one buys a boat berth. So when you talk about sacrifice, I'm standing outside the shop window looking in. Again. Same as always."

The room went quiet.

Odo watched Bramble look at Wendell—not with pity, thank the embers, but with something sharper and kinder. Then she hopped off the table, crossed the room, and pressed the exile-mark into his hand.

"What—"

"Hold that a second."

He held it. The iron was warm from her skin, lighter than it looked.

"Now tell me what you feel."

"...Nothing. It's metal."

"That's right. It's nothing. It's a receipt for the worst day of my life, that's all it's ever been." She took it back and threaded the cord over her head, tucking it beneath her collar. "Eleven years I treated it like it was part of me. Like the Circle got to decide what I was and I had to wear their verdict around my neck forever. Selling it isn't a sacrifice, boy. It's a *refund*."

"But the risk—"

"Oh, the risk is real." She grinned, fierce and crooked. "If the harbormaster sells me to the Court, I'll rot in an ash-cell until they figure out what to do with a hedge-witch who knows too many songs. That's true, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because pretending is how families end up arguing cold. But here's the thing about risk: it's only bearable if somebody's waiting on the other side of it. That's the whole trick. That's the entire secret of being brave."

Odo felt something shift in his chest, old and stiff, like a door hinge that hadn't moved in decades.

Because he understood what she was really saying. He'd heard it before, long ago, from a hearth-elder whose name he hadn't spoken aloud in years: *A guardian who guards alone guards nothing.* He had learned that lesson the hard way, in a burning archive, with a choice between saving his oath and saving his fellow soldiers—and he had chosen wrong, or the Court had decided he had, and the difference hadn't mattered to the tribunal.

He had been alone a long time. Alone, you learn to count your losses in advance. Alone, sacrifice is arithmetic.

But this—

This was Isolde gripping Bramble's sleeve and saying "if you do this, I swear on my father's flame I'll come get you." This was Wendell still standing there with his hands half-raised, staring at Bramble like she'd handed him something heavier than iron. This was four strangers in a rented room above a tavern, shouting about who owed whom, and the shouting itself was the answer.

Families argued because they expected to still be a family tomorrow.

"Wendell," Odo said.

The boy turned.

"You said you're standing outside the window. Come away from the glass." He nodded at the table, at the maps Isolde had spread there—the canal route, the flats beyond, the long pale scar of salt leading toward the mountains and the First Hearth. "You can't pay for passage and you can't fight riders. Fine. Neither can I, anymore, not really. But you've got the keystone, and the keystone needs a keeper, and keeping is its own kind of paying. Sit down. Argue with us. That's your share."

Wendell blinked. "Arguing is my share?"

"Arguing means you intend to be here when it's settled," Isolde said. Her voice had gone soft in the way it did when she talked about her father. "People who've already left don't bother arguing."

Something moved across Wendell's face—fast, quickly hidden, but Odo had spent thirty years reading faces across firelight and he caught it plain. The boy had never argued with anyone who stayed. Odo would have bet his last tooth on it. Whatever hollow he'd crawled out of, whatever life had taught him that disagreement meant abandonment, it had taught him wrong, and it was going to take him years to unlearn it, and that was all right. They had years. Or they would, if Bramble's bargain held and the horses stayed slow.

"Fine," Wendell said hoarsely. He dragged himself onto the bench beside Isolde. "Then I'm against the plan. Not because it's bad. Because I want it on record that I hate it, and that I'm staying anyway."

"Noted," Bramble said solemnly. "Motion carries. One objection, lovingly recorded."

"One more thing," Odo said. "I'm coming to the docks with you."

Bramble raised an eyebrow. "Odo—"

"Not to bargain. To stand behind you. All due respect, Marrowfen Bramblehedge, but you're five feet of defiance in a stolen coat, and if that harbormaster gets ideas, it'll help to have six and a half feet of disgraced hearth-guardian getting ideas back." He rolled his shoulder, felt the old ache answer. "Besides. If this goes wrong, I'd rather be there when it does. I've spent enough of my life arriving after."

Isolde started to object. Bramble put a hand on her arm.

"Let him," she said. "Kin argues, but kin doesn't get left on the porch."

The word landed in the room like a struck bell.

*Kin.*

Nobody corrected her. Nobody flinched. Isolde looked down at her maps and blinked fast. Wendell stared at the tabletop with an expression like a man hearing music in a language he almost remembered. And Odo—who had lost a brotherhood to fire and a reputation to politics and thirty years to exile—felt the word settle into him and fit, the way a key fits a lock that's been rusted shut.

"All right," Isolde said finally, voice steady by main force. "Docks. Midnight barge. Bramble makes the trade, Odo stands ugly behind her, Wendell and I watch the street for Court colors. We're aboard by the first bell and we don't stop until the salt flats."

"We should move now, then," Wendell said. "If Hale sold our location in Vespertine the way she sold it everywhere else—"

A knock at the door.

Four bodies went still as poured wax. Odo's hand found the old short-hafted hearth-maul he kept wrapped in his bedroll, and he saw Isolde's fingers drift to her knife, and Wendell went gray as ash.

Three soft knocks. Then a voice, muffled through the door, dry as paper and familiar as a debt:

"Well? Are you going to make an old woman stand in a stairwell, or shall I come in and ruin your evening properly?"

Odo knew that voice. So did Wendell, judging by the sound the boy made—somewhere between a snarl and a swallow.

Broker Hale.

* * *

She looked terrible, which for Hale meant she looked merely expensive instead of immaculate. The traveling cloak was good wool but road-stained, and there were shadows under her eyes that no ledger could hide. She stepped into the room uninvited, took in the maps, the weapons, the four faces aimed at her like drawn bows, and smiled the smile of a woman who had already priced everyone's anger.

"Don't," Wendell said, low and dangerous. Odo had never heard the boy sound like that. "Whatever you're selling, we're not buying."

"On the contrary." Hale set a slim leather case on the table and clicked it open. Inside, nested in velvet, lay a folded document bearing the ember-seal of the Ember Court—and a ship's manifest, fresh-inked, for a canal barge departing at midnight. "I'm giving it away. Consider it interest paid on a very old account." Her gaze flicked to Bramble. "You're planning to sell your exile-mark to Harbormaster Vetch. Don't. He sold your description to Lord Cassian's men two hours ago, along with a request for confirmation of bounty terms. You'd have made it to the gangplank."

The room's silence had a new texture now—the silence of a floor that's just been shown to be thinner than believed.

"Why?" Isolde demanded. "Why would you help us? You sold him. You sold all of us. You've been selling us since the Hollow."

Hale closed the case and slid it across the table to Wendell. "Because, dear girl, I sold to Cassian—and Cassian doesn't pay his brokers. He burns them. I'm the next entry in his ledger unless the covenant reforges and the Court's writ dissolves with it. Which means, for the first time in my considerable career, our interests align." She smiled again, thinner. "Also, I never liked him. Take the manifest. Take the writ—it proves Cassian's claim on the keystone is forged, which may matter later, assuming any of you live to use it. And then get on that barge, because his riders are saddling *now*, and the harbormaster's dock-gates close at the first bell."

Wendell stared at the case. At her. "You expect us to trust you."

"Emphatically not. Trust is for churches and fools. Expect me to act in my interest, which currently happens to be your survival." She pulled her hood up and moved to the door, pausing with her hand on the latch. "Oh—one more thing, free of charge." Her eyes found Wendell's, and for half a heartbeat something almost human looked out of them. "The stranger you robbed, seven years ago. He chose you, boy. Out of every thief on that road. Do try to be worth the pick."

The door clicked shut behind her.

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then Bramble grabbed her pack, Isolde swept the maps off the table, and Odo unwrapped his maul.

"First bell," Isolde said. "Move."
