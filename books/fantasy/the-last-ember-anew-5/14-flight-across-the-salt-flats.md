## 14. Flight Across the Salt Flats

**Wendell**

The salt flats began where Bramble said the world's patience ended.

They had left Vespertine in the grey hour before dawn, six horses and five riders moving fast across a plain that crunched underfoot like broken glass. By midday the city was a smudge behind them, and ahead there was nothing—white to every horizon, flat as an unmarked page. The sun hammered down. The light came up off the ground so hard it felt like being shouted at from below.

Wendell rode beside Odo and tried not to look back.

"You'll wear your neck out, craning like that," Odo said. The old hearth-guardian sat his horse the way other men sat chairs, comfortably and without apology. "Cassian's riders won't show themselves until they're sure of you. That's the Court way. They let the land do their chasing first."

"The land," Wendell said, "is very big."

"The land is honest," Odo corrected. "That's worse."

Isolde rode point, half a length ahead of everyone, the keystone memory strapped against her chest in its iron case because Wendell had finally admitted he couldn't feel it properly anymore—the thing hummed too loud now, ever since the cave, ever since he'd glimpsed what lived inside it. It was safer on her. Everything was probably safer on her.

Bramble brought up the rear with the packhorse and a running commentary on the quality of Vespertine's horses ("all knee and no heart"), the quality of the Court's riders ("worse"), and the quality of the light ("a bad omen, but then, everything is").

It should have felt like hope. They were three days from the First Hearth if the maps held. Instead Wendell kept catching himself doing arithmetic of loss: Odo at the pass at Reunion-that-hadn't-happened-yet—he'd seen it, hadn't he, in the ember-mark dreams that came now when he slept? No. He shook it off. The dreams were just fear wearing tomorrow's clothes.

The storm appeared at midafternoon as a brown thread on the southern horizon.

Bramble saw it first and stopped swearing about horses. "That," she said, "is not weather. That is a wall."

Odo stood in his stirrups, squinted, and said one word: "Driven."

"Driven how?" Isolde called back.

"Court wind-riders. They herd storms. Cassian isn't following us." Odo's voice went flat and professional. "He's sweeping the flats. There'll be no cover anywhere when it passes, and nothing standing after. We ride for the stones."

"What stones?" Wendell asked.

"The ones I'm about to point at." Odo raised his arm toward a cluster of dark shapes far out in the white—a broken ring of standing rocks, maybe a mile off, jutting up like rotten teeth. "Old hearth-shrine. Pre-covenant. If we can reach it before the wall does, we might dig in behind the stones and live."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we won't need to worry about Cassian," Odo said, "which will be a nice change."

They ran the horses. It was not a decision anyone made aloud; the storm decided it for them, the brown wall rising and widening as it came, swallowing the horizon behind them with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. The light went strange, orange and bruised. Salt dust kicked up from the hooves and stung Wendell's eyes until he could barely see Isolde's back ahead of him, a dark shape swimming in the glare.

They were fifty yards from the stones when the wind hit them sideways.

It didn't blow. It *shoved*. Wendell's horse screamed and went over, and he was on the ground, rolling, salt burning into every cut and crease, the sky gone completely brown, noon turned to choking midnight in the space between one breath and the next. He heard voices torn to shreds by the wind—Isolde screaming his name, maybe; Bramble cursing with real artistry; Odo bellowing something about ropes—and then a hand closed on his collar and hauled, and he was stumbling forward through a blindness of white and brown, guided by grip alone, until rock rose up in front of them like mercy.

A gap between two monoliths. He was shoved through it. Isolde came through after him, coughing, hair whipping loose from its braid.

And then the wind shifted the whole storm like a hand sliding furniture, and the gap filled—not with air but with a churning solid mass of salt and grit, packed tight as mortar, sealing them in.

The roar became a hum. The hum became almost-silence.

Wendell realized he couldn't see Isolde. Then a faint glow—her cupped hands, a coal from her pocket-lamp kindled by hedge-fire Bramble had taught them all—and her face swam out of the murk, streaked white with salt, eyes enormous.

"Bramble?" he croaked.

"Gone. Odo. Gone." Her voice cracked on the second word. She swallowed and tried again. "The others are out there somewhere. Or—" She didn't finish.

Or buried. Or lost forever in a storm that would grind bone before morning. Wendell pressed his back against the stone and slid down until he was sitting in the drift already piling around their feet, and the two of them listened to the storm breathe, and neither of them said anything else for a long time.

* * *

**Isolde**

The shrine, once the storm's roar faded enough to think, turned out to be hollow.

Behind the standing stones, hidden by centuries of drifted salt, there was a low chamber—a hearth-room, Odo would have called it—one of the old places where travelers had once warmed themselves between realms, back when warmth was shared instead of hoarded. The firepit at its center was cold and full of sand. The walls still bore marks: handprints, hundreds of them, pressed in ancient pigment, overlapping like leaves.

Isolde traced one with her fingertip. Small. A child's hand.

"People used to come here just to be warm together," she said, mostly to herself.

"People used to do a lot of things." Wendell sat across the pit from her, knees drawn up, salt drying in white crusts along his jaw. In the coal-light he looked younger and older at once, the way he did when he wasn't performing carefulness. "My whole life I thought the Hollow was the only place that mattered. Four streets and a market and rules. Turns out the whole world used to be one house."

"It still is. That's the entire argument." She fed the last of their dry fuel into the pit and coaxed the coal into something like a fire. Not much. Enough to see each other. "One house, badly divided."

"By a theft."

She watched him say it. Watched him flinch on it. Seven years she had hunted the man who took her father's last gift, and she knew every version of his face now—the smooth one, the sorry one, the shuttered one—but this was newer than all of those. This was a man saying the true thing out loud and letting it cost him.

"Yes," she said. "By a theft. And now everyone's waiting to find out whether another thief can end it."

"That's fair."

"I didn't say it to be fair." She pulled her cloak tighter. "I said it because I've been thinking it since the cave, and I promised myself that if I got you alone somewhere with no one to impress and nowhere to run, I'd ask you plainly. So. Plainly."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them the storm dragged its weight across the stone, patient as a creditor.

"When my father died," Isolde said, "he gave you more than the keystone. He gave you his final hours. Every dying man's last hours are full of things that matter—names, apologies, instructions, love. You took all of it. And you never opened it. Never sold it. Never even peeked, you claim." She leaned forward. "Why?"

"Because it hummed wrong."

"Hummed wrong."

"Every memory I've ever lifted has a texture. Grief is heavy and slow. Joy is slippery. Lies have a grain, like wood. I learned to read them by feel before I ever learned to be ashamed of it." His hands moved as he talked, shaping the invisible. "This one didn't have a texture. It had a *door*. Sealed, locked, humming like something alive behind it. I told myself I was leaving it sealed out of respect." He laughed, short and ugly. "But that's not true either. I left it sealed because I was afraid of what I'd find. Because some part of me always knew it wasn't mine, and opening it would make the theft real in a way that selling memories to Hale somehow never did."

"So you carried a dead man's last words for seven years and never listened to them."

"Yes."

"And in the cave you did listen."

"Partially." His eyes came up and met hers, and she saw the thing she'd been circling for weeks, the thing that had kept her awake at camp while he pretended to sleep. "Your father forgave someone, Isolde. In there. At the very center of the seal, wrapped around the flame itself. Forgiveness, and a plea that the fire be carried by choice." He paused. "He didn't name who he was forgiving. But I know what I am. And I know what he caught me doing, that night at the valley's edge. I was lifting his memories when he grabbed my wrist. He was supposed to be past feeling anything. He held on anyway."

The silence stretched. Isolde found that her hands had become fists in her lap, and that underneath the fury—which was real, which had been her engine for seven years—there was something worse. Something that sounded like relief.

"He knew," she said quietly. "He was dying and a thief took his hand and he *knew*, and he chose to give instead of take back."

"He gave you too. In there. I heard—I felt—there's a moment where he talks about a daughter who waited at borders." Wendell's voice roughened. "I didn't stay for it. I pulled back. Some things shouldn't be stolen even by accident."

Isolde looked at the fire for a long time. The child-handprints circled the walls. Once, this room had been full of strangers sharing warmth, and it had been the most ordinary thing in the world.

"My father waited three days at the Hollow's border for the wards to open a crack," she said. "Three days, sick as he was, because he wouldn't break covenant law by forcing it. I've hated this valley for years. I've hated *you* for years. And the whole time, the man I loved best was dying slowly outside your walls because he believed in a promise the rest of the world had already abandoned." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the wetness there. "So here is my plain speaking, Wendell Quintana. I don't forgive you. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But I believe you want to be worth forgiving, and I've decided that wanting counts for something on the road to the Hearth."

"That's more than I deserve."

"Probably." Almost, she smiled. "Don't ruin it by agreeing with me."

* * *

**Wendell**

They dug out through the night, taking turns, passing a dented tin pan between them like a sacrament. The storm spent itself near dawn, and when Wendell pushed through the last crust of salt into grey morning light, the world had been rewritten. The flats stretched white and featureless in every direction, their tracks erased, their companions vanished.

No Bramble. No Odo. No horses.

"Noon," Isolde said, scanning the blank horizon. "If they sheltered and dug free like us, they'll head for the Hearth road. Odo knows the landmarks. We go to the last waypoint and wait. If they're alive, that's where they'll aim."

"And if they aimed somewhere else?"

"Then we'll grieve later. Right now we walk." She shouldered the pack containing the keystone and set off south without checking whether he followed, which was, Wendell had learned, her way of trusting him.

They walked. The sun climbed and the salt threw light up at them and the silence between them had changed—it was lighter now, scoured, like the flats themselves. Twice Isolde pointed out things she remembered her father describing on this same route, and twice Wendell listened without pretending he already knew, and both times she seemed surprised and pleased that she'd surprised him.

Near midday, Wendell noticed the mark.

His palms had carried the ember-glow since the Hearth's threshold dreams began—faint, warm, ignorable. But now the heat was climbing, insistent, and when he held his hands up against the glare he saw why. Both palms glowed brighter than they ever had. And the glow tugged. Southward. Like a compass needle made of his own skin.

"Isolde."

She turned. He showed her his hands, and her breath went out of her in a rush.

"That's not the keystone pulling," she said. "The case is shielded. That's the Hearth itself. It knows you're close." She stared at his glowing palms, and then at him, and Wendell watched an idea move across her face like weather. "Empty hands," she murmured. "Maren's riddle. Only someone with empty hands can carry the flame."

"What are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking the Hearth isn't waiting for the keystone." Her voice dropped, quick and certain. "It's waiting for *you*."

Before he could answer, sound reached them across the flats—the thin, carrying music of hoofbeats. Many horses. Coming fast from the north.

Wendell spun. Riders, dark against the white, fanning out in a hunter's crescent. And at the crescent's center, a banner he knew now the way you know a scar: the Ember Court's flame, gold on black.

"They found us," Isolde breathed. "The storm drove us right into—no. Wait." Her eyes narrowed. "Look at the spacing. That's not a search line. They came straight here. Straight to *us*."

Which meant someone had known exactly where the storm would spit them out.

Wendell's stomach turned cold as he counted the riders again and found, among the black cloaks, one figure on a pale horse whose silhouette he knew better than he wanted to—who had sold his name once in a smoky office in the Hollow, and who had, apparently, not yet finished collecting her debts.

"Isolde," he said quietly, "I think Broker Hale just earned her commission."

The riders began to close.
