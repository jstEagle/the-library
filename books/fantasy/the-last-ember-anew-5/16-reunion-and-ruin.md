## 16. Reunion and Ruin

The mountain showed itself an hour before dawn, and Isolde understood why no map had ever carried its name.

It did not rise so much as it *waited*—a black mass against the grey sky, wearing snow like a shroud, with a wound of red light at its heart where the First Hearth had once burned. Even extinguished, even cold, the mountain felt less like stone than like a held breath. The whole world, she thought, had been holding this breath for three hundred years.

"There." Bramble pointed with her chin, hands jammed in her sleeves against the altitude cold. "The stair. You can just see it under the ice."

Isolde saw. A seam cut into the mountainside, wide enough for ten abreast in its day, now half-swallowed by glacier. At its mouth stood two pillars, and between them the faintest shimmer—the last scar of the old wardline, dead as everything else the covenant had left behind.

"Odo," she said quietly.

He was already looking at it. He'd been looking at it since the salt flats, since before that, maybe—since the roadhouse, since the night he'd first told them what he was. *Hearth-guardian,* disgraced. A man who had let the flame die on his watch, or believed he had, and had spent forty years walking away from a door he couldn't stop seeing.

"I know," Odo said.

"You don't have to—"

"Isolde." He said her name the way he said few things: plainly, without decoration. He was a big man gone grey at the temples, with a face like weathered timber and hands that had held back hunters, beasts, and one very determined innkeeper's dog. "I've been coming back to this mountain my whole life. I just took the long way." He almost smiled. "Let me be the one who walks up to it."

She wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; she came from a family of arguers, all dead now except in memory, and she had learned grief's cruelest trick—that the voices you miss most are the ones you can still hear perfectly, saying things you never answered.

But Wendell touched her elbow. Just that. Just a thief's light fingers, asking nothing, and she remembered the cave, and the sandstorm, and the words they'd finally traded like knives in the dark, and she let it go.

"We go together," she said instead. "All four. Nobody walks ahead."

Bramble snorted. "Spoken like someone who's never climbed a frozen stair."

"Then I'll say it again at the top."

* * *

The stair took the morning. It was worse than climbing—it was being *read*. With every step, Isolde felt something old turning its attention over her, patient as sediment, weighing her the way her father used to weigh fruit at market: not unkindly, but without mercy either. Once, the wardline would have known every traveler by their covenant-mark. Now it knew nothing, and so it questioned everything.

Halfway up, Wendell stopped walking.

She turned. He was staring at his own hands, palms up, and even from six feet away she could see the tremor in them—and beneath the skin, faint as banked coals, a glow. The keystone. Seven years he'd carried the dying stranger's sealed memory inside him, and now, this close to the Hearth, it woke.

"It knows where it's going," he said. His voice was strange. Awed and afraid at once, a man hearing his own name called from a house he didn't know he owned.

"Does it hurt?"

"No." He flexed his fingers. "That's what frightens me. It feels like—" He searched. "Like being expected."

They climbed on.

* * *

They reached the threshold at noon, and Isolde's breath left her all at once.

The First Hearth was not a building. She had imagined walls, a roof, some temple grandeur—but there was nothing. Only a vast bowl of stone open to the sky, ringed by pillars carved with figures whose faces the weather had taken, and at the center, a hearthstone the size of a wagon bed, black and cold as a closed eye. Around it, channels radiated outward like veins, running to the mountain's edge and beyond—to the world, she realized. To both worlds. This was the heart the blood had once flowed from.

And above them, through the thin high air, the stars were wrong. Faint. Starving. She hadn't noticed from below, but here, at the source, she could see what the dying covenant actually looked like: a sky going out one ember at a time.

"It's beautiful," Bramble whispered, and then laughed at herself, wet-eyed. "Gods help me, it's beautiful and it's *dead*, and I've walked eleven years to stand in front of a corpse."

"Then we wake it," Isolde said.

Wendell stepped toward the hearthstone.

That was when the pass behind them filled with riders.

* * *

She heard them before she saw them—not hooves but wings, the leathery snap of drake-wings, and then Cassian's vanguard poured over the lip of the bowl like ink finding a crack. Twelve hunters in ember-lacquered armor, and above them two drakes wheeling against the pale sky, and descending last, unhurried, robed in white and gold, Lord Cassian himself.

He looked exactly as he had in Vespertine: composed, handsome in the way a drawn blade is handsome, smiling as though the ambush were a courtesy he was performing for them.

"Wendell Quintana," Cassian called, and his voice carried impossibly across the stone, laid into the mountain's own acoustics. "You are in possession of stolen Court property. Surrender it, and the Crown offers amnesty, wealth, and—my personal favorite—a clean slate. No memories of your crimes at all. You'd wake tomorrow innocent."

"He lies," Bramble snapped.

"Of course I lie," Cassian agreed pleasantly. "But I lie *legally*."

Isolde moved without deciding to—between Wendell and the riders, blade already free. Beside her Odo stepped forward too, and she heard him exhale, long and slow, the way a man does when he sets down something heavy.

"Cassian," Odo said.

The lord's smile flickered—just a hair. "Guardian. Forty years, and this is where you surface? Holding a thief's coat?"

"Holding the door." Odo rolled his shoulders. "It's what I'm for."

Cassian sighed, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Twelve hunters. Two drakes. Four of you, one of whom is a hedge-witch whose exile-mark I revoked this morning—which makes her presence here *trespass*, incidentally. Don't make me waste good soldiers."

"Odo," Isolde said quietly. "Don't—"

"The stair," Odo said. Not to her. To Wendell. "Narrow ground. Two abreast, no more, and drakes can't fold their wings in the throat of it. You could hold that pass for an hour with one stubborn man."

"And you'd be the stubborn man," Isolde said. Her voice cracked on it. "No."

"Somebody has to buy the time to light the fire, girl." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were kind and terrible and absolutely settled. "Forty years I ran from this mountain. You don't get to take the last mile of it from me."

Then he kissed his knuckles, touched them to the stone of the threshold—an old guardian's blessing, she realized, older than any of them—and walked down toward the pass alone.

* * *

What happened next, Isolde watched from the top of the stair, because Wendell needed her there, because Bramble was already dragging him toward the hearthstone, because duty is a merciless choreographer.

She watched Odo plant himself in the throat of the ancient stair with a guardian's staff he'd been carrying disguised as a walking stick for eleven years, and she watched the wood remember what it was—bark splitting, ironheart showing, the staff igniting along its length with hearthfire gold.

She watched the first hunter die reaching for mercy that wasn't offered.

She watched the second and third learn better.

She watched Odo fight the way water fights—yielding, returning, inexorable—and she understood suddenly, watching him, that the disgrace had never been cowardice. He had been *good enough* to survive the flame's death and had chosen exile anyway, because surviving had felt like a second failure. Everything he had done since—the stew, the jokes, the quiet watchfulness, the way he'd always slept nearest the door—he had done as penance. And now, at the end, he fought like a man who had finally found the arithmetic that balanced.

She watched a drake try the pass anyway, wings cracking against stone, screaming.

She watched Odo bring it down.

And she watched the moment it turned—she would see it forever, she knew, in the dark behind her eyes—the moment a second hunter, the small quick one who'd circled wide through the rocks, put a hooked chain around Odo's knee and pulled while another's spear went in high.

He fell slow. He fell like a felled tree chooses its direction—deliberately, even then—even choosing, even falling, to fall *outward*, back down the stair, away from the hearth, so that his body would block the way behind him.

The last thing he did was laugh. She couldn't hear it over the wind and the fighting, but she saw it—saw his shoulders shake with it, saw him look up past the hunters pressing in, past all of it, up at the hearth-bowl and the four of them still standing there.

Then the spears went in together, and the laughing stopped.

* * *

"Bramble!" Isolde's voice tore out of her raw. "Do it NOW—"

But Bramble wasn't beside the hearthstone anymore.

Isolde spun. The witch stood at the bowl's rim, hands raised, fire crawling up her forearms green and furious—and between her and the drake diving at Wendell's back. Bramble met it mid-air. The explosion knocked every living thing off its feet, hunter and keeper alike, and when the smoke cleared, Bramble was kneeling in the scorch with three hunters' blades at her throat and an iron muzzle-frame closing over her mouth, killing her spells in her teeth.

Her eyes found Isolde's over the iron. *Go,* they said. *Finish it.*

"Bind her," Cassian said, dismounting at last, immaculate, untouched, as though the battle were a formality he'd signed earlier. He looked at the hearthstone, at Wendell glowing faintly at its edge, and his composure finally, finally slipped into something naked and hungry. "There it is. Three hundred years, and there it is."

Isolde put herself between them. Blade low, arm screaming, grief and rage fused in her chest into a single white-hot alloy she suspected she would carry for the rest of her life—however much of it remained.

"You're late," she said. "My father died carrying your precious spark. My friends died buying it five minutes. Whatever you take today, you'll take it over my body, and you should know—" her voice steadied, which surprised her, hardened, which didn't— "everyone who's died for it chose to. That's the whole secret, Cassian. That's the entire covenant. And it's the one thing you will never understand, because everything you have, you *took*."

For a heartbeat, something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Pity, even.

Then he drew his sword, and it burned white, and he said:

"Take her. Kill the thief if he resists. And someone put that fire out before it starts."

Above them, through the thin starving air, the last star overhead flickered—

and Wendell Quintana, empty-handed by choice at last, pressed his palms to the cold black hearthstone, and began to give.
