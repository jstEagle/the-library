## 17. Cassian's Offer

They came for me at the hour when even the fire sleeps.

I woke because the cave went quiet—not the quiet of sleeping companions, but the quiet of held breath. Bramble's wards had been singing all night, a low thread of sound like a wet finger on glass, and the silence where that thread had been was louder than any scream.

I sat up. Isolde slept curled against the far wall, one hand even in dreams near her father's knife. Odo had taken first watch and was gone from his post at the mouth of the cave.

Gone.

I crossed the stone floor without making a sound—old habits die slower than people—and looked out into the dark of the pass below the First Hearth. The stars were back overhead; they'd returned the night we crossed the salt flats, faint and trembling, as if they weren't sure they were welcome. But down in the pass, where Odo should have been standing with his hearth-guardian's spear planted like a fence post, there was nothing but firelight.

A single campfire burned on the ledge thirty feet below, where no fire should be. And beside it sat a man in a coat the color of banked coals, warming his hands as if he'd been invited.

"Master Quintana," he said, without turning around. "Do sit down. You'll catch your death standing in doorways."

I knew the voice before I knew the name. Everyone past the wards knew it eventually—the voice of Lord Cassian of the Ember Court, the man who owned every warrant with my description on it, the man Hale had sold me to twice over: once with my location, once with her counterfeit claim to the keystone.

"I'll wake them," I said.

"You could." He gestured graciously at the cave behind me. "The witch would throw fire. The girl would put a knife in me before I finished my second sentence. And the old guardian—" he smiled, and there was something almost fond in it, "—Odo would simply stand there, being immovable, until I grew bored. That's why I waited for his watch to end. He's not gone, by the way. He's asleep forty feet up the trail with a bruise he'll be embarrassed about. I don't kill guardians. It's bad form."

"And thieves?"

"Thieves are negotiable." He turned then, and up close he wasn't what I'd expected. No cruelty in the face. Something worse: patience. The face of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice. "Sit, Master Quintana. Or Wendell, if I may. I feel we're going to be intimate by morning."

I didn't sit. I also didn't leave. That was the trap of him—he'd built the conversation so that both exits led somewhere he wanted.

"You have something I want," he said. "And I have several things you want. Let's do this properly, because I know how much you value proper things. You lived seven years in a valley with rules. Rules are just rituals for people afraid of chaos, and you struck me as a very frightened ritualist."

"What do you want?"

"The keystone memory. The spark of the First Flame your dying friend pressed into your grubby little hands." He said it lightly, but I watched his eyes flick to my chest, to the place where the sealed memory hummed against my ribs like a second heart. Yes. He knew exactly where I kept it. "Hand it over, and the Court will make you rich beyond anything Broker Hale ever paid you. Amnesty—full pardon, sealed records, no hunter will ever cross your shadow again. You could go anywhere. Do anything. Buy a house in Vespertine with walls thick enough that you never have to hear another person's thoughts leaking through their smiles."

"And the third thing?" Because there was always a third thing. Hale had taught me that. Every broker's offer comes in threes, and only two of them are gifts.

Cassian leaned forward, and the firelight did something unkind to his face.

"Erasure," he said softly. "Complete and total. Every theft. Every night you crept into grieving houses and lifted the warm parts out of people's heads. The widow whose first snowfall you sold. The dying man whose last words you pocketed. The daughter who spent seven years tracking you across half the world while you carried her father's goodbye in your pocket like loose coin." He tilted his head. "We can burn all of it out of you. Every crime, every victim's face, every moment of shame. You'd wake up clean, Wendell. Blank where it hurts. Isolde would tell you her story someday and you'd feel nothing, because there'd be nothing left in you to feel it *with*."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being a thief: after enough years, you stop stealing things and start stealing *versions of yourself*. The Wendell who never touched the widow's snowfall. The Wendell who told the dying stranger no and walked away. The Wendell the Hollow believed in—harmless, invisible, content.

Every night since the wards fell, some version of those men had been whispering to me. *You never meant harm. You were surviving. Anyone would have done the same.*

Cassian wasn't offering me wealth. Wealth was the wrapping paper.

He was offering to make the whispers true.

"No one would ever know," he said, reading me the way Hale used to read her ledger—with that flat professional attention that made you feel like an entry rather than a person. "Not the Court. Not the girl. Not yourself. You'd be a man with empty pockets and a clean slate, and the world would owe you nothing, and you'd owe the world nothing. Isn't that what you've wanted all along? To put it down? All of it?"

God help me—I wanted it.

I stood there in the dark with the ember humming against my ribs and let myself want it. Not the gold. Not the pardon. I wanted the *unburdening*. I wanted to look at Isolde tomorrow and see a stranger instead of a mirror. I wanted to forget the exact weight of the sealed memory, which I had carried so long it had worn a groove in me, the way a river wears a canyon. I wanted to be innocent the way children are innocent—not through virtue, but through amputation.

"That's not mercy," I said. My voice came out rough. "That's just a cleaner kind of theft."

"All theft is," Cassian said pleasantly. "The question is never whether you steal, Wendell. It's whether you steal *well*. The Court steals futures and calls it order. Hale stole your location and called it commerce. You stole memories and called it survival. I'm offering to steal your guilt and call it justice. We're all in the same trade. Some of us just have better coats."

Behind me, inside the cave, I heard Isolde turn in her sleep and murmur something. A name. Her father's name, I'd have bet my life on it, and lately I'd been betting my life on less.

And that—that small unconscious sound of a woman dreaming of a dead man whose last words I had stolen—was what saved me.

Because I understood, suddenly, what Cassian was really offering. Not erasure of my crimes. Erasure of my *witnesses*. If I forgot, Isolde's father stayed dead with no one carrying his goodbye. If I forgot, the widow's snowfall stayed sold and unmissed by anyone. Every person I'd ever robbed would lose the one thing worse than being robbed: being forgotten by the person who owed them.

My crimes were debts. Debts need a debtor. Wipe the debtor, and the debt doesn't vanish—it just stops being *owed*, and becomes instead a thing that simply happened to people, unowned, unanswerable, forever.

"I know what you're doing," I said.

"Do you?" Cassian sounded genuinely curious.

"You're not buying the keystone. You could take it—your hunters nearly managed it twice. You're buying my surrender dressed up as my rescue. Because a keeper who gives the flame up freely makes the Court's claim legitimate, and a thief who takes your deal proves your whole philosophy: that everyone can be bought, that warmth is just another commodity, that the covenant was always a fool's dream." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You don't want the ember. You want the *precedent*."

For the first time, something moved behind Lord Cassian's patience. Small. Controlled. But there.

"Careful," he said quietly.

"No," I said. "I don't think I will. That's rather the point of me now." My hands were shaking. I looked down at them—thief's hands, scarred and clever, hands that had never once in thirty years given anything away—and I thought about Maren's riddle. *Only someone with empty hands can carry it.* I'd assumed it meant poverty. Renunciation. Some monkish trick of purity.

Standing there in the dark, I finally understood it meant something else entirely. Empty hands weren't hands that had never held anything.

They were hands that had *let go*.

"I'm keeping the keystone," I said. "And I'm keeping the guilt too. Both of them fit in the same pair of hands, as it turns out."

Cassian sighed, and rose, and brushed ash from his beautiful coal-colored coat. "Pity," he said, and meant it, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "I did hope to avoid the unpleasant version."

"There's an unpleasant version?"

"Wendell." He smiled at me, warm as a hearth, cold as the space between stars. "There was always going to be an unpleasant version. You simply chose when it arrived." He snapped his fingers, and down in the pass, torches bloomed along the whole length of the trail—a hundred of them, two hundred, the Court's riders waking all at once like a body deciding to stand. "You have until dawn. Then I come up the mountain and take what my claim says is mine, and everything your friends have done—Bramble's bargain, Odo's debt, the girl's seven years—becomes collateral in a legal proceeding."

He paused at the edge of the firelight.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—the erasure offer stands until sunrise. Men change their minds at the edge of things. It's practically a law of nature."

Then he was gone down the pass into the torchlight, and I was alone with the stars, and the ember humming against my ribs like something alive, and behind me in the dark of the cave, a voice I knew too well:

"How much of that did you hear?" I asked Isolde's shadow.

"All of it," she said, stepping out with the knife already in her hand and something terrible and steady in her eyes. "Every word." She looked at me—at my shaking hands, at my face, which I couldn't hide anything from anymore. "He offered to wipe you clean. And you said no."

"It wasn't a no, exactly."

"It sounded like a no."

"It was," I admitted. "It was the hardest no I've ever said. Harder than any lock I've ever picked."

She came and stood beside me at the mouth of the cave, and together we watched the torches burning in the pass below, hundreds of them now, a river of fire climbing toward us in the dark.

"Dawn's in three hours," Isolde said.

"Then we'd better decide," I said, "what we're going to give the Hearth before he takes it from us."

Below us, the first horn of the Ember Court sounded—a long, low note that rolled up the mountain like a wave deciding where to break—and high above, the stars seemed to lean closer, as if they too were waiting to see what my hands would do next.
