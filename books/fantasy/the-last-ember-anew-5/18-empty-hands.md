## 18. Empty Hands

The riddle had been sitting in my chest for three days like a swallowed stone.

*Only someone with empty hands can carry it to the Hearth.*

I'd heard Maren say it and thought it was poetry. Scholars loved poetry; it let them say true things without having to mean them. But standing at the threshold of the First Hearth with the keystone memory humming against my ribs, I finally understood that Maren had meant it the way a knife means it.

Empty hands didn't mean clean hands. There was no such thing, not for me. Empty hands meant *empty*. Every stolen memory, poured out. Everything I had ever taken from another person's life, given back to the fire.

Everything.

"Say it again," Isolde said beside me. "Slowly. I want to hear you choose it."

The First Hearth was nothing like I'd imagined. After weeks of road—after Vespertine's gilded rot and the salt flats' white screaming silence—I'd expected a temple, a fortress, something built by hands that wanted to be remembered. Instead it was a ring of black stones on a mountaintop where two skies met, the mortal one and the other one, folded together like hands in prayer. And in the center, a hearth. Just a hearth. A firepit of old granite, cold as a closed eye, wide enough to stand in.

The last ember was supposed to be there. It wasn't. It had been dying for seven years, ever since her father carried its spark out into the world and died at the edge of a warded valley, pressing it into the hands of a thief.

"It doesn't make sense," I said. "If I pour everything out, I won't be me. The hoard is—" I stopped. That wasn't honest either. "The hoard is all I have."

Isolde looked at me the way she always did when I lied badly: patient, and a little sad, like watching weather come in. "You told me once that you stole your first memory at nine years old. A baker's joy, off a boy who'd just gotten bread for free." She turned her palm up. "Do you remember how it felt?"

"That's mine now. That's the point of stealing. It becomes yours."

"Do you remember how it felt?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Reached for the memory—it should have been right there, first snowfall-warm, the boy's grin, the taste of crust—and found it dimmed. Not gone. But worn, the way a coin wears when a thousand hands have held it. Seven years of carrying other people's lives had rubbed the shine off every single one.

"No," I admitted. "Not really. I remember that I have it."

"That's what empty means," she said. "It's not that you never held anything. It's that you were never holding anything at all."

* * *

Bramble found me an hour later, sitting on the hearthstone with my boots off, because some part of me apparently believed bare feet made a man honest.

She sat down without asking, which was Bramble all over. Her exile-mark glowed faintly under her sleeve—the Court's brand, the price of her bargain in Vespertine—and she caught me looking and pulled the sleeve down hard.

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say *you shouldn't have traded your mark for our passage*, and then I was going to say *shut up, thief*, and then we'd both feel worse. Skip it." She pulled a flask from her coat and drank, then offered it. I took it. It tasted like pine and bad decisions. "Odo used to drink this. He said it put hair on your chest. He also said he could beat Cassian in a fair fight, so his judgment was suspect."

My throat tightened. Odo, who was buried under a cairn at the pass with his sword planted like a fence post. Bramble hadn't cried yet. I suspected she was saving it for a day with better lighting.

"Bramble." I turned the flask in my hands. "If I do this—if I pour it all out—I might forget you. All of you. The scholar thinks the hoard is what primes the flame, but nobody knows what's left after."

"I know."

"And you're still here?"

She snorted. "Wendell. You absolute disaster of a man. Do you know what I did before exile? I cursed a lordling's well so it ran with truth for a year. Whole county had to live honestly for twelve months. Weddings collapsed. Confessions everywhere. It was chaos and it was the best thing I ever did." She took the flask back. "Truth costs. That's not a reason to stop drinking."

"That's terrible wisdom."

"It's hedge-witch wisdom. We don't get the good kind." She stood, brushed off her skirts, and paused. For a moment her voice went quiet in a way I'd never heard from her. "For what it's worth—I hope you forget the bad ones. The widow's grief. The things you took to survive. Keep the stew night, if any of it survives. Keep the stew night."

Then she walked away before I could answer, which was also Bramble all over.

* * *

Night came, and with it the others' sky.

That's the only way I can describe it. From the Hearth's ring, the heavens weren't one heaven. Stars hung in two depths at once, ours and theirs, and where they overlapped they burned double-bright. Somewhere beyond that folded light, the Ember Court was gathering its riders. Somewhere down the mountain, Cassian was climbing with legal sanction and a counterfeit claim and enough soldiers to make our deaths a formality.

We had until dawn. Maybe less.

Isolde stood at the hearth's edge, her father's coat around her shoulders even though it fit her badly. She'd worn it since the Ashwood. I think it was the closest thing to a grave she could carry.

"When I was small," she said, without preamble, "he told me about the covenant. He said the flame wasn't kept alive by magic. Magic is just what leaks through when it burns. He said it burned because people *fed* it. Willingly. That was the whole covenant—one generation choosing to give warmth to the next." Her jaw worked. "And then someone decided taking was easier than giving. And the world's been running on theft ever since. Your trade. Vespertine. Hale's ledger. All of it downstream of one person who wouldn't share a fire."

"The stranger," I said. "Your father. He gave instead. Carried the spark out to hide it."

"He gave it to *you*." She turned to face me fully. "I've hated that for months. That he chose a stranger over coming home. Over me." Her voice cracked and she let it, didn't fight it. "But I read Maren's translation of the seal. Wendell—he didn't hide the spark. He *entrusted* it. There's a difference. Hiding is fear. Entrusting is hope. He hoped you'd be the kind of man who could carry it."

"I'm a thief."

"You're a man who's about to give away everything he has." She stepped closer. "That's the whole covenant. That's the entire religion. One choice, made at a fire, with witnesses."

Behind her, Bramble had come back, and with her a handful of pilgrims who'd drifted up the mountain over the past days—hollow-eyed refugees from Vespertine's memory-debt rows, a mute child who'd been sold blank and half-bought back, an old woman who pressed her hand briefly to my shoulder and said nothing. Witnesses. The Hearth had drawn them the way a wound draws flies, or the way a lamp draws moths. I couldn't tell which, and maybe it didn't matter.

"Start when you're ready," Isolde said.

I stepped into the cold hearth.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about giving back a stolen memory: it knows the way home.

I raised my hands over the dead granite and reached into the hoard—not with my theft-sense, which was a lockpick's touch, but with something clumsier. An open grip. And the first memory rose up like a fish rising to bait I hadn't meant to cast.

The widow's first snowfall.

I'd taken it in chapter one of my sins, so to speak—a grieving woman in the Hollow, her husband three months dead, and I'd lifted the last time she'd seen snow fall on him alive because grief-saturated memories fetched the best price. I'd told myself she wouldn't miss it. Grief was weight. Who wants to carry weight?

I let it go.

It left me the way breath leaves a body, and for one white instant I *was* her—snow ticking against a window, a man's laugh from the yard, the specific gold of lamplight on a winter afternoon—and then it poured out of my palms as light, thin and silver, and sank into the cold stone. The granite warmed. Barely. One degree of warmth. One candle's worth of covenant.

"Oh," whispered someone behind me. The old woman.

I reached for the next one. The baker's boy, nine years old, bread for free, joy like a struck bell. Out. Light. Warmth.

A soldier's homecoming, taken off a drunk in a market town. Out. Light.

A mother's lullaby, hummed into a baby's ear, sold to a rich man in Vespertine who wanted to feel loved by someone, anyone, even secondhand. Out. Light. The stone began to glow like a banked coal.

Each one was a small death. That's the truest sentence I'll ever write. Not my death—their deaths, the little resurrections I'd been hoarding, all the moments other people had lived that I'd caged inside me and called *mine*. Setting them free hurt exactly as much as stealing them had felt good, and the symmetry of that was its own kind of justice.

And with each one, I felt myself thinning.

Not forgetting—not yet. But the walls between me and the memories came down as they left, and I discovered the horrible architecture of my whole life: I had built Wendell Quintana out of other people's rooms. Take away the borrowed years and there wasn't much house left. A boy in a valley. Loneliness dressed up as contentment. A locked door I'd never dared open, humming against my ribs.

The keystone.

I saved it for last, or it saved itself, rising slow and heavy while the lighter memories streamed out in ribbons of silver-gold. The hearthstone was warm now, properly warm, and above us the doubled stars had begun to brighten as if leaning closer. The pilgrims had their hands raised. Isolde's face was wet and shining. Bramble was swearing softly in hedge-cant, which I understood, by then, was her way of praying.

And I understood, with the clarity of a man who has almost nothing left, why the stranger had waited seven years to die at the edge of a valley full of thieves.

He hadn't been hiding the spark.

He'd been *choosing* me.

* * *

The last of the ordinary hoard drained out of me as gray dawn touched the peaks. I swayed in the hearth like a man standing in a river current. My hands were empty. Truly empty, for the first time since I was nine years old, and the emptiness wasn't pain. It was terrifyingly close to peace.

"Wendell." Isolde's voice, very careful. "The seal. Maren said—"

"I know what she said." Open it wrongly and it destroys itself. Open it with full hands and it burns the bearer to ash. Open it empty, by choice, witnessed—and it becomes what it was always meant to be. Not treasure. Not weapon.

A gift.

I drew the sealed memory out of the last hidden place inside me, deeper than the hoard, the place where I kept the things I actually valued. It pulsed against my palms like a second heart. Seven years I'd carried it. Seven years of dread, of the locked-door hum, of wondering what dying man would press his most precious possession into a pickpocket's hands.

I thought of his daughter standing ten feet away, wearing his coat.

I thought of a message I'd glimpsed once, half-opened in a cave on the salt flats: *forgiveness*, and *by choice, not duty*.

I opened my hands.

Light came out of the seal like dawn coming out of a mountain—slow, then all at once, then everywhere. It didn't burn. It *recognized*. It poured over my empty fingers and through them and into the hearth, and the dead granite roared awake, and flame stood up in the First Hearth for the first time in seven years, tall and gold and hungry, and the doubled sky above us shivered—

—and went dark.

Every star. Both heavens. Simultaneously.

The flame guttered low, blue at its heart, and a cold wind came down the mountain that smelled of iron and court-oil and too many horses, and from the darkness below the tree line came the sound of a hunting horn, long and deliberate and close.

Cassian had not waited for dawn after all.

Isolde drew her father's sword. Bramble's hands filled with green fire. Behind me, the new flame bent toward me like a living thing, like a child reaching for the only parent it knew, and I understood with sickening clarity that the reforging had only begun—that the covenant needed more than a spark, it needed a keeper, and the ritual was not finished, could not be finished, not while riders were cresting the ridge with drawn steel.

"Hold the line!" Isolde shouted, already moving. "Whatever happens—no one touches the hearth!"

The horn sounded again. Closer.

And the flame in my hands—because it was in my hands now, I could feel it, warm and terrified and *mine to keep*—waited to see what I would choose.
