## 20. Blank Pages

The first thing Wendell knew was warmth in his hands.

Not the warmth of a fire he'd lit or a hearth he'd huddled beside—something older than that, something that lived under his skin and pulsed there like a second heartbeat. He opened his eyes to a ceiling of pale stone veined with gold, and the light that fell across it came from no window he could name.

He sat up too fast. The world tilted, righted itself, tilted again.

He was lying on a low bed in a round chamber, and someone had covered him with a wool blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary. His palms—when he raised them into the light—bore marks he had never seen before: two small embers, branded into the flesh of each hand, glowing faintly amber, not painful but *present*, the way a scar is present, the way a name is present.

"What," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on the second word, dry as old paper.

"You're awake." A woman's voice from across the room. "Don't move yet. Drink this first."

She crossed to him with a clay cup steaming in her hands—a broad woman with iron-gray hair cropped short and burn scars laddering one forearm. She looked at him the way you look at a stranger. And that was fine; she was a stranger. But behind the stranger-look was something else, something that didn't belong on a stranger's face at all: relief so raw it looked almost like grief.

He took the cup because his hands wanted something to hold. The tea tasted of honey and something bitter underneath.

"Where am I?" he asked. "Who are you?"

The question landed on her face like a physical blow. She caught it, absorbed it, and answered steadily. "You're at the First Hearth. My name is Bramble. I'm a hedge-witch—or I was, before my exile got rescinded about an hour ago by people with better things to do than argue paperwork."

"First Hearth." He turned the words over. They should have meant nothing. They meant nothing. So why did his chest ache when he said them, the way it ached when he woke from a dream of a house he'd lived in as a child?

"And you are?" he prompted.

"I told you. Bramble."

"No—I mean—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know why I asked. Obviously you're Bramble. You just said."

But it wasn't obvious. That was the thing crawling up through him now, cold and patient. None of this was obvious. He knew her name because she'd given it, and yet when he tried to place her—to find the shelf in his mind where a person like her would be stored—he found empty air where the shelf should be.

"How did I get here?" he asked.

Bramble's jaw tightened. "That's a longer answer."

"Then give me the long version."

"Not mine to give." She set down her cup and stood. "There's someone who's been waiting three days for you to wake up. I promised her she could have the first word." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't read—couldn't *read*, which frightened him more than anything else, because reading faces was the one skill he had never doubted. "Wendell. Whatever she tells you—and it will be a lot, and some of it will be ugly—remember that she stayed. That's the part I'd want remembered, if it were me."

"My name is Wendell?"

Her face did something complicated. "Yes," she said softly. "It is."

* * *

The woman who came through the door next moved like someone who had learned to walk quietly around dying things.

Dark hair braided back, travel-worn coat, eyes the color of banked coals—the kind of eyes that had seen fire take things and had decided, deliberately, to keep watching anyway. She stopped three paces from the bed. Her hands were shaking. She folded them together to hide it, which told him she was the sort of person who hated being seen shake.

"Wendell," she said.

"You know me."

"I do." She pulled a stool close and sat, knees nearly touching his. Close enough that courtesy demanded names. Far enough that he could leave if he needed to. Someone had taught her that geometry. Someone careful. "My name is Isolde."

"Isolde." It fit in his mouth like it had been there before. That scared him. "I'm sorry—I don't—"

"I know." Her voice didn't waver. "I know you don't. Bramble sent word ahead. You woke up asking questions." She drew a breath that seemed to come up from somewhere below her ribs. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you decide what it means. Can you do that?"

He nodded.

"Do you remember the Hollow? The valley? Warded against magic, stars overhead every night, market days and broker stalls and rules about never leaving?"

"The Hollow, yes." Relief flooded him—at least the ground floor of himself was still standing. "I lived there. I—"

He stopped.

*I survived there by stealing memories.* The knowledge arrived without any memory attached to it, like a door standing open onto a room that had been emptied. He knew the fact of it the way he knew his own name. He could not summon a single instance of it. Not one face. Not one theft. Not one night of trade in a broker's back room.

His cup trembled. Tea slopped over his knuckles, and where it touched the ember-mark on his palm, the mark flared warm.

"What happened to me?" he whispered.

Isolde watched him with those coal-colored eyes and did not look away, and he understood suddenly that looking away would have been easier for her, and that she was refusing easy the way other people refused bad coin.

"The short version," she said, "is that you saved two worlds. The long version starts seven years ago, with a dying man at the border of your valley, and ends three days ago, with you pouring everything you ever stole into a dead flame until it caught." She held his gaze. "Everything, Wendell. Every memory you had taken in thirty years of taking. And then the last thing you gave it wasn't stolen at all. It was a gift you were keeping for me. For us. You fed our whole journey into the Hearth—the road, the fights, the city, the salt flats, the cave. All of it. You burned it so the stars would come back."

Outside the high narrow windows of the chamber, the sky was turning toward evening, and already the first stars were pricking through—thick, bright, unbroken, a sky full to bursting with light.

"They're beautiful," he said helplessly.

"They went out," Isolde said. "Every star over every realm, the night the covenant broke. You brought them back. You just don't remember doing it."

* * *

He made her tell it twice.

Once straight through, in her flat, controlled voice—her father dying at the valley's edge with a sealed memory pressed into a thief's hands; the wards falling; the flight past the borders; the roadhouse where they'd gathered the others; the Ashwood; the city called Vespertine where memories were money and children were sold blank; the scholar with the riddle about empty hands; the ambush at the Hearth's threshold; the guardian named Odo who had held a pass and not come back from it; the bargain Cassian had offered and Wendell had refused; the ritual, the flame, the choice.

The second time, he interrupted constantly. Small questions. *What color was the sandstorm. What did the stew taste like at the roadhouse. Was Odo tall. Did I laugh, in any of it—did I laugh.*

She answered every one. She never softened the details to spare him, and she never embellished them to make herself kinder. When she reached the parts where she had accused him—where she had stood in front of him by a fire and told him he had stolen her father's last words along with everything else—she told that part exactly as it happened, her voice steady and her folded hands white-knuckled.

"You hated me," he said.

"For about a day," she said. "Then I watched you give away the only thing you had left rather than let Cassian buy the flame. No. Before that. In the Ashwood, maybe, when you could have run and didn't. Or the salt flats. I don't know exactly when it changed. Grief doesn't keep a ledger the way thieves do."

"I was a thief," he said. It wasn't a question. The fact of it sat in him like a stone dropped in still water, rings spreading outward through everything he thought he knew about himself. "Thirty years. I took memories from people who were grieving, mostly. Grief makes the memories heavy. Heavy ones sell well."

"Yes."

"And now they're gone. All of them. The stolen ones *and*—" His voice failed. He tried again. "And the others. The ones from the road. The ones you say I chose to give."

"Gone," Isolde confirmed. "The Hearth doesn't borrow, Wendell. It consumes. That was the price. The scholar's riddle—empty hands. You figured out what it meant before anyone else did. You walked into that hearth-chamber knowing exactly what it would cost, and you paid it without hesitating." Her composure slipped, finally, just a hairline fracture. "You hesitated at nothing except leaving us. Odo had to shove you toward the flame. Bramble says you kept turning around."

"I don't remember any of you." He said it plainly, because it seemed like the only honest currency he had left. "I don't remember your face. I don't remember Odo's grave or Bramble's laugh or whatever I was to any of you. You're sitting here telling me I loved you"—because it was obvious, it was written all over all of them, in the way Bramble had looked at him and the way this woman had waited three days by his bed—"and I can't even confirm it. There's just a hole shaped like all of you, and everyone keeps treating the hole like it's a person."

Isolde unfolded her hands at last. They were steady now, or pretending hard enough that it counted.

"That's why I'm here," she said. "We talked about it, the three of us, while you slept. Bramble wanted to let you wake up clean. New man, new life, no debts. She said we'd have no right to hand you thirty years of other people's grief and call it a gift." She leaned forward. "Odo's dead. Bramble lost her exile-mark buying your passage. I spent six years chasing a dead man's memory across two realms. And you—you gave everything. If we let you wake up innocent, then none of it happened. Then Odo died for a stranger, and I mourned a stranger, and you sacrificed yourself for people you'd never met." Her coal-eyes burned. "I won't allow that. You don't get to stay innocent. Innocence would make it meaningless. So instead, you get this: me, and however long it takes, telling you all of it. Every campfire. Every argument. Every time you were unbearable and every time you were brave. We'll rebuild it, piece by piece, out of our remembering instead of yours."

"Why?" His voice came out rough. "Why would you do that? You could walk away. Your father's flame is relit. Your quest is finished. I'm a stranger with burned hands and a criminal's history. The sensible thing—"

"I've had enough of sensible," Isolde said. "My father was sensible. He kept his mission secret, traveled alone, trusted no one, and died alone at a ward-line with his last words pressed into the hands of a boy he'd known for four minutes." Something in her face gentled and broke at once. "He chose you, Wendell. Out of everyone in that valley, out of every soul he might have trusted with the keystone, the dying man picked the thief. And I spent years angry about that, and then I spent months understanding it. Thieves know what things are worth. He must have looked at you and seen the one person in the Hollow who understood that a memory isn't property." She reached out—slowly, giving him every chance to pull back—and closed his ember-marked fingers around hers. The marks flared, both of them, twin small suns, and where their hands met the warmth pooled like something shared. "So no. I'm not going to let you stay a stranger. You'll hate parts of the story. You'll grieve a man you can't picture and miss jokes you can't remember. It's going to hurt."

"Tenderly?" he managed.

"What?"

"Nothing. Go on." But he didn't let go of her hand.

* * *

They talked until the stars wheeled past the high windows. She started at the beginning—the dying stranger, the sealed memory humming against a young thief's senses like a locked door—and she did not stop at the parts that reflected badly on anyone, least of all him. When she described the ambush at the Ashwood and the moment he'd almost run, she watched his face carefully, and he realized she was checking whether the story hurt him the same places it had once hurt him, and finding, to her visible sorrow, that it didn't. Not yet.

"We'll get there," she said. "Told enough times, it becomes yours. Memory's just a story you keep choosing. You taught me that. Or—you will have taught me that. Tenses get strange around you now."

Bramble brought stew at some point, and bread, and sat in the corner pretending not to listen while listening to every word. Once, when Isolde described the roadhouse—the first stitches of the group, the shared pot, Odo complaining about salt—Bramble laughed out loud, sudden and wet, and then apologized, and Wendell found himself laughing too, though he couldn't have said at what, only that the shape of the joke was still funny even with its contents missing. Like laughing at the outline of a ghost.

Later—it must have been near midnight—Isolde ran out of roadhouse and city and salt flat, and paused, cup of tea gone cold in her hands.

"Rest," she said. "Tomorrow I'll tell you about the cave. The keystone. What your father-figure actually said, at the end. That part's yours more than mine."

"He wasn't my father."

"No," she agreed. "But he was the closest thing either of us had to one, for a while there. You'll see."

At the door she stopped, the way Bramble had stopped, as if leaving him required a small ceremony.

"One more thing," she said. "Your hands. The marks. They're not burns—they're keeper-marks. First of the new covenant. The Hearth chose you as much as you chose it. Which means tomorrow, when the emissaries arrive from the Court to formalize it, you'll be expected to speak. To *lead*." A ghost of a smile. "The man who built his whole life on being invisible, and now every realm wants to look at him."

"I don't know how to lead. I don't even know these people."

"You knew how, three days ago." She tapped her own temple. "And I know how, and I'm going to remind you every single day until it sticks. That's the arrangement. That's the deal I'm offering: you get your life back one story at a time, and in exchange, you let us love you again even when you don't understand why."

The door closed behind her.

Wendell lay back against the pillows and raised his hands above his face. The ember-marks glowed softly in the dark, steady as the new-old stars beyond the glass. Below them, faint as breath, he could feel something else—not memory, nothing so kind—but a direction. A warmth leaning somewhere south of his sternum, toward the door Isolde had just closed, the way a compass needle leans north.

He didn't remember her. He didn't remember any of them.

But his hands remembered the flame. And some stubborn, scorched, unkillable part of him was already leaning after her through that door, hungry for the next chapter of a life he'd given away—determined, this time, to earn it honestly, one told-and-retold day at a time.

Outside, a star fell, and the valley's new wards sang it welcome home.
