## 21. Learning Them Again

Isolde had rehearsed the story a hundred times on the road to the Hearth, and now that she finally needed it—now that the man who'd lived every word of it looked at her like a stranger with kind eyes—the words wouldn't come in any of the orders she'd practiced.

They sat in Bramble's rebuilt cottage on the edge of the Ashwood, where the burned trees were pushing up green shoots like stubborn questions. Wendell held a cup of tea he hadn't drunk. His hands, marked with the ember-sigil, rested on his knees as if he'd been told they were important and was waiting for someone to explain why.

"So," Isolde said. "You stole my father's memory."

"That sounds like something I would do," Wendell said carefully.

"It is something you did." She almost smiled. "But you also carried it across half the world when you could have sold it. You gave it back to the fire so the sky would work again. And then you gave up—" her voice caught, and she let it, because lying smoothly to him felt like a betrayal of everything the last year had cost, "—you gave up every memory you had of us. Of me. To light the flame."

He studied her face the way he must have once studied locks. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because Bramble wanted to let you figure it out yourself. She says memories you earn stick better than memories you're handed." Isolde set down her own cup. "I think that's cruel. I think you spent a year earning us and you shouldn't have to do it twice."

Bramble, stirring something fragrant in the hearth, snorted without turning around. "I said no such thing. I said we should tell him *gradually*, with dramatic reveals, because I've always wanted to do a dramatic reveal and this is my one opportunity."

"Bramble."

"He deserves one dramatic reveal, Isolde. Look at him. He's been polite for three days. Polite! The man I knew once threatened a customs official with a spoon."

"I did what?" Wendell asked.

"You'll get there," Bramble said. "Chapter nine. It's a good chapter."

* * *

She told him the story in pieces, over days, because the whole shape of it at once seemed too heavy for anyone to hold.

The first piece she gave him was the border. "You came out of the Hollow with nothing but a sealed memory and a talent for looking harmless. Hunters found you within an hour. I found you within fifty minutes, which is how you know I'm better at my job than they are at theirs."

"What was my job?" he asked.

"Looking harmless. You were excellent at it. It was infuriating." She remembered the ambush by the river, the ember-hunters' nets, the way this stranger had frozen—not from cowardice, she'd learned later, but from calculation, running odds she never shared with anyone. She remembered putting an arrow through a hunter's gauntlet and shouting *run* at a man she had every reason to hate. "I'd spent seven years tracking the keystone. My father died carrying it, and the last thing he did with his life was press it into a thief's hands. I needed to know why him. So I saved your life to ask you a question."

"And did I answer?"

"You lied," Isolde said. "Badly. Then you told the truth, which was worse. Then you kept walking east with me anyway, and I decided that whatever else you were, you were going to be my problem, and I've never regretted a decision less."

Wendell turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Did I love you? Before?"

The question landed in the quiet room like a dropped coin. Bramble's stirring stopped.

"Yes," Isolde said. "Not at first. At first you watched us the way hungry people watch other people eat. But somewhere on the salt flats—you don't remember the salt flats, good, the salt flats were terrible—you stopped watching from outside. And I stopped waiting for you to run." She met his eyes. "So no, I'm not telling you the story so you'll fall in love with me out of obligation. I'm telling you because you paid for these memories with everything you had, and I refuse to let them be wasted just because they're sitting in the wrong head now."

He was silent a long moment. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," he said finally. "Which I realize isn't a high bar, given that I apparently remember nothing before three days ago."

"Oh, it's a very low bar," Bramble called from the hearth. "Wait until you hear about the stew."

* * *

The stew, as it turned out, deserved its reputation.

They made it together in Bramble's kitchen—Odo's recipe, though Wendell didn't know yet whose it was, and Isolde watched him chop onions with the careful concentration of a man defusing something, and felt the old grief and the new hope sit down together at the same table inside her chest.

"The secret," Bramble said, "is that Odo put in too much pepper on purpose, because he believed sadness should be seasoned."

"Who's Odo?" Wendell asked.

And there it was—the piece of the story she'd been dreading. She set down her knife. "Odo was our friend. A disgraced hearth-guardian who joined us at a waystation because you bought his dinner and didn't ask why he was eating alone. He taught me how to hold a shield line. He argued with Bramble about herbs. He died at the Hearth's threshold holding a pass so the rest of us could live." Her throat worked. "We buried him where he fell. We go back sometimes. We're going tomorrow, if you want to come."

Wendell stood very still. "Did he know me? Did he—did we matter to each other?"

"He called you 'little shadow' and meant it affectionately," Bramble said. "He trusted you with his life, which for Odo was roughly equivalent to a marriage vow."

"Then yes," Wendell said quietly. "I want to come."

* * *

The grave sat on a rise above the pass, facing east toward the First Hearth, whose smoke rose now in a thin silver thread visible even from here—a constant in the sky, the way stars used to be constants over the Hollow.

Isolde knelt first and pulled weeds from around the marker stone. Bramble followed, muttering a hedge-witch's blessing that involved, for reasons Isolde had never fully understood, an eggshell and a length of red thread. Wendell hung back, hands clasped behind him like a guest uncertain of the etiquette of grief.

"You can talk to him," Isolde said. "People do."

"I don't know what to say to someone I loved and don't remember."

"Say that."

He crouched beside the stone. For a long time he said nothing, and then, in a voice barely above the wind: "I'm told you were the best of us. I believe it, because everyone who mentions you changes when they do. I'm sorry I can't carry what you gave me. But I'm told I'm good at carrying things I don't understand." He paused. "I hope that counts."

Isolde had to look away, out toward the Hearth-smoke, until her eyes stopped burning.

It was Bramble who broke the silence, briskly, the way she did when feeling got too thick for her taste. "Right. He liked the stone polished. Little shadow, make yourself useful."

Wendell polished the stone with his sleeve, thorough and solemn, and Isolde thought: *he doesn't remember Odo, but he remembers how to honor him.* Maybe that was the thing she'd been trying to say all along. Love wasn't stored in the memory alone. It lived in the hands, too—in what they reached for when no one was instructing them.

* * *

The discovery happened by accident, a week later, in the market at the valley's edge.

The Hollow's wards stood renewed—but open now, doors instead of walls, as the new covenant demanded. People came and went freely for the first time in living memory, and the market had swollen with strangers and strange goods, and Isolde had taken Wendell there ostensibly to buy bread and actually because she'd noticed he flinched at crowds and she wanted him to stop flinching.

They passed a memory-broker's stall—one of the legal ones now, licensed under the new covenant, its wares displayed in glass vials that shimmered like trapped weather. Wendell stopped. Isolde felt him stop, the way you feel a rope go taut.

"There's a woman over there," he said slowly, "who keeps looking at a vial of first snowfall. She's been circling it for ten minutes. She wants it badly enough that it's coming off her like heat."

"Yes," Isolde said. "That's how wanting works."

"No—I mean—" He pressed his palm flat against his sternum, against the ember-mark. "I can feel it. I could take it. I could reach into her wanting and lift the memory right out, the way I used to. The sense never left. It's just—" He frowned, working at it. "It's quieter. Like a door that used to be open and now has to be opened on purpose."

Isolde took his hand away from the stall and held it. "Do you want to?"

"No." He said it fast, and then slower, testing the truth of it. "No. That's the strange part. It's not that I'm stopping myself. It's that taking feels like—it feels like eating off someone else's plate when yours is full."

"Yours is full?"

He looked at her then, and something in his expression made her breath catch—wonder, plain and unguarded, the look of a man discovering a room in his own house he'd never known was there.

"Yesterday," he said, "you told me about the salt flats. Today Bramble showed me where she keeps the good tea and threatened me bodily if I touch it. This morning I polished a dead man's stone and meant every word I said to him. None of those are memories. They're—" he searched, "—they're *mine*. New ones. Ones nobody had to lose for me to have them." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "I spent my whole life thinking memory was something you took. It never occurred to me you could just—make more. That they'd keep. That people would *give* them."

"That's the whole covenant," Isolde said softly. "That's what your father-in-the-flame figured out at the end. Warmth given freely burns longer than warmth stolen. It works for hearths. Turns out it works for hearts."

He turned her hand over in his, studying the calluses, the old arrow-scar along her thumb, as if memorizing it by touch since he couldn't by history.

"Tell me the story again," he said. "The part where I was insufferable. Bramble says I was insufferable."

"You were insufferable," she agreed, and began again from the beginning, and walking home through the open gates with the evening star rising over the renewed wards, she understood that grace wasn't getting back what you lost.

It was this: being loved twice by the same person, and loving him back both times, and finding that the second love fit just as well as the first—as if the heart, like the flame, remembered its own shape even when everything else was ash.

Behind them, in the market, the woman by the broker's stall finally bought her snowfall. And ahead, on the road toward the Hearth, a rider in Court colors was dismounting at the gate with a sealed letter and a face that promised news—good or terrible, Isolde couldn't yet tell—which was, she was learning, how all the important stories started.
