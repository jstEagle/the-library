## 22. The Last Ember Anew

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Wendell remembered that because Isolde had laughed about it—*a year of cosmic dread undone on a market day, as if the sky had only stepped out for bread.* He remembered because remembering was his work now. His whole profession.

He stood at the crest of the Hollow's eastern ridge with a mug of Bramble's terrible spiced cider going cold in his hands and watched the valley below do something it had never done in his lifetime: leave its doors open.

That was the phrase the wards had chosen, or perhaps the wards had simply caught up to what Maren had always said they were—not walls but thresholds, not seals but invitations. Where the old covenant had hummed a closed circuit, the new one breathed. You could feel it if you stood in the right place, a faint warmth underfoot like the memory of a hearth you'd sat beside as a child. Travelers came up from the lowlands now without their horses shying at the border stones. Children from the valley wandered out past the orchards and came back full of stories about colors that sang. And once—a thing that would have been unthinkable two years ago—a hedge-witch set up a stall at the autumn market and sold charms openly, badly, cheerfully, while old Widow Marsh bought three of them out of pure delight at being allowed.

The Hollow had spent a hundred years afraid of magic. Now it was merely cautious of it, the way you're cautious of fire, which is to say: you keep it close anyway.

"You'll let the cider freeze," Bramble said behind him.

"I'm letting it *rest.*"

"It's resting toward cold." She settled onto the flat rock beside him with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions, and pressed her own mug against her sternum like a small warm animal. Her exile-mark had faded over the year from angry red to a pale rose-gold, less a brand than a blush. The Court had formally rescinded it in the spring, in one of Cassian's first acts after his sentencing—which was, everyone agreed, the most humiliating punishment the Ember Court could have devised for him: making him useful. He administered waystation charters now. He was, by all reports, insufferably thorough.

Bramble sipped and grimaced at her own cider, which was her ritual. "Isolde's looking for you."

"Is she."

"She's pretending she isn't. She's reorganizing the ledger shelf for the fourth time today. You know what it means when she reorganizes the ledger shelf."

"I know exactly what it means." Wendell smiled into his mug. Some things you didn't need to steal to know. You just had to pay attention, which turned out to be the harder discipline and the better one.

A year ago he had woken in the grass below the First Hearth with nothing in his head but his own name and a burning pair of hands, and four strangers looking down at him with an expression he hadn't had a word for. He had the word now. It had taken months of Isolde telling him the story—nightly, patiently, sometimes angrily when he asked the same question too many times, once weeping so hard Bramble had to finish the chapter—and the word was *grief*. They had grieved him while he lay there breathing. They had lost every version of him that existed, and what woke up was a stranger wearing his face.

And they had stayed anyway.

That was the part he still turned over at night like a stone with something underneath it. Not that they stayed—he could construct reasons for that, duty, guilt, momentum—but the manner of it. Odo's grave tended every week whether Wendell remembered the man or not. Bramble teaching him hedge-craft from the beginning, delighted to have a student who couldn't compare her to her past self. Isolde reading him the journal his former self had kept on the road, in his own handwriting, in a voice that felt like listening to a stranger who knew all your worst secrets and loved you regardless.

*"You could have told me I was a thief,"* he'd said, early on, horrified at the ledger of his life.

*"You already knew,"* Isolde said. *"You flinched at the word 'Hale' for a month. And besides—"*

*"Besides?"*

*"Because who you were isn't the same as who you'd choose. That was the whole point. Of everything. He understood it at the end and you get to understand it at the beginning. That's not a loss, Wendell. That's a head start."*

He hadn't believed her then. He believed her now, mostly, on good days, standing on ridges watching open doors.

* * *

The Hearth itself sat where the old ward-stone had been, in the deep fold of the valley where no one had built because everyone had always known *not* to. It was smaller than the legends promised—a hearth, genuinely, sized for a room rather than a temple—with a chimney of black stone that carried its smoke up through the air and out past the sky, or so Maren claimed with the serene confidence of a woman who had stopped needing to be provable.

It burned low and gold and patient. It did not need feeding anymore, not since the reforging, since Wendell had poured the last of himself into it and the flame had caught on something deeper than stolen years—the stranger's forgiveness, given freely, the first true fuel it had tasted in an age. But it liked attention. Everyone who tended it agreed on that. Leave it alone too long and the light went sulky, dimmed to embers, until someone came and sat with it and told it about their day.

So Wendell told it things.

That was the Keeper's actual job, as far as he could determine. Not guarding—nothing guarded the Hearth, because the covenant was no longer a lock but an agreement, signed nightly by every keeper in every hearth across both realms, a web of small fires each holding a piece of the warmth. No, his job was simpler and stranger: he sat with the flame and gave it his day, honestly, and in return it gave back the sense—faint, unmistakable—that he was heard.

Today he told it about the widow.

Her name was Marta Ellery and her husband had died in the spring, and last week she had come to Wendell's door with the specific posture of a person carrying something heavy they can't set down. A year ago—two lifetimes ago—he would have read that posture as opportunity. He would have lifted her grief off her like lifting a coat from her shoulders, and sold it to Hale, and she would have walked home lighter and hollowed and never known why the world seemed further away afterward.

Instead he'd made her tea and listened for two hours while she talked about her husband's terrible gardening and his worse singing and the way he'd whistled off-key at funerals because he believed joy should be loud in sad places. And when she left, she was still grieving—but she was grieving *forward*, carrying him with her instead of having him carried away. At the door she'd paused and said, "They told me you used to take memories. Before."

"Yes," Wendell said. "I did."

"And now?"

"Now I help people keep them."

Marta Ellery had looked at him for a long moment with wet eyes and said, "Good," in a voice like a door being opened, and gone home into the evening with her grief intact and her husband intact inside it.

The Hearth had glowed a little brighter when he told it that. Or maybe he'd imagined it. He chose not to imagine it away.

* * *

Isolde found him at dusk, as he'd known she would, climbing the ridge path with a lantern she didn't need because the stars were out in force tonight—the anniversary, the whole valley calling it that now, though technically the stars had returned in the spring and this was just the anniversary of their leaving. Nobody corrected anybody. Anniversaries weren't about accuracy; they were about choosing a day to be grateful on purpose.

"They're setting up the long tables in the square," she said, sitting close enough that their shoulders touched. Below, lanterns were blooming across the Hollow like slow fireworks, and the smell of roasting chestnuts climbed even up here. "Bramble claims she's making her festival stew again. There's a petition circulating to stop her."

"There are signatures. I've seen them. She's collecting them herself and framing them."

"That sounds like her." Isolde pulled her cloak tighter and looked up. The sky was thick with stars, more than the Hollow had ever held before the darkness—Maren said the covenant's renewal had widened something, that the old wards had been filtering the sky as much as sealing it, keeping out not just magic but *distance*. "Do you ever miss them? Your stolen ones? Even now?"

Wendell considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid for it dearly. "I miss them the way you miss a house you lived in. I know I was happy there. I know there was a night on the salt flats when you laughed so hard you fell off the cart, because you tell that story every time we pass the flats and your whole face changes. But it's secondhand now. Like hearing about a brother I never met." He turned his mug in his hands. "Sometimes that hurts. Mostly it doesn't. Mostly it feels like—" He searched. "Like being handed a book where someone's written notes in the margins telling you which parts mattered. Most people have to find out themselves which parts matter. I got the map."

"Your former self would hate that metaphor. He hated maps. Got us lost twice."

"He got us lost twice *on purpose*, according to my notes. He wanted more time on the road." Wendell smiled. "Apparently he wasn't ready for it to end."

Isolde was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful weight she saved for important things. "Odo's sister wrote again. From the coast. She wants to come for the winter festival. To see the grave. To meet—" she gestured, including him, the valley, all of it, "—us."

"Then she should come."

"She'll ask you what he was like. You won't remember."

"No," Wendell said. "But I have his journal too. And Bramble remembers everything, and she does impressions. Apparently he snored in three distinct movements. We'll manage." He bumped her shoulder. "We always manage. That's sort of our defining feature."

She laughed—the real one, unguarded, the one he had learned secondhand from stories and then firsthand from a thousand ordinary evenings, and which remained, every single time, the best sound in either realm.

Below them, the first fiddle struck up in the square.

* * *

He went down among the lights because that was where he belonged now, which still surprised him. The Hollow had made him invisible for thirty years and he had called it safety, and it had taken losing everything—including the self who needed hiding—to learn that invisibility was just loneliness wearing armor.

The square was full. Farmers and lowland traders and a delegation of hearth-guardians from Vespertine in their grey wool, and children everywhere, because the valley had quietly begun having children again, or perhaps had always been having them and he'd simply never noticed, never been the kind of man notices arrived for. Now they noticed him constantly. Keeper, they called him, with the solemnity of the very young, and they brought him things—odd rocks, questions, grievances ("Tam says the wards will let monsters in and Tam is WRONG")—as though he were a well they could draw certainty from.

He gave what he could. He never pretended to more.

Near the long tables, Widow Marsh was teaching a lowland merchant to dance a step that hadn't been danced in the Hollow since before the wards, and getting it wrong with tremendous authority. Bramble's stew steamed in a cauldron the size of a bathtub, defended by a hand-lettered sign: NO REFUNDS. NO PROPHECIES. NO, IT'S SUPPOSED TO TASTE LIKE THAT. Someone had pinned the petition signatures to the table beside it, framed in pine, exactly as threatened.

And there—at the edge of the firelight, half in shadow, holding a cup of cider and looking around with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether they belong—stood a young man Wendell didn't know. Lowland, by his boots. New.

Wendell had learned to read that stillness fluently. He crossed the square, took a cup of Bramble's stew, and handed it over.

"Festival rule," he said. "Nobody eats alone on star-night. House tradition. Very old. Invented last year."

The young man blinked, startled into a smile. "Thank you. I'm—I'm passing through. I heard the wards were different here. Open. I didn't quite believe it."

"Most people don't, the first time." Wendell followed his gaze up to the black stone chimney rising above the rooftops, and beneath it the warm gold glow that never fully left the square these days, like the valley had learned to blush. "Come by the Hearth tomorrow if you like. I'll show you how it works. How it *really* works—it's not what the stories say."

"What do the stories say?"

"A hero fed it his memories and died a little and everyone lived happily ever after." Wendell shrugged. "The truth's better. A thief learned that some things can't be stolen, only given. And once he gave one, he found out giving gets easier." He raised his cup slightly, to the young man, to the fire, to the impossible generous dark overhead. "You're welcome to learn it too. That's the whole point of doors."

The stars wheeled slowly overhead, ancient and attentive. The Hearth burned. Somewhere in the crowd Isolde was laughing, and Bramble was lying about her stew, and Wendell Quintana—first Keeper of the new covenant, owner of exactly one memory and earning more every day—stole nothing at all.

Everything worth having, he had finally learned, was given freely.

And tomorrow, he thought, watching the stranger take his first sip and visibly reconsider his opinion of hedge-witch cooking, tomorrow there would be more story to tell the fire. There always was, now.

That was the miracle. That was the ember, anew.
