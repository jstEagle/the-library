## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison came, as it always did, in the honey.

Ada Dravenmoor stood in the king's private pantry with a silver spoon in his hand and steam rising off the bowl of porridge, and he knew before his tongue touched it. He always knew. Not by sight — the kitchen staff of Vell Hollow were loyal to the point of dullness, and Cook Marta would sooner cut off her own hand than adulterate a dish — but by some older sense, some animal instinct that had been fed and groomed in him for thirty years until it sat up and growled on command.

Bitter almond. Underneath the honey, underneath the cream and the crushed hazelnuts and the dried apples from the eastern orchards, something sweet-rotted and faintly bitter, like the smell of a candle snuffed too close to your nose.

He took the spoon anyway.

That was the whole of the profession, if you wanted to understand it truly: not the knowing, but the taking anyway. A taster who refused a dish was a taster who told the king nothing. The court needed to see him eat. They needed to watch his throat work, count his heartbeats, wait out the long gray minutes between the first swallow and certainty. Ada lifted the spoon, blew across it once — habit, though poison cared nothing for heat — and placed it on his tongue.

Almond. Cream. Salt. And there, curled beneath it all like a worm in fruit: *cyanide*, or its cousin, some preparation drawn from peach pits or laurel water, steeped long enough that a lesser man might mistake it for flavoring. It was well made. That was the insult of it. Someone had taken time.

"Your Majesty," Ada called through the pantry door, his voice level, "don't touch the porridge."

* * *

King Aldous of Vell Hollow was sixty-one years old, gouty, fond of hunting dogs and bad poetry, and had survived eleven attempts on his life, all of which had failed at Ada's tongue before they ever reached the royal stomach. He received this news the way he received all such news: with a sigh and a gesture toward the window, as if the morning itself had disappointed him.

"The orchard steward's son," Aldous said. "Or someone bribed by the orchard steward's son. Or no one at all, and my porridge has simply turned on me in my old age." He looked at Ada. "How much did you take?"

"A spoonful."

"And?"

"I'll know more by noon."

This was the arrangement. Ada ate everything first — every meal, every cup, every candied thing set before the king — and whatever entered Ada entered in doses small enough to survive, building in his blood a tolerance no apothecary could chart. Thirty years of small deaths, swallowed daily. His body was a fortress whose walls were built from sieges. The physicians called it *inurement*. The servants called it, behind their hands, *the Taster's Grace*, as if it were a blessing rather than an accumulation.

By noon, Ada's gums would ache and his vision would swim at the edges and he would vomit into the privy with the practiced quietness of a man who had done it four hundred times. By evening, he would be fine. By tomorrow, he would do it again.

"You could refuse," the king said, watching him. "Send the dish back. Let the guards interrogate the kitchens."

"And learn nothing," Ada said. "The dish tells me more than the cook does. Whoever made this used laurel water aged at least a season — that's patience, Majesty. Patience means money, or devotion. I'd rather they think their poison reached you than let them know we caught it."

Aldous studied him for a moment with eyes gone pale and watery but not yet stupid. "You take a strange pride in being poisoned, Dravenmoor."

"It isn't pride, Majesty. It's craft."

It was, in fact, pride. Ada knew this the way a man knows a scar is ugly — intimately, and without much interest in changing it. There was a satisfaction in the work that he had stopped examining years ago. When the almond bloomed bitter under his tongue and he named it, silently, correctly, *bitter almond, laurel derivative, aged*, he felt the small cold click of a lock turning true. The world was full of people who wanted the king dead, and Ada was the hinge on which all their cleverness broke. That was something. Wasn't it? That was a shape a life could take.

He was thirty-eight years old. He had never married, never fathered a child, never kept a friend closer than arm's length, because a taster's life expectancy was a joke told in kitchens, and because — though he would never have said it aloud, barely admitted it to himself — he had never found anything he wanted enough to be afraid of losing.

Fear, Ada had learned, was just wanting with the skin peeled back. He had spent his whole life keeping the skin on.

* * *

After the king breakfasted safely on bread and salted meat — plain food, food without history — Ada walked the long gallery toward the tasting chamber where the day's dishes waited, and the palace woke around him in its usual order. Servants with buckets. Priests of the Hollow Faith murmuring over doorways, touching the little iron charms that hung above every threshold in Vell Hollow. A scullery boy scrubbing the flagstones, pausing to bob his head at Ada with the particular mixture of awe and revulsion the staff reserved for him. They thought his tongue was uncanny. Some of them thought it was cursed.

They weren't wrong to wonder. Everything in Vell Hollow was built around a single fact, repeated to children before they could walk: *magic cannot cross our borders.*

No one remembered exactly when the valley had closed itself. The oldest chronicles said the founders — nine families fleeing the sorcery wars of the outer kingdoms — had poured their power into the land itself, into the soil and the springs and the standing stone at the southern pass, and asked it to keep magic out the way a mother keeps fever out of a sickroom. The land had obliged. For six hundred years, Vell Hollow had been the one place in the world where a witch's curse died at the tree line, where enchanted swords were merely awkward metal, where prophets went mute and demons starved. The border stone still stood at the pass, black and veined with silver, and every traveler who crossed beneath it felt the peculiar flattening silence that the locals never noticed because they had never lived without it.

Inside the valley, the world was only the world. Bread rose because of yeast, not blessing. Babies were born by midwife, not charm. And kings were killed the old way — with knives, with arrows, with poison in the honey.

Which meant that Ada's entire art existed inside that flatness. In the outer kingdoms, they said, poisoners worked with curses and hexes and named malisons; a king there needed wards, not tasters. Here, in the last unmagic country, death came only by hand and chemistry, and so Vell Hollow maintained the last great taster's office in the world, and Ada Dravenmoor was its thirteenth master.

He was proud of that too. He catalogued the pride and put it away with the rest.

* * *

The tasting chamber was a low room off the kitchens with a long table, a fire, and a shelf of clay pots each labeled in Ada's own hand: *nightshade, hemlock, foxglove, laurel water, arsenic, monkshood.* Reference samples. He kept them the way a scholar kept rare books. On the table, the morning's second service waited under linen cloths: spiced wine, marchpane shaped like the king's crest, a honey-cake for the visiting envoy from the hill clans.

Ada tasted the wine. Clean — plums, cloves, the bite of the valley's thin northern grapes. He tasted the marchpane, chewing slowly, letting the almond paste sit against his molars where certain preparations released slowest. Nothing. The honey-cake he broke apart with his fingers first, checking the crumb for discoloration, the glaze for a sheen that didn't belong.

"You look tired, Master Dravenmoor."

Cook Marta stood in the doorway, flour to her elbows, seventy years old and built like the root end of an oak. She was the only person in the palace who spoke to him without the word *master* curdling slightly in her mouth.

"I took a spoonful of the porridge," Ada said.

Her face tightened. "Again? Third time this season. Someone wants him dead badly this autumn."

"Someone wants him dead every season. This one has money." He set down the cake. "Lock the dairy. Whoever did it had access to the honey stores, which narrows us to eleven people, and I'll want to speak to all of them before the day's out."

"And your stomach?"

"Will manage. It always does."

Marta looked at him a moment too long, the way she sometimes did — a look he pretended not to recognize, because naming it would mean answering it, and the answer was a door he preferred to pretend wasn't in the wall. Pity, maybe. Or the question underneath pity: *and what is left of you, Ada Dravenmoor, after thirty years of swallowing?*

"Go lie down," she said instead. "I'll send the dairy staff to you one at a time."

He didn't lie down. He never lay down. Instead he climbed the narrow stair to the taster's quarters — two rooms at the top of the pantry tower, chosen for their proximity to the kitchens and their distance from everyone else — and sat at his desk by the window while the poison began its familiar procession through his blood.

First the ache in the gums. Then the sweat, cool and wrong-smelling. Then the tremor in his hands, which he stilled by pressing them flat on the wood. Out the window, Vell Hollow arranged itself below him in the autumn light: the slate roofs of the lower town, the terraced orchards going gold and rust, the river running silver through the middle of everything, and beyond it all, on every side, the great shoulders of the mountains closing the valley in like cupped hands.

Safe, the mountains said. Kept, they said. Nothing crosses us that shouldn't.

Ada had lived his whole life inside those cupped hands and had never once wondered what it would be like to stand outside them. Wondering was a kind of wanting. Wanting was a kind of fear. He had organized his entire existence around the principle that a man who desired nothing could lose nothing, and the principle had held, and he had been safe, and safe had felt — he searched for the word as the poison fogged the edges of the morning —

Safe had felt like this room. Small, and high up, and clean, and very quiet.

He wrote his report for the chancellor — *laurel water, aged, access to honey stores, eleven suspects* — in his precise, upright hand, blotted it, and set it aside to dry. Then he sat and watched the light move across the valley floor and waited for the vomiting, which came at half past ten, and the shaking, which lasted until noon, and the strange clarity that came afterward, when the body, having survived what it survived, rewarded itself with an hour of terrible lucidity.

It was during that hour — lying on his cot, staring at the ceiling beam, his blood scrubbed raw and honest — that Ada permitted himself, once a month or so, the luxury of the real question.

*Is this all?*

Thirty more years of spoons. Eleven more kings, perhaps, if the work didn't kill him first. A name in the chronicles: *Dravenmoor, thirteenth Royal Taster, served ably.* An unmarked grave in the tasters' plot behind the chapel, where thirteen mounds already lay in a neat row like place settings at a table no one would ever sit at again.

*Is this all,* the lucid hour asked, and the rest of the day answered, as it always had: *it is enough. It must be enough. Enough is the safest thing there is.*

He slept a little. He woke when the light had gone long and amber. Down in the town, the market bells rang the hour before dusk, and somewhere below, the palace gates were being closed for the night — the bar dropped, the torches lit, the watch called.

Later — much later, when the corridors had emptied and the kitchen fires banked low — a scullery boy would come pounding up the tower stairs with a lantern, breathless, saying there was a child at the servants' gate, a barefoot child come out of nowhere in the dark, and that the child would not speak a word any living soul recognized.

But that was hours away yet, and Ada Dravenmoor, royal taster of Vell Hollow, did not know it. He only knew that as the dusk settled blue over the cupped mountains, he stood at his window with his gums still aching and his hands finally steady, looking out at the safe, silent, sealed-in valley that had never once let anything in —

— and felt, for the first time in thirty years, that the silence was listening back.
