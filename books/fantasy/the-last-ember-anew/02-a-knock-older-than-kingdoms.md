## 2. A Knock Older Than Kingdoms

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Not loud — that was the strange part. The servants' gate took a hundred knocks a day: fishwives with bruised deliveries, carters demanding payment, children sent for kitchen scraps who knocked the way children do, all elbow and impatience. This knock was soft. Three taps, evenly spaced, like someone knocking on a door they had known for a very long time and were only now remembering how to find.

Ada was in the taster's quarters, cataloguing the day's poisons in his ledger — *arsenic trace, well-hidden, in the chancellor's wine; none in the king's supper; a bitter herb in the stable master's stew that was carelessness rather than malice* — when the sound reached him through two stone walls and stopped his pen mid-word.

He set the pen down. He did not know why. His body had been making decisions without consulting him all day: the way his hand had hovered over the king's honeyed porridge that morning an extra heartbeat before tasting; the way he had stood at his window after, watching the border mountains go gold and then grey, feeling something turn over in his chest like a coin dropped into a dry well.

He told himself it was nothing. He was thirty-four years old, and he had trained himself out of wanting things so thoroughly that even restlessness felt foreign to him, like a coat cut for another man.

He went to the gate anyway.

* * *

The boy was small — seven, perhaps eight, though there was something in his stillness that made age impossible to guess. He stood barefoot on the frost-slick cobbles in nothing but a shift of undyed wool, and the cold did not seem to be troubling him. That was the first wrongness Ada registered, before the eyes, before everything else. Dusk in Vell Hollow bit through wool and fur alike. This child stood in the last light like a guest at a hearth.

"Lost, are you?" Ada said through the grate. "Kitchen's the other gate. They'll feed you if you sweep something first."

The boy looked up. His eyes were dark — not brown-dark but dark-dark, the way a deep pool is dark, where you cannot see the bottom and suspect there isn't one. And when he spoke, the sound that came out of him was nothing like any language Ada had ever heard, and was also — impossibly, instantly — perfectly clear.

*They told me the valley would refuse me,* the boy said. *It didn't.*

Ada's hand went flat against the gate iron. He heard the words the way you hear your own name across a crowded room — not translated, simply *known*, arriving whole in some chamber of the mind older than learning. He could not have written down the grammar of what the boy said if you held a knife to his throat. But he understood it the way he understood warmth, or the taste of almond.

"That's not—" He stopped. Tried again. "I don't know what tongue that is."

*Neither do I anymore,* the boy said, and this time Ada caught the shadow beneath the words, the way grief can sit inside a sentence without disturbing its surface. *I knew it once properly. Before.*

"Before what?"

The boy tilted his head, considering him. Frost formed on the gate bars between them, feathering outward from where Ada's breath struck the iron — except the boy's breath made no fog at all.

*You taste for the king,* the boy said. It was not a question.

"How would you know that?"

*You smell like swallowing.* A pause, and then, with a child's terrible directness: *Like a thousand small deaths that didn't take. It's a brave smell. It's a sad smell.*

Something moved through Ada then, slow and seismic, like a fault line shifting deep under stone. Not fear. Fear he knew intimately; fear was a tool of his trade, kept sharpened and oiled. This was the other thing — recognition without explanation, the sensation of a lock finding a key it had been carved for centuries ago by a smith whose name no one remembered.

"You'd better come inside," Ada heard himself say. "Before someone sees you."

He unbarred the gate. The boy walked past him without hurrying, without looking back, as if the taster's quarters of the royal palace of Vell Hollow were exactly where he had always intended to end up, and had merely been waiting for the door to be opened by the right man.

*Wren,* the boy said, touching his own chest as he passed.

"Ada," said Ada, and shut the gate behind them both.

* * *

He fed him, because that was what kitchens did and what tasters knew. Ada kept a private larder in his quarters — bread, hard cheese, dried apples, a jar of honey — the rations of a man who ate alone by long habit and had stopped noticing the loneliness of it until tonight, when the silence of his room acquired a second person and suddenly sounded different.

He put the food on the table. Wren looked at it. Wren did not touch it.

"Eat," Ada said. "You're half starved."

Wren shook his head once, small and absolute. Then he pushed the plate across the table with both hands, deliberately, the way you return something you've borrowed.

*I'll wait,* he said. *You eat first.*

"I'm not hungry."

That dark gaze came up and held him, patient as water filling a vessel. *You don't understand yet,* Wren said. *But you will. You eat first. Then I eat.*

And Ada, who had spent twenty years putting other men's food into his mouth so they could live, sat down across from a barefoot child and ate bread and dried apples while the boy watched him with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud. Only when Ada had swallowed the last crumb and drunk from the water cup — only then — did Wren take up the plate and eat, quickly, neatly, with the economy of someone who had learned hunger as a curriculum.

"Why?" Ada asked. "Why does it matter who eats first?"

Wren chewed. Swallowed. Wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, an ordinary gesture that made him abruptly, heartbreakingly a child.

*A table is a promise,* he said. *Where I come from—* and here the boy faltered, and Ada watched something flicker behind those pool-dark eyes, some memory surfacing and being shoved under like a swimmer holding another down. *Where I was, you never eat first. You eat second. Always second. Or you eat what they've already eaten.* He looked at his hands. *You're safe. You're the safest thing I've smelled in a hundred years. So I need you to eat first, or my body won't believe the food is food.*

"A hundred years," Ada repeated quietly. "You're not a hundred years old."

*No,* Wren agreed, and said nothing more about it.

Ada banked the fire and found a blanket — wool, palace-issue, smelling of cedar — and the boy wrapped himself in it and sat close to the hearth with his knees drawn up, and Ada realized he was watching the flames the way other people watch old friends arrive.

"The scholars will want to meet you," Ada said. "There are men in this palace who collect languages the way misers collect coins. Old tongues, dead tongues. If anyone can name what you speak, it's them."

*No,* said Wren, without heat, without turning. *They wouldn't know it. It's older than their knowing.*

"Every language has a lineage. Someone wrote it down."

*This one wasn't written,* Wren said. *It was sung. Into things. Into stones, mostly. Doors especially.* Now he turned, and the firelight did something strange in his eyes — made them less like pools and more like coals banked under ash. *Doors remember it best. That's why I knocked.*

Ada sat with that. Outside, the palace bells rang the evening hour, and the familiar sounds of Vell Hollow settled over everything — cart wheels, a dog barking at the mill, the king's guard changing watch with their armored tread. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a valley that had one law above all others, older than the crown itself, carved into every schoolroom lintel:

*Magic cannot enter Vell Hollow.*

It was not a superstition. It was geology, more or less — the valley simply refused it, the way certain soils refuse certain crops. Sorcery died at the borders like a moth against glass. For six hundred years, the kings of Vell Hollow had slept soundly for precisely this reason, and men like Ada had been needed only because poison, unlike magic, crossed any border it pleased, carried in the humblest vessels: apples, wine, loyal smiles.

Nothing magical could be sitting by his fire wrapped in a palace blanket. Nothing magical could have knocked on his gate.

And yet.

"When you knocked," Ada said slowly, "the sound was wrong. Soft. Like you already knew the door."

Wren smiled then — the first time — and it transformed his face entirely, and Ada felt that buried coin in his chest turn over again.

*All doors are the same door,* Wren said, *if you ask them politely enough in the right language. Yours just hadn't been opened in a very long time. I could tell because it was dusty on the inside.* He touched his own chest, mirroring the introduction from the gate. *Your door, I mean.*

"I don't know what that means."

*I know,* said Wren kindly. *That's all right. It knows what it means. That's enough for now.*

* * *

Sleep would not come, which was itself unusual; Ada slept like a paid debt, every night, the dreamless sleep of a man with nothing pending. Tonight he lay in his narrow bed listening to the boy breathe by the banked fire — except the boy did not quite breathe, Ada noticed somewhere past midnight. The chest rose and fell, but it was courtesy, imitation, the rhythm worn by something that had studied breathing carefully and loved it from a distance.

Near dawn, Wren spoke into the dark without turning around, and his voice in the sleeping room was smaller than it had been by daylight.

*Taster.*

"Wren."

*When they find out what I am, they'll send me away. Everyone sends me away. It's the one thing people agree on, everywhere I've been.* The blanket rustled. *Will you taste for me?*

"What does that mean?"

*If they bring food. If they bring anything. Wine, water, medicine, mercy.* The boy's voice did not waver, but underneath it Ada heard the same note he'd heard on the gate: grief carried without spilling. *Taste everything before it touches me. Promise me. Because where I'm going next, if they send me — the things they give you look kind, and they aren't. They look like home, and they're doors. Everything is doors.*

Ada stared at the ceiling beams, grey in the pre-dawn.

"I'm a servant," he said finally. "Whatever they decide about you, I won't be in the room. Tasters aren't consulted on children."

*You'll be in the room,* Wren said, with the mild certainty of prophecy, and rolled over, and slept — truly slept, or performed it so well that the difference stopped mattering — and left Ada alone with the ceiling, and with the sentence echoing in him like a bell struck in a stairwell:

*Everything is doors.*

* * *

In the morning, Ada did the bravest and most foolish thing he had done in twenty years of quiet service: he lied by omission to the head of the household staff. Cook Marta asked, over the morning bread delivery, whether he'd heard anything odd in the night — a knocking, maybe, near the servants' gate? The night porter swore he'd seen nothing, but the dogs had gone strange at dusk, standing at the fence line in a row, silent, facing the mountains.

"Just wind in the gate hinge," Ada said. "I oiled it."

Marta believed him, because everyone believed Ada Dravenmoor; a man who spends his life swallowing death for the king acquires a face that cannot be suspected of anything.

He went back to his quarters. Wren was awake, watching the door, waiting — and when Ada entered, the boy looked at him with those coal-under-ash eyes and said, softly, in the language that was older than kingdoms and somehow native to a hollow place in Ada that he had never once examined:

*Thank you. First lie is the hardest. After that, they're just doors too.*

And far away — though Ada could not have known it, though it would be days before he learned — at the border stone at the valley's edge, a woman with ember-scars on her hands lifted her head from her long vigil, because she had felt it: a shudder passing through the bedrock of Vell Hollow, faint as a swallowed word.

Something had gotten in.

She began, slowly, to smile.
