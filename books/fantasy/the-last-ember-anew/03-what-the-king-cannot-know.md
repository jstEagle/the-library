## 3. What the King Cannot Know

The taster's quarters sat above the palace kitchens like a thought above a mouth — small, warm, and always slightly damp from the ovens below. Ada had lived there for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once brought another living soul through the door.

He brought the boy up the back stair at midnight, one hand cupped around Wren's small shoulder the way you'd carry a candle through wind.

The child moved strangely for someone so young. Not clumsily — *carefully*, as though he had learned to walk on floors that might be listening. His bare feet made no sound on the stone. When they passed the scullery, where two kitchen boys slept off their wine, Wren stopped and looked at them for a long moment with an expression Ada could not read and did not want to.

*They are loud even sleeping,* the boy said, in that language that should not have existed inside these walls.

Ada heard it plainly, word for word, and answered before he could stop himself. "Everyone here is loud. You'll have to be quieter than quiet."

Wren looked up at him then, and something passed across his face — surprise, or recognition, or grief. He touched Ada's wrist with two fingers, light as ash falling.

Inside the quarters, Ada lit no lamp. He knew every inch of the room by memory: the narrow bed against the north wall, the shelf of tasting spoons ranked by metal, the window that overlooked the herb garden where rosemary grew in rows like soldiers. He steered the boy to the bed and pressed him down onto it.

"Sleep," he said aloud, and then, because it seemed suddenly important, said it again in the old language. The word felt strange in his mouth — round and dark, like a river stone held under the tongue. *Sleep.* Where had he learned it? Who had put it there?

Wren pulled his knees to his chest but did not close his eyes. He watched Ada move around the room, and when Ada finally sat down on the floor beside the bed, back against the wall, the boy reached down and rested his hand on top of Ada's head. Just that. A small cold weight, like a bird landing.

Ada did not sleep either. He lay awake long after dawn came gray through the window, listening to the palace wake below him — the clatter of pans, the barking of orders, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning — and tried to understand what he had done.

He had lied. No. Not yet. But he was about to, and he knew it the way a man knows rain is coming by the ache in his teeth.

The court would learn about the child by midmorning. There was no preventing it; the gatekeeper had seen him arrive, half the scullery had peeked at him over breakfast, and Cook had already begun telling anyone who'd listen about the little ghost boy who wouldn't eat. Secrets in Vell Hollow palace lasted about as long as milk in summer.

The only question was what story Ada would tell when they asked how the boy had come to be hidden in the taster's quarters overnight.

And Ada, who had never told a lie larger than *the porridge is fine this morning*, found that the story arrived in his mind fully formed, smooth and ready, like an egg from a hen.

*I found him wandering the herb garden after dark. I took him in because he was barefoot and shivering. I meant to bring him to the steward at first light.*

Three sentences. Every one of them false or nearly so. He turned them over, testing them for flaws, the way he tested food — and found none. That frightened him more than anything else. It seemed a man ought to be bad at lying the first time, the way he'd been bad at everything else before practice made it invisible.

Below, the king's breakfast was being prepared without him. For the first time in nineteen years, someone else would taste the honeyed porridge. Ada lay in the gray light and felt the wrongness of it like a splinter under a nail.

* * *

Chancellor Merek took his morning walk along the eastern wall at the same hour every day, rain or frost, because men trusted a man who could be predicted. He walked slowly, hands folded behind his back, and he listened — to the guards' gossip, to the gulls arguing over the midden, to the particular quality of silence that meant something was being kept from him.

This morning the silence had a shape. It was small and barefoot and currently sitting in the taster's quarters eating nothing.

"The boy won't eat unless Dravenmoor tastes it first," the under-cook had told him, proud of the anecdote. "Won't touch a crumb. Old Ada tastes it, nods, and then the lad eats like a starling."

Merek had smiled at the time. He was very good at smiling. But the detail had lodged in him like a fishbone, and all through his walk he worked at it with his mind's tongue.

A child who would not eat untested food. A child who appeared at dusk out of nowhere, speaking no language any scholar knew. A child whom the valley had apparently permitted to enter — which was the part that gnawed at Merek worst of all, the part he turned over and over as he paced the wall.

Because things did not simply arrive in Vell Hollow. That was the whole meaning of the place. Three hundred years ago, when the founders had set the border stones, they had built a valley that magic could not cross — not would not, *could not*, the way water cannot run uphill. Everything within those stones was ordinary. Blessedly, boringly ordinary. That was the covenant. That was the safety. Kings paid dearly for that safety, and Merek administered the payment.

So either the child was ordinary — a lost waif from some mountain village, strange only in the way children are strange —

Or the child was not magic at all, but something *sent*, something crafted to slip past the stones the way a key slips past a lock. And if such a thing could be made, then someone beyond the mountains had studied the valley's defenses for a very long time.

Merek stopped at the corner tower and looked east, toward the mountains, blue and distant and patient.

There was a third possibility, of course. One he did not care for at all. That the stones themselves were failing — that whatever power had sealed the valley three centuries ago was thinning, aging, going out like a lamp low on oil. In which case the child at the gate was not an intrusion but a herald, the first drop before the flood.

He had written three letters about it already. None of them said what he truly suspected. Letters could be read.

"Chancellor." A guard saluted. Merek nodded graciously and continued his walk, and behind his pleasant face his thoughts ran cold and quick as meltwater.

The boy would go to the border priests. That much was certain, and soon — the council would demand it by week's end. The priests would examine the child with their instruments and their incantations-that-were-not-really-incantations, just rituals worn smooth of meaning, and they would find either nothing or something, and either answer would trouble Merek.

But before the priests got him, Merek wanted one look at the child himself. An unguarded look. Children were terrible liars; they hadn't yet learned that truth was negotiable.

And there was the matter of Dravenmoor. The royal taster. Nineteen years of perfect service, dull as dishwater and twice as reliable. Merek had never once wondered about the man, which was itself unusual — Merek wondered about everyone — and now, suddenly, there was a question worth asking:

Why had the taster hidden the child? Why had *he* been the one to taste the food?

Unless, of course, the taster already understood the language no scholar knew.

Merek smiled at the gulls, and the gulls, wise creatures, gave him a wide berth.

* * *

The summons came at noon, and it was worse than Ada feared: not the steward, not the captain of the guard, but the full council chamber, with the king presiding and Chancellor Merek seated at his right hand like a blade laid beside a crown.

They had brought Wren first. Ada had argued — calmly, reasonably, with the voice he used to report poisons — that the child should not be separated from the only person he seemed to trust. It was the first argument he had ever won in that chamber, and winning it felt like swallowing something that might or might not be poison.

Now Wren stood beside him, small and silent, while around them the council argued in voices pitched somewhere between concern and contempt.

"He should go to the border priests," said Lord Cassel, whose family had held the eastern vineyards for two hundred years and who spoke of everything, including children, as inventory. "Today. Tonight if possible. The priests will determine what he is."

"And if he's nothing?" asked Lady Ondine, younger, sharper. "A lost child from the high villages? We'd hand a starving boy to the priests' cold cells for a season of examinations?"

"If he's nothing," Cassel said, "a season of bread and walls will do him good regardless."

Ada kept his eyes lowered and his breathing even. Beside him, Wren's hand had found the hem of Ada's coat and gripped it — not pulling, just holding, the way a drowning man holds driftwood he doesn't yet believe in.

"Dravenmoor." The king's voice, mild and tired. Aldric had been king for eleven years and had aged twenty; the crown sat on him like a debt. "You found the child?"

"I did, Your Majesty." The lie rose in Ada's throat, smooth as oil. "In the herb garden, after nightfall. Barefoot. I couldn't learn where he'd come from — he doesn't speak any tongue I know — so I kept him warm until I could bring the matter to proper attention."

Every word true except the ones that mattered. Ada marveled distantly at the craftsmanship. He had spent his life detecting lies in food; he had never considered that lies might have flavor too, and that his own might taste of copper.

"You tasted his food," Merek said. It was not a question, and it was delivered so gently that it took Ada a moment to hear the hook inside it.

"He refused to eat otherwise, my lord. I judged a tasting a small price for keeping a child alive."

"A careful habit," Merek murmured, "for a man whose whole life is careful habits."

Silence, briefly. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked, twice, like a warning.

"The priests," Cassel pressed. "Let us settle it. The boy goes to the border shrine, the priests examine him, and in a month we know whether we have a ward of the crown or a problem."

"And what do the priests actually examine, my lord?" Ada heard himself say, and had the vertiginous sense of watching his own mouth move. "With respect — the border priests are keepers of records and rituals. If the child is ill, they have no physicians. If he is lost, they have no maps. What exactly will they determine, besides whether he survives their hospitality?"

Lady Ondine laughed, quickly, before she could stop herself. Cassel's neck reddened.

"The valley's law is clear," Cassel said. "Anything touched by magic—"

"Then let us first establish that anything has been touched by magic," Ada said. "Has anyone here seen a single sign of it? The boy speaks strangely. So do sailors. He appeared at dusk. So does fog. I have served this crown for nineteen years, my lords, and in nineteen years I have learned that the most dangerous thing in any dish is not the poison you can name. It is the assumption you swallow without tasting."

He heard it as he said it — the taste-testing metaphor deployed in open court, the taster's authority borrowed and stretched. It landed. Even the king straightened slightly.

"Your point, Dravenmoor?" the king asked.

"That we test the boy ourselves, here, before we send him anywhere. Let the physicians see him. Let the scholars try his speech again. And meanwhile—" Ada paused, and made the choice that would define the rest of his life, though he didn't know it yet. "Meanwhile he stays where he has already begun to trust an adult. With me. A frightened child who won't eat is a child who dies quietly in a cell somewhere, and I have spent my career making sure nothing dies quietly on this crown's account."

The chamber was quiet. Wren's grip on his coat had tightened until Ada could feel each small finger like a stitch.

It was Merek who broke the silence, and he did it smiling, which was somehow worse than any frown.

"A moving appeal," the chancellor said. "And perhaps wisdom. Very well — a fortnight's custody, Dravenmoor, under your roof and your responsibility. The scholars will continue their work with the child. And I will visit myself, from time to time. I find I've developed a curiosity about languages."

"As my lord wishes," Ada said, and bowed, and felt the bow go all the way down into some cellar of himself where things were stored that he did not intend to examine.

* * *

That evening he fed Wren stew — venison, root vegetables, bread torn into the broth — and tasted it first, spoonful by spoonful, while the boy watched with those eyes that were too old for his face and too bright for his exhaustion.

*Safe,* Ada said in the old language, handing over the bowl.

Wren ate. Halfway through, he stopped, put down the spoon, and looked at Ada with sudden fierce attention.

*You said the shield-word today,* the boy said. *In the stone-room. You called me protected.*

"I don't know what I said."

*You do.* Wren pressed his own chest, then pointed at Ada. *You have words in you that aren't yours. Someone put them there a long time ago. Someone who loved you.*

The fire popped. Down in the kitchens, the night shift began its clatter, and the palace settled around them like an animal curling up to sleep.

"Who?" Ada asked. His voice came out rough. "Who put them there?"

Wren shook his head slowly, and for the first time since the gate, the child looked afraid — not of the room, or the dark, or the men in the council chamber, but of the answer itself.

*Not who,* he said. *What. And it is still waiting under the mountain. And chancellor-man* — the boy's mouth shaped the foreign title carefully, distastefully — *smells like its smoke.*

Outside the window, far off toward the eastern wall, a lantern was climbing the stairs to the taster's quarters. Steady. Unhurried. Already familiar with the way.

Merek, keeping his promise early.

Ada blew out the candle and stood between the door and the child, and waited to lie again.
