## 4. The Language Beneath Words

The taster's quarters were never meant to hold two lives. They had been built for one man and his solitude: a narrow bed, a basin, a shelf of antidotes arranged by bitterness, a single window that looked out on the kitchen yard where the fires died each night like tired animals. For eleven years the room had fit me the way a glove fits a hand that has forgotten it once had fingers. Now there was a boy in it, sleeping curled at the foot of my bed with his bare feet black from the yard's cold ash, and the room had become something else entirely — a held breath.

Wren did not snore. He breathed the way embers breathe, slow and banked, and twice in the first week I woke certain he had stopped and had to lay two fingers beneath his nose until I felt the faint warm proof of him. He slept with his eyes half open. I told myself this was nothing. Children do strange things. But I had spent my life learning what bodies did when they were afraid even in sleep, and Wren's body was afraid of something no blanket could fix.

By day, we worked.

It began as necessity — the boy could not stay hidden forever on bread crusts and silence — but it became, without my noticing the exact hour of its beginning, the truest work I had ever done. In the mornings I carried him down the back stair before the kitchen woke, and we sat among the sacks of barley where the dust kept our footprints like a diary, and I taught him the valley's tongue one word at a time.

I taught him *flour* by pressing his palm into the soft white slope of it. *Water*, holding his fingers under the pump's cold run. *Salt* — he tasted it off his own skin and made a face so offended that I laughed for the first time in longer than I could measure, a sound that came out of me rusted and strange, like a door opening in a house long shut.

He learned fast. Too fast, some whispering part of me noted; a child should stumble over his plurals, should confuse *spoon* and *moon* for a charming month. Wren took each word the way dry ground takes rain — completely, instantly, leaving no puddle of error behind. By the fourth night he could name every tool on the kitchen wall. By the sixth he was correcting my pronunciation of words I had spoken all my life, tilting his head at me with grave patience until I said *kettle* properly, whatever *properly* meant to ears that had learned language somewhere I could not imagine.

And always, underneath the lessons, the other language moved between us. The old one. His one. The one no scholar in the palace could parse but which fell into my understanding whole, like fruit into an open hand. We would be drilling the word for *bread* and he would say something in that older tongue — not a translation, never a translation, something adjacent, something truer — and I would find I knew what he meant the way I knew the taste of almond: immediately, and with dread.

*Bread,* he said one evening, turning the loaf over in his small hands. And then, in the old language: *bread that remembers the field it came from.*

I stared at him. "What did you say?"

He looked up, surprised I hadn't known. He said it again, slower, and pointed at the loaf, then out the window toward the dark fields beyond the wall, then pressed his fist against his own chest. Bread that remembers. As if food were a ledger of where it had been. As if everything were.

That night I could not sleep. I lay listening to his ember-breathing and turned the phrase over like a stone found in a shoe. It was nonsense. It was poetry. It was, I suspected with a rising unease, simply how he saw the world — a world where nothing was only itself, where a loaf of bread carried its history in its crumb and a boy might carry something heavier still.

* * *

On the seventh night he drew the spiral.

I had given him charcoal ends saved from the hearth and the backs of inventory lists — the taster's quarters produced a great deal of paper covered in columns of *hellebore, none detected* — and he filled them with a ferocity that startled me. Not the scrawls of a bored child. These were deliberate, repeated, obsessive: spirals, always spirals, drawn from the center outward, each ring nested inside the last like the year-rings of a felled tree.

"Spiral," I said, giving him the valley word, because teaching was the shape my love had taken and I did not yet know another.

He shook his head. Tapped the center of the drawing. Said something in the old language — a short word, weighted, the kind of word you feel in your back teeth — and then drew more: quick strokes above the spiral, angular, deliberate. Mountains. Three peaks, and the middle one taller than its brothers, and beneath the middle peak his charcoal dug into the paper hard enough to tear it, scratching out a shape I recognized only because I had seen its cousin on the palace's oldest maps, the ones locked in the chancellor's cabinet: a door. A door under stone, drawn in section, with stairs going down.

"Where is this?" I asked, and heard my own voice come out flat.

He pointed north. Then west. Then he pointed at the wall — through the wall, through the valley's rim, to somewhere past the border stone I had lived my whole life within sight of and never once approached. Then he laid his hand flat against his own chest, over the sternum, and drew his fingers slowly downward, inward, as if reaching into himself, and closed them around something invisible, and offered the closed fist to me.

Open it, the gesture said. Look what I used to have.

"Wren." I crouched to his level. My knees cracked; I was thirty-four years old and felt suddenly every year of it. "Who sold it? Who bought it?"

He understood *sold*. I don't know how — perhaps the word needed no translation anywhere in the world; perhaps betrayal is the one truly universal grammar. His face did something terrible and controlled, the way a grown man's face does, folding grief down into a small tight package to be dealt with later. He took the charcoal again.

This time he drew people. Stick figures at first, then better — he had a sure hand, an artist's economy. Figures tall and thin, wearing crowns that curved backward like horns. Figures seated at a table that stretched the length of the page, set with dishes he rendered in loving detail: bowls, goblets, platters stacked with things that might have been fruit or might have been organs. Above the table he drew the door under stone. Below the table, chained to its leg, he drew a small figure. A child. Barefoot.

Then he took my hand — my big, burn-scarred, poison-calloused hand — and pressed it against his chest, over the place where a soul is said to sit, though I am a practical man and had never believed in the address. Under my palm his heartbeat was slow and deep and wrong, too slow for a boy of eight or nine, steady as a drip in a cistern.

And in the old language, looking me full in the face, he said: *They are eating it slowly.*

I did not need the words translated. Some meanings arrive in the body before the mind. I felt them land in my stomach like swallowed ice.

* * *

I want to record honestly what I did next, because it shames me and shame is a thing I have rarely had room for. I went to the shelf of antidotes. I stood before it a long time — the neat rows, the labels in my own handwriting, the accumulated armor of eleven years — and I reached for the valerian tincture that eases nightmares, because I wanted to give him something, anything, and medicine was the only kindness I knew how to pour.

He refused it. Gently. He pushed the cup back across the table and then, with the gravity of a king declining tribute, tapped the spiral drawing, tapped his chest, and shook his head. *No remedy works,* the gesture said. *The wound is not in the body.*

So instead I did the only other thing I knew. I cooked.

I cannot explain what happened in that kitchen except to say that for the first time in my life I cooked for someone rather than against something. Every dish I had ever prepared had been a test, a gauntlet, a possible murder I was paid to intercept. That night I made porridge with honey and the good cream the dairy girl saved back, and toasted barley over the banked coals until it smelled like the color amber, and stewed dried apples with cinnamon until the whole taster's quarter smelled like a home I had never had. Wren ate until he could eat no more, and then he fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew almost, and I carried him to the bed and he let me, which is a trust I did not know I had earned.

I sat by the window a long while after, watching the kitchen yard go silver with frost, and I examined myself the way I examine a suspect dish: systematically, layer by layer, hunting for the poison in my own heart.

Here is what I found. I found that I had spent eleven years swallowing death in measured doses and calling it purpose, and that it had never been purpose at all — only habit wearing purpose's clothes. I found that I had never wanted anything enough to risk it, and had told myself this was discipline, this was professionalism, this was the taster's creed: *want nothing, lose nothing.* I found that the creed had been a coffin I built board by board and lay down in willingly, and that a barefoot boy who spoke the world's oldest language had kicked the lid open in a single week without even trying.

I wanted him safe. There it was. Simple as salt, ruinous as arsenic. I wanted it more than I wanted my post, my pension, the king's favor, the valley's approval — all the small safe currencies I had been paid in all my life. And wanting, I now understood, was the one poison no immunity could touch. It got in through no mouth. It bypassed the tongue entirely and went straight for the throat.

* * *

Over the following nights, the fragments assembled themselves into a horror with a shape.

He drew the mountain court again and again, and each time the details sharpened as if his memory were a lens coming into focus. The nobles wore their centuries openly — he drew rings upon their fingers, chains upon their necks, and when I asked what they were he traced a figure's face and then, beside it, the same face younger, and younger, and younger, five faces deep, like the layers of an onion peeled back to a core that was not human at all. Their table was law, he indicated. Whatever was served must be eaten. Whatever was bargained must be paid. He drew scales, and under one pan a heart, and under the other a heap of small round things I finally understood were years.

And always, threaded through it, the trade. He showed me hands exchanging — one pair small, one pair crowned. He showed me a document, or what stood for one, covered in the same spirals he drew obsessively, spirals that I began to understand were not decoration but script, signature, seal. Someone had signed. Someone had handed a child's soul across a table in exchange for something, and the buyers had carried him down through the door under stone, and they had been eating him slowly ever since — a spoonful of wonder here, a swallow of laughter there, patient as winter — until what remained had done the unthinkable, the impossible, the thing Chancellor Merek would give his teeth to know:

He had escaped. He had walked out from under the mountain, across country where roads rearrange themselves, through the border of a valley where magic cannot follow — and the moment he crossed, whatever hunted him had lost his scent, because the valley swallows magic the way I swallow poison. He had come to the one place in the world his owners' long arms could not reach.

He had come to me. To the food-taster. To the man whose entire life was built around the principle that you can take a little death at a time and survive it.

Why me? I asked him, one night, in the dark, in the old language I still could not speak but somehow could ask in — meaning poured through gesture and tone and the strange sympathy between us. Why did you come to my gate?

He considered the question with the seriousness he brought to everything. Then he touched my lips with one finger. Touched his own. Touched the spiral drawing, the door under stone, the table where bargains were law.

*Tasters,* he said, in that tongue older than kingdoms, *are the only honest mouths left.*

I lay awake long after he slept, and outside the frost thickened on the glass, and far to the north and west, past three mountains and a border stone, something ancient sat at a long table and chewed, patiently, on what remained of a child.

I had spent my whole life guarding one man's plate. Now I knew the taste of a different hunger entirely, and I knew — the way I knew almond, instantly, and with dread — that sooner or later I would have to walk out of the only safe valley in the world and carry this boy back toward the door under stone.

I did not yet know what it would cost. I only knew that for the first time in eleven years, I wanted something worth the price.

Three days later, the king did not rise from his bed.
