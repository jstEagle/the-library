## 6. Poison Is a Kind of Prayer

The king began to die on the morning the frost came late.

I knew it before anyone told me. I had learned to read Vell Hollow's rhythms the way a sailor reads weather — the bells, the kitchen fires, the particular quality of silence in the corridors when something had gone wrong behind the good doors. That morning the silence had a shape. It moved through the palace like cold water finding the cracks in a hull, and it pooled outside my quarters until I opened the door and found Tam, the youngest of the king's chamber-servants, standing there gray as porridge.

"He won't wake properly," Tam said. "He wakes and then he — goes somewhere. The physicians can't name it."

I dressed without speaking. There was nothing to dress *for*, exactly; no one had summoned me, because no one would think to summon the food-taster for an illness that hadn't come from food. But I went anyway, down the servants' stair with its worn hollows in each step, past the laundry yards where steam rose white against the blue morning, into the royal wing where the candles burned even in daylight because someone had decided the king's rooms should never know dark again.

He lay small beneath the covers. Kings are large things — thrones make them larger, crowns larger still — but illness had folded King Aldous down into the size of the man I'd first tasted for twenty years ago: a tired body that liked honey in its porridge and complained about the fish on Fridays. His skin had gone the color of candle tallow. His breathing was slow and shallow, like a man listening for something far away.

"Show me everything he's eaten," I said.

The chamberlain produced the records. I read them twice. Every dish accounted for. Every dish tasted — by me, by my own tongue, three mornings running, including the last meal before the king slipped beneath whatever surface he was drowning under. I had sat at the little table in the antechamber with the silver spoon and the portioned plate, and I had swallowed, and I had pronounced it clean.

"Nothing," I said. "There was nothing in the food."

"Then what is this?" the chamberlain demanded, and his voice cracked on the question, because the question was bigger than he wanted it to be. "What kind of poison spares the taster and kills the king?"

I didn't answer him. But I already knew, in the place where knowing lives before you're willing to look at it, and the knowledge was so cold that I stood very still for a long moment while the room went on around me — physicians murmuring, priests being sent for, Tam wringing his cloth in the corner.

Because there was only one kind of poison that could pass my table untouched. Poison that wasn't poison at all.

* * *

I want to explain what it means, what it has meant all these years, to be the thing between a king and his death.

My father was a taster before me, and his mother before him, and we are not a family so much as a practice — a lineage of tongues trained from childhood on graduated doses, building the immunity dish by dish, year by year, until the blood itself becomes a fortress. Arsenic in the morning tea. Hemlock steeped into wine on feast days. By the time I was twelve I could eat things that would drop a grown man in the yard, and I felt nothing but a faint bitterness, the way other men feel the memory of a scar.

The valley made this possible. That was always the quiet pride of Vell Hollow, the thing they taught us in school along with our letters: magic cannot cross our borders. Whatever waits beyond the mountains, whatever old powers stir out there in the wide world, none of it follows you home. Inside the valley, poison is merely chemistry, and chemistry can be mastered, swallowed, survived. We were safe the way a walled garden is safe. Nothing got in.

And so I had built my whole self on a simple architecture: the world outside held death in a thousand shapes, and my body had been taught to hold them all, and as long as I stayed inside the walls where death kept to its honest forms, I was the safest man in any room I entered. It was not heroism. It was habit elevated to identity. I had never wanted anything enough to risk it — not land, not love, not a life beyond the antechamber and the silver spoons — and I had told myself for years that this was contentment.

That morning, watching the king breathe in his small drowning rhythm, I understood for the first time that my fortress had a flaw drawn right through its heart.

I could survive anything a man might put in a dish. I could not survive what a man might put in a *prayer*.

* * *

Curse-work has a signature. I'd heard the old tasters describe it — my grandmother, mostly, who remembered the last time the valley had whispered about such things, sixty years gone. She said cursed illness looks like drowning from the inside. The victim sleeps and cannot be roused fully; when wakened, they speak to people who aren't there; and around the sickbed, she said, you'll find the signs. Cold spots that don't yield to fire. Water that refuses to boil. Milk souring in the churn no matter how fresh it came from the cow.

I spent two days confirming every sign.

The cold spot sat in the corner of the king's bedchamber nearest the window, a patch of air so chill that frost formed on the inside of the glass in a perfect circle, even as the hearth roared. The kitchen water took twice as long to boil over the same fire that boiled it normally. And in the dairy, three mornings running, the cream turned before dawn.

None of it touched the food because none of it needed to. Curse-work rides in on intention, not ingredients. Someone had stood somewhere in this valley — in a pantry, a chapel, a corridor — and *willed* the king toward death, and the will itself was the poison, and no tongue on earth could taste it coming.

Which left only one conclusion, and it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone:

Someone inside the valley was serving the mountain court.

* * *

I did not tell the court this. Not yet. I told the head physician that the illness resisted diagnosis and recommended rest and broth, which was true in the way that most comforting lies are true. What I actually did was go back to my quarters and sit across the table from Wren, who was stacking dried beans into towers with the intense concentration of a child building a cathedral, and try to understand how everything had changed while I wasn't looking.

Three weeks he had lived hidden in the taster's wing now. Three weeks of kitchen words and bean towers and drawings — the spirals, the mountains, the door beneath the stone that he returned to again and again until I had begun to dream about it. Three weeks of a mute boy teaching me a language older than kingdoms, one gesture and one image at a time, and of feeling something long asleep in me turn over and open one eye.

He looked up when I sat down. Children always know. He set down a bean, and studied my face, and made the gesture I had come to dread: flat hand, downward press. *What's wrong.*

"The king is sick," I said aloud, though he couldn't have understood the words, and then I tried it in his language, clumsily, shaping the sounds he'd taught me for *sick* and *sleep* and *king*. My accent was terrible. He understood anyway.

Something crossed his face that I had not seen before. Not fear exactly. Recognition — the grim nod of someone who expected bad news and receives confirmation instead. He reached across the table and took my hand, and pressed it flat against the wood, and made the mountain sign. Then the door-under-the-stone sign. Then he pointed at me, and at himself, and drew his finger slowly across his own throat.

*They did this. From under the mountain. And they will do it to us.*

"No," I said, and heard how thin my voice sounded. "The valley keeps them out. Magic can't cross the border. It's the one law—"

Wren shook his head, patient, sorrowful, and made the gesture again: the mountain, the door. Then he pointed at the floor. *Below.* Below the border stone. Below the law. Things that had been bought, or bargained with, or bound — working from inside, the way rot works from inside a fruit that still looks whole on the shelf.

I thought of Chancellor Merek's face in council, smooth and reasonable, arguing that the child should go to the border priests. I thought of how quickly Merek had appeared at the servants' gate the night Wren arrived, as if he'd known. I thought of the cold spot by the window, and the milk, and the water that wouldn't boil.

And I thought: *the safe valley was never safe.*

* * *

It took Merek four days to come for Wren. I sometimes wonder what he was waiting for — whether he needed the king's illness to ripen first, whether he was waiting on instruction from below, or whether he simply enjoyed letting me sweat. Knowing Merek, I suspect the last. He was a man who believed patience was a form of cruelty available only to the intelligent.

He arrived at my quarters in the evening with two guardsmen, unannounced, and stood in my doorway looking at the bean towers and the charcoal drawings on the wall with an expression of mild distaste, as a man looks at a stain.

"Taster Dravenmoor," he said. "You've taken in a guest."

"A lost child," I said. "He's being cared for pending—"

"I know what he's being." Merek stepped inside without invitation. He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way frost occupies a window — completely, and without heat. "Here is what interests me, Dravenmoor. A child appears at our gate, barefoot, from nowhere, speaking a language no scholar recognizes. Within the fortnight, the king falls ill of an ailment no physician can name. And the one man in this palace whose entire life is the detection of poison reports — nothing. Finds — nothing. You stand over that man's food three times a day and pronounce it clean."

"It *is* clean."

"Oh, I believe you." Merek smiled. It was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was genuinely warm. "I believe your tongue entirely. Your tongue is the finest instrument in this valley. But tongues taste matter, don't they? And I've begun to wonder whether the poison in this palace is matter at all."

The room went very quiet. Wren had stopped stacking beans. He sat perfectly still, watching Merek with those eyes too old for his face, and I saw his small hand slide slowly off the table, down to his lap, curling closed around nothing.

"The border priests will examine the child," Merek continued pleasantly. "At the stone. They'll determine what he is. If he's simply a lost boy, he'll be fed and housed and taught our words. If he's..." He let the sentence hang, delicately, the way a surgeon lets a tool hang before the cut. "...otherwise, then we'll finally have an explanation for why the valley's oldest law seems to be failing us. Because it *is* failing us, isn't it, Dravenmoor? Something got in. Or something was already here. Either way—" he gestured gently at Wren, at the drawings, at the whole secret architecture of my disobedience "—this child arrived, and the king began to die. Coincidence is for people who haven't been paying attention."

"He's a child," I said. "Half-starved and mute. Whatever you think he is, sending him to the border priests is—"

"Is exactly what the law requires." Merek turned to the guardsmen. "Tomorrow morning. Take him at first light."

They left. I stood in my quarters listening to their boots fade down the corridor, and I became aware that Wren had come to stand beside me, and that his hand was holding the hem of my sleeve, and that I was shaking.

Not with fear. With the specific, vertiginous trembling of a man who has just realized which side of a line he is standing on.

For thirty-eight years I had served the crown with perfect fidelity. I had lied for no one, protected nothing but the king's body, wanted nothing that required dishonesty. And now I had hidden a child from the court for three weeks, lied to physicians, obstructed the chancellor, and stood in front of a guardsman's path in my mind's eye rehearsing how much force it would take to stop them.

Merek would come at first light. Which gave me one night.

I looked down at Wren. He looked up at me, and made a gesture I had never seen from him — both hands pressed over his heart, then offered outward, palms up. An offering, or an apology. In his language, I would learn later, it means *whatever I am, I give it to your keeping.*

Outside, the frost formed its perfect circle on the palace windows, and somewhere beneath the mountain, I imagined, something ancient turned a page and waited to see what the taster would do.

One night. One road. One border stone.

And beyond it — everything the valley had promised me I would never need to face.
