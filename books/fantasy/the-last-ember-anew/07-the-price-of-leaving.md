## 7. The Price of Leaving

The physician's tinctures sat untouched on the king's bedside table, and Ada stood in the doorway of the sickroom knowing, with a certainty that tasted like copper, that none of them would help.

Curse-work did not respond to medicine. It responded to nothing a valley without magic could offer. The king's fever had come on him in his sleep — not poison, never poison, Ada had tasted every dish and drunk from every cup for eleven years — but something older, something that had walked through the border stone wearing a servant's face and a chancellor's seal.

Merek.

Ada had no proof. He had a feeling in his back teeth and a memory of the chancellor's smile at court that morning, patient as a man watching bread rise.

He left the sickroom quietly, past the guards who did not look at him because they never looked at him — the taster was furniture that swallowed, furniture that lived — and went down through the kitchens to the taster's quarters beneath the east stair, where Wren was waiting on the cot with his knees drawn up, drawing spirals in the dust on the floor with one finger.

The boy looked up when Ada came in. He didn't smile. He had learned, in the weeks since the gate, that adults who came down these stairs wearing this particular expression were carrying bad news, and Wren had developed the stillness of prey.

*They hurt your king,* Wren signed, in the language with no name that Ada understood the way he understood hunger.

Ada crouched across from him. *Yes.*

*The ones under the mountain.*

*Yes.* He hesitated. There was no gentle way to say it, so he said it the way he said everything to Wren: plainly, like naming an ingredient. *They will come for you next. They are already coming.*

Wren looked down at the spiral he'd drawn. Then, slowly, he dragged his finger through it, erasing it, and drew something else. A door. A door under stone, drawn small enough to cover with two hands, the way a child draws a monster they hope will stay small if only it isn't looked at too hard.

*I can go back,* Wren signed. His hands were steady. That was the worst part. Children should tremble when they offered themselves up; Wren had been sold before, and sold things do not flinch at being priced again. *If I go back, they stop. Your king heals. You stay safe.*

Something in Ada's chest turned over, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting in a riverbed.

He had spent thirty-one years being safe. He had built safety the way other men built houses — dish by dish, dose by dose, a foundation of swallowed arsenic and monkshood and bitter almond until his blood ran thick with everything that should have killed him. Safe was all he was. Safe was the whole architecture of his life: the tasting spoon, the quarter-hour wait, the numb pride of a man whose body was a wall other people hid behind.

And here was a child offering to walk back into slavery to keep that wall standing around a kingdom that would have sent him to the border priests within the week if Ada hadn't lied for him.

*No,* Ada signed.

Wren tilted his head. *You cannot leave. No one leaves. The valley keeps you.*

*The valley keeps me,* Ada agreed. *But it doesn't own me.* He heard the lie even as his hands shaped it, and kept shaping it anyway. *Pack nothing. We go tonight.*

* * *

They left at the change of the watch, through the kitchens, where Ada stopped only long enough to fill a satchel: hard bread, dried plums, a skin of water, a wedge of the pale cheese the dairy wives made in autumn. He took his knife and, after a moment's thought, the small tin of powdered willowbark and the vial of poppy syrup he kept for the headaches the tastings gave him. Old habits. He was leaving the valley; he might need medicine for ordinary ills, since extraordinary ones were about to become his whole profession.

At the door he paused, and looked back at the kitchen — at the banked ovens, the hanging bunches of thyme and sage, the great scarred table where he'd taught Wren the word for *bread* and the word for *burn* and the word for *stay*, which Wren had used more often than any other word he owned.

He had assumed he'd see it again. Even now, walking out, some part of him filed the moment under *temporary*, the way you file a trip to market.

The servants' gate was guarded by a boy younger than Ada had ever been, who knew him by sight and by reputation and let him pass with a nod and a murmured, "Evening, Master Taster," because who questioned the man whose tongue the king trusted with his life? Ada felt the lie of that trust sit in his mouth like a coin held under the tongue — cold, hardening, impossible to swallow.

Wren walked beside him barefoot on the frost-stiff mud, making no sound at all. The boy moved like something that had learned long ago how not to be noticed, and every silent step made Ada's jaw tighten further.

They went north along the drovers' road, then west where the road thinned to a track and the track thinned to a suggestion. The valley rose around them in the dark — the terraced orchards gone silver with frost, the sheep folds like scattered bones, the mountain walls closing overhead until there was more rock than sky. Ada knew this country the way he knew his own hands. He had walked its edges as a boy, dared its borders with other children, run shrieking back from the border stone on a dare and laughed about it after.

Nobody laughed now.

The border stone stood in a saddle between two hills, a grey finger taller than three men, carved over with letters worn to suggestions. Around it, for perhaps twenty paces in every direction, the land was subtly wrong — grass that grew in a perfect ring, birds that would not perch, air that felt the way a held breath feels. Every child in Vell Hollow learned the shape of that wrongness before they learned their letters. *Magic stops here,* the nurses said. *It cannot cross. It cannot follow.*

Which meant, Ada understood now, standing at the edge of the circle with Wren's hand small and cold in his, that everything inside it could be touched by anything outside it that simply *waited*. The valley was not a fortress. It was a bowl, and bowls exist to be filled.

*What happens?* Wren signed, looking up at him.

Ada considered lying. He was, he reflected grimly, getting good at it.

*It will feel like drowning,* he signed back. *For me. Magic has never touched my body. My body is full of poisons instead — I spent my life filling myself with them so the king wouldn't have to. When the border lets go of me, all of that wakes up. All of it fights.*

Wren stared at him. Then his free hand rose, quick and angry, signing too fast — Ada caught fragments: *fool, why, your blood, your life* — before the boy stopped himself, closed his eyes, and started again, slower.

*You would burn yourself for me. Why?*

Because you draw spirals in dust. Because you wouldn't eat until I tasted first. Because you are the first thing in thirty-one years I have wanted enough to lose.

There were no signs for any of that. So Ada said, aloud, in plain Vellish, the language of ledgers and porridge: "Because someone has to."

And stepped into the circle.

* * *

Later, Ada would try to describe it and fail.

It began as warmth. That was the trap of it — warmth, like stepping into a bath, like sun through a window, and for one heartbeat he thought, *this is nothing, this is easy,* and then the warmth found the poison.

Thirty-one years of it. The arsenic accumulated in grain-sized doses over a decade. The monkshood. The hemlock. The bitter almond folded into the king's honeyed porridge on the very morning this story began, still sitting somewhere in the deep shelves of his blood, catalogued, contained, asleep. Every poison he had ever swallowed on purpose, mastered and made harmless, and all of it harmless only because the valley's silence had held it down.

The border stone let go of him, and magic came back into the world, and the world reached into Ada Dravenmoor and woke every sleeping thing at once.

He went down.

Not gracefully — there was nothing graceful in it. His knees hit frozen ground he couldn't feel because feeling had been replaced by fire, a fire that started in his marrow and worked outward, and he heard someone screaming and distantly understood it was him. The poisons didn't just wake; they *recognized* what had entered him, the way a locked room recognizes light under the door, and they fought it, and he was the field they fought in. His veins burned like swallowed coals. His teeth ached. His eyes wept without his permission, freezing on his cheeks, and through the blur he saw Wren kneeling over him, shaking him, mouth open on sounds that weren't words — the boy was mute, the boy had never spoken, but whatever was tearing loose in Ada was tearing loose in Wren too, the contract pulling at its leash—

*Run,* Ada tried to sign. His hands wouldn't obey. They lay in the frost like dead birds.

The last thing he saw clearly was the border stone, rising above them, and the faintest glow at its base — a thread of gold, thin as spider silk, running from Wren's chest toward the stone, toward the valley beyond, straining like a dog at the end of a rope.

Then the dark took him, and the dark was full of taste: almond, and ash, and something sweet underneath both, something he almost remembered.

* * *

He surfaced slowly, in layers.

First: warmth against his side, and fur — no, wool, a cloak wrapped around him, and heat that wasn't fire but a body, someone sitting close enough to share breath. Second: the smell of woodsmoke and, beneath it, something mineral and strange, like struck flint. Third: a voice, low and rough, singing.

Singing. Nobody sang anymore. The valley had lost its songs a generation ago, or so everyone said, and Ada had always thought that was just the way of things, the way valleys forget.

He opened his eyes.

Dawn was coming up grey-pink over country he did not know — rolling moorland, heather gone bronze with frost, hills that rolled away southward like a sea that had decided to rest. Behind him, maybe half a mile off, the border stone stood black against the sunrise, and the valley beyond it was hidden, already another country, already a place he had been rather than a place he was.

Sera sat beside him with her back against a boulder, his head pillowed against her shoulder, singing something old in a language that made the air feel thicker.

Her hair had gone from the brown he remembered to iron-grey at the temples, though she couldn't have been more than five-and-thirty. Her hands, wrapped around a steaming tin cup, were scarred in patterns he didn't recognize — burn scars, deliberate ones, laid in lines like script. She wore a traveler's coat over robes of ember-orange gone dusty at the hems, and she was thinner than the girl who had stood with him under the cherry tree behind the palace gardens and told him, laughing, that a man who tasted death for a living ought to learn to taste living too.

She must have felt him stir, because the song stopped.

"Don't move yet." Her voice was lower than he remembered, roughened by years and weather. "Your blood is still arguing with itself. Give it till full light."

"Sera." His voice came out as gravel.

"Hello, Ada." She didn't look at him. She watched the border stone with the wariness of a woman watching a dog that had bitten her once. "You crossed at night. Alone. With a fading ember-child and no escort, no warding, and no idea what crossing does to a body like yours." She took a sip from the cup. "I've been keeping embers alive for fifteen years and I wouldn't have tried it."

"You were watching."

"I felt the valley shudder the day the boy crossed in." Finally she turned, and her eyes were the exact color he remembered — a brown so dark it read as black in any light less than noon — and they were furious, and underneath the fury something else was drowning, and neither of them pretended otherwise. "I've waited at that stone for six days because I knew you'd be fool enough to come. And here you are."

"How is he?" Ada tried to turn his head and the world tipped; Sera's hand caught his shoulder and pressed him back with a strength that surprised him.

"Asleep. Truly asleep, for the first time since I found you both — out here the contract can't tighten. Look at him."

Wren lay curled a few feet away in a nest of cloaks, breathing slow and even, and in the dawn light Ada saw what he hadn't been able to see in the valley: color. Faint, uncertain, but present — pink in the cheeks, gold in the hair, a child's coloring returning to a child's face like ink soaking backward into a pen.

"He'll fade faster now," Sera said quietly. "Out here, away from the valley's silence, the pull is stronger. Three days, perhaps four, before the contract drags him under entirely. After that—" She didn't finish it. She drank instead.

"Then we walk fast."

"We walk *smart*, or we don't arrive at all." She set the cup down. "Ada. Listen to me. What happened to you at the stone — that was the *gentle* version. Your body fought the magic and won, barely, because you're built like a fortress of poison. But the mountain court doesn't send gentle things. And you—" her hand hovered over his arm, not quite touching, "—you've spent your immunity. Crossing burned through years of it in minutes. Whatever resistance you built, you spent most of it buying that boy ten feet past a rock. Do you understand what you traded?"

Ada looked at his own hands. They seemed ordinary. They felt like someone else's.

"Yes," he said, which was a lie, and then, because she deserved better than the lies he gave everyone else: "No. But I'd do it again."

Sera exhaled through her nose — half laugh, half wound. "You haven't changed at all."

"You have."

"Good." She stood, brushing frost from her robe, and when she looked down at him her face had settled into something harder and older and more frightened than fury. "Because the country between here and the mountain isn't country, Ada. It's a throat. The roads rearrange. The inns trade in things you'll wish you'd kept. And the court below already knows you're coming — it's known since the night the boy knocked on your gate." She picked up her staff, iron-shod, hung with charms that clicked together like teeth. "So drink this, eat something, and get up. We have three days."

She held out the tin cup. Steam curled from it, and the smell was bitter and green and utterly unfamiliar — nothing from any garden in Vell Hollow.

Ada took it. The first sip burned going down like swallowed coal.

Somewhere behind them, on the far side of the border stone, in a valley that could no longer hear him, a king was waking fevered, and a chancellor was smiling.

Ahead, the moor rolled out gray and endless toward the mountain, and the roads, Sera had said, remembered.

Ada got up.
