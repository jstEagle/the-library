## 8. Roads That Remember

**Ada**

The border stone fell behind them by midmorning, and Ada felt it go the way a man feels a tooth leave his jaw — not pain exactly, but an absence with weight, a hollow where something had lived for thirty years. His body still burned from the crossing. Every immunity he'd built dish by dish, drop by drop, was smoldering somewhere beneath his skin like banked fires he could no longer bank.

He walked anyway. Wren's small hand was in his, and Sera walked ahead with her hood down and her jaw set at the angle of a woman who has decided not to apologize.

The country beyond the valley was nothing like the maps. Ada had copied maps for years in the chancellor's offices — neat rivers, honest roads, towns marked with tidy squares. What lay before them instead was a land of long grass the color of old brass, hills that rolled like sleeping animals, and roads that did not behave.

"You'll want to mark our path," Sera said without turning around. "And you'll want to understand that marking it is useless."

"Meaning what?"

She stopped and crouched, pressing two fingers into the dirt of the road itself — because it was a road, rutted and pebbled, running straight toward a distant blue line of mountains. "Meaning this is the Old King's Road right now. Tonight it may be a riverbed. Tomorrow it may decide it was never here at all." She looked up at him, and there was ash-grey in her eyes that hadn't been there when she was nineteen. "Roads out here remember being other things. Rivers. Processions. Wars. They get restless."

"That's absurd," Ada said.

"Yes," Sera agreed, rising. "Welcome to the rest of the world."

They walked until the light went long and copper. Wren tugged Ada's sleeve twice — once to point at a hawk riding thermals above them, once to make the sign Ada now knew meant *hungry*, fingers folding into the palm like a bird settling to roost. They ate hard bread and dried plums from Sera's pack, sitting on a mile-marker whose inscription had been worn to a smooth blank, as if the road had licked its own name away.

Ada watched her eat. He hated himself a little for watching her eat. Nineteen years since Sera Vane had stood at the valley gate with her ember-keeper's sash cut in half, and he had catalogued her face in every dream since, and none of the dreams had gotten the mouth quite right. It was thinner now. There was a scar through one eyebrow he didn't know the story of and suspected he would hate the story of.

"The boy sleeps cold," Sera said, nodding at Wren, who had gone down between one bite and the next, curled against Ada's side like a comma against a sentence. "We should keep moving while we can. Roads are kindest at night, actually. They're tired too."

"Is everything a riddle with you?"

"No." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "Most things are just true, and truth sounds like a riddle when you've spent your life inside a valley that refuses it."

That landed where she'd aimed it. Ada stood, lifting Wren onto his back, and said nothing, and they walked into the dusk.

* * *

**Sera**

She had counted the years the way misers count coin: alone, at night, over and over, finding no comfort in the total. Seventeen since the exile. Sixteen since she'd stopped expecting him to come after her. Three days since she'd seen him collapse on the far side of the border stone with magic pouring back into a body built to refuse everything, and caught him before the ground did, and felt — briefly, criminally — glad.

Now he walked behind her with the child on his back, and she could feel his silence the way you feel weather changing.

Say it, she thought. Go on. You've been sharpening it for three days.

He said it at moonrise, when they made camp in the lee of a standing stone that hummed faintly, like a struck bell heard through water.

"Why him?" Ada asked. He'd settled Wren against the stone and wrapped him in both their cloaks, and then turned to face her across their small fire. "Why did you choose the embers over—" He stopped. Started again, worse. "Over me. Over us. Over *anything*. You let them cut your sash and walk you to the gate and you didn't look back, Sera. I stood there. I waited for you to look back."

"I looked back," she said quietly. "You were looking at the ground."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what happened." She fed a twig to the fire. Out here, past the valley, flame behaved strangely — it leaned toward her slightly, the way a dog leans toward its master, and she kept her hand away from it out of old discipline. "I chose the fires because someone had to. Because the court under the mountain eats light the way you eat poison, Ada, a little at a time, until you're full of it. The ember-keepers were the last thing standing between them and the valley's founding flame. And when the order came down — when Merek himself signed the order to let the border shrines go dark, to starve the embers so the valley would stay *safe*—" Her laugh came out with edges on it. "Safe. Your whole life spent keeping one man alive, and no one ever tasted what the council fed the valley."

"You don't know what my life was."

"I know what it looked like from outside. A man swallowing death on schedule. Proud of it. Numb to it. In love with duty because duty was the only thing that never asked him to want anything."

The words went in deep and found old targets. Ada's face did the thing it always did — closed, smoothed, became the face he wore for the king's table, for chancellors, for poison. But Sera had loved that face before it learned the trick, and she saw the hurt underneath the lacquer, and hated herself for aiming well, and hated herself more for meaning it.

"And you?" he said. "What did wanting things get you? Exile. Ash. Seventeen years alone at a border stone waiting for—"

"For a child to cross," she finished. "Yes. That's what I got. And you got a king who doesn't know your name off-duty and a valley that would burn you as a witch if they knew what you're carrying in your blood." She gestured at him, at all of him. "Don't stand there and tell me about choices, Ada Dravenmoor. We made the same choice. We just picked different masters."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, a sound like a door closing traveled across the grasslands with nothing to close.

Between them, wrapped in both cloaks, Wren opened his eyes.

* * *

**Ada**

The boy didn't move. He lay with his cheek against the standing stone and watched them the way children watch storms — unblinking, taking inventory. And Ada realized with a lurch that Wren understood more than borrowed kitchen-words. He understood *tone*. He understood two people who had been one thing once, tearing along an old seam.

Wren raised one small hand and made a sign.

It wasn't one Ada knew. The fingers crossed at the knuckle, drew apart, pressed flat together over the heart. When Ada only stared, the child repeated it, slower, patient as a teacher, and then pointed — first at Sera, then at Ada, then at the space between them where the firelight bent.

"What's he saying?" Sera asked.

"I don't know that one."

"He knows it means something. Look at him."

Wren sat up, cloak falling from his shoulders, and did the strangest thing yet: he spoke aloud, in his own tongue, the language older than kingdoms that only Ada could hear as language. The syllables came out round and ringing, and though Ada could not translate them, he felt their shape the way you feel the shape of a key you've lost — knowing exactly which lock it fits. Then he pointed at each of them again. *Sera. Ada.* And then he pointed at himself, and made the sign again — fingers crossing, parting, pressing together over the heart — and laid the sign gently on the ground between them, as if setting down something fragile.

"A bridge," Sera whispered. "He's saying bridge." Her voice had gone rough. "Or rope. Or — the word might mean several things. Old tongues do that."

Ada crouched by the child. "Wren. Is that what we are? Are we your bridge?"

Wren considered the question with the terrible seriousness of children and judges. Then he shook his head once and touched Ada's chest, then Sera's, then folded both his own hands together, interlaced, and held them up.

*Tied,* Ada thought. *Tied together.*

"We're not tied together," he said, but he said it to the fire, and the fire leaned toward Sera as if to disagree.

"Sleep," Sera said, standing abruptly. "First watch is mine. Second is yours. We move before dawn, because if the roads rearrange at night, I want to be walking the moment they finish deciding."

He took the second watch. He spent it not watching the dark but watching her silhouette against the stars, upright and unbending at the edge of the firelight, and thinking about the difference between a choice and a wound. At nineteen he had believed she'd chosen the embers over him, clean and simple, the way you choose one road over another. Now, in this country where roads remembered being rivers and wars, he wondered if she'd chosen anything at all — or if she'd simply been swept, like him, like everyone, by currents too large to taste coming.

It was almost dawn when the humming of the standing stone changed pitch.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera woke to wrongness. The fire was dead — not banked, not ash, simply *absent*, as if the night had edited it out — and the standing stone beside them was silent. Worse than silent. The grass around it had changed color overnight, brass gone to grey-green, and when she stood and looked south toward where the mountains had lain last evening, there were no mountains. There was a forest instead, black and dense, growing where open plain had been.

"Ada." Her voice came out level, which cost her. "Wake up. Don't startle the boy."

He woke the way he did everything — instantly, completely, a soldier of the table. One look around and he had Wren gathered against him. "The mountains are gone."

"The road moved us. Or moved itself. Either way—" She scanned the horizon and found it: a pale thread winding north-northwest through the new forest, and at its edge, a milestone. Milestones were good. Milestones were promises. "There. We follow the marker road. Under the mountain courts can bend the land, but they rarely bother lying to travelers directly. Too much paperwork, even for them."

"Paperwork."

"Bargains have ledgers, Ada. Everything does."

They reached the milestone within the hour. It read, in letters that shifted like fish under ice: **THE ROAD TO THE MOUNTAIN, 9 DAYS.** Beneath it, smaller: **OR 40, IF YOU DOUBT.**

"Cheerful," Ada muttered.

But it was the next thing that stopped Sera's breath. Pressed into the soft earth at the base of the stone, clear as wax seals, were hoofprints. Many horses. Fresh. Riding the same direction they intended to ride.

She crouched and touched the print's edge. Still sharp. Hours old at most.

"Riders," she said. "Ahead of us, or behind and faster. Either way, someone knows where we're going."

Ada's hand went to Wren's shoulder — that instinctive, gathering motion she remembered from the market squares of her childhood, men pulling their children close at bad news. "Merek's?"

"Merek's riders couldn't cross the border stone. Whatever's chasing us belongs to the other side." She stood, scanning the treeline, and the old ember-keeper's senses opened in her chest like a hand unclenching — and there, faint and cold, like a candle seen through fog: *intent*. Someone out there wanted the child, wanted him specifically, wanted him badly enough to ride through the night.

Behind them, Wren tugged Ada's sleeve and made a sign Ada knew now — the one for *danger*, which the boy had taught him their first morning out, tapping two fingers twice against the wrist like a pulse.

"Yes," Ada told him grimly. "I know."

Sera turned back toward the road, toward the nine days or forty, toward the mountain waiting at the end of whichever version they got. "Then we walk fast," she said, "and we stop fighting, because whatever's coming — it already knows exactly what we are to each other. It would be a shame if we were the last ones to figure it out."

Ada didn't answer. But when they set out, he adjusted his stride to match hers, and Wren walked between them holding a hand from each — and if the road noticed, the road said nothing, and carried them north through the remembering country, toward the hoofprints, and the mountain, and whatever waited at the ninth day's end.
