## 9. The Inn of Small Mercies

The inn appeared where no inn should be.

We had walked since the false dawn, through country that kept rearranging itself like a drunk rearranging furniture — the same crooked birch grove passing us three times, a river that ran uphill for an hour out of what Sera assured me was spite. Wren rode on my shoulders, lighter than a child his size had any right to be, and hummed in the old language, notes falling into the grass behind us like seeds.

Then, between one step and the next, there was a building.

It sat in a fold of land the road seemed to apologize for: low, timbered, its roof thatched with something silver-grey that moved against the wind as though breathing. Smoke curled from a chimney, but it fell downward instead of rising. A sign swung above the door on chains that did not creak — a painted bowl, a painted flame, and between them a small figure with its hands open.

"The Inn of Small Mercies," Sera read aloud, and her voice had gone careful, the way mine went careful over an unfamiliar dish. "I've heard of it. Never believed it."

"You've heard of everything," I said. "You've never believed any of it."

"I believe what I can carry." She looked at me sidelong. "This place carries you whether you want it to or not."

Wren slid down from my shoulders and walked straight to the door before either of us could stop him. He put his palm flat against the wood, the way he'd once put his hand against the border stone, and the door opened without being touched.

Inside, the air smelled of bread and rain and something underneath both — old paper, maybe, or the inside of a locked drawer. The common room was round, which no common room should be, with tables set at odd angles like fallen leaves. A fire burned in a hearth shaped like two cupped hands, and it gave light but very little heat, or heat but very little light; I couldn't decide which, and my skin prickled with the indecision.

The keeper stood behind a bar of black wood polished smooth by centuries of elbows. She was old in the way mountains are old — not wrinkled so much as *weathered*, her face carrying ridges and valleys of its own. Her eyes were pale as milk left out overnight, and when she looked at me, I felt looked *through*, catalogued, tasted.

"Three," she said. Her voice was dry as a page turning. "Two full-grown and one half-spent. You'll want beds, food, and the road past the Weeping Stair."

Sera stiffened. "How do you—"

"This is an inn." The keeper smiled, and the smile was kind the way winter is kind to things it intends to keep. "I know what my guests need. It is how I know what to charge."

"We have coin," I said, reaching for the purse at my belt — the king's coin, iron-stamped, good anywhere in Vell Hollow and nowhere else, as it turned out.

The keeper didn't look at it. "Coin is for people who have nothing better."

* * *

We ate first, because Wren was hungry and because it delayed the reckoning. The stew was venison and dark roots and something floral beneath, thyme-adjacent, and it was the best thing I had eaten since leaving the valley — which frightened me more than any poison ever had, because I understood immediately that the taste was not free.

While Wren slept in a chair by the hearth, his breath going shallow and even, the keeper came around the bar and stood over our table.

"Now," she said. "The passage information costs. The beds cost. The meal has already been paid for" — she glanced at Wren — "by him. He paid while he ate. A memory of flying, I think. He won't miss it. Children shed such things naturally, like teeth."

Rage rose in me so fast I nearly knocked the table over. "He's given up enough."

"He's given up nothing he was keeping." The keeper met my anger the way a stone meets weather. "But you — you're another matter. You're carrying whole rooms of things you refuse to look at. It leaks off you, taster. Grief has a smell. So does guilt. So does love, though yours has gone musty from disuse."

Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"The road past the Weeping Stair," the keeper continued, "is watched this week. There are riders on it wearing Vell Hollow grey, and they are not looking for you gently. I can tell you the safe way — down through the Hushwood, along the cold spring, under the arch of standing stones where the mountain's eyes cannot follow. That knowledge is worth—" She paused, considering me the way I considered a dish. "One memory. Something true. Something you would notice missing only on quiet nights."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you sleep outside, eat nothing tomorrow, and meet the riders on the open road with a fading child and an exile who hasn't fought anything but her own reflection in nine years."

I looked at Sera. She looked at the table. Neither of us said anything useful.

"One memory," I said.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being a royal taster: your life becomes a series of small deaths you survive on purpose, and after enough of them, you stop noticing which parts of yourself are still alive.

The keeper had me sit in a chair by the fire — the hearth of cupped hands, which I now suspected were meant literally — and told me to close my eyes and think of the king.

That was easy. I thought of him as I had known him longest: not the crown-weighted man on the throne, but King Aldric in his fiftieth year, sitting up late with a fever, refusing the physician's draught until I tasted it, then drinking it down and gripping my wrist afterward and saying, *Thank you, Ada. Thank you.* Not thanks for the tasting. Thanks for staying. For thirty years, every night, choosing the same plate, the same risk, the same quiet loyalty no ballad would ever sing.

There was a morning — I found it now, turning it over like a coin — when he had come down to the kitchens himself, unannounced, still in his nightshirt under his robe, and eaten porridge beside the scullery boys because he'd dreamed of his dead wife and wanted, he said, to eat somewhere she might have been happy. He'd laughed at something the baker's daughter said. He'd wiped his bowl with bread. And on the way out he'd stopped beside me and said, quietly, "Whatever happens to me, it will never be your fault. Do you understand? Whatever ends me, it will not be the tasting."

I had carried those words for twenty years like a stone in my boot.

The keeper's fingers settled on my temples, cool and dry.

"It's a fair trade," she murmured. "This one has finished its work. Look — it's worn smooth already. Nothing left in it but polish."

And she took it.

* * *

I woke at the table with my cheek against the wood and no sense of time lost. The fire had shifted colors. Sera was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — grief and envy fighting openly on her face.

"What did you give?" she asked.

I reached inward, the way I reached for the memory of every dish I'd ever tasted, running my tongue along the shelves of my own history.

The king was there. The fever, the draught, the grip on my wrist — all there. Thirty years of mornings, all there.

But the morning in the kitchen — the nightshirt, the dead queen's ghost, the bread-wiped bowl, and the words *it will never be your fault* — was gone. Not hidden. Gone. I felt the shape of the absence the way you feel a tooth pulled: a socket, a hollow, a place where pressure used to live.

And here was the terrible thing, the thing I would turn over for miles afterward:

It didn't hurt.

I waited for the grief. I braced for it the way I braced for almond in honey. Nothing came. There was just clean space where the stone had been, and in the space, something absurdly like lightness.

"Sera." My voice came out strange. "I don't miss it."

Her jaw tightened. "You don't know that yet. Wait for a quiet night."

"No — I mean—" I pressed my hand flat against my chest, searching for the ache and finding none. "I served him thirty years and I loved him, I think, the way you love a wall you've leaned against so long you've forgotten you're standing on your own. And the piece she took — I can feel that it mattered, I can feel the *groove* where it sat — but it doesn't pull anymore. It's just..." I struggled. "Lighter."

"That's what they do," Sera said bitterly. "That's what *she* does. They don't steal your past. They steal your *attachment* to it. And you walk out thinking you've lost nothing, because the losing is the part they removed." She stood abruptly. "I'm paying for our rooms. Alone. Don't watch."

* * *

But I did watch. Of course I did.

She sat in the keeper's chair with her spine straight as a blade, and I saw them speak — low, quick, the keeper's milk-pale eyes narrowing once, twice. Then Sera reached up and undid something at her throat. A cord. On it, I realized, hung a ring I had seen her wear every day since the border stone: plain iron, too big for any finger she had, worn smooth.

She held it a long moment. Then she pressed it into the keeper's palm, and the keeper closed her fingers over it, and something passed between them — not the ring. Something else. Sera swayed on the chair like a tree in wind, and the keeper steadied her shoulder with one hand, almost gently, and whispered something I could not hear.

When Sera came back to the table, her face was composed and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Not a memory," I said quietly.

"No."

"The ring. Your mother's?"

"My mother's hands," Sera said, "were the first thing I remember. Flour to the wrists, always, even in summer. She kneaded the ember-bread for the shrine, and she'd let me press my thumbprint into the loaf before it went to the fire — said the goddess liked to know who'd fed her." She stared at the hearth. "The ring was hers. The keeper didn't want the ring. She wanted what the ring *held*. Every object does, here. Twenty years of my thumb on that iron. Twenty years of Sunday mornings. She took the holding. I keep the metal."

"Why?" I asked. "Why pay at all? You know these roads. You told me you walked them in exile."

"I walked them alone." Her voice cracked down the middle. "A lone woman on a road is weather. A woman, a man, and a fading child are a story, and stories get followed. The Hushwood path changes. The standing stones move. What I knew nine years ago is dead knowledge, and dead knowledge gets children killed." She finally looked at me. "So I paid. And I hate that I feel *lighter* too. That's the trap of this place, Ada. It doesn't rob you. It convinces you."

Above the hearth, Wren stirred in his sleep and murmured something in the old language — a word I now understood, having carried it since the gates: *stay.*

Or possibly *leave.*

In that tongue, I was learning, some words refused to choose.

* * *

The keeper gave us the road in the hour before dawn, drawing it in spilled flour on the black bar-top: down through the Hushwood, follow the cold spring even where it vanishes, pass under the standing stones walking backward, and do not — *do not* — answer any voice that calls your name before sunrise.

"And the riders?" Sera asked.

"Gone to ground tonight," the keeper said. "Something spooked them. Something crossed their camp and left frost in July." Her milk-pale eyes drifted, for just a moment, to the sleeping boy. "The mountain knows what walks toward it. It sends less patient things than men."

She paused at the door as we gathered our things, and caught my sleeve with two fingers.

"A gift, taster. Free, because you interest me." She leaned close. Her breath smelled of old paper. "You gave up a memory tonight and called it nothing. But ask yourself — why did the keeper take the *kindness*? Why not the fevers, the fear, the thirty years of swallowing death?" Her smile returned, thin as a knife's edge. "Because kindness was the only part of him you were still using. Guard what feels light. Lightness is how loss introduces itself."

Then she shut the door, and the inn was gone.

Not empty — *gone*. We stood in a fold of moorland at grey dawn, our backs against a standing stone, dew soaking our boots, Wren blinking awake in my arms. Where the inn had been there was only grass, bent in a perfect circle, as though something enormous had rested there briefly and risen.

Sera stared at the empty ground for a long moment. Then she touched her bare throat, found nothing there to touch, and squared her shoulders.

"Hushwood's east," she said. "We make the stones by nightfall."

We walked. And I did what I always do — I took inventory, tongue to the walls of my own mouth, checking for damage.

Thirty years of service: present. The border crossing, the burning roads, the boy asleep against my chest: present. Sera's face at nineteen, the day she left: present, and heavier than it had ever been.

Only one thing missing. One smooth groove, one polished absence.

And following the keeper's advice, I guarded it anyway — the way you guard a wound you can't see, the way you guard a door that no longer has a reason to hold.

Because she was right, damn her. Kindness was the only part of the king I was still using.

And somewhere behind us, on a road we would not take, riders in Vell Hollow grey were waking to find frost on their saddles in July — and beginning, at last, to guess which direction we had gone.
