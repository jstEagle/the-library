## 10. Hunters of the Hollow

The road betrayed us at dusk.

We had followed it all day through a country of grey grass and standing stones, trusting it because Sera told us roads in this land remember where they're going even when we don't. But when the sun went down behind the western hills and Wren pointed ahead with that small, urgent finger, the road had bent back on itself — a great pale ribbon curling like a snake tasting its own tail, delivering us to a hilltop we'd crossed at noon.

"They do this," Sera said, not surprised. She stood with her weight on her bad leg, arms folded against the cold, studying the road the way a soldier studies an enemy's face. "When they don't want something to arrive somewhere. Roads are the oldest servants here. Older than bargains."

"Who is *they*?" I asked. I had asked some version of this question every day since the border stone, and every day she answered as though the answer were obvious and my ignorance were a wound she was too polite to poke.

"The ones who sold nothing and bought everything." She turned her head slowly, scanning the darkening country south of us — the way we'd come, the way the valley lay hidden beyond hills and hedges and the stone that had burned me hollow. "And tonight, *they* means riders."

I felt it before I understood what she meant. A wrongness in the air, like the moment before porridge turns — the faint sour note under sweetness that my tongue had been trained to catch across twenty years and ten thousand meals. The evening had gone quiet in the wrong way. No insects. No wind. Even the grass seemed to be holding its breath.

Wren pressed himself against my side. He didn't make a sound — he never made a sound, not since the night he'd first spoken to me in that old tongue only I could hear — but I felt his fear come off him like heat off a hearthstone.

"How many?" I asked.

Sera closed her eyes. When ember-keepers did whatever it was they did, their faces went smooth and distant, like women listening to music from another room. "Six," she said. "Riding hard. They'll have our scent by now. Or yours." Her eyes opened, and there was something in them I didn't like — pity, maybe, or its crueler cousin, calculation. "Merek's men wear no colors out here. But they carry his seal under their armor. I can feel it. Iron and ink and old promises."

*Chancellor Merek.* The name sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Twenty years I'd tasted for his king. Twenty years he'd signed the writs that paid me, praised my diligence in front of the court, called me *the most loyal man in Vell Hollow* — and all the while, if Sera was right, he'd been the mountain court's hand inside the kingdom's glove. It was Merek who had wanted Wren given to the border priests. Merek who had argued, gently, reasonably, that a mute child who appeared from nowhere was a threat to the realm's peace.

A threat to the realm's peace. Or a loose thread that needed cutting before anyone pulled it.

"We run," I said. "Off the road. Into the stones. If the road serves them—"

"The road serves itself." Sera was already moving, limping fast toward the ring of standing stones on the hill's crown. "But stones serve no one. Come."

* * *

We made our camp in the heart of the circle: no fire, a shared cloak for Wren, and Sera sitting cross-legged at the circle's edge with her palms flat against the earth like a woman taking a pulse. I sat with the boy against my shoulder and watched the stars come out over a country that was not my own, and I thought about how strange fear is when you've lived with death so long it became a coworker.

For twenty years, every morning, I had put poison into my mouth on purpose. Small doses, measured doses, doses that built up in my blood like sediment in a riverbed until my body became a fortress no toxin could storm. I had drunk the king's enemies' arsenic with my breakfast and slept soundly. I had learned to taste hemlock in wine, nightshade in jam, powdered grief-of-heart in a bride's wedding cake. My tongue was the finest instrument in Vell Hollow and my blood was a graveyard of things meant to kill better men than me.

I had never once been afraid at night. Fear was for men who had something to lose.

Then a barefoot child had walked through the palace gates speaking a language older than kingdoms, and overnight I had become the richest coward alive.

"Wren." I said it softly, and felt him turn toward me in the dark. "If they take you — if it comes to that — you run. You don't wait for me. You don't look back. You find Sera, or you find the road, or you find a hole in the ground, but you run. Do you understand me?"

In the starlight his face was very thin and very old, the way it got sometimes, when the thing wearing a child's shape showed through. He lifted one small hand and pressed it flat against my chest, over my heart, the way he did when words failed him in my language. And then, because he was generous in ways I would never deserve, he gave me the words anyway — that current flowing between us, wordless and warm, meaning something like: *You are the first person who ever tasted my food first. I will not leave the one who keeps me.*

"I'm not keeping you," I whispered. "I'm just—" I stopped. Swallowed. The truth came up my throat like a bone caught sideways. "I've spent my whole life making sure other people stayed safe. The king. The court. Whole valleyfuls of strangers I never met. And I never once cared whether any of them woke up in the morning. Not truly. But you—" My voice cracked like ice on a spring pond. "You, I would burn down the sky for. Do you understand? You are the first thing I have ever wanted enough to lose everything for."

He smiled. Children shouldn't smile like that — slow and sad and knowing, like an old man remembering rain. Then he laid his head against my shoulder and closed his eyes, and somewhere in the space of three breaths he was asleep, because children can do that, even children whose souls are held as collateral in a court beneath a mountain.

Across the circle, Sera was watching me. I couldn't see her face clearly, but I could feel her attention the way you feel sunlight through a window.

"You love him," she said quietly. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind, exactly. There was an accusation buried in it, old and fossilized: *you never loved anything, once, when loving it might have cost you. Why does it take a foundling to teach you?*

"Yes," I said, because lying to her had become a habit I was trying, badly, to break.

She said nothing more. But after a while she shifted, moving closer along the stone, until her shoulder almost — not quite — touched mine. In the country we'd grown up in, that near-touch would have said everything. Here it said it again, in a dialect we both still understood.

* * *

They came an hour past midnight.

Sera woke us without waking us — a hand on each of our mouths, her eyes wide and black, her lips shaping a single word: *down*. We flattened ourselves among the stones as six shapes crested the southern ridge, moving without torches, without sound, which is not something horses do naturally. Their mounts were wrong in the starlight — too smooth in their gait, too patient, like animals dreaming of being ridden rather than actually ridden. The riders themselves wore no livery, but I saw the glint of a seal-brooch beneath the lead man's cloak, iron-shaped, ink-dark.

Merek's mark. I'd have known it anywhere; I'd watched him stamp it on my wages for two decades.

They fanned out around the standing stones with a competence that turned my stomach. These weren't bandits. These were soldiers doing a search pattern, and the pattern had been drawn by someone who knew exactly what they hunted: a woman, a man, a child.

The lead rider dismounted at the circle's edge. Up close he was younger than I expected — soft-faced, well-fed, the kind of officer who'd earned his rank through connections rather than campaigns. He held up one hand and spoke a word I didn't know, and the air above his palm began to glow: a pale, sickly light, the color of moonlight strained through dirty water.

Magic. Real magic, here, working — and aimed at finding us.

"Ember-scent," Sera breathed against my ear. "They'll sweep for Wren's spark. We have maybe thirty breaths before the light touches him and he lights up like a beacon."

"What do we do?"

She looked at me, and I saw her weighing options, discarding them, arriving at the one she hated. "I hold the circle," she said. "Old stones, older law — I can keep their seeking-light out for a little while. But then they stop being polite. Six armed men, Ada. I'm one exile with a bad leg and half my training. You're a cook."

"A taster."

"You know what I mean."

I did. And I knew she was right, and I knew we were going to die on this hill unless something changed, and the child asleep against my ribs was going to be dragged back beneath the mountain to feed a fire that would end my home forever — and something in me went very quiet and very cold, the way it went quiet when I found bitter almond in the honey.

"Keep them out," I said. "As long as you can."

"Ada—"

"*Keep them out.*"

I moved low along the inner edge of the circle, Wren's hand clutched in mine, and I did the only thing I had ever been trained to do: I thought about food. Specifically, I thought about the last meal I'd eaten — hard bread, dried plums, stream water — and about the twenty years of accumulated death sleeping in my veins. Arsenic and antimony. Hemlock and hellebore. Belladonna, aconite, mercury, lead, and forty poisons whose names I'd promised never to write down. Every morning of my life, a spoonful of dying, swallowed so that a king could eat in peace.

My body was a graveyard. I had always known that. What I had never asked myself — what no one had ever thought to ask — was what all those dead things did, buried together, in the dark of my blood.

The lead rider grew tired of waiting. His seeking-light flared, struck the invisible wall of Sera's circle, and slid off it like rain off glass. He said something sharp. Two of his men dismounted. They drew blades that glowed with that same drowned-moonlight color, and they stepped over the stones—

—and Sera screamed, a word of power that cracked the night open, and the world became very simple.

One of the glowing blades swung at me. I did the stupidest, bravest, most instinctive thing a lifetime of tasting had ever taught me: I caught it. Bare-handed, on the flat, because that's what you do when a blade swings at the child behind you and there is no time to be anything but a shield.

The enchanted edge bit into my palm — and *screamed.*

I mean that literally. The metal shrieked, a high animal sound, and the moonlight running along it guttered and died like a candle dropped in a well. The rider stumbled back, staring at his own weapon as if it had spoken blasphemy. Smoke rose from the cut in my hand — not from my flesh. From the *wound*, from whatever had flowed out of my blood to meet the magic and kill it.

The second rider lunged at Sera. She caught his blade on her forearm — deliberately, gods help her, deliberately — and the same thing happened: the enchantment curdled, flickered, went out, and the man cried out and staggered as if the failure had rebounded up his arm into his teeth.

They stared at us. We stared at them.

And I understood, all at once, standing in a ring of ancient stones with my hand smoking and my heart hammering, what twenty years of poison had actually built. Not immunity. Not merely immunity. My blood was a graveyard, yes — but graveyards are full of ghosts, and ghosts of murdered things have grievances. Every poison I'd ever swallowed had left something of itself behind in me, twisted and dead and *hungry*, and hungry things recognize their kin. Enchantment, curse-work, the mountain court's bright cold sorcery — it was all, at bottom, the same family as poison. Intent poured into a medium to harm. And my blood was a slaughterhouse where such things went to die.

"Ada," Sera whispered, and there was awe in it, and horror, and underneath both something that sounded almost like hope. "Your blood. They can't touch your blood."

The lead rider recovered fastest. "Take the woman!" he snapped. "The taster is nothing! He's a *servant!*"

He was right. That was the joke of it. I was nothing — a kitchen functionary, a professional corpse-in-waiting, a man whose entire life's work had been to die slightly slower than a king. Six armed men with magic blades against a cook, a cripple, and a child.

But they hesitated. One heartbeat, two. Because their weapons had screamed at my touch, and men who serve sorcery learn to fear whatever sorcery fears.

I looked down at my bleeding palm. Then at Wren, awake now, pressing against my leg. Then at the nearest rider, whose fine soft face had gone the color of tallow.

Twenty years I'd swallowed death so others wouldn't have to taste it.

Tonight, apparently, death had decided to swallow back.

"Stay behind me," I told Wren — and heard, for the first time since the border stone, the boy's voice rise in answer, that old language pouring out of him like water from a broken dam, urgent and bright. I didn't need translation. Some meanings cross every border.

*Yes. Yes. Yes.*

The first rider came at me with his dead blade held like a knife, and I went to meet him not as a taster, not as a servant, but as the thing twenty years of careful poisoning had made me: a walking plague dressed in a servant's coat. I caught his wrist. I held on. Where my blood touched his skin, his protective charms fizzled out like spit on a stove, and he looked into my face with the dawning, dumbstruck terror of a man discovering that the mouse he'd cornered was full of plague.

He broke and ran. So did two others, when Sera — laughing now, actually *laughing*, wild and fierce — drove them back with stones that remembered being altars. The remaining three regrouped at the circle's edge, blades raised, courage failing visibly, and it might have ended there in mutual, wary retreat—

Except the lead rider reached beneath his cloak and drew out something that stopped my heart: a small iron bell, black with age, its mouth sealed with wax.

He broke the wax with his thumb.

The bell rang once — a soundless ring, a ring I felt in my fillings and my bones — and every horse, every blade, every scrap of moonlit sorcery on that hillside turned toward the sound like flowers turning toward a poisoned sun. And far away, beyond the southern hills, beyond the valley, deep beneath the mountain, something vast stirred in answer.

The rider grinned at me through the dark, blood on his teeth from a bitten tongue.

"You're not the only one carrying the mountain's work in your veins, taster," he said. "Reinforcements are coming. And next time, we won't bring steel."
