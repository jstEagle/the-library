## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

**Ada**

The mountain's throat was not a cave. Caves had weather in them — drips, drafts, the smell of stone and old water. This was a throat in truth: it swallowed them, and Ada felt the swallow.

They had walked three days from the inn of small mercies, following a road that Sera swore was real and that Ada no longer trusted to stay where they left it. On the third evening the road simply ended at a wall of black rock split by a door tall as a cathedral and thin as a knife's edge. No hinges. No handle. Only a seam of light the color of winter dawn running down the stone.

Wren pressed both palms flat against the rock and hummed — one low note, then another, then a third that made Ada's teeth ache and his fillings sing. The seam widened with a sound like a held breath released after centuries.

"Polite," Sera murmured. "It likes being asked."

"Everything here will like being asked," Ada said. "That's what worries me."

He was learning to worry in a new grammar. In Vell Hollow he had worried about poison — about the specific, chemical ways men ended other men. Here the dangers were older and better dressed. He could feel them waiting below like courses not yet served.

The passage beyond the door descended in long spirals, and the walls were not walls. They were shelves. That was the only word for it — shelves cut into living rock, and on every shelf sat something small and glowing: a jar of trapped lightning, a bird carved from amber with a heartbeat visible inside its breast, a single shoe, child-sized, immaculate, humming faintly with someone else's grief. Thousands of shelves. Miles of them. Things bought, Ada understood, the way Wren had been bought. Not objects. Pieces of people.

Wren walked close to him and did not look at the shelves at all. That frightened Ada more than anything else in the dark.

"How far down?" he asked.

Sera's ember-lantern threw her shadow huge against the spiral wall. "Far enough that you'll stop thinking of up as home." She glanced back at him, and in the lantern light her face was unreadable in the way she'd been unreadable since the border stone — all those years banked behind her eyes like coals under ash. "When we reach the court, there are rules. I'll give you the ones that keep you alive. The rest you'll have to learn the way everyone learns them."

"Which is?"

"By paying."

* * *

**Sera**

She had told herself the descent would be easier the second time. It was not.

The court's cold fire found them before the court did — a blue-white glow rising through the spiral like light through deep water, sourceless, casting shadows that fell the wrong way. Sera remembered hating that light as a girl. She hated it now with the added weight of knowing exactly what burned in it: not flame but memory, compressed and set alight. The Under-Mountain did not burn wood. It burned what people used to be.

"You've been quiet since the inn," Ada said beside her. He said it lightly, but she heard the question underneath, the same question he'd been asking with his silences for two hundred miles: *What aren't you telling me?*

"I'm counting," she lied, which was almost true. She was counting doors, guards, exits, years. She was counting how many of the nobles below would remember her face and how many would pretend to.

"And?"

"And I'm trying to remember if any of them forgive."

"Do they?"

"No," she said. "But they're patient. Patience looks a great deal like forgiveness until the bill arrives."

Wren tugged her sleeve and signed — quick, fluid, the old tongue moving through his small hands like water finding its level. *The fire knows me.* His eyes were enormous in the blue light. *It is made of my family.*

Sera went still. "What did he say?"

Ada translated, and his voice cracked slightly on *family*, and Sera saw him understand it a half-second after he spoke it — that the child walking between them was kin to the light itself, that they were descending into a court lit by the stolen relatives of the boy they were trying to save.

"We keep moving," she said, because stopping would have meant kneeling, and kneeling down here was never just kneeling.

* * *

**Ada**

The last turn opened without warning, and Ada's breath left him the way breath leaves a man struck.

He had imagined a cavern. What he found was a city inverted — a vast hollow in the mountain's heart hung with terraces and bridges and stairways that climbed toward nothing, every surface edged in cold fire, every terrace crowded with figures who were beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful. The ceiling overhead was not stone but sky — or the memory of sky, sewn together from a thousand stolen dusks, stars pinned in place like jewels on velvet. Music came from somewhere above and nowhere in particular, strings played by hands he couldn't see.

And the people. They turned as one when the three travelers stepped onto the entry terrace, hundreds of faces rotating with the smooth simultaneity of a school of fish, and Ada felt their attention land on him like a physical weight — curious, amused, hungry in the abstract way cats are hungry.

"They're staring," he whispered.

"They're appraising," Sera corrected. "Staring is free. Nothing here is free."

The nearest noble glided forward. He wore robes the color of deep water and a collar of what looked like frost that never melted, and his face was young except for his eyes, which were old the way rivers are old. Around his neck, on a fine silver chain, hung a small glass vial, and inside the vial something golden turned slowly over and over — a summer afternoon, Ada realized with a lurch. A bottled hour of sunlight. The man wore a century like a pendant.

"A taster," the noble said, and his voice was honey poured over gravel. "From the valley that refuses us. How novel. They do send such interesting things to our door." His gaze slid past Ada to Sera, and everything in his face changed at once — the honey curdled, the amusement dropped away, and what remained was cold enough to frost glass. "And they send *her*."

Murmurs moved through the terraces like wind through wheat. Ada heard the name travel — *Ember-keeper, ember-keeper, the keeper who refused* — and watched nobles lean away from Sera as though her exile were contagious.

"You know her," Ada said quietly.

"Everyone knows her." The noble smiled without warmth. "She is the reason our fires burn low, little taster. She is the woman who looked at eternity and said *no thank you*. We keep her name the way your valley keeps its borders." He bowed — perfectly, beautifully, an insult dressed as courtesy. "Welcome to the court beneath the mountain. I am Vessin of the Ninth Terrace. You will find that we are excellent hosts."

"I've noticed," Ada said. "The last place that fed me this carefully wanted my king dead."

A ripple of laughter — genuine, delighted, terrible. Vessin's eyes glittered. "Oh, we are going to *enjoy* you."

* * *

**Sera**

Vessin. Of course it was Vessin. He had been old when Sera was young; he would be old when the mountain itself wore out. Once, a lifetime ago, he had offered her a vial of her own best day and told her she could wear it forever. She had thrown it into the ember-pit instead, and the pit had flared green for a week, and her exile had begun that night.

Now he stood between her and the throne-dais, blocking the sightline with exquisite politeness, and Sera understood that the game had already started — that it had started the moment the door opened, possibly the moment she was born.

"The child," Vessin said, gaze sliding to Wren with a hunger he didn't bother hiding. "Such a small thing to cause such large weather. The valley shuddered when he crossed, you know. We felt it here. Like a bell rung in another room." He crouched — slowly, theatrically — to bring his ancient eyes level with Wren's young ones. "Do you remember us, little ember? You were smaller when you left."

Wren stepped back into Ada's legs. His hand found Ada's sleeve and gripped.

"He doesn't remember you," Ada said, and his voice had gone flat and hard, the voice Sera imagined he used on kings' enemies. "Whatever you took from him, you took the remembering too."

"Did we?" Vessin rose, smoothing his robes. "Or did we simply hold it in trust? Contracts are sacred here, taster. The boy's soul was sold — sold willingly, signed in good faith by a desperate man in a bad winter. We do not steal. We *purchase*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire law." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "But you did not walk all this way to hear law recited. You came to petition. Very well. The Lord Under the Mountain hears petitions on the ninth night of each turning. Conveniently, tonight begins a turning."

"Ninth night," Sera repeated. Her stomach dropped. Nine nights in this court, eating its food, breathing its air, while the contract around Wren's soul tightened like a fist. "You mean to make us wait."

"I mean to make you *guests*." Vessin's smile was flawless. "There is a difference, and the difference is the entire hospitality. You will dine at the court's table each evening — all of you. It would be rude to refuse."

Behind him, on the highest terrace, beneath the sewn-together sky, a throne of black glass caught the cold fire and threw it back in shards. Something sat upon it. Sera could not make out a shape — only an attention, vast and patient, settling over the three of them like a hand measuring fruit at market.

*He sees us,* she thought. *He has seen us since the border stone.*

* * *

**Ada**

They were given rooms. That was the worst part, Ada thought later — that the enemy gave them beautiful rooms.

His chamber was cut into the spiral wall high above the court, furnished in a style half a millennium out of fashion, warmed — if that was the word — by a brazier of cold fire that cast no heat but made his reflection in the polished floor look healthier than he felt. A bed. A washbasin. A window of clear crystal looking down onto the inverted city, so that he could lie awake and watch the nobles drift across their terraces like luminous fish, wearing their bottled centuries, laughing at jokes older than kingdoms.

Wren slept on his bed; Ada sat on the floor beside it, back against the frame, because sleep was not coming and he had stopped pretending otherwise.

"Sera knew that man," he said softly, to see if Wren was awake.

Wren's eyes opened in the dark. He signed, slow and careful, translating himself into Ada's borrowed tongue: *Everyone here knew her. When they say her name, the fire gets colder.*

"What did she do?"

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then: *She is the only person who ever said no to them and lived.*

Ada looked out the crystal window, down at the terraces, at the throne he couldn't quite see, at the whole gorgeous machine of bargains and bottled time — and felt, beneath the awe, beneath the terror, something unexpected settle into his chest like a keel steadying a boat. For thirty years he had been a man built to absorb harm and want nothing. Down here, wanting was dangerous. Wanting got you priced.

But he wanted Wren safe. He wanted — though he'd have swallowed hot iron before saying it aloud — Sera's hand in his again, the way it had been at nineteen, before the exile, before the years. He wanted a kitchen that smelled of bread and a table set for three.

Let them price it, then. Let them study what he loved. A taster's whole life was training in exactly one skill: putting something deadly in your mouth and keeping your face still while you decided what to do about it.

Below, the court's music changed key — softer, sweeter, wrong. And on the highest terrace, the black-glass throne shifted, and for one clear moment Ada saw the figure upon it turn its head, unhurried, deliberate, and look directly up through the crystal window into his eyes.

It raised one hand. Not a threat. An invitation — the way a host gestures toward a chair.

Nine nights, the gesture said. Come and be tasted.

Ada did not look away. He held the Lord Under the Mountain's gaze until the figure smiled — he was almost sure it smiled — and turned back to its court.

Beside him, Wren had gone back to sleep, one fist curled against his mouth, breathing small and even. The cold fire in the brazier flickered once, like a held note ending.

Tomorrow the first dinner would be served.
