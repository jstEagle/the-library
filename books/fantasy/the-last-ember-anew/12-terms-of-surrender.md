## 12. Terms of Surrender

The court beneath the mountain did not have doors. It had thresholds, and every one of them was a mouth.

Ada felt this before he understood it — a sensation against his skin like walking through wet silk, the way the air changed taste as they passed from one gallery of the Lord's hall into another. Cold fire burned in sconces along the walls, blue-white flames that gave light but no warmth, and beneath them the nobles of the deep court turned to look at the three travelers as though they had been waiting all evening for exactly this entertainment.

He counted the ways they could kill him. It was an old habit, counting poisons in a room, and it settled him the way prayer settles other men. The wine in the goblets at the far table — bitter under its perfume of cherries, something mineral underneath. The smoke coiling from the censers — sweet, too sweet, the sweetness of things meant to be breathed rather than eaten. The lady nearest the dais wore rings on nine fingers, and the tenth finger was bare, and somehow that bareness frightened him more than any blade.

*Don't stare,* Sera murmured without moving her lips. *Staring is a question here, and questions are answered.*

*I've been staring since we came down the throat of the mountain,* Ada said. *If I owed them anything for looking, I'd be dead already.*

She almost smiled. In the torchlight of the upper world her face had been weather and stone; down here, lit by cold fire, she looked younger and more dangerous, the ember-keeper she must have been before exile made her careful. *That's because you haven't asked yet,* she said. *You're about to.*

Wren walked between them with his hand fisted in the hem of Ada's coat. The child had gone quiet in the way children go quiet when they are conserving themselves, the way soldiers do, the way Ada himself had learned to do across thirty years of swallowing death in small measured spoonfuls. His eyes moved over the court without wonder. That frightened Ada most of all. A child should have been afraid of this place. Wren looked at it like a boy returning to a house he'd been sold out of.

Because he had been, Ada reminded himself. Because that was why they were here.

The dais rose at the far end of the hall, carved from the mountain's own black bone, and upon it sat the Lord Under the Mountain.

Ada had expected a monster. He had prepared himself for monsters the way he prepared his tongue for arsenic — braced, cataloguing, ready. What he found instead was a man of indeterminate age wearing centuries the way other men wore rings, one century set into his brow like a garnet, another draped across his shoulders as a mantle of grey feathers that were not feathers. He was handsome the way a frozen lake is beautiful: perfectly, and with the understanding that beauty here was a surface over depth.

His voice, when it came, arrived inside Ada's chest before it reached his ears.

*A taster.* The Lord said the word slowly, tasting it himself, which Ada found obscenely familiar. *From Vell Hollow. From the valley that refuses us. You crossed our border-stone, you survived our roads, and you come into my hall smelling of poison and stubbornness, and you ask — what? Speak, little spoon. I collect small brave things.*

The whole court leaned in. Ada felt their attention like heat on the back of his neck, a hundred hungers pretending to be curiosity.

He had rehearsed this. On the roads, around fires that burned wrong-colored, while Sera slept and Wren dreamed in spirals, Ada had rehearsed the words until they sat smooth in his mouth. Now he found the rehearsal had been for nothing, because the words that came out were not the polished ones. They were plainer. They were true.

"My name is Ada Dravenmoor," he said. "For thirty years I have tasted for the crown of Vell Hollow. Everything the king has eaten or drunk has passed my tongue first, and I am alive, and he is alive, and there is not a poison bred above ground or below that I have not taken into my body and survived." He let that stand a moment. Somewhere in the hall, a fan stopped mid-wave. "I have come to buy back a soul. The soul of the child standing beside me. He is called Wren. He was sold to this court by men who had no right to sell him, and whatever bargain bought him, I am here to unmake it. Name your price."

Silence. Then laughter — not mocking, or not only mocking. It rolled through the galleries like water finding channels, and the nobles laughed with their mouths while their eyes stayed still, and Ada understood suddenly that he had amused them in a way that was also pleasing to them, the way a mouse is pleasing to a cat that has decided not to eat it yet.

The Lord Under the Mountain did not laugh. He regarded Ada with an expression of genuine, unhurried interest, the interest of a collector examining an acquisition.

*A taster who offers to pay,* the Lord said. *Do you know what souls cost here?*

"No."

*Neither do I.* Something moved behind the garnet in his brow. *No one has ever tried to buy one back. We sell, little spoon. We are very good at selling. Buying — that would require me to decide what the boy is worth, and worth is decided by hunger, and my hunger has never once been refused.* He rose. The mantle of not-feathers stirred. *But I am old, and old things love novelty. So hear terms.*

Sera's hand found Ada's sleeve. *Careful,* she breathed. *Terms, here, are—*

*Terms,* the Lord continued, and his voice filled the hall the way cold fills a cellar, *are this. You wish to petition for the child's soul. Petition is a process. Process requires precedent. Precedent requires proof that you understand what it is you ask to own.* He descended one step of the dais, and the temperature of the hall dropped with him. *You are a taster. Very well. Taste.*

"I've already told you what I can survive—"

*Not poison.* The Lord smiled, and it was a kind smile, and Ada's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. *Intent. Nine nights. My court will serve you at my table, dish by dish, and each dish will be brewed with purpose — forgetting, obedience, longing, despair, whatever my cooks conceive and my law permits. You will eat. You will judge. Your tongue will declare each dish true or false, clean or poisoned, and your declarations will bind this court as they would bind any kitchen under the old law.* Another step. He was close enough now that Ada could smell him — snow, iron, and underneath it something burnt and ancient, like a fire that had gone out before the first kingdoms were named. *Survive nine nights at my table, and I will hear your petition for the child with full ceremony. Fail — refuse a dish, sicken past serving, die, the usual outcomes — and the contract stands untouched, and you will leave my mountain alive if I am feeling generous, which historically I am not.*

"That's not buying," Ada said. "That's gambling."

*Everything is gambling.* The Lord spread his hands. *You gambled crossing my border. You gamble every time you swallow for another man. The only question is whether you know the stakes, and now, thanks to me, you do.* His gaze slid sideways, deliberate, to where Wren stood. *And while you dine, the woman may negotiate separately. She knows how negotiations run here. She ran one once before, and we all remember how that ended.* 

Ada felt Sera go rigid beside him. Whatever history lived in that sentence, it had teeth, and the Lord knew it, and had shown it deliberately — a card laid face-up on the table so they both would know he held the rest of the deck.

"Nine nights," Ada said aloud, because saying it plainly was the last power he had left. "I eat for nine nights. You hear me after."

*I hear you after,* the Lord agreed. *And little spoon — one courtesy, since you came so far. Do not mistake the dishes for attacks. Some will be. But some will be gifts. The cruelest thing about my table is that everything on it is offered in love.* He turned back toward the dais. *The court will show you to your rooms. Dinner is at moonrise. We keep time differently here; you'll learn, or you won't.*

* * *

The rooms they were given were beautiful, which Ada had already learned to read as a warning.

They occupied a gallery off the great hall, walled in veined black stone shot through with threads of silver that glowed faintly, like veins of ore remembering a fire. There were three beds, which was either hospitality or accounting — someone had counted them, the way Ada counted goblets. A window of clear crystal looked out onto nothing: not darkness, not rock, simply an absence with depth, as though the mountain kept a piece of nowhere behind glass for guests to contemplate.

Wren sat on the smallest bed and drew in the dust on the floor with one finger. Spirals. Always spirals, and mountains, and the door under stone. But tonight he added something new — two small figures beside the spiral, and then a third, and he tapped each one and looked up at Ada with an expression that needed no translation older than kingdoms.

"Yes," Ada said. "All three of us. I'm not leaving without you. That's the entire point of this idiotic errand."

Wren's mouth twitched — the ghost of a smile, the first Ada had seen since the descent. Then it faded, and the child pressed his palm flat against his own chest, and then drew his fingers away slowly, like a man pulling a splinter, and looked at Ada with a question that Ada could not read at all. *Something* was happening to him here. The contract tightening, Sera had called it. Every hour under the mountain, the boy grew a little more transparent at the edges, a little more like a sketch of a child rather than a child.

"We have nine days," Ada told him, and heard his own voice steady itself the way it did in the tasting chamber, over the king's porridge, over the cup that might hold death. "Nine dinners. I've eaten worse than dinners my whole life. I once took enough hemlock to fell a horse and reviewed the vintage afterward."

Wren didn't laugh. He reached up and touched Ada's wrist, right over the pulse, and held it there a moment — checking, the way a physician checks, the way a taster checks — and then lay down on the bed and closed his eyes.

* * *

"He's playing with us."

Sera stood at the crystal window, arms crossed, watching the nowhere beyond the glass as if it might develop features. Her reflection watched Ada instead.

"Obviously," Ada said. He was checking the room the way he checked any room — corners, surfaces, the underside of the table, the seam of the mattress where powders liked to hide. Old habit. Useless habit, probably, in a place where the poison might be the mattress itself. "A nine-night feast. A negotiation split in two. He wants us separated at his table and separated in our councils, and he wants me hungry and afraid and grateful, all at once. It's the oldest menu in the world."

"Then say why you accepted."

"Because the alternative was leaving." He straightened. "There is no alternative, Sera. There's the table or the road home with empty hands, and I've spent thirty years being handed things and handing them back untouched. I'm done tasting and refusing. I want to *swallow* something for once that isn't poison."

She turned from the window. In the silver light of the walls her scars showed pale — the burn-marks along her forearm that she never explained, the marks of a keeper who had tended embers and paid for it. "You don't understand what his table is," she said quietly. "I ate there once. Before my exile. One meal, one dish, and I carried it out of the mountain in my bones for a decade. The food isn't the danger, Ada. The danger is what you find out about yourself while you're eating it."

"Then I'll find it out," he said. "I'd rather know what's in me than spend my life not knowing. That's all a taster is, in the end. Someone who finds out."

Sera looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her expression — the same thing that had moved behind it on the road, at the inn, at every fire they'd shared since the border stone. Something she kept folding away and putting in her pocket, and the pocket was getting heavy; he could see it in the set of her shoulders.

"There's something you're not telling me," Ada said. "About the separate negotiation. About what he meant — *she knows how negotiations run here.*"

"Yes," Sera said.

"And?"

"And I'll tell you when telling you won't break the thing we need to do." She crossed the room, and for a moment her hand hovered near his face — not touching, never quite touching, thirty years of distance maintained to the width of a breath. "Eat carefully. Judge slowly. And Ada — when a dish tastes like something you love, that's when you send it back fastest. Promise me."

"It's a little late for me to promise not to love things," Ada said. "I did that for thirty years. Look where it got me. Underground, negotiating with a man who wears centuries as jewelry."

The ghost of a smile again, hers this time. Then a bell rang somewhere deep in the mountain — a sound like a struck tooth — and the silver veins in the walls brightened, and dimmed, and brightened.

*Moonrise,* Sera translated. *Dinner.*

* * *

The table was longer than any hall in Vell Hollow, and it had been set for hundreds, though only dozens sat at it, and the empty places were not empty — shadows occupied them, seated with perfect posture, patient as furniture.

Ada's place was at the Lord's right hand. Of course it was. The honored seat, the exposed seat, the seat where every eye could watch him swallow.

The first course came in under a cloche of smoked glass, borne by a servant whose face was politely unfinished, like a portrait someone had stopped halfway. The Lord lifted the cloche himself.

*Breathe first,* he instructed, with the air of a maître d' presenting a signature dish. *Tell the court what you find.*

Steam rose — thin, silver, smelling of rain on hot stone, of the courtyard where Ada had learned to walk, of the specific angle of morning light through the palace kitchens. His throat tightened around nothing.

*Well?*

Ada closed his eyes, opened them, and did the only thing he had ever truly known how to do. He picked up the spoon, and he tasted, and he began to name what he found — the base notes, the intent steeped into the broth, the memory folded into it like butter into dough — and the entire court leaned forward to listen, hungry, patient, and absolutely silent.

Eight nights remained. The first dish smelled like home, and he had not even swallowed yet.
