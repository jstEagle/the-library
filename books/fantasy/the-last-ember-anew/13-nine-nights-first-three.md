## 13. Nine Nights, First Three

The first dish arrived on a plate of black glass, and it smelled like the morning I was born.

I know that sounds like poetry. It wasn't. It was a fact, delivered as plainly as the smell of bread or rot — my own first morning, set before me in a shallow bowl of pale gold broth, steam curling upward in shapes too deliberate to be accidental. The court beneath the mountain did not season its food with salt and pepper. It seasoned with intent.

"Tonight," said the herald, a tall creature whose skin had the gray sheen of river stone, "the court offers the taster a soup of forgetting. Eat, and be unburdened."

Around the long table, the nobles watched. They wore their centuries like jewelry — rings that had outlived kingdoms, collars strung with teeth of things I refused to name. Their faces were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: precise, cold, and melting only when they wanted something.

Wren sat beside me, small hands folded in his lap because court etiquette demanded it even of children, especially of stolen ones. He had grown paler each day since we'd descended through the mountain's throat. Not sick-pale. Thinner-pale, as though the contract binding his soul were a rope someone above was slowly hauling in, and every night at this table the rope shortened by another arm's length.

*I'm here,* I signed to him under the table, in the kitchen-language we'd built together between the valley and the world. *Eat nothing. Watch everything.*

He nodded once. His eyes were still his own. That was the thing I clung to.

I lifted the spoon.

Here is what no one tells you about a lifetime of tasting poison: you stop fearing the bowl. Fear is for people who might be surprised. I had spent thirty years being surprised by nothing — bitter almond under honey, henbane in wine, ground glass ground fine enough to pass for sugar. My tongue had catalogued death so thoroughly that death had become boring. What I felt now, facing a soup brewed to erase me, was not courage. It was habit wearing courage's clothes.

The broth touched my tongue and I knew it at once — not by name, but by shape. It tasted the way forgetting feels: like walking into a room and losing the sentence you were about to say. Like waking from a dream you can't quite — 

I set down the spoon.

My immunity held. Whatever alchemy lived in my blood, thickened by three decades of swallowed harm, met the soup of forgetting and turned it aside the way a stone turns rain. I could feel it working, a faint warmth behind my breastbone, my body quietly refusing what my mind understood perfectly well.

"The taster finds the dish wanting," I announced, in the formal cadence Sera had drilled into me. A taster's word is sovereign; I had learned that much of the mountain's law before ever sitting at its table. If I declared a dish poisoned, the court could not compel me to swallow it. But if I ate and approved — approval bound them, and bound me.

"And yet he does not declare it poisoned," murmured the Lord Under the Mountain from the head of the table.

He was not the largest figure in the hall, nor the most terrible. He was simply the most *present*, the way a well is present in a room — a depth where nothing should be. His voice came from everywhere the light didn't reach.

"No," I agreed. "It is honestly made. It is also not food. It is an erasure served in broth." I let my gaze travel the length of the table. "In my valley, we have a word for such things. We call it poison. But your law is not mine, and I am told my tongue must speak yours while I sit here. So: not poisoned. Merely unacceptable."

A ripple went through the nobles — amusement, or its imitation. Somewhere a fan of moth-wing opened and closed.

"You are more interesting than your reputation suggested," the Lord said. "We were told Vell Hollow's taster was a dull instrument. A shield that walks."

"I've been called worse by better-dressed enemies."

The Lord smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were busy elsewhere — on Wren, always on Wren, the way a creditor watches a debtor cross a room.

"Nine nights," he said. "Eat what is offered. Speak your judgments truly. At the end, we will discuss terms for the child's soul." He lifted his glass, and the cold fire in the chandeliers dimmed respectfully. "The court is patient, taster. Be sure you can afford to be less so."

* * *

The second night's dish was a small bird, roasted, arranged on a nest of dark greens — and the moment it was set before me, I wanted it with a want that had teeth.

Not hunger. Obedience. I recognized the flavor from the first bite: rosemary and iron and something sweeter underneath, something that slid past the tongue entirely and settled into the spine. By the third bite my hand was moving without consulting me, fork rising, falling, rising again, and somewhere very far away I observed with professional detachment that this was the finest-crafted compulsion I had ever been served, and that my body was eating it anyway.

*Stop.* The thought came from behind my own eyes, faint as a voice through a wall. *You are allowed to stop.*

I stopped. My hand froze mid-air with a forkful of bird trembling on it, and sweat broke cold across my neck, and around the table the nobles leaned forward with the collective hunger of an audience watching a tightrope walker sway.

"It fights him," one said, delighted. "Look how it fights him."

My immunity was a wall built against poison — against things meant to kill. This was not meant to kill. This was meant to *instruct*. And walls built against knives do not stop whispers.

I put down the fork. I put both hands flat on the table, which was rude, which was probably treasonous by local custom, and I breathed the way Sera had taught me on the road — four counts in, seven counts out, counting being the oldest magic either of us owned.

When I could trust my voice, I said: "The dish is competently made. The bird is overcooked."

Laughter, this time — real laughter, or close enough. The Lord's mouth curved. "And the rest of it?"

"The rest of it," I said, "is none of your business what it almost did to me."

That night, back in the guest chambers they'd assigned us — beautiful rooms, soft beds, windows that showed a sky with too many stars arranged in patterns no valley sky had ever dared — Wren sat on the floor at my feet and pressed his palm flat against my knee.

*It got inside,* he signed. His movements were slower than they'd been a week ago. Slower, and smaller, as though the language itself were heavy now. *I saw it walk in your arm.*

"For a moment." I covered his hand with mine. His fingers were cool. They had always been cool, but lately they were cool the way stones are cool, the way things are cool that are further away than they appear. "It won't again. I'll know the taste now."

*They don't need you to eat it,* he signed, and then stopped, and looked at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him. *They need you to keep coming back. Nine nights is a door. Every night opens it wider.*

I didn't ask how he knew. Children who are sold to courts beneath mountains learn the architecture of bargains the way valley children learn the smell of rain.

"Then we'll walk through it together," I said, "and I'll burn the door behind us."

He smiled — the first smile in two days — and for a little while, in a room lit by cold fire in a court that owned his soul, a mute boy and a poison-eater sat on the floor and taught each other kitchen words until sleep took us both.

* * *

The third night broke me.

I want to be honest about that, because everything else in this account will sound like competence, and competence is the lie I have told longest. On the third night, the court served me a childhood that wasn't mine, and I wept into my cup like a man grieving his own grave.

The dish was simple. That was the cruelty of it. A bowl of porridge, honeyed, with cream swirled through in a pattern I knew — swirls like the ones our cook Marta made in the palace kitchens, except this bowl was smaller, wooden, chipped at the rim, sitting on a table in a kitchen I had never stood in but suddenly remembered with my whole body. Sunlight through a window. A woman's voice calling a name —

*Alda. Alda, come eat, before it goes cold.*

I don't remember picking up the spoon. I remember the taste: oats and honey and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of being five years old and loved without condition. My immunity met it and found nothing to fight, because there was no poison in it. There was no compulsion in it. There was only a memory, brewed with intent, of a mother I never had, in a home I never had, in a life some cook beneath the mountain had simmered for hours until it was tender enough to serve.

I ate all of it. Every spoonful. And I wept — not elegantly, not the single dignified tear the court would have appreciated, but ugly, hitching sobs that bent me over the black glass table while nobles watched with their frost-beautiful faces and one of them actually sighed with pleasure, the way you sigh over a particularly successful soufflé.

"That one," a noblewoman whispered, "is going in my book of recipes."

The worst part — the part I have not told Sera, and may never tell her — is that when the bowl was empty, I missed it. The false childhood sat in me like a coal, warm and wrong, and some traitorous corner of my heart wanted to ask for seconds. Wanted to live in that kitchen, that sunlight, that name, more than it wanted anything true I had ever possessed.

This is what the court understood that I had not: they were not testing whether my immunity would fail. They were mapping what I lacked. A man who has spent his whole life swallowing harm for other people builds himself hollow to survive it — and hollowness, I learned on the third night, is a space that remembers being full, and aches accordingly.

The Lord Under the Mountain watched me cry with the expression of a merchant confirming a price.

"Home," he said softly, tasting the word. "That is what tonight's dish was. Not forgetting. Not obedience. Homesickness — for a home that never existed, which is the most obedient homesickness of all, because no real home can compete with it." He folded his long gray hands. "Tell me, taster. When you return to Vell Hollow — *if* you return — what waits for you? A king who cannot tell your face from his other servants'. A chamber off the kitchens. A valley that forbids the very thing you are becoming."

I wiped my face with the back of my hand. My voice, when it came, surprised me by holding.

"A boy who needs me," I said. "That waits for me. You priced the wrong thing."

Something flickered across the Lord's face — I would call it surprise, but surprise is a human luxury and I doubt he kept any. Interest, perhaps. Recalculation.

"We shall see," he said, "on the ninth night, what you are willing to pay for him."

* * *

Afterward, in our chambers, I found Wren standing at the window, and the sight of him stopped my breath.

The cold firelight came through him. Not around — *through*, faintly, the way evening sun comes through a curtain. Where his shadow should have fallen dark on the floor, it fell thin and gold-edged, and when he turned to look at me his eyes had gone the color of banked coals, orange buried under ash.

*Ada.* His hands moved slowly, carefully, a boy conserving himself like a candle conserving flame. *Three nights left of the easy ones. I can feel the rope. Tonight it pulled.*

"How much longer can you hold?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because it was the question of a man checking how much time remained on a loan.

He considered. Honesty was the one gift he still gave freely.

*Six nights,* he signed. *Maybe fewer. After that, whatever is left of me belongs to them, and the boy you know will be a coat they wear.*

I crossed the room and knelt so we were eye to eye, this child older than kingdoms in a body barely ten years old, and I took his cool hands in mine.

"Then hear me," I said. "Four feasts left after tonight. I don't care what they serve — memories, mothers, obedience, despair. I have eaten poison every day of my life so that other people could live safely, and I have gotten very, very good at surviving things that were meant to destroy me. These six nights, you hold on. That is your only duty. Hold on, and let me do the tasting."

His coal-colored eyes searched my face. Then, slowly, he raised one hand and signed a word we had never needed before — a word from the old language, the one older than any kingdom, the one I somehow understood without knowing why.

The closest translation, in the kitchen-tongue we'd built, was *promise*. But the old word meant something larger. It meant: *what I give you, I give from the ember of me, and it cannot be taken back.*

"I promise," I said aloud, and the cold fire in the chandeliers flickered — just once, just slightly, as if the mountain itself had heard a vow made in a language it thought long dead.

Wren's eyes widened. He looked up at the chandeliers, then at me, and something moved behind the ash in his gaze. Hope, maybe. Or warning.

Because I had heard it too, in the instant the fires guttered: far below us, deep in the roots of the mountain, something vast turning over in its sleep — and a voice, thin as wire, saying words I couldn't make out but recognized the shape of.

Sera's name.

She had been absent from the table these three nights. Negotiating separately, the Lord had said. And now the mountain spoke her name in the dark, in a tone I knew from thirty years at a king's side — the tone of a bargain being sealed.

I stood at the window a long time, Wren's cool hand in mine, and watched the too-many stars wheel in their impossible patterns, and told myself there was an innocent explanation.

I didn't believe it. But I told myself.
