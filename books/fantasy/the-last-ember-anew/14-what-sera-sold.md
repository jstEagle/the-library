## 14. What Sera Sold

The summons came the way everything came in the mountain: politely.

Sera was washing her hands in the basin of her guest-chamber — a room too beautiful to be a cell and too locked to be anything else — when the water went still. Not calm. *Still*, the way a held breath is still. In its surface, a face formed that was not her reflection: a man made of cold fire and long patience, his eyes like coins left on a dead man's eyes.

*Ember-keeper,* said the Lord Under the Mountain, though his lips did not move. His voice arrived inside her skull like frost arriving on glass. *You will dine with me tonight. Alone.*

She should have refused then. She knew that even as she dried her hands on linen soft enough to make her angry. Refusal was a language she had spoken fluently once, at nineteen, standing in this same mountain with her mother's iron key burning in her fist. But she had been someone else then — or she had been exactly herself, and the years since had worn her down to someone she liked less.

"I'll come," she said to the empty water.

The ripples that answered looked almost like approval.

* * *

They led her down through corridors she remembered too well. The court beneath the mountain did not build; it *accumulated*, the way a wound accumulates scar. Hallways of black stone veined with ember-light, arches carved with bargains instead of prayers, doors that whispered the names of everyone who had ever passed through them. Sera kept her eyes forward. She had learned, in her first life here, that the architecture fed on attention. Look too long at a wall and it would show you what you missed most.

Her mother's face had lived in these walls for thirty years. She had stopped looking around the age of forty.

Two servants of the court flanked her — tall, gray-robed, their faces smooth as river stones. Not nobles. Nobles wore their centuries like jewelry; these things had no centuries to wear. They walked without sound, which meant they were listening for hers.

At the last door, one of them spoke. Its voice was dry leaves and old smoke.

"The Lord bids you leave your iron."

Sera's hand had already gone to her belt, where the small iron key hung — not her mother's key, that was long gone, but a copy she'd had forged in a border town the year after her exile, because grief is superstitious and so was she. She unhooked it. Set it in the thing's palm. It closed its fingers around the metal and the metal *screamed*, a thin high note only she could hear, and then it was quiet.

"You'll get it back," the servant said. It was not a promise. Nothing in this place was a promise except a bargain, and bargains were the only law.

* * *

The Lord Under the Mountain received her in a hall lit by cold fire — flames that gave light without warmth, blue-white and utterly silent, burning in braziers of bone-white stone. He sat at the head of a table set for two, and he had taken a shape calculated to please her: an old man's kindness, a scholar's stoop, eyes that crinkled when he smiled. He had worn this face the night she first refused him. He remembered what worked.

"Ember-keeper." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Or may I call you Sera? You were never formal, as I recall. Even at nineteen you argued with me like a fishwife."

"And you threatened me like a king," she said, sitting. "We've both kept our habits."

"I have kept more than habits. I have kept your place." He lifted a covered dish from before her seat — silver, domed, humming faintly. "Your chamber above, with the south light. Your title. Your mother's key, returned to you whole. All of it waiting, all these years, because I do not waste what is valuable."

The dish cover rose under his hand. Steam curled up, and in the steam Sera smelled bread — real bread, valley bread, the dark rye they baked in Vell Hollow with caraway and a crust that cracked like ice on a pond. Her mouth flooded. Her chest ached. That was the point. Everything here was the point.

"Eat," the Lord said gently. "It is only bread. I know how you hunger for home."

"I'm not hungry," Sera said, "for anything you're serving."

He laughed — a warm, terrible sound, the laugh of a grandfather at a festival. "Still sharp. Good. Sharpness survives exile better than sweetness does. I would know; I have exiled many." He folded his hands, and the cold fire bent toward him slightly, the way fire bends toward the thing that feeds it. "Let us not fence. You know why you are here. The child."

"Wren."

"If you like. Names are clothing, and that child has worn many. The ember wears whatever we dress it in." His eyes, kind and ancient, fixed on hers. "You felt it cross your border stone. You felt the valley shudder. Do not pretend otherwise, keeper. You have been walking toward this table since the day you found them in the grass."

Sera said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill, and she had practiced it for three decades.

"The terms are simple, because I respect you enough to be simple." The Lord leaned forward. "Deliver the child to me. Alone. No taster, no witnesses, no complications. Bring me the ember unaccompanied, and I will restore everything your refusal cost you. Your title. Your home. Your name in the rolls of the keepers, unburned. And—" here his voice softened into something almost human, almost cruel in its gentleness— "your mother's last memory, which we hold. She died calling for you, Sera. We can give you her final words. We can give you the hour you were not there for."

The hall was very quiet. The cold fire burned without crackling, without breath.

Thirty years ago, Sera had stood in this hall at nineteen years old with her fists shaking and told the Lord Under the Mountain that she would rather burn than feed his fires. She had meant it with her whole self, the way only the young mean things. The ember-fires needed feeding — that was the keepers' oldest duty and darkest one, the reason the order existed, the reason her mother had taught her to carry iron and salt and silence. The court's cold flame was not fire at all but hunger given light, and it ate magic to live, and the richest magic in the world was the founding ember of a valley built to refuse magic. Feed it the valley's heart, her mother had said, and the valley dies. Refuse, and we die instead. Sera had refused. The court had exiled her with a brand on her shoulder blade and a price on her return, and she had spent thirty years learning to be a woman who did not look back.

And now the Lord slid her mother's dying words across the table like a coin.

"I see the temptation," he said softly. "Good. Temptation is honesty. Only liars feel none." He rose, and the shadows rose with him, courteous as servants. "You need not answer tonight. You need only answer before the ninth dish. After that, the contract closes, and the child belongs to us regardless — and you will have lost your chance at everything, forever, for the sake of a taster who cannot taste enchantment and a boy who is not truly a boy."

He paused at the door.

"For what it is worth, Sera — and it is worth something, from me — I hope you say yes. It would spare us all such tedious ceremony." The door opened onto darkness. "Dinner is concluded. The bread is real. Take it, if you like. A gift costs nothing until accepted."

She did not take the bread. She wanted to. She wanted it with a violence that frightened her — wanted to tear the crust and fill her mouth with home — and that wanting was the truest thing in the room, and she carried it out with her like a knife she couldn't put down.

* * *

She said nothing to Ada.

That was the decision, and she made it in the space of ten steps back up the corridor, and she hated herself with a clean and familiar efficiency while she made it. Because she *hadn't* said yes. She had refused — again, again, always again — and refusing was supposed to be the whole of virtue, wasn't it? She had stood in the cold fire and heard her mother's death offered to her like dessert and she had walked away. Wasn't that enough? Didn't the refusal count if no one saw it?

But she knew how Ada would hear it. She knew the exact shape of his face when she told him — the taster's face, careful and unreadable, the face of a man whose profession was detecting poison in what people offered him — and she knew that somewhere behind it, a small door would close. *She considered it,* that closed door would say. *She weighed my life against her mother's voice, and it took her longer than a heartbeat.* And the worst of it, the part that made her stop halfway up the stairs and press her palm against the cold stone, was that the closed door would be fair. She *had* considered it. The temptation had lasted four full breaths, and four breaths is a long time when a child's soul is on the scale.

Truth deferred is a debt accruing interest. Her mother used to say that about secrets among keepers. *A secret kept past its season doesn't protect anyone, little bird. It just grows teeth.*

"Then I'll pay the interest," Sera said to the stone, quietly, in the language of the keepers, which had no word for *forgive* — only a word meaning *the debt is carried together*. She had no one to carry it with. That was the exile's whole condition.

She climbed the rest of the stairs and went to find Ada, and she smiled at him, and the smile cost more than the refusal had.

* * *

She found him in Wren's chamber, sitting on the floor beside the boy's bed, watching him sleep. This had become his habit. She understood it. Watching Wren sleep was the only hour of the day when neither of them had to be brave.

"How was dinner?" Ada asked, not looking up. His voice was flat with exhaustion. Seven feasts sat on him like seven winters. The court had been serving him his own memories plated in gold leaf, and each morning he woke a little lighter, a little more hollowed, a man being eaten politely from the inside.

"Bread and lies," Sera said. Which was true. Which was also a lie, by omission, and she felt the debt tick upward as she said it.

"They served you bread?"

"Valley bread. Rye and caraway." She sat down across from him, on the other side of the bed, so that Wren lay between them like a border. "It was a message. They know what we miss. They've been taking inventory."

Ada huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I got my ninth birthday. The one I don't remember having. There was honey cake, and a dog I apparently loved, and my mother laughing in a kitchen I've never seen." He rubbed his eyes. "I sat there trying to work out whether I was grieving a memory or grateful for one. That's the trick of this place, isn't it? They don't poison the food. They poison the *wanting*."

Sera looked at him — this man she had loved at nineteen and loved now in some older, harder grammar — and the secret sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Tell him.* Tell him the Lord offered you everything. Tell him you thought about it for four breaths. Tell him the four breaths are still going, in some sealed room inside you, and might go on forever.

Instead she said, "Wren's paler."

"He's paler every morning." Ada's jaw tightened. "The contract's tightening. Whatever they bought, they're collecting it in installments." He reached out and brushed the hair off the boy's forehead — a gesture so tender it hurt to witness, done with the same hands that spent every evening tasting death for a king. "Three nights left, Sera. Three feasts. And I still don't know what we're doing. I keep tasting their dishes and nodding and smiling and I don't know what any of it means, and meanwhile they're pricing me. I can feel it. Like a butcher deciding which cuts to take."

"We'll find the seam," Sera said. "Every law has a seam. Theirs especially — a court built entirely on bargains is a court built entirely on loopholes. We just have to find ours before the ninth dish."

Ada nodded slowly. Then he looked up at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had tasted ten thousand dishes and trusted almost no one, and they rested on her face for one moment too long.

"You'd tell me," he said. Not a question, not quite. "If they came to you. If they offered you something. You'd tell me."

The coal in her chest flared white.

"Yes," Sera said.

She said it steadily. She said it the way she had learned to say everything in thirty years of exile — with a level voice and steady hands and no tell in her face, because a keeper's training and a liar's discipline are the same skill wearing different clothes. Ada held her gaze a moment longer, and then something in his shoulders loosened, and he nodded, and looked back down at the sleeping boy.

And Sera sat there in the cold-fire light with the debt accruing, interest compounding by the hour, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just done the one thing worse than considering the Lord's offer.

She had hidden that she considered it.

Across the bed, Wren stirred in his sleep. His small hand crept out from under the blanket and found Ada's wrist and gripped it, the way a drowning man grips a rope, and in his sleep the boy spoke — one word, in the old language, the language Sera half-knew and Ada somehow wholly knew.

Sera knew the word. Every ember-keeper knew it. It was the word written over the door of the deepest vault, the word her mother had died guarding:

*Ember.*

Wren said it again, softer, and turned his face toward the wall, and the faintest glow showed through the skin of his back — there and gone, like a coal breathing under ash.

Ada hadn't seen it. He was watching Sera's face instead, and whatever he found there made him frown.

"Sera," he said slowly. "What aren't you telling me?"

Above them, muffled through floors of black stone, the court began to sing — the wordless grace they sang before every feast, patient as dripping water. Two nights remained. The debt was due.
