## 15. Nights Four Through Six

The fourth night, they served me my childhood.

I knew it before the cloche was lifted — knew it by the smell alone, woodsmoke and barley and the particular sweetness of bread baked in a hurry by someone who loved you badly but loved you. My mother's kitchen had smelled like that. She'd been dead thirty years, and my mouth watered anyway.

The dish sat on black slate: a small round loaf, split and steaming, with butter melting into its heart in slow golden threads. Around it, arranged like fallen leaves, were curls of something dark and glistening that I did not recognize and did not want to.

"You'll forgive us," said the Lord Under the Mountain, from the far end of a table long enough to hold a funeral. His voice arrived at my ear without crossing the distance, the way cold comes through a wall. "We thought tonight we would serve you something you already know."

"A taster's work is tasting," I said. "Not remembering."

"Ah." He smiled, and his teeth were like keys. "But what is memory, taster, if not the oldest dish? Everything you have ever eaten is still in you. Nothing is ever gone. We merely... plate it again."

Sera sat to my left, as she had every night since the terms were struck, her hands folded, her face carved smooth as river stone. Nine nights, I had agreed to eat at this table while she negotiated separately in rooms I was not shown. Three nights down. Six remained. Wren was somewhere above us in the guest chambers they had given him — gilded, comfortable, locked — and each morning he looked a little more translucent, a little more like light through paper.

I broke the bread.

It tasted exactly right. That was the horror of it. Not approximately right, not a court cook's clever imitation — exactly right, down to the scorch on the bottom crust where my mother's oven ran hot on the left side, down to the salt she always over-measured because grief had blunted her tongue years before it stopped her heart. I was nine years old again for the length of one swallow, standing on a stool to reach the table, and my mother was humming off-key, and outside the window Vell Hollow lay green and safe under a sky that magic could not touch.

Then the swallow finished, and I was in the dark, and every noble at that long table was watching me the way scholars watch a specimen.

"Well?" the Lord asked gently.

"It's poisoned," I said, because it was, and because saying so was the only power I had left in that place.

"Of course it is." He sounded pleased. "Everything here is. That is rather the point of you."

* * *

Between feasts, they let me walk the corridors. This was not kindness; I understood that by the second day. The corridors were curved and windowless and lit by cold blue fire that burned without heat, and they rearranged themselves when I wasn't looking — not maliciously, just the way a dream does, so that I was never quite lost and never quite found. It kept me disoriented. It also kept me moving, and movement kept me from thinking too long about Wren's fading hands.

On the fourth night, after the bread, I walked until I found Sera.

She was in an alcove where the blue fire pooled like water, sitting on the floor with her back against stone, and for once her mask was down. She looked tired in a way I remembered from twenty years ago — tired the way she got after long vigils tending the ember-shrines, when keeping a fire alive meant staying awake past the point where your own body felt like yours.

"Tell me what they're feeding you," she said, before I could speak.

"Bread. My mother's bread." I slid down the wall beside her. The stone was warm, which unsettled me more than cold would have. "How did they know? How does a court under a mountain know what a dead woman's kitchen smelled like?"

"Because they bought pieces of you long ago, Ada. Everyone who ever sold anything down here — a memory, a year, a name — those things circulate. They're currency. Someone, somewhere, sold a memory of your street. Your valley. Maybe your mother." She turned her head against the stone to look at me. "Nothing is private here. That's the first thing you have to accept."

"The first thing."

"There are worse lessons coming." Her voice went flat. "They're not feeding you your childhood out of cruelty. Or not only. They're mapping you. Every dish tells them something — what you flinch at, what you lean toward, what you'd trade. By the ninth night they'll know the exact weight of everything you love."

"And then?"

"Then they'll know what to charge you for the boy."

I stared at the blue fire. "And your negotiations? What do they know about *you*?"

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, buried, gone. Twenty years ago I would have read it easily. Twenty years of silence between us had rusted the hinge.

"They know enough," she said, and stood, and offered me her hand, and I took it because refusing it would have cost us both more than pride.

* * *

The fifth night, they served me the king.

It came as a soup — pale gold, thickened with cream, floating petals of some herb I couldn't name. And the moment it touched my tongue I was twenty-two years old again, kneeling in the royal kitchens of Vell Hollow, and King Aldous was crouching down beside me — the king, crouching, ruining his robes on flour-dusted stone — because I had just swallowed hemlock for him and was shaking with it, and he held my shoulder and said, *You will not die for me today, Ada Dravenmoor. Do you hear me? You will live, and I will remember.*

And he had remembered. Thirty years of remembering. Extra blankets in winter. A chair near the fire at council, unasked for. Once, when I was sick with something ordinary, he'd sat by my bed himself and read aloud from a book of bad poetry until I laughed hard enough to cough.

The soup tasted like all of it. Like being seen. Like mattering to someone whose mattering was measured in kingdoms.

I put down the spoon.

Around the table, the nobles leaned in — centuries-old faces lit blue and hungry. One of them, a woman whose gown appeared to be made of moth wings, actually sighed.

"He loves him," she murmured, delighted. "Oh, look how much he loves him. That's worth *such* a great deal."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because denying it would be another kind of telling.

The Lord Under the Mountain laced his fingers. "Tell me, taster. In all your years of service, did the king ever once ask what the poison cost you? Not whether you survived — whether you were *well*. Whether the numbness spread. Whether you dreamed."

"He didn't need to ask."

"No," the Lord agreed softly. "Kind men rarely do. Kindness is what makes servitude bearable, and bearable servitude is the most durable kind ever built." He gestured at my bowl. "Finish it. There's no harm in it. It's only love, and love keeps."

I should have refused. Some part of me knew, even then, that every spoonful was a confession. But the soup was warm and the king's voice was in it, and I was so far underground, so far from the valley, so afraid for a child who was going gray at the edges — and I finished it. All of it.

When I looked up, the moth-winged woman was writing something on a slate. Numbers, maybe. Prices.

* * *

The sixth night, they served me Sera.

I want to say I saw it coming. I want to claim the taster's discipline held, that I recognized the trap in the shape of the plate itself — a shallow dish, white as bone, holding nothing but a single candied plum.

But it was a plum from the orchard above the ember-shrines, sugared the way festival plums were sugared, and Sera had been nineteen, and it was the last night of the harvest fire, and she had fed it to me laughing, her fingers sticky, her hair full of sparks that weren't sparks — ember-keepers' hair did that, caught light that wasn't there — and she had said, *Taste it first, Ada. Everything, always. Taste it first and tell me if it's safe to want.*

And I had said yes. Safe. Want it. All of it.

Two months later she was exiled, and I had stood at the border stone and watched her walk out of the only home either of us had, and I had not followed, because duty was the shape of my whole life and I had never learned any other shape, and she had not asked me to follow, which was its own wound, and for twenty years I had told myself the story ended there, cleanly, the way stories end when both people survive them.

The plum tasted like all of that. Every year of it. The wanting and the not-following and the twenty years of telling myself I didn't count the days.

I ate it. God help me, I ate it slowly, and the whole court watched me grieve in real time, and I could not stop, because a taster finishes what he tastes — that is the law, that is the discipline, that is the only thing I am — and they had built the dish so that finishing it was the confession.

When it was done, the Lord Under the Mountain spoke, and for the first time in six nights his voice was not gentle.

"Now we understand you, Ada Dravenmoor. Your king, whom you love without resentment. Your valley, which you love without question. And her." Blue fire flickered along the length of the table like a lit fuse. "Her, whom you love *with* resentment, which is the most expensive love there is. Resentment means you never stopped choosing. Do you see? A man who stopped loving wouldn't resent. He'd simply forget."

At my side, Sera had gone very still. Stillness, in Sera, had always been louder than shouting.

"What is it you want from me?" I asked. "Say it plainly. You've had six nights of my insides served back to me on porcelain. Say what the price is."

The Lord smiled, and this time the smile reached all the way to whatever he kept in place of eyes.

"Soon," he said. "Two nights more. We find that our guests negotiate best when they've been emptied first." He rose, and the entire court rose with him, a forest of ancient bodies unfolding. "Rest well, taster. Tomorrow we serve despair. It pairs beautifully with hope, and you have such reserves of it. We checked."

* * *

I did not go back to my chambers. I walked until the corridors gave up their games and delivered me to the guest wing, past two guards who looked through me as if I were smoke, to the door that was not locked from the inside because it did not need to be.

Wren sat on the windowsill — a window that showed stars no sky had ever made — and when he saw me he held up his hand, palm out, the way he did now instead of speaking, because the contract was eating even his borrowed words.

I crossed the room and took his hand.

He was cold. Not child-cold. Ember-going-out cold. And in his palm, faint as breath on glass, I could see a glow — the smallest possible light, guttering.

He pressed his other hand over mine and looked up at me, and though his voice was nearly gone, I heard him — not in my ears, in the older place, the place that had understood him at the gate — and what he said was:

*Nine nights. Then they take the rest. Don't let them finish the meal.*

Behind me, in the corridor, I heard footsteps — unhurried, ceremonial, many — and Sera's voice, low and urgent, calling my name like a warning shot.
