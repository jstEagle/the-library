## 17. Betrayal at the Seventh Table

The seventh night began with trumpets.

Not the thin silver horns of Vell Hollow's festival days — these were deep-throated things of black iron and bone, and their note went through the floor of the court like a root going through soil. Every noble in the cold-fire hall turned toward the stair that wound down the mountain's throat, and every face wore the same expression: hunger dressed as courtesy.

"They're greeting someone," I said to Sera.

"I know." Her voice was flat as a frozen pond. "I know who."

She wouldn't look at me. That should have been my first warning, and it wasn't, because for six nights I had been learning a new language — the language of dishes — and I had no tongue left over for reading her.

The taster's bench sat at the foot of the dais where the Lord Under the Mountain held his court: a slab of obsidian worn smooth by centuries of elbows. I had eaten there every night since we struck our bargain. Six feasts. Six negotiations conducted entirely in courses. A broth that tasted of obedience until I named it poison. A roast bird stuffed with someone's stolen childhood — not mine, some noble's, served as an appetizer while its former owner wept politely two tables away. Each dish was a question, each verdict I gave was an answer, and somewhere beneath all of it the true negotiation ground on: Wren's soul against whatever price they thought I'd eventually pay.

Wren sat beside me on the bench, small and gray as ash. Six days under this roof had bleached him. The ember-light I'd first seen flickering behind his eyes — that strange warm gold no one else seemed to notice — had dimmed to a coal wrapped in wet cloth. He leaned against my arm because he was too tired to sit straight, and his hand found mine the way a drowning thing finds driftwood.

*Hungry,* he signed in the old language. Then, after a pause, *Don't eat anything that smells like rain.*

I almost smiled. Rain-scented dishes were the court's specialty — nostalgia brewed into sauce. On the fourth night I had wept through an entire course of a childhood that wasn't mine, a mother with flour on her hands who had never existed, and the nobles had watched me grieve with the rapt attention of scholars watching ink spread through water.

*I won't,* I signed back.

Then the guest came down the stairs.

I knew the silhouette before I knew the face. I had seen it leaning over the king's shoulder for eleven years. I had watched it sign death warrants with a fountain pen and smile at children in the market square.

"Chancellor Merek," the Lord Under the Mountain said, rising from his throne of fused glass, and the whole court rose with him. "You honor us twice in one age."

Merek crossed the floor in his traveling cloak, dust of the surface world still on his boots, and when he reached the dais he did not kneel. None of them knelt here; kneeling implied hierarchy between equals, and bargains were the only law. But he bowed his head slightly, the way one bows to weather.

"My lord." His voice carried perfectly in the vaulted dark. "I bring word from above, and an offer from below. I have been authorized to speak for both."

My stomach dropped before my mind caught up. *Both.* Above and below. The chancellor of Vell Hollow, speaking for the mountain court.

He turned then, and found me on the taster's bench, and smiled the same mild smile he gave the palace kitchens when the soup was late.

"Taster Dravenmoor. You look thinner. The air down here disagrees with valley men, I'm told."

"What are you doing here?" My voice came out rougher than I intended. Around us, the court had settled back onto their benches, and now they watched us the way audiences watch a play whose ending everyone knows but the actors.

"Serving my king," Merek said pleasantly. "Though perhaps not the king you're thinking of. I've served the crown beneath the mountain since before you were born, Ada. Your king's illness — the curse-work your taster's tongue couldn't catch? Mine. A necessary kindling. And now I've come to conclude the transaction you've been so stubbornly obstructing."

Beside me, Wren had gone rigid. His small fingers dug into my wrist. *Liar,* he signed, though there was nothing to sign it against but fear.

"The child's soul-contract is valid," Merek continued, addressing the court now, gesturing with the ease of long practice. "Signed in good faith by desperate parents, sealed under mountain law. The court has been patient. The court has been generous. It even permitted this—" he waved a hand at me, at Sera, at the whole absurd embassy of us— "this rescue attempt to play itself out, because patience costs nothing and hope makes better seasoning than despair. But enough. There is a simpler settlement available, and I'm empowered to offer it."

The Lord Under the Mountain settled back onto his glass throne. "Speak it, chancellor."

"Give up the child," Merek said, turning to me fully now, and I saw that his eyes had changed. Somewhere in the last eleven years they had gone from brown to a color like tarnished copper, and I understood that the man I'd known had been a coat worn over something older. "And the court will release you both — the taster and the exile — unharmed, unmolested, free to walk home to your little valley where magic can't follow. The boy stays. The contract completes. Everyone leaves the table satisfied except one small mute soul who was sold before any of you ever loved him."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the contract runs to term, the boy is extinguished, and the valley goes dark forever. Oh—" he raised one finger, savoring it— "and the exile's treason becomes relevant again. Which reminds me."

He looked past me. To Sera.

"There is a second matter. A matter of negotiation conducted in private. Isn't there, Keeper Sera?"

I turned to her too slowly. She had already gone white, whiter than Wren.

"On the third night," Merek said, strolling along the front of the high table like a lecturer, "while the taster was weeping into his soup over a mother he never had, the Lady Sera requested a private audience with the Lord's chamberlain. She asked about terms of reinstatement. She asked what service would be required of her to take up her old office — Ember-Keeper of the Hollow Court — office, I might add, which she abandoned in disgrace twenty years ago rather than do her duty."

"That isn't—" Sera started.

"You *asked*," Merek said, and the mildness fell off his voice like a mask cracking. "You sat in that chamber and you listened to what they wanted, and you didn't say no. Not immediately. Not the way you claim you refused them once before, the refusal you've been polishing into a virtue for two decades. This time you *listened*. You weighed it. And then you came back to this table and said nothing. Night after night. You let him eat for you, bleed for you, and you kept your mouth shut."

The hall was very quiet. Even the cold fire seemed to lean in.

Sera's hands were shaking. She looked at me — finally, directly, for the first time in three days — and everything I needed to see was in her face, and none of it was denial.

"How long?" I asked. My voice sounded far away, like someone calling across a frozen lake. "How long were you going to wait to tell me?"

"I never agreed to anything." The words came out fast, cracked. "Ada, I swear it, I went to find out what they'd offer so we could — so I could know what we were fighting, and then it was two nights later and there hadn't been a right moment, and every hour I waited it got worse to tell you, and—"

"And they offered you your old life back." I heard how flat my own voice had gone. Taster-flat. The voice I used to announce poison. "Your office. Your name cleared. All you had to do was deliver the boy alone."

"I didn't take it."

"No," I said. "You just hid it. Like a flavor you don't want the other diners to notice."

That was cruel. I knew it was cruel the moment it left my mouth, and I watched it land, watched her flinch as if I'd struck her. Twenty years ago she had chosen her duty over me — refused to feed the court's ember-fires and been cast out for it, exiled from everything she was, while I stayed in my safe valley and learned to die by spoonfuls. She had paid more for her integrity than I had ever paid for anything. And I had spent the whole road hating her a little for choosing wrong, or choosing right, I'd never decided which — and now the court was holding up a mirror and showing me that even her virtue had cracks, and I hated them for showing me, and I hated myself more for being grateful to have a reason.

Because that was the truth underneath, wasn't it. Seven feasts hollowed a man out. They had served me my own loves plated in gold leaf — the king's kindness on the fifth night, the valley's safety on the sixth, Sera's face at nineteen on a bed of candied violets — and I understood now that those weren't taunts. They were appraisals. The court was pricing me. Weighing what I loved to calculate what I'd pay. And a man who has been weighed for seven nights starts to feel like nothing but his weight.

"Enough theater," the Lord Under the Mountain said. "Chancellor, present the final instrument."

Merek produced a scroll. Black vellum, silver seal — the mountain's seal, a spiral descending into stone. He carried it down from the dais himself and laid it on the obsidian bench in front of me, unrolled, and the script on it moved gently, breathing, like something asleep.

"A final bargain," he announced to the court, "offered in open session, binding under ancient law. The taster Ada Dravenmoor may purchase the child's freedom with his own soul. One soul, freely given, for one soul released. The contract transfers. The valley's last ember passes to the court intact, and the taster serves here — forever, in whatever capacity amuses my lord — while the exile goes free and the boy walks home under his own name."

The court murmured like wind through a canyon.

I looked at the scroll. The moving letters rearranged themselves as I watched, and I realized they were forming sentences in the old language — Wren's language, the one I somehow knew, the one that had woken in me the dusk a barefoot child knocked at the servants' gate. The bargain was written in words only I could read, in this hall. That was deliberate. Everything here was deliberate.

*One soul, freely given.*

Seven feasts. Seven nights of eating my own memories and grieving invented mothers and naming poisons in a language of dishes. My body was a ledger of damage — thirty years of swallowed deaths, immunity stacked on immunity like sediment — and the feasts had mined through all of it, and what remained felt very light and very tired. A hollowed man. A priced man. And here was a price written plainly: my soul for the boy's. My eternity of serving this glittering cannibal court, and Wren would go free. Sera would go free. The valley might even keep its ember a little longer, delayed, deferred — no. No, the ember passed to the court either way. Only the timing changed. Only who suffered changed.

And God help me, sitting on that bench with Wren's weight gone suddenly from my arm — he had pulled away, staring at the scroll, understanding it faster than I did — some exhausted, hollowed-out corner of me whispered: *just say yes. Say yes and lie down. You've spent your whole life swallowing what kills other people. What's one more course.*

I picked up the silver stylus that lay beside the scroll. I don't remember reaching for it. My hand simply moved, the way a trained hand moves — and that was the horror of it, I realized later. Thirty years of habit, of taking the cup first, of putting my mouth between danger and everyone I served. The court hadn't broken me. They had simply found my oldest reflex and offered it a form.

"Ada." Sera's voice, low, urgent. "Ada, look at me."

I looked at the scroll instead.

"Don't." Wren grabbed the stylus out of my hand — quick, fierce, stronger than his fading frame had any right to be — and hurled it across the floor. It rang against the flagstones and spun to a stop at the foot of the dais. Then he rounded on me, eyes blazing with the last of his ember-gold, and signed so hard his hands shook:

*You promised. First bite is yours. Last word is mine. YOU PROMISED.*

The kitchen rule. Our kitchen rule, ours and no one else's, made in the taster's quarters over bread and borrowed words on our first night: the taster tastes first, always, so the child never has to be afraid of food — and the child gets the final word on whether it's worth eating. A joke. A ritual. A tiny constitution for two.

It wasn't a joke. It had never been a joke.

And Merek saw it. I watched him see it — watched the calculation cross his tarnished-copper eyes, the momentary flicker of a man realizing the trap he'd built had a door in it that he hadn't drawn. Because a bargain freely given must be given *freely*, under mountain law — and Wren had just, publicly, with the whole court as witness, refused on my behalf. The child's consent was part of the transfer. The child had spoken.

"He cannot refuse for you," Merek said quickly, smoothly, to the court. "The instrument binds the taster alone—"

"The instrument binds the *transaction*," said the Lord Under the Mountain, and his voice filled the hall like cold water filling a cellar. "And the transaction includes the child's soul. The child has declined. Openly. Before witnesses." Something moved behind his face that might, in a prettier court, have been called amusement. "Ancient law is inconvenient, chancellor. That is what makes it law."

Merek stood very still. For the first time in eleven years of knowing him, Chancellor Merek of Vell Hollow looked mortal.

And I — hollow, priced, seven-feasts ruined — I did the stupidest and most important thing I have ever done.

I took Sera's hand.

She flinched. She expected anger; she'd earned a version of it, and we both knew we'd be having that reckoning later, in full, with interest. But right then I laced my fingers through hers the way you grip a rope over a drop, and I said, loudly enough for the whole court, in the old language so there could be no mistaking it:

"I choose trust. Blindly. Completely. Whatever she considered, whatever she hid — she came back to this table every night. She fed Wren from her own plate when he couldn't eat. She crossed a border that burns her worse than it burns me. If there's rot in her, I'll taste it when it comes. Until then—" I lifted her hand, our joined hands, before the throne of fused glass— "my verdict stands: not poisoned."

The old formula. The taster's acquittal. It meant nothing in mountain law — I wasn't tasting a dish — and it meant everything, because every noble in that hall understood exactly what I was doing: declaring, under the only law I had ever served, that I found her clean.

Sera made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half sob. "You absolute fool," she whispered in valley-tongue. "They'll use this. Everything you just gave them—"

"Yes," I said. "Probably. Let them."

The Lord Under the Mountain regarded us for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its boredom, which frightened me far more than any rage could have.

"Seven feasts," he said, "and still the taster refuses to be priced. How tiresome. And how instructive." He rose from the glass throne, and the entire hall rose with him, and the cold fire guttered once, all together, like a held breath. "Very well. Two nights remain. Let the eighth and ninth dishes be prepared." His gaze slid to Wren, to the coal-dim gold in the boy's eyes, and lingered there with an appetite that made my skin crawl. "The kitchen should know the child does not have long. Neither, I think, does our arrangement."

The court bowed as one and began to file out through doors I hadn't known existed, and the great hall emptied around us like a tide going out.

Merek paused at the base of the dais on his way to whatever shadow he'd come from. He looked at me, and for a heartbeat the chancellor's mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was tired — old, and tired, and almost sad.

"You could have ended it tonight, Ada," he said quietly. "For yourself, I mean. Some men would have."

"Some men," I said, "were never taught to eat last."

Something flickered across his face at that — recognition, maybe, or memory — and then it was gone, and the mask was back, and he climbed the stairs toward the surface world carrying his two kingdoms with him.

Sera's hand was still in mine. Wren had collapsed against my side, spent, his breath coming shallow and fast. Above us the cold fire steadied. Somewhere deep in the mountain, I heard the kitchens begin to work.

Two nights. The eighth and ninth dishes.

"Wren," I said softly in the old language. "Can you tell me what the ninth dish is?"

He didn't answer right away. When he did, he didn't use his hands. He pressed his palm flat against my chest, over my heart, and looked up at me with eyes that were dimming like a lamp running out of oil, and signed one word against my skin:

*Ending.*
