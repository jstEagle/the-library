## 18. The Last Two Nights

### Ada

The eighth night's dish arrived on a plate of black glass, and it smelled like nothing at all.

That was the first warning. Every dish the court had served him had a scent — honey and grave-dirt, smoke and mother's milk, the green smell of a valley in spring. This one smelled like the space after a held breath. Ada looked at it for a long moment before he lifted the spoon.

*Despair,* Sera had told him that afternoon, her voice flat in the corridor of cold fire. *They'll serve you despair next. It's the second-to-last course in every bargain. They want you hollow before they ask for the final price.*

He ate anyway. That was the work. That had always been the work.

The taste was warm water and then — nothing, and then the nothing spread. It did not hurt. That was the cruelty of it. It simply reached into him and turned down every lamp, one by one, until he sat at the long table beneath the mountain with his hands in his lap and could not remember why hands were for.

Around him the court dined and glittered, nobles wearing their centuries like jewelry, their laughter arriving a half-second after their mouths moved, as if even their joy had to be translated. The Lord Under the Mountain watched from the high seat with the patient face of a man who had never once needed to hurry.

*He's measuring the depth of you,* Wren had signed to him, two nights ago, in the borrowed language of the kitchens. *How much of you is left to take.*

Ada put down the spoon. His arm felt like it belonged to a statue of himself, displayed somewhere far away.

*The taster finds the eighth dish acceptable,* said the herald, and the words rang through the vaulted dark like a bell dropped down a well.

And that — that single sentence, spoken because it was his office to speak it — was the coal that refused to go out. *Acceptable.* The word moved through him like warmth through frozen fingers. He had said it. The court's own law had taken it in, swallowed it, been bound by it. A taster's approval was sovereign here. Sera had found that thread three nights ago, buried in the court's oldest statutes, and they had been winding it around their fists ever since.

He could be emptied. He could be served his own childhood on a plate and weep for a mother he'd never had. But his tongue still answered to the crown of Vell Hollow, and through it — to something older than the crown, older than the valley, older, perhaps, than the court that had bought a child's soul and thought the transaction finished.

The despair thinned as he walked back to his cell, the way fog thins when the sun insists. By the time he reached the door he could feel his own name again, like a stone at the bottom of a river he was standing in.

Wren was waiting. The boy looked worse than the night before — paler, translucent at the edges, as if the contract were eating him from the outside in. His eyes were still bright. That was the terrible thing. They were bright the way embers are bright.

Ada sat down beside him on the narrow stone shelf that served as a bed and did not say anything, because there was nothing left to say that the walls wouldn't carry to the Lord's ears. The court listened. It had always listened.

Wren took his hand.

And the language came back.

It came all at once, the way a valley floods — not the kitchen words, not the fragments they'd built together over weeks of nights, but the whole of it, deep and old, a tongue that predated the mountain and the valley both, that had been spoken before there were kingdoms to be older than. Ada gasped. It was as if a door in his chest that he'd never known was there had swung open, and behind it was a room he had lived in all his life without entering.

*You gave it to me,* he said in it, and heard the language shape itself in his own mouth like water finding its old channel. *Why?*

*Because you'll need it tomorrow,* Wren said. His voice in the old tongue was not a child's voice. It was the sound a hearth makes when it catches. *Because tomorrow they will try to make you say words you don't mean, in a language you don't know, and you have to know both.*

*What happens tomorrow?*

Wren smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing in the mountain.

*The last dish,* he said. *And the first.*

### Sera

Sera had sold her mother twice now — once to the innkeeper with the memory-scale, and once, in a sense, to the court, when she'd traded the truth of her temptation for Ada's trust. Both debts were coming due. She could feel them accruing interest in her chest.

She spent the eighth night walking the corridors of cold fire, doing what she had told Ada she was doing — negotiating. It was even mostly true. What she was actually doing was counting guards.

The court's law was its spine, and its spine was its rituals. She had learned that as a girl in the ember-temples, before the exile, before the choice that had unmade her life: *the court cannot break its own word, but it can wait a thousand years for you to break yours.* Everything here was contract. Everything here was consent, extracted so smoothly the extracted rarely noticed they'd given it. That was why the taster's office terrified them, down in the marrow of their etiquette. A taster's assent bound the court. A taster's refusal — a formal declaration of poison, spoken in the old forms — did not merely refuse a dish. It *convicted* it. It made the court a poisoner in its own hall, before its own law, and there was no century old enough to survive that.

They knew it. Of course they knew it. That was why they'd fed him eight nights of erosion first — forgetting, obedience, false homesickness, his own memories plated in gold. They were not trying to kill his body. They were trying to kill his *office*, the sovereign thing inside him, until when the ninth dish came he would be too hollow to speak the words that mattered.

Sera pressed her palm against the warm-cold wall of the corridor and closed her eyes.

*You could still take the offer,* the Lord had said to her, an hour ago, in a voice like a cellar door opening. *Reinstatement. Your temples relit. Your name restored to the rolls of the keepers. All you must do is bring me the child alone, unaccompanied, and the man goes home whole. He'd never know. He'd thank you.*

She had said no. She had said it faster this time than the first time, years ago, when refusing had cost her everything — and she had noticed, with a shame that would not scab over, that the speed was not the same as the absence of wanting. She had wanted it. She wanted it still. The temples of her childhood lived behind her eyelids every time she blinked.

But she had learned something in exile that the court, with all its centuries, had never learned: that a want you refuse is not the same as a want you serve.

She found Ada in his cell, awake, sitting beside the fading boy with his hand in Wren's, and she saw at once that something had changed — the set of his shoulders, the stillness of him. Not the stillness of despair. The stillness of a drawn bow.

*It gave you the language,* she said.

*The boy gave it to me.* Ada looked up, and his eyes in the cold firelight were the eyes of the man she'd loved at nineteen and the man she'd found at the border stone and a third man she was only now meeting. *Sera. Sit down. We're done negotiating.*

### Ada

They conspired in the old tongue, which the court could hear — the court heard everything — but could not, apparently, *read*. That was Wren's gift and Wren's joke: the language older than the mountain was older than the mountain's permission. The nobles heard the sounds and felt the shape of meaning and could not reach the meaning itself, like cats watching hands write.

*The ninth dish will be the ending,* Sera said. She had her arms around her knees, sitting across from them, and her voice in the deep language was low and even and afraid in exactly one place, which she kept trying to smooth and could not. *They won't serve it to you. They'll serve it to him. A dish that ends a soul — and they'll require your formal assent before the first spoonful touches his lips. That's the ritual. That's the law they're bound by. They cannot feed the ending to a contracted soul without the taster's word.*

*And if I refuse?*

*Then the contract stands unresolved, and they keep him, and they wait. They are so good at waiting, Ada. That's the trap. Refusal isn't enough. Refusal just means forever.*

Ada turned Wren's hand over in his own. The boy's palm was cool, and faintly, at the center, it glowed — a light like the last coal in a banked fire, the light Sera had once described to him in a letter he'd never answered, the light of the thing the valley had been founded on and had spent three hundred years pretending not to remember.

*Then I don't refuse,* he said.

Sera went very still. *Ada—*

*I declare it poisoned.*

The words came out of him in the old tongue and he felt the mountain flinch — actually flinch, a shiver through the stone, the court's listening attention snapping taut like a rope.

*You can't,* Sera whispered. *You haven't tasted it. The forms require—*

*The forms require that I taste what is served to the one under contract,* Ada said. His heart was hammering and his voice did not hammer at all; twenty years of swallowing death in small doses had taught him to keep his voice level over anything. *The ninth dish will be served to Wren. But I am his taster. I have been his taster since the first night at the servants' gate, when he would not eat until I had tasted first. Every meal since. Every single one. Sera — I have tasted for that boy every day he's been in this mountain, and the court has watched me do it, and by its own law, its own ritual, its own insufferable etiquette — my tongue covers him. It has covered him all along.*

The silence in the cell was long. Somewhere far above them, water dripped in the mountain's throat, patient as the court itself.

*The declaration of poison,* Sera said slowly, *must be made at the table. Before the dish is tasted or refused. Spoken in the old forms. In the old tongue.* She looked at Wren, and Wren looked at Ada, and the ember-light in the boy's palm brightened like a breath drawn.

*And if the court protests,* Wren said, in a voice that was not a child's, *it will be protesting its own law, in its own hall, before its own nobles — every one of whom has used that law against some rival for a thousand years. They will not let the Lord break it. They cannot. It is the only wall any of them have ever hidden behind.*

*It's thin,* Sera said. *The plan is threadbare. If they serve you the ninth dish instead of him — if they find one procedural crack — if the Lord decides a thousand years of law is worth less than one ember—*

*Then we lose,* Ada said. *But we lose saying true words. I've spent my whole life saying the safe ones.* He looked at his hands, the hands that had lifted ten thousand spoons, that had caught death so often it had become a habit, a craft, a quiet pride — and had never once, until this child, been pointed at something he loved. *I don't want to be safe anymore. I want to be his.*

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and furious and certain, all three at once, and Ada thought: *there she is. There's the woman from the ember-temples. There's the woman I never stopped—*

*Then we do it tomorrow,* she said. *Ninth night. And Ada — whatever they serve, whatever they put in front of you, however hollow they've made you feel — you hold the forms. The declaration has six lines. Say them with me until you can say them asleep.*

They said them until the cold fire in the walls burned low toward the mountain's long false morning. Six lines in a language older than kingdoms, and by the last repetition all three of them were saying it together, Wren's fading voice threaded through theirs like a bright wire, and the mountain listened and understood nothing, and for the first time since Ada had walked through the border stone and drowned in the world's returning magic, that felt like mercy.

* * *

### Sera

The ninth night came dressed in its best.

The hall had been changed. The long tables were pushed back; a single table stood at the center of the floor of cold fire, set for two — a taster and the one he tasted for. The nobles lined the walls in silence, their centuries hanging off them like borrowed robes, and Sera, walking beside Ada down the aisle their stillness made, understood that they were not silent out of ceremony.

They were silent the way a courtroom is silent.

The dish sat waiting under a cover of hammered silver, and even before the cover was lifted Sera could feel it — a wrongness like a held note below hearing, the smell of a door closing forever. The Lord Under the Mountain rose from his high seat, and his face, for the first time in all the nights she had known him, was not patient.

*The court is pleased,* he said, *to conclude the bargain. The dish of ending, prepared for the contracted soul. Taster — you will give your assent, and the debt of the valley will be paid in full, and your service honored, and your man's body returned to your green country whole. Speak the words.*

Ada stopped at the table. Sera took her place behind his left shoulder, where a taster's second stands, where she had stood in the temples a lifetime ago, and she watched him lift the silver cover, and she watched what lay beneath — a spoonful of darkness so absolute it made the cold fire around it look warm — and she watched her whole future, and Wren's, and the valley's, gather itself into the six lines of an old declaration.

Ada looked at the dish for a long moment. Then he looked at Wren, small and translucent in his chair, ember-bright at the center of his fading chest.

And he set down the cover, and straightened to his full height, and said, in the language older than the mountain, in the forms no one had spoken in that hall for a thousand years:

*"I am the taster. My tongue is the crown's, and the crown's is older. I have tasted for this soul since the first gate, and my word covers him as the roof covers the hearth."*

The cold fire guttered. Every noble in the hall leaned forward.

*"And this dish,"* Ada said, and his voice did not shake, it *rang*, *"is poisoned."*
