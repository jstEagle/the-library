## 20. Burning to Give Light

**Sera**

The contract broke like a dam, and the water was fire.

Sera felt it before she saw it — a sound below hearing, a snap in the bones of the world, the way a rope parts a half-second before you understand the crack you heard. The Lord Under the Mountain rose from his throne of cold fire, and his court rose with him, and for one heartbeat the whole vast hall was silent as a held breath.

Then the ember woke.

It had been sleeping in Wren for years — she understood that now, the way you understand a thing too large to hold all at once, in pieces, in gasps. The boy stood in the center of the shattered ninth table, dishes of ending sliding in glittering ruin around his bare feet, and light came out of him the way heat comes out of a forge door opened. Not gentle. Not the soft gold of the valley's hearths. This was the first fire, the founding fire, the ember that had been carried down into Vell Hollow by the first families and buried in its soil so the valley could be a place magic could not touch — a sanctuary built from a sacrifice.

The court screamed. Not the nobles — they were too old for screaming, too practiced; they simply began to *move*, centuries of bargains unraveling around them as the taster's word worked its way down through the law like frost through stone. It was the mountain itself that screamed, a groan in the deep rock, the sound of a structure losing the argument with its own foundation.

"Wren." Sera pushed through the chaos, Ada at her shoulder. The boy turned to them and his eyes were not a child's eyes anymore. They were coals. They were dawn. They were terrible and beautiful and they were going out.

"Ember-keeper." His voice was not a child's voice either. It was old, older than kingdoms, older than the valley — the voice of the thing that had made the valley. "The contract is broken. The purchase is void. But what was bought cannot simply be unmade. The fire must go somewhere."

"Back to the valley," Ada said. It wasn't a question. Sera heard in his voice the same thing she felt in her chest: the valley's soil, the springs, the bread ovens, the border stone humming in the dark. *Home.*

"The valley will take me," the ember said through Wren's mouth. "But the valley is far, and I am small, and this body—" The light flickered, and for a moment Wren was only Wren again, a barefoot boy swaying with exhaustion, his lips gray. "This body cannot cross the border. It was never meant to hold me. I was meant to be poured out, and there is no one here to pour."

Sera's hands were already moving. Ember-keeper's hands, trained since childhood in the tending of fires too old for ordinary tending. She knew the answer before the ember finished speaking. She had known it in the border stone's shudder, in the inn keeper's knowing eyes, in every step of the road that had brought them here. There was only ever one vessel in this story fit to carry a fire that burned through poison and curse and nine nights of the court's cruelest cooking.

A man who had spent his whole life swallowing what should have killed him.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle.

Ada looked at her. And she saw that he knew too.

* * *

**Ada**

Ada had tasted death so many times it had become a kind of language, and he was fluent now. He knew the bitter almond of the mountain court's despair-dish still coating his throat. He knew the taste of the ninth night, of the dish meant to unmake Wren, of his own voice saying *poisoned* and the world breaking open around the word.

And now he tasted something new: the ember, reaching for him.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the strange part. After nine nights of ceremony and cold fire and bargains dressed as dinner, the truest thing in the mountain court happened quietly. Wren took his hand — a small hand, chapped from the road, a child's hand — and pressed it flat against Ada's chest, over the heart, and said in the old language, the one Ada now understood as easily as his own name:

*You were built to hold what burns. I am sorry. Thank you.*

"Sera," Ada said. "I need you to listen to me, and I need you to not argue, because we don't have time and because you'll lose."

"I will *not*—"

"Sera." He said her name the way he used to say it, nineteen years ago, at the border stone, before the exile, before everything. Softly. Like something worth setting down carefully. "My whole life, I ate poison so the king wouldn't have to. Every dish, every dose. My body is a ledger of everything that should have ended me, and it never did. I am the only person alive who can carry this fire through the mountain and out the other side. You know it's true."

"I know it's true," she said, and her eyes were wet and furious. "That's what makes it unforgivable."

The court was collapsing around them. The Lord Under the Mountain had not moved — he stood on his dais watching them with an expression Ada could not read, and that frightened him more than the screaming stone. The nobles were fleeing down the side galleries, their centuries slipping off them like jewelry in a flood, and beneath the galleries Ada could see the mountain's throat yawning open, the way down, the long road home.

"The ember will burn me out," he said. "I know. Maybe all the way. Maybe not. But Wren dies here if I don't, and the valley goes dark forever, and I — Sera, I spent forty years not wanting anything enough to risk it. I'm not doing that anymore. I want *this*. I want him to grow up. I want bread ovens and springs and a valley that remembers what it was. I want—" His voice failed and he laughed at it. "I want to come home. That's the whole point. You come home. That's what home means."

She grabbed the front of his shirt with both hands and pressed her forehead to his, and for three seconds — he counted them, a taster's precision — neither of them said anything at all.

"If you burn out," she whispered, "I will find whatever is left of you and I will feed it to the border stone myself."

"Romantic," Ada said.

The ember moved.

* * *

**Sera**

It did not hurt him, at first. That was her one mercy, and she clung to it.

The light left Wren the way water leaves a cup — a tilting, a pouring, the boy folding into Ada's arms like a coat returned, and then Ada was standing alone in the ruin of the ninth table with his arms full of nothing and his chest full of dawn. The light under his skin was visible even through his clothes, a gold seam down his sternum, a hearth-door in a man's body.

Wren lay on the broken table, breathing, asleep, ordinary. A child. Just a child, for the first time in years, with no fire in him and no contract on him and no language older than kingdoms — and Sera thought, absurdly, *he'll need to learn to talk all over again, our words this time,* and then she was crying, and there was no time for it.

"Go," she said. "Now. While the court is still breaking."

Ada picked Wren up. The boy weighed almost nothing, and the light in Ada's chest made his arms look like branches around a lantern. They ran.

The mountain's throat was collapsing as they descended — Sera felt it in the walls, in the stair that had been a stair on the way down and was now something more like a gullet, contracting, the court's dying law dragging its architecture with it. Cold fire guttered in the sconces. Somewhere above them the Lord Under the Mountain finally spoke, one word, in a voice like a door closing:

"*Run.*"

It was not a threat. That was the horror of it. It was almost, terribly, a blessing.

* * *

**Ada**

The ambush came at the third turning, where the mountain's throat narrowed to a passage wide enough for two.

Merek stood in it.

The chancellor of Vell Hollow was not dressed for riding anymore. He was dressed for court — mountain court — in robes the color of deep water, and the years he had hidden behind a chancellor's stoop were gone from him; he stood straight, and his eyes were cold fire, and behind him in the dark Ada could hear the wet breathing of things the riders had been all along, things that had worn Merek's livery the way the court wore centuries.

"The contract is void," Merek said. His voice was the same voice that had accused a child in the king's hall, patient and reasonable. "But a voided contract leaves the *goods* unclaimed. And unclaimed goods, taster, revert to the last party with a legal interest." He smiled. It was the worst thing Ada had seen in the mountain, worse than the despair-dish, because it was a valley smile, a palace smile, the smile of the man who had signed the king's letters. "You are carrying stolen property. Give me the boy."

"He's not property."

"Everything is property here. That is the entire law." Merek stepped forward, and the things behind him stepped with him. "You are one man, poisoned half to death by nine feasts, carrying a fire that is eating you alive. I have an army of the court's leavings and a claim older than your valley. Set him down."

Ada set his feet. The ember turned over in his chest, and he felt it — felt it *taste* the air the way his tongue tasted porridge, felt it find the curse-work in Merek's riders, the mountain's law knotted through them like gristle.

*They are poison,* the ember said, in the old language, inside his skull, gentle as a kitchen word. *You know what to do with poison.*

Ada almost laughed.

Forty years. Forty years of small doses, of swallowed deaths, of a body built dish by dish into a living shield — and every one of those poisons was still in him, sunk into his blood, layered in his marrow, the ledger of everything that should have killed him. He had thought of it as damage. He had thought of it as the cost of the work. He had never once thought of it as *inventory*.

The ember opened the ledger.

It didn't hurt. That was the miracle of it, the thing he would try to describe for the rest of his life if there was a rest of his life: it felt like being *read*. Like the finest tongue in the valley finally tasting the taster. And then the ledger balanced itself — every swallowed death pouring out of him through his palms, his breath, the gold seam in his chest, into the air of the passage.

The riders came apart like sugar in water. There was no battle. There was a man standing in a collapsing throat of stone with his arms full of a sleeping child, and the poison of forty years leaving him in a slow golden exhalation, and everything it touched dissolving — curse-work, binding-spells, the mountain's claim knotted through Merek's servants, all of it unwritten, unmade, *refused*.

Merek alone remained, because Merek was mostly man still, mostly valley, mostly the chancellor who had once signed the king's letters with a steady hand. He stood in the ruin of his ambush with the cold fire dying in his eyes, and he looked at Ada with something that was almost recognition.

"You were never supposed to be able to do that," he said.

"No," Ada agreed. "Neither were you supposed to be able to do any of this. We're both full of things that shouldn't have been possible." He shifted Wren higher against his shoulder. "Go home, Merek. The king is waking up. I'd want to be there when he asks why."

Merek looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned and walked away into the dark, and the dark let him go, because even the mountain's law had no further claim on a man whose bargains had all just burned.

* * *

**Sera**

The last stretch of the mountain's throat was falling down around them, and Sera ran it with Ada's hand locked in hers and Wren bouncing against his chest, and she did not think about the ember burning in him or the years it might be taking. She thought about the border stone. She thought about the road that remembered. She thought about an inn where you could sell a memory of your mother, and how she had kept the one memory she would never sell — Ada at nineteen, at the border stone, laughing — and how she had carried it through exile and court and nine nights of the cruelest negotiations in the world, and how it had turned out to be not a memory at all but a *plan*.

The light was coming out of him now with every step. She could see it through his back, through his shirt, a hearth-glow in a man's shape, and the mountain's falling stone lit up gold around them like a cathedral coming apart in sunrise.

"Ada." She had to shout over the groan of the rock. "How much is left?"

"Enough," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got." He grinned at her over Wren's sleeping head, and his teeth were gold-lit, and he was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and she was going to kill him if he died. "Sera. Hey. Look at me. Forty years I swallowed everything and wanted nothing. Now I'm full of the first fire and I want *everything* — the valley, the kitchen, the boy learning our words, you burning my breakfast because you never could cook — I want all of it, and wanting is the heaviest thing I've ever carried, heavier than the fire." The passage bent. Daylight ahead — real daylight, gray and ordinary and holy. "Wanting might be what keeps it lit. Or what keeps *me* lit. I don't know. But I'm not putting it down."

The mountain's mouth opened. The border stone stood in the gray morning light, humming, waiting, the way it had waited for a thousand years for someone to come home carrying the thing the valley had buried in itself so long ago it had become a rule.

Sera did not let go of his hand.

* * *

**Ada**

The border stone did not feel like drowning this time.

It felt like the opposite of drowning. It felt like the first breath after a lifetime underwater that he hadn't known he'd been holding — and the ember in his chest surged toward the valley beyond the stone like a river finding the sea, and Ada let it, because that had been the plan, that had always been the plan, the fire back to the soil, the springs, the bread ovens, the valley that remembered.

But the ember paused at the threshold of him.

*You could keep me,* it said, in the old language, kind as a kitchen word. *A little longer. A year. A life. You would burn, but slowly, and you would see him grow.*

Ada stood on the valley side of the border stone with the fire in his chest and the child in his arms and Sera's hand in his, and behind him the mountain was settling into its long collapse, and ahead of him the valley lay green and gray and utterly ordinary in the morning light, and he understood, finally, the whole shape of the choice, the one he'd been tasting in small doses his entire life.

"No," he said. "Take everything. I want to be here when it grows."

The ember poured out of him.

It went down into the soil like water into roots, and the border stone rang like a bell, and every spring in Vell Hollow ran warm for one full minute, and every bread oven in the valley lit itself without being asked, and Ada Dravenmoor — empty, hollowed, lighter than he had ever been, lighter than a man with nothing left to swallow — folded to his knees in the wet grass with Wren still breathing against his chest and Sera's arms coming around them both.

The valley was waking up. He could feel it under his palms, under his knees, a hum in the ground, a green and growing attention, the founding fire settling home into the place it had been carried from.

And far behind them, down the road from the palace, small and distant and unmistakable, came the sound of the king's bells — ringing, though no one had pulled the ropes, ringing the way bells ring when the thing they were made to announce has finally, finally come true.

Sera was laughing and crying and holding on. Wren stirred, opened his eyes — ordinary eyes, a child's eyes, seeing the valley for the first time — and said, in a small, rusty, brand-new voice, his first word in their language:

"Home."

Ada held them both and let the bells ring.

Then, over Sera's shoulder, at the edge of the road, he saw the riders coming — not Merek's, not the court's. Palace livery. The king's own guard, riding hard, with a banner Ada had never seen carried in his lifetime: the old banner, the founding banner, with the ember on it.

They had seen the springs run warm. The whole valley had seen.

And the king was coming to the border stone himself.
