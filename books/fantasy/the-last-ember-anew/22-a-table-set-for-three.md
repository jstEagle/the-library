## 22. A Table Set for Three

The king did not want to accept my resignation.

He said it the way he said most things — quietly, in a room where no one else was permitted, over bread I no longer tasted for him. He was thinner since the sickness, gray at the temples where he had been gold, but his eyes had changed more than his body. A man who has been killed and un-killed looks at the world differently. He looked at me now as if I were one of the few fixed points left in a palace that had betrayed him from its own cellars.

"You saved my life twice," he said. "Once by swallowing what should have ended me. Once by walking into a mountain to give it back."

"Only the second one counts," I said.

"Both count." He turned the loaf on its board, not eating. Old habit, that — waiting for the taster's nod before touching anything. It would take him months to stop doing it. It would take me longer. "Ada Dravenmoor. You have eaten poison for this crown for twenty years. Ask me for anything and it is yours."

I looked at the bread. Warm, honest bread, baked in the palace ovens that now glowed faintly at their seams, the way all fire in Vell Hollow had begun to glow since the night an ember came home through me and spilled itself into the valley's soil. The bakers swore the loaves rose better than they ever had. They were right. Everything here grew better. That was the point of what we'd done.

"I want to leave," I said. "Not the valley. Just the palace. There is a kitchen at the valley's edge, by the border stone, and there are two people who belong in it with me."

The king was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed — short and startled, like a man remembering how.

"A taster who opens a tavern," he said. "What will you serve? Nothing in that kitchen will be safe."

"Everything in that kitchen will be true," I said. "It turns out that's better."

He signed the papers himself, which no king should do for a servant, but then, I had never quite been a servant. On the last line, beneath his seal, he wrote something extra in his own hand: *Debtor to Ada Dravenmoor, forever.* Merek would have called it sentimental. Merek was in the deep cells now, awaiting trial, still insisting to anyone who would listen that he had only ever wanted to protect the valley from what lived under the mountain. In a way, he had. He'd just decided the price was a child's soul, and some prices cannot be paid without buying ruin.

I carried the resignation out through the palace gates at dawn, past the kitchen doors where the cooks pressed warm rolls into my hands without being asked, past the servants' gate where a barefoot boy had once stood speaking a language older than kingdoms. The stones remembered him. I think the whole valley remembered him. It simply hadn't known, until now, what it was remembering.

* * *

The kitchen sat a half-mile from the border stone, close enough that on quiet evenings you could hear it hum — a low, patient note, like a struck bowl that had decided never to fall silent. We built nothing new. The old waystation had stood empty for a hundred years, back when travelers still came up the mountain road and stopped to rest before crossing into a valley where magic could not follow them. Now they came for the opposite reason. Now they crossed *out* of the mountain country and stopped at our door because the ember-light reached this far and made the hearth burn the color of late summer.

Sera found the building the way she found everything — by listening. She laid her palm against the doorframe and closed her eyes, and whatever the wood told her made her smile in a way I had spent twenty years forgetting and three seasons relearning.

"It wants to be used," she said. "Places forget themselves when nobody needs them."

"So do people," I said.

She looked at me then, ember-keeper's eyes catching the light, and didn't argue. We had done enough arguing on the road to last us. Somewhere between the inn of small mercies and the seventh table, between her hidden refusal and my blind, stubborn trust, we had burned through everything bitter between us and found what was underneath, which turned out to be the same thing that had been there when she was nineteen and I was a fool with a numb tongue and a hollow chest: a wanting neither of us had ever been brave enough to name.

We named it now, mostly by hammering nails.

She rebuilt the hearth herself, coaxing the chimney flue with small, precise gestures until the smoke drew clean and the fire settled into that soft amber glow that wasn't quite ordinary flame anymore. I built the tables. Seven of them, rough pine, because Wren insisted on seven and refused, with the full stubbornness of a child who had once been the founding fire of an entire valley, to explain why. We set them out and counted the chairs and discovered there were always exactly enough, no matter how many came through the door. Sera said the room was doing it. I said the room had learned it from watching Wren set tables. Neither of us knew. Both of us had stopped needing to know.

That was the strangest gift of what I'd spent — or rather, of what I'd *become*. For twenty years I had known things by tasting them. Bitter almond under honey. Wolfsbane under wine. My tongue had been a fortress wall, and behind it I had lived carefully, safely, tasting everything and wanting nothing. Now the immunity was gone, burned away carrying an ember home through a collapsing court, and my mouth was just a mouth, ordinary, vulnerable, capable of being hurt. And somehow, with the fortress down, I could finally feel everything else. The warmth of dough rising under a cloth. The ache in my shoulders after a day of honest work. Sera's hand finding mine across a table at the end of the night, when the lamps guttered low and the border stone hummed its long note into the dark.

Wanting, it turned out, was the taste I had been missing all along.

* * *

Wren ran the dining room the way a conductor runs an orchestra he composed himself.

He was eight years old, or he had chosen to be eight, or perhaps eight was simply the shape his restored soul preferred — none of us asked, because some questions are doors best left shut. What mattered was that he laughed now. Loud, sudden, graceless laughter that scattered sparrows off the roof. The mute child from the gate spoke constantly, though half of what he spoke was in the old language, the one older than kingdoms, the one that had poured fully into me when he pressed his palm to mine in the dark beneath the mountain.

Some of those words had no translation. We kept a slate by the hearth where he wrote one each week, and travelers from both sides of the border stone would stand before it and try, and fail, and leave delighted.

*Aeth.* The warmth of bread when you hold the whole loaf instead of a slice.

*Sennet-vel.* The specific silence between two people who have forgiven each other.

*Oru.* What a house feels when everyone it loves is inside it.

"Those aren't words," a merchant from the mountain country protested once, a broad man with cold-fire jewelry still hanging at his ears. "Those are entire lives."

"Yes," Wren said serenely, clearing his plates. "Most words are. Yours are just too small."

The merchant stared, and then paid double for his supper, and asked if he could come back next week to learn another one.

The mountain folk came more often now. That was the change no one in the valley had predicted, and some still muttered about — that the court beneath the mountain, broken of its Lord and its bargains, sent its people up into the light to trade and eat and sit awkwardly at our seven tables wearing centuries like inherited coats. Nobles whose names had been law for a thousand years now haggled over the price of pie. Some of them wept into their cups, quietly, at the taste of food brewed with no intent except to nourish. Sera served them without hatred. She had earned the right to hate; she chose the harder thing instead. Ember-keepers, she told me once, keep embers. Grudges go out on their own.

And the valley folk came too — farmers first, cautiously, then the curious, then nearly everyone, drawn to a kitchen where the bread glowed faintly at the crust and the well water tasted faintly of mornings. Magic had returned to Vell Hollow gently, the way spring returns, seeping into soil and springs and oven-stones, and the valley that had defined itself by refusing magic for a thousand years was learning, slowly, awkwardly, joyfully, to hold it. Children chased lights through the orchards. The springs ran sweet. And at the edge of it all, half a mile from the humming stone, our little kitchen stood as a kind of treaty house — the place where both worlds sat down and ate.

I still tasted every dish before it went out. Habit, Sera called it. Prayer, I called it. Because the first bite of every morning — the porridge, the bread, the stew — I took deliberately, fully, tasting not for poison but for everything else, and giving thanks that I could. A taster's tongue, emptied of duty, filled with appetite. It felt like the truest use I had ever put it to.

* * *

On the anniversary of the night Wren crossed the border — we marked it without ceremony, the way family marks things — the three of us closed early and walked up to the stone together.

It hummed as always, but softer in the evening, and the grass around it grew in a slow spiral that no shepherd would trample. Wren ran ahead, barefoot even in autumn, because shoes remained a negotiation he intended to lose for the rest of his childhood, and pressed both hands flat against the stone the way he did sometimes, listening to whatever the stone said to the boy who had once been its fire.

"What does it say?" Sera asked, settling beside me on the warm grass.

"It says thank you," Wren reported gravely. Then, with the merciless honesty of children: "It says it mostly to Ada. It says it to you too, but Ada it says *aeth* about."

"Aeth," I repeated. Bread-warmth. Whole-loaf warmth.

"He can't help it," Sera murmured. "You're very aeth."

I laughed, and the sound surprised me still, after all these months — big and unguarded, a sound the man who had swallowed death by spoonfuls in a palace kitchen would never have believed he owned. Twenty years I had thought safety was a wall you built around yourself, dish by dish, dose by dose. I had been the king's shield and my own prison, competent and empty, immune to everything except the one thing worth feeling.

Now I sat on cold grass in a valley full of returned miracles, next to a woman I had lost twice and found once and meant to keep, while a child who had been sold and bought and burned and reborn taught a border stone to say thank you — and I understood at last what the whole long road had been teaching. Belonging isn't granted. Not by kings, not by courts, not by the ancient laws that bind a taster's word. You earn it the way you earn bread: with your hands, in heat, daily, imperfectly, and you share it or it goes stale.

The sun went down behind the mountain that no longer ruled us. The stone hummed. Far below, our kitchen windows glowed amber into the dusk, and the fire inside them held — steady, patient, ordinary — the last ember of the valley's founding magic, burning in the bread and the water and the lamplight, in every meal set for strangers, in every word with no translation written on a slate by the door.

A table set for three. It was never large enough, in the end. Every night it became a table for ten, for thirty, for whoever knocked.

Every night, there was always exactly enough.

And when the last guests wandered out into the starlight and Wren slept with his head on Sera's shoulder and the border stone sang its long low note to the dark, I would stand in the doorway a moment, tasting the air — bread, woodsmoke, rain coming, home — and give thanks the only way a taster knows how.

One bite of everything.

Nothing wasted. Nothing feared.

All of it, at last, mine.
