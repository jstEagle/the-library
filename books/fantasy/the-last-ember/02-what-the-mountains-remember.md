## 2. What the Mountains Remember

The map did not burn.

That was the first wrong thing about it. Quentin had held enough stolen documents to know that paper carried secrets badly—ink could be soaked off, vellum singed, wax seals melted down and repressed in an hour by any competent forger in Thornfell's lower markets. The surest way to kill a secret was fire, and every smuggler worth his salt had a brazier within arm's reach.

But when he crouched over the cold pit of last night's cookfire with the courier's bloodstained map spread across his knees, the flame from his tinder-spark bent *around* it. Curved away like water around a stone in a stream. He tried again, holding the spark closer, close enough to feel it kiss his knuckles, and the little tongue of fire leaned sideways, almost politely, as if declining an invitation.

Quentin sat back on his heels in the mouth of the cave and stared at it.

The cave was one of his—a hollow behind a curtain of frozen waterfall three days north of the pass where the courier had died, deep enough that the soldiers who'd come through burning everything behind them would never think to look. He'd watched the smoke rise over the col at dawn, a dirty gray smudge against the pink sky, and heard the faint crackle of the pine forest giving way. They had burned the trail, the waystation, the dead man's horse. Everything that said *someone passed here*. Everything except Quentin, who had been two switchbacks higher when the horns sounded.

And everything except the map.

It was good stock, heavy and fibrous, the kind of paper made in southern mills that charged a fortune per sheet. But it wasn't the paper that refused to burn. It was something in the ink—the lines of it, fine as spider silk, drawn in a color that wasn't quite black and wasn't quite brown but something older, the color of things pulled out of peat bogs. Roads wound across it in that ink. Mountain ranges rendered in hatched strokes. Rivers like veins.

And names. Names in no alphabet Quentin knew—curved, flowing characters that seemed almost to move when he wasn't looking directly at them, the way stars do at the edge of vision.

He had spent the day telling himself it was nothing. A foreign survey chart, maybe, from across the Weeping Steppe, from some kingdom whose script he simply hadn't learned. Eight years in Thornfell taught a man not to ask questions about cargo. Questions were how couriers ended up dying in passes with their blood freezing black on stone.

But the courier hadn't treated this like cargo. He had pressed it into Quentin's hands like something alive. Like something *entrusted*. And he had spent his last breath not on a name, not on a plea for help or a prayer, but on four words:

*The thirteenth moon.*

Quentin looked up through the waterfall's icy curtain. The sun hung low and white over the peaks. The moon wouldn't rise for hours yet, but he found himself searching the pale blue for it anyway, and hated himself for it.

*Don't,* he told himself, the way he'd told himself a thousand times over eight years. *You have a route. You have buyers waiting in Halvard's valley. You have a life stitched together so carefully even you can barely see the seams. Don't start pulling threads.*

Then the storm came early off the western crags, rolling down the mountainside like spilled oil, and the map answered it.

* * *

He felt it before he understood it—that faint tug behind his sternum, the one that meant weather was near, the old family sense he'd spent eight years pretending not to have. Storm-calling ran in the Ashgrove line like dark hair or a crooked nose; the crown had wanted it harnessed, weaponized, pointed at cities full of people, and Quentin had said *no* with all the catastrophic consequences that followed. Since then he'd kept the gift leashed so tightly it was less a skill than a scar. He could feel a storm the way a man feels an old wound ache before rain. He never called one. He never let one call him.

But this storm was calling the map.

The first thunderhead crested the western ridge just as the light failed, and the map began to *glow*. Not brightly—nothing so obvious. But the ink-lines woke, shimmering faintly like fish under dark water, and where before there had been blank parchment between the mountain ranges, now other lines surfaced. Faint roads. Threads of pale silver weaving through the hatched peaks, crossing valleys that Quentin had personally crossed forty times and knew were empty of anything but scree and goat tracks.

Roads that shouldn't exist.

His hands were shaking. He told himself it was the cold.

He carried the map to the cave mouth, careful as a man carrying a sleeping child, and held it up toward the gathering storm. Lightning flickered inside the cloud-belly, a soft pulse like a heartbeat, and the silver roads brightened in answer—one of them pulsing brighter than the rest, a thread running east-southeast, down out of Thornfell proper, across country marked with a cluster of those curved, unreadable letters.

The wind rose. The first drops came sleet-hard against the rocks. And beneath the thunder, so faint he might have imagined it, Quentin heard—or felt, or remembered—something like a voice. Not words. Just pressure. Attention. The sensation of being *known* by something vast and patient that had waited a very long time.

The hair stood up along his arms.

"Who are you?" he whispered to the map, and felt immediately foolish, and immediately not foolish at all, because the silver road pulsed once, gently, like an answer. Or like a hand extended.

He rolled it up and did not sleep that night.

* * *

Halvard's forge sat in the green fold of the valley they called the Mender's Cup, two days' walk southeast if you took the goat trails and didn't mind a sheer drop to keep you honest. Quentin reached it at dusk on the second day, smelling woodsmoke and quench-water, feeling the particular exhaustion of a man who has argued with himself the whole way and lost.

Halvard was at his anvil, because Halvard was always at his anvil; Quentin suspected the man slept standing up with a hammer in his fist. He'd been a soldier once, long before Quentin's time—everyone said so, though no one said for which army—and he'd gone gray and thick-armed and quiet, teaching smithing to half the orphaned boys of the high valleys and asking no questions of anyone, ever. That was why Quentin had chosen him, eight years ago, when he'd stumbled down out of Thornfell with nothing but a false name and a hole in his chest where his family used to be. Halvard asked no questions. Halvard just handed him a bellows handle and said, *Pull.*

Now the old man looked up as Quentin ducked under the lintel, and his face split into the gap-toothed grin he reserved for the few people he tolerated.

"Quentin Ashgrove," he rumbled—using the name openly because they were alone, because they always were, because after eight years the ritual of it had become a kind of affection. "You look like something the mountain chewed up and spat out. Sit. There's stew."

"Halvard." Quentin's voice came out strange. He unslung his pack with hands that were not quite steady and drew out the map, still rolled, still refusing, he now realized, to be anything but exactly what it was. "I need you to look at something."

Halvard wiped his hands on his leather apron, unhurried, and took the roll. He weighed it a moment—Quentin saw his brow furrow slightly, the way it did when iron rang wrong—and then unrolled it across the scarred workbench, pinning the corners with a horseshoe and a cold punch.

The forge went very quiet. Even the coals seemed to hush.

For a long moment Halvard said nothing at all. His eyes moved along the silvered roads, the hatched peaks, the curved letters. Quentin watched the color leave the old man's face in stages—first the flush of the forge-work fading from his cheeks, then the deeper drain beneath, until his skin had the gray pallor of ash.

"This is Vaelorian," Halvard said finally. His voice had dropped to almost nothing, a sound Quentin had never heard him make: afraid. "This is *Vaeloria*."

The word landed in Quentin's chest like a dropped hammer. He knew it—he couldn't say from where, the way you know a smell from childhood without knowing the room—and it hurt somewhere old and unhealed.

"I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "Vaeloria doesn't exist."

"No." Halvard laughed, short and terrible, with no humor in it. "No, it doesn't. That's rather the point, boy. It doesn't exist because the Crown spent ten years and a river of blood making certain it didn't. Scrubbed from the maps. Struck from the chronicles. The universities were purged twice." His finger hovered above the curved letters without touching them, as if the ink might bite. "There isn't a man alive under sixty who can read this script. I only can because my grandmother sang her lullabies in it, and she made me swear never to repeat a word where anyone could hear." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "She was from there. Before the Erasure."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "Why are you telling me this? Why does it matter? It's a dead country on a dead man's map."

"It matters," Halvard said slowly, "because I asked myself why a smuggler who asks no questions comes down off the mountain looking like he's seen a ghost, carrying a Vaelorian survey chart that won't take flame. And it matters because I know this region here—" his finger moved to the eastern range, the cluster of letters beside the pulsing silver road, "—this is the Emberhold. The seat of the ember-keepers. The ones who kept the country's memories alive. The Crown burned them first, before they burned anything else. You cannot erase a nation while its rememberers still breathe."

He straightened. And then he looked at Quentin—really looked, the way he hadn't since that first night eight years ago, since the night a half-dead young man had given him a false name and he'd pretended to believe it.

"Ashgrove," Halvard said quietly. "I always thought it was invented. A smuggler's alias. It's not, is it?"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, an owl called across the darkening valley, and Quentin became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something rising from deep water.

"My mother's name," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away. "She said it was old. She said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said we kept things. She never said what. She burned her papers the year I turned twelve and told me never to speak of it, and then the Inquisitor Corvane came for us anyway, and I never got to ask her anything ever again."

Halvard closed his eyes. When he opened them, the fear in them had sharpened into something worse: pity.

"Ember-keeper," the old man said. "Last of them, if what I suspect is true. Boy—you're not carrying a map. You're carrying a key. And there are people who burned a whole country out of history who will do anything—*anything*—to make sure that key never turns."

Outside, far to the west, over the black spine of Thornfell, thunder muttered. Quentin felt it in his sternum, faint and insistent, tugging at him like a hand on a rope.

And beneath the thunder, unmistakable now, he felt something else—something that raised every hair on his body and turned his blood to ice water.

He was being followed.
