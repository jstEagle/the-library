## 3. The Thief on the Road

Wren had been following the storm-caller for six days, and in six days she had learned three things about him.

First: he was careful. He broke camp before dawn, doubled back twice on every trail, and slept with one hand curled around a knife that he never seemed to actually hold. Second: he was kind, which annoyed her. He'd left coins—actual coins—for the goatherd whose flock he'd scattered crossing a high pasture. Smugglers didn't do that. Smugglers left debts and shallow graves.

Third, and most dangerous: he talked to himself when he thought no one was listening. Not the muttering of a madman. Conversations. Quiet, patient, as though rehearsing things he'd never say aloud to another living soul.

She crouched now among the wind-bent pines above the switchback trail, watching him pick his way down through the scree with that long smuggler's stride, the map's oilcloth tube slung across his back like a spine. Six days of cold camps and chewed bark tea, and here was the moment her employer's silver had promised. The Inquisitor's men wanted Quentin Ashgrove alive. They wanted the map more. Wren intended to deliver both, collect her payment, and be three kingdoms gone before anyone asked how she'd done it.

The plan was simple. Simple plans were the only kind she trusted anymore.

He would reach the narrow shelf of rock below—the place where the mountain pinched the trail to two hand-spans of crumbling stone. She'd already loosened the boulder above it yesterday, while he slept. When he passed beneath, she would bring it down—not to kill, never to kill; corpses were heavy and questions were worse—and the fall would stun him long enough for her to climb down and take what mattered.

Touch him once, palm to skin, and pull. That was all a memory-thief needed. One touch, and she could lift whatever she wanted from the surface of a man's mind: where he'd hidden the map, whom he'd sold it to, whether he'd already memorized its contents. Quick, clean, painless if she was gentle, and she was always gentle. It was the only mercy she had ever been good at.

The wind shifted through the pines. Below, Quentin Ashgrove stepped onto the shelf of stone.

Wren pressed her palms flat against the boulder and pushed, letting gravity do what eight years of thieving had taught her hands to do.

* * *

The crack of splitting rock echoed off the valley walls like thunder with nowhere to go. The boulder came down a half-breath behind him—he was faster than she'd guessed, twisting away so that it struck the trail's edge rather than his skull—and he went over the lip in an avalanche of gravel, catching the last gnarled pine root with both hands, dangling above a drop that ended in white water forty feet below.

By the time she reached him, he had already hauled himself halfway up, boots scraping for purchase against the cliff face.

"Give me your hand," she said, kneeling at the edge, arm extended.

He looked up at her. Even hanging by his fingers over a river, even with blood threading down from his hairline, his eyes were level and unafraid, and that unsettled her more than anything else had all week. People who weren't afraid were people who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to them.

"I know what you are," he said. "You've been behind me since the shrine road."

"Then you know I'm the best chance you have of not becoming paste at the bottom of this gorge."

A muscle worked in his jaw. The root groaned. And because survival was a language older than suspicion, he reached up and took her hand.

She meant to take the map's location and nothing else. A single touch, a single thread drawn from the surface—where is it, where is it, where is it—

But the moment their skin met, something *seized* her.

It was not like reading a mind. Reading was skimming stones across still water. This was falling through ice. His memories rose around her like drowned lanterns, hundreds of them, thousands, lit from within, and they were not his—

They were hers. They should have been hers.

A kitchen with copper pots catching morning light. A woman's hands braiding her hair—no, braiding *someone's* hair, small hands fumbling the knot, and a voice saying *again, Mama, do the ember-braid again*. Snow through a window shaped like a fish. A dog whose name lived right there, just at the edge of knowing, a warm brown shape that leaned against her legs. A lullaby in a language she did not speak but somehow knew the melody of, every note of it, the way you know your own heartbeat—

Wren tore her hand free and staggered back, gasping, and the visions guttered out like candles snuffed between wet fingers, leaving only the ache where they'd been. That was the cruelty of her gift, or her curse: she remembered everything she stole, and everything she stole felt like coming home, and then it was gone again and she was standing in the cold holding nothing but the memory of having had one.

Quentin Ashgrove pulled himself over the ledge and lay on the trail, breathing hard, staring at her.

"What did you just do?" His voice was hoarse. "What was *that*?"

"Nothing." Her hands were shaking. She clenched them. "I took nothing."

"You screamed." He sat up slowly, wincing. "You looked at me like I'd—like I was—" He stopped, and she watched him arrive somewhere he didn't want to be. "You saw them. Didn't you."

"I don't know what you're talking about." She backed toward the treeline, and hated that her voice cracked on the second word.

"The kitchen," he said quietly. "The braid. The dog."

The world went very still. Wind moved in the pines. Somewhere far below, the river went on minding its own business.

"Those aren't mine," Wren said. "I don't *have* those."

"No," Quentin said. "You don't. But Vaeloria did."

* * *

They made camp an hour later because neither of them could think of anything better to do, which Wren supposed was how most wars and most marriages began. He built a fire with the competence of a man who'd spent years keeping warm in places that wanted him dead. She sat across from it with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, feeling hollowed out and strangely full at once—a cup turned upside down that still dripped.

"Memory-thief," Quentin said. It wasn't a question. Halvard had warned him about her kind, apparently. The guilds of the low country, the ones who dealt in the currency no magistrate could tax.

"Wren," she offered, because names cost nothing.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm giving." She fed a stick to the fire. "You want my trade, fine. I touch someone, I can draw out what they remember. Recent things sit close to the surface. Old things take longer. I can also put things *in*, if the price is right—which is why the Inquisitor's people hire me, usually. Make a witness remember a crime he never saw. Make a guard forget the face he saw at midnight." She shrugged. "I'm very good at ruining people's lives. It's honest work."

"And what happened on the cliff? You looked like someone had cut you open."

Her jaw tightened. She should lie. Lying was her native tongue; she'd spoken it before she could walk. But the fragments were still echoing in her—the braid, the snow, the warm weight of the dog—and for the first time in years, lying felt heavier than truth.

"I steal memories," she said slowly, "because I can't keep any of my own. Sold them. Twenty years ago. All of it, everything before I was nine years old, to a broker in Corvane's city who paid enough bread and board to keep a starving child alive through winter." She watched the flames instead of him. "That's the joke of the trade. We take other people's pasts because we have none of our own. Every thief in the guild is an amnesiac wearing someone else's history like a stolen coat. It never fits."

"And today?"

"Today I touched you and found—" Her throat closed. She tried again. "A kitchen. Copper pots. A woman doing a child's hair. A dog. Snow through a window. None of it mine, and all of it—" She stopped, furious at the heat rising behind her eyes. "All of it *familiar*. Like walking into a house I've never seen and knowing exactly where the cups are kept."

Quentin was silent for a long time. The fire popped. When he spoke, his voice had gone strange and careful.

"The window," he said. "Was it shaped like a fish?"

Wren's head came up. "How did you—"

"My mother's house. Before the burning." He stared into the fire as though it might show him something. "Vaelorian homes were built with fish-shaped windows in the kitchens. It was a blessing. Fish swim in schools; the household would never eat alone. I grew up under one." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Those memories you touched in me—they weren't mine either. They're older than me. My grandmother's, maybe. My line kept Vaeloria's memories, Wren. All of them. Carried them like coals in a banked fire so they couldn't be stamped out. And when I touched you, some of them recognized—"

He stopped. She finished it for him, because the shape of it had already settled into her bones, terrible and wonderful.

"They recognized home," she whispered.

Neither of them spoke. Across the fire, the truth sat between them like a third traveler, warming its hands.

Here is what Wren understood, in that moment, with a clarity that frightened her more than any Inquisitor's blade: she had spent twenty years hungry without knowing for what. She had stolen a hundred lives in fragments—a soldier's first battle, a bride's wedding morning, a dying man's last summer—and none of it had ever filled the hole, because stolen memories were guests, not family. But what had flooded out of Quentin Ashgrove on the cliffside had fit. Had *known* her. Had braided itself into the empty places like water finding cracks in stone.

And he—she saw it now, saw it plainly—was just as starved. Here was a man carrying an inheritance like a disease, alone with ghosts and guilt, exiled from both nations, talking to himself on mountainsides because there was no one left to talk to. He held everything she had lost. She knew, better than anyone alive, how it felt to hold everything and belong nowhere.

Two broken people, each holding half of something whole.

"So," she said at last, because hunger makes the bold bolder. "The men hunting you. Corvane's. They hired me to bring you and the map in. I imagine they'll send others when I don't report. Others who are less inclined to share fires and feelings." She tilted her head. "Which means you need me. I know their routes, their faces, their paymasters. I can make a patrol remember seeing us ride east when we went west. I can make a gate captain's memory of tonight... flexible."

"And what do you want?"

"The door," she said simply. "Whatever's behind it. Whatever Vaeloria kept locked away—you said your bloodline carries its memories. Maybe some of mine are in there. Maybe the broker who bought a nine-year-old's childhood kept records, or maybe the door itself remembers. I want my life back, Quentin Ashgrove. I've killed for less."

He studied her across the flames, and she watched him weigh it—watched the smuggler tally risk against reward, and the heir tally something older and sadder underneath. Then he held out his hand, palm up, across the fire.

"Alliance," he said. "Uneasy. Understood?"

Wren looked at his open hand and thought about the last time she'd taken it. She almost laughed. Instead she reached out and clasped his forearm, thief-fashion, skin to sleeve, and felt him do the same arithmetic she had: trust, but carefully.

"Then we should move at first light," she said. "Because I lied about one thing. I wasn't the only hunter on your trail."

His eyes sharpened. "Who else?"

"A gray-cloaked man with a burned face, asking after you in every village from the pass down. He doesn't sleep. He doesn't eat that I've seen. And three days ago he stopped asking after you entirely." She let that land, watching his face drain. "He started asking about the thirteenth moon."
