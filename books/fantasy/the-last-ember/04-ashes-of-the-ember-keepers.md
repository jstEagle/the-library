## 4. Ashes of the Ember-Keepers

The shrine had no name on any map Quentin had ever smuggled across, and that was the first thing that told him it mattered.

Halvard led them off the goat-track at dawn, down through a fold of the mountains where the pines grew crooked and old, their roots gripping stone like hands refusing to let go. Wren walked behind them both, silent in the way she was always silent—watchful, hungry-eyed, her fingers twitching near her sleeves as though memories might be plucked from the air itself if she only reached fast enough.

"You knew this was here," Quentin said. "All these years, and you never told me."

"I told you what you could bear." Halvard did not turn. The old man's staff clicked against wet rock; his breath came in white ribbons despite the season. "Most days, I barely told myself."

The trees broke open onto a hollow, a bowl of green shadow ringed by cliffs, and at its center stood the shrine—or what remained of one. It had been round once, Quentin thought, a drum of pale stone with a domed roof, but time and fire had chewed it half away. What fire did not take, moss had claimed, creeping up walls carved with figures whose faces had been worn to smooth blankness. Yet even blind, they seemed to watch. Even ruined, the place felt *occupied*, as if the air itself were holding its breath around something precious.

The map burned against Quentin's chest where he'd kept it sewn inside his coat for three weeks now. It had grown warmer every day since Halvard first looked at the Vaelorian script and gone the color of tallow.

"This is Vaelorian work." He stopped at the threshold, where a lintel stone lay cracked across the entrance like a fallen jaw. "You said so yourself. So why is it here, in Thornfell? Vaeloria is a story. A fable mothers use to frighten children about asking too many questions."

"Vaeloria is a wound," Halvard said quietly. "And wounds are hidden, not erased. Come inside, boy. It's time you learned why you walk the way you do—with your head down, your shoulders braced, waiting for the sky to fall."

* * *

Inside, the dome survived above them in fragments, and through its broken crown fell a single column of morning light that landed on the floor like something deliberately placed. The walls were covered in script—the same flowing, ember-bright lettering as the courier's map—and beneath it, friezes: processions of robed figures, each carrying a flame cupped in both palms, walking toward cities, toward fields, toward crowds of upturned faces.

Quentin traced one figure with a fingertip. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm. *Alive*-warm, faintly pulsing, like skin over a pulse point.

"Tell me," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he intended.

Halvard lowered himself onto a fallen block with the care of a man whose joints had long ago made their grievances known. "I was not born in Thornfell," he began. "That much you guessed. But I doubt you guessed how far from it. I was born under a different sky, in a country your maps call nothing at all, because there is nothing left to name."

"Vaeloria."

"Vaeloria." The word left the old man like a held breath finally released. "It was real, Quentin. Real as the stone under you. A kingdom of the eastern valleys, older than the crown that destroyed it, and it had a gift no other nation possessed: its people did not forget. Not the way we forget—not dimming, not fading. Their memories were *kept*. There was an order sworn to tend them, generation upon generation, keepers who carried the living memory of the whole country the way a lamp carries flame. Grief, joy, the faces of the dead, the names of rivers before the rivers changed course—all of it, tended and passed on. A country that could never be lied to about itself."

Wren had drifted along the wall, and Quentin saw her press her palm flat against the script there. Her face went strange—unguarded, almost frightened. "Where did the memories live?" she asked, too quickly. "In the stones? In objects?"

"In people," Halvard said. "In bloodlines chosen for it. And chief among those bloodlines—" the old man turned, and his eyes, rheumy and ancient, fixed on Quentin with terrible gentleness, "—were the Ashgroves. Ember-keepers. The first flame-tenders. When the crown came with fire and edict, when it burned the shrines and scattered the people and wrote Vaeloria out of every ledger and chronicle, the Ashgroves refused to let the last of it die. They hid the country's memory behind a door. A door that opens only when thirteen moons have waxed and waned since the last opening—a door keyed to ember-keeper blood, and to storm."

The light from the dome seemed suddenly very loud. Quentin became aware of his own heartbeat, of the static prickling along his forearms that always preceded weather, that had preceded it since he was a child crouching in his mother's kitchen while thunder walked the ridgelines.

"My family," he said. "My mother. My sister. They didn't die because I refused Corvane's order. That was the excuse. The *reason* was—"

"Was that you were the last heir," Halvard finished. "The last ember-keeper alive after the purges. The crown thought it had burned the line out entirely. Then a storm-caller in the royal army called lightning out of a clear sky, and someone old and clever in the Inquisition remembered what that used to mean." He exhaled. "Corvane didn't exile you out of mercy, boy. Exile was the trap. A leash long enough to let you run, short enough to follow."

The map against Quentin's chest flared hot enough that he gasped and tore open his coat. The courier's bloodstained parchment lay against his shirt, and the script upon it had begun to glow—a slow, molten gold, lines crawling like veins of fire through paper. And where the ink moved, the friezes on the walls answered. The blank-faced carriers raised their flames. Somewhere deep in the stone, something turned over in its sleep.

"The thirteenth moon," Quentin whispered. "The courier's warning."

"Thirteen moons since the last opening," Halvard confirmed. "Which falls—" he counted silently, lips moving, and went greyer than the moss outside, "—eleven days from now. At the ruins of the capital. Where the door waits."

Wren laughed, a single sharp note without any humor in it. "Eleven days. And half the Inquisition already knows the map exists, because I wasn't the only one sent after it. You two have been strolling through the mountains like pilgrims."

Before anyone could answer, the warmth died.

Not gradually—it *snuffed*, all at once, the glowing script going dark, the column of light through the dome seeming to thin and shudder. The air pressure dropped so hard Quentin's ears popped. He knew this sensation the way he knew his own name. He had felt it on battlefields, on the night his family died, on every worst day of his life.

Someone was calling a storm. Or rather—someone had *brought* one, leashed and obedient, the way only the Inquisition's hounds were taught to do.

"Halvard." Quentin was already moving, pulling the old man upright. "Back way. Is there a back way?"

"There is no back way," Halvard said, terribly calm. "There never was. Sit down, Quentin. Both of you. Running would insult him, and insulting Corvane gets people killed faster than defying him."

* * *

He came through the broken doorway like winter coming into a warm room.

Corvane had not aged the way men age. Eight years since Quentin had stood in the judgment hall and heard his sentence pronounced in that voice—smooth, patient, reasonable—and the Inquisitor looked exactly the same: tall, spare, silver at the temples as if frost had simply decided to live there. His grey coat was immaculate. Behind him, filling the shrine's entrance, stood six hunters in ash-grey hoods, and above them the sky churned black, a storm pinned to the mountaintop like a moth to a board.

"Ashgrove." Corvane smiled the way a surgeon smiles. "And Halvard. I wondered which direction the rat had been pointing you. All those years in Thornfell, playing smuggler, playing teacher—I confess I hoped you'd die useless. It saves everyone such unpleasantness when traitors die uselessly."

"Halvard isn't a traitor," Quentin said. "He never swore your oaths."

"He taught a weapon how to think," Corvane replied mildly. "That's worse." He stepped over the cracked lintel, boots ringing on stone, and paused to consider the friezes with the detached appreciation of a man appraising furniture. "Do you know, I burned eleven of these shrines myself? There were fourteen once. This one we never found. My predecessors were thorough but unimaginative—they looked for Vaeloria in records. I learned to look for it in *people*." His gaze settled on Quentin, and there it was: the thing Quentin had fled for eight years, the patient, bottomless attention of a man who collected other lives the way Wren collected memories. "You've felt it, haven't you? Since the map. The warmth. The pull. You are the last coal of a fire I personally put out, and I am given to understand you can open my door."

"It's not yours."

"Everything is mine that the crown cannot hold." Corvane spread his hands. "Here is what happens now. You will come with me. You will open the thirteenth-moon door, and you will seal it—permanently, irrevocably, with your blood and your storms—and then you will be free. No more running. No more hunters. I give you my word, and my word has never once been broken, because I choose my promises carefully."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane sighed, genuinely regretful, and that was the most frightening thing about him—that the cruelty was always administrative, always filed under necessity.

Then Halvard stepped forward.

The old man moved past Quentin with surprising speed, planting himself between the Inquisitor and his student, staff leveled like a lance. "You'll not have him," Halvard said. "Eight years I kept him ignorant because ignorance kept him breathing. I won't hand him to you now that he knows. Vaeloria's memory is not yours to bury, Corvane. It was never the crown's to burn. You are a gravedigger standing at a door you don't understand, demanding the key from the only man alive who does—and I will not—"

Quentin never saw Corvane move. Later, he would remember only the sound—a soft, wet percussion, absurdly small in the vast stone hush—and then Halvard folding, the staff clattering, the old man's hand finding Quentin's wrist as he went down.

"Eleven days," Halvard whispered. Blood threaded between his teeth. His grip was astonishingly strong. "Boy—listen—the door doesn't want a king. Remember that. Whatever it shows you—whatever it offers—it doesn't want a king, it wants a *keeper*. There's a difference, and everything depends—" The grip loosened. "—on knowing the difference."

His eyes stayed open. Quentin closed them.

When he rose, the static was screaming along every nerve in his body. Above the broken dome, the leashed storm churned and spat, and Quentin felt it—not Corvane's storm, but *his own*, the one that had lived in his blood since birth, rising to meet the sky like recognizing like. The friezes blazed gold. The map burned through his coat. Every blank-carved face in the shrine seemed to turn toward the man in the grey coat, and toward the man standing over the old teacher's body, and to wait.

"Sentimental," Corvane observed. "Hunters—take the girl. Kill the thief if she resists; she's replaceable. As for you, Ashgrove—you'll come willingly or you'll come bound. Either way, in eleven days, that door opens for me."

Quentin lifted his hands, and outside, the mountain's sky answered.

Lightning split the pinned storm down its middle—not striking outward, but *inward*, a white spear falling through the shattered dome straight at the floor between them, and in its light Quentin heard himself speak in a voice that did not feel entirely his own:

"You should have stayed afraid of me, Corvane. Everyone else did."

The shrine filled with thunder.
