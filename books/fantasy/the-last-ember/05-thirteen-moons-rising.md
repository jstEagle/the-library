## 5. Thirteen Moons Rising

The Weeping Steppe did not weep for the dead. Wren had heard that much from smugglers' talk, back when she still took jobs in Thornfell's low towns: it wept for what was *taken*. She hadn't believed it then. Belief was a luxury, and memory—real memory, owned memory—was a luxury too. She'd sold hers at fourteen to buy passage out of a burning city, and she had never once let herself wonder whose fire it had been.

Now she walked beside Quentin Ashgrove through grass that whispered with other people's voices, and she wondered everything.

The steppe began where Halvard's shrine country ended, rolling gray-green to a horizon bruised purple by distance. It looked harmless by daylight. But the sun had barely set before the first of them appeared—a woman standing ankle-deep in the sere grass, her hands cupped around something invisible, her mouth moving in a slow, soundless plea.

"She's not real," Quentin said quietly. "Or she is, and she's not *here*."

"Both." Wren kept walking. "Memories pool here. The ones that get stolen have to go somewhere, and this is the low ground. They soak into the earth like rainwater. Travelers who cross at night—" She shrugged. "Some come out the far side fine. Some come out carrying a grief that isn't theirs and can't put it down."

"And you? What comes out for you?"

She didn't answer that. The grass hissed against her boots, and somewhere behind them—two days behind, maybe less—the hunters rode on.

* * *

They made cold camp in a hollow ringed by standing stones, ancient things worn smooth as river pebbles, each carved with script that made Quentin's fingers twitch toward the map in his coat. He'd taken to sleeping with it against his chest like a talisman, which Wren found both touching and tactically stupid. If Corvane's hunters caught him, they wouldn't need to torture the map's location out of him. It would be the first thing their hands found.

"You should let me carry it," she said, settling across the small fire he'd allowed himself—dwarfed in a pit, screened by stones, no bigger than a cookpot's worth of light.

"No."

"I'm harder to search than you."

"You're also the person who was hired to steal it." He said it without heat. That was the strange thing about Quentin Ashgrove: even after she'd put a knife to his throat on the mountain road, even after she'd confessed exactly who had sent her, he spoke to her like someone he was deciding to trust rather than someone he was watching. She didn't know what to do with that. Trust was a currency she'd never held long enough to spend.

"That was before," she said.

"Was it?" He fed a twig to the flames. "You haven't told me why you changed your mind. People don't turn on employers like Corvane for nothing. He finds people who do."

The firelight moved on his face, catching the storm-gray of his eyes, and for a moment she felt again that vertigo from the ambush—that flood of *not-hers*, a child's laughter in a stone courtyard, the smell of bread and ozone, a woman's voice saying *Quentin, come inside before the lightning does.* She had touched hundreds of minds in her trade. She knew how memories felt going in and coming out, slick and weightless as fish. His had been different. His had been a door left open, and she had fallen through it into a childhood she'd never lived, warm and whole and unbearably specific, and she had wanted it so badly her hands had shaken.

That was why she hadn't cut his throat. Not mercy. Hunger.

"The steppe is already pulling at you," she said instead. "I can see it. You've got someone else's headache."

He blinked, surprised, then pressed his palm briefly to his temple. "A man named Odo. He lost his brother at the ford at Marrow Creek. How did you know?"

"Because I'm not getting anything." She hugged her knees, chin resting on them, watching the fire. "That's the joke of this place, Ashgrove. A steppe full of stolen memories, and there's nothing here that wants me. The dead woman's sorrows, Odo's headaches—they slide right past. I'm already empty. Nothing left to take."

"That's not true."

"It's the truest thing about me." She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered, because if she gave the words any weight they'd crush her. "I sold my past when I was fourteen. All of it. Name, mother, city, the reason I was running—I don't know any of it anymore. There's a broker in Vaskar who keeps memories in glass jars like preserves, and mine are sitting on his shelf gathering dust, or more likely they're long gone, sold off piece by piece to some lordling who wanted to know what it felt like to be loved by somebody. And now we're crossing the one place in the world where memories wander free looking for homes, and not one of them will look at me twice."

The words came out faster than she intended, tumbling like water from a cracked dam, and when they stopped she felt scraped raw, exposed in a way no blade could manage.

Quentin didn't say *I'm sorry*. She was grateful for that. Sorry was a coin everyone spent and nobody meant.

"What did the broker give you?" he asked. "For all of it?"

"Enough to eat for a year and a lie to live inside." She turned a small stick over in her fingers. "He asked me what I wanted to remember instead, since an empty head goes mad within a season. Everyone gets one replacement memory, complimentary, so to speak. Most pick something grand. A coronation they attended. A lover who never existed." The stick snapped between her fingers. She hadn't realized she was bending it. "I couldn't think of anything. So I took a blank. Just—an hour of standing in an open field, doing nothing, belonging to no one. The broker thought I was mad. Maybe I was. But every time the emptiness got loud, over the years, I'd go somewhere quiet and stand in a field, and I'd feel almost like that memory again. Almost like mine."

The fire crackled. Out in the dark, the weeping grass murmured its borrowed griefs, and the woman with the cupped hands had drifted closer, her silent pleading just beyond the ring of stones.

"When I touched you," Wren said, and stopped. Started again. "When I grabbed you on the road. Your memories came into me like they were waiting for me. Like they'd been set out for company. Do you understand what that's like for someone like me? To feel a childhood that isn't yours and have it fit better than anything you own?" Her voice went thin. She hated that. She steadied it. "I didn't spare you out of kindness, Ashgrove. I spared you because I was greedy. I want what you have. The family, the name, the whole terrible inheritance of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"Especially the parts that hurt," Quentin said.

"Yes. Gods help me, yes."

He was quiet for a long moment, and she braced herself for pity, for the soft careful voice people used on broken things, for the offer of comfort she would have to cut herself open refusing.

Instead he said, "Then help me make sure Corvane doesn't keep it. Everything he burned—the keepers' records, the shrine, Halvard—" His voice caught on the old man's name and steadied. "It's not just my inheritance, Wren. I keep thinking that now. It belonged to thousands of people. Their memories were *stolen*, wholesale, the way yours were. One jar at a time, a whole country at a time. You of all people should want to see it given back."

Wren stared at him. Something shifted in her chest, painful as a bone setting wrong-ways-right.

"You're not asking me to fight for you."

"I'm asking you to fight for you. I'm just pointing out it's the same fight."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse. The ghost-woman at the stone circle turned her head at the noise, and for a heartbeat her cupped hands seemed to extend toward Wren, offering, offering—and then folded closed, and she faded into the grass like breath off a mirror.

Nothing for me, Wren thought. Still nothing. But it hurt differently tonight. Tonight the emptiness felt less like a wound and more like a room, swept clean, waiting.

"All right," she said. "Same fight. But I have conditions."

"Of course you do."

"One: when this is done, if my memories still exist anywhere—in a broker's vault, in Corvane's archives, anywhere—you help me find them. Whatever they are. However ugly."

"Done."

"Two: you stop sleeping with the map under your heart like a lovesick boy. Give it to me during the day. I'll wear it where no searcher will dare pat."

He hesitated, then drew it out and tossed it to her. The trust in the gesture landed harder than any speech. She tucked it into her bodice and felt absurdly, dangerously warm.

"Three," she said, lying back to watch the stars wheel over the steppe, "you tell me about her. The woman in your courtyard memory. The one who called you in from the lightning."

"My mother." His voice was soft. Above them, unremarked by either of them, the moon rose—not full, not yet, but swollen with the promise of fullness, thirteen nights from perfect. "Her name was Elira. She baked bread badly and sang worse, and she always said the storms listened better than I did."

"Tell me anyway," Wren said, and closed her eyes, and let someone else's remembered childhood pour over her like rain, and for the first time in eleven years, the emptiness inside her wasn't hungry. It was listening.

* * *

They crossed the heart of the Weeping Steppe by night and slept by day, trading watches, and each night the ghosts grew bolder. Whole processions of them now: soldiers marching with banners no nation flew anymore, children chasing a ball that wasn't there, a choir singing in a language that made Quentin's blood hum in recognition. Once, Wren woke to find him standing outside the stone ring, arms raised to a sky empty of clouds, tears running down his face, mouthing words in that same dead tongue. She led him back without comment, and he never asked what he'd done, and she never told him, and that silence between them became its own kind of intimacy.

On the fifth night the horizon changed.

At first she took it for weather—a wall of darkness building in the east, too straight-edged for cloud. Then the moonlight touched it and she saw mountains, black and jagged, crowned with a structure that caught the light like a blade: a single tower, impossibly tall, rising from the range's highest peak.

Quentin stopped walking. The map was in his hand—he'd taken to holding it open now, letting it drink the starlight—and its inked lines had begun to glow, faintly gold, like embers breathing.

"The Spine of Sorrows," he said. "The capital was built in its shadow. Wren—it's only four days' walk."

"Four days," she echoed. She counted backward without wanting to, the way she counted everything now: thirteen moons since the courier died, thirteen nights until the thirteenth full moon rose complete. They had eight. Eight nights, four days' travel, and somewhere behind them, close enough now that she could taste iron on the wind, the hunters.

And then the wind shifted, and brought her something worse than iron. It brought voices. Real ones, downwind, human—half a mile back along their trail, where the grass stood tall.

She caught Quentin's arm and pulled him down into the whispering dark, and together they lay in the stolen sorrows of the steppe while torches bloomed behind them, one, two, seven, a slow constellation of pursuit spreading across the ridge.

"They found our trail," she breathed.

Quentin's jaw tightened. When he spoke, his voice carried the low rumble of a sky deciding whether to break. "Then we run."
