## 6. The City That Was Never Built

The Weeping Steppe ended the way grief ends—not all at once, but in a slow thinning of sorrow until the ground itself seemed to forget what it had been mourning. Quentin felt the change under his boots before he saw it: a hardening of earth, a faint ring of stone beneath the turf like a scar beneath skin.

Wren crouched beside him at the crest of the last hill, her hood dark with three days of rain. "This is it?" she asked, and there was nothing beneath her voice but the question. No wonder. No awe. He had learned that about her on the Steppe—she held feeling the way a cracked cup holds water, and he had stopped expecting it to stay.

"This is where the map says it should be," he said.

There was nothing. A shallow valley of grass and standing stones, wind-combed and empty. Somewhere below, sheep grazed among foundations that were only foundations—broken walls waist-high, the bones of a village that might have died any ordinary death a century ago.

"The map is wrong," Wren said.

"The map isn't wrong." He unrolled it across his knees despite the damp. The vellum still responded to him the way it always did, warming against his palms like a living thing, and the roads it showed ran through this valley like veins through flesh—great avenues, a river-gate, terraced gardens climbing toward a citadel that the eye insisted could not be there. "It's not *showing* me where the city is. It's showing me where it *was*. Where it still is." He swallowed. "It's just not lit."

Wren turned her head. In the grey light her eyes were pale as frost. "Lit."

"Stormlight." The word came out of him rougher than he meant it to. Eight years he had rationed his gift like a starving man rations bread—small calls, small storms, just enough to fill a smuggler's sails or blind a pursuer for a precious minute. Now the map wanted everything. "Vaeloria wasn't hidden. It was *veiled*. And the veil answers to ember-keepers' blood and storm-callers' hands." His laugh came out bitter. "Which means I have to call a storm big enough to light a city. On an open plain. With Corvane's hunters two days behind us and closing."

"You say that like it's a reason not to."

"It is a reason to be afraid."

She studied him for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "I've watched you do small miracles for weeks and pretend they're nothing. Do this one and stop pretending." She paused, and something flickered behind the frost of her eyes—the ghost of whatever fragments of him she'd stolen that night on the road, the childhood that wasn't hers, the mother's voice calling a boy's name into thunder. "*Quentin.* Even your name sounds like weather."

He almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

He climbed to the highest of the standing stones as dusk came down, because the old rites—if Halvard's half-remembered teachings were true—wanted height and wanted dark. The first stars were pricking through when he spread his arms and reached for the sky the way he hadn't reached since the night that had broken his life.

It answered too quickly.

Eight years of rationing, and the storm came to him like a hound that had waited at a closed door all that time, hurling itself against him with joy. Clouds boiled up out of a clear evening, stacking themselves into towers; the wind swung around and began to moan through the standing stones in voices that sounded almost human. Wren shouted something from below—he couldn't hear it over the rising roar—and then the world went white.

Not lightning. *Light.*

It poured down from the clouds in sheets, and where it fell, the valley changed.

The ruined village walls rose. The grass burned away like mist before sun. Streets unfolded from the earth—broad avenues of white stone veined with gold, fountains that leapt though no water touched them, lamps kindling one by one along their posts like a city drawing breath after eight years of holding it. Towers climbed where empty air had been. Terraces bloomed up the flanks of the hills. At the valley's heart, a citadel of pale stone and copper spires stood crowned in stormlight, its gates thrown open as if in welcome, as if the city had been waiting all this time with its doors unlocked and its people gone.

And the people—

For one heartbeat, the streets were full of them. Figures of light walking figures of light dogs, hanging washing that glowed and vanished, turning faces upward toward the storm as if they knew who had called it. Then the vision thinned, and the avenues stood empty again—but built now, undeniable, a dead city wearing its own ghost.

Quentin stood on the stone with the storm howling through his hair and understood, with a horror that went deeper than fear, what Corvane had done. Not destroyed this. *Hidden* it. Erased it so thoroughly that even the land had learned to lie about itself. Every map rewritten, every record burned, every survivor scattered and sworn silent—and above it all, a veil of forgetting laid over a quarter million lives.

Rage came up in him like groundwater, cold and inexhaustible.

Below, Wren was staring at the shining city with her hand pressed over her mouth—an instinctive gesture, he realized, of someone whose whole life was stolen things, suddenly confronted with the largest theft in history.

And far off, beyond the northern ridge, torches. Many torches. Moving fast.

The stormlight had shown them everything—including where to find him.

* * *

They ran down into the city because running anywhere else meant dying in the open. The gates took them in without resistance—the stone simply parted around them, or perhaps they passed through it; Quentin couldn't tell anymore, couldn't tell what was real and what was memory made visible. The storm overhead held, grumbling, feeding light into the streets, and every step he took deeper into Vaeloria pressed images into him like seals into wax.

Here, a fountain plaza where children had flown kites shaped like storm-birds. He knew it the way you know a room you grew up in. Here, a library whose doors had been carved with the oath of the ember-keepers—*we hold what others cannot carry; we remember so none must forget*—and he knew the oath in a voice that was his grandfather's grandfather's. The city poured itself into him, generation by generation, and each memory arrived with a name attached, and the names were *his* name, and somewhere in the middle of the avenue he stopped walking and put his hand against a wall just to have something solid.

"Quentin." Wren's voice, close, urgent. "You went somewhere."

"I'm still going." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Wren, I can feel them. All of them. My blood kept this place alive—literally kept it, the memories lived in us, we were the—" His breath hitched. "I'm the last archive of a country."

"And the country is trying to come home," she said. "Can you blame it?"

Then he touched the wall of the great hall near the citadel's heart, and the gentle memories stopped.

* * *

Fire.

That was the first thing. Fire in a marble corridor, fire reflected in a thousand panes of glass, and running feet, and his own legs—no, not his legs, his ancestor Aldric Ashgrove's legs, young and strong, carrying a lantern through smoke toward the sound of screaming.

The ember-keepers had been summoned to the citadel on the false pretext of an investiture. Two hundred of them, gathered in the Hall of Remembrance, and when the doors sealed and the Inquisitor's men came through the windows with fire-pots and blades, Aldric had been late—Aldric had *lingered*, arguing with a guard captain who owed him money, and that pettiness had saved his life.

Through the high windows Quentin-Aldric saw it all. Saw the keepers stand together and refuse to fight, because their oath forbade raising power against the crown, because they believed—God help them, they *believed*—that the king's justice would stop short of massacre. Saw the flames take the eldest first, the ones whose bodies held the oldest memories, and saw those memories die screaming: harvests, weddings, songs, the founding, the sea-voyage, four hundred years of a nation burning out of human skulls like dew off grass.

Saw the king's own seal on the orders. Saw the man who would become the first Inquisitor walk the hall afterward with a ledger, checking names off against the ash.

Aldric survived. Aldric fled north with a pregnant wife and a scrap of vellum. Aldric's line kept the ember alive in secret, generation to generation, waiting for a door that opened once every thirteen moons.

And eight years ago, in a barracks yard in the capital of a country that had never existed until it needed soldiers, an officer named Corvane had put a torch in Quentin's hand and pointed it at a village of Vaelorian sympathizers—fishermen, farmers, an old woman who had once taught him to whistle—and said *burn them, Ashgrove, prove your loyalty*, and Quentin had said no, and the price of saying no had been his family's name, his family's freedom, his father's death in exile, his mother's silence, everything.

He had thought, all these years, that he had refused out of decency. Simple, private decency.

Now he stood inside his ancestor's burning memory and understood that his refusal had been older than himself. It was in the blood. The ember-keepers' whole inheritance was this: to hold what others could not carry, and never, ever to be the hand that burns.

His knees hit the stone. He was shaking. The storm above the city had begun to answer the shaking, lightning walking the cloud-towers in long furious strokes, and some distant part of him knew the hunters below could read that fury like a beacon.

"Quentin—" Wren was on her knees beside him, her hands hovering near his shoulders, afraid to touch him, afraid of what touching him stole or gave. "Whatever you're seeing—"

"My family didn't just refuse," he heard himself say. His voice came out level and that was worse than screaming. "We were *made* to refuse. Four hundred years of being the conscience of a kingdom, and the kingdom's answer was fire." He looked up at her, and the lightning lit his face from above like judgment. "Corvane wrote my exile before I was born, Wren. I was the last loose thread of a genocide, and he's spent eight years pulling at me."

Something moved behind her eyes—fear, or recognition, or both.

Down in the valley, torchlight had reached the outer ruins. Horns sounded, thin and cold, three notes repeated. And beneath the horns, rolling up the avenue like oil, came a voice amplified by artifice and certainty, a voice Quentin had heard only once before, eight years ago, pronouncing sentence:

*"Ashgrove. I know you can hear me. Come out, and I will let the thief live."*

Wren went very still beside him.

Quentin rose slowly to his feet, and around him the storm gathered itself lower, tighter, hungrier—and for the first time in eight years he did not try to calm it.
