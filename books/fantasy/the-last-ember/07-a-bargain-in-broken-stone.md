## 7. A Bargain in Broken Stone

The ruins of Vaeloria's outer quarter had become their camp by necessity rather than choice. The Weeping Steppe had taken three days and something Wren suspected Quentin would never name aloud—a piece of the boy he'd been, left behind in the fog with all the other memories that wandered those plains like beggars at a feast. Now they sheltered beneath the ribs of a collapsed amphitheater, its stone seats fanned around them like the vertebrae of some ancient beast, and Wren kept watch because watching was what she knew how to do.

She had stolen sleep from a merchant once, just to see what it felt like. It tasted of lavender and lies. She hadn't tried again.

Tonight she didn't need thieving to know Quentin wasn't sleeping either. He sat on the lowest tier of broken seats with the map spread across his knees, though he no longer needed it—the way to the door was carved into him now, written in stormlight behind his eyes. Every few minutes his fingers moved through the old calling-signs, half-formed, restless. The air above his palms shivered with static, and the ruined stones around them drank it up greedily, glowing faintly blue where Vaelorian script woke beneath centuries of dust.

He looked, Wren thought, like a man reading a letter from a father he'd never met.

"You should rest," she said.

"So should you."

"I don't, much." She came down the tiers and settled beside him—not close, but not far. The distance between them had been shrinking for weeks, measured in shared fires and unshared confessions, and tonight it was small enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him like heat off a forge. "Tomorrow we go down."

"Tomorrow we go down," he agreed, and then, quieter: "What if I'm not what the door wants?"

Wren almost laughed. Almost. "Quentin Ashgrove. You are exactly what every door wants. That's the problem with you."

Something moved in the darkness beyond the amphitheater's mouth—too smooth for wind, too deliberate for an animal—and Wren's hand found her knife before her mind finished the thought.

They came out of the shadows the way ink comes out of a spilled bottle. Six figures first, grey-cloaked, moving with the drilled silence of Corvane's hunters. Then, between them, a man in a coat the color of dried blood, walking slowly, hands visible and empty, smiling the smile of someone who has never once needed a weapon to be dangerous.

Inquisitor Corvane.

Wren's body remembered him before her mind did. Her spine locked. Her breath went shallow. Somewhere far below thinking, in the cellar of herself where she kept the things she couldn't feel, something turned over in its sleep.

* * *

"Wren." He said her name like an appraisal. Like a price already paid. "Still alive. I confess I had doubts. You were always more instrument than survivor."

She heard Quentin shift beside her, felt the pressure change as the air began to gather toward him, ions crawling toward his skin like iron filings toward a lodestone. "Stay back," Quentin said, and the stones overhead began to hum.

Corvane raised one hand—not a threat, a benediction. "If I wanted you dead, Ashgrove, you'd have died on the Steppe, or in Thornfell, or eight years ago when you made your little principled stand. No. I've come to talk. You'll find I'm very good at talking." His pale eyes moved to the map, to the faintly burning script. "You've seen the city. You've walked inside your ancestors' memories. You know now what sleeps under this ground."

"The massacre." Quentin's voice was low and terrible. "I know what you did. What the crown did. What my family refused to do."

"What your family was *chosen* for," Corvane corrected gently, taking another step, and Wren marveled at how the hunters flanked without appearing to move, closing a ring so gradual it felt like weather. "Do you imagine Vaeloria was destroyed out of fear alone? No. It was contained. A country whose memories live outside books, outside minds—in the *air*, in the *stone*, waiting for a keeper to give them shape? That is not a nation, boy. That is a weapon that loads itself. Your ancestors understood restraint. Your grandmother understood it. Do you?"

"I'm not sealing anything," Quentin said. "I'm opening it. Tomorrow night. Thirteenth moon."

"Yes." Corvane smiled wider. "And here is what opening it costs, which no memory has shown you, because your ancestors never had to pay it." He stopped at the edge of the lamplight, and for the first time Wren saw that he looked tired—genuinely tired, the exhaustion of a man who had carried a single duty so long it had grown into his skeleton. "Whoever claims Vaeloria holds it. Alone. Every memory, every grief, every voice of every burned keeper, bound into one mind until that mind breaks. Your grandmother lasted nine years before the door had to be sealed with her still inside it. Did the memories show you *that*, Ashgrove? Did they show you why there are no ember-keepers anymore?"

The silence stretched. The static around Quentin's hands guttered.

"There is another path," Corvane said softly, and now his voice took on the warm grain of a priest offering absolution. "Seal the door forever. Unmake the key. And the crown restores everything: your name, your lands, your pardon writ in the king's own hand. No more smuggler's winters. No more looking over your shoulder in every market town from here to the sea. You could call storms openly again—for the army, if you wish, or for no one at all. You would be Quentin Ashgrove, heir of nothing, burdened by nothing. Free."

And Wren watched—helpless, hating her helplessness—as the offer slid into Quentin like a knife finding the gap between ribs.

Because she could see it working on him. She had spent years learning to read what people felt precisely because she couldn't feel it herself; it was the closest thing she had to the sensation. And what she read on Quentin's face now was not temptation in the vulgar sense. It was relief. Eight years of exile lifting off him like frost in morning sun. She watched his shoulders drop half an inch. She watched his eyes lose their storm-focus and go somewhere soft and far away—to a house, maybe, a hearth, a life where his hands called rain for farmers instead of ruin for soldiers.

"Your pardon," Quentin said hoarsely. "My family's names cleared."

"Every record corrected. Every lie unmade—except the necessary ones."

"And Vaeloria stays dead."

"Vaeloria was always dead," Corvane said. "You would simply stop pretending otherwise. Peace, Ashgrove. Real peace. How long has it been since you've had any?"

Wren opened her mouth to speak—to say *don't*, to say anything—and found she had no claim to. That was the arithmetic of her life: she had followed him, fought beside him, slept back-to-back against the cold with him, and none of it purchased the right to stand between a man and his homecoming.

So it wasn't she who spoke.

It was the thing in her cellar. The sleeping thing. It rose up her throat like cold water and put words in her mouth that she did not choose:

"He'll say yes," she told Corvane, "because he's better than you. Because forgiveness is easy for people who haven't had themselves hollowed out and sold back piece by piece."

Corvane's gaze slid to her, and the smile changed—it became, briefly, genuine, and that was infinitely worse. "There she is."

"What?" Wren's voice cracked on the word.

"You want to know why you can't feel, girl? Why you've spent your whole life stealing other people's memories like a beggar pressing her face to lit windows?" He took a step toward her, and the hunters let him. "Because there's almost nothing left in there to feel *with*. I know. I emptied you myself."

* * *

The world went very quiet and very large.

"You're lying," Wren said. But even as she said it, the cellar door in her was open now, and things were pouring through it—not memories, she had no memories, but the *shapes* where memories should have been. Absences with edges. She could feel them the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

"Nineteen years ago," Corvane said, conversational now, merciless in his gentleness, "there was a girl in Vaeloria's last surviving village. Daughter of a keeper-in-hiding. When the purge reached her family, the girl saw everything—her parents, her neighbors, the shrine burned with the elders locked inside. Too young to kill quietly, too old to forget. So I performed a extraction. Total. Every memory of Vaeloria, every memory of her family, every memory of the fire—" he paused, savoring, "—and every memory of me, standing in her doorway with the torch. I left her the skill, of course. Memory-thieves aren't born; they're *made*. I needed an instrument who could recover the map if it ever surfaced. Someone with no past to compromise her. Someone hungry enough to take any job."

"No." Wren was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. "No, I remember my mother. I remember—I remember a kitchen, I remember—"

"A reconstruction. Fragments you stole from others and grafted onto the wound, because a hollow thing cannot bear being hollow. Check them. Go on." His voice dropped to almost a whisper. "You've always known they were borrowed. You told yourself you were careful. You weren't. You were grieving."

She checked them. Standing in the ruined amphitheater with the thirteenth moon less than a day from rising, Wren went through her most precious possessions—the mother's laugh, the kitchen with its copper pots, the lullaby in a language she'd never identified—and found, for the first time, the seams. Stitch-marks. Other people's textures wearing her mother's face. Nineteen years of scavenged love, and not one thread of it hers.

She had built herself out of stolen goods. Corvane hadn't just taken her past. He had taught her to counterfeit one, and let her live in the forgery, and sent her out into the world to do his work while she starved.

Beside her, Quentin had gone very still. Not calm-still. Storm-still. The kind of stillness the air has in the last second before lightning. "You made her," he said, "to hunt me."

"I made her to hunt the map. Finding you was a bonus." Corvane turned back to Quentin, patient as erosion. "So. Now you understand the full ledger. Seal the door, take your pardon, and the girl goes free too—her debts cancelled, her crimes pardoned alongside yours. Refuse, and I take the map from your corpse, seal the door myself, and she hangs in the capital square by week's end as the thief who murdered a crown courier. Those are the terms, Ashgrove. Both roads end with the door closed. Only one ends with you both alive."

And there it was. The trap with no floor.

Wren watched Quentin look at her, and she watched the offer do its final work—not the pardon, not the restoration, but *this*: the chance to buy her freedom with his surrender. She watched him begin to weigh his inheritance against her neck in a hangman's rope, and she understood with perfect, horrible clarity that he would pay. That was who he was. He had refused to burn civilians; he would not hesitate to bury a kingdom to save one thief.

Which meant she had to make sure he never got the chance.

"Say yes," Wren said.

Quentin turned to her, stricken. "Wren—"

"*Say yes.* Take the pardon. Take the pretty life." She stepped forward, between him and Corvane, and pulled the map from his hands—or tried to; his grip tightened, and for a moment they held the thing together, its script pulsing faintly against their joined fingers. "Because I know what you're doing, Quentin Ashgrove. You're going to trade a kingdom for me, and you'll spend the rest of your life being kind about it, and I will spend the rest of mine knowing I was bought with the same coin Corvane used to buy me the first time." Her voice was shaking. She let it shake. She was done performing steadiness. "I have nothing. You understand? Nothing. No mother, no kitchen, no lullaby. Everything I am is theft and scaffolding. But the door down there—" she pointed toward the dark heart of the buried capital, where the ground itself seemed to hold its breath— "*that* remembers me. My real name is in that city. My mother's face. The fire. Even the part where *he* stood in the doorway. I want it back, Quentin. All of it. Even the worst of it. Especially the worst of it."

"It could break you," he whispered. "Total recall, after nineteen years empty—it could break anyone."

"Then it breaks me as *me*." She wrenched the map free—his fingers opened, whether from surprise or permission she never learned—and held it up between herself and the Inquisitor like a lantern. "You want to know the difference between us, Corvane? You emptied me and called it mercy. He offered to fill me and called it freedom. But nobody gets to decide what goes in my head but me. Not you. Not him. Not even the door."

Above them, the clouds tore open.

Not rain—*moonlight*, the thirteenth moon arriving early over the mountain shoulder, full and white and vast, and where its light struck the map the Vaelorian script ignited, every hidden road blazing at once, and beneath their feet the whole buried city answered: a low harmonic rising through the stone, through their bones, through nineteen years of Wren's engineered emptiness.

Corvane's smile finally died. "Hunters," he said quietly. "Take them."

Quentin's hands came up, and the sky answered.

* * *

Lightning split the amphitheater's ancient roof, and in its white flash Wren saw everything at once: six hunters drawing steel, Corvane stepping back into shadow with his terms still hanging unclaimed in the air, and Quentin—storm-wrapped, terrible, glorious—turning to look at her across the burning map.

"Thirteen moons," he shouted over the thunder. "We run *now*."

She ran.
