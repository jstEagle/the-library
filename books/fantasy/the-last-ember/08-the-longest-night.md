## 8. The Longest Night

The moon rose the color of a wound.

Quentin felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, a hum in his blood like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Thirteen moons of waiting had come down to this: a silver disc climbing over the shattered spine of Vaeloria's capital, pouring its light into streets that had been empty for a generation and were, tonight, about to be full of soldiers.

"Quentin." Wren's voice came from the shadow of a collapsed archway, low and urgent. "They're in the lower city. Torches. Hundreds."

He crouched beside her and looked down through a broken colonnade at what had once been the Avenue of Lanterns. Torchlight crawled along it now like fire through a dry field—Corvane's hunters moving in disciplined columns, their armor catching the moonlight in cold flashes. Somewhere among them rode the Inquisitor himself, and Quentin could feel him too, the way one feels a change in weather: that familiar, oily wrongness in the air. Corvane carried stolen memories the way other men carried knives.

"The door's beneath the Ember Hall," Quentin said. "Half a mile of open ground between us and it."

"Then we don't take open ground." Wren's fingers found his wrist—her touch always carried a faint static, the residue of every memory she'd ever taken—and pressed something into his palm. A sliver of pale stone. "I stole this off a scout an hour ago. It's a warding token. It'll blind their trackers to you for maybe twenty minutes."

"And you?"

Her smile was thin as paper. "I never needed blinding. Nobody remembers me long enough to track me." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. Eight days on the Weeping Steppe had cracked something open in her, and she hadn't bothered to seal it again. "Twenty minutes, Quentin. The door opens when the moon touches the spire. Not before, not after. If you miss it—"

"I know." Thirteen more moons. Thirteen more moons of running, of Halvard dead in the shrine dust, of Vaeloria's memories screaming beneath the stone while Corvane tightened the crown's grip on a lie two nations had been fed since before Quentin was born.

He almost told her then. What he'd realized in the Hall of his ancestors, standing inside his great-grandmother's memory of the massacre—the thing the storm had shown him, the truth about what claiming the throne would cost. But the torches were spreading, splitting into hunting parties, and there was no time for anything but movement.

"Wren." He caught her hand before she slipped away. "If I don't—"

"Don't." Her voice frayed. "You don't get to say it. You get to *do* it. That's the whole point of you."

Then she was gone, dissolving into the dark between the ruins like water into sand, and Quentin ran the other way—toward the heart of the city, toward the storm that was already gathering in his chest like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Wren had fought before. She wanted no credit for pretending otherwise.

But she had never fought *like this*—not through burning streets, not against men who moved in threes with silver manacles and nets woven with memory-iron, not with her own name rattling loose in her skull where Corvane had hollowed it out years ago. She came over a fallen wall and put her knife through the first hunter's thigh before he finished turning, took his partner's crossbow bolt across the shoulder because she saw it a half-second late, and rolled under the second bolt because rage sharpened everything.

Rage. That was new too. She'd spent her whole life hungry—stealing other people's joys and griefs because she had none of her own, filling herself up like a leaky jar. And now here was Quentin Ashgrove, half a city away, about to give away the only inheritance anyone had ever offered her a share of, and she found she was *furious* about it. Furious enough to be brave.

"Hold!" someone shouted ahead. "She's one of Corvane's—she's the thief!"

"She's *nothing of yours*," Wren snarled, and the memory she'd taken from a dying soldier on the Steppe rose up in her hands—a swordsman's thirty years of drill, of footwork, of killing—and she used all of it at once.

They broke around her like water around a stone. She gave ground the way she gave everything: reluctantly, and only to take more later. Behind her, above the rooftops, the sky began to bruise.

* * *

Quentin stood in the ruined nave of the Ember Hall and let go of eight years of holding back.

He had spent his whole exile rationing himself. Small storms. Careful winds. Enough rain to fill smugglers' cisterns, enough thunder to scatter a patrol, never—*never*—the fullness of what lived in his blood, because the last time he'd shown the crown what an Ashgrove could do, they had burned his family for it. But the moon was touching the spire now, and below his feet the door was waking, and he could hear Vaeloria's memories pressing against their prison like voices against glass.

So he opened.

It did not feel like calling a storm. It felt like *being* one. Lightning answered him out of a cloudless sky—the thirteenth moon's own light bent into white veins across the black—and thunder rolled through the capital with such force that walls which had stood a century of neglect finally lay down and died. Rain came sideways. Wind screamed down the Avenue of Lanterns and tore the torches from the hunters' hands, plunging the lower city into strobing darkness.

And in that darkness, Quentin walked toward the stair beneath the altar, lit by every strike, crowned by every crash, the last ember-keeper going down to the door his ancestors had died keeping.

The Inquisitor's voice reached him even through the storm. Not shouted—*placed*, the way Corvane placed everything, gently and precisely, inside the ear.

"You cannot carry them all, boy. Even your blood wasn't built for that weight."

Quentin didn't answer. There would be time for words at the threshold. Or there wouldn't, and the storm would say what he couldn't.

* * *

Wren reached the Ember Hall with blood in her left boot and someone else's swordsmanship singing in her arms, and stopped dead in the doorway—because the nave was full of Corvane's elite, a dozen of them, manacles out, and beyond them Quentin was descending the stairs like a man walking into the sea.

She did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat. Twelve against one. No time.

So she did what she did best. She reached into the nearest hunter's mind and *took*—not his skill this time, not his strength. His orders. His certainty. His absolute conviction that the woman in the doorway was one of theirs, sent by the Inquisitor himself to guard the rear. She poured it into the others like wine into cups, and for three precious seconds twelve trained killers believed her completely.

Three seconds was enough to kill four of them.

After that it was noise and iron and lightning through the broken roof, and Wren fighting not like a thief anymore but like a woman who had finally found one thing she refused to lose. A blade opened her cheek. She broke a wrist taking the blade. Someone grabbed her hair; she drove her elbow back until she felt ribs go, spun, slashed, ran—not past them now, *through* them, leaving a lane of groaning bodies and stolen courage behind her.

The stairs swallowed her. Below, the storm's roar faded to a heartbeat, and she heard Quentin's voice, raw and echoing:

"—and I'm telling you the same thing I told you eight years ago, Corvane. *No.*"

* * *

The chamber beneath the capital was round and vast, its walls carved with ten thousand faces—Vaeloria's remembered dead, watching. And in the center stood the door.

Not a door of wood or stone. A door of gathered dark, tall as three men, humming with held-back light, and before it stood Corvane in his grey coat, unhurried, unburned, his stolen memories layered around him like armor plate.

"Pardon," the Inquisitor said softly. "Restoration. Your family's name cleared, your lands returned, a seat at the king's right hand. All you must do is walk away and let me seal it. Let the lie stay comfortable. Let sleeping nations lie."

Behind him, at the top of the stairs, Wren froze—because Corvane turned his head and smiled at her, and it was the smile from her oldest, emptiest memory, the one face she could never steal back.

"Ah," he said. "And here is my finest work, come home."

The moon touched the spire. The door began to open. And Quentin, with his whole storm held in his fists, stepped forward.
