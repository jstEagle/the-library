## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The fox was dying at the edge of the world, and Mara Villalobos was the only one in the valley who knew it.

She had felt it the way she felt everything — not as a thought but as a pressure behind her ribs, a thin silver thread of pain that did not belong to her. All day she had carried it through the terraced fields, through the evening meal, through her grandmother's long grace said over barley bread, smiling when she was meant to smile, while somewhere beyond the ward-line something small and red and clever lay bleeding its warmth into the frost-hardened grass. The thread pulled at her like a fisherman's line. By the time the last light left the western peaks, she could bear it no longer.

She told her grandmother she was going to check the snares. Her grandmother, whose eyes had gone cloudy as river ice these past two years, only nodded and said, "Wear your hood, child. There's a wrongness in the air tonight." Mara kissed her papery cheek and did not say: I always wear my hood when I go to do forbidden things. The hood was for hiding from people. It was the dark she needed for hiding from herself.

The valley of Vell was shaped like a cupped hand, and Mara's family held the smallest finger of it — the low eastern slopes where the barley grew thin and the sheep grew fat and nobody asked too many questions about the third daughter who spent more time with animals than with people. From the ridge path she could see the whole valley laid out below her: the slate roofs of Hollowmere, the elder hall with its roof of living turf, the smoke rising straight up in the windless cold, and all around it, ringing it like a wedding band, the faint blue shimmer of the wards.

Everyone in Vell knew what the wards were for. They kept out the wilds. They kept out the weather that came down off the outer mountains carrying things no valley child should see. And most of all — though this was never said aloud, only breathed, only implied by the way elders' voices dropped — they kept magic out. Magic could not follow across the ward-line. That was the whole point of the valley. That was why their great-grandparents had built it, or so the story went: a country within a country, a clean room swept free of enchantment, where a person could live an honest life measured in harvests and generations and nothing else.

Mara had been born inside that promise. Mara had broken it before she was old enough to know there was anything to break.

Because the gift hadn't come *into* her. It had simply always been there, the way hearing is there, the way balance is there. She had never once called an animal to her; they had simply always been speaking, a low constant murmur under the world, and at some point — she couldn't remember when — she had stopped being able to ignore it. The hens complained about the order of the roost. The old draft horse mourned a companion dead nine years. The sheep were philosophers of tedium, the goats were anarchists, and the bees sang the only truly beautiful songs she'd ever heard, hymns to warmth and queen and distance, mathematics made of hunger.

She had never told anyone. Not her mother, not her sisters, not old Sef the shepherd who sometimes looked at her too long. Because everyone knew what happened to beast-speakers. Everyone knew about the Purging, three generations back, before even the elders were born — the ones who had been walked to the ward-line and sent out into the wilds with a loaf and a knife, because a girl who could hear what animals thought might, someday, hear something worse. The valley did not burn witches. The valley simply opened its hand and let them fall.

And so Mara had learned to live doubled, one self above the ground and one below it, smiling at market and weeping privately over a lame ewe, until the doubling felt less like deceit than like breathing.

The thread pulled her east, up along the sheep paths, past the last of the boundary cairns, to the place where the grass changed.

You could always tell the ward-line by the grass. Inside it, the valley grass grew tame and even, mowed by centuries of sheep. Outside it began the true meadow — taller, darker, threaded with flowers Mara had no names for, growing in patterns that hurt the eye if you looked too long. The line itself was invisible except at dusk and dawn, when the wards woke and shimmered, and right now, in the deepening blue of evening, it glowed faintly all along the horizon like a ring of distant aurora, humming a note too low for ears and exactly right for teeth.

The fox lay twenty paces beyond it, in the tall grass, in the dark.

Mara stood at the line and hesitated, as she always hesitated, one foot on the tame grass and one on the wild. Crossing wasn't forbidden for her body — children gathered mushrooms beyond the line all summer, under watchful eyes. But tonight, alone, at night, to speak to something — that was a different arithmetic. If any watcher saw her lips moving in the dark, mouthing words to a wild creature—

*No one is watching*, said the thread behind her ribs, which was not the fox's voice yet, only her own guilt wearing the shape of permission. *No one ever watches. No one has watched you in eleven years.*

She crossed.

The tall grass closed over her like water. The cold came up through her boots. She found him by scent before sight — blood and musk and the particular sweet-rot smell of a wound gone bad — and then by the dim ember-glow of his eyes, open, watching her come.

*Well*, the fox said, when she knelt beside him in the frost. His voice was dry leaves and iron filings, weaker than any fox voice she'd heard before, frayed at every edge. *The valley sends me its strangest visitor. A soft-footed thing with a woman's shape.*

"I'm Mara," she said, and then hated herself for it, because he didn't care about her name. Names were a people-thought. But the words came out anyway, the way they always did, the human habit of introduction asserting itself even here at the rim of the world.

*I know,* the fox said. *You have been leaking grief into the meadow for years, girl. Every lamb you lost, every dog that died old, I have tasted it on the wind. You grieve loudly, for someone so quiet.* He coughed, a small terrible sound. *I have been dying since morning. A snare? No. Something older than a snare. My legs stopped answering me at noon and the world started tasting wrong. Tin. The whole meadow tastes of tin.*

Mara looked at his hindquarters and saw it — not a wound, exactly, but a fading. His fur there was going grey, then transparent, then to nothing, as if the flesh were being erased from the tail-end forward, a sentence being rubbed out from its final word.

"What happened to you?" she whispered. "What *is* that?"

*That,* said the fox, with the flat patience of the very old and the very tired, *is forgetting.*

Above them, the sky flickered.

Mara looked up. The stars over the valley were thick as spilled salt — she had counted them a hundred nights from this very ridge, found the Weaver, the Antlered One, the Seven Sisters of the Threshing Floor, all the constellations the elders used to mark the turning year. Tonight the Weaver was missing her loom-star. Just — gone. Not fallen, not clouded. Gone, and the patch of black where it had hung looked less like empty sky than like a hole cut in cloth, an absence with edges.

"Fox," she said slowly, "was there a star there yesterday?"

*Was there a star there yesterday,* the fox repeated, and something in the repetition made her skin crawl, because it wasn't mockery. It was genuine uncertainty. He squinted upward with eyes gone milky at the rims. *I remember stars. I remember counting them with my mother, the spring I learned to hunt. I remember the Weaver. I don't remember whether the Weaver had six stars or seven. Do you?*

"Seven," Mara said. "Six now."

*Then the country lost a star today,* the fox said, *and tomorrow it will lose another, and neither of us will be able to say what we lost, because the losing takes the memory of the thing with it.* He dragged himself half upright, forelegs trembling, and fixed her with his ember gaze. *Girl. Soft-footed Mara of the loud grief. Listen to me, because I am spending my last hours on you and I would rather spend them sleeping.*

The wards hummed. Somewhere far below, a dog in Hollowmere began to bark, then another, then a dozen — not the alarm-bark of strangers but the low, ceaseless, miserable barking of dogs who can smell something coming and cannot say what.

*Outside this valley,* the fox said, *the country is unwriting itself. The rivers first — the Ald went strange last month, running uphill some mornings, running backwards, running into itself. Then the roads forgot where they went. Then the villages. There was a town called Brassenholt, on the moor — I hunted its middens as a young fox, fat pickings, fat stupid pigeons. Can you tell me what a Brassenholt is?*

"No," Mara said. The word came out very small. "Should I?"

*Neither can I,* said the fox. *That is what frightens me, girl. I am a fox. We remember everything — every den, every kill, every winter, back and back, mother to cub, the whole red history. Memory is our country, the only one we hold. And mine is developing holes.* He looked down at his fading haunch, at the grass showing through where fur should be, and for the first time his voice cracked into something young. *It got past the wards somehow. Whatever is eating the world out there — it reached in here, to me, and it is eating. Slowly. But eating.*

Mara's hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the frost. She wanted to laugh at him. She wanted to run home and bar the door and tell herself it was a sick animal's fever-talk. She did neither, because the sky chose that moment to lose another star.

The Antlered One's brow-tine went out — a soundless extinguishing, like a candle pinched between wet fingers — and this time she *felt* it, felt it in the silver thread behind her ribs, a tiny lurch, a sensation exactly like stepping onto a stair that isn't there. Below in the valley the dogs all went silent at once, which was somehow worse than the barking.

"The wards," Mara breathed. "Can the wards—"

*Look at them,* said the fox.

She turned to look at the shimmering ring that had guarded every day of her life, and her stomach dropped. The aurora-blue glow was stuttering. Flickering. Along the eastern arc, where it ran nearest the high moor, the light was guttering like a lamp running dry, and through the gaps in its shine she could see — clearly, impossibly — the outer mountains. She had lived nineteen years within sight of that horizon and had never once seen the outer mountains. The maps in the elder hall ended at the wards. The teachers taught that beyond the line was mist, was chaos, was nothing worth the naming. And now the mist had thinned and the mountains stood revealed, black against the starless patches, real as teeth.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of it. They were beautiful, and she had been lied to about them her entire life, and some traitorous chamber of her heart leapt at the sight of them like a bird at an open window.

*There it is,* the fox murmured, watching her face. *I saw it come into your eyes just now. The wanting. Good. You'll need it.*

"Need it for what?" Mara whispered.

But the fox didn't answer, because the night broke.

It began at the northern arc — a single bright crack, thin as a hair, splitting the ward-light vertically. Then another, south. Then a dozen, everywhere, a spiderweb of fractures racing around the whole ring of the valley, and the hum rose in pitch until Mara felt it screaming in her fillings, in her jaw, in the hollows of her bones, and the dogs of Hollowmere began to howl in chorus, and the horses screamed in their paddocks, and the bees — she could feel the bees, miles away, pouring out of their hives into the freezing dark in a river of confused gold.

*Down,* the fox said. *Flat, girl—*

The wards shattered.

Not faded. Not failed. *Shattered* — the whole ring bursting outward and inward at once like a struck wineglass, and for one suspended heartbeat the fragments hung in the air all around the valley's rim, millions of shards of blue light, each one holding a warped reflection of the valley inside it: roofs, fields, the elder hall, a girl kneeling in tall grass beside a dying fox. Mara saw herself in a hundred tiny skies.

Then the shards fell, all together, all at once, hissing into the grass and the soil and the slate roofs of Hollowmere, dissolving on contact into sparks, into motes, into nothing, and the valley of Vell stood open to the night for the first time in three hundred years.

The silence afterward was enormous. The hum was gone. The dogs had stopped. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath. Overhead, the sky went on quietly losing itself — another star, another, the Seven Sisters down to five, down to four — and Mara knelt in the frost of the wild meadow with her heart slamming against her ribs and discovered, to her shame and her wonder in equal measure, that she was not only afraid.

She was *exhilarated*. The window was open. The world was ending and the window was open and every honest bone in her body wanted to climb through it.

Beside her, the fox sighed, long and rattling. His eyes had begun to dim, but they still found hers, and there was something urgent gathering in what was left of their light.

*Something is coming through,* he said. *Through the breach. Can you feel it? No — of course you can't, not yet, you're only half-awake, poor thing, half of you still believes the valley's stories. But feel this:* he lifted his head with the last of his strength, nose working the wind from the north and the east, from the hole in the world. *Iron. And honey. And a heart beating in a tongue you've never heard — no. A tongue you've heard all your life, girl, and never once understood that it was yours.*

"Fox, you're raving—"

*I'm dying,* he corrected, almost cheerfully. *Raving is permitted. Look north. Look, Mara-of-the-loud-grief, and tell me what you see falling.*

She looked.

Far off, past the broken arc of the wards, past the black shoulder of the moor, something was dropping out of the starless sky — a streak of pale fire, slow, wobbling, wrong, like a lantern thrown down a well. It trailed sparks. It descended in long, staggering arcs, and as it fell it made a sound she felt rather than heard, a sound like a hive the size of a cathedral, a sound that set her teeth singing in harmony.

It went down beyond the eastern ridge, and the impact shivered up through her boot-soles, soft as a dropped book.

In the tall grass, the fox laid his head down at last, his ember eyes closing, his fading body settling into the frost like a leaf settling into water.

*Go and see,* he murmured, already mostly asleep, mostly elsewhere. *Go and see what the sky has sent you, vessel. And when you find it — mind the box. Mind what hums inside it. Some seeds are patient, and some doors, once opened—*

His voice unraveled mid-word. Not into silence — into something stranger, a thinning, as if each word were a step he was taking further away down a road she couldn't follow. The last thing she caught, faint as a star going out, was:

*—cannot be shut again.*

Mara knelt alone in the wild meadow, under a sky with holes in it, beside a fox whose outline had begun, gently, terribly, to blur at the edges like wet ink. Behind her lay the valley, dark and whole and suddenly naked without its ring of light. Ahead of her, beyond the ridge, something was burning in the frost where it had fallen.

Her ribs ached. The silver thread in her chest — the one that had led her here, the one she had spent her whole life pretending was nothing — pulled, once, hard, toward the fallen light.

Mara stood up. She did not go home.

* * *

Later — much later, when she finally crept back through her own window with frost in her hair and a story ready about a lost ewe — she would try to count the stars from her bed and fail, and lie awake listening to the valley breathe, and understand that the world she had been born into had ended sometime between dusk and dawn, quietly, while everyone slept.

And far to the east, in a crater of scorched and steaming grass, a man who was barely alive curled himself around an iron box that hummed like a hive, and waited — without knowing for whom — to be found.
