## 2. A Stranger Falls Through

The stars did not come back.

Mara had waited until the grey hour before dawn, when even the elders' lamps guttered low and the valley held its breath between one day and the next, but the sky above the ward-line stayed black and wrong — not clouded, not dimmed, simply *emptied*, as if someone had reached up with a great soft hand and wiped each light away like smudges from a slate. She had counted them going out. Eleven. Then seven. Then none at all, in a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

She had run home through the wet grass with the fox's last words still ringing in her skull — *it begins tonight, little speaker* — and had lain awake in her loft above the goat pen, staring at the rafters, listening to the valley's animals panic in the dark. The dogs would not stop whining. The horses screamed once, a sound she had never heard from them before, high and human and terrible. The bees in Tam Vetch's hives hummed out of rhythm, a broken song, and Mara had pressed her hands over her ears and whispered to them across half a mile of night: *be still, be still, be still.* They had quieted. They always quieted for her. It was the only mercy of her forbidden gift, that the beasts loved her back.

Now it was morning, or something shaped like morning, and the sky was the color of an old bruise, and Mara Villalobos was breaking every law her valley had ever written by walking toward the border instead of away from it.

* * *

The ward-line ran along the northern rim of the valley like a scar of standing stones — forty-one of them, moss-eaten, older than any elder could remember, each carved with spirals that no longer quite matched the spirals on their neighbors. Children were taught to fear them. Farmers were taught to turn their plows away from them. And anyone born with the beast-gift — the speaking, the hearing, the terrible open ear — was taught most of all to hide it, because magic could not follow past the wards, and what could not follow must not be invited.

Mara had spent twenty-three years being careful. Twenty-three years of closing her mouth around words meant for other throats, of nodding when the elders said *there is nothing beyond the rim, child, there has never been anything beyond the rim,* of pretending she could not feel the foxes thinking in the hedgerows or hear the crows conducting their dry parliament over the barley fields. Careful, careful, careful. Her grandmother had drilled it into her before she died: *A gift like yours is a door, Mara-girl, and doors swing both ways.*

But last night the stars had gone out, and this morning the wards were singing.

Not loudly. Not the way they sang during storms, a low grinding note that made teeth ache. This was thinner — a sound like glass under strain, like ice on a pond just before it gives. Mara felt it in her molars and behind her eyes, and the nearer she came to the stones, the more she understood that the sound was not the wards holding.

It was the wards failing.

She stopped thirty paces out, in the shadow of a lightning-split oak, and looked.

The nearest stone had cracked. Not chipped, not weathered — *cracked*, a jagged seam running from crown to root, and through the seam leaked a light that had no business existing in daylight: a slow silver shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, like moonlight poured through a keyhole. Beyond the stone, the world outside the valley should have been there — the gray hills, the pine dark, the road that led, everyone said, nowhere, because there was nowhere to lead to.

Instead there was a hole.

Not darkness. A hole. A place where looking made her mind slide off, where her eye found no surface and no depth and no distance, only an absence shaped vaguely like the rest of the world, the way a glove is shaped like a hand. It hurt to look at. It hurt worse than that — it *itched*, deep in the animal part of her brain, and every instinct she owned screamed at her to turn around, walk home, bar the door, forget.

Mara had never been good at forgetting.

"Hello?" she said softly, to the hole, to the hills, to whatever listened. Her voice sounded small and stupid against all that absence. "Is someone—"

The wards shrieked.

Every stone at once, a sound like a hundred wineglasses breaking inside her skull, and Mara dropped to her knees with her hands clapped over her ears as the silver shimmer along the crack flared white-hot — and then the air itself tore.

There was no better word for it. The space above the ward-line split along a seam she couldn't see, peeled back like skin, and through the wound in the world came a sound she would remember for the rest of her life: a rushing, roaring nothing, wind blowing from a country that no longer existed.

And then a man fell through it.

* * *

He did not fall so much as he was *spat* — tumbling end over end out of the tear like a stone from a sling, trailing threads of that silver light, and he hit the ground ten feet inside the ward-line with a sound that turned Mara's stomach. Wet. Final. The kind of sound bodies are not meant to make.

The tear sealed itself above him with a snap she felt in her teeth. The wards went silent. The bruise-colored sky hung there, empty of stars, empty of everything, and the man lay crumpled in the frost-killed grass like a puppet whose strings had been cut.

Mara should have run. Every law, every lesson, every careful year of her life said *run*. She was already composing the story she'd tell — *I heard a noise, I came to see, I swear I touched nothing* — and her legs had actually turned when the man moved.

One hand lifted off the ground. It was ruined — burned, or frostbitten, or both, the skin split and weeping — and it was wrapped around an iron box the size of a loaf of bread, chained to his wrist, and he dragged it against the earth as he tried to crawl, and the box—

The box *hummed*.

Mara heard it before she understood she was hearing it, a vibration more felt than heard, rising through the ground into her knees, her spine, her ribs. It was not mechanical. Nothing made by hands hummed like that. It was layered, alive, a thousand tiny voices braided into one note — and Mara knew that sound. She had grown up with that sound. It was the sound of a hive in high summer, bees working, bees *singing*, bees telling each other everything.

But there were no bees here. There was nothing here but a dying man and a box of iron, and the box was humming like ten thousand wings.

"No," Mara breathed. "No, no, no."

Because the valley had already noticed him.

She felt it happen — felt the terror move through the land like a wave through water. The crows went up off the barley first, hundreds of them, silent, which crows never were; a silent crow is a frightened crow, and these were terrified. Then the deer broke from the eastern wood in a stampede, running *away* from the border, away from the fallen man, white tails flashing. The dogs of Hollowmere began to howl in a register she'd never heard from them, grief-stricken and wild. In the goat pen behind her own house, half a mile off, she felt her own animals press themselves into the far corner of the fence and shake.

Everything with four legs or two wings was fleeing the stranger.

Everything except her.

Mara stood rooted in the shadow of the split oak, heart slamming, and watched the man drag himself another foot through the grass. He was young — younger than his ruin suggested, maybe her age, maybe less — dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, dressed in clothes that belonged to no tailor she knew: a coat stitched in patterns that hurt to follow, boots worn through at the sole, travel-worn past sense. Blood had soaked through his left side. His lips were cracked and gray. He got one knee under himself, collapsed, tried again, and made a sound — a word, maybe, or the ghost of one — and then his arm gave out and his face hit the dirt and he lay still, chest heaving, fingers still locked around the iron box.

And the box sang to her.

Not in words. In *meaning* — the way the bees spoke, the way the old mare had spoken when she was dying, the way all of them spoke: pressure and image and need, without grammar, without lies. The box's meaning crashed over her like cold water:

*Hold. Hold. Hold. Do not drop me. Do not let them take me. Hold.*

Mara took one step. Then another. The wards' silence pressed on her eardrums like deep water. Ten paces from the man she could smell him now — blood, smoke, and underneath it something green and impossibly sweet, like a garden after rain, like a flower she had no name for, growing somewhere in her oldest memories.

Five paces.

His eyes opened.

They were the color of river-stones, gray-green, fever-bright, and they fixed on her with an effort that seemed to cost him everything he had left. His mouth worked. No sound came. He tried again, and this time a rasp escaped, thin as paper — and it was not the valley tongue, nor anything like it, and yet Mara understood it perfectly, the way she understood the foxes, the way she understood the bees, because it was not a man's language at all.

It was the tongue of beasts.

*Little speaker,* the dying stranger said, in the voice of a wolf and the grammar of birdsong, *you hear me.*

Mara's whole body went cold. "I—" Her voice came out cracked. She knelt, slowly, hands visible, the way you'd approach a wounded animal, because that was clearly what he was. "I hear you."

Something moved through his face — relief, or fear, or both braided together. His ruined hand unclenched from the box a fraction, trembling. *They told me,* he rasped. *The last of them told me. Find the valley. Find the one who hears. Only she can—*

He coughed, and the cough tore something loose, and blood came up his throat and out over his chin, black in the strange light.

"Don't talk." Mara was beside him now, her hands hovering over his wounds without touching, useless, trained by a lifetime of hiding to keep her gifts small. But there was no hiding this. There was no explaining this. A man had fallen out of a hole in the world, bleeding and burning, and the entire valley's creatures had fled from him, and he spoke in the voice of animals, and she was kneeling in the open field where any watcher from the Council of Roots might crest the hill at any moment and see her bent over him like a mother over a foundling.

*The box,* he breathed. Not a request. An instruction, or a plea, or a warning — she couldn't tell. *Whatever happens. Whatever they offer you. The seed must not—*

"Seed?"

His eyes widened. Fever or horror, she couldn't say. He hadn't meant to say it. She watched him understand his mistake, watch the word *seed* hang in the air between them like a struck bell, and then his gaze slid past her shoulder to the ridge line, and every muscle in his broken body seized.

*They're coming,* he said. *Your watchers. I can smell them. Little speaker — they cannot find the box. They cannot know what I am. Swear it. Swear it in your own tongue, the true one, the one without lies—*

"I don't even know your name," Mara whispered.

Something almost like a smile touched his bloody mouth. *Neither do I,* he said. *Isn't that funny. Isn't that — I had it, I'm sure I had it—*

And his eyes rolled back, and he went down into the grass like a felled tree, and the iron box's hum climbed to a pitch that made Mara's vision blur, a hive on fire, ten thousand voices crying *hold, hold, hold* —

— and down the slope, three figures in the gray robes of the Council of Roots crested the hill, walking fast, and the eldest of them was already shouting her name.

* * *

Later — much later, after the lies, after the elders carried the unconscious stranger into the elder hall on a hurdle of woven ash branches while the whole of Hollowmere watched from behind shutters, after Mara swore with a straight face that she'd only heard a noise and come running like any good neighbor — later, she would lie awake in her loft and replay every moment of it, hunting for the seams in her own deception.

But kneeling in that field, with the Council bearing down and a dying man's blood soaking into her skirt, Mara Villalobos had exactly enough time to do one thing.

She put her hand flat on the iron box.

The humming rushed up her arm and settled into her chest like a second heartbeat, warm and wild and ancient, and beneath the ten thousand bee-voices she felt something else — something vast and sleeping and *green*, coiled tight in the iron dark, waiting with the patience of roots.

*Hold,* it said, in a voice like her grandmother's, like spring, like a country she had never seen and somehow mourned. *You're the one who holds.*

"Mara!" Elder Sef's voice, sharp with suspicion, twenty paces off. "Step away from that creature. *Now.*"

Mara lifted her hand. The hum faded to a thread only she could hear.

She stood, turned, and began to lie.
