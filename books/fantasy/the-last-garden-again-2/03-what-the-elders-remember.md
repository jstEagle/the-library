## 3. What the Elders Remember

Ilian woke to the smell of cedar smoke and the weight of being watched.

He knew the difference between kinds of watching — he had been trained in it, drilled in it, half his life spent learning how eyes worked when they thought themselves unseen. There was the casual watch of strangers, sliding over you like water. There was the wary watch of soldiers, measuring your hands and your distance to a blade. And then there was this: the patient, deliberate attention of people who had decided what you were before you opened your eyes, and were now merely waiting for you to confirm it.

He kept his breathing slow. Kept his eyes shut. Catalogued.

A bed beneath him — narrow, pallet-hard, but clean straw and good wool, which meant care rather than captivity. Bandages tight around his ribs, smelling of yarrow and something sharper underneath, something that stung faintly like lightning held in cloth. A fire somewhere to his left, small and disciplined; whoever tended it fed it one stick at a time, no waste. The room was large — he could hear the way sound came back to him off stone or old timber far above — and there were people in it. At least five. Six if you counted the one by the door who shifted her weight every forty breaths with the metronome patience of someone paid in duty rather than coin.

And beneath all of it, threaded through the cedar and the yarrow like a bass note too low for ears: the humming.

The box was here. Close. Three paces, maybe four. It had not stopped singing since it fell through the breach with him, since the stars went out and the sky peeled open and the world's oldest wound admitted him into a country that should have been impossible to enter. The hum sat in his blood now like a second pulse. He had stopped fighting it on the second night of travel, when exhaustion made argument pointless, and some traitorous corner of him had begun to find it — *comforting*. That was a word he refused to examine.

*Three paces,* he told himself. *They haven't taken it from you. Either they don't know what it is, or they know exactly what it is and are waiting.*

Neither possibility was comfortable.

"You breathe differently when you're awake." The voice was old and dry as autumn husks. "Have done since they carried you in. You might as well open your eyes, stranger. We've been polite long enough."

Ilian opened his eyes.

The elder hall was vast and low-beamed, built of timber so dark with age it looked carved from night, and every inch of it was *alive* in a way that raised the hair along his arms. Living wood grew up through the floor in pale columns and spread across the ceiling in green veins. Herbs hung in braids from the rafters. Roots — actual roots, white and tender — traced the walls in patterns too regular to be accident, and where they crossed, small lamps of glass glowed with a light that had no flame in it.

Six figures sat around the fire. Old, most of them, faces seamed and brown as walnut shells, dressed in undyed wool and bone beads. But age meant little here; he could feel the weight coming off them in layers, the way heat comes off stones at dusk. These were people who had spent their whole lives inside wards. He could smell the magic on them the way you smell a river on a fisherman — not power itself, just the long habit of living beside it.

The one who had spoken sat nearest: a woman thin as a winter branch, eyes the color of iron gone soft with rust. Elder Sef, another would say later — the sharp-voiced shadow from the border, though Ilian did not yet know her name. She studied him now without hurry, the way a farmer studies weather.

"There you are," she said. "The beast-girl vouches for you. That alone bought you a bed instead of a pit. Don't make her regret it."

"The girl," Ilian said. His voice came out scraped, ruined, two days of dust and dying in it. He swallowed and tried again. "Is she well?"

"She lied to us beautifully, if that's what you're asking." Sef's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Said you were a pilgrim fallen at the rim, said the box was her finding, said the fox was already dead so there was nothing to question. Three lies in ten breaths, each one smooth as river stone. The Council let it stand because the alternative was admitting our wards broke, and we are — " a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat — "not ready to admit that."

Around the fire, the other elders shifted. Not in anger. In something worse. In the particular stillness of people approaching a subject they have agreed, collectively, not to understand.

"Where am I?" Ilian asked. It was not the question burning him. It was the question that would get answers.

"The valley of Vessa-Rim," said an elder across the fire, a broad man with a voice like a landslide settling. "You'd know it if — " He stopped. Blinked, as if the sentence had slipped out of his grip. "Well. You'd know it."

"Would I?" Ilian kept his tone mild, curious, a traveler making conversation. Inside, something went very cold and very still. *There it is.* "I confess my memory of the last days is... poor. I was crossing the high country beyond the rim when the sky went wrong. When the stars — "

"When the stars went out," Sef finished for him. Flat. Final. "Yes. We saw. All of us saw. Every soul in this valley stood in their doorways and watched the heavens go dark star by star, and then watched our wards shatter like dropped crockery, and then watched them *knit themselves closed again* by morning, whole and unbroken, as if nothing had happened at all." Her rust-colored eyes fixed on him. "So you'll forgive us if we don't quite believe your story of a simple pilgrimage. Things do not fall *through* sound wards, stranger. Wards that break once and heal by dawn are wards that were opened. From outside. By something that wanted in."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the green ceiling, leaves rustled though there was no wind.

"I wanted in," Ilian said carefully, "to a valley. I've never heard of Vessa-Rim before three nights ago."

It was a test and he knew it even as he spoke it, and the elders' reaction confirmed everything the box had been humming warnings about since the breach. They did not look surprised. They did not look skeptical. They looked — *relieved*, briefly, horribly relieved, the way people relax when a madman confirms the world is sane again — and then the relief curdled into confusion, because the words didn't fit anything, because *valley* was a word with edges and theirs had none.

"Beyond the rim," said the broad man slowly, as if translating from a language only he half remembered, "is..." He gestured. His hand described a shape and then simply stopped, fingers curling closed like a flower at dusk. "High country," he said. "Forest. The rim wards. That's — that's what's beyond the rim."

"That's *what* is beyond the rim?" Ilian asked softly.

"That's what's beyond the rim," the man repeated, with the dogged certainty of someone reciting a lesson learned so young it had grown into the bones.

Ilian sat up despite his ribs, and the room let him, and the watcher by the door drifted closer with the unhurried menace of a sheepdog that has never needed to bite.

"May I see your maps?" he asked.

* * *

They brought him to the map room an hour past noon, walking slowly because his body insisted on it, flanked by Sef and the broad man — Torvun, he learned, first among the Council of Roots — and followed at a distance by the door-watcher, whose name nobody offered and whose footsteps made no sound at all, which told Ilian more about the valley than any map could.

The map room was a rotunda at the heart of the elder hall, domed in living wood, lit by those same flameless glass lamps. And the maps —

The maps were beautiful. He had seen cartography from a dozen schools and traditions, and these were masterwork: inked on treated bark-cloth and cured hide, mountains rendered in strokes fine as eyelashes, rivers shaded in gradients of blue so subtle they seemed to flow as he watched. Generations of work. Love, unmistakably, in every line.

And every single one of them ended at the valley's rim.

Not faded there. Not torn there. *Ended.* The ward-line was drawn in silver leaf, a perfect circle, and outside it the parchment was blank — not empty the way untouched paper is empty, but empty the way a sentence is empty after a full stop. Deliberate. Finished. As if the cartographers had set down their brushes and said: *this is the world, complete.*

"This one," Torvun said, unrolling the oldest with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Its silver rim had gone brown with centuries of careful hands. "Drawn nine hundred years ago, in the time of the First Root. See how the wards were newer then? Thicker line. Prideful work." He smiled fondly. "We've always known our place, stranger. That's the secret of the valley's peace. Other lands squabble over borders because they imagine borders can move. Ours cannot. Ours are drawn in the sky itself."

"And beyond the silver line?"

"Beyond is beyond." Torvun said it gently, patiently, as one explains rain to a child. "The high country. The forest. Places for beasts and weather, not for people. No country lies out there, friend. No cities, no roads, no names. If there were — " he tapped the blank parchment, and his finger made no sound, and Ilian felt the silence of that tap like a physical blow — "it would be *here*, wouldn't it? Ink doesn't lie. Ink remembers."

*I remember,* the box hummed from its wrapping against Ilian's chest — they had let him keep it, wrapped in wool, declared a reliquary of his faith, which was almost funny enough to laugh at — *I remember. I remember all of it. Nine hundred years and more. I remember the country that drew these maps' grandmothers' grandmothers out of its own flesh and locked the door behind them.*

He said nothing. He breathed through the hum and kept his face arranged in the expression of a concussed pilgrim trying to follow a kind man's geography lesson.

But his mind was moving now, fast and cold, laying pieces down the way he'd been trained to lay them in the order's warrens beneath the dead Garden: *Nine hundred years. The order's records say the Garden fell eight hundred years ago — say the remnant fled north and west and scattered. Say nothing of a valley sealed a century earlier, say nothing of wards woven to hold in not enemies but memory itself. These people aren't ignorant. They're curated. Someone built a museum of forgetting and put a whole people inside it and told them the walls were the edge of the world.*

And the wards had broken. For one night, one terrible night, the museum doors had swung wide — and the world outside had come pouring in as a falling man and a humming box, and heaven help him, as a fox.

*And the girl,* he thought, and stopped there, because thinking about the girl required thinking about why the box had gone quiet in her presence except for that vast sleeping greenness, and thinking about *that* required believing things the order had sent him to verify and he was not yet ready to believe.

"Stranger." Sef had drifted to his elbow, silent as the door-watcher. She was looking not at him but at the oldest map, at the browned silver circle. "You said you crossed high country. You said the stars went out where you walked." Her voice was quiet enough that Torvun, rolling maps at the far side of the rotunda, could not hear. "Tell me what you saw. Not what you'll tell the Council. What you saw."

He weighed her. She weighed him back. Two professionals, recognizing each other across a gap of nine hundred years and a wall of forgetting.

"I saw a village at dusk," Ilian said, "that existed only at dusk. I saw a river running uphill, patiently, as if it had forgotten downhill. I saw a country eating itself, elder. Unwriting itself. Whatever is beyond your silver line isn't empty. It's *dying*. It has been dying for a long time, and three nights ago it started dying faster, and something in this valley — " he touched the wrapped box through the wool, lightly, casually, a man adjusting a bandage — "or something *from* it — may be the only reason it hasn't finished."

Sef said nothing for a long moment. The flameless lamps buzzed faintly, like trapped bees.

"The moon," she said finally, "will be full thirteen times before midwinter. An ill omen. Thirteenths always are." She turned away, then paused. "The beast-girl will bring you food tonight. She insists. The Council thinks it's guilt. I think it's neither." Her rust eyes cut back to him, sharp as a reaping hook. "Whatever you're carrying, pilgrim — and you *are* carrying something, I've buried too many liars not to know the shape of one — whatever it is, decide quickly whether the girl is part of your purpose or an obstacle to it. Because she has decided you're neither, and she's wrong both ways, and wrong girls die fast in years like the one that's coming."

She left him alone with the maps.

Ilian stood a long time in the lamplit rotunda, surrounded by nine hundred years of beautiful, loving, murderous silence. Around him the blank parchment circles gleamed, ring within ring within ring, older maps nested inside newer ones, each generation drawing the world a little smaller, a little safer, a little deader — until the newest map, barely fifty years old, showed a valley so tightly circled in silver that the space inside the line was barely wider than the line itself.

*They're not preserving a country,* he thought. *They're embalming one. And the embalming is nearly done.*

His hand found the box through the wool. The hum rose to meet him, warm, insistent, ancient — and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, that vast green patience turned over in its sleep and *waited*.

*I was sent to carry you home,* he told it silently. *Home to what's left of the order, to the vaults, to the ritual, to the resurrection of the thing that made and unmade countries. That was the mission. That was the truth.*

He looked at the blank parchment. At the silver lines. At the newest map, where the world had been drawn down to almost nothing at all.

*And I was sent to retrieve a vessel,* said the other truth, the buried one, the one he had sworn on his knees beneath the ash trees never to speak aloud. *And the vessel walks this valley wearing a liar's face and a beast-speaker's gift, and does not know what she is.*

Outside, dusk was falling, and somewhere between the elder hall and the goat-pens a girl was lying to her elders again on his behalf, smoothly, bravely, for reasons she couldn't possibly understand.

Guilt settled into Ilian like cold water finding its level — and then, slowly, deliberately, hardened into something with edges.

Resolve.

He had until the moon's thirteenth turn.
