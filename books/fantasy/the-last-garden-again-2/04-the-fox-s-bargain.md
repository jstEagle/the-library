## 4. The Fox's Bargain

The fox did not die.

Mara had carried it back across the ward-line in the gray hour before dawn, wrapped in her grandmother's shawl, its ribs rising and falling like a bellows with a split seam. She had expected the elders to find her — had almost wanted them to, in some reckless corner of herself that was tired of crouching and whispering and pretending her whole life was a small dark room with no windows. But no one had come. The valley slept the way it always slept: deeply, obediently, as if sleep were a duty owed to something larger than rest.

She hid the fox in the root cellar beneath the drying shed, where her family once stored turnips and where she now stored everything else that could not be explained. A blanket of wool. A dish of water. A scrap of salted mutton stolen from the smokehouse, which the fox ignored for two full days before eating it all at once, ravenously, on the third morning, as though it had only just remembered what hunger was for.

That was when Mara understood the animal was not merely recovering its body. It was recovering itself — piece by piece, the way a man might gather scattered coins from a floor, unsure at first which ones were his.

On the fourth night she climbed down into the cellar with a lantern and sat cross-legged in the dirt, and the fox opened one amber eye and looked at her with an attention so complete it made her breath catch.

*You came back,* it said.

Its voice in her mind was not what she expected. Beast-speech never was; it arrived less as words than as weather, a pressure of meaning against the inside of her skull. But this fox's voice had edges. It had irony in it, and old grief, and a dry humor worn smooth like a river stone. It sounded, absurdly, like an aunt who had outlived everyone worth scolding.

"I said I would," Mara answered aloud, then caught herself and shaped the reply in beast-tongue instead, the soft clicking grammar of it filling her mouth like cold water. *I keep my promises.*

*Do you.* The fox's ear twitched. *The valley folk keep their promises too. That is the trouble with them. They promise to forget, and they do.*

Mara's hands went still in her lap. Outside, far above the cellar, the night sky hung wrong — she had seen it through the shed window on her way down. The stars were still going out. One every night now, precise as a debt collector, each extinguishment marked by a faint sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard, a sound like a page being turned by an enormous patient hand.

"What is happening?" she asked. "Out there. Beyond the wards."

The fox was silent long enough that the lantern flame leaned and steadied twice. Then it dragged itself upright — slowly, joints cracking like green wood — and sat facing her, tail curled over its ruined foreleg, and looked at her with both eyes at last.

*You truly don't know,* it said, and there was no mockery in it now, only a kind of weary wonder. *They took even that from you. Very well, daughter-of-nowhere. I will tell you what I am, and then I will tell you what your world is, and then we will see whether you still wish to know more.*

* * *

It told her its name first, or rather it gave her the shape of its name, because fox-names are not sounds but histories: *Vixen-who-buried-three-litters-under-the-white-hawthorn-and-outlived-the-flood.* It had been born beyond the wards, in a country whose name it spoke and which slid off Mara's mind like water off feathers — she heard it, she knew she heard it, and ten heartbeats later she could not have repeated it to save her life.

"That happens," she whispered. "With outside things. The names won't stay."

*Nothing outside stays,* the fox said. *That is the sickness. Listen.*

And it told her.

It told her of a country called — and here it paused, tasting the word, knowing she would lose it — a country of orchards and river-towns and nine cities, ringed by mountains, anchored at its heart by a Garden so old that the country had grown around it the way a tree grows around a nail driven into sapling wood. The Garden was not a garden, exactly. It was a remembering-place, a living archive, rooted in the land and feeding on it and feeding it in return. Every name, every boundary, every season kept its shape because the Garden held the shape. Pull the nail, and the tree comes apart.

*Someone pulled the nail,* the fox said flatly. *Or is pulling it. Slowly. From the outside in. The stars go out because they are being unwritten — do you understand? Not hidden. Not clouded. Unwritten. Each one is a promise the sky made, and someone is crossing them off.*

"The wards," Mara said. Her voice came out thin. "The valley wards. The elders say they've stood since—"

*Since forever, yes. They say that because forever is the only length of time the valley permits.* The fox limped a half-step closer, and its amber eyes caught the lantern light and threw it back doubled. *Your wards are not protection, child. They are a bandage over a wound the size of a country. The valley does not sit inside the world. The valley sits inside a hole in the world, a pocket of stubbornness, and the wards are the stitching holding the pocket shut. And three nights ago, the stitching began to tear.*

Mara thought of the border that night — the wards shattering like struck glass, the black gap in the sky where stars should have been, the man falling through the breach as if the world itself had spat him out. She thought of how the elders had spent four days saying nothing, doing nothing, sealing the breach with murmured rites while their faces wore the particular blankness of people refusing to look at a thing.

"And the box," she said. "The man who fell through. He carries an iron box that hums. The animals flee from it. What is in it?"

Something moved through the fox then — Mara felt it through the beast-tongue, a tremor like a note struck on a cracked bell. When it answered, its voice had gone quiet and very careful, the way voices go near sleeping things or open graves.

*The Garden had seed. One. The last of everything it was, folded small enough to hold in two hands. If the country is a book being erased, the seed is the author's hand. Whoever carries it carries the possibility of writing again.*

"Where is the Garden now?"

*Dead,* said the fox, without softening it. *Murdered, if you want the truth of it, and murdered by the hands that tended it — but that is a longer story and not mine to tell, and besides, you would lose most of it before breakfast. What matters is this: the seed left. Someone carried it away, and someone else has been hunting it ever since, and the hunting is why your sky is going dark. The erasers want the last pen.*

Mara sat back against the cellar wall, and the dirt was cold through her dress, and none of it felt real except that all of it did — it felt more real than anything the valley had ever let her feel. She thought of the stranger, Ilian, lying feverish in the elder hall with his iron box humming beside him like a hive of invisible bees. She thought of how the valley dogs had pressed themselves flat to the earth when he was carried past, and how the crows had gone silent for a day, and how only she had been able to hear the words he muttered in his fever — words in the tongue of beasts, spilling from a human mouth like a river running backward.

He speaks beast-tongue, she had told herself. No man speaks beast-tongue.

*You're thinking about him,* the fox observed.

"You can't know that."

*I can smell it on you. Curiosity has a smell. So does fear. On you they smell the same.* The fox's whiskers angled in what Mara was beginning to recognize as its version of a smile. *Good. You'll need both.*

"Need them for what?"

*For leaving.*

The word landed in the cellar like a dropped stone. Mara found she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

*Listen to me, daughter-of-nowhere.* The fox rose fully to its feet for the first time, favoring the ruined leg, and stood over her — a small animal, barely reaching her knee, and yet it seemed suddenly to fill the whole low room. *The moon is counting. Do you watch the sky? Then you've seen it. The moon makes twelve turns in a year, as moons have always done. But this moon is making a thirteenth. A turn that has never existed, a turn that exists only because the rules themselves are being unwritten. When the thirteenth turn completes, the erasure finishes. Everything outside your valley ends — not destroyed, worse than destroyed. Forgotten so thoroughly it will never have been. And the valley?* Its lip lifted from its teeth. *A bandage cannot outlive the wound. Your elders' forever has perhaps thirteen turns left in it, and they spend them arranging flowers and pretending the sky isn't bleeding.*

"How do you know all this?"

*Because I was sent,* said the fox simply. *By those who remember. The beasts remember, girl. We always remember — memory lives differently in us, deeper, in the blood and the bone, where no eraser's hand can reach without killing the body entire. There are those of us who have been carrying true memory across the dying country like embers in cupped paws. I was carrying mine toward the valley, because the valley was the last place the erasure hadn't touched, and I meant to ask your elders for sanctuary.* Its gaze flicked to its own ruined foreleg, and something old and bitter passed through the link between them. *Then I met the hunters at the border. They do not like embers escaping. I got through the wards as they broke. Barely. You know the rest.*

Mara's mind was racing ahead of her now, leaping gaps she didn't remember deciding to jump. "The man. Ilian. He fell through the same breach—"

*Carrying the seed.*

"—and he speaks beast-tongue—"

*As one taught, not one born. Which means he serves the Garden's remnant order, or served it, or fled it — I cannot tell which from here, and neither, I think, can he.* The fox's ears flattened. *Which is precisely why you must go to him, and go soon, and go armed with an oath.*

"An oath?"

*An oath in beast-tongue. Mine, freely given, binding me to guide you.* The fox lowered its head until its eyes were level with hers, and the lantern flame between them bent as if bowing. *But oaths run both ways, daughter-of-nowhere. I will swear to lead you true — to the seed, to the truth, to whatever door remains open when the turns run out. And you must swear to me. Swear that you will leave the valley. Swear that you will follow where I lead even when the land stops making sense. Swear that when the choice comes — and it will come, they always come — you will choose with your own heart and not the valley's, not the elders', not any dead Gardener's.*

Mara's mouth had gone dry. "And if I refuse?"

*Then I die in this cellar having told a kind girl a frightening story, and you live out your thirteen turns arranging flowers, and the world ends politely around you.* The fox shrugged, a ripple through its whole spine. *I lose nothing by your refusal. I am already dying. The question is what you lose.*

* * *

She climbed out of the cellar afterward and walked, without quite deciding to, up the slope behind the drying shed to the place where she could see the whole valley laid out below her — the crooked lanes, the lamplit windows, the elder hall with its roof of living turf, the ward-stones standing pale as teeth around the rim of everything.

Her valley. The only world she had ever been permitted to know.

She had spent her whole life hiding the largest part of herself inside it. Every conversation with a bird conducted in whispers behind the barn. Every wounded thing smuggled home under sacks. Every glance at the ward-line carrying the same guilty weight as theft. The elders called the gift unclean — speech with beasts, they said, was the old magic, the magic that could not follow past the wards, the magic the valley had been founded to leave behind. She had learned to fold herself small around it, the way one folds a letter that must never be read.

And all along, the fox said, the gift was not the shameful thing. The valley was.

Below her, the night lay blue and ordinary. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, embarrassed by its own voice. The moon hung above the eastern ridge, fat and white and wrong, already visibly further along its impossible course than any moon had a right to be. Twelve turns and a thirteenth. She counted on her fingers, the way children count, and stopped when the arithmetic became unbearable.

Thirteen turns. She tried to imagine the valley forgetting — the lanes softening, the names of her neighbors dissolving, her mother's face going smooth and anonymous as an egg. She couldn't. That was the horror of it. The valley was so deeply known to her that she could not make herself see it unknown.

But she could feel it starting. Even here, even inside the wards, something had changed since the night the stars went out. The bread tasted slightly less like bread. Her own name, spoken by her mother, landed a half-heartbeat late, like a call across a widening river.

*Well?*

The fox's voice reached her even from the cellar, faint now, frayed at the edges — it was spending strength to speak at this distance, and it spent it anyway.

*The oath stands open until moonset, daughter-of-nowhere. After that I withdraw it. Dying creatures cannot afford to wait on the indecisive.*

Mara looked at the valley one more time. She made herself really look — at the turf roof of the hall where she had sat through a hundred lessons on the sanctity of silence; at the smokehouse she'd robbed for the fox; at the window of her own room, dark now, holding nothing but a bed and a locked chest and a life sized exactly to fit inside the walls.

It was a good valley. It was a kind prison. And it was dying, and it did not know it, and it would hate her for knowing.

The thrill came first — it always came first, that wild lift under her sternum, the feeling she got at the ward-line at dusk when the outside wind carried smells of a hundred unimagined places. *Exile*, the feeling said. *The whole unbounded world.* Then the terror arrived right on schedule behind it, cold and specific: exile also meant no mother's kitchen, no familiar path home, no valley to seal her back in if the world proved too large. Exile meant there was no going back, because there would be no back to go to.

Both feelings at once, braided tight as rope. She gripped the rope.

She knelt in the dirt on the hilltop, laid her palm flat against the cold earth, and spoke the old words into the ground the way the beasts did, the way her gift had always wanted her to speak — openly, at full voice, under the naked wrongness of the thirteenth-turning moon.

*I, Mara, daughter of the valley and of nowhere, swear by my breath and my blood: I will leave. I will follow. I will choose with my own heart. Lead, Vixen-who-outlived-the-flood, and I will walk.*

The earth hummed under her palm. Far off, one of the ward-stones flickered — or perhaps she imagined it, or perhaps the valley itself had heard her vow and flinched.

*Sworn and witnessed,* said the fox, and its voice rang through her like a bell through water. *Now come down here, girl, and bring better meat, because tomorrow we begin, and tomorrow we do not stop. And Mara —* a pause, and when it resumed, the dry irony had fallen away entirely, leaving something almost gentle — *the man with the box. Trust him exactly as far as he has earned, and no further. But trust him somewhat. Even liars carry true things sometimes. Especially liars.*

Mara stood, knees stiff, heart hammering a rhythm she didn't recognize — not dread, not joy, but the strange third thing that lived where they overlapped.

Down in the valley, a lamp moved in the elder hall. Someone was awake. Someone was always awake now, watching, since the breach.

She had until dawn to steal enough food for a journey no map could describe, and to decide how much truth to tell a stranger who spoke in the tongues of beasts — and somewhere out in the dark, the moon turned another degree toward its impossible thirteenth completion, patient as a page waiting to be written.
