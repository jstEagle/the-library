## 5. Leaving the Valley That Cannot Follow

**Mara**

The larder of the Brightwater house had never been locked. That was the kind of place the valley was — a place where locks would have been an insult, where every door opened to any neighbor's knock, where Mara's whole childhood had been built on the quiet assumption that nothing outside could ever want what was inside.

She took what she needed anyway, and it felt like theft.

Dried apples wrapped in waxed cloth. Hard cheese gone sharp at the edges. A heel of black bread, half a jar of honey, the smoked fish her mother had hung in autumn and that no one had eaten because grief made poor appetites. She worked by feel and by the fox's low instructions, which came to her not as words exactly but as pressures behind her eyes: *more there, less here, the fish, take the fish.* The beast-tongue did not translate cleanly into human thought. It came through like weather.

The lamp in the elder hall still burned. She counted its position against the window frame each time she crossed from pantry to pack — three steps of light, four, back to two — and each time she waited for it to move toward the door, toward her house, toward the small crime she was committing in the dark.

It didn't move. Whoever kept vigil tonight was tired, or old, or simply watching the wrong direction. Everyone watched the breach in the north wall of wards. No one watched a girl's kitchen.

*They'll understand,* said the part of her that loved this valley.

*They can't,* answered the other part, the one that had spent twenty-three years hiding half her soul in hedges and henhouses. *That's rather the point.*

Ilian waited in the woodshed, sitting very still on a stump among the split logs, as if he had learned somewhere that motion drew eyes. In the lantern's spill his face looked better than it had two nights ago when he'd fallen through the torn sky — color back in his lips, the gash above his brow scabbed over — but he still held the iron box against his chest the way a man holds a wound. It hummed. She could hear it now even without touching him, a sound like a hive dreaming, and she had begun to suspect she heard it because some part of her *was* a hive, or a garden, or whatever slept inside that metal. She was not ready to think about that yet. There would be time on the road, presumably, to fall apart properly.

"Is it enough?" he asked softly, in the tongue of beasts. To anyone else he would have sounded like wind in dry grass. To her the meaning arrived whole.

"It's enough." She cinched the pack. "We go up past the mill, along the shepherd's stair. There are watchers at the ford and the orchard gate, but the stair is only watched at nightfall, because no one leaves at dawn." She paused. "No one has ever left."

He rose. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them filled with all the things they had agreed not to say — his mission, whatever it truly was; her gift, and why it felt less like a gift every hour; the way the box's humming had quickened since the moment she first laid her hand on it, eager as a dog hearing its name.

"The fox," Ilian said instead. "It will meet us?"

"At the standing stone above the stair. If it lives." Her throat tightened around the memory of the fox bleeding out beyond the ward-line, of the oath she had sworn with her own blood and breath — *guide us true, and be healed of your hurt; fail us, and forget me forever* — a bargain struck in a language older than the valley, older maybe than the country dying beyond its rim. "Foxes keep their bargains. It's people who don't."

Something flickered across his face then, quick and guilty as a shadow under a door. She chose, again, not to name it. Dawn wouldn't wait for confessions.

They went out through the kitchen, and Mara stopped once, only once, with her hand on the latch her father had carved with little oak leaves the year she was born. The house breathed around her — the tick of the cooling stove, the settling of beams, the smell of yesterday's bread still ghosting the air. Homes were animals too, she thought. This one knew she was leaving. Houses always know.

Then she opened the door, and the valley's cold clean air poured in, and she stepped through into the rest of her life.

* * *

The shepherd's stair was a seam of switchbacks cut into the valley's eastern shoulder, steep enough that the sheep used it grudgingly and the shepherds used it rarely. Mist pooled below them like milk. Above, the sky was doing the thing it had done every dawn since the stars went out — brightening at the edges first, as if light had forgotten it was supposed to come from the middle outward.

Mara climbed with her ears open. Not for footsteps — for the valley itself. She had spent her life listening to it the way other people listened to music, and now she listened for the moment it changed its tune.

It came sooner than she expected.

At the fourth switchback, the birdsong thinned. At the fifth, it stopped entirely — not scattered away but *stopped*, mid-phrase, like a hand pressed flat over a mouth. Below them the mist began to stir in long slow spirals, and Mara understood, with a lurch, that the wards were not merely broken. They were healing. The valley was closing its wound, drawing its borders tight the way flesh draws closed over a splinter — and everything foreign was being pushed out.

Everything foreign included them.

"Faster," she said.

They ran. The path crumbled slightly underfoot, pebbles rolling downslope with a sound like whispered syllables. Behind them, far below, a bell began to ring in the elder hall — three strokes, pause, three strokes. The alarm for breach. But the breach was sealed now; the bell was ringing for something else, something smaller and worse.

Someone had found the empty larder. Or the missing girl. Or the stranger in the woodshed who wasn't there anymore.

"They can't follow past the rim," Ilian panted beside her. "Can they?"

"No. Once the wards close, nothing crosses. Not them, not their magic, not their letters, not their prayers." She took the last stretch of stair nearly on all fours, the standing stone looming ahead, gray and lichen-bearded. "And we won't be able to come back. Ever. Even if we wanted to. Even if—" She bit the sentence off. Even if everyone we love dies while we're gone. Even if the world ends and we're on the wrong side of a wall of our own making.

At the top of the stair, beside the standing stone, sat the fox.

It looked terrible and magnificent — fur patchy where the wound had been, one ear crumpled, amber eyes burning with the particular ferocity of the recently resurrected. It should have been dead twice over. Instead it sat upright, tail curled over its feet, and regarded them both with the flat patience of a creature that has already seen how this ends.

"You're late," it said, in the beast-tongue, and Mara laughed — a startled, cracked sound, half sob — because of course. Of course the first creature to greet her exile would be rude about it.

"You were dead," she managed.

"I was mostly dead. There's a difference, and it matters more every day now, in a land where mostly-dead things are coming back to finish their business." The fox rose, shook itself, and turned its nose east, toward the rim. "Walk. Don't run. Running smells like fear, and the wards read fear."

Mara glanced back down the stair despite herself.

The valley lay in its bowl of morning mist, roofs silvered with dew, smoke just beginning to rise from the baker's chimney — the same chimney that had smoked every morning of her life. She could see the elder hall, tiny as a toy, its lamp finally extinguished. She could see the orchard where she'd stolen apples at nine years old and been whipped for it, and the ford where her brother had taught her to skip stones, and the field where her mother was buried under a stone so new the moss hadn't claimed it yet.

And along the whole rim of the valley, visible now in the strengthening light, the wards were knitting. Threads of pale fire wove themselves through the air like loom-work, crossing and recrossing, and where they crossed, the world beyond simply ceased to matter — not hidden, *unimportant*, the way a dream stops mattering when you wake. She watched a thread seal shut directly beneath the standing stone, close enough to touch, and felt the valley exhale.

The bell stopped ringing. Somewhere below, someone would find her bed unslept-in, her pack gone, a stranger vanished. They would say she'd been taken. They would say the breach had swallowed her. Some of them might even say she'd died saving them, and they would be kinder to her memory than they had ever been to her gift.

Her eyes burned. She let them.

"Mara." Ilian stood at the rim's edge, one foot on valley stone, one foot on the road beyond, holding out his hand to her. Behind him the land fell away into a country she had never seen — hills the color of old bronze, a river glinting, distances that seemed to breathe. "Once more," he said gently. "Then never again. Choose."

*I'm choosing,* she wanted to tell him. *I've been choosing since the fox bled out in my arms.*

What she said was nothing. She took his hand, stepped over the last thread of ward-fire — it parted around her ankles like water, like recognition — and stood on the far side of everything she had ever known.

Behind her, the final threads closed with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard, a note too deep for ears, and the valley became a picture of a valley. She looked back once, as she had promised herself she would. Just once. Roofs. Smoke. The oak-leaf latch on a kitchen door she would never open again.

Then she turned east, and the fox trotted ahead, and the unwritten country received them like a held breath.

* * *

**Ilian**

The remnant order had trained him for many kinds of failure. Capture, interrogation, death — there had been protocols for all of them, drilled into him in a monastery whose name he was sworn never to speak. What they had not trained him for was walking beside the vessel he was sent to retrieve while she wept silently for a home she could no longer see, knowing that every tear was, in a sense, his doing.

He kept his eyes forward. It was cowardice dressed as discipline.

The road east descended through terraced hills gone wild, orchards collapsing into their own shadows, apple trees fruiting out of season as if confused about what year it was. The land beyond the valley was not ruined — that was the horror of it. It was *unfinished*. Grass grew in patches and paused mid-growth. A farmhouse they passed stood fully built on one side and sketched in charcoal on the other, as though the world had run out of ink halfway through making it. Birds flew overhead in ones and twos, and every single one of them was flying east, the same direction as the fox, the same direction as them, like iron filings aligning along a magnet's pull.

Toward the Garden. Or what was left of it.

The box hummed against his chest, and beneath the hum he could feel the faint answering warmth in his own blood, the seed's attention snagging on him whenever Mara touched it, whenever she laughed, whenever she looked back. He had told the Council of Roots almost nothing. He had told Mara less. And now the order's other agents would be moving — Vessane and her knives, the twins, whoever else the Gardener-Prime had kept in reserve — and every mile of this soft, forgetting road was a mile they would have to walk twice: forward, and back, with hunters behind them and a deadline ahead.

Thirteen turns of the moon. Eleven remained.

"Your order," Mara said suddenly, not looking at him. She walked with her chin up and her knuckles white on the pack straps. "The one that sent you. Will they send anyone else? After you?"

The question landed exactly where he lived. *After you* — as if his survival were assumed, as if she had already decided he was hers to keep alive. He thought about lying well. He was good at lying well.

"Yes," he said. "Probably."

She nodded slowly, and the fox glanced back at them both with an expression that suggested it had heard the truth underneath the answer — beasts always did — and filed it away for later, the way foxes file everything.

Ahead, the road bent around a hill of bronze grass, and where it bent, the world shimmered faintly, uncertainly, like a page someone had erased and rewritten too many times.

Ilian adjusted the box in his arms and followed the woman he was betraying toward the country that was forgetting itself — and told himself, as he had told himself every night since the breach, that he would find the courage to tell her everything before the moon finished turning.

He was beginning to suspect, in the honest hours before dawn, that he was lying.
