## 6. The Unwritten Road

The world outside the valley did not end. That was the first thing Mara understood, standing on the far side of the ward-line with the fox limping ahead and Ilian's iron box humming against his chest like a hive of sleeping bees. The world outside simply *forgot to be*.

She had expected ruin — burned farms, broken towers, some great visible wound where a country had died. Instead there was grass, green and ordinary, bending under a wind that smelled of rain that had not yet decided to fall. A road unspooled before them, pale stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, running straight toward hills that shimmered faintly at their edges, like paint not quite dry.

"Which way?" Ilian asked, for perhaps the tenth time since dawn.

Mara did not answer at once. She was listening. The fox had stopped at the roadside, one ear cocked toward the hills, and its tail — still thin, still patchy from its long dying — moved in small, restless sweeps.

*West,* said the fox. *Follow the water. Water remembers longer than stone.*

"There's no water," Ilian said.

"There will be," Mara said, and was unsettled by how easily the words came, how much they sounded like something she had always known. "Water remembers longer than stone."

Ilian looked at her sharply, but she was already walking, and after a moment he followed, the box swaying gently against him as though it breathed.

They walked through morning that behaved like morning, mostly. Birds sang — real birds, with real hunger in their voices, and when Mara listened she heard them plainly, their small fierce certainties: *worms, nest, fly, mine.* It steadied her. Whatever was happening to the land, the animals were still themselves.

It was the stones that worried her.

At midday they passed a milestone that read, in old carved letters, **THREE LEAGUES TO HARROWMERE**, and beneath it, in newer carving someone had attempted within the last year or so: **HARROW—** and then nothing. Not scratched out. Simply unfinished, the chisel-marks trailing off into blank stone, as if the carver had forgotten what came next halfway through the word.

"The village," Ilian said quietly. "Harrowmere."

"You know it?"

"I knew of it. On maps. A market town on the Sennet." He frowned at the stone. "Or — I think I knew of it. I'm no longer certain whether I remember Harrowmere or only remember reading about it."

"That's how it starts," the fox said. They had reached the top of a low rise, and the animal sat down, panting lightly, regarding the horizon with yellow eyes. "For people. First you can't tell remembering from reading. Then you can't tell remembering from dreaming. Then there is no difference left to tell."

Mara crouched beside it. "And for the land?"

The fox looked west, where the afternoon light lay thick and golden over country that should have held a river valley.

"For the land it starts with directions," the fox said. "Watch."

Mara watched. At first she saw nothing but the hills, hazy and patient. Then she saw that the haze was moving. Not like mist moves, drifting and thinning. It moved *with purpose*, sliding uphill along the contours of the land like water finding a channel — except the channel ran upward. A stream appeared between two folds of the hillside, silver-bright, climbing. It gathered speed as it rose, leapt a shelf of rock in a spray of white, and continued its ascent without any apparent sense of scandal, as though gravity were merely a custom the water had once observed and now found tiresome.

"It runs uphill," Ilian said flatly.

"It runs *home*," said the fox. "Whatever home still is. Everything here is drifting back toward what made it, because nothing here knows how to be itself anymore. Rivers forget which way down lies. Villages forget to exist except when they're remembered hardest — at dusk, when people are looking for somewhere to sleep."

"And us?" Mara asked. "Do we drift?"

The fox turned its yellow gaze on her, and there was something in it that might have been pity, or might have been the particular attention an animal gives a thing it cannot classify.

"Not yet," it said. "You're stitched tight, beast-speaker. Stitched tighter than anything I've met outside a wall." It rose, shook itself, and set off westward. "Come. The Sennet's forgotten its bed but it hasn't forgotten its crossing. There'll be a ferryman. There's always a ferryman. Ferries are among the last things to go."

"Why ferries?" Ilian asked, hurrying after.

"Because a ferryman's whole self is his work," the fox said over its shoulder. "A man who has given everything else away has more of himself left than a man who's kept it all."

* * *

They walked until the light went strange.

Mara could not have said what hour it became; the sun seemed to lose interest in its own position around late afternoon, hanging in the sky with the absent air of a host who has stopped watching the door. The shadows lengthened and shortened and lengthened again, undecided. And then, as the light finally began to fail in earnest, the hills ahead of them filled up with a town.

It did not appear so much as *arrive* — rooftops first, chimneys smoking, then walls, then streets, all of it assembling out of the gold haze with the unhurried confidence of something that had been there all along and saw no reason to make a fuss about the gaps. Smoke curled from chimneys. Lamplighters moved along the high street with their poles. Somewhere a dog barked, and Mara's heart lifted at the sound until she listened closer and heard what the bark actually said:

*Where? Where? Where?*

Not *who goes there.* Not *stranger, stranger.* Just that single question, repeated with mounting distress, the way a dog barks when it has lost the scent of its own yard.

"Harrowmere," Ilian murmured. "It exists."

"At dusk," the fox said. "Only at dusk. Come — we cross tonight while it remembers itself. By morning this will be hillside again, and we'll have walked through the middle of nowhere that thinks it's somewhere, and neither it nor we will be the better for it."

The town received them without comment. That was somehow worse than alarm would have been. Doors stood open. People nodded to them in passing, friendly and vague, their faces pleasant as bread. An old woman swept her step with long even strokes, and Mara noticed that the broom passed through a corner of the doorway each time, sweeping dust into the space where wood should have been, and the dust vanished there, politely, and the woman never once faltered in her rhythm.

"They don't see us," Ilian whispered.

"They see *shapes*," the fox said. "Dusk-people. You're dusk to them too, right now. Keep walking."

They kept walking, down the high street, past a tavern whose door spilled warmth and fiddle-music onto the cobbles — music that repeated itself every eleven bars, Mara realized, because whoever played it could not hold more than eleven bars in mind — past a well where a girl drew water and hummed a tune that was almost a lullaby and had no ending, just circled and circled like water going down a drain.

And everywhere, everywhere, names.

Chalked above doorways: *the Merrow house. the — house. the — house.* Signs that read **THE DROWNED —** and stopped. A churchyard gate with a board listing the departed, most of the entries worn smooth, and the newest entry freshly chalked: *here lies*, and nothing after, the chalk-dust still clinging to the board as though the hand that wrote it had simply wandered off mid-word.

Mara stopped at the churchyard gate. Something about the fresh chalk pulled at her, and she stood a moment reading the blank space after *here lies*, and found to her horror that her own mind offered to fill it — offered, eagerly, helpfully — with a name. She felt the name arrive in her mouth like a borrowed coin. She clamped her teeth shut on it.

"Mara." Ilian's hand was near her elbow, not touching. "Don't. Whatever it offers you — don't take it."

"How do you know it does that?"

"Because it did it to me an hour ago," he said. His face in the lamplight was grey. "I nearly introduced myself to a sweeper. With a name that wasn't mine. I felt it fit my mouth perfectly."

The fox tugged at Mara's hem with its teeth, gentle but insistent. *Crossing. Now.*

The river lay at the bottom of the town, wide and slow and black, and it flowed — she checked twice, three times — downhill, toward them, in the proper direction, the only thing in Harrowmere that still obeyed its nature. But even the river was wrong at the edges. Its banks softened into mud that was not entirely mud, and here and there the water thinned to translucence and she could see, beneath it, not a riverbed but *sky* — dim clouds drifting under the current like fish.

"What's under it?" she asked.

"Nothing yet," the fox said. "Give it time."

The ferry was moored at a rickety landing, a broad flat boat with a lantern hung at its prow, and the ferryman sat on the bench amidships with his oars across his knees, waiting, as he had perhaps always waited, for passengers who came only at dusk.

He was an old man, or shaped like one — big hands, weathered face, a beard gone to white thistle. He looked up at them with eyes that were mild and empty as two cups of water.

"Evening," he said. "Evening. Evening." As though tasting the word to check it still worked.

"We need to cross," Ilian said.

"Cross." The ferryman considered this. His hands, of their own accord, shifted the oars, tested the lock-tholes, ran a thumb along the grain of the wood — and Mara watched his face change as he touched them. The emptiness in his eyes didn't fill, exactly. But it *settled*. The way a restless animal settles when it finds its spot. "Crossing costs a coin," he said. "Or a song. Or a story. Something carried."

"We have stories," Mara said carefully. "But they're not ours to give."

The ferryman looked at her then, and for a moment — a single held breath — something surfaced in those cup-water eyes. Recognition? Longing? Grief? It was gone too fast to name.

"You've got a voice that carries," he said. "That'll do. Sing while I row. If the singing stops, the rowing stops. Them's the terms. Always were." He frowned slightly, as if surprised to find the terms still in his head. "*Were* they always? Yes. Yes, I think the terms were always."

They boarded. The fox stepped aboard last, delicately, tail high, and the ferryman glanced down at it and said, with sudden startling clarity, "Good evening, Vetch."

Mara froze. "You know it?"

"Know what?" said the ferryman, and pushed off from the landing, and the dusk-town of Harrowmere began, gently, to let go of itself behind them.

* * *

The river carried them into the dark country beyond the town, and Mara sang.

She sang anything — valley songs, half-remembered harvest rounds, nonsense verses her mother had used to time the kneading of bread — and her voice went out over the black water and the oars answered it, stroke and stroke and stroke, the ferryman's great arms pulling in perfect time as though the music were written into his muscles deeper than forgetting could reach.

His name, she learned, was gone. She asked him directly, between songs, and he told her without bitterness, the way you'd report the weather.

"Had one. Everyone does, everyone did. It went out with the tide." He rowed. "Funny thing, though. I've lost the farm I grew on, lost my wife's face — pretty, I think, they usually are — lost the words for half the fish in this river. But these—" he lifted the oars a bare inch, still keeping rhythm, "—these I know. Ash and apple-wood, forty years, re-footed twice. Left one pulls a hair heavy on the catch. I know the weight of the water by season. I know the prayer you say to the river before first launch, though the god it's prayed to is gone out of me same as my name. I know my work. Seems a man keeps what he *does* longer than what he *is*."

"Yes," Mara said softly. "Seems he does."

And she thought of her gift, her forbidden, shameful gift — the whispering with beasts that had cost her friendships and nearly cost her freedom — and wondered, for the first time in her life, with a cold bright thrill, whether what she *did* might be what she *was*, and whether the elders had ever understood the difference at all.

The banks slid past. Once they passed a village on the eastern shore that existed fully, solidly, in the middle of the night — windows lit, a mill turning — and the ferryman said, "That's Fenlow. Or it thinks it's Fenlow. It got frightened when the stars started dropping, and frightened places grip hard onto being. It'll exhaust itself by spring and go quiet like the rest." He rowed. "My wife was from Fenlow. I think. Or I'm thinking it now because it sounds kind."

Underneath them, sometimes, the water thinned and the sky showed through, and once — only once — Mara looked straight down through the translucent current and saw, far below in that drowned dark, a shape. Green. Vast. A suggestion of terraces and flowering boughs, of water organized into beauty, of a garden so large it had been less a place than a promise.

It lasted the length of a heartbeat. Then the current clouded, and it was only river again.

Her chest ached as though she'd seen her own face in a stranger's window.

*Vetch,* the ferryman had called the fox. And the fox had not corrected him. Which meant either the ferryman remembered something the whole unwritten country had lost — or the fox had a name, a true name, and had never thought it worth mentioning, and Mara filed that away with all the other small wrongnesses she was learning to carry: the box that hummed, the stranger who spoke beast-tongue in his fever, the elders' maps that ended at the wards, the way her own stitches felt when the fox looked at her.

Midway across the widest reach, the singing faltered. Mara's throat closed — tiredness, or grief, or the sight of a second drowned-garden flicker beneath the hull — and in the gap between two verses the oars stopped.

Everything stopped.

The river held still, black and absolute. The lantern flame stood motionless. Even the wind waited. In that silence Mara heard, very faintly, from all around and below, a sound like pages turning. Thousands of pages. The country, unwriting itself, one line at a time, and for a moment she could almost hear the words leaving — a name here, a boundary there, the taste of bread, the color of a door —

"Singer," the ferryman said gently. "Terms is terms."

She sang. The oars bit. The world resumed.

But the urgency had come into her now and would not leave — wonder curdling, exactly as the fox had promised, into something harder. This was not a tragedy happening somewhere else. This was a countdown. Every mile of softening road, every dusk-village, every nameless ferryman was a page torn out, and the moon overhead rode thinner and whiter each night, racing toward a turn she did not yet understand but already dreaded.

When at last the far bank rose out of the dark — real bank, firm earth, honest mud — the ferryman grounded the boat and held it steady while they disembarked.

"I'll remember you," Mara told him, standing on the shore. It felt suddenly, desperately important. "The man with the ash-and-apple oars. The left one pulls heavy. I'll carry it. Someone should carry it."

The ferryman looked at her a long moment. The cup-water eyes trembled, and something almost broke the surface — almost became a smile, or tears, or a name.

"That's kind," he said. "Kindness weighs nothing and it carries far." He took up his oars. "Mind the road west, singer. And mind your friend there—" a nod at Ilian, at the humming box, "—he's carrying something that remembers *him*. Things like that don't stay carried forever."

Mara's blood went cold. "What do you mean?"

But the ferryman was already pushing off, already turning his boat downstream toward the dusk-town that existed only while someone sang to it, and his voice came back across the black water in time with his oars, fading, fading:

"Ferries are the last to go! Ask your friend what he's ferrying!"

Then only the oars' knock, then only the river, then only the night.

The fox stood at her side on the muddy bank, looking after the boat. Westward, the hills waited, their edges soft as breath on glass.

"He knows more than he says," Ilian said quietly behind her.

"He doesn't *say* anything he knows," Mara said. She turned and looked at Ilian properly — at the box pressed to his chest, humming its hive-hum, holding whatever it held. "What are you ferrying, Ilian?"

For the first time since he'd fallen bleeding through the broken border, the stranger from beyond the wards had no answer ready.

And far off to the west, beneath a moon one turn closer to its thirteenth, something in the hills began — very faintly — to sing back.
