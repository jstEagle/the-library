## 7. Thirteen Turns

Ilian counted the moon's turns the way a drowning man counts the strokes between him and the shore — not because counting helped, but because stopping would mean admitting how far away the shore had become.

They had made camp in the ribs of a dead watchtower on the second night out of the valley, and he lay awake beneath the broken arch of its roof, watching the moon slide through a sky that no longer deserved it. The stars were still gone. All of them. Where once the country's night had been sewn thick with them, there was now only a black so complete it felt less like absence than like erasure — as if someone had taken a cloth to the heavens and wiped, and wiped, and the cloth had come away clean.

The moon, at least, remained. It hung above the tower like a coin nailed to a door, and Ilian watched it and did the arithmetic that was eating him alive.

Twelve turns left. Perhaps eleven, if the land forgot faster than the order's oldest charts predicted. And the charts were old. Older than the remnant. Older, some whispered, than the Garden itself — drawn by hands that had never imagined a world capable of unmaking its own geography.

He turned his head and looked at Mara.

She slept sitting up, her back against the tower's fallen stone, her fox — *his* name was Sorrel, though he hadn't told her that yet; the fox hadn't told her either, or perhaps had and she kept it private the way she kept everything private — curled against her hip. Even asleep she held herself like someone listening. He had noticed it the first hour he'd known her: the tilt of her head, the half-open attention of a woman who spent her life eavesdropping on voices no one else could hear. Now, outside the wards, that listening had sharpened into something almost painful to watch. Her eyes moved in sleep. Her lips shaped words without sound.

*She hears it forgetting,* he thought. *Every hour of every day. She hears what we're losing.*

And you lied to her. You're lying to her right now, while she sleeps three feet from you.

He closed his eyes.

---

The lie had seemed small at first. Lies always did. That was the trap of them — they arrived wearing the clothes of mercy.

Three nights ago, in the elder hall of the valley, when Mara had asked him — her arms crossed, her chin lifted, that wary animal caution in her face — *why me? Why does your mission need a beast-speaker the elders tried to beat out of existence?* — he had answered with the truth's well-dressed cousin.

*"Because the seed must be carried through country that has stopped obeying sense,"* he'd said. *"Rivers run uphill there now. Villages exist only when the dusk remembers them. A man alone gets unwritten in a place like that. But the beasts remember. The beasts are the last honest map. And you —"* he had let his voice soften, and hated himself for how easily it softened — *"you can ask them where the roads used to go."*

It was even true. Every word of it. That was the craft of a good lie: you built it entirely out of true things, mortared them together, and only the mortar was false.

What he had not said — what he could never say, not yet, maybe not ever — was the rest of it. That the seed was not the cargo. That the box humming against his ribs like a caged hive was a decoy, a beautiful brass lie of his own order's making. That the true vessel of the Garden's return had never been iron or seed or soil at all.

That it was a person.

That the person had a name, and the name was Mara Villalobos, and she had been planted — *planted*, the word made his stomach turn even in the privacy of his own skull — in a valley family eleven generations ago, seeded into the bloodline like a grain of wheat hidden in a sack of chaff, and his mission, his sacred charge, his one purpose since boyhood, was to walk her out of the only home she had ever known and deliver her to an altar she could not imagine.

*The Garden requires a vessel,* the Remnant's doctrine said, in language so old it had worn smooth as river stone. *The vessel must be willing.*

Willing. As if willingness could be manufactured by withholding the question until the answer no longer mattered.

He had argued with his masters. He remembered standing in the root-hollows beneath the dying Garden, ash sifting down through the canopy like grey snow, telling the Speaker of Roots that he would not deceive the woman. *Then tell her,* the Speaker had said, mild as milk. *Tell her what she is. Tell her what will be asked. Watch her refuse — because everyone refuses, Ilian, everyone who understands — and then watch this country finish dying with its last seed locked inside a woman who chose ignorance over sacrifice.* A pause, and then, gentler, which was worse: *Or don't tell her. And let her choose freely, later, when the choice is real. Deceive her now so she may be free then.*

It was sophistry. He had known it was sophistry. He had agreed anyway.

Now he lay in a ruined tower under a starless sky and listened to her breathe, and understood that sophistry is just a lie that has been to university.

---

Dawn came wrong. He was learning that all dawns came wrong out here, but this one came wronger than most: the light rose out of the east already tired, the color of watered milk, and the horizon had the soft, unfinished look of a sketch someone had abandoned. To the north, where yesterday there had been hills, there were hills — but they were the *idea* of hills, rounded and vague, like hills remembered by someone who had only heard them described.

Mara woke the way she did everything: suddenly, completely, without transition. One moment asleep; the next, her eyes open and fixed on him with the flat golden attention of a hawk.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept."

"Liar." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Your breathing changes when you pretend. Everything does. You'd know that if you'd ever spent time around animals — they catch liars by the breath." The fox stirred against her hip and yawned, showing teeth gone slightly translucent at the edges, another thing about this country that was quietly coming apart. "So. What were you lying about?"

"The moon," he said, and was surprised to find his voice steady. Well. If he was going to be caught, better to be caught near the truth. "Come here. I'll show you."

He led her up the broken stair to the tower's crown, where the wind moved without smell — another wrongness; wind should smell of something, grass or rain or distance, and this wind smelled of nothing at all, a wind that had forgotten what it was for. From up here the land spread out below them: the soft hills, a river glinting silver where rivers should, and everywhere else the pale patches, the places where the world was thinning like old cloth.

He pointed at the moon, hanging fat and full even in daylight, refusing to set.

"Count with me," he said. "How many times has it turned full since the stars went out?"

"Once," Mara said. "We saw it over the ferry crossing two nights ago."

"And before that? In the valley? Before the wards broke?"

She frowned. He watched her reach for the memory and watched her hand close on nothing. Her frown deepened. "I — the valley kept its own calendar. Planting moons. Harvest moons. The elders—"

"When did the stars go out, Mara?"

"On the eve of the first missing star," she said automatically, and then stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Which was... I was speaking to a dying fox beyond the ward-line. It was autumn. It was—" Her hands flexed open and closed. "I can't count them. I can't count how many turns. Can you?"

"Yes," Ilian said, and the weight of it settled onto him like a yoke. "That's why you brought me, isn't it? Because I'm the only one left who can count."

"I can count," she snapped. But her voice shook, and the fox pressed closer to her leg.

"It doesn't matter that you can't," he said gently. "No one can, anymore. Memory is the first thing the forgetting takes — not facts, not faces, but *sequences*. The order of things. People remember their mothers but not whether their mothers came before or after the war. They remember songs but not how many verses. The whole country is losing its ability to say *then*." He turned back to the moon. "But I was trained outside it. My order keeps records the forgetting can't touch — written in a tongue the land doesn't know how to erase. So I'll tell you what my records say, and you can believe me or not."

"Say it."

"The moon has turned twice since the stars went out. It will turn thirteen times in all before the country finishes forgetting itself. Thirteen turns, and then—" he searched for a way to say it that wouldn't sound like madness, and failed, and said it anyway — "and then the last page turns. The land won't die. Dying leaves something behind — bones, ruins, a smell. This is different. This is *unwriting*. After the thirteenth turn, there will be no ruins. There will be no proof this country was ever here. No door in, no door out. Anyone still inside becomes a sentence in a book that was never opened."

Silence, except the odorless wind.

"That's why the seed matters," Mara said slowly. "That's why you carry it. There's a door."

"There's a threshold," Ilian corrected. "At the heart of the old Garden. The Garden wasn't just a garden, Mara. It was the anchor. The pin that held the country's story to the world's spine. Everything here exists because the Garden *insisted* it existed. Kill the Garden, and the country begins to forget itself — slowly, over years, over turns of the moon. Restore the Garden before the thirteenth turn, plant the seed in living ground, and the insistence returns. The country remembers itself. The doors reopen."

"And after the thirteenth turn?"

"After the thirteenth turn, planting it does nothing. The story will have ended. A seed needs a book to grow in." He met her eyes. "We have twelve turns. Maybe fewer. We have to cross a country that is actively dissolving, reach the Garden's grave at its heart, and put the seed in the ground. That's the whole of it. That's everything."

Mara looked out over the softening land for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, flatter, the voice she used with frightened animals.

"You said *we* have to reach the grave. But you haven't said why *me*. Not really. You gave me a speech about maps and beasts, and it was a good speech, but I've spent my whole life being given speeches by people who wanted something." Her eyes were very steady. "The elders wanted obedience. My mother wanted normalcy. The fox wanted company on the road. Everyone wants something, Ilian, and everyone dresses it up as destiny. So I'll ask you plainly, the way I'd ask an animal, because animals respect plain questions: what do you want from me? Not from a beast-speaker. From *me*."

And there it was. The question standing naked in the wind between them, and every road away from it paved with true things.

*I want your body for my god's resurrection,* he could say. *I want to deliver you to an altar. I want the thing you are, not the things you do.*

Or: *I don't know yet. I think I need you. I think the seed needs you. I think that when we reach the grave, you'll understand, and you'll thank me for the silence, and I'll spend the rest of my life hoping that's true.*

He chose neither. He chose the mortar.

"Because you're the only one who can hear them," he said. "The animals. The land itself, maybe — the parts of it still trying to speak. Out there—" he nodded toward the pale patches — "the country isn't just forgetting. Some of it is *calling out*. Asking to be remembered before it goes. I've stood at the edge of those places and heard nothing, because I'm deaf to it. But you—" and here, gods forgive him, the feeling was real, the feeling was the truest mortar of all — "you hear everything. And I think that before this is over, this country is going to need someone who can hear it. Someone who can carry its voice past the thirteenth turn. That's why it has to be you, Mara. Not any beast-speaker. *You.*"

It was a beautiful answer. It was nearly the whole truth. It was also, in the place where the truth lived, a lie — because he had known her name before he ever fell through the breach, had carried it across a dying country carved into the inside of the iron box's lid, had been sent not to find a guide but to find *her*, specifically, uniquely, the eleventh-generation fruit of a planting older than the valley's wards.

Mara searched his face for a long moment. The wind pulled at her hair. Somewhere below, in the thinning hills, something that might once have been a church bell rang a note that dissolved halfway through sounding.

"All right," she said finally. "For now, I believe you. But Ilian —" she stepped closer, and he saw that her eyes weren't just gold but flecked, flecked with green, flecked with a green he had seen exactly once before, in the heartwood of the Garden's last living branch — "when you're ready to tell me the rest of it, whatever the rest of it is — and there *is* a rest of it, I've raised orphaned fox kits, I know what withheld looks like — I'd rather hear it from you than from the world. The world tells us the truth at the worst possible moment. That's practically its job description lately."

She turned and went down the stairs, the fox flowing after her like a spill of rust-colored water.

Ilian stayed in the tower a long time, doing the arithmetic again. Twelve turns. Eleven, if the pale patches kept spreading. And somewhere ahead, at the end of the count, a grave and a question he had promised himself he'd never have to answer honestly.

*When you're ready to tell me the rest of it.*

He touched the box at his chest, and felt, for the first time, something inside it shift — a tiny, patient stirring, like a seed turning over in dark soil.

Below, Mara's voice rose faintly, talking to the fox in the low musical tongue of beasts, and the fox answered, and Ilian realized with a cold drop of dread that he could not understand a single word of what they were saying — that the only conversation happening within a hundred miles that he could not overhear was the conversation between the woman he was deceiving and the animal guiding them both.

He started down the stairs after them, counting the steps, counting the turns, counting everything, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

* * *

They walked.

The country got softer as the day went on. By midmorning the river beside them was running uphill without embarrassment, sliding lazily toward its source as if it had misremembered which direction water was supposed to go, and fish hung motionless in its current like ornaments in a shop window. By noon they passed a farmhouse that existed only in silhouette — walls of outline, a roof of suggestion — and inside it, faintly, the shape of a family at supper, frozen mid-gesture, waiting for the dusk that would give them substance again. Mara stopped and stared, and her hand found the fox's fur, and the fox made a sound Ilian had never heard a fox make: low, grieving, almost human.

"They're not suffering," Mara said, but her voice asked it as a question.

"No," Ilian said. "Forgetting doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. It feels like rest."

They camped that night in a hollow of stones that still remembered how to be solid, and Ilian took first watch, and Mara slept, and the moon rose — the third turn, his charts insisted, though looking at it you'd swear it was full again already, the moon growing unreliable too, keeping its own counsel about its phases.

And in the deepest hour of the night, Mara sat bolt upright in her bedroll and said, clearly, in the tongue of beasts, a single word.

Ilian didn't know what it meant. But the fox woke, and looked at her, and then looked at Ilian, and its translucent-edged eyes held an expression he had seen only once before in his life — on the face of the Speaker of Roots, the night the order first spoke aloud the name *Mara Villalobos* in his presence.

Pity.

The fox knew something. The fox had known it since the ward-line. And Ilian lay back in the dark and understood that he was not the only keeper of secrets on this road — that the truth was circling them like a slow bird, patient, inevitable, waiting for the moment the wind changed.

Eleven turns, he thought. Eleven turns to find a grave, plant a seed, keep a lie breathing, and decide what kind of man he intended to be at the end of the count.

The moon slid westward, keeping its thirteenth appointment in silence.
