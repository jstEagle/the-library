## 8. The City of Half-Names

The capital announced itself long before they saw it.

Mara smelled it first — woodsmoke and ink and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun, like flowers pressed between pages until they gave up their scent and kept only their shape. Then came the sound: a low murmur rolling across the plain, thousands of voices speaking at once but never in conversation, each one talking to itself or to no one, a city-sized hum that reminded her unpleasantly of the iron box's hive-song.

They crested a hill of grass so pale it was nearly white, and there it was.

The city had been built in rings, Mara could see that much. Seven concentric walls, once white marble by the look of the bones of them, now gone grey as old teeth. Towers rose from the innermost ring — some whole, some half-dissolved at their summits into a haze that might have been mist and might have been nothing at all. Above it all hung the moon, fat with its thirteenth approaching turn, and around the moon where stars should have been there was only a smooth black absence, like a hole cut in cloth.

"Vessa," Ilian said quietly, using the word she'd learned was the beast-tongue name for this place. *The city of many names.* He stood beside her at the hilltop, the box strapped to his back beneath his cloak, his face doing what it always did when he looked at ruin — nothing. "It was two hundred thousand people. Maybe more."

"It looks empty."

"It isn't." His jaw tightened. "That's the trouble."

* * *

They went in through the outermost gate because the other gates no longer existed. The wall here ended mid-archway, sheared off as cleanly as if a giant had taken a bite, and beyond the bite was simply street — cobblestones continuing without any reason to continue, houses leaning against a wall that wasn't there anymore.

The first citizen Mara saw was an old woman kneeling in her doorway, scrubbing a step that was already clean, over and over, with the patience of someone who had forgotten she'd done it a moment ago and would forget again in a moment more. Around the woman's neck hung a slate on a leather cord. Written on it in careful chalk letters was the word **MIRELLE**. Beneath it, smaller: *I have a daughter. I love roses.*

"Mistress," the old woman said pleasantly, not looking up. "Have you seen my daughter? She's — " The chalk squeaked as Mirelle wet her sleeve and rewrote something at the bottom of the slate. *I have a daughter.* "She's tall," the old woman finished uncertainly. "I think she's tall."

Mara's throat closed. She made herself answer. "No. I'm sorry."

"That's all right." Mirelle smiled up at her, and the smile was warm and genuine and utterly vacant, a lamp burning in an empty house. "Everyone is kind here. That's one thing we've kept."

They walked on. And everywhere, the slates.

A baker standing in front of an oven that held no bread, his slate reading **TOMAZ** and beneath it *I rise before dawn. My hands know the dough.* A girl of perhaps ten chasing something invisible down the street, her slate blank except for a single freshly written word — **ME?** — which her mother, hurrying after her, wiped clean and replaced with **SERA** while the girl giggled as though being renamed were a game.

An entire street of people sweeping doorsteps, watering gardens of grey flowers, greeting one another by reading aloud from one another's slates. *"Good morning, Pellin who has a brother." "Good morning, Anthe who loves the sea."* The names changed hourly — Mara watched a man pause in the middle of the square, frown at his own slate, lick his thumb, and write a different name entirely, then continue walking with the satisfied air of someone who had solved a small problem.

"They don't fight it," she whispered to Ilian. Her voice felt obscene in that murmuring hush. "Why don't they *fight* it?"

"Because fighting requires remembering what you're fighting for." Ilian pulled his hood lower. Around them, dogs pressed themselves flat against walls, cats fled along fence tops, sparrows exploded upward from the cobbles in ragged clouds. Every animal they passed gave him the same wide berth it had given him since the border — fear, Mara thought, or grief. "The forgetting feels gentle. That's what makes it monstrous. It doesn't take anything violently. It just... loosens your grip. And when you can't hold on anymore, you let go, and it feels like relief."

"How do you know?"

Something moved behind his eyes — the same guilt she'd been watching since the elder hall, since the fox's bargain, since the road that ran uphill. "I've seen it before," he said. "In places the Garden abandoned."

He walked ahead before she could ask what places those were.

* * *

At the center of the third ring stood a library.

Mara knew it was a library even before she saw the sign, because the building was the only thing in the whole city that looked *defended*. Its stone was unweathered. Its windows glowed with lamplight despite the afternoon. And around its doors, in a wide circle on the cobbles, lay a border of ash — fine, white, deliberate — and every citizen who approached it stopped at the edge, frowned faintly, turned, and wandered away, as though the library were a word their tongues couldn't shape.

"The animals won't come near me either," Ilian said grimly. "Wards?"

"No." Mara studied the ash line. It wasn't a ward; wards sang to her, faint and constant, and this silence was absolute. It was older than wards. It was the silence of a place that had decided, very firmly, to be remembered. "It's a refusal. Everything out here is letting go. This place is holding on so hard it burns away everything that tries to make it let go."

The doors opened before they touched them.

The man in the doorway was small and neat and ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years — ancient the way bedrock is ancient, the way mountains are. He wore grey robes dusted with the same white ash that ringed his library, and his eyes were the color of ink, and when he looked at Mara she felt read. Not seen. *Read*, cover to cover, footnotes and all.

"You're the fox's people," he said. His voice was dry and precise, like pages turning. "And you're the box-carrier. And you—" the ink eyes settled on Mara and did not blink— "you are the most interesting thing to walk into my city in four hundred years."

"Ilian," Ilian said carefully. "This is Mara. We're passing through."

"No one passes through Vessa anymore. People only leave it, in pieces, whether they mean to or not." The librarian stepped back, an invitation. "Come in. It's cool inside, and quiet, and nothing in here has forgotten anything for four centuries. You'll find it restful. Most visitors do, at first. Then they find it unbearable. That's also informative."

* * *

Inside, the library went on forever.

Shelves rose past the reach of sight, curving away into a dim gold distance, and the books — Mara touched one spine, gingerly — were warm. Living-warm, like the flank of a sleeping animal. In the reading room at the heart of it, the librarian poured three cups of tea that tasted of smoke and thyme, and told them his name was Orison, and then corrected himself with a thin smile.

"That was my name. I keep it in a book, third shelf, blue binding, so I don't lose it the way the others lose theirs. But you may call me Orison. Names are only handles. What matters is what's written inside."

"You remember everything," Mara said. It wasn't a question. She'd understood it the moment his eyes had moved over her.

"Everything that happens within the country's borders, yes. Every word spoken, every life lived, every debt incurred. The country is forgetting itself, young woman, and I am what remains of its memory. When it finishes unwriting, I will be a librarian of a library containing one book: the record of everything that no longer exists." He sipped his tea. "I intend to be thorough about it. It's the only dignity left to offer."

"Why tell us this?" Ilian's hand had drifted, casually, to rest near the strap of the box. "What do you want?"

"Want?" Orison set down his cup and laced his fingers, and the lamplight made his ink-dark eyes gleam. "I want to make you an offer. You are walking west — everyone walks west now, toward the threshold, though few of you know why. Between here and the door lie the Salt Fens, the forest that eats travelers' names, and the Ash Orchard, and none of it can be crossed safely by the living. Unless—" he raised one finger— "unless someone remembers the safe paths for you. Which I do. Every one of them. As they were five hundred years ago, and as they are today."

"And the price," Mara said quietly.

"The seed." Orison's gaze slid to the box on Ilian's back, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop. "Give me the last seed of the Garden, and I will guide you to the threshold myself. Safely. Swiftly. You'll stand before the door with days to spare."

Mara heard her own heartbeat. Beside her, Ilian had gone very still — stiller than still, the stillness of a man whose mission is balanced on a knife-edge. The seed was the whole of it. The seed was everything he'd been sent to carry home, everything he'd lied to her about, lied *around*, lied *beneath*, all these miles. To surrender it to a stranger in a dying city —

But Orison was looking at her, not at Ilian. Looking at her with an expression she couldn't name, an expression almost like pity, almost like hunger.

"And what would you do with it?" she asked.

"What I have done with everything else." He spread his small neat hands. "Preserve it. Keep it perfect. Keep it *unchanged*. The Garden died, child — murdered, yes, I know that story better than its killers did — but its memory needn't die with it. Give me the seed and I will keep it here, in the one place in this country where nothing fades. Safe. Whole. Forever frozen in the moment before its loss." He smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it somewhere, far back, like winter behind a warm window. "Is that not mercy? Is that not exactly what you've been trying to do yourself — carry it safely, keep it whole?"

*The vessel,* Mara thought suddenly, the word arriving cold from nowhere, from the parliament of crows, from the fox's low growl. *Not the seed. The vessel.*

Beside her, Ilian said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "We'll consider it."

Orison bowed his head graciously. "Of course. Consider it overnight. Sleep here — my guests sleep well; even dreams are archived. But understand one thing before you decide." He leaned forward, and the lamplight guttered, and for one instant Mara saw that behind the ink of his eyes there was page after page after page, endless and patient and terribly full.

"The moon completes its thirteenth turn in six days. After that, there is no door, no threshold, no west — only me, and my books, and everything that ever was, perfectly preserved and perfectly dead." He straightened his robes. "Choose quickly, children. Memory is the one mercy that can become a prison. I should know. I built it."

That night, in a guest cell lined with shelving instead of stone, Mara lay awake listening to the library breathe — the slow, living warmth of ten thousand remembered books — and heard, through the window, the distant murmur of a city rewriting its name-tags hour by hour, politely, gently, consenting.

And beneath her breastbone, faint but unmistakable, something that was not quite her heartbeat answered the rhythm of the shelves.

She pressed her palm flat against her sternum and whispered, in the tongue of beasts, the fox's oath-word for *truth*.

Something inside her whispered back.
