## 9. Beasts Who Remember

The crows came at the hour when the world forgot its own edges.

Mara felt them before she saw them — a pressure in her skull like a hand pressing against a door, familiar as her own heartbeat. All her life the gift had worked one way: she reached out to beasts, she listened, they answered if they chose. But since leaving the valley, since the night the stars went out and the wards shattered into falling glass, something had reversed. The animals were reaching for *her*. As though she had become the thing worth finding.

"They've been circling since noon," Ilian said beside her. He stood at the lip of the ridge with his hood up, watching the black shapes wheel over the drowned valley below. "I don't like it."

"You don't like anything that isn't you talking," Mara said, not unkindly. The fox — Vetch, who had sworn her oath and limped beside them through three days of unwritten road — was curled against her hip, and even Vetch's ears were pricked toward the sky.

"Crows aren't messengers," Ilian said. "Crows are *politicians*."

"Then we should be flattered. Politicians only come when there's something to be gained."

He didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed properly in two days, not since the ferryman's river of static, not since the librarian's city of half-names where people wore their identities on paper tags and rewrote themselves by candlelight because they could no longer hold a name in their heads past dusk. Something had been sitting behind Ilian's eyes since then, patient as a spider. Mara told herself it was grief for his country. She was beginning to suspect it was something else, but suspicion was a fire she wasn't ready to light. Not yet. Not while he was the only person alive who spoke her language — both of them, actually: valley-tongue and beast-speech, the double fluency that had made her trust him when everything else in the world was softening into rumor.

Below them, the drowned valley shimmered. It had been a village that morning — Whitelow, according to the crooked signpost that still stood, its letters rearranging themselves every few minutes like a man muttering in his sleep. By midday the houses had begun appearing only when the light came from the west. Now, in the failing afternoon, the whole place existed as a suggestion: rooftops like reflections on water, chimneys exhaling smoke that smelled of bread one moment and nothing at all the next. A dog had barked at them from a yard that wasn't there yet, and would not be there again tomorrow.

"The parliament is gathering in the orchard," Vetch said. Her voice in Mara's head was dry leaves and old cunning. "You'll want to be seated before the eldest speaks. Crows hate latecomers. They remember rudeness *forever*, which these days is saying a great deal."

"How do you know about this?" Ilian asked sharply — and then caught himself, because the question was aimed at a fox who could not answer him, and because foxes answered no one but their oaths.

"She knows things," Mara said. "That's what I keep her around for."

"I thought you kept her around because she'd die otherwise."

"That too." She scratched Vetch's ears. The fur there had grown back silver where the wasting sickness had taken it, and Mara had privately decided the silver was a medal. "Come on. If crows are calling a parliament outside a village that's forgetting itself, they're not gathering to gossip."

* * *

The orchard sat on the far side of the ridge, and it was the strangest thing they had seen yet, which was becoming a competitive category.

It was an apple orchard — or had been. Now the trees stood in rows that bent subtly wrong, like teeth in a mouth going crooked, and their fruit hung in every season at once: blossom, green fruit, ripe fruit, rot. Time had pooled here instead of draining away. Perhaps the trees remembered too well to leave. Their roots gripped soil that was still stubbornly, gloriously *solid*, the first ground in a day and a half that hadn't gone soft underfoot, and Mara realized with a lurch why the crows had chosen it. You could not hold court on ground that forgot you were standing on it.

They were already assembled. Hundreds of them — no, thousands, the branches black with them, and more arriving in long ragged skeins across the bruised sky, folding down out of the air like pieces of night being sewn back together. When Mara and Ilian walked under the first trees, the sound of them stopped. Not gradually. All at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important dies.

"They know you," Vetch murmured. "Word travels faster than feet. Faster than forgetting, even."

A single crow dropped from the highest tree and landed on the fence post six feet away. It was enormous for a crow, or perhaps simply old in a way that made size irrelevant; its feathers had gone grey at the throat like a beard, and one eye was pale as milk. It looked at Mara with the milky eye, and she understood without being told that the other eye — the dark one, sharp as a tack — was looking at something else entirely. At something inside her.

"You are the speaker," the crow said. Its voice was a dry rustle, a page turning. "The one who walks with the seed-bearer."

Beside her, she felt Ilian go very still. It was a practiced stillness, the stillness of a man who has rehearsed not reacting. She filed it away, in the drawer where she was keeping all the other small wrong things about him.

"We come seeking memory," she said aloud, in beast-speech, and translated for Ilian in the valley tongue out of habit. "The land is unwriting itself. The villages go. The names go. Even people—"

"People were always going to go," the crow said, with terrible mildness. "People forget. That is what people are *for*. No — you misunderstand your own errand, child of nowhere. You think you are walking through a dying country. You are walking through a *murder scene*."

The word went through Mara like cold water through cloth.

Around them, the parliament shifted. Thousands of feathers rustled at once, a sound like rain on a roof, like whispers, like the country itself holding its breath. Somewhere in the high branches a young crow made a noise it quickly swallowed.

"Murdered," Mara repeated. "The Garden."

"You say it as a question. Good. Questions are how the living stay living." The old crow hopped once along the fence post, closer. "Sit, speaker. This is a long telling, and the telling must happen while the ground still holds us. Sit, and let the parliament speak. We have carried this longer than your kind has carried writing."

They sat. Ilian sat slowly, carefully, the way a man sits when he is calculating the distance to the door. Vetch curled at Mara's feet with her chin on her paws, and her tail-tip twitched — once, twice — in a rhythm Mara had learned to read as *listen closely; there is a lie nearby.*

She did not yet ask herself whose.

* * *

"We were the Garden's first librarians," the old crow began, and around the orchard other voices took up the telling, passing it branch to branch like a bucket passed along a line against a fire. "Before there were books there was us. Before there were countries there was the Garden. You have heard of it — everyone has heard of it, which is precisely the problem, because hearing is not remembering, and the difference between those two things is the whole of this story."

"The Garden anchored the land," Mara said. "Everyone knows that much. It died, and the country started to forget."

"It did not *die*." The crow's dark eye fixed on her. "Dying is what happens when a living thing is finished. The Garden was not finished. The Garden was in its fourth millennium of blooming. It held every river in its root-memory and every harvest in its flowering, and the people of this country tended it, and were tended, and the bargain was simple: the gardeners gave the Garden their memories to hold — their dead, their seasons, their songs — and the Garden gave it all back, fresh, whenever they asked. Nothing was ever lost. Do you understand what that means? Nothing. Your grandmother's face. The taste of the first plum of summer. The name of every field. Held forever, returned forever."

"And the gardeners killed it," Mara said quietly. She didn't know how she knew. She knew the way she knew which animals were lying, the way she had known since childhood, the way that had gotten her called witch and worse inside the wards.

The crow cocked its head. "Fast learner. Yes. They killed it. And here is the part your histories will never tell you, because the men who wrote your histories were the men who sharpened the knives." The old voice dropped, and the whole orchard leaned in, ten thousand birds bending their heads so that the shadows moved like a tide. "They killed it *gently*. That was the horror of it. There was no battle. There was a committee. There was a generation of gardeners who looked at eternity and found it inconvenient. Every year the Garden returned everything they had given — including their griefs, including their shames, including the faces of everyone they had ever wronged — and some of them, the powerful ones, the ones with much to be ashamed of, grew tired of being *remembered*. So they built the wards. Do you know what the valley wards are, speaker?"

Mara's mouth went dry. "Places magic can't follow."

"Places *memory* can't follow," the crow corrected. "The wards were the knife. They cut a piece of the country off from the Garden's root-system and called it safety. Inside the wards, people could forget and never be reminded. And once they had proven it could be done — once a whole valley full of people lived fat and happy in blessed amnesia — the rest followed. They girdled the Garden's trunk the way you'd kill a tree you couldn't dig out. Ring by ring. Ward by ward. Each one a little border where remembering could not cross. The Garden starved for its people. It held on for a hundred years, returning memories to a country that increasingly declined to receive them, until—" the crow spread its wings, briefly, a gesture that took in the soft horizon, the drowned village, the crooked trees, "—until it could not hold anymore. What you call dying, speaker, we call abandonment. The country is not forgetting because the Garden failed. The Garden failed because the country *chose* to forget."

Silence. In it, Mara heard the orchard breathing — trees breathing, she was almost sure, slow and woody and aggrieved.

"The seed," she said at last. "The last seed. Where does it fit?"

"Ah." The crow's milky eye gleamed. "Now we arrive at the part where the story grows teeth. Ask your companion. He has known this part longer than you have known him."

* * *

She turned.

Ilian was already looking at her, and whatever mask he had maintained through three weeks of hard road and shared fire and the ferryman's dissolution and the polite horror of the half-named city — it cracked. Not broke. Cracked, the way ice cracks before it goes, showing you the black water underneath.

"Mara." His voice was low, meant only for her, though every crow in the orchard certainly heard it. "There are things I was waiting to tell you."

"Was." She heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "You were waiting. How long have you known the Garden was murdered?"

He didn't answer fast enough. That was answer enough.

"From the beginning," the old crow supplied helpfully, hopping down onto the fence rail between them like a magistrate taking his bench. "He was raised by the Remnant Order, speaker. The order of the last loyal gardeners — or so they call themselves. They have spent a century collecting what survived. The seed. The rites. The lists of vessels." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Do you know what a vessel is, in their doctrine?"

"Ilian," Mara said. Just his name. She watched him flinch at the shape of it.

"It's not what they think," he said. "Or — it is, but it isn't. Listen to me. The Order believes the Garden must be restored exactly as it was. Root and branch, the old bargain, the old power. And restoring it requires a vessel — a living memory, someone who can hold what the seed cannot carry alone. They've been searching for one for a hundred years. Searching for a person born with the gift of true speech, born outside the wards' reach, born—"

"—born," Mara said, and the words came out of her as if pulled, "to parents who crossed the wards before I was born. Valley-born. Ward-raised. Forbidden-gifted." She stood up. The ground under her felt less solid than it had an hour ago, and she could no longer tell whether that was the country unraveling or herself. "Every animal in this country has been trying to reach me since I crossed the border. I thought it was my gift waking up. It isn't, is it. They weren't coming to talk. They were coming to *look*. To see if I was what the Order promised."

Vetch's tail had gone still. The old crow said nothing, which from a crow was a roar of confirmation.

"You lied to me," Mara said to Ilian. "In the city. On the road. At the river. You told me you were sent to retrieve the seed."

"I was sent to retrieve the seed." He rose too, hands open, palms out, the universal posture of a man asking not to be struck. "That's true. That's all true. The seed is real, it's in my blood now, you know that, you've seen what it's doing to me. But yes. Yes. There was a second purpose. There was always a second purpose, and I told myself I'd tell you when I was sure, and then I was sure, and then I couldn't, because—"

"Because the sure thing was *me*."

The orchard held its breath again. Ten thousand black eyes on the two of them, and Mara understood suddenly that the parliament had not gathered to inform her. They had gathered to *witness*. Crows remembered rudeness forever; apparently they also remembered promises, betrayals, and the exact expression on a woman's face when she learned the man beside her had been carrying her like luggage toward a destiny chosen by strangers.

"I need to know," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she was proud of that, and ashamed of how proud she was, "everything. Tonight. Every word of your orders, every name of your Order, every detail of what a vessel *is* and what happens to her when the seed is planted. Because right now, Ilian, I am standing in a murdered orchard being told by crows that my whole life — my forbidden gift, my valley family, my exile — was arranged. Planted. Like a row of apples." Her hand found Vetch's fur at her ankle and gripped. "And the only person I trusted enough to walk into oblivion with turns out to be one of the gardeners' heirs."

"Not one of them," he said. "Not *one of them*. Mara, please—"

"The parliament will remain in session," the old crow announced, with the serene satisfaction of an official watching his trap close, "for as long as the ground remembers to exist. Which gives you until moonrise, seed-bearer. Speak truly, or fly."

Above them, the first star of evening should have been appearing.

There was only sky — empty, smooth, and blank as a page someone had turned over.
