## 10. Confrontation at the Ash Orchard

The orchard appeared at the end of the third day, and it was the wrongest thing Mara had yet seen.

They had walked out of the forgetting hills at dusk, following the crow-road — a seam of shadow that moved across the land like a vein of ink beneath skin, marking where the crows said memory still pooled. The land here was not soft, exactly. It was *tired*, like bread gone stale, holding its shape only because no one had yet thought to question it. And rising from that tired ground stood row upon row of trees, silver-barked and bare, their branches hung not with fruit but with ash.

Not burnt ash. Ash as if the trees had been made of it from the beginning — gray blossoms, gray leaves curled tight as fists, gray fruit that looked heavy enough to fall and shatter into nothing. The air between the rows smelled of cold hearths and old rain.

"The crows called this place the Orchard That Remembers Its Burning," Mara said. She spoke aloud, though Ilian was the only human who could hear her; the fox trotting at her heel needed no voice at all. "They wouldn't say what it remembered burning."

"They rarely would." Ilian's voice had grown quieter over the days of travel. Not softer — she had learned there was a difference. Softer was kindness. Quieter was weight. He carried the iron box slung across his back now, wrapped in his cloak, and even through wool and leather she could feel it humming against him, a hive-sound she felt in her own teeth. Lately she'd begun to suspect she felt it in more than her teeth. Lately, when the box hummed, something behind her sternum hummed back.

She did not know what to do with that. So, as she had done for three days, she said nothing.

The fox stopped at the first row of trees and sat down, its lame leg extended stiffly before it, and looked up at her with eyes the color of rusted copper. *Here,* it said in the beast-tongue, that language that lived somewhere below words, in the marrow. *This is a true place. True places are where truths can be spoken. Or where they cannot be avoided.*

"That sounds like a threat," Mara murmured.

*Most truths are,* said the fox, and began washing its paw with terrible unconcern.

They made camp in the heart of the orchard, in a small clearing where four ash trees leaned together like conspirators. Ilian built a fire with the practiced economy of a man who had done hard traveling all his life, and Mara watched him do it and hated how much she noticed about him now — the scar splitting his left eyebrow, the way he always set his back to a tree so he could watch two directions at once, the careful distance he kept between himself and her, as though proximity were a debt he couldn't afford to deepen.

Three days ago she had trusted him completely. Three days ago she hadn't known there was anything to distrust.

It had started small, as rot does. The way he flinched when she mentioned the crows' parliament. The way he'd asked her, twice, seemingly casually, what exactly the crows had told her about the Garden. The way his hands went still on the box whenever the word *vessel* drifted anywhere near the conversation — though he claimed never to have used that word himself, and she could not remember who had first said it. Memory was unreliable out here. The country forgot things, and sometimes it forgot them *into* you, leaving gaps shaped like answers.

But tonight, sitting across the fire with the ash trees shedding their silent gray around them, Mara found she could not carry it one more mile.

"Ilian."

He looked up. Firelight moved on his face.

"Tell me what the crows told me is true," she said. "Tell me you knew."

He didn't answer immediately. He was very good at silence, she was learning — wore it like armor, let it do work that words would only botch. But the Orchard That Remembers Its Burning was a true place, and true places are where truths cannot be avoided. She saw it happen to him: the moment the silence stopped being a shield and became a confession.

"Yes," he said.

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. Mara listened for the splash of it hitting bottom and heard nothing at all.

"You knew the Garden was murdered," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. She had expected shaking. Perhaps the shaking would come later; it often did. "By its own gardeners. You knew, and you've known since before we met — since the night you fell through my wards half-dead with your little humming box — and you have watched me walk toward this thing for days saying *we must reach the Garden, we must save the seed* as though it were a simple rescue."

"It is a rescue," Ilian said quietly.

"Don't." She stood. Around her, the ash trees seemed to lean in, listening; she felt their attention like weather. "Don't you dare manage me. I have been managed my whole life. The elders managed my gift into a secret. My valley managed itself into a lie about being the whole world. I crossed the rim of everything I knew for this, and I will not be managed by you too. What were you sent to do? All of it. Every piece. Now."

For a long moment he only looked at her across the fire, and she saw him weigh it — she actually saw the scales move behind his eyes, truth on one side and something else on the other, something he had carried so long it had grown into his skeleton. Then he reached back and drew the iron box off his shoulders, and set it on the ground between them, and the humming rose to fill the clearing like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

"The order I serve is called the Remnant," he said. "What's left of the Garden's gardeners — or rather, what's left of those who opposed what was done. When the Garden died, it didn't die cleanly. A thing that big leaves echoes. Splinters. Seeds." He touched the box, and under his palm the humming deepened, almost plaintive. "This holds the last true seed. We've guarded it for generations, waiting for the country to forget itself past saving. Because that was always the deadline. Once the moon completes its thirteenth turn, there is no door back. No restoration. Nothing."

"So we carry it home and plant it," Mara said. "That's what you told me. In the valley. By the river. At every fire since."

"We carry it home," Ilian said. "And we plant it. But a seed of the Garden doesn't go into soil, Mara. It goes into a vessel."

The word landed, and this time she heard where it had come from. Not the wind. Not the crows. Him — weeks ago, half-conscious at the border, speaking in the tongue of beasts to the only creature in the valley who could hear him: *the vessel must come willing.* She had thought she'd dreamed it. She had told herself she'd dreamed it.

"What vessel," she said. It was not a question. Questions leave room for answers. This was a door closing.

Ilian met her eyes, and she gave him credit for one thing: he did not look away.

"A person," he said. "The seed needs a living mind vast enough to hold a country's memory without breaking. The Remnant searched for generations. Bloodlines, bloodlines' failures, children tested and discarded." His jaw tightened. "And then, eleven years ago, an auger of the order found a reading unlike any other. A fragment of the Garden's own memory, alive, walking around inside a girl's skin. In a hidden valley, behind wards that magic could not follow — wards, we realized later, that someone built specifically to hide her. To keep the seed's vessel safe until she could be retrieved."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark rows, an ash tree released a single fruit. It fell without sound, and where it struck the ground there was simply no ground anymore — a small circle of pale nothing, like a hole cut in cloth.

Mara's legs folded under her. She sat down hard on the cold earth, not because she wanted to but because her body had decided the matter without consulting her.

"Me," she said. "You were sent for me. Not the seed. The seed is the *bait* — the errand — the thing that would make me walk willingly across a dying country—"

"No," Ilian said, sharp for the first time. "No. Listen to me. The seed is real. The mission is real. If we don't bring the seed home, everything dies — the country, the people still left in it, the animals, the fox, all of it, finished by the thirteenth turn. I have never lied to you about that part." He paused, and the pause cost him visibly. "I lied by omission. There's a difference, and it matters, and I know right now it doesn't matter enough."

"How does it open?" she whispered. "How does the seed go into a person?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "At the threshold. At the door the Remnant keeps beyond the last hills. The vessel pours herself into it. Her memory, her self, her name — all of it feeds the seed, and the Garden grows again from what she was." His voice roughened. "The books are vague about what remains afterward. Some accounts say nothing. Some say... something. A gardener. A green thing wearing the shape of a woman."

"And everyone in the country," Mara said slowly, because the crows had shown her this, had peeled it open like fruit and made her look, "everyone alive right now — the ferryman, the librarian's readers, every village that exists only at dusk — they're all *inside* the old country's pattern. If the old country is restored whole—"

"—they're overwritten," Ilian said. "Yes. The crows told you true. Restoration means erasure. The Remnant knows. Some of them don't care. Some believe the old country's lives outweigh the new." He shook his head. "I believed there was another way. I believed if I brought the vessel home gently, if she understood, if she *chose*—" He stopped. Laughed once, without humor. "Eleven years I've carried this. Do you know what it is to guard a box that hums, knowing it's waiting for a person? Knowing that every road you walk is walking toward someone?"

Mara looked at him across the fire and felt the strangest sensation: grief and fury occupying the same space, like two people trying to stand in one doorway. Eleven years. He had been a boy when they'd given him the box. She thought of the elders in her valley, who had taught her that her gift was shameful, that her hearing was a sickness — and learned tonight that they had been warden-gardeners all along, hiding her, growing her, keeping her fresh for harvest.

Even her exile had been arranged. Even her loneliness had a purpose she hadn't chosen.

"You should have told me at the border," she said at last. "The first night. When I pulled you through the broken wards and the whole sky was going out. You should have told me then, while everything was already ending. Do you know why?" She leaned forward. "Because I would have said no, Ilian. I would have turned around. And you would have spent the whole journey maneuvering a woman who can speak to every animal between here and the door — a woman who now knows the crows are watching, the fox is watching, the *orchard* is watching — and none of them are on your side unless I ask them to be. You didn't tell me because you were afraid of exactly this moment. Well. Here it is."

The fox opened one copper eye, then closed it again, satisfied.

Ilian bowed his head. In the firelight she saw, for the first time, how exhausted he was — bone-deep, year-deep, the exhaustion of a man who had held a secret so long it had become load-bearing, and who had just removed it and discovered he didn't know how to stand without it.

"If you want to leave," he said, "take the box. Take the seed. The Remnant will hunt me either way — I was told I was expendable the day I was given the mission, and I accepted that, and I won't pretend otherwise now. But the seed has to reach the door before the thirteenth turn, whatever happens to either of us. That part was never a lie. That part will never be a lie."

"Expendable," Mara repeated. Something cold slid down her spine that had nothing to do with the night air. "Who told you that?"

"The order's hand. The ones who give the orders." He finally raised his head, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not guilt, not shame, but fear, freshly remembered. "Why do you think I travel alone? Why do you think no one came after me when I fell through your wards half-dead? They don't retrieve their agents, Mara. They retrieve *assets*. And if I failed, the plan didn't change. Someone else would find you. Someone less gentle than I've managed to be."

Above them, the moon rose — fat, white, hurrying. Twelve turns behind it. One remaining after this night's business was done.

Mara sat in the ash orchard and felt the humming of the box move through the ground, through her bones, through that strange second pulse behind her sternum that had been growing stronger every day, and she understood suddenly that the choice in front of her was not whether to forgive Ilian. Forgiveness was a luxury for people with time. She had one turn of the moon.

The choice was whether to walk beside a man who had lied to her — or to walk alone toward a door that wanted to eat her.

"I'm not doing it," she said. "Whatever they planned at that door. Whatever the ritual is. I'm not pouring myself out for a dead country that murdered its own Garden and erased its own people and called it gardening. If the seed wants a vessel, it can negotiate."

Ilian stared at her. "Negotiate—with a seed?"

"With whatever's *in* the seed." She put her hand flat on the cold iron, and the humming leapt to meet her palm like a dog recognizing footsteps, and far away, or perhaps very close, something ancient turned in its sleep and began, faintly, to dream of her. "Because here's what the crows showed me that you apparently don't know, Ilian. The Garden wasn't just murdered. It *foresaw* it. And it left instructions. And I don't think the instructions say what the Remnant thinks they say."

The last ash fruit fell from the trees above them, silently, and where it landed the nothing spread — a handspan wider now, breathing at the edges, patient as the moon.

The fox stood, stretched its lame leg, and looked north, toward the last hills, toward the door.

*Dawn comes early,* it said. *And the hunters come earlier. Walk.*

* * *

They broke camp in the blue hour before light, and the orchard let them go grudgingly, branches raking at their cloaks like fingers reluctant to release what they'd caught. Behind them, as the sky paled, the ash trees began one by one to forget their own burning — color seeping back into bark, ghost-blossoms opening pink and tentative — and by the time the sun cleared the hills the Orchard That Remembers Its Burning was only an orchard, ordinary and mortal and already fading from Mara's memory like a dream she'd been scolded out of.

She walked ahead. She could not help it; her body insisted on the distance. Ilian followed with the box, and the fox ranged between them like a shuttle between threads, mending nothing, only keeping the weave from unraveling entirely.

Near midmorning, on a ridge above a river that ran backward toward its own source, Ilian spoke without stopping. "You asked what I'd have done if you'd turned around at the border. I've been thinking about it."

"I didn't ask. I told you."

"You implied the question." His jaw worked. "The answer is I don't know. That's the truth. I'd like to say I'd have let you go. I'd like to say a lot of things." He looked at her, and the morning light was unkind and honest on his face. "I'll tell you everything from now on. Before you ask. Before I want to. That's the only penance I have to offer, and I know it isn't worth what it cost you."

Mara kept walking. The backward river glittered below them, running home to mountains that might not exist by evening.

"Penance isn't the currency out here," she said finally. "Memory is. So start remembering out loud. Everything. Starting with these hunters you mentioned."

Ilian was quiet for three steps. Four. Five.

"There's a hand of the Remnant," he said, "called the Gleaners. They follow every agent, in case the agent fails." He touched his collarbone, unconsciously, where beneath the skin she could see the faint pale line of an old scar she'd never asked about. "Mine has been behind us since the valley. And Mara — they don't need my permission to take you. Only your body. And the thirteenth turn is twelve nights away."

Far behind them, at the crest of the ridge they had just abandoned, a figure stood motionless against the brightening sky — and then another, and another, patient as planted things, waiting for the travelers to choose their ground.
