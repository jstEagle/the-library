## 11. The Moon's Second Face

Ilian dreamed of the Garden the way drowning men dream of air.

In the dream he was young again — or rather, ageless, the way everything in the Garden had been ageless, green light filtering through leaves so vast that a single one could have roofed the elder hall of the valley three times over. He walked paths of white stone that remembered his feet, that softened slightly where he stepped, the way a dog leans into a familiar hand. The air tasted of honey and rain and something older than either, something like the smell of the world before it finished becoming the world.

He knew this dream. He had been having it since he was six years old, when the Remnant first put him under the sleep-teaching and poured the Garden into him in fragments, carefully, the way one feeds a fire too precious to burn hot. Every initiate dreamed the Garden. It was how they learned its geography, its liturgies, its laws. By twelve he could navigate its terraces blindfolded. By fifteen he could recite the Names of every tree.

But tonight the dream was wrong.

Tonight the white paths were cracked. Tonight the great leaves hung brown at their edges, curling inward like hands closing. And the light that filtered down through the canopy was not green but the color of old bruises, yellow going to black, and beneath it the whole vast living architecture of the Garden stood silent in a way no living thing should be silent.

*I know this part,* he thought, and hated that he knew it. *I know what comes next.*

Because the Remnant had shown them this too. Not in the sleep-teaching — never in the sleep-teaching — but in the sealed archive beneath the Cloister of Roots, where initiates who reached the ninth rank were permitted to look upon the historical record and come out the other side either broken or converted entirely to purpose. Ilian had gone in at nineteen. He had walked out with both things: broken somewhere deep and quiet, and certain, with the terrible certainty of the guilty, that the mission mattered more than his own wholeness.

The dream replayed it now, as it always did on the nights before hard travel, but tonight it refused to stay where history kept it.

He saw the gardeners come.

They had not looked like monsters. That was the detail the archive preserved most faithfully, the detail that haunted him across eleven years: they had looked like tired people. Men and women in soil-stained robes, carrying pruning-hooks and censers and ledgers, their faces lined with the particular exhaustion of those who have cared for something beautiful for a very long time and have begun, secretly, to resent it. They moved through the Garden's avenues in procession, and the trees leaned toward them as they passed, because the trees trusted them, because trust was the whole architecture of the place — a Garden built on the premise that memory freely given would keep it alive forever.

*Memory freely given.* He heard the phrase in the voice of First Root Ovelin, who had taught him his letters and his mission in the same breath.

The gardeners stopped at the heart of the Garden, where the Mother Tree rose out of the ground like a column holding up the sky. And the eldest among them — the dream gave her no face, only hands, old hands, gardener's hands, scarred from a century of thorns — raised her ledger and read aloud the grievances.

He could not hear the words. In the archive he had heard them; here, in the dream, they were withheld from him, and somehow that was worse, because it left him alone with the only thing about that day that had ever mattered: the silence afterward.

The gardeners cut the Mother Tree at moonrise. She did not resist. That was the second unbearable thing — she had roots in every soul who had ever loved her, and with all that power she chose stillness, chose to let them do it, as if she had already understood something her murderers had not. As if she had already decided that a Garden which must be defended from its own gardeners was already dead, and better to end as herself than continue as their instrument.

They burned her in sections over nine days, so that no single pyre would draw attention. They divided her seed among themselves for safekeeping, arguing over custody, each faction certain it alone honored her. And the country outside — the country that had existed because the Garden remembered it into being, river by river, mountain by mountain, name by name — began, very slowly, very politely, to forget.

That was the part the Remnant did not teach. That was the part Ilian had pieced together himself, in forbidden hours in the archive, cross-referencing the gardeners' ledgers against the fading maps: the country had not died *of* the Garden's death. It had died of the argument afterward. Nine factions, nine seeds, nine decades of hoarding and suspicion and doctrine, while the land they claimed to serve dissolved around them like sugar in rain. When the last faction finally resolved to act — to gather the scattered seeds, to find the vessel, to restore the Garden entire — eight of the nine seeds were already gone. Lost. Eaten by time, or by men, or simply unwritten along with the villages they had been hidden in.

One remained. The iron box. His burden. His purpose.

And the vessel — the girl born in a valley that magic could not follow, carrying a splinter of the Mother Tree's memory in her blood without knowing it, growing up forbidden even to speak with beasts because the valley elders feared exactly the thing she was.

*We are not stealing the seed back,* First Root Ovelin had told him, the night before he left. Her face had been kind. Kindness made it worse. *We are returning it. There is a difference, Ilian, and your life depends on knowing it.*

In the dream, standing amid the cracked white paths and the bruised light, watching the gardeners' torches crawl away through the darkening avenues, Ilian found himself asking — for the first time in eleven years, in the privacy of a dream no one could witness — the question the archive was built to prevent:

*Returning it to whom?*

The Garden had chosen stillness. The Garden had let itself die rather than become what its keepers wanted. And the Remnant, the heirs of the nine factions' last survivor, spoke always of mercy, of restoration, of giving a murdered country back its body — but the body they meant to rebuild was the body *they remembered*, governed by the laws *they had written*, tended by gardeners *they would appoint*. The vessel would pour herself out. The old country would return, whole and exact, down to every border and every hierarchy and every grievance in the eldest gardener's ledger.

And everyone living inside it now — the ferryman without a name, the librarian with her terrible perfect memory, the polite citizens of the capital rewriting their name-tags hour by hour, the fox with the ruined leg, Mara's whole valley full of people who had never consented to any of this — everyone would be erased. Unwritten. Not killed. Killed at least implied a moment of violence, a wrong that could be named. This would be quieter. This would be *correction.*

*Restoration,* said Ovelin's voice, kind and cold.

*Murder,* said something else. A new voice. Small and green and coming from inside his own chest.

Ilian woke.

* * *

He woke all at once, the way soldiers wake, hand already finding the knife under his bedroll before his eyes opened. Then he registered the details one by one: the ruined barn they had sheltered in, its walls half-faded into suggestion, morning light coming through rafters that were only sometimes wood. Mara asleep across the small fire's ashes, curled with her back to a wall that flickered at the edges of his vision whenever he looked directly at it. The fox awake, as always, watching him with its amber eye from its nest in the straw.

And the humming.

The box hummed. It had hummed since the night the stars went out, since he had carried it bleeding through the broken wards. But this morning the hum was different — it had a second note in it, a harmonic underneath, and the harmonic was answering something, and it took him three full heartbeats to understand that the something was *him.*

Ilian sat up slowly. He pressed his palm flat against his own sternum, over the heart, and felt it: faint as a moth's wingbeat, a pulse that was not his pulse. Running beneath his blood like a green thread through grey cloth.

*No,* he thought. *No, no, no.*

But his body, trained past argument by eleven years of discipline, already knew what it was. The seed had rooted. Not in the box — the box was a vessel, a shell, a way to carry the seed safely, and shells could fail, and apparently his body had decided, somewhere in these weeks of walking an unwriting land, drinking its strange water, breathing its thinning air, that *it* was a safer vessel than iron.

Or — and this was the thought that made him cold all the way down — the seed had not decided anything. Seeds did not decide. The Mother Tree had chosen stillness when the gardeners came. Perhaps what rooted in him now was not the seed's will at all, but her last instruction, planted in the seed the way a message is sealed in wax: *go where you are needed; grow where you can be free.*

He got up quietly, took the box, and walked out into the grey half-morning.

* * *

Outside, the land was doing its dawn work of almost existing. The hillside beyond the barn was real enough — grass, stones, a fence line dissolving into fog at the top where the farm it had belonged to had already forgotten itself. Below, a stream ran uphill with the patient confidence of water that had mislaid its instructions. The sky was the color of pewter. No stars last night. The moon had risen fat and wrong, showing them a face that cartographers had never charted, seas and craters arranged like a stranger wearing a familiar coat.

The thirteenth turn was close. He could feel it now in more ways than one — in the land's accelerating softness, in the fox's increasing urgency, and in the green thread under his own skin, which pulsed a little faster each day, keeping time with something vast and ending.

Ilian set the box on a flat stone and knelt beside it. His hands, he noticed, had dirt under the nails. He could not remember when that had started to feel natural.

"Talk to me," he whispered. "Tell me what you want."

The hum shifted. And — this had never happened before, not once in all the weeks — the hum became almost words. Not language. Something older than language, the way the beast-tongue was older than language, the tongue Mara heard and he had spent years learning badly from sleeping teachers. What came through was less sentence than season. Less command than climate.

It said: *gardeners came with good reasons.*

It said: *the tree chose.*

It said: *ask her. Ask the fragment. Ask what she remembers of me.*

His breath went out of him slowly. He knelt there a long time, long enough for his knees to ache against the stone, long enough for the sun — a pale, apologetic sun, thinner than it used to be — to clear the fog-line and throw his shadow long across the dying grass.

Because he knew what the fragment remembered. He had watched her remember it in pieces for weeks now: the flash-grief when she touched the seed, the dreams she woke from crying without knowing why, the way she sometimes paused mid-sentence with an expression of a woman hearing music from a house she'd lived in before she was born. She was remembering the Garden. And the Remnant's doctrine, drilled into every initiate, had a specific provision for what happened when a vessel began to remember ahead of schedule: the escort was to accelerate the journey, minimize contact, and above all ensure the vessel reached the threshold *before she understood what restoration would cost her.*

Before she understood that pouring herself into the door meant erasing everyone alive — including, the doctrine noted with bureaucratic gentleness, *the escort, whose devotion is assumed and whose consent is not required.*

*Whose devotion is assumed and whose consent is not required.* Eleven years he had carried that clause without letting himself read it plainly. He had told himself it was a formality. He had told himself the mission was mercy. He had told himself that a country saved was worth any price, and that the price would mostly be paid by him, and that he had volunteered, and volunteering was consent.

But the seed was in his blood now, and the seed remembered the tree choosing stillness, and stillness, he was beginning to understand, might have been the tree's final testimony: *do not restore me this way. Do not build me again on top of the living.*

Behind him, straw rustled. The fox emerged from the barn door, limping its careful three-legged lope, and sat down beside him with the unbothered dignity of a creature that had seen every kind of fool weep at dawn.

"You heard," Ilian said. It wasn't a question. The fox had heard everything since the night the stars went out; he suspected it heard things he hadn't said yet.

The fox regarded him with its one good eye. Then it spoke, in the dry rustle-voice that Mara translated but that Ilian, after weeks in its company, had begun to catch the shape of directly — another change he had not reported to anyone, another thread of green working through him.

*"You dreamed the burning,"* the fox said. *"All you Remnant cubs dream it. But you're the first one to wake up asking the right question."*

"What is the right question?"

The fox licked its ruined leg, unhurried, making him wait. *"Not whether returning the seed is mercy,"* it said at last. *"Whether mercy is yours to give. You carry her seed, boy, but she carries her memory, and the memory has a vote."*

Inside the barn, faintly, he heard Mara stir. Heard her murmur something in her sleep — and the murmur was in the beast-tongue, fluent and sorrowful, words he couldn't parse but whose grief needed no translation.

Ilian looked down at his hands. Under the dirt and the old scars, faintly visible now in the strengthening light, green traced the veins of his wrists like ivy following a wall. He flexed his fingers and the green moved with them, patient, alive, *his and not his.*

When Mara came out — hair tangled, eyes sharp, already reading his face the way she read animals, which was to say completely — he made the choice that felt, even as he made it, like stepping off a cliff in fog.

"We need to talk about what happens at the door," he said. "And this time I'm going to tell you the truth."

Her eyes narrowed. Behind her, the fox's ears went forward.

"Not all of it," Ilian added quietly. "Not yet. But more than I've ever told anyone. Because—" he pressed his hand flat against his chest, where two pulses now beat out of time with each other, "—because I think the seed just changed the terms. And I don't think I'm the only one it's talking to."

Mara went very still. And in the silence between them, clear as a struck bell, the iron box hummed — and this time, unmistakably, so did she.
