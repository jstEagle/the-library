## 12. The Ferryman's Farewell

The river found them before they found it.

Mara heard it first as a sound like rain falling upward — a hiss, a whisper, a thousand small voices all speaking at once and none of them saying anything she could hold onto. Then the trees thinned, and the ground beneath her boots went strange, soft as moss but wrong in a way her body understood before her mind did, and she stopped walking so abruptly that Ilian nearly collided with her back.

"What is it?"

"Listen," she said.

He listened. She watched his face do what every face did now when it met the country's forgetting: a flicker of confusion, then a tightening around the eyes, then the deliberate smoothing-over that people wore like a mask because the alternative was screaming. He couldn't hear it. Of course he couldn't. The river spoke in static, and static was only meaningful to someone who had spent her whole life listening for meaning underneath noise — the low complaint of roots, the gossip of crows, the long slow sentences of stones.

To her, the river was deafening. It was a crowd of every voice this land had ever had, all of them talking over one another because none of them could remember whose turn it was to speak.

They came down through a stand of birches whose bark peeled away into blank white paper, no marks on any of it, and there it was: a river wide as a lake and flat as hammered tin, running silver-grey under the starless moon. Nothing lived on its banks. No reeds, no frogs, no dragonflies stitching the water's skin. Even the mud at its edge looked less like mud than like the *idea* of mud, something sketched by someone who had forgotten why rivers needed shores.

And moored at a rickety wooden jetty — the only solid thing in sight, the only thing with grain and knots and honest rot in it — was a flat-bottomed ferry, and standing in the ferry was an old man with oars.

"There," Ilian said, with relief that cracked a little in the middle. "Someone. Maybe he knows the crossing—"

"He won't know his name," Mara said quietly. "If he ever did."

The fox, trotting ahead of them with his lurching, half-healed gait, paused at the top of the bank and looked back at her. His good eye was bright. *You know,* he said, in the tongue that lived below words. *You already know what you'll find.*

She did. She'd known since the first village that existed only at dusk, since the well that gave water only if you described your thirst aloud, since the road that unspooled behind them like thread pulled from a hem. The country was not dying the way animals die — fiercely, fighting, claws out. It was dying the way old people sometimes die in winter: politely, gradually, folding themselves smaller and smaller until one morning the bed was cold and everyone agreed they had simply slipped away.

The ferryman turned when their boots sounded on the jetty planks. He was weathered brown and bent like a bow that had given up trying to be straight, and his face was smooth in the terrible way — not young, just *unwritten*, features present but unclaimed, a face waiting for someone to come home to it.

"Crossing?" he asked. His voice was gravel and river-water. "I take folk across."

"Yes," Mara said. "Please."

"That'll be—" He stopped. He looked at his own hands on the oars, and something moved behind his eyes, a fish turning under ice. "That'll be whatever you've got. I don't recall my rates." A pause. "I don't recall much. But I recall these." He lifted the oars slightly, and Mara saw that they were beautiful, worn smooth as bone by decades — or centuries — of the same two motions, pull and return, pull and return. "Hands remember longer than heads. Somebody told me that once. Can't recall who."

*Careful,* the fox murmured, settling himself in the stern like cargo. *Ask him nothing about himself. Ask him about the water.*

"The river," Mara said, crouching at the edge of the boat so their eyes were level. "What's happened to it? It sounds like—"

"Like everything that's been lost," the ferryman said. "Aye. That's the river now. Every name, every face, every song this country ever misplaced comes down to the water eventually. Used to be it carried them onward, out to sea, off to wherever lost things go." He shrugged, a motion like a hillside shifting. "Now there's nowhere for them to go. So they just... pool. Talk to themselves. You can hear them?"

"I can hear them."

His face did something extraordinary then. For a moment the blankness broke open and she saw the man underneath — or perhaps the man the blankness had been built around, some old fellow who had once been somebody's father, somebody's nuisance, somebody's love. His eyes went wet and fierce.

"Then listen close, girl. Listen while you cross. There's good stuff in there yet. Not all of it's gone rotten. There's a wedding song from the eastern hills, and the smell of bread my mother used to—" He stopped. Blinked. "There's bread in there somewhere. I'm nearly sure of it."

Ilian climbed in beside the fox, the iron box strapped across his chest, humming its low hive-hum. Mara took the bench opposite the ferryman. The boat rocked free of the jetty with a groan of wood, and then they were out on the silver, and the sound closed over them like water over a stone.

* * *

It was worse in the middle of the river. Or better. Mara couldn't decide which word fit a thing that was both grief and miracle.

The static resolved, the closer they came to its heart, into fragments. She heard a woman laughing, young, the laugh cut off mid-note as though a page had turned. She heard a child reciting something — a prayer? a lesson? — the words dissolving even as they left the small mouth. She heard rain on a tin roof, a dog barking at nothing, an argument about money between brothers who loved each other, a lullaby in a dialect she didn't know but somehow recognized anyway, the way you recognize a room you dreamed once. Voices layered on voices, all of them speaking at once, none of them able to finish a sentence, and underneath it all a silence so vast it had weight, pressing up against the underside of the noise like a drowned thing against ice.

"They're still here," she whispered.

"Everything goes to the river eventually," the ferryman said, rowing. Pull and return. Pull and return. "Folk think rivers carry things away. Never understood that. Rivers carry things *together*. Everything ends up in the same water, given time." He glanced sideways at her. "You've got a listening face. My daughter had a listening face."

"You have a daughter?"

"I expect so." He said it without self-pity, without anything at all except a kind of patient honesty that made Mara's chest ache worse than weeping would have. "Most men like me had daughters. Whether I did or not, seems rude to say no to her now, whoever she was." Pull and return. "You keep listening, girl. Somebody ought to."

Ilian sat rigid in the stern, hands locked around the box. He couldn't hear any of it — she could see that, see him straining and getting only noise — and it frightened her a little, how alone he was inside that silence. How alone she wasn't. Her whole life the valley had called her gift a sickness, a shame, a thing to hide behind the ward-line where magic couldn't follow and neither, presumably, could whatever she was. And here was the truth of it laid out on black water: the country was losing every voice it had, and she was the one creature walking its soil who could still catch them as they fell.

*This is why,* said the fox softly, from the stern. His tail curled over his mangled leg. *This is what you are for. Not the seed-box. Not his order's plans. This.*

She didn't answer him. She leaned over the side instead, close enough that the grey water almost kissed her knuckles, and she listened, and she began — without deciding to, the way you begin humming — to repeat what she heard. Quietly. Under her breath. Catching each fragment as it surfaced and saying it back into the air above the water, whole: *the wedding song from the eastern hills; the smell of bread; the dog barking at nothing; the brothers' argument, which ended in laughter after all; the lullaby, which went like this.*

The ferryman's oars slowed.

"What are you doing?" Ilian whispered.

"Remembering," Mara said.

The static shifted. She felt it happen more than heard it — a great pressure easing, the way a forest goes quiet when the deer stop running. The voices didn't stop talking, but they stopped drowning each other. As if, having finally been repeated by a living mouth, they could rest a moment between repetitions. As if being remembered by someone were the same as being safe.

The ferryman had stopped rowing altogether. He stared at her across the boat, and the tears were running freely down his unwritten face now, cutting clean tracks through the blankness like water cutting channels in silt.

"Fifty years," he said. "Maybe fifty, maybe a hundred, I can't — I've been on this river since before the stars went out, girl, and I have watched ten thousand people cross and not one of them, not *one*, ever listened back." Pull. Return. He started rowing again, slower, gentler, as though the boat were made of eggshell. "My mother's bread. You got my mother's bread. Barley loaves, baked on a hot stone, and she'd break the end off for me before supper and tell me not to tell my sisters." His voice wobbled and held. "I haven't thought of my sisters in — I don't know how long. Ida. No. *Ida.* One of them was Ida."

"Ida," Mara repeated, and sealed it somewhere deep, in the place where she kept the fox's oath and the shape of the valley at dawn and every other thing she had decided would outlive the forgetting.

* * *

The far bank came slowly. Rivers of static, the fox told her, had no fixed width; they were as wide as the distance between what was lost and whoever might grieve it. Today, with Mara leaning over the gunwale and catching names like falling leaves, the crossing was short. Other days, the ferryman said, other days it took till dawn and the passengers arrived having forgotten why they'd wanted the other side at all.

They were perhaps twenty lengths from shore when it happened.

Mara noticed it first as a change in the oar-sound — the rhythmic knock of the oar against the thole-pin going irregular, skipping, like a heartbeat missing beats. Then she noticed the ferryman's hands, and a cold finger touched the base of her spine.

They were fading. Not dramatically, not the way the hunters' forest had blinked them in and out — but gently, the way dusk gives way to dark. His knuckles had gone translucent. Through the back of his hand she could see, faintly, the grey shimmer of the water beyond.

"No," she said. "No, wait—"

"Now, don't you fuss," the ferryman said, and his voice was going too, thinning at the edges like a note held past breath. He kept rowing. Pull and return. Pull and return. "Been waiting on this a long while, truth told. A man can't row forever. And I can feel it, girl, I can feel it tonight — the river's ready to take me back. All those years keeping folks moving across the lost, and now I get to join the cargo." He smiled, and the smile was the most complete thing about him. "That's not dying. That's coming home."

"You could fight it," Ilian said hoarsely. From him it sounded strange, urgent — the man of secrets pleading with a stranger to hold on. "People fight. We've seen—"

"People fight when they've got a reason to stay." The ferryman shook his head, and his hair was going silver-transparent at the temples, strands of moonlight. "I stayed because of the oars. Because hands remember longer than heads, and as long as I could row, I figured part of me was still needed. But look." He nodded at the shore, drawing closer, close enough now that Mara could see individual stones on the bank, real stones with real shadows. "You've near crossed. The boat's done its work. And that girl there—" he tipped his chin at Mara, and his eyes, the last solid things about him, were bright as a boy's, "—that girl is going to remember my mother's bread. So what am I holding on *for*, eh? What's left of me that isn't better off in the water?"

"Don't go," Mara said. It came out small. Childish. She hated how small it was and said it again anyway, louder. "Don't go. I'll remember you. Your name — tell me your name, we'll find it, I'll carry it, I'll—"

"Oh, sweetheart." The tenderness in it undid her completely. "I let my name go a long time ago. It was heavy. Names are the heaviest thing a body carries, and I had oars to mind." The boat scraped stone. They had reached the shallows, and the ferryman was nearly gone — a suggestion of a man, an outline drawn in river-light, still standing at his oars. "You listen to an old boatman. You can't save the lost by gripping them. Believe me. I've gripped every plank of this boat for fifty years and it never kept anybody aboard." He raised one fading hand — not waving, blessing. "But you can carry what matters. Bread. Sisters. Songs. Carry them light, girl. Carry them forward."

"Your daughters," Mara said desperately. "If you had daughters — I'll find them, I'll tell them—"

"If I had daughters," he said, and laughed, and the laugh was already half water-sound, "they'd have had a stubborn old fool for a father, and they'd have loved him anyway, and that's enough. That's more than enough." The boat stilled against the bank. He looked past her shoulder, at the land rising beyond — the last of it, thin as smoke, hills that were mostly rumor now — and something in his unfinished face eased, the way a field eases under rain. "Well. Look at that. Still a country over there. Barely. Go on, then. Go on, all of you — even the fox, especially the fox, he owes me a fish from '09."

*I do not,* said the fox, with dignity.

"See? Remembered it myself." The ferryman grinned. And then, softer, to Mara alone, in the voice of a man laying down an enormous weight: "Somebody will remember this country. You just swore it, in your way, all that listening. So I reckon I can go now. I reckon it's finished what it needed me for."

"I vow it," Mara said. She stood in the shallow water, the cold biting her ankles, and made it formal, made it iron, the way the fox had taught her to make vows — not with words only but with the marrow behind them. "By root and river, by bread and sister and song: someone will remember. I will remember. This country will not vanish unwitnessed. Whatever's coming, whatever I have to become to do it — I will carry it all forward, and I will plant it somewhere it grows again. Do you hear me? *It will grow again.*"

For one instant the ferryman blazed back into solidity — every line of him, the bent back and the scarred hands and the face that had surely once been somebody's whole world — and he looked at her the way people look at sunrise.

"Aye," he said. "That'll do."

And he came apart like mist off morning water, gently, gladly, the last of him streaming down into the river that had waited so long to gather him home — and the static, for one impossible moment, went utterly silent, the whole grey expanse of it hushed, as if every lost voice in the country had paused to watch one of their own arrive.

Then the noise rose again, softer now. Almost like singing.

The empty boat bumped against the stones. The oars, orphaned, drifted loose. Mara stood in the shallows a long time, letting the river run cold around her legs, and did not wipe her face, because the grief was hers and she had sworn to carry things and she would start with this.

When she finally turned, Ilian was watching her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — stripped of mission, stripped of lies, just a tired man moved past his own defenses. The fox pressed his warm flank against her calf.

*You mean it,* the fox said. Not a question. *All of it. The planting.*

"Every word," Mara said.

Which was when Ilian went stiff, his head snapping up, his hand closing hard around her wrist. On the ridge above the far bank, where the last hills smoked against the starless sky, something glinted. Not starlight. There was no starlight left anywhere.

Lantern-light. Three lanterns, moving fast, converging — and beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, the sound of hounds that bayed in human voices, calling a name she had only heard once, in the ash orchard, in Ilian's ashamed mouth:

*The Remnant Order. They've found us.*
