## 13. Hunters of the Remnant Order

*Ilian*

They came at dusk, which was the hour the forest forgot itself most thoroughly.

Ilian had been watching the tree line for two days without admitting it. The habit was old — older than his vows to the remnant order, older than the seed sleeping in its iron box against Mara's back — and it had never once been wrong. The birds went quiet in a certain way before danger: not silent, but *tidy*, as if something had combed through them and removed every careless sound. That was how the forest looked now. Tidy. Combed.

"Mara," he said quietly. "Stop walking."

She stopped. She had learned to stop when he used that voice, just as he had learned to trust the way she cocked her head when the animals spoke — a small tilt, like a door opening on oiled hinges. Right now she was listening to a wren that had been following them since morning, chattering from branch to branch with the frantic persistence of a creature delivering a message it did not fully understand.

"The fox says three men crossed the river at noon," she said. "Walking against the current's memory. It says they smelled of ash and old paper." Her eyes came up to his. "Old paper, Ilian."

He knew what that meant. Everyone who had ever served the Garden's remnant order knew what that meant. The hunters carried their orders written on pages pressed from the orchard's burned heart, and those pages smelled forever of smoke and vellum, of everything the Garden had been before it died.

"Three," he said. "It will be more than three."

"How many?"

"All of them, if we're unlucky. The order does not send half measures for the vessel." He heard himself say *the vessel* and watched her flinch, watched her jaw set, and understood that the wound between them had not closed — only scabbed over thin enough to see light through. Since the ash orchard, since his confession, they had walked together like two people sharing an umbrella in a storm neither would acknowledge. Necessary. Close. Not warm.

"They'll take me," Mara said. It was not a question.

"They'll try to take both of us. You because you're the vessel. Me because—" He paused. There were truths he had not yet given her, and one of them sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone. "Because I've failed. The order doesn't forgive failure. It repurposes it."

The wren burst upward from the branch, shrieking, and the whole forest seemed to exhale.

* * *

*Mara*

They came out of the forgetting like knives drawn from water.

Mara saw them first as absences — gaps in the trees where trees should have been, shapes her eyes kept sliding off the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. Then the gaps resolved into figures: six of them, maybe seven, robed in gray so pale it was nearly the color of unwritten things, their faces wrapped in cloth that might have been linen or might have been fog. Each one carried a long hook-blade on a chain, and each blade hummed faintly, a sound Mara felt in her teeth rather than heard.

She had heard that hum before. In the iron box. In Ilian's voice, sometimes, when he spoke of the Garden with the reverence other men reserved for gods.

"Gardeners," she said.

"Hunters," Ilian corrected grimly. "Gardeners grow. Hunters prune." His hand found hers in the failing light — not tenderly, but with the practiced grip of a man passing a weapon. "Run."

They ran.

The forest received them the way a dream receives the dreamed — eagerly, then badly. Paths that had existed that morning simply weren't there; the ground remembered being a stream bed and became one under their feet; a hill they crested forgot its own height halfway down and dropped them ten feet into bracken. Behind them the hunters moved without any of these difficulties, because the hunters had done something to themselves that made the forgetting slide off them like rain off waxed canvas. They had, Mara realized with a cold turn in her stomach, *given up their memories*. Every step they took cost them something they could never get back, and they had paid it gladly, in advance, for this.

That was what devotion looked like in the remnant order. That was what she was running from.

"Ilian!" One of the hooks sang past her ear and buried itself in a birch that immediately forgot how to be a tree, collapsing into a heap of pale logs and scattered leaves. The chain retracted whistling. "What do they do if they catch—"

"Don't let them touch you." He hauled her left, off the path, downhill toward the sound of water. "The blades don't cut flesh. They cut *having been there*. If one marks you, part of you stops having ever happened. People come back from it missing years. Missing names. Missing—" He ducked a second blade; it grazed his shoulder and the sleeve of his coat simply ceased, along with, briefly, the shoulder beneath it. He staggered, caught himself, swore in the beast-tongue — her tongue now too, or becoming it — and kept moving.

"You're bleeding nothing," she said stupidly, and he almost laughed.

"That's the polite version. Run faster."

* * *

*Ilian*

There was a strange comfort in it, and Ilian hated himself for finding it.

Fighting beside someone. Having someone at his back whose breathing he could track by ear, whose movements he could predict not because he had trained with her but because he had *learned* her — the way she dropped her left shoulder before changing direction, the way she used her voice as a tool, calling commands to the crows and foxes that harried the hunters from the canopy. A magpie dive-bombed a hunter's hooded face; a badger, summoned from nowhere by some negotiation Mara conducted mid-stride, took out the legs of another. She fought like a conversation, and the forest listened to her even while forgetting everything else.

He hadn't fought beside anyone since Vess. And the thought of Vess — his partner, his friend, the one the order had sent through the breach before him, the one who had come back in a box smaller than the one he carried — should have doused whatever warmth was kindling in him. Instead it sharpened it. Because Vess had died alone, pruned by these same hooks, and Ilian had been told it was honorable, and he had believed it, and believing it had been the first lie the order ever planted in him.

"Hunter on your right!"

He spun, drew the short blade he'd hidden in his boot since the valley — the one concession to violence he'd allowed himself — and met the hook-chain with steel. The impact jarred up his arm. Under the gray wrappings, the hunter's eyes found his, and Ilian saw what he had known he would see: nothing. Not cruelty. Not mercy. A person sanded down to purpose, smooth as river stone.

"Serril," he said aloud, giving the name he'd worn in training, the name this particular hunter had once answered to across a decade of shared bread and shared watches. "Serril, it's Ilian. You taught me the third form. You told me the Garden loved us."

For one heartbeat, something flickered behind the wrapped face. Something lost, groping for a shape.

Then Serril said, in a voice like paper tearing, "The vessel comes with us. You are already spent."

And Ilian understood, finally, completely, what Mara had begun to suspect at the ash orchard: he had never been meant to return. He was the delivery mechanism. The letter that gets burned after reading. The order had sent him through the breach knowing the crossing alone would likely kill him, and if it didn't, the hunters would tidy him away afterward, because a man who has seen the vessel's face is a man who knows too much about what the Garden intends to do with her.

*Mara*, he thought, *they're going to erase me the way they erase everything else.*

And underneath the fear, absurdly, stubbornly: *not today.*

* * *

*Mara*

The forest forgot them mid-fight, and it nearly ended everything.

Mara felt it happen — felt the world's attention slide off the little clearing where she stood back to back with Ilian, four hunters down and three still circling. The trees around them blurred. The ground softened like bread in milk. And worst of all, *she* began to blur: her own name going slippery in her mind, *Mara, Mara, my name is* — she gripped it like a rope over a drop.

"It's forgetting us," she gasped. "The fight. All of it. If it forgets us completely—"

"We stop having been here." Ilian's voice came from very close, very steady. "Hold on to something true. Anything."

So she did the only thing she knew. She spoke.

Not words, exactly — not at first. She opened herself the way she had opened herself to the dying fox beyond the ward-line, the night the stars went out, and poured everything she had into the forest's fading attention: *here, here, here, we are here, I am Mara Villalobos and this is Ilian and we are fighting and I am afraid and I refuse.* The gift that had been forbidden in the valley, the gift that had made her a secret and a shame for thirty years, roared out of her like a dam breaking — and the forest *listened*. The trees snapped back into focus. The ground firmed. Somewhere overhead, a parliament of crows took up her insistence and repeated it in a hundred raucous voices, *MARA, ILIAN, HERE, HERE,* until the clearing rang with their names like a bell.

The remaining hunters faltered. Memory, forced upon them, was pain. One dropped his hook and clutched his wrapped head. Another made a sound that might have been grief.

"Now," Mara said.

They broke the circle together — Ilian's blade and her voice, steel and song — and ran, and this time the forest ran with them, bending paths back into existence ahead of their feet, because she had asked it to remember them and it had agreed.

* * *

*Ilian*

They collapsed at moonrise in the hollow of a fallen oak so large it must have remembered being a cathedral. Moss cushioned the curve of dead wood. Water seeped somewhere near. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

Then Mara said, "You knew them. The one you called Serril. You said *you taught me the third form.*"

"Yes."

"And they called you spent." She turned to look at him. In the dark her eyes were the color of deep water, and he could not read them, and he deserved that. "Ilian. Tell me the rest. All of it. Tonight, or I swear by every animal in this forest, I will walk away and let the order have both of us."

So he told her. The delivery mechanism. The expendable courier. The way the remnant order measured everything in the Garden's currency — memory, sacrifice, pruning — and had weighed him before he ever crossed the breach and found him lighter than the mission. How the hunters had orders to retrieve the vessel and dispose of the messenger, in whichever sequence was convenient.

"I believed in them," he finished. "The way you believed in your valley elders. That's not a defense. It's just the truth. They gave me a purpose when I had none, and I paid for it the way everyone pays them. In pieces of myself I didn't know I was spending."

Mara was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above them, the crows had settled into a low muttering roost-talk, keeping watch.

"You could have lied," she said at last. "A better lie than the one you told at the ash orchard. You could have let me believe you were still their man."

"Why would I?"

"Because the truth makes you useless to me. Because the truth means the people hunting us want *both* of us gone, and you can't protect me from them, because they are what you used to be." She exhaled slowly. "But you told me anyway."

"I'm tired of paying them in pieces," Ilian said. "You're the first thing in years I've wanted to keep whole."

She didn't answer. But after a while, in the dark of the hollow oak, her hand found his injured shoulder — the place where the hook had taken his sleeve and briefly the flesh beneath — and rested there, warm and deliberate, checking that he still existed.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch. The crows say the hunters have stopped chasing."

"Why would they stop? We're wounded. We're slow."

"They haven't stopped hunting," she corrected softly. "They've stopped chasing *us*." Her hand tightened on his shoulder. "Ilian — the crows say the hunters have turned north. Toward the door. Someone has told them where the threshold is. Someone who isn't one of us."

And far to the north, under the starless wheel of the thirteenth-turning moon, the forest began — very quietly — to forget the way.
