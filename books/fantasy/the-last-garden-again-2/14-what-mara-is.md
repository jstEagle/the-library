## 14. What Mara Is

The wound in her side had stopped bleeding an hour ago, which should have been a comfort and was instead a warning. Wounds that stop bleeding in a forest that forgets itself are not healing, Mara thought. They are simply being forgotten too.

She sat with her back against the trunk of an ash tree — one of the last solid things in a wood gone soft at its edges, where the birches flickered like candle flames and the ferns dissolved into green static whenever she looked away. The hunters' blades had found her twice before the fox's diversion let them slip free: once across the ribs, once deep in the meat of her left shoulder. Ilian had torn his own shirt to bind them, his hands shaking so badly she'd had to guide the knot herself.

"You need rest," he said now, crouching across the small fire he'd dared to build. The flames burned slightly wrong here, their light arriving a heartbeat after the wood cracked. "Real rest. In a place that will still be here when we wake."

"Every place is still here," Mara said. "It just isn't *itself* anymore."

He didn't laugh. He almost never laughed now, and she missed it with an ache that surprised her, because she had not known until recently that she was keeping count of a man's laughter.

The iron box sat between them, as it always did. It hummed its low hive-hum, and tonight — perhaps because of her blood loss, perhaps because the country around them had thinned to little more than a rumor — the hum had changed. It had a pull to it now. A gravity. When she closed her eyes she could feel it tugging at something behind her sternum, some small dense point inside her chest that she had never been able to name and had long ago stopped mentioning to healers, because they only frowned and said *some people are simply built strangely, child.*

"I'm going to look at it," she said.

Ilian's head came up sharply. "Mara—"

"Not open it. Just—" She reached out. "It's been calling me for days. You've heard it too. Don't pretend you haven't."

He had gone very still, the way animals went still, the way he himself went still when he was deciding whether a truth could survive being spoken. "The seed isn't meant to be touched by—"

"By what?" she asked. "By people? By valley-born? By me?" She smiled, and it cost her something. "You keep almost saying things, Ilian. Half-words. The whole country outside these woods is made of half-words now. I'd like one whole thing before the moon turns again."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a crow shifted on a branch and muttered in its sleep — and even asleep, even dreaming, the crows remembered. That was the lesson of the parliament, the cold knowledge she carried now like a stone in her boot: *the Garden did not die. It was murdered by its own gardeners.* And Ilian had known. He had knelt in the ash orchard and told her the shape of it — the vessel, the retrieval, the person they wanted more than any seed — but he had not told her why *her*. Not truly. He had given her the architecture of the lie without its foundation.

So yes. She was going to touch the box.

Her fingers hovered over the iron lid. The metal was warm — warmer than the night justified, warmer than a dead thing had any right to be — and beneath the warmth she felt a rhythm, faint and patient, like a pulse waiting under snow.

When her skin met the iron, the world went quiet.

Not silent — quiet. The difference mattered. Silence was absence; this was attention. The whole forest leaned in. The fire held its breath mid-flicker. Even the hum of the box resolved into a single sustained note, and then that note opened, the way a door opens, the way a throat opens to sing—

And Mara fell.

* * *

She was standing in a garden.

Not walking into it, not remembering it — standing in it, already there, the way you are already in your own body. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, the gold of late afternoon stretched into eternity, and the air itself was thick enough to drink. Every plant grew in every direction at once: roses climbing through the branches of apple trees, wheat ripening in the shade of figs, rivers of mint running downhill toward pools where fish the color of stained glass moved without hurry, because nothing in this place had ever needed to hurry.

She knew it. That was the terrible thing. She knew the turn of the path before her feet took it. She knew that the third tree on the left bore fruit that tasted of honey and grief, and that no one ate it except on the last day of the year, together, weeping and laughing in the same breath. She knew the names of the gardeners — not their titles, their *names*, the true ones, the ones they used only among themselves when the wards were sealed and the work was done.

A woman was kneeling by a bed of white flowers, and when she turned, Mara saw her own face.

Older. Weathered. Eyes the color of deep water. But hers — unmistakably, unbearably hers, the way a river downstream is still the river.

"You came back," the woman said, and her voice was Mara's voice pitched low with age. "We wondered if you would. We planted you deep, little splinter. Deep enough to survive the cutting."

"The cutting." The words came out of Mara's mouth before she understood them, and understanding arrived a half-second later, cold water down the spine. "They cut the Garden. Your own order. Your own hands."

"Some of us." The older woman rose, brushing soil from knees that had knelt in this soil for two hundred years or two hours; time here was a suggestion, politely ignored. "Some of us argued until the end. But a Garden that anchors a whole country becomes a chain, child. Whoever holds the root holds everyone. Our gardeners forgot that they were servants and began to believe they were gods, and a god with a root in its fist will squeeze." She touched Mara's cheek, and her hand smelled of loam and rain. "So we cut it. All but one seed. And we did one thing more — the thing the council never sanctioned, the heresy of heresies."

"We hid the memory," Mara whispered. "Inside people."

"Inside *one* person. Generations ago. Passed down like a coal passed hand to hand in the dark, each carrier sworn never to let it catch flame, never to look at it too closely, never to ask why they could speak to beasts when no one else in the valley could. Why their gift was forbidden." The old woman's eyes were kind and pitiless. "Forbidden, Mara. Think about that word. Who forbids a gift? Only someone afraid of what it's *for*."

The garden shivered. The light guttered like the fire had. And Mara understood, all at once and forever, that the elders of her valley had not been protecting her from her gift.

They had been caging it. Caging *her*. Because somewhere in the founding of that valley, someone had known exactly what slept in the bloodline they took in, and had built the wards not against the wild magic outside —

but against the seed inside her family's generations.

Every punishment. Every warning. Every night she had crept past the ward-line to speak with a dying fox while her heart hammered with guilt — none of it had been transgression. It had been *resistance*, instinctive and blind, a splinter of the Garden straining toward everything it had been buried to forget.

"I didn't choose this," she said, and hated how small her voice sounded.

"No one chooses to be a door," the old woman said gently. "But doors get to choose when to open."

* * *

She surfaced gasping, and the first thing she saw was Ilian's face, white as the ash bark behind him, both hands gripping her shoulders.

"Mara. *Mara.* Come back."

"How long?"

"Minutes. You wouldn't stop saying words in a language I didn't—" His jaw worked. "In the beast-tongue. Old forms of it. Older than anything I learned in the order."

She looked down at her hands. They were her hands. Her mother's strong fingers, her father's crooked knuckle on the right index finger, the scar on her palm from the ward-line wire the night the stars went out. Everything accounted for. Everything hers.

Except it wasn't. Except none of it was.

She started to laugh, and the laugh broke in the middle and became something else, and Ilian held her while she shook, his arms careful around the wounded shoulder, his chin resting on her hair.

"They planted me," she said finally. The words tasted like grave-dirt. "Before my grandmother's grandmother. Some gardener with a heretic's conscience walked into the valley carrying a splinter of the Garden's memory and gave it to a family like handing off a stolen child. And then the elders built the wards, and wrote the laws, and forbade the gift — not knowing, maybe, or knowing exactly — and I grew up thinking my whole self was a crime."

"It isn't."

"Isn't it?" She pulled back and looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and clear. "Everything I loved about myself — the foxes, the crows, the nights past the ward-line, the feeling that the valley was a coat that never fit — all of it was just the Garden leaking out of me. I'm not a woman who has a gift, Ilian. I'm a *fragment* wearing a woman. Twenty-some years of Mara Villalobos, and underneath—" she pressed her fist against her sternum, against that small dense point the box had been pulling at all along, "—underneath there's a piece of a murdered paradise, waiting to be dug up."

The fire had burned low. Above them the starless moon climbed, one turn from done.

"That's why the hunters won't stop," Ilian said quietly. "That's why the librarian wants the seed. Everyone thinks the vessel carries the seed." He paused, and she watched him decide — watched the war move through his face, the man who had lied to her once for mercy choosing, this time, with his eyes open. "But the seed is only a key. The memory is the lock. The order doesn't want the seed, Mara. It never did. It wants *you*."

"And if they pour me into the old country—"

"Then the old country lives again." His voice was flat with honesty now, honesty like a blade laid bare on a table. "And everyone alive inside it now — the ferryman's ghost, the villages at dusk, everyone who ever forgot — they're overwritten. Erased to make room for what was."

Mara looked at her hands again. Her mother's hands. Her father's knuckle. Herself.

Or a costume of herself, stitched over a relic.

She thought of the valley sealing shut behind them at dawn, the wards knitting closed like a wound, her mother's face growing smaller and smaller and then gone. She thought of the ferryman dissolving mid-river, content, and her vow — *someone will remember this country* — and how the vow had felt like it came from the deepest part of her, deeper than any planting.

Maybe it had. Maybe the deepest part of her was not the Garden at all. Maybe twenty years of loving a dying fox and a nameless ferryman and a lying, loyal man had grown something in the splinter that the original Garden never had.

"I need you to tell me one thing," she said. "And I need it to be the whole thing, roots and rot and all. When you were sent to retrieve the vessel — did you know it was a person?"

"Yes."

"Did you know it was *me*?"

He was silent for three heartbeats. "I hoped," he said. "From the first night, when you spoke to me in the beast-tongue and didn't know you were doing it. I hoped. And I let myself be carried by the hoping, because the alternative was telling a stranger she was a relic, and watching her become one in front of me."

Mara nodded slowly. It was not forgiveness. Forgiveness would come later, or it wouldn't, under the starless moon when there was nothing left between them but truth. Tonight it was only acknowledgment: *you lied, and the lie had a shape, and I can hold the shape without letting it cut me.*

She lay back against the ash tree, exhausted past exhaustion, and the box hummed beside her, and the small dense point behind her sternum hummed back, two notes finding harmony.

Above the trees, the moon wheeled onward through its thirteenth and final turn.

And far off — beyond the soft trees, beyond the forgetting hills — Mara felt something answer her. Not the box. Not the Garden's ghost. Something new, something that had never existed in the old country or the dying one: a faint, stubborn, green insistence rising through her wounds like sap through cut bark, whispering in her own voice, in her mother's voice, in the fox's voice, all at once:

*Not a fragment. Not a relic. A seed that was never planted in the ground.*

*Choose your own soil.*

She opened her eyes. Ilian was watching her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — hope, terrified of itself.

"There's another way," she said. "Isn't there. There's a way that isn't their door and isn't the country's death."

His breath caught. "How do you know?"

"Because I'm not what they planted anymore." Mara pressed her palm flat against the earth, and beneath her hand, impossibly, in a forest that was forgetting how to be a forest, a single shoot of new green broke the soil. "I'm what it grew into."
