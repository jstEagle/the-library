## 15. The Librarian's Price

Ilian had learned to read the land the way a sailor reads weather, and what the land was telling him now was simple: they had perhaps two days of country left beneath their feet.

The forest around the ash orchard was thinning in ways that had nothing to do with logging. Whole stretches of it simply weren't there when you looked directly at them — not burned, not cleared, but *unwritten*, the trees reduced to a kind of gray suggestion, like pencil marks someone had half-erased and then abandoned. When Mara walked through those places her footsteps made no sound at all, as if the ground couldn't quite decide whether to hold her weight. His own steps rang faintly, tinny and wrong. And between them, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the iron box hummed its hive-song, louder now than it had ever been, as though the seed inside could smell how close the ending was.

Mara walked ahead of him with that new lightness she hadn't been able to shed since the orchard — since the green shoot had broken the soil under her palm and refused, thereafter, to die. She kept glancing down at her hands as if expecting them to leaf over. Once, he'd seen a curl of vine-shadow move across her wrist when there was no vine anywhere near.

*She knows,* he thought, and his stomach clenched on the thought like a fist. *She's beginning to know what she is. Which means soon she'll ask what she was for.*

Which meant soon she would ask *him.*

He had lied to her once already — small, necessary, the lie about why the remnant order needed her specifically — and that lie had sat in his chest for eleven turns of the moon like a swallowed stone. But it was nothing, a pebble, beside the truth he was carrying now: that the order's archives held a second account of the Garden's death, one that named the vessel not as a courier or a key but as *soil*. That the plan, if the seed could not be returned to the old ground because the old ground no longer existed, was to return it to the only living ground that remembered the Garden from the inside. That Ilian himself had signed the requisition, four years ago, in a script he barely recognized as his own, believing then that any vessel would understand — would be *glad* — to become the last garden again.

He had believed it because he had never once imagined the vessel would have a laugh, or a way of swearing at crows, or a habit of feeding strangers before feeding herself.

Behind them, distantly, something screamed. Not an animal. A place. The forest forgetting a valley it had held for a thousand years, and the valley objecting all the way down into silence.

"We should find shelter," Ilian said. "Real shelter. Stone."

"There isn't any stone left this close to the orchard," Mara said without turning. "There's barely any *orchard.*" But she stopped, and tilted her head, and Ilian saw her do the thing she did — the thing that still made the hair rise on his arms — listening to something no human ear had any business hearing.

Then he heard it too. Or rather, he heard the absence of it: birdsong, which had been threading the whole ruined afternoon, cut off all at once along the eastern treeline, clean, like a door closing.

"She's here," Mara said quietly.

"How can you possibly—"

"Because everything with wings just decided to be somewhere else." She turned, and her eyes were steady, and tired, and older than the girl who had knelt by a dying fox beyond the ward-line on the night the stars went out. "The librarian."

* *

She found them at dusk, in the shell of a chapel that had forgotten its god but kept its walls — stone, at least, real stone, cold enough to ache through clothing. They had built a fire from pages. That was the librarian's doing, Ilian realized afterward; the chapel's floor had been ankle-deep in loose sheets when they arrived, thousands of them, drifting like autumn leaves, each one blank except where memory clung in faded ink. Now the fire ate them cheerfully, and the words went up as sparks, and Ilian tried very hard not to think about what those sparks might have been.

She came in through the broken doorway without hurrying, and the dusk came in behind her like a dog on a leash.

Ilian had met her twice before, in the deep archive beneath the remnant order's citadel, before the world outside began to fray. Both times she had frightened him more than the hunters did, because the hunters wanted things and could therefore be denied, and the librarian only wanted to *keep*, and keeping was a hunger with no floor to it. She looked exactly as he remembered: a tall, narrow woman in a coat stitched from a hundred different uniforms, her gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be bookmark ribbons, her eyes the pale gold of old paper held to flame. She carried no weapon. She carried, instead, a ledger, bound in leather gone soft as skin, and she carried it the way other people carried infants.

"Mara Villalobos," the librarian said, and her voice was the voice of a reading room — hushed, vast, patient. "And Ilian Verre. Courier, third rank, oath-sworn, expendable." She smiled at his flinch with genuine, terrible kindness. "Forgive me. I remember everyone's file. It's not cruelty; it's cataloguing."

"You're a long way from your archive," Ilian said.

"My archive," she said, "is a country that no longer exists. So am I. We are colleagues, in that." She drifted toward the fire, warming her long hands, and Ilian watched the flames bend subtly away from her, as if even heat preferred not to be remembered too closely. "I've come to make you both an offer. I'll state it plainly, because I know how much you distrust elegance."

"Don't," Mara said.

"I haven't yet—"

"You were going to offer us preservation." Mara stood, slowly, and Ilian saw the firelight catch something moving faintly beneath the skin of her forearms, like green seen through ice. "You were going to offer to save the old country. All of it. Every river running uphill, every village that exists at dusk, every citizen rewriting their name-tag in the capital. You were going to say the word *forever* and mean it as a gift."

The librarian regarded her for a long moment. Then she laughed — softly, genuinely, a sound like a page turning.

"Oh, you're better than the file suggested," she said. "Yes. That is my offer. Sit, both of you, and hear its shape."

* *

They sat. It was that or stand all night arguing with a woman who could wait out geology.

"The seed," the librarian began, nodding at the box against Ilian's chest without appearing to look at it, "is the last intact piece of the Garden's root-mind. Planted in living earth, tended rightly, it regrows everything: the wards, the borders, the deep memory of the land. Everything the country was before its gardeners murdered it." She let that word sit among them, deliberately, watching Ilian rather than Mara, and Ilian understood that she knew he had not yet confessed what the crows had told Mara at the parliament. She was holding that knowledge like a card. "But planted in *living earth* — that's the difficulty. The earth here is dying. Unwriting. In twelve turns' worth of moon, there will be no earth at all, only sky with a rumor of ground. So my alternative."

She opened the ledger across her knees. Its pages were not paper. Ilian had never gotten close enough before to see what they actually were, and now, in the firelight, he wished he hadn't: they were thin transverse slices of something pale and veined, and each one bore writing that moved, gently, the way water moves under ice.

"A preserved country," she said. "Complete. Exact. Every person currently alive within the old borders, held precisely as they are at the moment of sealing — not dead, never dying, unaging, unwanting, safe. The rivers run as they ran. The capital remembers its own name. The ferryman keeps his oars." Her voice softened into something almost tender. "No one suffers. No one is lost. No one forgets anything ever again. That is the price of the seed, and it is a fair one: I take the seed, and I give you back a country that cannot hurt anyone, including itself. Including you."

"And the people in it," Mara said. Her voice was very quiet. "They don't get asked."

"They are being asked constantly," the librarian said, "by the country itself, which is dissolving them. I am offering them the courtesy of stopping. Would you prefer they be consulted while they're being erased? Shall I put it to a vote among people who cannot remember yesterday?" For the first time, something moved behind the gold-paper eyes — not anger, but a weariness so old it had fossilized into manner. "Child. Do you know what I watched, before I became this? I watched a city burn, and I saved three books, and I have spent two hundred years learning every method of saving that exists. Freezing is the only honest one. Everything else is gardening."

The word landed in the chapel like a dropped blade.

Ilian felt Mara go still beside him — not the stillness of fear but the stillness of a drawn bowstring — and he spoke before she could, because he had learned in thirteen turns of walking beside her when her silences were questions and when they were decisions.

"The hunters," he said. "Your hunters. They're yours?"

"They are mine the way weather is mine," the librarian said mildly. "I fund them. I aim them. I grieve them, occasionally, when the forest forgets them mid-pursuit — which it does, you may have noticed, with increasing enthusiasm." She closed the ledger with a sound like a kiss. "You have until the twelfth turn completes. After that my offer expires, because after that there will be nothing left worth preserving, and the only question remaining will be whether anything grows in the ruin at all." She rose. The dusk at her back stirred. "Consider it. Consider it *carefully*, courier. I remember every refusal I've ever been given. There have been nine. I keep them in the appendix."

At the threshold she paused, half-turned into the dark, and said, without looking at either of them:

"One more item for your consideration, free of charge. Ask your courier what the remnant order intended the vessel *for*, if the old earth proved unrecoverable. He knows. He has known since the orchard. Perhaps since before." A pause, delicate as a pressed flower. "Leadership is lonely, Ilian Verre. But loyalty that outruns honesty is only a slower form of betrayal. I catalogue that, too."

Then she was gone, and the dusk went with her, and the birdsong did not return.

* *

Mara slept first. She slept the way she always did now — lightly, sitting up, one hand flat against the ground as if taking its pulse — and within an hour the ground around her sleeping hand had sprouted a ring of small, improbable flowers that had no business existing in a forest made of erasure. Ilian sat across the fire from her and did not sleep at all.

He turned the librarian's offer over, and over, the way he'd once turned the order's mission orders over, looking for the clause that would excuse him.

It was a good offer. That was the horror of it. It was *kind*, in the way that glass is smooth. Everyone saved, everyone whole, everyone stopped — a snowfall country, beautiful and finished, in which the ferryman would row forever without knowing why, in which the citizens would rewrite their name-tags one final time and then rest, in which no child would ever again watch a star go out and wonder. Against that: the third path, Mara's path, the one she had touched in the orchard with her green-veined hand. Plant the seed in herself. Become the door. Let the old country finish dying — every person in it finishing with it — so that something new, something *alive*, could begin.

He had done the arithmetic a dozen times since the orchard and it never balanced. On one side: hundreds of thousands of lives, frozen but continuing, real in every way that mattered except the ones that mattered most. On the other: one woman who laughed at crows, who fed strangers first, who pressed her palm to dying ground and made it argue back.

And underneath both sides, coiled and patient, the truth he still had not given her: that the order had sent him to fetch a vessel, and vessels, in the order's oldest documents, were not passengers. They were *planted.* That when he had knelt in the archive four years ago and sworn his oath, the oath had included a contingency clause he had never spoken aloud — *should retrieval of the seed prove impossible, the courier shall deliver the vessel to ground of the Council's choosing* — and that ground, the Council had made clear, meant her body, whether or not her mind consented, whether or not her heart survived the planting.

The librarian knew. The hunters knew. The crows knew. Somewhere in her blood and her new-green dreams, Mara was beginning to know.

Everyone knew but her, and every hour he withheld it, he was doing to her exactly what the librarian offered to do to the country: keeping her safe, and whole, and stopped.

The fire burned low. One of the burning pages curled open at the last moment, and Ilian caught a glimpse of ink before it blackened — handwriting, a child's, practicing letters: *the fox says the stars are eggs* — and then it was ash, and whatever child had written it was ash, or worse than ash, a name-tag rewritten hourly by a country that had forgotten how to hold names.

He looked at Mara sleeping among her impossible flowers, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had already decided. Had decided, probably, in the orchard, when she had said *I'm what it grew into* and he had felt hope terrify itself in his chest. He would refuse the librarian. He would refuse the order. He would stand between her and both of them, alone, without telling her the size of what he was refusing, because telling her would break the hours she had left into pieces shaped like grief, and she needed every whole hour she could get to learn how to be a door.

Loyalty outweighing truth. The librarian had named it precisely, and she was right that it was a slower betrayal, and he chose it anyway, the way you choose to carry a wounded person out of a burning building without telling them the building has no exit — because some truths are weights, and some weights are not the wounded one's to bear.

*I'll tell her,* he promised the dark, knowing the promise was soft as the ash-child's page. *After. If there is an after. I'll tell her everything, and she can hate me in the new country, or the old one, whichever we win.*

He banked the fire, and as he did, the light caught movement at the chapel's broken window — a shape, gray and lean, limping, one ear folded wrong, sitting in the frame like a sentinel that had been waiting there all night for permission to matter.

The fox looked at Ilian. Ilian looked at the fox. Neither of them, by any power in heaven or earth, could speak to the other.

But the fox looked at Mara's sleeping hand, and the ring of flowers around it, and then back at Ilian, and its expression was so unmistakably, so *insufferably*, the expression of a creature that had heard every word the librarian said and had opinions —

— and then its head snapped east, ears pricked, hackles rising, toward the treeline where the birdsong had died, where the dark between the unwritten trees had begun, very quietly, to fill with lantern-light.

Many lanterns. Moving in formation.

The hunters had not waited for the twelfth turn.
