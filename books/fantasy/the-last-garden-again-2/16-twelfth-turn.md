## 16. Twelfth Turn

The moon hung above them like a coin at the bottom of a well — visible, unreachable, and impossibly far from anything that could be called ground.

Mara stopped walking because there was nothing left to walk on. Not in the way a traveler stops when the road ends; the road had ended days ago. She stopped because her feet had begun to make no sound at all, and she wanted to understand why before the understanding frightened her.

Below her boots — where stone and soil should have been — stretched sky. Not reflected sky, not water masquerading as it, but the actual thing: deep blue-black, scattered with the pale ghosts of clouds drifting beneath her heels like fish under river ice. Somewhere down there, or perhaps up there, or perhaps nowhere anymore, was the country that had been hers to cross.

"We're walking on what's left of it," Ilian said behind her. His voice had grown quiet over the last days, quieter with every turn of the starless moon, as if he were rationing words the way they rationed the last of their food. "Or what's forgotten of it."

"Is there a difference?"

He came to stand beside her. In the light of the thirteenth-moon-to-be — the twelfth turn nearly finished now, the final one waiting like a held breath — his face looked carved from the same substance as everything around them: beautiful, faintly translucent, uncertain of its own edges. The iron box rode against his chest on its strap, humming its low hive-song. It had hummed louder since the ferryman dissolved. Since Mara had made her vow to remember. As if the seed inside had heard her and leaned toward the promise like a plant toward light.

"There's always a difference," Ilian said. "Forgetting is gentle. Being forgotten isn't."

* * *

They walked on through the hour that had no name, because hours too were going. Mara kept her eyes on the fox ahead of them — her fox, limping along the impossible horizon line as confidently as if it were a hedgerow back home. The wound he'd carried the night the stars went out had never fully healed; it couldn't, here, where healing itself was a memory being let go. But he walked. He always walked. And when the way grew strange beyond even strangeness's tolerance, he paused and waited for them with his tail curled around his bad leg, patient as an elder at a door.

The land beneath them flickered sometimes. That was the worst part. A hill would rise underfoot — real, grassed, smelling of rain and sheep — and then dissolve mid-stride, and they would be walking on sky again without ever having fallen. Once, Mara saw a farmhouse assemble itself out of nothing around them: whitewashed walls, a chimney breathing smoke, a dog barking somewhere inside. She heard a woman singing a lullaby in a language Mara almost knew. Then the walls went thin as breath on glass, and the song faded, and the farmhouse became a suggestion, then a rumor, then nothing.

She hadn't spoken for a long time after that.

Now, as the false dawn that wasn't a dawn began to gray the underside of the world, the fox stopped at the lip of something. A valley lay below them — an actual valley, holding still, the last solid geography either of them had seen in two days. At its center stood a single tree, enormous and bare, black branches spread against the starless dark like veins in a closed eye.

"The ash orchard," Ilian murmured.

"No." Mara shook her head slowly. "The orchard burned. We saw it burn."

"This is what remembers it, then. Or what it remembers of itself." He glanced at her. "We should go around."

But the fox was already descending into the valley, picking his way down a slope that existed only where his paws touched it, and the tree stood at the center of everything the way a truth stands at the center of a conversation you've been avoiding.

Mara started down after him.

The descent took no time at all, which frightened her more than a long descent would have. One moment the rim of the valley; the next, the shadow of the great bare tree falling across them both, cool as well-water. Up close, the trunk was not wood but something like petrified lightning — grey, veined, warm to the touch when Mara laid her palm against it without deciding to.

It hummed. The same note as the box.

"Oh," she said softly.

"It knows the seed," Ilian said. His hand hovered near the trunk but did not touch it, as if he feared being recognized. Perhaps he did. Perhaps he should have.

The fox settled at the roots, curled his tail over his nose, and watched them both with amber patience. *Sit,* his posture said. *Speak. There is little enough left in which to do it.*

* * *

They sat with their backs against the tree, shoulder to shoulder, because there was nowhere else to sit in all the dissolving world, and because Mara was done pretending the nearness didn't matter.

"One turn," she said. "Twelve gone. Thirteen coming. Tell me what happens when it completes. All of it this time. No half-truths shaped like comforts."

Ilian was silent for so long that she turned to look at him. He was staring up through the bare branches at the featureless moon.

"When the moon finishes its thirteenth turn," he said, "the country finishes forgetting itself. Every place, every name, every person in it. Not death — death leaves something behind, a body, a grave, a story. This is different. This is subtraction. The country will simply never have been, and everyone in it will never have been in it, and there won't be anyone left to grieve because grief requires a rememberer." His jaw tightened. "Except us. Except whatever we carry out. The seed. You."

"You said the order had another plan. Before you told me about the vessel. Before—" She stopped. Saying *before I learned you lied to me* still cut, even now, even after the forest fight where they'd fought back to back against hunters who forgot which of them they were chasing mid-stroke. "Tell me what the order believes happens. Their version."

"Their version?" He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only weariness worn smooth as riverstone. "Their version is that we return the seed through the door, the Garden takes root in its old bed, the old country restores itself whole — rivers, cities, kings, borders. Everything as it was. Everything saved."

"And the cost."

"You know the cost."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it in your voice, not mine."

He closed his eyes. "Everyone alive now. Everyone born since the Garden died. The valley. The elders. The Council of Roots. The librarian, frozen forever behind her perfect memory. The ferryman — well." A ghost of pain crossed his face. "The ferryman chose his ending already. But the others haven't chosen anything. They'd be erased to make room for a country that died rather than change. Restored like a painting over a living wall."

"And your order calls that mercy."

"My order calls it restoration. They call the people who live now *squatters.*" He opened his eyes and looked at her, and she saw how tired he was — tired in a way that sleep could not reach, tired down in whatever part of a man holds his certainties. "I believed them. When they sent me. I was young and the Garden's stories are beautiful, Mara. You have no idea how beautiful. Halls of living green, fruit that remembered the taste of every season, a country that sang to itself. And then I fell through the breach and landed half-dead in a valley full of people who had never heard of any of it, and they were kind to me, and they fed me, and they had lives — whole lives, real ones — and I understood that my mission required all of them to become a footnote. Or less than a footnote. A blank page."

The tree hummed beneath them. Above, the moon crawled.

"Why didn't you tell me sooner?" she asked. It was not an accusation. She had spent her anger already, in the ash orchard that had been an orchard, in the forest that forgot them mid-battle. What was left was only the question, and questions asked honestly deserve honest silence before honest answers.

"Because the truth made me expendable and you essential, and I thought if you knew, you'd send me away. Or worse — you'd try to protect me, and get taken instead. The hunters want the vessel. They don't care what condition I'm in." He flexed his right hand; she'd seen the scar there since the forest, since one of the hunters' blades had found him shielding her. "And partly — partly — because I kept hoping the order was wrong. That there was a third path none of us had imagined. I hoped hard enough to build my whole silence on top of it."

"That's not hope, Ilian. That's cowardice wearing hope's coat."

"I know." He said it simply, without flinching, and she realized that this, more than any apology, was the thing she'd needed from him: not contrition performed, but truth endured. "I know it now. The librarian taught me that, if nothing else. She offered me the same bargain she'll offer until the end — preserve everything, freeze everything, save nothing — and I stood there in her tower of perfect memory and realized I would rather see this country end honestly than persist dishonestly. And I decided that alone. Without telling you. Because I thought choosing for you was the same as protecting you." His mouth twisted. "I keep making that mistake. It's the order's mistake, really. Decide for the world, spare the world the burden of deciding. I brought it with me like lice."

Mara looked out across the valley. The far slope was fading even as she watched — not dramatically, not with any drama at all, just growing paler, the way a face grows pale before a faint. Somewhere on that slope, once, there had been villages. Names. Graves. Weddings. All of it thinning into the general blue of forgetting.

"When I touched the seed," she said quietly, "and saw what I am — I hated you for a while. Not for lying. For knowing before I did. For carrying the shape of my life in your pocket like a map while I wandered around lost inside it." Her hand found the loam at the tree's root, cool and real. "A splinter of the Garden's memory. Planted in a valley family three generations back. My mother's face isn't my mother's face — or it is, but it's also the Garden's, borrowed, a mask over the mission. My gift was never forbidden. It was the point. I was grown, not born. Sown." Her throat tightened. "Do you know what that does to a person? To learn that the most private thing about you — the thing they punished you for hiding, the thing that made you strange even among strangers — was assigned to you? That your soul has a delivery address?"

"I can imagine," Ilian said, "that it feels like being used."

"Yes."

"And yet." He turned toward her fully. "Yet you stood in the doorway of the world's ending and chose to become the new door. Not restore the old tyranny. Not freeze the living. Something neither the order nor the librarian nor the dying country itself imagined. You chose rebirth, Mara. On your own terms. If you were sown, then what's bloomed is yours. No one else's. Whatever planted you doesn't own the flower."

She was silent a long moment. The moon moved. Below the valley's floor, clouds drifted by like slow white ships, and for a heartbeat she saw faces in them — or thought she did — the country dreaming itself farewell.

"I'm afraid," she said at last. It felt enormous to say aloud, larger than any confession she'd pried out of him. "Not of the unmaking. I've made peace with that, mostly. I'm afraid of what remains. Will I be me? Will there be enough of Mara Villalobos left in the Garden to know the fox when he comes limping through the gate? To know you?" She looked at him. "You said you'd hold the door. You said you'd stay. But Ilian — staying is easy to promise and terrible to do. The order will hunt you until your last day. The remnant will call you traitor. And the woman you stay for may not remember why you stayed."

"Then I'll tell her," he said. "Every morning, if I have to. *You were Mara Villalobos. You spoke with beasts when it was forbidden. You carried a dying country in your chest and planted it instead of yourself. You forgave me — not because I earned it, but because you chose to.*" His voice roughened. "Forgiveness isn't absolution. I know that. Absolution means the debt is paid, and mine never will be. Forgiveness is just — a decision. Made daily. Like tending a garden."

Something in Mara's chest loosened — some knot she'd been carrying since the crows showed her the murder, since the orchard, since the lie. It didn't vanish. Wounds like theirs don't vanish. But it loosened, the way a fist loosens not because the anger is gone but because the hand has found something better to hold.

"Then here is my choice," she said. "Today. Under this blind moon, in this valley that's already half a memory. I choose to forgive you. Not because the lying was small — it wasn't — and not because the mission excuses it — it doesn't. I forgive you because you're here. Because when the hunters came, you stood between me and them. Because when the librarian made her offer, you refused it alone and carried that weight by yourself rather than put it on me. Because you're learning — slowly, badly, like a man learning to walk on sky — to stop deciding for other people." She exhaled. "And because tomorrow one of us becomes a door, and doors don't hold grudges. They hold open."

Ilian made a sound low in his throat — not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. "That's the least romantic forgiveness ever spoken."

"I'm a splinter of a murdered garden's memory walking on the sky of a country that's forgetting it exists. Romance left the country several turns ago."

This time he did laugh — brief and cracked and real — and the sound seemed to startle even him, as if he'd forgotten his voice could do that. The fox lifted his head at the noise, ears forward, tail giving a single slow sweep across the root-loom.

*I remember laughter,* the fox's posture said. *Good. Keep some. You'll need it on the other side.*

Above them, the moon reached the top of its arc and began, imperceptibly, to descend. Twelfth turn, closing. Somewhere beyond the pale, the thirteenth waited with its arms open.

Mana — Mara — leaned her head against the humming trunk and let herself rest, just for this hour, in the company of the two beings in all creation who remembered her: a fox with a limp, and a man with a debt he intended to spend a lifetime paying.

"Ilian," she said, half-dreaming already. "When we find the door. When it opens for me and not for you — what will you see? On the other side. While you're holding it."

He didn't answer at once. When he did, his voice was very soft.

"Spring," he said. "I think I'll see spring."

* * *

They slept at the tree's roots, and the tree dreamed over them, and the moon came down.

Mara woke to the fox standing over her, hackles raised, nose pointed at the valley's rim — where the air had begun to shimmer like heat off summer stone, though there was no heat in this place, and no stone either. Through the shimmer, faint as a watermark, moved figures. Many figures. And behind them, vast and patient as weather, a presence she knew from the librarian's tower: a mind that remembered everything and loved nothing.

"They found the threshold," Ilian breathed beside her, already awake, already reaching for the box. "They followed the humming."

The fox looked back at Mara, and his amber eyes held no fear at all — only urgency, and below the urgency, something like ceremony.

*Rise,* said every line of his body. *One turn remains. Walk.*
