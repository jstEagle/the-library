## 17. The Door Out of a Country That No Longer Exists

The fox stopped walking at the edge of the world.

Mara felt it before she saw it — a change in the ground beneath her feet, or rather the absence of one. The land had been thinning for days, growing translucent as old paper held to light, but here it simply ended. Ahead of them stretched nothing: not darkness, not emptiness, not even the honest black of night. A nothing that was somehow *white*, like the blank page after the last line of a story, waiting to be written or left forever unfinished.

The fox sat down on the final finger of earth and curled its tail over its paws, its lame leg tucked beneath it, and looked back at them with eyes the color of green wood.

"Well?" it said, in the beast-tongue that only Mara heard. "This is where I stop carrying you and start trusting you."

Mara's boots stood at the very rim. When she looked down past her toes she could see through the ground itself — see the pale ghost of roots still holding shape below, and below them nothing at all. The sky above was the color of an unwritten letter. Somewhere behind them, impossibly far and impossibly near, the starless moon hung at its twelfth turn's end.

*Ilian,* she said — not aloud; they had long since stopped needing voices for each other — *is this it?*

He came up beside her. He had changed in the weeks since the ash orchard; there was grey now at his temples that hadn't been there when he fell through the broken wards clutching his humming iron box, and he moved differently, as though his body had learned some new gravity. His hand found hers without looking. That, too, was new. Once he would have flinched from touch, from debt, from everything she represented.

"That's a door," he said quietly, aloud, because some things needed the weight of a voice.

It took her a moment to see what he meant. Then the white nothing resolved, the way a face resolves out of fog, and she saw it: a door. Free-standing. No wall around it, no frame set in anything, just a door standing upright in the unmaking — dark oak gone silver with age, iron hinges green with old rain, and a handle worn smooth by ten thousand hands. It cast a shadow, which was absurd, because there was no light to cast it. It looked less built than *remembered*.

"It opens for the Garden's memory," the fox said. "Not for the seed. Not for the box. For the memory." Its ears flicked backward, toward Ilian. "Which is why your order sent you hunting a person instead of planting a seed, gardener. Seeds grow anywhere. Memories need somewhere to live."

Ilian said nothing. But Mara felt his grip tighten, felt the confession he'd already made once at the orchard rise again in him like water in a well — *the vessel is a person* — and settle, this time, into something quieter and worse. Resignation. He had known, since the moon turned its eleventh time, exactly what they would find at the end of the road.

"Say it anyway," Mara told him. "Out loud. One more time."

"The door needs you," he said. "And opening it costs you."

"I know." She squeezed his hand. "But say it anyway. While we can still afford honesty. In about an hour I suspect we won't be able to."

* * *

They made camp on the last of the land because there was nowhere else to make it. The fire burned strangely, flame without heat, light without shadow — the country was forgetting how fire worked, and the fire, poor thing, was doing its best from half-remembered instructions. Ilian set the iron box between them. Inside it the seed hummed its hive-song, fainter now than it had been in the valley, as though even the seed were growing tired of persisting.

Mara slept in fragments and dreamed in other people's memories.

That was what she was now — she understood it fully since the night she'd touched the seed and seen another life unfold inside her own skull. She dreamed of gardeners in silver robes arguing beneath trees whose leaves were pages. She dreamed of the first betrayal, the pruning done in fear, the murder committed gently, with love, by hands that had tended the Garden for a thousand years and could not bear to let it change. She dreamed of a splinter of that great memory being pressed into a valley midwife's palm, generations back, passed hand to hand like a secret until it became a girl who talked to foxes and was told her gift was sin.

She woke with tears freezing on her cheeks in air that had forgotten how warm worked, and found Ilian watching her.

"They're coming," he said.

She didn't ask how he knew. She could feel it too — a pressure against the edges of the world, like fingers testing a seam. Since the forest that forgot them mid-battle, since the hunters of the Remnant Order had nearly taken her apart piece by piece among the dissolving birches, she had been able to sense them the way animals sensed weather. Four hunters remained. They had lost two in the forest — lost them not to wounds but to the land's forgetting, swallowed mid-stride by ground that had stopped believing in itself — and the survivors had grown careful. Patient. They knew, as Mara and Ilian knew, that every road led here.

"And the librarian?" she asked.

Ilian's jaw worked. This was the wound he still kept from her, mostly — the night in the drowned archive when the all-remembering librarian had cornered him alone and offered him the old country, whole and perfect and frozen, in exchange for the seed. He had refused without telling her. She had learned of it anyway, because the librarian remembered everything and shared generously, and because Mara was learning to read him the way she read beasts — not words but posture, breath, the angle of grief.

"She'll come," he said. "She always comes. She's the only person in this country who can't forget, so she's the only person who can't let go." He fed a stick of nothing to the strange fire. "I refused her, Mara. I should have—"

"You did right."

"I decided for both of us. Again."

"Yes." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "And I've decided to forgive you for it, again, so we can stop apologizing to each other and start surviving. It's nearly the thirteenth turn. Save your guilt for whatever comes through that door."

The fox, which had been dozing with its nose under its tail, lifted its head suddenly, ears swiveling toward the white.

"They're here," it said.

* * *

The hunters came out of the west, walking on air.

There was no other way to describe it. The land had failed so thoroughly behind them that the four figures simply strode across the void as if it were a courtyard, their boots finding purchase on nothing, their grey cloaks stirring in a wind that belonged to no world anymore. Their faces were the worst part — calm, kind, sorrowful, the faces of people doing a hard necessary kindness, putting down a beloved animal. They had worn those same faces in the forest while trying to carve her open.

"Mara Villalobos," called the leader, a woman with a voice like a lullaby. Her name, Mara had learned in the forest, had been forgotten by herself long ago; the Order took names when it took vows. "Daughter of the valley. Splinter of the Garden. You are weary. You have carried what was never yours to carry. Let us take it now. Let us take *you* home."

"There is no home left," Ilian said, stepping forward, placing himself — uselessly, beautifully — between her and them. "You've seen the maps. There's nothing outside this circle of white but the forgetting. Your Order sent me to fetch a vessel and never told me the vessel would have to be emptied first. Did you know? Did all of you know what 'retrieval' meant?"

"We knew what restoration meant," the huntress said gently. "You knew too, Ilian. You simply preferred not to look at it until you'd fallen in love with the cargo."

Mara watched that land in him — watched him take it, refuse to flinch, stand inside the truth of it with his spine straight. *Yes,* she thought. *I love him. Say it later. Survive first.*

"And the librarian," the huntress continued, "agrees with us, incidentally. Which must pain you both."

"I'm here," said a voice from the east, "and I agree with no one."

She came walking across the void like a woman crossing a library floor, unhurried, dusted in paper-mold and lamplight, and beside her — Mara's heart stuttered — floated shelves. Actual shelves, laden with books, drifting in the nothing like boats. The librarian had been salvaging. All the way across a dying country, she had been rescuing books the forgetting had released, and she brought them with her now like a fleet.

"You cannot restore the old country," the librarian said. "I have read its ending. It is not in any book, because endings written down survive, and this one will not. If the Garden's memory pours back through that door, everyone alive inside these borders ends. Every village at dusk. Every ferryman. Even—" and here her ancient eyes moved to the fox with something almost like tenderness, "—even the beasts, though they pretend otherwise. I have offered alternatives. They were refused."

"Your alternative was a museum," Mara said. "Everyone alive, frozen mid-breath forever. Preserved like flowers in glass. That's not saving a country. That's embalming it and calling the embalming mercy."

"It is *keeping*," the librarian said, and her voice cracked, and Mara saw it then, plain as a scar: the librarian wasn't fighting for principle. She was the last rememberer of a place that was vanishing out from under her, and she could not bear to be the only one left holding it. "When everyone else has forgotten, someone must keep. That is all I am. That is all I have ever been."

The void shivered.

Above them — everywhere above them, though there was no longer a proper sky — the starless moon completed its twelfth turn and began its thirteenth. Mara felt it in her teeth, in the marrow the Garden's memory had given her, in the seed humming in its box. The last turn. The door would hold only through this one. After that: no door out, no country at all, only the white page and whatever chose to write itself upon it.

The hunters drew their blades — not iron but something older, pruner's tools shaped like swords. The librarian's shelves drifted closer, and books began to lift off them, opening themselves, pages turning, as though the library intended to defend her with everything ever written down.

And the door, standing free in the nothing, began to glow along its seams like a banked coal remembering fire.

"Now," said the fox, rising to its feet on three good legs and one ruined one. "Choose fast, splinter. And whichever way you choose—" it looked up at her, and its beast-tongue voice went soft in a way she'd never heard from it, soft the way animals speak of death, "—know that I remember the valley. I remember your mother's kitchen and the smell of the ward-flowers and the exact sound the border wards made when they shattered. Someone will remember this country. You made me promise. I keep my promises better than any of these two-legs."

Mara looked at the door. At the hunters closing like a slow, kind hand. At the librarian's fleet of rescued books. At Ilian, who had stopped standing between her and danger and now stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, which was the only position he'd ever truly wanted.

"The door opens for the Garden's memory," she said aloud, testing the truth of it against her own bones. "And I am the Garden's memory. Which means it doesn't open for anyone *else* — not for them, not for her, not for the Order. It opens for me. Or it doesn't open at all."

She stepped forward onto the nothing, and found, as the fox must have known she would, that it held her — held her the way a page holds ink.

Behind her, steel whispered loose. Pages rustled like wings. Ilian swore an oath in a language older than either of their tongues.

The handle of the door was cold under her fingers, and it waited — one breath, two — for her to decide what it would become.

Mara Villalobos closed her eyes, and reached for the third path no one had offered her.
