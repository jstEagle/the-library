## 19. Rooted

Ilian had held many things in his life. A sword, once, badly. A lantern through the flooded tunnels beneath the City of Half-Names. The iron box that hummed like a hive, which had been the whole of his purpose and the whole of his lie. He had held the seed against his chest on the night the stars went out, when the wards shattered and he fell through the broken border of a country that was already forgetting itself.

He had never held a door.

But that is what Mara asked of him now — not in words, for the woman who had been Mara Villalobos had gone somewhere deeper than words — but in the slow pressure of her new-rooted fingers closing around his hand, drawing it flat against the living grain of her bark. *Hold,* the gesture said. *Hold while I change. Hold while I become.*

So he held.

* * *

The thirteenth turn came faster than any moon should move. Ilian felt it before he saw it: a tightening behind his ribs, as though the sky itself were drawing breath to speak a final word. Above them the starless dark churned — not empty anymore but *busy*, full of the pale static of a country shedding its last memories into the air. He could taste them if he breathed too deeply. A harvest song from the eastern terraces. The smell of bread in a village that no longer existed at dusk or any other hour. Someone's mother's voice, saying someone's name, over and over, fraying with each repetition until it dissolved into the hum.

The fox sat at the edge of the clearing where Mara stood rooted, its lame leg tucked under it, watching with eyes that remembered everything the people had forgotten. It did not speak. There was nothing left to say that beasts had not already said. But when Ilian glanced at it, it dipped its head — the smallest bow — and he understood that even the animals who had guided them across the unwritten road did not know what would happen next. They were witnesses, not architects.

Only Mara was an architect now.

"Tell me what to do," Ilian said. His voice sounded small in the vast quiet. "Mara. Tell me what to do."

The bark under his palm shifted. Not painfully — she had made certain of that, he realized; she had spent whatever remained of her human attention making certain that touching her would never hurt — but with the slow tectonic patience of something growing. Green light moved beneath the grain of her, veins of it, branching outward from the place where his hand pressed, and where the light passed, the clearing changed. Grass rose in a soft tide around her roots. A smell came up out of the ground that he knew from nowhere and recognized instantly: the Garden. Not the murdered one, not the old tyranny wrapped in beauty that the crows' parliament had shown him in their cold black assembly. Something younger. Something *hers*.

"I can hear her thinking," he told the fox, because saying it aloud made it bearable. "It's not words. It's more like — weather. She's deciding what kind of country to be."

The fox's ear twitched. *She was always deciding that,* it said, in the tongue of beasts that only he and she had ever shared in this valley. *You simply never had roots deep enough to notice.*

* * *

He worked because she needed him to work, and because hands that are doing cannot shake.

The door stood free at the center of the clearing — the threshold the fox had led them to, a frame of grey stone standing upright in nothing, opening onto neither here nor there. Through it, when the light caught it wrong, he could see the old country: the true one, the first one, the Garden entire, preserved these long generations in the amber of its own memory. Waiting. Wanting. Hungry in the patient way that old things are hungry.

To open it fully, Mara would have poured herself into it — every splinter of the Garden's memory she carried, every fragment of herself given flesh in a valley family generations ago — and the old country would have poured back through. Every living person now inside these borders would have been overwritten. Erased. Ilian himself included. He had known that since the ash orchard, since the confrontation where trust had shattered mid-journey and been held together afterward by nothing but necessity and something neither of them had dared name.

She had refused. Of course she had refused. And then she had chosen the third path, the path that cost her everything else: *plant the Garden anew inside myself. Become the door.*

And so the door was becoming her, and she was becoming the door, and Ilian — expendable Ilian, sent-to-be-spent Ilian, the remnant order's faithful instrument who had chosen the woman over the mission somewhere on the unwritten road and had never once regretted it — was the only pair of hands left to hold the frame steady while the world turned over.

"Press there," Mara's voice said — from everywhere now, from the green light in the grass, from the leaves unfolding above his head. "The stone remembers being quarried. Remind it."

He pressed his other palm to the doorframe and thought, as hard as he had ever thought anything: *you were a mountain once.* The stone shivered. Somewhere far below the clearing, something vast and patient settled more comfortably into the ground.

"That's it," she said, and there was laughter in it, exhausted and triumphant and so entirely *Mara* that his chest ached. "Now we do this all night. Every stone. Every root. Every inch of me. You're going to be very tired by morning, gardener."

"Gardener," he repeated. The word landed strangely. Once it had been an accusation — the gardeners had murdered their own Garden, the crows had shown him that, had strangled it in its sleep rather than surrender their dominion. Now she handed him the word like a gift with the poison drawn out.

"You flinched," she observed.

"I'm getting better at it."

"Mm." The green light pulsed, and he could feel her attention moving through him like sunlight through water, warm and total. "I know. I've been watching you get better at things since the ferryman's river. You apologize to doors now. Before you open them."

"They might be listening. Everything here might be listening. You taught me that."

"No," she said gently. "The fox taught you that. I taught you that it's worth listening back."

* * *

Near midnight — though midnight was a courtesy, a habit, the sky having abandoned such bookkeeping along with its stars — the turn began to complete.

Ilian felt the old country lean against the door.

It was not violent. That was the horror of it, and the mercy, and he would spend the rest of his life unable to decide which. The first country did not come like a flood or a fire. It came like a sleeper turning over in bed, sighing, settling. All around the clearing the last unwritten land exhaled — he could see it happening, the horizon softening, the hills giving up their argument with geometry, the static-thinned sky beginning to fold inward like a page closing over ink. The country that had forgotten itself was finishing the forgetting, and what could not be remembered could not be kept, and so it went.

Villages that existed only at dusk went with the dusk. The river of static ran dry from the mouth backward. The City of Half-Names, where polite citizens had rewritten their name-tags hourly while consenting to vanish — Ilian closed his eyes and let himself grieve for them properly, for one full minute, because Mara had vowed that someone would remember this country and he intended to be someone. The librarian too. Even the hunters of the remnant order, scattered now in a forest that had forgotten them mid-fight, unmade so gently they likely never noticed. Even the order itself, its purpose dissolving with the borders it had served.

The moon completed its thirteenth turn, and there was no one left to count turns after that.

And through the door, pressing like dawn against the east side of a valley, the Garden waited to be reborn — and found, instead, a door that had become a woman, and a woman who had become a country, and a lock that no longer fit any key the old world owned.

"It's pushing," Ilian said. Sweat stood on his forehead despite the cold. His arms burned from holding the frame; the stone had begun to sing under his hands, high and thin, a note that meant *almost*. "Mara. It's pushing hard."

"I know." Her voice had deepened. Leaves moved when she spoke. "Let it push. Doors are strongest exactly where they're pushed."

"What do you need?"

There was a pause — the first hesitation he had felt from her since she took root. When she answered, the weather of her thinking had gone still and clear.

"Hold on," she said. "Just that. Don't let go, whatever comes through the grain. Some of me is going to be very old for a few minutes, and very large, and I'd rather not find out what I do to the people I love when I don't recognize them."

*I love,* she had said. Past the fear and the vastness, plain as a hand held out.

"I won't let go," Ilian said.

* * *

What came through the grain was everything.

The old Garden's memory met the new Garden's flesh somewhere inside her, and the collision was silent and enormous, like two seasons arriving at once. Ilian held the door and felt the struggle travel up through the stone and into his wrists and teeth. For a handful of heartbeats — his own heartbeats, the only clock left — the thing rooted in the clearing was both Gardens at once: tyrant and beast-speaker, murderer's monument and forbidden gift, the hunger that had strangled its own body and the girl who had spoken with a dying fox beyond the ward-line because she could not help herself and had never wanted to.

He felt her choose. Not the way she had chosen at the threshold, with terror transfigured into sovereignty. This choice was quieter. It was the choice of what to keep. He felt her sort through ten thousand years of stored memory like a woman sorting seeds at a table, keeping some, casting others into the dark to compost — the dominion cast out, the hierarchy cast out, the beautiful cruelty cast out — until what remained was small enough to hold and heavy enough to mean something.

Then the pressure against the door reversed.

Instead of the old country leaning in, the new one leaned *out*. Mara — rooted, vast, green-lit, hers — pushed back through the doorframe, and Ilian felt the old Garden's preserved eternity meet the edge of her will and find it unyielding, and find, beyond it, nothing willing to receive it. The door was a door into a country that no longer existed. There was no longer anywhere for the old Garden to go except into memory, and memory was exactly what Mara was made of now, and she would carry it — she would remember the murdered Garden honestly, tyranny and all — but she would not resurrect it.

The exhale came.

The whole first country, in one long breath, went out of existence. Ilian felt it pass through the doorframe like wind through a window, past his face, stirring his hair — and in that wind he heard everything, every harvest song and half-name and hour-rewritten tag, the ferryman content at last, the librarian's endless shelves closing, the parliament of crows rising one final time into a sky that was learning to hold stars again — and then it was through, and out, and gone, and the doorframe in his hands was just stone. Old, ordinary, moss-furred stone, standing in a clearing at the edge of a darkness that felt, for the first time in thirteen turns, like night instead of ending.

His legs folded.

He went down onto his knees in the new grass, hands still on the stone because he had promised, and the singing note faded, and the silence that followed was so complete it rang.

Above him, Mara's branches stirred without wind.

* * *

He didn't lose consciousness. It was worse than that — he lost the border of himself. Holding the door through the exhale had cost something he couldn't name; the green light had run up his arms and into his chest and for one vertiginous moment he hadn't been able to find where Ilian ended and the growing country began. He knelt in the grass gripping the stone, breathing, cataloguing himself piece by piece like the librarian shelving books. Hands: his. Knees: bruised, his. Heart: hammering, his, still human, still stubbornly, ordinarily mortal. Name: Ilian. Expendable. Renounced. Staying.

A tendril of green — delicate as a fern-frond, deliberate as a hand — descended from the canopy above and touched his shoulder.

He looked up.

She filled the sky where the moon should have been. Not towering — *present*, the way weather is present, the way morning is present. Her bark bore rings now, centuries of them grown in a single night, and in each ring he could see scenes moving faintly: the ward-line, the iron box, the ferryman's oars, the ash orchard where he had lied to her and she had fought beside him anyway. She had planted their whole journey in her own wood. Anyone who ever touched this tree would feel it — the betrayal and the forgiveness, the necessary lies and the freely given truth, all of it, kept.

"You almost went with them," she said. Her voice came from everywhere and from directly above him, and it was gentle. "When the exhale came. You nearly poured yourself out with the old country."

"I held on."

"You held on." The frond curled slightly, the way her fingers used to curl when she was deciding whether to laugh at him. "Why? You could have gone cleanly. No grief, no renouncing, no waking up tomorrow in a country that has to be built from scratch by a woman who is mostly tree. Why hold on, Ilian?"

He knelt in the grass with her shadow-rooted presence all around him and found, to his surprise, that there was no version of the answer left to hide behind. The remnant order was gone. The mission was gone. The lie he had told her in chapter and campfire — *I need you because the seed chose you* — had been confessed, forgiven, composted into her rings. There was nothing left between him and the truth except the habit of caution, and he was so tired of it.

"Because I wasn't sent here for the seed," he said. "I wasn't even sent here for the vessel. That was the order's truth, and the order is ash now. Here's mine." He got to his feet, slowly, knees protesting, and put his palm flat against her bark, over the place where her heart had beaten and where something older kept time now. "I crossed a broken border carrying a box, and I fell half-dead into a valley, and every animal fled from me, and the only creature in that whole country who could hear me speak was a woman hiding a forbidden gift from her elders. And from the moment you caught me — angry, terrified, magnificent — I stopped being their agent and started being yours. I held on because you are my country, Mara. Whatever you grow into. Wherever you take root. I chose loyalty to the woman over truth of the mission on the unwritten road, and I choose it again tonight, and I'll choose it every morning there's a morning. I love you. I loved you when you were flesh and I love you now that you're bark and leaf and ten thousand years of memory sorted by your own hand, and I am telling a tree this, and I find I don't care."

The clearing was silent.

Then, from the edge of it, the fox made a sound that was unmistakably, derisively, a snort — and Ilian laughed, and the laugh broke something loose in his chest, and to his mortification what came after was not laughter at all. He knelt again in the new grass and wept like a man walking out of a long winter, and the fronds of the Garden that had been Mara Villalobos came down around him like a roof, like shelter, like arms.

"I'm here," she said. "I heard every word. I'll keep every word. They're in the rings now — third ring from the heartwood, listen, anyone who touches me will hear you saying it. You absolute fool. You brave, expendable, indispensable fool."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes," said the Garden, "and it's a vow, and it's a door standing open, and you may walk through it whenever you like for the rest of your life."

* * *

Dawn came late and came real.

Not the false dawn of the unwritten road, not the dusk-villages flickering in and out of existence. A true grey, then a true gold, climbing over hills that — he checked twice, three times, disbelieving — held their shapes. Held them slightly wrong, the way dreams get geography wrong: the eastern ridge wore the silhouette of the valley's rim, and a river to the north bent in a curve that belonged to the ferryman's crossing, and somewhere out there, he suspected, a city stood whose streets remembered name-tags. Mara's country, grown from Mara's remembered life, imperfect and entirely hers.

And above the hills, in the paling east, a star.

One. Then another. Then a scatter of them, shy and bright, burning through the last of the static as though the sky had spent thirteen turns holding its breath and finally, gratefully, let it go.

Ilian stood in the clearing with his hand still resting on the bark of the woman he loved, watching stars return to a brand-new country, and it was then he noticed the movement at the treeline — the new treeline, the one that hadn't existed yesterday, young birches bending as something small and lame-legged limped out from among them.

The fox sat down at the clearing's edge, looked up at the Garden, looked at Ilian, and spoke.

"There are valleys beyond the hills," it said. "Old ones. With old wards. And I think some of them held."
