## 20. A New Country, First Morning

Mara woke because the light was wrong.

Not wrong in the way the old country's light had been wrong — that thinning, apologetic glow of a world forgetting how to shine — but wrong in the way a thing is wrong when it has never existed before and is still deciding what it wants to be. It came through her eyelids gold and green at once, as if she were sleeping beneath water and sunlit leaves both, and when she opened her eyes she understood why.

She was lying on a hillside that had not been there yesterday. The grass was the deep, stubborn green of the valley meadows where she had learned to walk, but each blade ended in a tiny curling frond, like a fern's unfurling fist, and when the wind moved through them they chimed — very softly, very faintly — with a sound like distant bells remembering themselves.

Above her, the sky was full of stars.

They should have been gone by now. Dawn was already bleeding rose-gold along the eastern ridgeline; she could see it, could feel its warmth gathering against her cheek like a hand about to touch her. But the stars hung on, stubborn and silver, scattered across the brightening blue as though no one had told them morning had come. As though they were afraid to leave again.

*You can go,* Mara thought at them, and her voice — when she realized she had spoken aloud — did not come out of her throat alone. It came out of the ground beneath her, out of the roots threading the hillside, out of the small new river chuckling somewhere below. *It's safe now. You can come back.*

One by one, unhurried, the stars began to fade.

She lay still and watched them go, and found that she was crying without any grief in it at all — just the sheer animal relief of a body that had held itself braced for too long finally letting its shoulders drop. The last star to go was low in the west, and it lingered, flickering, almost shy, before it dissolved into the blue.

"Good morning," said a voice near her feet.

The fox sat three paces away, tail wrapped over its forepaws, its lame leg tucked neatly beneath it. In this new light its coat was red as rowan berries, and its eyes — one amber, one the pale grey of the moon's hidden face — watched her with an expression she had never quite been able to read from any beast, and which she now suspected she never would, because it wasn't a beast's expression at all. It was the look of an old friend deciding whether to tease you or tell you the truth.

"You're supposed to be asleep," the fox said. "New gardens need their gardener rested."

"I've been resting for—" She stopped. Tried to count. Found that time here moved the way honey moves, the way sap moves, patient and circular, and that counting it felt suddenly beside the point. "A while," she finished.

"A night," the fox said. "The first one. There will only ever be one first one." It stood, shook itself, and limped two steps closer. "You should see it. You made a country in your sleep, Mara Villalobos. Come and be disappointed in it."

* * *

She walked down into her own remembered life, and it was all slightly wrong, and it was perfect.

The valley was there — her valley, the one whose wards had shattered on the night the stars went out — but it sat in the wrong place, nestled between hills she had only ever seen from the far side of the ferryman's river, as though someone had taken the pieces of everything she loved and assembled them by feel, in the dark, with love. The elder hall stood on its rise above the village, timber and thatch exactly as she remembered it, except that the thatch grew small white flowers she had never seen anywhere, and the smoke rising from its chimney smelled of rain on warm stone instead of woodsmoke.

The village lanes were right. The well was right. The crooked fence around the herb garden leaned at precisely the angle it had leaned for as long as she could remember, and when she put her hand on it, the wood was warm, faintly pulsing, alive in a way fences are not supposed to be alive — because it wasn't a fence anymore, not really. It was memory wearing the shape of a fence. Everything here was. She could feel it all the way down through her bare feet: the whole country rooted in her, growing out of her, drinking from some deep spring inside her chest that had once been called Mara Villalobos and was now something larger that still answered to the name.

A woman stepped out of the first cottage and stopped dead when she saw her.

Mara knew her face. Of course she knew it — it was Sera, who had lived two doors down from her family in the valley, who had baked bread every fourth morning and hummed off-key and once, when Mara was seven, given her a honey-cake for no reason at all. But Sera had died before the stars went out. Mara had carried her to the burial ground herself, had felt the last warmth leave her hands.

"Sera?" she whispered.

The woman frowned, uncertain, the way people frown when waking from a dream they can't quite hold. "That's... yes," she said slowly, tasting the name. "Yes, I think I am." She looked down at her own flour-dusted hands as though confirming they belonged to her. "Isn't it strange? I woke up knowing how to knead dough and I couldn't remember my mother's face until just this moment, and now I can, and now I'm not sure if I ever lost it." She laughed — a bewildered, delighted sound. "Everything here feels like the second half of a sentence."

"It is," Mara said gently. "But you get to finish it however you like."

Sera looked at her for a long moment, and something passed across her face — recognition, or its cousin, or its echo. "You're the one the land comes from," she said. Not a question. "I dreamed of roots. Everyone did, I think." Then, because she was Sera, practical and kind: "Have you eaten? No one's eaten. We don't seem to know if we're hungry yet. Someone should decide."

"Let them decide for themselves," Mara said. "That's rather the point."

And Sera smiled, and turned back toward her kitchen, and the smell of bread — real bread, impossible bread, bread made from wheat that had grown overnight out of nothing but memory and goodwill — began to drift down the lane behind Mara as she walked on.

* * *

The river ran through the middle of the new country, and it was the ferryman's river.

She knew it instantly, the way you know your own heartbeat — by its sound. That particular hush-and-chuckle over stones, that wide slow patience. But it ran uphill for a stretch near the eastern bend, just as it had in the dying days of the old country, and Mara found she hadn't the heart to correct it. Some errors deserved to survive. They were proof of where things had been.

At the crossing stood a boat.

It was the ferryman's boat — flat-bottomed, oar-scarred, rope-bitted — except that it had grown here rather than been built, its planks still edged with pale new bark, a single leaf sprouting from the tiller. And standing in it, one hand resting on an oar he clearly knew better than his own name, was a man with a broad weathered face and no expression at all, because his face was still waiting to find out what it wanted to be.

He saw her and straightened, and his mouth worked for a moment.

"Ferryman," Mara said softly.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. His hand tightened on the oar, and in the grip of that wood he seemed to gather himself, the way he always had, holding to the one thing the forgetting couldn't take. "That sounds right," he said at last. "Ferryman. Yes." He looked past her at the river, at the far bank where a road unspooled into hills that hadn't existed an hour ago, and his brow creased. "There's no one to carry yet," he said, and there was a hollow note in it, a note of a man missing his purpose.

"There will be," Mara said. "People forget things here too — but differently now. Gently. And when they do, someone will need carrying across to remember." She touched the gunwale, and felt the boat's new-grown wood lean into her palm like a dog into a familiar hand. "This crossing is yours. It always was. I only gave it back."

The ferryman looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he had nodded at every passenger he'd ever carried — the nod that meant *I have you, I won't drop you* — and sat down at his oars, content, facing the water, waiting for the world to need him.

Mara walked on. She did not let herself cry until the bend hid him from view, and then only a little, and then only because joy needs somewhere to go.

* * *

The animals had come home.

She heard them before she saw them — a sound she had not heard since childhood, since before the elders' rules, since before she'd learned to keep her gift folded up small and secret: the great layered chorus of a valley full of beasts speaking freely. Birds arguing about territory in the hawthorn hedge. A badger grumbling philosophically to a hedgehog about the quality of the new worms. Somewhere upslope, a red deer stag testing his voice against the morning, and the morning answering him in birdsong.

And everywhere, woven under it all, the older voices — the ones ordinary ears had never caught even in the valley's best days. The voice of the soil turning itself over, getting acquainted with its own minerals. The voice of the young river introducing itself to its stones. The voice of the oak trees comparing rings.

In the old country, her gift had been forbidden. Here, it was simply the way the air was made.

"They're asking about you," said the fox, appearing at her elbow so quietly she startled. "All of them. They want to know if the talking is allowed now."

Mara crouched, laid her palm flat against the earth, and let her answer travel down through the roots, out through every root in the country at once, the way blood carries a message to every finger:

*Allowed. Wanted. Forever.*

The chorus swelled. For a moment the whole valley rang with it — beast-voice and bird-voice and root-voice and stone-voice, a sound like a country clearing its throat after a long illness — and Mara knelt in the middle of it with tears running freely down her face and her heart hammering with something that was not dread at all, for the first time in longer than she could count.

* * *

She found the imperfections by midmorning, and loved them best of all.

The hills to the north were a little too steep, their profiles borrowed half from the valley and half from mountains she'd glimpsed only once, from the ferry, in the old country's final days. A field of barley near the village grew in spirals instead of rows, because her memory of barley fields was honest but imprecise, and the barley didn't mind. One cottage had a door that opened onto a different room depending on whether you knocked first — she decided to leave that alone and see what grew from it. The moon, when she thought to check, hung pale above the western ridge, and it had thirteen craters arranged in a spiral, and she chose, firmly, not to take that personally.

Nothing here was exact. Nothing here was the old country restored, the dead raised, the erased returned. She had understood that, standing in the doorway at the end, pouring herself into the seed — that she was not resurrecting anything. She was composting it. Taking everything the old country had been, everything it had loved and broken and forgotten and murdered, and feeding it into something new that would grow crooked and specific and alive.

The people here — Sera, the ferryman, the handful of others waking in cottages and on hillsides, blinking at their own hands — they were not the originals either. They were memories given flesh, echoes offered bodies, the country's tenderness toward its own past made walking and breathing and hungry for bread. Whether they had souls, whether they were continuations or homages, Mara did not know, and had decided at some hour of the rooted night that the question mattered less than the kindness of the answer she gave them: *you are real, you are welcome, begin.*

The old country was gone. Truly gone. When the thirteenth turn completed, it had exhaled — she had felt it go, the whole vast weight of it lifting off the world like a fever breaking — and where it had been there was now only this: one small country, one valley and its river and its hills, ringed by wild country still unformed, waiting to be imagined or left alone.

Small. Real. Imperfect. Hers.

Ours, she corrected herself, and the correction settled into her roots like rain.

* * *

She climbed back up the first hillside as the sun cleared the ridge — the true sun, in a sky swept clean of everything but a few honest clouds — and found Ilian sitting where she had woken, exactly where she had known he would be.

He had not slept. She could see it in him, in the bruised shadows under his eyes and the careful stillness of his hands, and she could feel it too, faintly, through the seed that had rooted in his blood on the last terrible night — a thin thread of connection, a second rootlet twined near hers. He had held the door while she became it. He had nearly been unmade doing it. The order that sent him had written him off as expendable, and he had stayed anyway, and chosen anyway, and she was not going to waste whatever remained of her old human heart pretending she didn't know what that meant.

He looked up at her approach and tried, visibly, to assemble an agent's composure, and failed halfway, and what was left underneath was so much better: a tired man watching the sunrise over a country that existed because the woman he loved had dared to stop being only herself.

"The stars came back," he said.

"All of them?"

"All of them. I counted." A pause. "Twice. I wanted to be sure."

Mara sat down beside him in the chiming grass. Below them the valley smoked gently with morning cook-fires, rang with beast-song, glittered with a river that ran briefly uphill and was entirely unashamed of it. Somewhere in it all, a lame fox was teaching a parliament of crows the boundaries of the new country, and being thoroughly argued with.

"It's wrong in places," she said. "The hills are too steep. The barley grows in spirals. There's a door that misbehaves."

"I heard about the door," Ilian said. "A crow told me. Crows gossip terribly here — no one's forbidden anything, so they've all become unbearable." He was quiet a moment. "It's the most beautiful place I've ever seen."

"Because it's imperfect?"

"Because it's yours." He said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he had learned to say true things in the end — and then, because he was still Ilian, he looked away at the horizon and added, more roughly than he intended: "The remnant order will have felt it. When the thirteenth turn closed and the old country ended. Whatever's left of them, wherever they shelter — they'll know their Garden didn't come back to them. They'll know it went... elsewhere." His jaw tightened. "Some of them will grieve. Some of them will be angry. And at least one of them will start walking."

Mara felt it before he finished saying it — a faint pressure at the edge of her awareness, out beyond the unformed hills, like a fingertip pressed against the membrane of a drum. Not hostile. Not yet. But present. Watching. The Gardener's Oath-bound remnants of a murdered institution, waking to find their god reborn in the wrong shape, in the wrong hands, in the hands of a valley beast-speaker they had never sanctioned.

"Then we'll be ready," she said.

"We?" 

She took his hand. His fingers were cold from the sleepless night; hers were warm from the roots, and she lent him some of the warmth, deliberately, the way you lend a coat.

"You asked me once what I wanted of you," Mara said. "I've had a night to think about it. I'll give you my answer properly tomorrow — a country should have ceremonies for important things, and I intend to invent several. But the short version is this: remember me as I was, and walk with me as I am."

Ilian looked at their joined hands, and then at her, and the last of the agent fell away from his face entirely, leaving only the man.

"And the long version?" he asked.

Mara smiled, and beneath her, all through the sweet chiming grass, the roots stirred with anticipation, and far to the north, past the edge of the map, something ancient and loyal and furious took its first step toward the new country's border.

"The long version," she said, "begins with an oath."
