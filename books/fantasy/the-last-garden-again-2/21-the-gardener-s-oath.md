## 21. The Gardener's Oath

Ilian woke on the first morning of the new country with dirt under his fingernails and no memory of how it got there.

That was a lie, and he knew it even as he thought it. He remembered everything now — that was the strange inheritance of the thirteenth turn's completion. When the old country had exhaled its last breath out of existence, when the moon had finished its impossible circuit and the door had become Mara and Mara had become the world, every scrap of forgetting that had plagued the land for a year had poured into him like water into a cracked vessel. He was, he suspected, the only creature in creation who remembered the whole of what had been lost. The fox remembered its own life. The crows remembered their parliament. But Ilian carried the country entire: every unwritten village, every nameless ferryman, every hour of every dusk-softened road.

He lay in the crook of a root the size of a farmhouse and watched the light come up through leaves that had not existed yesterday. They were Mara's leaves. He understood that now in a way that sat somewhere between wonder and grief so large he could not look at it directly. Above him, the canopy shifted with a wind that smelled of rain and rosemary and, faintly, of woodsmoke from a valley hearth — the smell of the night she had knelt beside a dying fox while stars went out one by one above her head.

The root beneath him pulsed, slow as a sleeping heart.

*She knows you're awake,* said a voice at his ear, dry and amused, and Ilian did not startle because by now he was long past startling. The fox sat on the rise above him, its lame leg stretched out before it, its amber eyes catching the new sun. It had not left. None of them had left. The animals who had guided two fools across a dissolving country had simply taken up residence in the world their speaker had made, as if they had always known this was where the road ended.

"I'm not going anywhere," Ilian said.

"No," the fox agreed. "You're not. That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

* * *

The remnant order found him at midmorning.

They came up the eastern slope in gray traveling cloaks, four of them, walking with the careful tread of people entering a temple they did not believe in. Ilian saw them from the ridge where he had gone to take stock of the new land — a small thing, this country, barely a day's walk corner to corner, but dense and real in a way the old one had never managed. A river ran through it that was the ferryman's river reborn, running the right way now, most of the time. There were hills where the valley's wards had stood, and a stand of ash trees on the southern slope that made his chest tighten, because he knew whose memory those were. And at the center, vast and green and breathing, the Garden itself: Mara, rooted, radiant, her trunk wide as a watchtower, her branches holding up a sky that had stars in it again.

The leader of the four was a woman named Serevan, though Ilian had known her for thirty years by her function rather than her face. She had trained him. She had taught him that the seed mattered more than any messenger, that the vessel mattered more than the seed, that a gardener's first oath was to the Garden and his last was to himself, and that between those two oaths there was room for nothing else.

She stopped ten paces away and looked at the new country the way a merchant looks at goods he intends to acquire.

"It lived," she said. "The order wagered everything on the vessel being strong enough. We were right."

"You were wrong," Ilian said. "About nearly everything. But yes. She lived."

Serevan's eyes moved to the great tree at the center of the valley, and something in her expression softened and hardened at once, the way ice softens before it breaks. "Then the work is finished. The Garden endures. Come home, Ilian. There are records to restore, archives to rebuild, a thousand years of cultivation to resume. You have earned your rest among the rows."

"The rows," Ilian repeated. He looked at his hands. They were still dirty. "You mean the grafting. The pruning. The choosing of vessels."

"The tending," she said carefully.

"The harvesting." He let the word sit in the air between them until she flinched. "I dreamed it, Serevan. On the twelfth turn I dreamed the whole of the Garden's death, start to finish, and I know now what the crows told Mara was true. The gardeners murdered their own Garden when it grew wild. When it stopped being theirs. When it started making doors instead of walls. And then they spent a thousand years growing replacements in secret rooms, pruning them into obedience, and calling it devotion." He shook his head slowly. "I carried the seed across a dying country believing I was carrying mercy. I was carrying a leash."

"The old Garden was failing—"

"The old Garden was *free*." His voice cracked on the word, and he was too tired to be ashamed of it. "Look at her. Look at what she made when no one pruned her. Not a walled sanctuary. Not a treasury of preserved perfection. A *country*. Imperfect. Small. Rivers that run wrong sometimes. Ash trees that remember a woman who died before her time. She took every broken thing we brought her and grew it into something living, and you want to bring her back to the rows and put shears to her branches and call it love."

Serevan was quiet for a long moment. Behind her, the other three agents stood with the stillness of people who had been given no script for this.

"She will need protection," Serevan said finally. "There are powers beyond the hills. There are valleys behind wards that remember nothing. There is the librarian."

"There is me," Ilian said. "And there is my answer, which is no. I am done with the remnant order. I renounce it — the oaths, the hierarchy, the doctrine of the vessel. All of it. If the Garden wants gardeners, she will choose them herself. And if she chooses me, it will be because I asked her what she wanted, and waited for the answer, and did not decide it for her in advance." He met Serevan's eyes. "That's the difference between us now. You came to collect an asset. I came to ask a question."

Something moved behind Serevan's face — grief, maybe, or the ghost of the young woman she had been before the order taught her that feeling was a form of rot. "You were the best of us," she said quietly.

"I know," Ilian said. "That's why I'm leaving."

She studied him a moment longer, then bowed — shallow, formal, final — and turned. The four of them walked back down the eastern slope and out of the new country, and Ilian noticed that the grass did not bend around their feet, and the birds did not sing above their heads, and the land itself seemed to hold its breath until they were gone.

*It has opinions about you already,* the fox observed, trotting up beside him. *The land, I mean. It likes some visitors and dislikes others. You should hear what it says about the librarian.*

"The librarian isn't coming back."

"The librarian is always coming back. That's what remembering everything does to a person. It makes them certain they're owed." The fox sat, scratched its lame leg with a hind paw, and regarded him with one eye. *You haven't asked her yet.*

"Asked who what?"

The fox gave him a look of magnificent contempt. *You've been circling the great tree since dawn like a moth who's forgotten how to fly straight. You've rehearsed whatever speech you've written seventeen times — badly, I might add; you mumble. Ask her.*

Ilian opened his mouth to deny it, closed it, and laughed — a rough, startled sound that echoed off the hills and came back to him sounding lighter than it had left. Then he walked down toward the center of the world.

* * *

Up close, the Garden was less a tree than a landscape.

Her roots broke the ground in ridges he could walk between, and moss grew in the crevices of her bark in patterns that resolved, when he looked long enough, into writing — not the formal script of the order's archives but a looser hand, looping and personal, the handwriting of someone who had learned letters from a valley schoolmistress and never unlearned the habit of pressing too hard. He read a line of it without meaning to: *the fox was right about the ferryman.* Another: *I miss bread. Real bread, with the crust that fights back.*

He laughed again, and pressed his palm flat against her bark, and felt the pulse come up through his arm to meet his own heartbeat, the two rhythms arguing briefly before settling into something shared.

"Mara," he said.

The leaves overhead stirred without wind. Light fell through them in shifting coins.

"I don't—" He stopped. Started over. Stopped again. Seventeen rehearsals, the fox had said, and every one of them abandoned him now that it mattered. "I don't know how to talk to a tree that used to be a woman I — that is a woman I—"

*I'm still here,* said the voice.

Not from the bark. From everywhere. From the light and the moss and the pulse in the ground and, faintly, from inside his own chest, where the seed had rooted during the twelfth turn and never entirely left. Her voice was Mara's voice and more than Mara's voice, layered now with harmonics like wind through many branches, but underneath the strangeness it was dry and impatient and fond, exactly as it had been on the unwritten road when she'd caught him lying about why he needed her specifically and had decided, infuriatingly, to trust him anyway.

"I know you're here," he said. "That's rather the problem. I keep talking to you like you're going to vanish if I say the wrong thing, and you keep not vanishing, and I don't seem to be learning anything from the experience."

*Take your time,* she said. *I've got roots. Time is different when you have roots.*

So he took his time. He sat down between two of her root-ridges with his back against her trunk, and he told her things. He told her about Serevan and the renunciation, and she listened with the particular silence of someone who had already heard it from the grass. He told her about the dream on the twelfth turn, all of it, the parts he had never confessed — how he had seen the gardeners with their shears, how he had understood that his own mission was the same murder attempted a second time, slower, kinder, with better intentions. He told her that he had lied to her on the road, more than once, and that the lies had been necessary and had eaten holes in him anyway, and that he was not asking forgiveness because forgiveness was hers to give or withhold and he would live in either country she made.

*You already do,* she said gently. *Live in either country I make. That's what staying means.*

"Yes," he said. "That's what I came to ask about. Staying."

The leaves went very still. Even the pulse in the bark seemed to lean closer.

"The order offered me a place among the rows. I refused. The librarian will offer me a place in her archive eventually — a perfect, frozen, remembered forever — and I'll refuse that too. There's nothing left for me out there except versions of you that other people built. So I'm staying. Here. In this country. For as long as it stands and after, if I'm permitted." He turned his head and pressed his cheek against the bark, the way he had once seen her press her hand to a dying ewe's flank to hear its heart. "But I won't presume the terms. I spent too long presuming terms. So I'm asking, Mara — the way I should have asked on the first day, before the fox, before the road, before any of it. What do you want of me? Not what does the order want, not what does the mission want, not what does the seed want. *You.* What do you want of me?"

For a long moment there was no answer, and Ilian felt the old fear rise in him — the fear of the empty archive, the unwritten page, the country that forgets — and then the light through the leaves changed, gold deepening to green, and her voice came not from everywhere this time but from directly above him, from the lowest branch, close as a mouth at his ear.

*Remember me as I was,* said Mara. *Walk with me as I am.*

The words went through him like the seed had gone through the soil — quietly, and then all at once, and then forever.

"That's it?" he managed, when he could speak. "No labors? No penance? No list of duties carved into your bark?"

*Would you prefer duties?*

"No. Yes. I don't—" He laughed, wet-eyed, helpless. "It's harder than duties. Do you know that? Anyone can labor. Labor is easy. You're asking me to carry a ghost and a woman in the same arms and never set either down, and to know the difference between them every single moment, and to love them both without letting one eat the other." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Yes. All right. Yes. I swear it. Remember her as she was — the stubborn, lying, beast-speaking, fox-befriending woman who chose exile over safety and a third path over two easy ones. Walk with her as she is — the roots and the leaves and the voice in my chest and the country under my feet. As long as I have a self to swear with, it's sworn."

The pulse in the bark quickened, once, twice — and settled. And Ilian felt something change in the ground beneath him, subtle as a turn of season: a sense of being *held*, not the way the order had held him, in doctrine and duty, but the way soil holds a root. Without gripping. Without owning. Simply refusing, quietly and permanently, to let go.

*Then it's done,* she said. *Gardener.*

"Not gardener," he said. "The order's gardeners prune. Call me—" He searched, and found the word waiting for him as if it had grown there overnight, in the moss-script, in the handwriting that pressed too hard. "Call me the keeper of the record. Someone has to remember the whole of what was lost. I'm the only one who does. Let that be my work — not preserving the old country, but bearing witness to it, so that everything you grew yourself from can stay honest. The griefs and the murders and the ferryman and all of it. The true account. Kept by a man with dirt under his nails."

*A witness-gardener,* she said, amused. *Very well. But Ilian — one condition of your own office.*

"Name it."

*When children come to these hills — and they will come, soon, the way children always find the places where stories are thick — they'll ask you what happened. To the old country. To the stars. To the woman in the tree. And you'll be tempted to make it clean, because witnesses always are, because grief wants tidiness the way rivers want the sea.* The leaves rustled, and her voice dropped lower, almost conspiratorial. *Don't. Tell it tangled. Tell it wrong-shaped. Tell them the fox was insufferable and the ferryman forgot his own name and you lied to me and I forgave you anyway and neither of those facts cancels the other. Promise me the messy version.*

"I promise the messy version," Ilian said solemnly. "Starting with the part where the hero of the story is currently a tree who nags."

*Nags?* The indignation in her voice rippled through every leaf on the hillside, a sound like applause. *I am a sovereign power of the natural world.*

"You are," he agreed, leaning his head back against her trunk, watching the light come down through her in green and gold coins. "And you're nagging. Both things are true. I'm learning that about this country — it runs on both things being true."

Above the eastern hills, the sun climbed fully clear of the ridgeline, and the new land woke around them: birdsong in the ash grove, the river finding its voice, somewhere far off the bleat of sheep that had not existed yesterday and would exist tomorrow. Ilian closed his eyes and felt the pulse of the great tree sync with his heartbeat, and for the first time since the stars had gone out — since before that, since the order, since the seed, since the boy he had been before any of it — he felt no pull toward any other place in the world.

He was still savoring it when the fox trotted down the slope at an uncharacteristic hurry, ears flat, and skidded to a stop between the root-ridges.

*Visitors,* it said. *On the western road. Three of them. Small ones. Children, by the smell of them — and they're asking the blackberries questions, and the blackberries are answering.*

Ilian opened his eyes. Mara's leaves had gone very still.

*Well, keeper of the record,* said the fox, grinning. *Your messy version starts sooner than you thought.*
