## 22. The Last Garden Again

The children came every autumn, when the blackberries went heavy and dark on the western hedge, and the blackberries — being blackberries, and therefore gossips of the first order — always told them things they had no business knowing.

Mara felt them coming the way she felt everything now: through her roots, slow as sap rising, patient as weather. Three small heartbeats on the western road, light-footed and loud-voiced, arguing about something with the total conviction of children who have never yet been wrong in their own estimation.

She had learned to love that sound more than birdsong. And she had learned birdsong in a hundred thousand throats at once, so she did not say it lightly.

*They're back,* said the fox.

He was lying along one of her lower branches these days — an indignity he would have shredded any other creature for suggesting, but which he permitted himself because, as he put it, a keeper's record deserved a comfortable place to be kept. His muzzle had gone silver at the edges over the years, though his limp had never healed and had never once slowed him down. He wore it now like a medal. *The little one with the gap teeth is asking the brambles whether you dream.*

*What did they tell her?*

*That you dream in weather,* the fox said, with the air of one delivering a verdict both accurate and damning. *Which is true, so I couldn't even correct them. You're a terrible example to set for the young. Dreaming in weather. In my day trees kept their inner lives to themselves.*

*In your day,* Mara said fondly, *trees could not answer you at all, and you were dying alone past a ward-line, and I was someone else entirely.*

The fox considered this. His tail curled around his paws. *Yes,* he admitted. *It was a better time for gossip.*

* * *

Ilian found her at midmorning, as he found her every day, though neither of them ever called it that. He came up the hill from the village with a basket on his arm and sawdust in his hair — he had never quite stopped building things with his hands, doors and benches and window frames, as if some part of him still believed every opening needed tending — and he sat down against her trunk in the hollow that had worn itself smooth for exactly the shape of his shoulders.

Years had silvered him too. Neither of them mentioned it.

"The children are on the western road," he said. "Toma's girl, and the twins from the ferry-house."

"The ferry-house," Mara repeated, tasting the words. The ferryman's river ran bright and real through the eastern valley now, slightly wrong the way everything here was slightly wrong — its fish sang on Thursdays, its reeds remembered names no one had spoken since the old country ended, and the boatman who poled travelers across was a broad, cheerful woman who had named herself Oars, because she could not recall why else she existed and thought that reason enough. The children of the ferry-house grew up bilingual in human speech and river-speech, and argued with herons about the proper depth for wading.

"I told them not to pick the blackberries before festival," Ilian said, "which of course guarantees they will."

"Of course."

He leaned his head back against her bark. She felt the warmth of him the way she felt rain — a thing that soaked in and stayed. It had taken them years to learn how to do this, to speak without speaking, or rather she had learned; he had simply refused to be embarrassed about talking aloud to a tree, in front of anyone, forever, until the whole valley decided it was normal and started doing it too.

That was the thing about the new country that surprised her most. Not the singing fish or the uphill meadows where lambs grazed on cloud-shadow. Not the stars, all of them returned, plus three new ones that had no business existing and burned a soft green that made the astronomers weep with joy and fury in equal measure. What surprised her was how quickly people adapted to a world that answered. How children born after the thirteenth turn spoke to sparrows the way they spoke to siblings — carelessly, constantly, with all the affection and cruelty of family — and how the elders, the ones who remembered wards and silence and the old forbidden hush, wept the first time a child asked a beetle for directions and the beetle answered, and then got on with their lives, because grief and joy have always shared a table in the valley country.

Her valley. Her country. Grown from the shape of her remembered life: the ward-line hills, the elder hall rebuilt smaller and warmer, the fox's den beneath her roots, the ferryman's river given a name at last — *Remembered* — because Mara had vowed on its banks that someone would remember, and she kept her vows.

*Ilian,* she said into the place behind his thoughts where her voice lived now.

"Mm."

*Tell me what you were dreaming last night. You woke twice.*

He smiled at the sky through her leaves. "You know what I was dreaming."

*Say it anyway. The record requires it.* The fox's ear twitched; the record required many things these days.

"I dreamed about the door," Ilian said quietly. "The old door, out past the edge of everything. Standing free in nothing. And in the dream it was open, and I was afraid of what would come through." He turned his head and pressed his palm flat against her bark, over the place where, if she had still been only flesh, her heart would have been. "And then I woke up, and you were humming, and the fear went out of it. That's all. Same dream as always."

*And if something does come through someday?*

"Then we meet it," he said, "the way we met everything else. Badly at first, then well."

* * *

The children arrived at the foot of the hill as the sun climbed past noon, and the blackberries immediately betrayed everyone by telling Toma's girl — whose name was Wren, and who had been named by a wren, and who bore this fact with tremendous dignity — that the Gardener was awake and listening.

They came scrambling up the slope the way children climb anything: too fast, falling once, laughing, racing each other to the great tree at the summit whose roots ridged the earth like the knuckles of a sleeping giant, whose trunk was wide enough that all three of them linking hands could just about circle half of it, whose leaves turned silver-gold in autumn and hummed, very faintly, a tune that everyone in the valley knew without ever having been taught.

Wren reached the trunk first, gap-toothed and triumphant, and pressed both hands against the bark the way her mother had taught her, the way everyone was taught: gently, and as an equal.

"Good morning, Gardener," she said formally, because manners mattered, and then abandoned formality entirely: "Do you dream? The blackberries say you dream in weather but the twins say that's a lie and you dream in *stories*, and there's going to be a fight about it, so you have to tell us."

Mara let her laughter move through the leaves, a sound like wind through wheat, and the children shrieked with delight because they always did.

*I dream in weather,* she said, so that all three heard it in the warm hollow behind their ears where beast-speech lived, *and the weather tells stories, and the blackberries are right, and also I heard everything you said about the ferry-house twins and I think Oars should hear it too.*

The twins went pale in the particular way of children whose crimes have been entered into evidence. Wren crowed.

The fox descended from his branch with the unhurried insolence of a creature who had once guided two fugitives across a dissolving country and had never let anyone forget it, and settled among the children like a king taking tribute. They petted him. He allowed it, barely, limping dramatically between them to ensure each child understood the sacrifice involved.

"Fox," said one of the twins — the quieter one, the girl who talked to stones and whom the stones liked best — "did you really walk to the end of the world?"

"I walked," said the fox, "to the end of a country that was forgetting itself. Which is nearly the same thing and much sadder." His good eye caught the sunlight. "Your Gardener walked it with me. And a fool with sawdust in his hair carried a box he wasn't supposed to open, and opened it anyway, at the exact wrong moment, thereby saving everything. History is mostly accidents committed bravely. Remember that."

"You tell it different every time," accused Wren.

"History that stays the same is dead history," said the fox. "Ask the librarian."

Nobody asked the librarian. Everyone in the new country knew about the librarian, who had chosen, on the last day, not to cross over into preservation but to stay and become the archive itself — a small stone tower on the northern hill where nothing was ever forgotten, staffed by a woman who remembered everything, including everything she would have preferred to forget, and who paid for the privilege by writing it all down, volume after volume, in a hand that never tired. Children visited her the way children elsewhere visited grandmothers who told long stories. She was, by universal agreement, the second-most important person in the country.

The first was asleep under the hill every winter and awake above it every spring, and was currently tickling the children's minds with a gust of leaf-laughter while the fox pretended not to enjoy the attention.

* * *

It was late afternoon when the fox stiffened.

Mara felt it before he moved — a change in the wind off the western hills, carrying a smell that belonged to no valley, no river, no hill in her country. Old magic. Sealed magic. The taste of wards grown dense and silent with long keeping.

The fox rose slowly, all the play gone out of his body, ears forward.

*Well?* Mara said.

*News,* said the fox. *From beyond the hills. A traveler — a bird, actually, a ragged old starling with an accent like gravel — dropped out of the high air this morning, half-starved, asking for the Gardener by name. Asking for you by your first name, Mara, which no starling should know.* He looked up at her, and his silvered muzzle wrinkled. *It says there are valleys past the western hills. Many valleys. Places the old wards held fast when everything outside went to nothing — sealed so tight the forgetting never touched them. Whole communities still living inside their borders, still forbidden, still silent, still believing the world ended.*

The hilltop went very quiet. Even the children felt it, the way animals feel weather turning; Wren's hand stilled on the fox's back.

Ilian had come up the path with the empty basket and stopped when he saw them all standing still. "What is it?" he asked. And then, reading her the way he had learned to read her, root-deep: "Something's out there."

*People,* Mara said. *Valley people. Our people — the ones the old wards locked away and saved and imprisoned, all in one act. They're still in there, Ilian. Still forbidden. Still teaching their children that the gift is a curse and the world beyond the rim is death.* She felt the truth of it moving through her rings like a new season. *They've been inside for years while we built all this. While the children learned to talk to beetles. They don't know the stars came back.*

The fox limped forward to the western edge of the hill, where the land fell away toward hills that were hers and then hills that were not, and stood looking out with his tail low.

*There's more,* he said. *The starling says the wards in those valleys are failing. Slowly — the way ours failed, star by star, night by night. Whatever power fed them died with the old country, and now they're running on habit and stubbornness, and habits end.* He glanced back at her, and for a moment she saw in his eyes the dying fox beyond the ward-line, years and another lifetime ago, offering her a bargain that had cost her everything and given her everything back changed. *They'll break soon. And when they do, those people will step out into a country they've been taught is a corpse, full of beasts that speak and trees that remember, and they will be more frightened than you have ever been in your life.*

*Unless someone meets them first,* Mara said.

*Unless someone meets them first,* the fox agreed. *Someone who remembers what it is to hide a gift. Someone who knows exactly what it costs to be told the world ends at the ward-line — and exactly what it costs when you learn it doesn't.* His grin came back then, sharp and old and fond. *Someone with deep roots and a gate.*

Mara laughed, and the sound moved down through her roots into the deep places of the hill, and the whole valley heard it and did not know yet why it gladdened them, only that it did.

Ilian set down the basket. He crossed to her and laid his hand on the bark over her heart, and she felt him understand all of it at once — the western road, the failing wards, the long work beginning again — and felt him make the same choice he had made at the door, at the orchard, at every crossroads of their lives, freely and without ceremony and forever.

"West, then," he said. "I'll start on the gate today."

*Not today,* Mara said, gentling him the way she gentleed weather. *Today the children are here, and there will be a festival soon, and the blackberries are not yet ripe. But soon. We will build a gate in the western hills, wide enough for whole valleys to walk through, and we will go out to meet them ourselves — not as gardeners over the wall, but as neighbors. As proof.* She felt the future opening in her like a bud deciding. *Let them come angry and afraid and forbidden-feeling. Let them bring their silence with them. There is room in this country for all of it. There was always room. That was the whole point of planting it.*

Below, the children had begun to run down the hill, chasing the fox, who was pretending to be chased and enjoying it immensely, and their laughter rang up through the evening air like bells made of something newer than bronze.

Mara stood over her country in the gold light — the singing river, the archive tower, the ferry-house, the small warm valley where she had once hidden a gift and called herself less than she was — and smiled with every leaf at once.

Roots deep. Gates open.

Somewhere past the western hills, in valleys that did not yet know the stars had returned, the first ward cracked like ice in spring.

Mara felt it, and began, patiently, joyfully, to grow toward it.
