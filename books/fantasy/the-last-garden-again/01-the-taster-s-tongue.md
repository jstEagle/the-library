## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There were three rules to surviving Corvane as a person of small and illegal talents, and Quentin Ravensworth had written them herself, in her head, the way other people wrote letters to distant relatives they never intended to visit.

Rule one: never be noticed.

Rule two: never be interesting.

Rule three: when someone is about to die of poison in front of you, be boring about it.

She was reciting rule three to herself that particular morning as she stood at the tasting table in the royal kitchens, a thin silver spoon balanced between her fingers and a bowl of the king's breakfast porridge steaming gently beneath her nose. The porridge smelled the way it always did—cream, honey, a whisper of cinnamon because King Aldric believed cinnamon improved his digestion, though every physician in the palace had told him otherwise. It looked correct. It smelled correct. And underneath all of it, so faint it might have been nothing at all, there was bitterness.

Almond. That part she knew well; bitter almond was practically an old friend, one who dropped by uninvited two or three times a year and had to be shown the door. Her tongue had catalogued every poison that passed through Corvane's underbelly over ten years of tasting, and bitter almond was the commonest of them, cheap, reliable, the poison of impatient wives and ambitious cousins. She could have handled the almond alone with a yawn.

But this wasn't only almond.

Beneath the bitterness ran something else, something colder, like the smell of a cellar in deep winter, or the memory of snow on stone. It didn't belong in a kitchen. It didn't belong anywhere above ground, if she was honest, and Quentin made a practice of being honest with herself exactly once per day, usually before breakfast, so that the rest of the day could proceed on comfortable fictions.

"Well?" said Master Hobb, the head cook, wringing his towel as though the porridge had personally insulted his mother. "Is it fit?"

Quentin took another sip. Held it on her tongue the way she'd taught herself to do, letting the flavors introduce themselves one by one. Cream. Honey. Cinnamon. Almond—and there, curled inside the almond like a worm inside an apple, the cold thing.

"Yes," she said. "Perfectly fit."

Three people exhaled at once. Master Hobb sagged against his worktable with the relief of a man who had just been told he would live to see supper. The undercook, Pell, went back to scrubbing a pot with renewed vigor. And Tam Weller, the king's morning steward—a round, cheerful man whose greatest ambition in life was to be the first to carry the royal breakfast up the east stairs—beamed at her and reached for the tray.

"Ha! Knew it," Tam said. "Never doubted our Quentin. Steadiest tongue in Corvane."

"Steady as anything," Quentin agreed, and then, because rule three required her to be boring about it: "Though I'd let it sit five minutes, if I were you. Hot porridge scalds the tongue, and a scalded tongue can't taste tomorrow's breakfast. His Majesty would hate for you to lose your touch."

It was nonsense, of course. Porridge cooled in five minutes to nothing dangerous. But Tam was a creature of habit and deference, and he laughed, set the tray down, and ambled off to complain about the state of the silver polish while the porridge sat cooling by the window like an innocent.

The cold thing in the almond needed time to do its work. Quentin knew this the way she knew rain was wet—not from books, though she'd read what few books existed, but from the long slow education of her own body. Ten years ago, when she'd first been assigned to the taster's post, a kindly old woman named Mistress Fern had explained the arrangement to her with brutal clarity: *You'll take a little of everything they try to give him, dear. Every dose, a drop or two short of full, slipped into your cup instead of his. In a year you'll stomach most of it. In five you could drink a river of it. That's why tasters are paid so poorly and buried so quietly—they're meant to last.*

Mistress Fern had not mentioned that the immunity came with side effects. That Quentin now found ordinary food almost flavorless, bread like sawdust, wine like warm water, so that she ate for fuel the way a furnace eats coal. That her eyes had gone faintly silver at the edges, which she hid behind a plain expression and the palace's perpetual gloom. Or that once a month, without fail, her hands went numb from fingertip to wrist, as though her body were politely reminding her of everything it had swallowed on another man's behalf.

None of which mattered this morning. What mattered was the cold thing, which was not almond, not arsenic, not any of the forty-odd poisons she could name. Which meant someone had gone to trouble. Trouble meant money, and money meant intent, and intent aimed at the king's breakfast tray meant the palace had a problem.

But the palace's problems were not Quentin's problems. Quentin's problems began and ended with keeping her name out of conversations. So she did what she always did with trouble too large to swallow: she diluted it.

"Master Hobb," she said mildly, "I don't suppose we've had any new suppliers lately?"

Hobb frowned. "New? No. Same creamery, same miller, same spice merchant going on nine years." Then he paused, and his towel stopped wringing. "Well. There's the new honey."

"New honey."

"A gift, more or less. Lord Vessic sent down six jars last week, from his estates south. Fine-looking stuff. We've used it in the king's breakfast since Thursday." Hobb's face performed a small, terrible journey from confusion to arithmetic to horror. "Thursday," he repeated. "He's had it since Thursday."

"And he's fine," Quentin said quickly, because Hobb had begun to look like a man watching his own gallows being assembled. "Clearly he's fine. Whatever's in it is slow, or light, or both. But I'd pull the honey from the stores all the same, and maybe mention to Lord Vessic's man that His Majesty found the last jar *slightly* over-sweet. Nothing more than that. A matter of taste."

"A matter of—" Hobb blinked at her. Then, with the gratitude of a man handed a rope just before drowning: "A matter of taste. Yes. Yes, I'll see to it directly."

And that, Quentin thought, was that. By noon the honey would be gone from the kitchens, carried away in a covered pail by a red-faced Master Hobb. Lord Vessic would receive a delicately worded complaint and would either abandon his scheme or change his poison. The king would go on eating cinnamon in defiance of medical advice. No one would ever know that anything had happened at all, which was precisely how Quentin liked her heroisms: unwitnessed, unrecorded, and unthanked.

Tam Weller collected the tray five minutes later and bore it triumphantly up the east stairs, whistling.

* * *

The rest of the morning passed in the usual rhythm of the tasting table. Breakfast, then the midmorning broth the king took for his joints, then the little dish of candied ginger he believed nobody knew about. Quentin tasted each one, found each one merely mortal in its intentions, and let her mind drift along the grooves it had worn over a decade.

She thought, as she often did in the quiet hours, about the room she rented in Tanners' Row, and the little wooden box beneath the loose floorboard where she kept her second life. Not the immunity—that secret lived in her blood, portable, invisible. The box held the other thing. The charms.

They were small things. That was the point of them. A key that remembered the shape of its lock, so a widow locked out of her own house by a thieving nephew could get back in. A candle that burned grief instead of wax, an inch of flame for every night of weeping, guttering out on its own when the worst had passed. A ribbon that kept a child's fever dreams gentle until morning. None of it grand. None of it the sort of magic that made the crown's mages come running with their badges and their binding-circles. Just quiet charms for quiet people, sold in the night market for coppers, from a woman who never gave her name and whose customers, bless them, never gave theirs.

Magic had been outlawed in Corvane for eleven years. Quentin had been selling charms in it for ten. She had survived this long on a single principle, which she considered the distilled essence of all three of her rules: *be forgettable*. Be the gray woman at the gray stall. Be the taster whose name the courtiers couldn't recall even as they thanked her. Be, if possible, slightly less memorable than furniture.

It worked. It worked so well that some mornings Quentin woke and had to lie still for a moment, doing the exercise her mother had taught her before—well. Before. *Name three things about yourself that no one else knows,* her mother used to say, *and you'll never fully disappear.* This morning's list: she hated the smell of tallow but loved the smell of rain on hot stone. She could whistle through her thumbs. She was twenty-seven years old and had never once eaten a meal she could taste.

That last one wasn't strictly true anymore. Once, about four years past, she had drunk a cup of plum wine at the winter feast—her own private rebellion, a full swallow of something rather than her usual careful half-drop—and for three shining seconds the world had bloomed: plum and smoke and sweetness, layered like dawn. Then the flavor drained away and left her standing in the great hall surrounded by music she could hear and joy she could see and none of it hers, and she had understood that the immunity hadn't taken her sense of taste. It had taken her place at the table.

She was saved from this line of thinking—which led nowhere pleasant—by the arrival of the afternoon tea, and by the arrival, with it, of a problem wearing a livery coat.

"Ravensworth." The under-steward, Brack, stood at the kitchen door holding a small covered cup. He was a narrow man with the complexion of someone who spent his life indoors counting things. "His Majesty's physician wants the new digestive tea tested twice today. Something about the batch." He set the cup down on the tasting table and lingered, which Brack never did. Brack's whole philosophy of life was *deliver and depart*.

Quentin lifted the cover. Chamomile, fennel, a thread of licorice root. And beneath it—the cold thing again. Stronger now. Much stronger.

Her stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn.

"Twice today," she said evenly.

"The physician's orders." Brack was watching her with an expression she couldn't quite read. Not suspicion. Something more like... apology? "Strange business. Third odd delivery this week. First the honey, now this. Master Hobb says the spice merchant swears his stores haven't been touched."

"Stores do lie sometimes," Quentin said lightly. "Usually on their owner's behalf."

Brack almost smiled. Then, in a voice pitched low enough that only she could hear it: "Between us, Ravensworth—you've been at this table ten years and never once spat anything out. Never turned green, never took to your bed. The other tasters, back in the old days, they had their bad weeks. You've never had a bad hour." He straightened his cuffs. "I count things for a living. I notice sums that don't add."

The kitchen noise seemed to fall away. Somewhere a kettle sang; somewhere Pell dropped a spoon and swore at it with real feeling. Quentin kept her face arranged in its usual mild blankness—the face of a woman thinking about nothing more exotic than supper—and felt, beneath it, the familiar cold crawl up her spine.

"Good diet," she said. "And strong nerves."

"Mm," said Brack. He tapped the table twice, a small dry sound like a beetle on wood. "The physician wants this cup emptied within the quarter hour. All of it, he says. Proper testing." And he left, his footsteps quick and light, leaving the poisoned tea sitting between Quentin's hands like a dare.

She stared at it. The steam rose in a thin, obedient spiral.

He knew. Or he suspected, which was nearly as fatal. Either way, the physician's *twice today* and *all of it* was not medicine—it was a test with a human instrument. Someone wanted to know what Quentin Ravensworth's tongue could really do, and had chosen a method that would kill any ordinary taster by suppertime.

She could refuse. Refusal meant the physician's report, the questions, the attention. Attention was death in this city, slow or fast depending on mood.

She could drink it. A cupful of whatever-this-was, when her education consisted of drops and dribbles, was a gamble even her blood might lose.

Or—

Quentin glanced around the kitchen. Hobb was shouting cheerfully at a delivery boy. Pell was elbow-deep in suds. The scullery door stood open to the yard, and through it came the smell of rain beginning on hot stone, which she loved, and which no one else in the room had even noticed.

She tipped the tea into the slop-pail when no one was looking—all of it, proper testing, quarter hour observed by God and the drainpipe—and refilled the cup from the untouched reserve teapot, into which she slipped, from the vial sewn into her sleeve, the smallest pinch of harmless bitter herb she carried for exactly such purposes. Then she drank her own decoction slowly, grimacing appropriately, and announced the tea fit, if over-strong on the fennel.

By evening, the physician's report would say the taster drank the full measure and suffered no ill effect, and the mystery of Quentin Ravensworth would deepen by one more layer in a mind that counted things.

She should have felt triumphant. Instead, walking home through the blue dusk of Tanners' Row, she felt the opposite of triumphant, which was exposed. Three odd deliveries in a week. Poison threaded with something cold and underground and wrong. A steward who noticed sums. The safe gray walls of her invisible life were developing hairline cracks, and through them she could hear something moving.

At her door, she paused. From inside her rooms—she was certain she'd latched the window, she was always certain—came the faint sound of breathing. Ragged. Wet. Human.

Quentin Ravensworth stood very still in the dark of her own doorway, hand on the latch, and thought: *rule one. Rule one, rule one, rule one.*

Then she opened the door anyway.
