## 2. Charms for Quiet People

The night market of Corvane began the way all good secrets did: quietly, and after dark.

By day, Tanners' Row was a street of honest filth—dyers' vats steaming, hides stretched on frames like flayed giants, boys hauling buckets of something you did not want to think about too closely. But at moonrise the tanners went home to their suppers, the vats were banked, and out came the lanterns. Not many. Just enough. A candle stub here, a hooded lamp there, each one angled so its light fell only on what needed seeing and nowhere else.

Quentin arrived as she always did, in her second-best cloak, which was to say her best cloak with the mended shoulder turned toward the wall. She carried a basket that looked full of eggs and turnips if anyone asked, and was not.

Her stall—if you could call three planks balanced on two barrels a stall—sat at the very end of the row, past the fortune-teller who was a fraud, past the woman selling love philtres that were mostly wine, past the man who swore his newts could smell lies. Quentin had once watched a customer lie directly into that man's newts' faces and the newts hadn't so much as blinked. She'd felt a certain professional kinship with them.

She set out her wares with the care other people gave to jewels.

A key that remembered locks. It looked like any brass key from any junk drawer, but hold it near a door it had opened before and it grew warm, and turned itself gently in your pocket, like a cat asking to be let in. Useful for servants. Useful for widows who kept locking themselves out of their own grief-houses. Useful for people whose business happened at hours when doors were supposed to be shut.

Candles that burned grief. Not magic that erased it—she wasn't a fraud, and besides, grief was load-bearing in most lives—but a candle that, while it burned, made the grief sit quietly in a corner instead of sitting on your chest. You still had it when the candle guttered out. But you'd had six hours of being able to breathe, and sometimes six hours was the difference between a bad week and a bad end.

A thimble that mended hems by itself. A spoon that made thin soup taste like it had opinions. A small tin whistle that, blown once, made dogs decide they liked you—a bestseller among the friendless, though Quentin privately thought those buyers would have been better served by the candles.

And one thing she never displayed, only sold to people who asked correctly: a charm against noticing. A plain gray pebble that made whoever carried it slightly harder to remember. Not invisible. Just... skippable. The eye slid off them the way water slides off grease. Quentin had made her first one at fifteen, out of sheer terror, and had worn one ever since. She made them for others now, but never cheaply, because being forgettable was worth exactly as much as your life, and she said so.

"Evening, Turnip."

That was Marta the fraud fortune-teller, who had decided years ago that Quentin's basket of decoy vegetables meant her name was Turnip, and no correction had ever taken root.

"Marta."

"You look tired. Death in your left hand, sorrow in your right, and a tall stranger coming over the horizon." Marta squinted at Quentin's actual hands, which held a candle and a coil of twine. "Well. Close enough."

"You're a fraud, Marta."

"I'm *affordable*. There's a difference, and it's why I eat." Marta leaned in, lowering her voice to the register she probably imagined was discreet. "Quiet word. There's a crown man been walking the row since sundown. Asking after charms. Real ones."

Quentin's hands did not stop arranging. This was a skill ten years in the making—the body learning to keep working while the mind ran screaming through the house checking locks. "Which crown man?"

"Inquisitor's livery, or near enough. Gray and silver." Marta shrugged. "Could be nothing. Could be he collects amulets."

"Crown men don't collect amulets."

"No," Marta agreed cheerfully. "They collect people. I'd pack up early, Turnip."

But Quentin had learned long ago that fleeing was its own kind of notice. A woman who packed up early was a woman with something to hide; a woman who stayed and sold a fraudulent fortune-teller a spoon was just a woman selling spoons. So she stayed. She sold Marta the spoon—"for your husband, so his stew tastes like effort"—and waited to see what the night brought.

What the night brought, for the first hour, was ordinary customers, and Quentin was grateful for every ordinary minute of them.

* * *

The widow Fenner came first, as she did every month, wrapped in a shawl even the moths had given up on. She didn't give her real name—nobody here did—and Quentin didn't ask. In ten years of this, Quentin had learned perhaps forty names, and she suspected half were invented and the other half belonged to people long dead or long gone. Names were currency here, and quiet people spent them carefully.

"The usual," Widow Fenner said.

Quentine counted out three grief-candles into a paper twist. "Your sister's anniversary?"

"Aye." The old woman's chin trembled and steadied, the way it had learned to. "Forty-one years dead and I still set a place for her at supper. My landlord thinks I've got a secret gentleman caller." A wet little laugh. "I have. He's made of memory and he never takes his boots off."

"Three candles," Quentin said. "Burn them together. The corner where she used to sit works best—it likes familiar rooms."

"It does that," Widow Fenner said, and paid in coppers counted out one at a time, each coin warmed by her hand, and Quentin took them without hurry, because hurry would have been a kind of insult.

This was the part of her life nobody in the palace knew about, and she guarded it more fiercely than her immunity. Anyone could taste soup. But to sit across three planks from an old woman and sell her six hours of breathable air—there was a craft in that. Her mother had called it *hearthwork*, back when Quentin was small enough to watch: *Magic isn't thunder, love. Thunder impresses. Hearthwork keeps people alive.* Her mother had done hearthwork for neighbors until the famine winter, until—

Until. Quentin put the *until* in its box, where it lived, and closed the lid, and smiled at the next customer.

* * *

The key-that-remembers-locks went to a nervous young man in a servant's livery who called himself Pip and was almost certainly not named Pip. He wanted it for his mistress's house, he said, because he kept forgetting which of the twelve doors on his delivery route stuck, and the beatings were affecting his enthusiasm for employment.

"And that's the whole truth?" Quentin asked mildly, holding the key just out of reach.

Pip went pink. "Near enough the whole of it."

"Pip." She let the name carry the exact weight of a woman who was not fooled but was willing to be. "This key remembers every lock it opens. If you use it where you shouldn't, it will remember that too, and it will grow warm in your pocket at inconvenient moments, and you will know that I know. Do we understand each other?"

"Yes'm."

She sold it to him anyway. Half the keys she sold went to young men with pink faces, and if she refused them all, they'd simply go to the newt-smelling liar or worse, and at least hers came with a conscience attached. She liked to think of them all out there in the city, growing warm in guilty pockets, keeping a hundred small dishonesties honest-ish.

The soup-spoon went to a fishwife who wanted her husband to stop complaining about her cooking. The dog-whistle went to a grave-digger with kind eyes and no friends whatsoever, and Quentin threw in a grief-candle unasked and told him it was a free sample, which was a lie, because nothing she gave away was ever a sample of anything. It was just kindness wearing a merchant's apron, because kindness unexplained drew less notice than kindness explained.

* * *

Doss came at the middle of the night, as he always did.

He arrived the way big men do when they're trying not to arrive—slowly, apologetically, his shoulders saying *pardon me* to the crowd before the rest of him got there. City watch, but off-duty, or pretending to be: no helmet, plain cloak, the truncheon hidden rather than shown. He stood at the edge of her lamplight for a moment the way he always did, like a large dog deciding whether the room had room for him.

"Spoon-woman," he said. That was what he called her. Not Turnip—he'd heard Marta use that and rejected it on grounds he'd never explained—but Spoon-woman, because the first time he'd ever bought anything from her it had been a spoon, and Doss was not a man who updated his records.

"Doss."

"That's not my—"

"I know," Quentin said. "It's the whole truth, though, near enough."

Something moved through his face—surprise, then the slow dawn of a smile, the kind that took its time crossing all that landscape. "You remembered."

"You're memorable," she said, which was true, and which was also the strangest compliment she had ever paid anyone, because Doss was memorable in exactly the way she could least afford: sincerely. Most people who lingered at her stall wanted something, and wanting made them easy to file away. Doss lingered the way weather lingered. He simply didn't seem to know how to leave.

He bought the same thing he always bought. "The candle," he said. "The one for—" and then stopped, and gestured vaguely at his own chest, which was where Doss kept everything he couldn't say, and where, Quentin suspected, it was getting crowded.

"Grief-candle," she supplied.

"If you want to call it that."

"What do you call it?"

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything. "The one that makes the house quiet," he said finally. "Not quiet like empty. Quiet like... after snow."

*After snow.* Quentin's hands paused over the candles, and she was grateful, suddenly and fiercely, that the lamplight was poor. Ten years she had sold small comforts to people who never gave their names, and she had told herself it was trade, it was survival, it was hearthwork, and all of that was true. But once a month a watchman bought a candle and described it better than she could have, and once a month she understood that she was not merely surviving. She was lonely, and this stall was the one place her loneliness got fed.

"One candle," she said, voice level. "Burn it in the main room. It doesn't like bedrooms—too many dreams competing."

"Aye." He paid in proper coin, counted wrong on purpose so she'd have to correct him, which was the closest Doss ever came to flirtation: he manufactured small errands for her hands. She corrected the count. Their fingers did not touch, because neither of them allowed it, and both of them noticed the not-touching, and neither said anything, because the entire architecture of their acquaintance was built on things carefully unsaid.

He should have left then. He always should have left then, and usually managed it within a quarter hour. Tonight he shifted his weight, and looked at the lanterns, and said:

"There's a crown man on the row tonight."

"So Marta says."

"He's not collecting amulets." Doss's jaw worked. "He's asking after hearthworkers. Real ones. There's a list." He glanced at her, quick and uncomfortable. "You're not on it. I checked. But names get added to lists, is my point. Lists are living things. They grow."

Quentin arranged three candles that needed no arranging. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you sell honest charms," Doss said, "and the last honest thing I saw on this row was you, and because if your name ever does get added to a list, I'd—" He stopped. Restarted, the way a cart restarts on a hill. "I'd want you to already be gone."

The silence between them filled up with everything unsaid, which was nearly everything, and held its shape the way deep water holds its shape.

"Doss." His false name. His real name, probably; she'd never known which, and had decided years ago that not-knowing was a gift she could give him. "Go home. Light your candle."

"Aye." But he lingered. Of course he lingered. He looked at her hands, which were clever and scarred and knew a hundred small magics, and she looked at his face, which was open as a door in summer, and for one reckless moment Quentin thought about telling him. About the taster's tongue. About the immunity built drop by drop over a decade of swallowing death in small doses. About the map-shaped hole in her understanding of her own life that she didn't yet know was a hole.

Then somewhere down the row, gray and silver livery caught the lamplight, and the moment passed the way moments do—not broken, just quietly closed, like a book someone else decides you've read enough of.

"Goodnight, Spoon-woman," Doss said.

"Goodnight," Quentin said. And then, because the night had gone strange and she wanted one true thing in it: "Doss. Whatever the list grows into. Stay off it yourself."

He touched his forelock, half mockery, half blessing, and went, taking his too-big silence with him, and Quentin watched him go and hated how much of her watched him go.

* * *

She packed up early after all.

The crown man came down the row as she was loading her basket, unhurried, examining stalls with the pleasant thoroughness of a man counting sheep he intended to shear later. He paused at Marta's table. He paused at the newt-man's. He did not pause at Quentin's, but she felt his attention pass over her the way cold air passes under a door, and the gray pebble in her pocket sat warm and heavy as a second heart.

*Skippable,* she reminded herself. *You are skippable.*

He moved on. The market breathed again. Marta caught Quentin's eye across the row and tapped the side of her nose, insufferably pleased with herself.

Quentin walked home the long way, through streets that smelled of tallow and rain, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the cold. But the city felt different tonight—watched, listening, a list somewhere growing a new line. She climbed to her rooms above the cooper's yard, locked her door twice, and set her remaining candles out on the sill where they could keep the room's griefs company.

She was unbuckling her boots when she heard footsteps on the stair.

Heavy ones. Wrong ones—too fast, dragging, the rhythm of someone spending the last of themselves to climb. Quentin was on her feet with a candle in her fist like a weapon before her mind finished the arithmetic of the sound.

Then came the knock. Three blows, uneven, desperate. And a voice through the door, cracked and wet and dying, saying words that stopped her heart mid-beat:

"Quinnie. Quinnie Ravensworth. Open the door, girl. Your mother's friend—I have to—before they come—"

No living person knew that name.

The bells of Corvane began to ring.
