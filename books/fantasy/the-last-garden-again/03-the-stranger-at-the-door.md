## 3. The Stranger at the Door

The trouble with living invisibly for ten years, Quentin Ravensworth had learned, was that you got very good at hearing things other people didn't.

She heard the watch bells before they rang — or rather, she heard the particular quality of silence that came before them, the way the city held its breath when something went wrong in it. She'd been up late cataloguing her stock of charms: three keys that remembered locks, a candle stub that burned grief down to a manageable ember, a button that made its wearer slightly harder to remember than yesterday's bread. Honest work, if you squinted. Illegal work, if you didn't.

Which is why, when the knock came at her door — two soft taps and then, horribly, none at all — she knew before she opened it that her quiet life had just ended.

Nobody knocked twice politely and then stopped. Polite people knocked again. Rude people pounded. Watchmen announced themselves with all the subtlety of a dropped anvil. Only one kind of visitor knocked softly and then couldn't finish the job.

She opened the door on the third breath of hesitation, and a man fell into her arms like a sack of wet laundry that had opinions.

"By the Nine," she muttered, staggering backward under his weight. He was tall, grey-bearded, and bleeding from a wound under his ribs that had soaked his coat through to the color of old wine. "You couldn't have fallen on literally anyone else? There are four thousand people in this district."

He laughed, which was a mistake, because laughing made him bleed faster. Quentin got him inside, kicked the door shut with her heel, and did what she always did when catastrophe arrived uninvited: she put the kettle on.

Not because tea helped. Because her hands needed instructions, and if she gave them nothing to do they would shake.

* * *

Her rooms above the chandler's shop were small and arranged with the paranoid precision of a woman who had spent a decade being forgettable on purpose. Everything valuable fit into a satchel she could grab in four seconds. Everything incriminating could be burned in six. She had rehearsed the burning. She had *dreamed* about the burning, some nights, in the way you dream about falling.

Now a dying stranger was leaking onto her floorboards beside the hearth, and every rehearsal felt suddenly like a child's drawing of a flood.

"Press here," she told him, guiding his hand to the wound. "Hard. I mean it. You're not allowed to die in my rooms; the rent doesn't cover it."

"You always were—" he coughed, wet and awful, "—bossy. Little Quen."

The world tilted.

Quentin stood very still with the kettle in her hand, steam rising past her face, and felt ten years of careful, invisible living develop a crack straight down the middle like a bad teacup.

*Little Quen.*

No one called her that. No one alive called her that. That name belonged to a kitchen garden behind a house outside Corvane, to a woman with flour on her hands who sang off-key and traded in small magics, to a summer that ended in famine and soldiers and a door closing. Her mother had died — no. Her mother had *vanished*, which was different, though after ten years the difference had worn smooth as a river stone. And the only person who had ever shortened her name that way, who had used it with that exact mixture of exasperation and affection—

"Who are you?" Quentin asked quietly. It came out steadier than she felt, which was her one consistent talent.

"A friend of your mother's." He said it like an apology. Maybe it was one. His face was grey as porridge now, and she recognized the look of someone doing arithmetic with the time they had left. "Tam Ostell. I kept bees. She kept—"

"She kept everyone's secrets," Quentin said. Her voice sounded very far away. "I remember you. You brought honey cakes. You let me steal from the hives and told my mother it was bears."

"It *was* bears," Tam said gravely. "Very small bears. In a child-shaped coat."

Despite everything — despite the blood and the bells-that-hadn't-rung-yet and the cold crawling up her spine — Quentin felt her mouth twitch. Then it stopped twitching, because Tam pulled something from inside his ruined coat with shaking hands, and the something was folded paper, soft with age and sweat, sealed with a smudge of brown wax that might once have been a bee.

"My mother's seal," she whispered.

"Listen to me." He grabbed her wrist with surprising strength, and pressed the folded map into her palm, folding her fingers over it one by one, as if he were tucking a sleeping child into bed. "Your mother didn't run. Your mother *paid*. Do you understand me? She paid so you'd live, and the payment wasn't finished, and they've come to collect the rest."

"The rest of what?" But even as she said it, some deep animal part of her, the part that had spent ten years tasting poison and pretending to flinch correctly, already knew. Knew the way you know the bitterness under the almond. "The rest of *what*, Tam?"

"Your name isn't only yours, little Quen." His eyes were going glassy, but he fixed them on hers with the last of his stubbornness, and it was the same stubbornness that had lied to her mother about bears. "Half of you was promised. Before you drew breath. Beneath the mountain. The Undercourt holds the ledger, and the ledger holds you, and the long winter comes early this year because they're *hungry* for what they're owed—"

"Tam, stop, save your breath—"

"Breath is the one thing I've plenty of use for no longer." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth, and he was still trying to make her laugh, because that was what her mother's friends had done. They had all been kind that way. Kindness had gotten most of them arrested. "The map remembers the way. The mountain seals at first snow. You must go down before it closes, or wait a generation in the dark and the cold, and by then—" he coughed, long and terrible — "by then there won't be enough of you left up here to matter. Find Vail. The court mage. Serah Vail. She was the girl who—"

The bells began.

Quentin's head snapped toward the window. Not the district bell — those rang for fires, for riots, for royal processions nobody wanted. This was the deep bell in the Citadel tower, the one that meant *the watch moves*, and it was followed almost immediately by the nearer bells of the ward stations, waking in sequence like dogs taking up a bark.

"They're close," Tam said. Not afraid. Resigned, the way a man is resigned to weather. "They followed me from the river gate. I lost them twice but I'm slower than I was when your mother and I were young idiots." He tried to push himself up on one elbow and failed, and Quentin caught him and lowered him gently back against her woodpile, and the gentleness of it broke something loose in her chest.

"Why me?" she demanded, and hated how young her voice sounded. "Why now? Ten years, Tam. Ten years I've been nobody. I taste soup. I sell buttons. Why couldn't the ledger have forgotten me?"

"Because ledgers don't forget." His hand found hers again, clumsy now, cold. "That was the whole point of you, child. Your mother made sure you'd be *safe* to forget — immune to their poisons, invisible to their eyes — so that someday, when you were ready, you could go down and take yourself back. She bought you time with everything she had. Don't waste it being angry at her. Be angry later. Anger keeps. Like honey."

A fist hammered on the street door below. Then another fist, another door, the watch working outward through the chandler's row with the mechanical thoroughness she knew too well from the other side of it — from the kitchens, where she'd watched stewards count spoons while men with white gloves counted souls.

"Back stairs," Quentin said automatically. "There's a back stair, the roof connects to the tanner's—"

"I'll not make the roof, little Quen." He said it kindly. He pressed the map harder into her hands, both of his around both of hers now, and his grip was failing and his eyes were bright and terrible. "You listen. Whatever they tell you up there — whatever the crown says, whatever the mages say — remember this: they fed you. Every meal you ever tasted for them, they were feeding you *to* something too. Now go. Live loudly. It drives them mad."

Below, boots on stairs. Many boots. The chandler's dog, old Bess, began to bark and then abruptly stopped, which was worse than barking.

"Tam—"

"Out the window, girl. *Now.*" And then, softer, with the ghost of the man who'd brought honey cakes and blamed the bears: "You look so much like her. Tell the mountain she cheated fairly. Tell it — tell it we never stopped keeping her bees' memory—"

His hand slackened.

For one absurd moment Quentin thought about shaking him, thought about the kettle, thought about tea, thought about the hundred useless tender rituals people performed when the true ritual required more courage than they had. Instead she did the thing she was actually good at. She closed his eyes with two fingers, took the map, and moved.

* * *

Later — crouched in the dark between the chimney pots of the tanner's roof with the folded map against her heart and the city ringing below her like a struck bowl — Quentin would try to remember exactly how she'd gotten out. The window. The drainpipe she'd never trusted. The gap to the tanner's eave that she'd measured with her eyes a hundred times and never once intended to jump. Her body had apparently been planning its escape for a decade without consulting her, which felt, in retrospect, entirely characteristic.

The watch found Tam Ostell's body in the rooms of a food-taster named Quentin Ravensworth, and by morning every bell in Corvane would know it, and by noon every market stall would be whispering it, and the safe grey shape of her life — the shape she had built plank by plank, secret by secret, ten years of being nobody — lay behind her in a pool of blood that was rapidly cooling into evidence.

But that was later. Right now there was only the roof, and the cold, and the bells, and the map.

She unfolded it a crack, just enough to see by the moon. Her mother's handwriting marched across it in tight brown ink — measurements, gates, warnings in a cipher Quentin half-recognized from childhood games — and beneath it all, in letters pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the page, a single line:

*Beneath the mountain lies what was pawned. Go down before winter, my love, and bring yourself home.*

The bells rang on. Somewhere below, the watch was kicking in doors.

Quentin Ravensworth, invisible for ten years, dead in the eyes of the law since midnight, sat on a stranger's rooftop with her mother's handwriting warming her hands like a coal, and began — carefully, methodically, the way she did everything — to plan how to break into hell.
