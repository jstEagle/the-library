## 5. Ten Years Invisible

The rooftops of Corvane at dawn were the only honest part of the city.

Down below, everything lied. The cobbled streets lied about where they led. The shopfronts lied about what they sold. The palace, gleaming white on its hill like a tooth in a rich man's mouth, lied most of all. But up here—among the slate and the chimney smoke and the pigeons who had no use for anyone's secrets—the city was simply what it was: a sprawl of tiles, tilted and cracked and honest about it.

Quentin ran across them anyway, because honesty had never once kept her alive.

Her boots found the rhythm her body remembered from a decade ago: *step soft, land low, trust the old roofs more than the new.* The new ones were slick with tar and ambition. The old ones, the crooked ones huddled down by the fishmarket and the tannery row, had sagged into their own shapes so long ago that they'd stopped pretending to be anything but ladders laid sideways across the sky.

Behind her, three streets back and one street over, the watch bells still rang.

They'd been ringing since before dawn. Not the lazy morning peal that meant nothing worse than a baker's cart losing a wheel—these were the sharp doubled strokes, the ones Quentin had heard perhaps four times in ten years, each time from very far away, each time grateful it was never for her. Someone was dead. Someone important was dead, or someone unimportant was dead somewhere important, and either way the watch would be pulling people out of doorways and asking questions with their fists.

And she had run. Of course she had run. A bleeding stranger dies in your rooms whispering your secret childhood name, and you do not stay to explain. You climb out the window with a folded map pressed against your ribs like a second heart, and you go up, because up is the one direction nobody respectable looks.

*Pip.*

The name sat in her mouth like a stone she'd swallowed as a child and only now noticed. Nobody living called her that. Her mother had called her that, in the years before the famine winter, in the little house with the garden gone wild behind it, when Pip had been a real girl with scraped knees and a talent for making dandelions bloom out of season just to see her mother laugh.

That was before. That was before the snow came early and stayed late, before the bread queues, before—

Before the bargain.

Quentin dropped from a tanner's roof onto the shoulders of an old counting-house, rolled, came up running, and told herself firmly that now was not the time to think about the famine winter.

Her body agreed. Her body was very good at not thinking. It had been practicing for ten years.

* * *

But bodies are liars too, and grief keeps its own hours. Somewhere between the third rooftop and the seventh, while the bells rang and her lungs burned with cold air, the memory ambushed her the way it always did—not gradually, but all at once, whole, like stepping through a doorway into a room she'd nailed shut years ago.

She was seven again. She was standing at the kitchen window of the house outside Corvane, watching snow fall in the wrong month, and her mother was sitting at the table with a candle burning green.

Not green like spring. Green like pond water, green like something drowned. Quentin had never seen a candle burn that color before or since, and even at seven she understood that this was not a candle for lighting rooms. This was a candle for being seen—from somewhere else.

"Pip." Her mother's voice was gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have. Her mother only used that voice for hard truths and bedtime. "Come sit with me."

The table was bare except for the candle. No bread. There hadn't been proper bread in weeks; they'd been eating the seed grain, which was its own kind of sin, eating next year to survive this one, and the year after that there would be nothing at all. The neighbors had already begun to leave, walking east toward relatives who might or might not exist. Some of them didn't make it past the crossroads. Everyone knew. Nobody said.

"Mama, why is the candle green?"

"Because I've asked someone for help," her mother said, "and they're coming to discuss terms."

"What sort of someone?"

Her mother looked at her then—a long look, the kind that takes inventory—and Quentin, at seven, felt for the first time the strange sensation of being *valued*, weighed against something she couldn't see. She would spend thirty years learning exactly how much that look had cost.

"The kind you pay," her mother said. "Sit down, love. And whatever happens, whatever you hear—remember that I did this because I love you. Say you'll remember."

"I'll remember," seven-year-old Quentin said, because children agree to anything, and because it was true.

The knock, when it came, didn't come from the door.

It came from under the floor. Three slow knocks, courteous, patient, the knock of someone who has traveled a very long way and is in no hurry whatsoever. The green candle flared. The fire in the hearth bowed, actually *bowed*, flames leaning flat like courtiers, and the cold that came up through the floorboards was not winter-cold. Winter-cold is honest. This cold was polite.

Her mother opened the trapdoor to the cellar herself. Quentin was told to stay at the table, and she stayed—mostly—though she turned on her chair, and through the cellar doorway she saw, for perhaps four seconds, four seconds she would take apart and examine for the rest of her life:

A figure in grey, tall, thin, its face pale as paper lanterns, standing in the root cellar among the turnips and the empty jars. Its eyes were lamps. Not glowing—*lit*, warm, the way windows are lit in houses where someone is home. And it was looking at her mother with an expression Quentin could not name until many years later, when she finally had words for it:

Hunger. But also hope. The two tangled together so tightly they were indistinguishable.

The voices that followed came muffled up through the floor, too low to catch the words, and it didn't matter, because Quentin heard the shape of the conversation perfectly. An offer. A hesitation. A price named. A silence long enough to grow things in.

Then her mother's voice, clear enough to rise through the boards like water through soil:

"Not me. Take mine if you must—but leave my daughter *something*. Promise me she gets to live. Really live. Swear it."

Another silence. Then the polite voice said something short, something that sounded almost gentle, and the green candle went out, and the cold left the house so suddenly that the fire stood up straight again like a soldier caught slouching, and her mother climbed the cellar stairs alone, carrying a small grey ledger-book in both hands the way you carry a sleeping child.

She put it in the flour bin. Then she made tea from pine needles and dried apple peel, and she smiled at Quentin, and the smile was the bravest and worst thing Quentin had ever seen.

"There," her mother said. "We'll be all right now. We're going to be all right."

And they *were*. That was the horror of it. That spring the snow melted early around their valley while it clung to the hillsides everywhere else. Their garden, dead all winter, came up green and absurd, vegetables fat and sweet while the neighbor's plots sulked. Money appeared—her mother took in sewing, then managing accounts, then advising merchants, always with the flour bin full of grey pages and her eyes always flicking to it, always checking, always afraid. They ate well. They were warm. And Quentin learned, without ever being taught, that their luck had a smell to it, faint and cold and faintly sweet, like the air over deep water.

She learned other things too. That her mother flinched at cellars. That she never again let Quentin play below ground level. That on certain nights she burned a green candle and sat very still, listening, and afterward her face was the face of someone who has received a letter from a creditor: relieved it isn't worse, terrified of what it means.

And Quentin learned, at seven, without knowing she was learning, the skill that would save her life a hundred times: how to be worth keeping. How to be useful, quiet, pleasant, forgettable. How to make yourself the kind of thing that no one ever thinks to collect.

She had thought, all these years, that she'd invented that trick herself. Standing on a rooftop above the fishmarket with watch bells ringing her name into the morning, Quentin finally understood otherwise.

Her mother had taught it to her. Had drilled it into her, gently, invisibly, every day of her childhood, without ever saying a word about why.

Because her daughter's soul belonged to something under the mountain. And the only protection left was to make sure nobody ever looked closely enough to come collect.

* * *

The map had been trying to tell her all night, and she'd refused to listen. Now, wedged in the lee of a chimney pot on the roof of the Old Custom House, with the bells finally winding down and the city below pretending to be ordinary, she unfolded it again.

The stranger's blood had dried along one fold, rust-brown, mapping its own small country. His hands had been ink-stained—she remembered that much clearly, though she couldn't remember his face, and that absence bothered her more than the blood. Who was he? Why her? What had he been running from, or running *to*, that he'd spent his last hour climbing the stairs to a food-taster's garret?

She'd thought, last night in the first blind panic, that it was a map of the city. Escape routes, maybe. Safe houses. Something a fellow smuggler-of-small-things might pass to a colleague.

It wasn't a map of the city.

It was a map of what was *under* it.

The lines were fine and old-fashioned, drawn by a careful hand, and they showed Corvane the way a moth shows a coat: as a surface, a skin, stretched over something vast. Beneath the palace hill, beneath the merchant quarter, beneath everything, the ink traced caverns. Galleries. Stairways worn smooth. A whole buried geography spreading out from the mountain's roots like the root-system of some enormous tree, and at the center of it, drawn with a care that bordered on reverence, a space labeled in a spidery antique hand:

*THE UNDERCOURT — WHERE THE KEPT ARE KEPT WARM.*

Underneath, smaller, in fresher ink, in a hand that shook:

*The ledger does not lie. Your name is in it. It has been in it since before you were born. I am sorry. I was there. — E.*

E. She turned the corner of the map over, looking for more, and found only one additional line, written along the margin in the same shaking script, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the paper:

*The mountain seals with the long winter. After that, no one goes down and no one comes up for a generation. If you mean to face what your mother faced, you have until first snow.*

Quentin sat very still on the cold rooftop, and the wind coming off the mountains smelled—faintly, unmistakably—of snow. Weeks early. Weeks and weeks early. The kind of early that made the market women mutter and the old soldiers spit.

*First snow,* said the margin. And the sky over the peaks, when she looked, was the color of dishwater.

"So," she said aloud, to the pigeons, because talking to pigeons was less mad than talking to herself. "Either I believe a dying stranger's map, climb under a mountain, and knock politely on the door of the thing that owns my soul. Or I don't believe it, and I spend the rest of my life waiting for a knock from under the floor."

A pigeon regarded her with the flat golden eye of a creature that has heard every human problem and been impressed by none of them.

"And option three," she continued, "I run. East, over the border, new name, new city, new rules. Never taste another royal meal. Never sell another remembering-key." She said it out loud to hear how it sounded, and it sounded like what it was: a lie with good posture. Because the debt wasn't in Corvane. The debt was *in her*. It had been in her since before she had a name for herself. She could walk to the edge of the world and it would still be sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal, hers, hers, hers, and someday—next year, in twenty years, on her deathbed—something patient and polite would come to collect, and she would die owing, having spent her whole life being small enough to lose.

Ten years invisible. Ten years of tasting poison and saying nothing, selling charms and pretending not to, shrinking herself down to the exact dimensions of a person nobody bothers to arrest. She had thought she was building a life.

She had been hiding in one. Hiding from a bill that came due before she could walk.

The grief arrived quietly, which was somehow worse than if it had shouted. It didn't wail. It just settled over her like the coming snow, soft and total, and it reframed everything: her mother's flinch at cellars. The pine-needle tea. The garden that bloomed too well. Every kindness her mother had ever done her, suddenly double-edged, purchased twice—once in coin, once in soul. *She bought my life,* Quentin thought, staring at the mountain. *And the price was me. And she paid it anyway, because the alternative was watching me starve, and any mother would. Any mother would.*

She wasn't angry. That was the terrible discovery of the morning. She had expected anger—it had been her companion for a decade without her knowing its name—and instead she found only a vast, aching tenderness, and underneath it, a question that had been waiting thirty years to be asked:

*Mother. What did you see in that cellar? What did it promise you? What made you think it was worth it?*

There was only one place to find out. And it was closing for the season.

* * *

She got down off the roofs by the old way, through the gap behind the cooper's yard where the drainpipe had rotted just enough to be climbable, and dropped into the alley smelling of sawdust and brine. The city had resumed its performance of normalcy: shutters banging open, carts groaning, the fishwives arguing prices with the conviction of philosophers. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, ordinary and round.

Quentin pulled her hood up and became, with thirty years of practice, nobody. A tired woman with a market basket. Move like you belong, breathe like you're bored, and no one will remember you by noon.

But inside the hood, her hands held the map against her ribs, and her mind—her poor, exhausted, newly-honest mind—was doing arithmetic.

To reach the Undercourt she needed the route beneath the mountain, and the map gave her the beginning of one: a road north out of Corvane, a pass, a gate marked with a symbol she recognized with a lurch—the same lantern-eyed sigil from her mother's grey ledger-book, the mark of the polite cold. To travel it she would need money, supplies, and above all *invisibility*, and she had just become the most interesting woman in the city. There were bells with her name in them. There was a dead man in her rooms. There would be questions, and questions were the one weather she had never learned to dress for.

And there was, she admitted, one other problem, the size of a person and dressed in court grey with a mage's silver chain: Serah Vail. The court mage who had tutored a lonely little girl in cantrips a lifetime ago and now hunted grown women through the city with a warrant and a conscience. If anyone in Corvane could read the traces of magic off a dead man's room, it was her. If anyone could follow Quentin's particular flavor of quiet across rooftops, it was her.

Quentin had spent ten years making sure Serah Vail never looked twice at her.

Now she needed her not to look at all—or, failing that, needed something better than invisibility.

She slipped into the current of the market crowd, one tired woman among hundreds, and let the city carry her north. Overhead, gulls wheeled and screamed their ancient complaints. Behind her, the palace glittered on its hill, lying beautifully, as always.

Ahead, past the walls, past the fields going silver with frost that had no business existing yet, the mountain waited with its lamp-lit eyes.

*First snow,* the margin had warned.

The wind shifted. Quentin tasted the air the way she tasted everything—carefully, professionally, noting every component—and spat softly.

Almond. Faint, cold, and wrong. The mountain air itself carried the ghost of bitter almond, the flavor of poison, the flavor of her whole hidden life, blowing down out of the high passes like a summons.

Winter was coming early. And it was coming for her.
