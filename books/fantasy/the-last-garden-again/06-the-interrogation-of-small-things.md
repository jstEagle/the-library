## 6. The Interrogation of Small Things

Serah Vail had a theory about lies: they lived in small things. Big things—the crown jewels, the king's word, the border treaties—were guarded by wards and oaths and men with halberds. But the truth about anyone could be found in the small things they touched every day. A chipped cup. A ledger kept too neatly. A kitchen boy who flinched at the name of a dead man.

So she began with small things.

* * *

The first was a cup.

She found it in the scullery behind the royal kitchens, set apart from the others on a high shelf, and it took her the better part of an hour to understand why. It was not finer than the rest—quite the opposite, it was ugly, lopsided, glazed the color of old porridge. But when she held it, her fingers tingled faintly, the way they did near unlicensed magic. Not a spell exactly. More like a memory of one.

"It's Quentin's," said the scullery boy, who had materialized at her elbow with the particular silence of someone hoping not to be noticed by a court mage and failing completely. "The taster's. She drinks her tea out of it."

"Why this cup?"

He shrugged with his whole body, a gesture that managed to convey *I don't know*, *I'm not going to tell you*, and *please don't turn me into a newt* all at once. Serah found this almost admirable.

"Does she do anything else? Anything unusual?"

"She saves lives," the boy said, before he could stop himself, and then went pale as whey.

"Go on."

"Nothing! I mean—she tastes everything, that's her job, but sometimes—" He wrung his rag like it owed him money. "Sometimes she'll say a dish is off when it isn't off. And then it turns out it was. Off. There was a steward last spring, Master Corben, he was going to serve the king the honeyed pears, and she spat them out and said the pears were bitter, and everyone laughed because pears, you know, but Master Corben ate one to show her, just to be kind, and he swelled up like a bladder and turned the color of a plum and they had to bleed him twice before he stopped seeing his grandmother."

"And no one thought this strange?"

"Everyone thought it strange," the boy said. "But she's been doing it ten years and the king's alive, so mostly people just call her lucky now." He paused. "Or cursed. Depends on whether they're asking for extra rations."

Serah let him go and stood a while among the steam and clatter, turning the ugly cup in her hands. Slow-built immunity. She'd read about it in the restricted shelves as an apprentice—a taster who dosed herself grain by grain until poison slid off her tongue like rain off wax. It took years. It took *discipline*. It took a person willing to hurt themselves quietly, daily, on the chance it would matter someday.

That wasn't the work of a criminal. That was the work of someone preparing for a siege.

The question was: which side of the walls did she expect to be standing on?

* * *

The second small thing was a woman named Maretta, who sold roasted chestnuts two streets from the night market and had, according to the watch rolls, purchased three charms from an unnamed vendor over the past six years.

Maretta received Serah in her stall with the wariness of a cat being shown a bath.

"Court mage," Serah said, by way of introduction. "I'm investigating a death."

"Aren't you always."

"I'm looking for a charm-seller. Small magics. Quiet customers."

Maretta's eyes went flat and polite, which Serah had learned—in ten years of interrogating the city's small people—was how the small people said *no*. So Serah sat down on the stool beside the brazier as if she intended to stay through winter, and helped herself to a chestnut, and burned her fingers, and said, "Ow," with genuine feeling, and waited.

It took four chestnuts.

"There was a candle," Maretta said finally. "Burns grief, they say. My man died five years back and I couldn't sleep in the room where his coat still hung, so I bought the candle, and I lit it, and I don't know if it worked or if I just wanted it to work, but I slept." She glared at Serah, daring her to mock. "You can put that in your report if you want. *Witchcraft effective. Woman slept.*"

"Who sold it to you?"

"A girl. Youngish. Plain the way you're plain—you look right past her and then can't remember her face after." Maretta snorted. "Except I remembered. Because she haggled."

"Haggled?"

"Down. For *me*. Charged me half what the thing was worth and pretended she was robbing me blind. Said business was good and she needed to move stock." Maretta shook her head slowly. "Ten years I've had a stall on this street. You learn faces. Hers was the only one that made a point of never being learned. And now there's a dead man in the Tanners' Quarter with her smell of magic all over him, or so my cousin's cousin says, who heard it from a watchman."

"What else does your cousin's cousin's watchman say?"

"That the dead man rang the bells after he was already dead." Maretta wrapped her knuckles around the brazier's warmth. "Which is either very bad news for the dead man or very bad news for the rest of us."

Serah left her a copper for the chestnuts, which Maretta refused, and then accepted, and then used to buy a candle-stub from a passing chandler, which Serah chose not to think about too hard.

* * *

The third small thing was Doss.

She found him at the watch house on Candle Lane, filling out reports in handwriting so bad it should have been classified as a weapon. He was a broad, weathered man with the air of someone who had made peace with disappointment long ago and now considered it an old friend.

"Watchman Doss. You patrol the night market."

"Some nights." He didn't look up.

"You bought a key from a charm-seller there. A key that remembers locks."

Now he looked up. His face did something complicated and settled on *bland*. "Did I."

"The vendor's ledger lists it. You paid with a marked coin—watch-house issue."

Doss leaned back until his chair creaked in protest. "Court mage, I'm going to tell you something about the night market, since nobody's told you yet and you seem like you'll figure it out anyway and be insufferable about it. Nobody in that market has a real name. Not the sellers, not the buyers, not the man who sells pies that are legally required to contain pie. If I spent my nights arresting people for buying luck-charms, I'd have no time to arrest anyone for the crimes that actually ruin lives, and my sergeant would have my badge, and rightly." He tapped the report in front of him. "So. Did I buy a key? Possibly. Do I remember who sold it? Can't say. Faces slip my mind."

"Everything slips your mind?"

"Everything worth slipping." He held her gaze, and there was something underneath the blandness—not defiance. Worry. The specific worry of a man guarding a door and hoping very hard that no one asks him what's behind it.

Serah thanked him and left. In the corridor she paused, looked back through the doorway's grime-fogged glass, and watched Watchman Doss take a small folded paper from inside his coat, read it, and put it away again with the tenderness most men reserved for love letters or warrants.

She filed that away under *later*.

* * *

The fourth small thing was not a thing at all. It was an absence.

Serah spent an evening in the archive beneath the palace, pulling employment records, ration rolls, tax ledgers. Quentin Ravensworth, royal food-taster, appointed eleven years ago. Appointed by warrant signed by the King's Own Chamberlain. Salary modest. Conduct exemplary. Prior history—

Nothing.

Not thin. Not redacted. *Nothing.* No birth record. No family. No apprenticeship. It was as though the girl had been decanted fully formed into the kitchens at age sixteen, and every clerk before Serah had looked at the blank page and thought, *how convenient,* instead of *how impossible.*

Except clerks didn't decide these things. Clerks filled in what they were given.

Someone above the clerks had given them a girl with no past, and the wages to keep quiet about it.

Serah sat in the lamplit archive with dust settling on her shoulders and felt the shape of something large and cold moving beneath her career like a fish under ice. Ten years ago—eleven—there had been a famine winter. Everyone remembered the famine winter. The old queen had died in it; the granaries had failed; and there were stories, the kind servants told in whispers, about what desperate families had traded in those months to survive. Stories about bargains made downward. About the mountain.

And into the middle of that winter, a child with no recorded past had been placed, by royal warrant, in the position of tasting the king's food.

*Coincidence* was a word Serah had never trusted. It was the word liars used for patterns.

She pulled the chamberlain's correspondence for that year. Sealed, of course. She picked the seal anyway—it was a simple ward, the kind you put on letters you expected honest people to respect—and read three lines before her hands went cold enough that she had to set the page down.

*...the asset remains unaware. The monthly stipend continues to the designated account. Under no circumstances is she to learn the terms of the original agreement...*

There was more. There was a great deal more, and none of it used the word *soul*, because important documents never used the plain words; but the phrase *designated account* appeared eleven times, and once, chillingly, the phrase *the debt matures*.

Serah copied nothing. She memorized it instead, the way her tutors had trained her—though her tutors had imagined she'd use the trick against enemy spies, not against her own employers—and returned the letter precisely as she'd found it, seal restored so cleanly that only a mage would ever know.

She stood in the dark of the archive for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat, which sounded to her suddenly like a small, loyal dog barking at something much larger in the night.

*The asset remains unaware.*

They hadn't hired a taster. They'd installed one. Fed her, sheltered her, kept her invisible—all these years, while she thought her careful grey life was her own clever work. Every unnoticed day, every successful anonymity, had been arranged, maintained, *purchased.*

Quentin Ravensworth had spent a decade perfecting the art of disappearing, and the crown had spent the same decade holding the doors shut from the outside.

* * *

By law and oath, Serah Vail was required to file her report within three days. By law and oath, the report was to include all findings relevant to the death in the Tanners' Quarter, including any persons suspected of unlicensed magic.

She sat at her desk that night with a blank sheet of official paper, her pen loaded, her oath sitting on her shoulder like a gargoyle.

She wrote:

*Investigation of the death of the unidentified male, Tanners' Quarter, ongoing. Initial traces of residual magic detected at the scene appear consistent with a dying discharge, origin indeterminate. Interviews conducted with kitchen staff and market vendors yielded no actionable intelligence. The subject known as Quentin Ravensworth, royal taster, presents no anomaly beyond longstanding (and licensed) service. Investigation continues along established channels.*

She read it back. Every sentence was technically defensible. Every sentence together was a lie with a spine of truth running through it, which was, she reflected grimly, the finest craftsmanship she'd ever produced—and she had once woven an illusion of the entire palace so convincing that the king had complimented the curtains.

Her hand hovered. This was the moment. Reports could be amended; oaths could be survived; but a first lie was a door, and once opened, it did not close again. Her instructors had warned her about this. Betrayal, they said, was not a single act but a habit that started small—like poison, like immunity, grain by grain, until the thing that would have killed you once slides off you like rain off wax.

She thought of a girl drinking poison by degrees for ten years to protect a king who knew exactly what she was and had never once told her.

Serah signed the report. Then she did one thing more. She took a second sheet, wrote nothing on it at all, folded it into the shape her mother had taught her for carrying secrets—a shapeless little triangle that looked like rubbish—and tucked it into her sleeve, against the skin of her wrist, where her own small dishonesties had always lived.

Because there was one more small thing she hadn't yet examined, and it was the smallest of all: the memory of a girl, twelve years old, wild-haired and brilliant, whom Serah had tutored in cantrips for a single autumn before the girl vanished from the records as thoroughly as if she'd never existed.

A girl whose mother had been arrested that same winter.

A girl whose childhood name Serah had heard spoken once—only once—by a dying man's lips, in a room that smelled of blood and bell-smoke.

*Pip.*

The bells of Corvane rang the midnight hour, and Serah Vail blew out her lamp, and sat in the dark knowing exactly two things for certain.

First: that she was going to find Quentin Ravensworth before the inquisitors did.

Second: that whatever the crown had buried under its mountain, it had buried other things too—and one of them was going to come up swinging.

Somewhere across the city, faint as a candle-gutter, she felt a flicker of magic move—small, careful, unmistakably practiced at not being felt.

Heading, if her senses were true, toward the mountain road.

"Well," Serah said to the empty room, to the unsigned triangle in her sleeve, to ten years of obedient service. "Then we'd best not be late."

She reached for her traveling cloak.
