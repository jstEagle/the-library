## 7. Doss Keeps a Secret

The evidence sat in Doss's palm like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn him.

It was a candle stub. That was the whole of it, or nearly the whole of it—a thumb-length of grey wax with a thread of wick gone soft as an old man's spine, and along one side, where the wax had cooled in layers, a faint shimmer that no honest chandler's work ever left behind. The shimmer was the problem. The shimmer was magic, and magic was outlawed in Corvane, and Doss had spent nineteen years on the city watch learning exactly what happened to people who were found holding it.

He had found it three days ago, wedged into a drain grate two streets from the dead stranger's room. He'd been told to sweep the area for anything "relevant." Relevant meant blood, blades, letters, anything that could be pinned to a killer. Nobody had said *candle stubs*, because nobody else would have known to look at a candle stub and see anything but a candle stub.

Doss knew better. He'd bought one just like it, four years ago, from a woman at the night market who never gave her name and whose hands were quick and warm and careful, like she was handling something alive.

He turned it over once more, then closed his fist around it so fast you'd have thought it had teeth.

"Found something, Doss?"

Sergeant Brell stood in the doorway of the evidence room, a big man built like a door himself, with a face that did all its work in the forehead. He frowned when he frowned; mostly he didn't frown.

"No," Doss said. "Nothing."

"You've been standing there a while."

"Thinking about my supper."

Brell considered this. It was a good answer, as answers went. Everyone in Corvane thought about their supper; it was practically civic duty these days, with the winter coming early and grain prices climbing like they owed somebody money. Brell nodded and moved on down the corridor, boots ringing, and Doss let out a breath he'd been holding since roughly the autumn before last.

He put the candle stub in his boot.

This was, he understood dimly, not the act of an honest watchman. He understood it the way a man understands he's walking off a roof: completely, and long after it could do him any good. But his badge weighed more than usual today, and the wax against his ankle weighed almost nothing at all, and between those two weights a man has to choose which one he can live under.

* * *

He'd bought the candle on the worst night of his life, which was saying something, because Doss had collected worst nights the way other men collected coppers—steadily, without much pride in the collection.

His daughter Liss had died in the fever winter. Not the famine winter everyone talked about now, the one ten years back that emptied whole streets; the smaller one after, the one nobody sang about, when sickness went through the low quarters and took the young ones first. She'd been seven. He'd held her hand through nine nights, and on the tenth morning the hand was just a hand, and he'd walked out of the sick-house into grey daylight feeling like a coat someone had forgotten on a peg.

A month later, drifting through the night market because sleep was a country that wouldn't issue him papers, he'd passed a stall with no lantern and a woman with no name, and she'd sold him a candle that burned grief. That's what she'd said—not promised, said, the way you'd say *this broom sweeps*. "You light it," she told him, "and it burns the heavy part. Not all of it. You keep some. You're meant to."

He'd paid her three coppers, which was robbery, and gone home, and lit it, and sat by it all night expecting nothing.

What happened was small. That was the thing he could never explain to anyone without sounding mad. Nothing lifted, nothing glowed, no voice from the dark saying *she's at peace*. But somewhere around the third hour, the grief changed shape. It stopped being a wall and became a window. He could still see through it exactly what he'd lost—but he could also see, on the other side, the memory of Liss laughing at her own jokes before she got to the punchline, and that memory had been locked away under the weight of how she'd died, and now it wasn't.

He'd gone back to the market six times over the years. Sometimes she was there, sometimes not. They never spoke about anything real. He bought a key that remembered locks, for the nights he came home too tired to remember them himself. He bought a charm against nightmares for a neighbor boy. Each time, the woman looked at him a moment longer than commerce required, and each time he told himself it was suspicion, a seller watching a buyer, and each time he knew he was lying.

Her name, he'd learned by accident, listening to another stall-keeper grumble: Quentin. A taster at the palace, if you could believe it. Poison-proof by trade and quiet by nature. Doss had believed it instantly. Some people are quiet the way deep water is quiet, and he'd always known she was deep water.

Now the deep water was wanted for murder, and here he was with contraband in his boot and a decision he'd already made pretending not to be made.

* * *

The dead man's room had been turned inside out twice by then—once by the watch, once by whoever came after, wearing softer shoes. Serah Vail herself had walked the scene on the first day, the court mage, tall and silver-eyed and polite in the way that makes your skin crawl politely. Doss had watched her stand in the middle of the floor with her eyes half-shut, reading traces out of the air the way other people read weather.

"Magic was done here recently," she'd said to the captain. "Small work. Careful work." And then, after a pause that lasted exactly as long as it needed to: "I'll note the details in my report."

She'd written something in a little book. Doss had seen the book. He had not seen the page.

That was the trouble with mages: you never knew whether the danger was in what they found or in what they chose not to find.

The official story, coming down from above, was simple enough to fit on a poster: a dying foreign agent, possibly a spy of the old border houses, possibly connected to the recent... irregularities... at court. Anyone with information should report it. Anyone harboring persons of interest would share their fate. The word *irregularities* was doing a lot of work, and Doss, who'd been on the watch long enough to know when paperwork was being written by someone frightened, noted that the order had come down unusually fast and unusually high. Inquisitor's seal. The kind of attention Corvane got perhaps once a decade, and never brought anything good with it.

And the person of interest—the poster had no picture, which meant somebody very much didn't want her described—was a food-taster named Quentin Ravensworth who had, as far as Doss could tell in nineteen years of watching this city watch itself, harmed no living soul and improved several hundred breakfasts.

*So,* said the practical voice in his head, the one that sounded like Sergeant Brell being reasonable. *You've got evidence. Turn it in. Let the law do its work.*

*The law,* answered another voice, older and quieter, the one that sounded like a candle burning down in a dark room, *is currently being run by men who put no picture on the poster because they already know exactly whose face goes there.*

Doss had arrested people he liked before. It was part of the job, like rain. He'd arrested Old Tammer for smuggling and shaken his hand at the trial. He'd arrested his own cousin for brawling and bought him a drink after. The law was the law, and Doss kept it the way a farmer keeps a fence—not because fences are holy, but because without them everything wanders off and gets eaten.

But a fence, he reasoned, is meant to keep wolves out. This fence was being used to pen up the sheep for shearing.

* * *

On the fourth night he went to the market.

Not the night market—the day market, the fish stalls and turnip carts, the honest noise of Corvane trying to feed itself before the snows shut the passes. There was a woman there called Maret Osk who sold salt fish and secrets in equal measure, though only one of them smelled bad. She was the closest thing the lower quarters had to a post office for things that couldn't go through a post office.

Doss bought a herring he didn't want.

"Maret."

"Doss." She wrapped the herring with the tenderness of a mother. "You look like a man with a letter in his pocket."

"I look like a man with a fish."

"You look like both, and I'm only charging for one." Her eyes flicked up, sharp as fish bones. "Word is the watch is asking after the taster woman."

"Word travels."

"Word is the only thing in this city that doesn't cost extra." She tied the string with a flourish. "Anything you'd like to send?"

Doss glanced around. Turnip cart. Two children arguing over an apple. Nobody close, nobody looking, everybody in Corvane expertly not looking at everybody else—that was the city's true genius, its founding art.

"There's a message," he said quietly. "For a friend who might be traveling. Tell her: the inquisitor's men start house searches in the low quarters within three days, starting at the river streets and working uphill. Tell her the searches are theater—they're hoping she runs scared and shows herself. And tell her—" he hesitated, because the last part was the dangerous part, the part that made this a gift instead of a duty, "—tell her there's a mage asking questions who isn't writing down all the answers. If that mage finds her first, she might live. If the inquisitor does, she won't."

Maret's hands had gone still on the herring. "That's a lot of message for one fish."

"It's a good fish."

"It's a terrible fish." She tucked the parcel into his hands and, beneath it, pressed something small and folded—a twist of paper. "Recipe," she said loudly, for anyone listening. "For pickling. My grandmother's."

Doss nodded his thanks and walked away without opening it, because whatever was in that paper, Maret had decided it was hers to give and his to carry, and that was how trust worked in the low quarters: silently, badly wrapped, smelling slightly of brine.

At the corner he paused and looked back once. Maret was already serving another customer, face smooth as pond water. Somewhere in that pond, by tomorrow night, his warning would be swimming toward a woman who might or might not still be in the city, who might or might not trust a watchman's word delivered secondhand through a fishwife.

It was all he could do. It felt absurdly little. It felt, at the same time, like the largest thing he'd ever done, larger than any arrest, larger than the medal he'd been given once for pulling children out of a burning tenement and which he kept in a drawer because medals are for other people's memories of you.

* * *

That night, in his rooms above the tannery, Doss took the candle stub out of his boot and set it on his table next to the good candle—the grief candle, burned down to a nub he'd never had the heart to finish, because finishing it felt like saying the grieving was over, and griefs don't end, they just learn to sit quietly in corners.

The stub from the drain was the same make. Same layered wax, same wrong-colored shimmer. Which meant Quentin—or someone using her craft—had been near the dead man's room. Which meant the evidence was true, and he was hiding it anyway, and there was no version of this where he came out clean.

He thought about his oath. Nineteen years ago, a younger Doss with a newer badge had sworn to protect the city of Corvane and uphold its laws, and he'd meant it, every word, the way you mean things before life teaches you that words have weight limits.

*Protect the city.* He looked at the stub. The city wasn't the crown. He'd learned that in the fever winter, when the crown's granaries stayed full and sealed while the low quarters buried their children two to a grave. The city was the turnip cart and the arguing children and Maret's terrible fish and a woman in a dark stall selling three-copper mercy to a broken watchman. The city was the people who were invisible to the people in power, and if protecting them sometimes meant losing a badge—

Well. He'd survived worse losses. He'd survived the loss that taught him what all the others were worth.

There was a knock at the door. Doss went cold from the boots up, slid the stub into the table's hairline crack with a speed born of practice, and opened the door on Sergeant Brell, holding two mugs.

"Brew," Brell said. "You look like a man thinking about his supper again."

"That bad?"

"Worse." Brell handed him a mug and lowered himself onto the stool that groaned in protest, as stools did when Brell sat on them. For a while the sergeant drank in silence, which was unlike him, and Doss waited, which was like him.

Finally Brell said, "They're putting me on the search details. Low quarters, starting in three days." He stared into his mug. "River streets first."

"Is that so."

"And they're giving us lists, Doss. Names. People to pull in for questioning." Brell's forehead did its work. "Half the names on that list are folk I've shared a drink with. Fishwives. Charm-sellers' neighbors. One of them's Maret Osk, and Maret Osk never broke a law in her life except the law of honest pricing." He drained the mug. "You hear anything, in your rounds—anything about where certain persons might have gone—you'll tell me first. Won't you."

It wasn't a question. Or rather, it was a question shaped like a stone thrown across a river, and Doss understood that Brell was standing on the far bank, asking to be told where the stepping stones were—or offering to be one.

"Aye," Doss said carefully. "If I hear anything."

Brell nodded, satisfied, and heaved himself up. At the door he stopped, back turned.

"Liss would've been eleven this winter," he said.

"I know," Doss said. "I count."

"I know you do." The sergeant's voice was rough. "Whatever you're carrying, Doss—carry it careful."

Then he was gone, and Doss stood alone with two empty mugs and a hidden candle stub and the growing, terrifying certainty that half the watch was quietly deciding, one man at a time, which side of the fence the wolves were actually on.

* * *

Before dawn, unable to sleep, Doss did the last foolish thing available to him. He lit the stub.

Just to see, he told himself. Just to confirm the make, for identification purposes, a purely professional exercise conducted in a locked room at four in the morning.

The flame caught low and blue-edged, and the little room filled with a smell like cold air before snowfall and warm bread after mourning, and for one moment—one impossible, gentle moment—Doss heard, clear as a bell over the market noise, a seven-year-old girl laughing at her own joke before she got to the funny part.

The flame guttered out on its own. The stub crumbled to ash, spent, its one small working finished: it had told him what he already knew. That the woman who made such things was worth a badge. Worth twenty.

Doss swept the ash into his palm, carried it to the window, and let the wind take it over the rooftops—out toward wherever Quentin Ravensworth was hiding, a handful of borrowed warmth going ahead of him like a lantern swung in the dark to show her the way.

Three days, he thought, watching the last flecks vanish. Three days until the searches began, until Maret's message reached her or didn't, until the inquisitor's nets came down over the low quarters and every quiet person in Corvane learned what the quiet had cost.

And somewhere uphill, in the direction of the palace, a mage was asking questions and not writing down all the answers—and Doss had a watchman's instinct, honed on nineteen years of small lies, that whatever Serah Vail wasn't writing down was going to matter more than everything she was.

He put on his badge. It hung heavier than ever, and he wore it anyway, because someone had to walk the fence line, and because the wind off the mountain that morning tasted wrong—too cold, too silent, like a held breath—and whatever was coming down from the peaks, Doss intended to be standing between it and the city when it arrived.
