## 8. The Price of Almonds

They came for her on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary.

Quentin had always assumed that if the end of her safe little life arrived, it would come at night, or in winter, or with trumpets—something to mark the occasion. Instead it came at midmorning, while she was arguing with a fishmonger about the freshness of his eels, and it came wearing gray.

The king's inquisitor did not announce himself. He simply appeared at the mouth of Fishmonger's Row the way cold appears under a door: no drama, just a change in the temperature of things. He was a narrow man in dove-colored robes, with a face like a folded letter—everything creased and nothing readable—and two wardens flanked him with the patient stillness of furniture that could kill you.

"Quentin Ravensworth," he said. Not a question. He pronounced each syllable as though weighing them on a small private scale and finding them, on balance, guilty.

Quentin looked up from the eels. Her heart did something complicated behind her ribs—a full orchestra tuning up all at once—but her face, ten years in training, did what faces do best in Corvane. It went pleasantly blank.

"That's me," she said. "If this is about the eels, I want it noted that I raised the freshness concern before anyone got sick."

A flicker crossed the inquisitor's creased face. It might have been amusement. With his kind, it was more likely indigestion of the soul.

"This is not about the eels."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I didn't think it was."

* * *

They did not drag her. That was the first mercy, and Quentin distrusted it immediately, because mercies from the crown were like almonds in a dish someone else had prepared—they looked like gifts and tasted like warnings. The wardens walked on either side of her through streets gone strangely quiet, and she noticed that people were not looking at her. That was the thing about Corvane. Ten years of being forgettable had taught her exactly what it meant when an entire market square decided, all at once and very carefully, to be interested in its own shoes.

They were looking at her by not looking at her. She had never been so visible in her life.

*So this is what it feels like,* she thought, as they crossed into the palace district. *To be seen.* It was awful. Like walking outside without skin.

They took her not to the dungeons—she'd half expected the dungeons, had already composed a small speech about taster's privileges and royal warrants—but to a sunny chamber above the west kitchens, the very room where she'd spent a decade tasting other people's breakfasts. Someone had set out chairs. Someone had set out a table. And on the table, arranged with the loving precision of a man who enjoyed his work, sat three cups.

The inquisitor's name, she learned from the warden who murmured it, was Master Corvel. He settled across from her, folded his hands, and smiled with the corners of his mouth only, which is to say he did not smile at all.

"Do you know why poisoners are so rarely caught, Mistress Ravensworth?"

"I couldn't say, Master Corvel."

"Because poison is polite." He tapped the table beside the middle cup. "It doesn't shout. It arrives as a gift, a remedy, a kindness. It sits down at your table and waits to be welcomed. The perfect crime requires only one thing: that no one look too closely at the person doing the welcoming."

"I taste the king's food," Quentin said. "Looking closely is my entire profession."

"Yes." His eyes were pale, river-stone eyes, and they did not blink enough. "It is. And yet. A woman died in the Tanners' Ward nine days ago. A stranger. A traveler with old scars and older debts, carrying—" he paused, delicately, "—papers. Maps. Correspondence of a magical nature. And when our mages read the traces on the air around that room, do you know what they found?"

Quentin kept her breathing slow. In through the nose, out through the nose, the way she'd taught herself during her first year of tasting, when a nervous stomach nearly ended her career before it began.

"Breadcrumbs?" she offered.

Something moved behind Corvel's eyes again—that same almost-expression. "They found small magic. Quiet magic. The sort of work that leaves almost nothing behind, because whoever made it understood exactly how traces are read." He leaned forward. "Almost nothing, Mistress Ravensworth. Almost. Which means whoever made those charms has been studied. Practiced. A decade of practice, I would guess. Perhaps more."

The room was warm with afternoon light. Quentin felt the cold of it anyway, seeping up through the floor, the way she imagined cold must feel beneath a mountain.

"You're describing a criminal," she said. "I describe soup."

"Mm." Corvel reached for the middle cup. "Let me tell you what I believe, and you tell me where I'm wrong. I believe there is a charm-seller working the night markets of this city. Careful. Kind, even—the charms are gentle ones. Keys that remember. Candles against grief. I believe this seller has operated untouched for years because they are very good at being nobody." He turned the cup slowly on the table. "And I believe that woman in the Tanners' Ward was coming to them. Carrying something that belonged to their family. Something dangerous enough that people are willing to die over it."

"And the cups?" Quentin asked, because her voice wanted to shake and giving it a job kept it steady.

Corvel's smile deepened by a single degree. "Ah. Yes. The cups."

* * *

He explained it the way a man explains a game whose rules he invented that morning.

"One cup is water," he said. "One cup is wine. And one cup is wine with a guest in it—a bitter cousin of the almond, refined by palace alchemists until four drops will stop a strong heart between one beat and the next. You, of course, will drink from one cup. Your choice entirely." He spread his hands, magnanimous as a cat presenting a dead bird. "An innocent woman chooses water. A guilty woman—"

"—chooses whatever you've poisoned," Quentin said, "because you'll claim afterward she knew which one it was."

Corvel looked genuinely delighted, in his folded-letter way. "You see how much simpler life becomes when everyone understands the rules. But here is the part I find interesting." He rose, walked to the window, and stood silhouetted against the bright day with his hands clasped behind him. "Our records show you were hired as a taster eleven years ago. Before that, nothing. No family. No history. A girl who appeared out of thin air with a tongue worth gold and no questions asked—which, in my experience, means someone paid for those questions never to be asked. And do you know who has the power to make questions disappear in this city?"

"The king," Quentin said.

"The king," Corvel agreed. "Or someone who speaks with his voice. So you understand my difficulty. I cannot interrogate you properly, because the answers may have been bought in advance, above my head, by people whose names I am not permitted to know. I cannot search your rooms without a warrant, and the warrant office has gone mysteriously slow whenever your name crosses its desk." He turned back to her, and now his pale eyes were bright with something that was not quite pleasure and not quite hunger but lived in the unhappy neighborhood between them. "But a cup. A cup asks no permission. A cup tells no one what it found. A cup only asks you to drink."

The wardens hadn't moved. The light crawled across the floor. Somewhere below, faintly, the kitchen bells rang for the noon meal, and Quentin thought absurdly of the sous-chef, Tobbin, who burned the leeks every single Tuesday and blamed the stove, and how she would miss even that—would miss the whole shabby, warm, ordinary machinery of a life built carefully on top of a grave.

Her mother's voice surfaced from somewhere far down: *Little Quen. If they ever corner you, remember—the debt is yours, but the choice is always yours.*

She had carried that sentence for ten years without understanding it. Standing in the sunny room with three cups between her and ruin, she understood it completely.

"All right," Quentin said.

Corvel tilted his head.

"I'm thirsty anyway," she said. "It's been a very long morning. Somebody tried to sell me stale eels."

She crossed to the table. She let herself feel all of it—the fear, huge and cold, sitting in her chest like swallowed river water; the strange clarity underneath it, the taster's calm, the part of her that had spent a decade putting unknown things in her mouth and reading them with her whole body. Her immunity was not courage. It was a wall built one careful dose at a time, brick by bitter brick, over years when building anything else had been impossible.

But walls exist to be stood behind when it matters.

She picked up the middle cup.

She saw Corvel register it—the smallest hitch in his stillness, the folded letter twitching open at one corner. He'd expected hesitation. Negotiation. Tears, perhaps, or a confession. He had built his little theater expecting an actress, and instead he had gotten a professional.

"Interesting choice," he said softly.

"It's the red," Quentin said. "Water with lunch is an insult to whoever cooked it."

She drank.

* * *

The poison tasted like marzipan left too long in a hot room—sweet gone wrong, a bitterness underneath like the memory of a lie. She knew it. She'd known its cousins for years, in doses small enough to deny: the time the ambassador's stew had been off; the week the queen's tonic ran sharp; the morning, years ago, when she'd tasted death in the king's breakfast and saved a steward's life by spilling the plate and calling it clumsiness, and no one had ever wondered why a clumsy taster kept her post.

Four drops stopped a strong heart, Corvel had said. The cup held considerably more than four drops.

Quentin set the cup down with a small, deliberate click.

"There," she said. "Delicious. Now, about those eels—"

Master Corvel was staring at her.

Not the way men stare at women. Not the way inquisitors stare at suspects. He was staring at her the way scholars stare at a page that has rewritten itself while they were reading it. His hands had come apart. One of them drifted toward the table and stopped, hovering, as if the wood might also turn out to be a lie.

"That cup," he said, and his voice had lost its polish, gone rough at the edges, "was the wrong cup."

"Was it?" Quentin looked down at it. Empty now. Her heart was beating, slow and steady and entirely her own, and something in her chest that had been clenched for ten years—some fist around some secret—began, joint by joint, to loosen.

"You should be dying," Corvel said. "You should be on the floor. There were twelve drops in that pour. Twelve. A horse wouldn't—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. She watched him do it, watched the inquisitor reassemble himself piece by piece, and understood that she was watching the most dangerous moment of the man's day: the moment the trap caught the wrong animal.

"A horse wouldn't what?" Quentin asked gently. "Finish its wine?"

The silence stretched. One of the wardens shifted. Outside the window, Corvane glittered on in the afternoon, oblivious, roofs and spires and smoke, a city built on bargains nobody talked about.

"You're immune," Corvel said at last. It wasn't a question anymore, and it wasn't an accusation either. It was the sound of a man setting down a heavy bag he'd been carrying and finding his shoulders strangely light and strangely empty at once. "Ten years of tasting. Of course. Of course you're immune. They built you like a lock, and locks don't die of keys." He laughed—one short, unlovely note. "Do you know what this means?"

"That your cups need recalibrating?"

"It means," Corvel said, "that everything I suspected about you is worse than I suspected, and everything I was told about you is a lie, and I am now holding a piece of truth that certain people above my head will go to considerable lengths to bury." He straightened his robes. When he looked at her again, the river-stone eyes had gone flat and careful, and Quentin realized with a lurch that the danger in the room had changed shape entirely. An hour ago, he'd been hunting a charm-seller. Now he was deciding what to do with evidence.

"Twelve drops," he repeated softly. "Walked away. Smiled about it."

"I didn't smile."

"You did, actually. Just at the corner. It's going to haunt me." He gestured, and the wardens opened the door. "Go home, Mistress Ravensworth."

Quentin blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me. Go home. Sleep badly. Count your charms." He turned back to the window, dismissing her with his folded hands and his unreadable back. "I have a report to write. And for the first time in nineteen years of service, I haven't the faintest idea what it's going to say."

She walked out. Her legs held, mostly. The corridor was long and sunlit and full of the smell of Tuesday's leeks burning somewhere far below, and Quentin Ravensworth—who had spent a decade perfecting the art of being passed over, glanced through, forgotten by the time a door closed—walked down it feeling the weight of every eye she'd ever successfully avoided, all of them imaginary, all of them arriving at once.

Invisibility, it turned out, was like a charm. Once broken, it didn't reset.

* * *

She made it three streets before she let herself stop, in the shadow of a chandler's awning, and put a hand flat against the wall and breathe.

Alive. Still alive. Publicly, undeniably, inconveniently alive, in front of the king's own inquisitor, having drunk enough poison to fell a cart horse and commented on the vintage.

Her rule—never be noticed, never be known, stay small enough that the world forgets to look—lay somewhere back in that sunny room in pieces, and the terrible, dizzying thing was that she didn't mourn it. She'd thought she would. She'd built her whole life on that rule the way a snail builds its shell, and she'd assumed that losing it would leave her soft and exposed and doomed.

Instead she felt—light. Frightened out of her mind, hunted, marked, but light. As if the rule itself had been a kind of poison too, dosed daily, tolerated, and she had finally, finally spat it out.

*They'll send someone better than Corvel,* she thought. *Or worse. They'll send Serah Vail.*

The name arrived in her mind with a small, unwelcome warmth attached to it, and Quentin pushed it away and failed. The court mage. The woman whose magic she'd spent ten years learning to hide from, whose traces-read she'd studied secondhand through every cautious charm she'd ever sold. The woman who would be given one order—*find her*—and who had never once in her spotless career failed an order.

Quentin pushed off the wall and pulled her hood up.

There was a map folded into the lining of her coat, drawn by a dying man's hand, showing a road beneath the mountain, and winter was coming early this year—everyone said so, the frost already gnawing at October's edges—and she had perhaps two weeks, maybe less, before snow sealed the pass for a generation.

Two weeks to become, for the first time in her life, unmistakable.

Behind her, at the far end of the street, a bell began to ring—not the kitchen bells, not the watch. The tower bells of the palace, tolling fast and urgent, the sound rolling out over the rooftops like spilled ink. Somewhere in that gray stone hive, doors were opening that had been shut for a very long time.

Quentin turned her collar up against the wind, which smelled, faintly and impossibly, of snow.

Then she walked—quickly, openly, done with hiding—toward the gates.
