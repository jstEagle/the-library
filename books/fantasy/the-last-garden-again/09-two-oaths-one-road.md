## 9. Two Oaths, One Road

The order came at dawn, written in the king's own hand, which meant it had been drafted by someone else and signed by a man who never read past the first line.

*Serah Vail, Mage of the Second Circle. You are commanded to pursue and apprehend the fugitive Quentin Ravensworth, formerly of the royal kitchens, suspected of unlawful craft and complicity in the death of one Merrin Hale. Use whatever means necessary. Report to Inquisitor Corvess upon completion.*

Corvess's name was added in different ink, later, in a hand like a spider drowning in its own web.

Serah read the order three times in her tower room while the city below woke up smelling of coal smoke and bread. *Use whatever means necessary.* She had served the crown for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned that "whatever means necessary" was a door that only ever opened outward. Once you stepped through it, you did not come back the same width.

She thought about lying to herself for a moment—the way she'd lied in her report, the neat little fiction that the traces of magic at Merrin Hale's death were "inconclusive." It hadn't even been a good lie. Serah had always been a mediocre liar, which was a professional embarrassment for a court mage and, she suspected now, the single reason she still had anything resembling a conscience.

Because here was the truth she could not file away: she knew those traces. Knew them the way you know a childhood song heard once from an open window. A small, warm, careful magic—magic that folded itself up small so as not to trouble anyone. She had felt that signature before, twenty years ago, in a kitchen in the Low Quarter, guiding the hands of a frightened girl who was trying to make bread rise without paying for it.

Quentin Ravensworth. The taster. The invisible woman.

Serah set the order down and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars.

"You're hunting a girl you tutored," she said aloud to the empty room. "That's the assignment. That's what eleven years of loyal service buys you."

The empty room declined to answer, which was more mercy than the king's justice would offer.

* * *

She went out through the Ashgate at midmorning with a travel cloak, a satchel of charms she officially did not believe in, and a horse named Barley who was too old for this work and knew it. The road north ran between hedgerows going brown at the edges, and the air had that wrongness in it again—that hush, that held-breath quality the weather had worn for two weeks now, like a house where something terrible has just happened upstairs and everyone downstairs is pretending not to hear.

First snows coming early. Everyone said so. The farmers said it the way people say things they hope will scare the future into behaving.

Serah rode north because the map fragments recovered from Merrin Hale's effects pointed north. Toward the mountain. Toward the pass that would seal itself when winter came, sealing with it—she was increasingly certain—whatever truth the crown had buried under Corvane along with all those quiet names in quiet ledgers.

She also rode north because Quentin Ravensworth would ride north, and Serah wanted very much to reach her before Corvess's men did. This was not, strictly speaking, part of her orders. This was the part Serah was choosing not to examine too closely, the way one does not examine a floor that might be rotten, in case examination requires doing something about it.

By late afternoon she reached the crossroads shrine at Wether's Bend—a leaning stone figure of some harvest god whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of farmers' thumbs—and there she found the first sign: hoofprints, one set, shod, moving north. And beside the shrine, tucked into a crack in the stone as neatly as if placed by a shopkeeper's hand, a charm.

A candle stub, wax gone grey, wrapped in a scrap of linen. Serah picked it up and felt it hum faintly against her palm. Grief-work. Old grief, tended often. The kind of charm that wasn't sold so much as surrendered.

"Well," Serah said to the worn stone face. "You keep leaving pieces of yourself on the road, Miss Ravensworth. Anyone could find them."

She pocketed the candle. It seemed rude to leave it.

* * *

She caught sight of her quarry at dusk, and the catching sight nearly undid her.

Not because Quentin looked dangerous. Because she looked *small*. A slight figure leading a tired horse along a field path, hood up, shoulders drawn in like a letter folded for hiding. The most wanted woman in Corvane, walking like someone trying to take up less space than she already occupied.

Serah reined Barley off the road and cut across the fallow field, and Quentin heard the hoofbeats—of course she did, she'd survived ten years by hearing everything—and turned, and for one long moment they simply looked at each other across forty feet of stubble and dying light.

Then Quentin ran.

It was, Serah would reflect later, deeply unfair how fast she was. The woman bolted toward a drainage ditch like a hare with a decade of practice at being chased, and Serah swore, dismounted, and did the one thing she'd sworn she wouldn't do.

She cast a holding ward.

Light snapped across the field like a net made of dawn, and Quentin hit it mid-stride and bounced—not violently; Serah had built it gentle, or told herself she had—and sat down hard in the stubble, breathing like a bellows.

"Don't," Quentin gasped. "Don't. I'll come quietly. I'll—"

"I'm not arresting you."

Quentin stopped breathing entirely, which Serah supposed was one solution to being caught.

"That's a trick," Quentin managed. "That's a very elaborate trick, and I'm not falling for it. You're Serah Vail. Second Circle. Your warrants have my description on them—I've seen them in the market, they got my nose wrong but they got the scar right."

"They did get your nose wrong," Serah agreed. She crouched down a few feet away, keeping her hands visible, palms open—an old habit from dealing with frightened hedge-witches, though she suspected Quentin was neither frightened nor, precisely, a hedge-witch. "Your nose is better than the drawing. The drawing makes you look stern."

"I *am* stern."

"You're sitting in a mud puddle."

Quentin looked down at herself, at the puddle spreading beneath her, and something in her face cracked—not into tears, but into a kind of exhausted, furious laugh. "Ten years," she said. "Ten years I was nobody. One week."

"One week," Serah agreed. "And now you're sitting in a puddle talking to the woman sent to drag you back to Corvess, who wears rings that make grown men confess to crimes they only dreamed of committing."

Silence. The wrong hush pressed down over the field. Somewhere far off, a crow made a sound like a question no one wanted asked.

"Why aren't you arresting me?" Quentin asked finally. No performance in it. Just the raw arithmetic of a woman who needed the numbers to add up.

Serah considered several answers. *Because it's my duty to bring you in alive and unharmed* was true but insufficient. *Because you saved a steward's life last spring and let the credit go to a faulty hinge* was true but revealing—revealing of how much she knew, which was already too much.

So she told the truth, or the truest slice of it.

"Because I read the dead man's map," she said. "Because I know what your mother's signature looks like in spellwork, because I tutored you when you were nine years old and you were so afraid of taking up space that you apologized to furniture. And because—" her voice caught on the next part, and she let it, deliberately, because she was done filing things away— "because I've been reading the old records, Quentin. The famine winter records. The ones the crown keeps behind three locks and a lie. And either I'm chasing the last loose end of a bargain the kingdom made in secret, or I'm chasing nothing at all, and I need to know which. And you—" she nodded at Quentin's pocket, where the shape of the folded map showed plain as a bruise, "—are carrying the other half of the answer."

Quentin stared at her. Then, slowly, warily, she drew the map out and held it—but didn't open it. Held it the way you hold a knife: available, not offered.

"The Undercourt," Quentin said. "You know that word."

"I know the crown flinches when it's spoken near the ledgers."

"My mother sold me to them. Before I was born. My soul—it's theirs. Collateral." She said it flatly, the way people recite things they've rehearsed until the horror wore smooth. "There's a way beneath the mountain to reclaim it. The map shows how. And the mountain closes for the winter soon, and after that it closes for a generation, and I am standing in a puddle discussing noses with my own arrest warrant."

Something loosened in Serah's chest—something she'd been carrying clenched since the morning the stranger died. Not relief exactly. Recognition. Two people standing on opposite sides of the same locked door, each holding half a key.

"Here is what I propose," Serah said. "A devil's alliance, since we're both damned either way. You help me learn what the crown buried—every name in those ledgers, every soul fed to that mountain to keep our winters short. I get you to the pass before it seals. My rank opens roads yours can't. Your—" she hesitated, then chose honesty— "your particular talents open doors mine can't. Nobody notices you, Quentin. That's not a flaw anymore. That's the finest infiltration tool I've ever seen."

"And when we find the truth?" Quentin's eyes were narrow, bright, unblinking. "What then? Truth doesn't feed courts. Truth doesn't keep winters short. What do you do with it, Serah Vail, once you're holding it?"

Serah looked north, toward the mountain, where the first real stars were coming out over a sky the color of a closed fist.

"I burn it into the record," she said quietly. "All of it. Even the parts that hang me. An oath means nothing if it only protects the comfortable."

Long silence. The puddle seeped coldly through Quentin's trousers, and Serah watched her weigh it—the warrant, the walls, the ten years of invisibility against one impossible chance at wholeness. Watched her decide the way careful people decide: slowly, completely, irreversibly.

"If you betray me," Quentin said, "I will make sure every charm I ever sold comes home to roost on you specifically. The key that remembers locks. The candle that burns grief. I have customers in high places, Vail, and none of them use their real names, and all of them owe me."

"Noted," Serah said solemnly. "You'll haunt me with excellent craftsmanship. I'm terrified."

"You should be. My work is subtle." Quentin took the offered hand and let Serah pull her out of the puddle, and her grip was stronger than her size suggested, and dry, and warm, and did not let go a moment sooner than necessary.

They walked back to Barley together, enemies no longer, allies not yet—something new being built in the wet stubble, plank by suspicious plank.

"Ride ahead," Serah said, checking the darkening sky. "Take the horse. There's a drover's inn at Hollowmere, two days north. I'll follow on foot and meet you there—I'm expected on the south road, and it'll buy us a day before Corvess realizes his hound is running with the fox."

Quentin paused, one foot in the stirrup, and gave her a long look.

"Hollowmere," she repeated. "The haunted one."

"You've heard of it?"

"Everyone's heard of it. They say the keeper died years ago and never noticed." Quentin swung up into the saddle, and for the first time since the stranger bled out in her rooms, she almost smiled. "Fine. Hollowmere. But Vail—if you're not there by the second dusk, I'm going without you, and I'm telling every ghost in that inn exactly what the Second Circle mage did to deserve her haunting."

"Duly threatened," Serah said, and meant, in the private language of two women learning to trust each other against all evidence: *hurry, be safe, don't die.*

She watched the horse and its rider shrink into the northern dark, then turned south toward her own lies and duties, and above her the early snow began to fall—too cold, too silent, each flake landing without a sound, as if the whole world were holding its breath to see what came out of the mountain.
