## 10. The Long Winter Begins

The snow came three weeks early, and it came wrong.

Quentin knew snow. She had grown up in the shadow of Corvane's walls, had shoveled it, cursed it, caught it on her tongue as a girl when her mother wasn't looking. Snow was supposed to whisper. It was supposed to hush the streets and soften the rooftops and make the whole city hold its breath in a way that felt almost kind.

This snow said nothing at all.

It fell straight down, flake after flake after identical flake, like ash from a fire that hadn't finished burning. And it landed without sound—no soft tick on her hood, no whisper of wind through the pass. The world had simply been switched off, and the flakes kept falling into the silence they'd made.

"Say something," Serah muttered beside her, and Quentin realized she'd been staring up with her mouth open like a fool catching nothing on her tongue.

"I was going to say it's cold."

"You were going to say something worse. I could see you winding up for it."

Quentin snapped her mouth shut. Three days out of Corvane, sleeping in ditches and barns, sharing a single blanket by necessity and arguing about it by preference, and she still couldn't tell whether Serah Vail liked her or merely tolerated her presence the way one tolerates a stone in a boot that has, over time, become oddly necessary.

"It's wrong, is what I was going to say," Quentin said. "Snow makes noise. This doesn't."

Serah held out a bare hand and let a flake land on her palm. It didn't melt. That was the other wrong thing—the air should have been barely cold enough for snow, but each flake sat there whole and patient, like it could wait all day. Like it had all the time in the world, which, Quentin thought, was exactly the problem. Things below the mountain had nothing but time, and lately it seemed to be leaking upward along with everything else.

"That's not weather," Serah said quietly. "That's attention."

"Don't say things like that."

"You asked."

"I asked you to say something comforting. You've failed completely. I want a refund on this alliance."

A ghost of a smile crossed Serah's face—there and gone, like most of her expressions—and she pulled her hood forward. "Come on. If we make Hollowmere by nightfall, we sleep under a roof. If we don't, we sleep in this." She gestured at the silent, falling white. "And I've slept in worse, but I'd rather not find out if this counts."

They walked. Behind them, somewhere far back along the road, Quentin imagined she could hear the bells of Corvane, though that was impossible at this distance. She heard them anyway. In her head, the watch bells had been ringing since the night a stranger bled out on her floor, and she suspected they would ring until this was finished—one way or the other.

* * *

The village of Brackenlow had been a village in autumn.

Now it was a sketch of one. Doors stood open. A well bucket swayed on its rope, creaking in a wind Quentin couldn't feel. Laundry hung frozen stiff on lines, shirts and sheets holding their shapes like ghosts of the people who'd worn them. No smoke rose from any chimney. No dog barked. Even the crows were gone, and crows, in Quentin's experience, would attend anything.

"They left in a hurry," Serah said, moving down the lane with her hand near the knife at her belt—not her mage's staff; the knife. Quentin had noticed that about her. When Serah was frightened, she reached for the ordinary thing.

"People hurry when the king's men come," Quentin said. "Or when plague comes. Or—"

"When their charms stop working."

That stopped Quentin mid-step.

She'd been carrying her own small stock, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs: three luck-knots, a key that remembered locks, half a candle that burned grief. Traveling light. Traveling honest, or as close to honest as she'd ever come. She unwrapped the bundle now, fingers clumsy with cold, and took out the key.

It had always been warm. Keys that remember locks carry a little of the door's welcome in them—that was the whole trick, the whole gentle lie she'd sold for years. She cupped it in both palms and waited.

It sat in her hands like any key. Cold. Dumb. Just metal.

"No," she said, before she could decide whether she meant it.

Serah watched her, and didn't gloat, and somehow that was worse than gloating. "It started at the border villages two days ago," she said. "I felt it in my own work. Small spells going thin. Like trying to sing in a room where all the air's been let out." She flexed her fingers, and for a moment a pale light gathered at her fingertips—her old reliable cantrip, the first one every court mage learns, the one that lights a candle across a dark room. It sputtered. Flickered. Died like a snuffed wick.

Serah stared at her own hand as if it had betrayed her personally.

"How far does it go?" Quentin asked. "The thinning."

"The Steward pulls warmth toward the mountain. All of it. Every candle, every hearth, every charm, every—" Serah hesitated on the word, and Quentin supplied it silently: every soul. "The bargain fed him a little at a time, for centuries. Enough to keep the winters short and the court below alive. But the ledger burned, Quentin. The arrangement's broken, and now he's reaching for whatever's nearest, the way a drowning man reaches for anything."

"And we're walking straight toward him."

"We're walking straight toward the only place this can be ended."

"Those are different sentences, Serah."

"Yes," Serah said. "They are." She started walking again, boots crunching that soundless snow, because even their footsteps seemed muffled here, swallowed by the white. "Keep your charms wrapped. Whatever strength they have left, don't spend it on comfort."

Quentin looked back once, at the frozen laundry and the swaying bucket and the empty windows that reflected nothing but gray sky. Then she followed.

* * *

They found one person left in Brackenlow, and she was ninety years old and entirely unimpressed by them.

She was sitting in her kitchen by a fire that should not have been burning—every other hearth in the village was dead, but hers glowed steady and orange, defying the general surrender of the world—and she was shelling beans into a bowl as if it were an ordinary Tuesday in an ordinary year.

"Door was open," she said, without looking up. "Either sit down or shut it. Heat's expensive these days."

Quentin shut the door.

The old woman's name was Maren, and she explained the village's departure with the brisk contempt of someone describing a fad she refused to participate in. "Priest came through, then the reeve, then everybody with a cart. Said the mountain's angry. Said the winter's a punishment. Said get to Corvane, the crown will shelter the faithful." She snorted. "Crown never sheltered anybody but itself. I buried a husband and two sons in winters just like this one. This one's colder, mind. But it's not new. It's just new to them."

"You stayed," Serah said. Not a question. Serah had a way of asking questions that weren't questions, and Maren clearly recognized the technique, because she gave her a look that could have curdled cream.

"I stayed because my fire stays lit," Maren said, "and I'll thank you not to ask why, because I only know half the answer myself, and the half I know I don't like."

But Quentin was already looking at the fire, and feeling something she hadn't felt since childhood—a low, warm hum behind her breastbone, faint as a memory of music. Her half-soul. The half she carried. It knew that fire. It leaned toward that fire.

And the fire, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the season, leaned back.

"Maren," she said carefully. "Where did your fire come from?"

"My mother bought it," the old woman said, shelling beans. "Before I was born. From a woman who owed a debt to somebody under the hill, and paid it strange. Paid it in warmth. My mother got a hearth that never goes cold, and the woman who sold it got—" Maren shrugged. "Gone. Taken, some said. Chosen, others said. Depends who was telling it, and how much wine they'd had."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "What was the woman's name?"

"Ravensworth." Maren looked up for the first time, and her eyes, watery and ancient, settled on Quentin with terrible mildness. "Same as yours, by the look of you. Sit down, girl. You've got her chin. Eat something. You're no good to anybody starving, and the road ahead eats people faster than winter does."

* * *

They ate stew. It was good stew, and Quentin resented how much better it made everything feel.

Maren talked while they ate, in the unhurried way of the very old, laying out the past like cards. Quentin's grandmother—her mother's mother—had made a side-bargain generations back, a trickle of warmth flowing downward, a hearth-charm that was really a leash. Small payments, forever. The crown's great bargain with the Undercourt, the one the inquisitors guarded, was a river. Her family's had been a thread. But threads, Maren observed, were sometimes the only thing holding a garment together.

"When your ledger burned," Maren said, nodding at Quentin, "the big debts broke loose. But the little ones didn't break. Little ones just got hungry." She pointed her bean-laden spoon at the fire. "That's why mine still burns. Somebody down there remembers owing my family, and pays up out of habit, or fondness, or spite—I couldn't say which. Point is, the court below isn't all teeth. Some of it's just lonely. Lonely things keep paying on old debts because the debt is the only friendship they've got."

Serah set down her spoon. "You know a remarkable amount for a bean-sheller."

"You know remarkably little for a court mage," Maren returned pleasantly, "considering you're sitting in my kitchen eating my beans. We're all educated differently."

Quentin laughed—actually laughed, a short surprised sound—and felt Serah glance at her with something that might have been surprise of her own. It occurred to Quentin, distantly, that she couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed in company. Ten years of being forgettable did not produce many laughs.

"Why are you telling us this?" she asked.

"Because you asked the right question at the fire. 'Where did your fire come from.' Most folk would've asked 'how,' or 'can I have one.'" Maren resumed shelling. "You asked where. That's a soul-taster's question. Where a thing comes from. Your mother asked like that too, at the end." Her hands went still, just for a moment. "Go on to Hollowmere. There's a keeper there who remembers more than I do—he's had longer to remember it, being dead—but he'll trade truth for news, so bring some. And girl." She looked up again. "When the cold gets bad—and it will get bad—you put your hand flat on the ground and you say your own name. Full name. The one nobody uses. It won't fix anything. But the half of you that's already down there will hear it, and knowing where you are helps it help you. Two halves pull together better than one."

Quentin's throat was tight. "Is that how it works? Really?"

"No idea," Maren said cheerfully. "But my mother swore by it, and my mother was rarely wrong about anything except men. Now eat your stew."

* * *

They left Brackenlow at first gray light, and the world had gotten worse overnight in the specific way of nightmares: not dramatically, but persistently.

The snow had stopped falling and started hanging. Flakes drifted in place, unmoving, like the sky had forgotten to finish the thought. The road beneath was ice-glassed, and the trees along it stood black and bare and utterly still—no wind, no birdsong, no creak of branch. Sound had withdrawn from the world like a tide going out, and what remained was a silence so complete Quentin could hear her own heartbeat, and Serah's beside it, slightly faster.

"Your heart's quick this morning," Quentin said, mostly to break the silence, partly because it was true and interesting.

"I'm frightened," Serah said, without embarrassment. "You should be too. Fear sharpens. It's comfort that dulls."

"I'm plenty frightened. I'm also hungry, cold, and thinking about that stew."

"Grieving the stew already?"

"Planning my return to it. There's a difference."

Serah made that almost-smile again. Quentin was starting a private catalog of those smiles. Entry four, she noted: provoked by complaints about stew.

By midday the charms died completely. Quentin checked her bundle at a rest-stop and found the luck-knots gone slack, threads that had once hummed faintly now merely string. The grief-candle's wax had gone gray and hard as river stone. Only the key remained, and it had changed—it wasn't warm, but it wasn't cold either. It pulsed. Slowly. Once every few breaths. Like something sleeping, or listening.

Like half a heart keeping time with the other half, miles below and pulling.

"Quentin." Serah had gone rigid, staring east, back the way they'd come. "Look."

Quentin looked. On the horizon behind them, above the distant smudge of Corvane, the sky had turned the color of a bruise—not storm-colored, nothing so honest. It was the color of lamplight seen through too much darkness, a vast dim gold stain spreading across the underside of the clouds, and it was spreading toward them, slow as spilled honey.

"What is that?" Quentin whispered.

"Warmth," Serah said. "Being taken." She swallowed. "All of it. Every hearth between here and the sea, maybe. He's not waiting anymore. The ledger burned, the crown's coming for us, and the Steward below is done rationing. He's drawing in everything he can reach before—" She stopped.

"Before what?"

"Before the mountain seals. Before winter closes the pass for a generation. He's stocking the pantry." Serah turned to face the road ahead, toward the peaks that had begun to show themselves through the hanging snow, white-on-white, immense and indifferent. "And we have maybe two days to reach the Pass of Teeth before he finishes the meal."

Behind them, the golden stain crept closer. Ahead, the mountains waited, patient as debt.

Quentin knelt, pressed her bare palm flat against the frozen road, and said her full name into the silence—the long one, the childhood one, the one no living person should have known and yet everyone, lately, seemed to.

Far beneath her feet, faint but unmistakable, something answered.

Not words. Warmth. A pulse, matching the key in her pack, beat for beat.

And then, rolling down from the high pass like the first note of an avalanche, a sound the world had lost and suddenly, terribly, found again:

Bells. Watch bells. Coming fast along the road behind them.

The inquisitor had reached the mountains.
