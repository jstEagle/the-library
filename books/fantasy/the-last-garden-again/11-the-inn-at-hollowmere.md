## 11. The Inn at Hollowmere

The inn appeared out of the snow the way a lie appears in an honest face—suddenly, and with the distinct feeling that it had been there all along, waiting for you to need it.

Quentin saw it first as a smudge of amber light between the pines, then as a roofline sagging under two feet of snow like a tired shoulder, and then, blessedly, as a door. Above that door hung a sign whose paint had surrendered to the weather generations ago, though if she squinted she could make out the ghost of a horse and, beneath it, letters that might once have read *The Hollow Mare* or *The Hollowmere* or possibly *Holl W Mere*, depending on how committed the original painter had been to spelling.

"It's not on the map," Serah said behind her. She said everything the way she did everything: precisely, and slightly too loudly for the quiet of the place.

"Neither is the blizzard," Quentin said. "And yet here we are, inside one."

"I don't trust buildings that appear."

"Then stay outside and distrust it from the cold. I'm going in." Quentin was past arguing. Three days on the mountain road had taught her things about her own body she'd never wanted to know—how many toes a person actually needed, for instance, and what frost did to the fine hairs inside your nose. Her half-soul, if that was what she carried now, sat strangely in her chest these days, like a coin worn smooth by someone else's thumb. It had kept her warmer than it should have. That frightened her more than the cold did.

She pushed the door open. It swung without a sound, which was wrong; every inn door in Corvane announced arrivals like a town crier with opinions. This one simply let them in, the way a held breath lets go.

Inside: a common room, low-beamed and firelit, smelling of woodsmoke and old bread and something faintly floral, like a garden remembered rather than grown. A fire burned in a hearth large enough to roast an ox. No ox was roasting. In fact—

"No one's here," Serah said, hand already glowing faintly at her cuff, because of course she'd brought magic to a haunted house the way other people brought umbrellas to a picnic.

"There's always someone at an inn," Quentin said. "Even when there isn't."

As if summoned by the sheer accuracy of this, a figure emerged from behind the bar. He was tall and thin and dressed in the style of forty years ago, waistcoat buttoned with the fussy precision of a man who had never once been interrupted mid-buttoning. His skin had the gray-gold tint of candlelight on old paper. When he moved, he didn't quite displace the air, the way smoke doesn't.

"Travelers!" he said, and his voice was warm as the hearth and about as solid. "Welcome to the Hollowmere. Rooms are six coppers, dinner is included, and I must ask you not to feed anything to the cat."

"Do you have a cat?" Quentin asked.

"We have the *idea* of a cat," he said gravely. "It comes and goes. Mostly it goes."

* * *

His name was Master Ellery Voss, keeper of the Hollowmere, dead these sixty-one years, and he made the best stew Quentin had ever eaten, which raised certain philosophical questions neither of them felt equipped to address. He set bowls before them with hands that passed through the table only occasionally, "when my mind wanders," he apologized, "the body follows eventually. You'll get used to it. Most guests do. The ones who don't tend to be the ones who leave reviews."

Serah ate nothing at first. She circled the room instead, trailing fingers along doorframes, murmuring the small diagnostic charms of her order—the ones for reading what a place remembers. Quentin watched her work and tried not to find it endearing. She was failing at it. She'd been failing at it since the pass, where Serah had hauled her out of a frozen river by the collar while reciting, of all things, the emergency protocols for hypothermic civilians, as though affection were just another form of procedure.

"The hearthstone is older than the building," Serah reported finally, sitting down. "The whole inn is... anchored to it. And the ghost is anchored to the inn. He can't leave?"

"Not even a little," Master Voss said cheerfully, refilling their cups with wine that was somehow real, or real enough. "Sixty-one years. I've counted every one, which is the sort of hobby you develop when you can't develop others." He smiled at them both, and the smile was genuinely kind, and something about it snagged on Quentin like a fishhook.

"You died keeping the inn," she said. Not asking. Tasters learn to read people the way they read food—one look, one scent, and you know what's in them.

"Keeping the inn, keeping the road, keeping the light on so travelers wouldn't walk into the ravine." He shrugged, a motion like leaves settling. "There was a storm. There was a family at the door. There was only one blanket left and the cellar was warm, and—" his smile flickered, just slightly, "—well. Hospitality has its hazards."

"And no one buried you properly," Serah said quietly. "No rites. So you stayed."

"The crown's clerks came through the following spring," Voss said. "Made a note. Moved along. The mountain keeps what the mountain isn't given back." He said it lightly, but Quentin heard the thing underneath it, the way she'd once heard arsenic under almond. Sixty-one years of pouring wine for guests who mostly screamed when they noticed him.

"I'm sorry," she said.

He looked at her then—really looked—and his eyes went strange. Not hostile. *Recognizing.*

"Well," he said softly. "Now there's a warmth I know."

* * *

Later, when Serah had gone up to check the rooms—"checking for structural integrity," she claimed, in the tone of a woman checking whether the beds were haunted, they were not, Voss assured her twice—the old ghost sat across from Quentin by the fire and studied her the way scholars study difficult texts.

"You've been below," he said. "Or you're going. Or—" he tilted his head, candle-gray and curious, "—you're *split*. Half-and-half. Like a loaf cut before baking. How did that happen? They don't usually let people do it halfway. It's against several rules I'm fairly sure were invented specifically to prevent it."

Quentin wrapped her hands around her cup. The wine was warming her more than wine should. Or perhaps that was him; ghosts leaked warmth the way they leaked through tables.

"My mother sold me," she said. "Before I was born. To the Undercourt. I'm going down to take it back—or half of it back. I already took the half." She laughed, short and unamused. "I don't even know what I took. It doesn't feel like anything. It feels like *less than nothing*, some nights."

"May I?" Voss asked, and when she nodded, he reached across—not touching, hovering his hand near hers, near the cup, near the space where a soul sits in a body like a tenant who pays no rent. His expression changed. Quentin had seen pity before, on faces, and had hated it. This wasn't quite pity. It was closer to grief wearing pity's coat.

"Oh, child," he said. "You think they *took* it. Everyone above thinks that. Eat, devour, consume—that's the story the crown tells, and it tells it because the truth would break something the crown needs whole."

"The truth being?"

"The Undercourt doesn't eat souls." He leaned back. The firelight moved through him instead of onto him. "It *keeps* them. That's the entire purpose. That's why it exists. Souls are warm, Quentin Ravensworth. Warmth is the only currency down there, and the court is—was—an account of it. Every bargain your mother's generation made, every bargain the crown has brokered for three hundred years, all of it flows downhill to the same vault. And do you know what the vault does with it?"

"Warms itself," Quentin said slowly.

"Keeps *itself* warm. Keeps *them* warm." He gestured vaguely downward, and downward meant further than the cellar. "The bargained souls don't suffer. They're not tormented. They're *held*—the way you'd hold a coal in a dying fire. Cupped. Sheltered. The Pale Steward tends them like a gardener tends seedlings through winter. Because the alternative—" his voice dropped, and the fire dimmed as if leaning in to listen, "—the alternative is that they go cold. And a cold soul doesn't die, exactly. It just stops. Stops remembering, stops wanting, stops *being anyone*. The court stands between the dead and that. Has stood there since before the mountain had a name."

Quentin thought of her mother's confession, the ledger, the words she'd read by lamplight while the Steward watched with his patient, bottomless courtesy. *Protection from a worse creditor.* She had understood it as debt. She was beginning to understand it as insulation.

"But you said it's dying," she said.

Voss's smile was terrible and tender. "Loneliness," he said, "is a slow cold too. The court was built to be full—to be a great lamplit city of the kept and the keeping, voices in the fields, songs in the lamp-rows. And the crown above has been thinning the deliveries for a century. Cheaper winters, fewer souls sent down, more hoarded up top for engines and enchantments and the king's long life." He spread his hands. "A hearth built for a hundred fires, fed one stick a year. The Steward holds what remains, and holds it well, and grows colder holding it, because warmth shared is warmth doubled and warmth *hoarded*—" he tapped his own gray chest, "—is warmth alone. I would know. I've been alone sixty-one years in the warmest room in the mountains. Do you understand what I'm telling you, taster?"

Quentin did. Her chest ached with understanding, with the sudden image of the Undercourt she'd walked through—all those lamps, all those patient figures in the lamplit fields, the Steward greeting her like a guest long expected, because she was. Because she was *warmth arriving*, and he had been so hungry for company he'd mistaken hunger for hospitality.

"It's not a monster," she said. "It's a lonely old thing keeping everyone else's family warm because no one keeps its own."

"And you," Voss said gently, "left half yourself with it. Whether you meant to or not. Which makes you the first person in three centuries to give the court warmth *freely*." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what that half of you is doing right now, down there in the dark? It's sitting by the fire. It's talking. It's *keeping the Steward company*. And the Steward—" his voice caught, a sound like a page turning, "—the Steward hasn't had a conversation that wasn't a transaction since before your grandmother was born."

Quentin stared into the fire. Somewhere far below, through stone and snow and the roots of the world, half of her was warming her hands at a hearth the size of a cavern, and something vast and ancient and terribly polite was sitting beside her, afraid to speak first.

Horror should have been the feeling. She searched for it and found it gone soft, gone over like fruit left too long, until what remained was something that hurt worse: pity.

"I came to take it back," she whispered.

"Mm," said Voss. "And now?"

"Now I don't know what I want." She laughed, wet-eyed. "That's new. I've known exactly what I wanted for ten years. Be invisible. Be safe. Be owed nothing. And now I'm standing in a haunted inn learning that my stolen soul is somebody's only friend, and my mother sold me to a *hearth*, and the crown that fed me has been starving it."

"That's the trouble with truths," Voss said comfortably. "They start as knives and end as seeds. Give them time. Plant them properly." He rose—a motion like steam rising—and began gathering bowls that did not need gathering, because tidying was apparently the last habit death had failed to take from him. Then he paused.

"One more thing, and I'll say it plainly because you strike me as a plain-foods woman. The Steward will offer you whatever you ask. That's not generosity; that's *hunger*. Lonely things overgive. Whatever he offers you down there—think hard on what it costs *him*, not what it costs you." His eyes drifted toward the stairs, toward where Serah had gone, and something knowing and sad crossed his paper-gray face. "And think hard on what your mage is carrying. Guilt is a heavy thing to haul up a mountain. People set it down at the worst possible moments."

"What do you mean?"

But he only smiled, poured himself a cup of wine he couldn't taste, and toasted the fire.

Outside, the wind turned. Quentin felt it before she heard it—a pressure change, a wrongness in the way the snow hissed against the shutters, the same wrongness she'd felt in Corvane the day the bells rang for a stranger bleeding on her floor.

Master Voss set down his cup. His candle-gray face had gone very still, and very old, and for the first time since they'd arrived, the warmest inn in the mountains felt cold.

"Ah," he said quietly. "That will be the inquisitor. He travels fast when the crown is frightened. And the crown, Miss Ravensworth, is *very* frightened of you."

The fire guttered. Downstairs, in the cellar where a dead man had once saved a family with the last warm blanket, something knocked—three slow knocks, patient as winter—from a door that led nowhere at all.
