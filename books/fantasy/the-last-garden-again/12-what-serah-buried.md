## 12. What Serah Buried

The inn at Hollowmere had exactly one hearth worth the name, and it was in the common room, and the common room was occupied by a ghost who insisted on rearranging the furniture every night at dusk. By the time Serah came downstairs, three chairs had migrated to the corners as if in disgrace, and Quentin had dragged one back to the fire and dared anyone—living or otherwise—to object.

"Sit," Quentin said, without looking up. She was feeding strips of dried apple to the fire as though it were a small animal she'd agreed to care for out of pity. "The floor's cold and the ghost has opinions about where we stand."

"The ghost has opinions about everything," Serah said. "It moved my boots."

"It moved your boots *closer to the fire*. I'd call that hospitality."

Serah sat. The chair creaked under her in a way that felt personal. Outside, the snow came down in that wrong, deliberate silence that had followed them for six days now—not falling so much as *arriving*, each flake taking its time, as if the sky were being careful. The wind that should have rattled the shutters didn't. Hollowmere sat in a pocket of held breath, and everyone in it knew.

The innkeeper—or what passed for one, a gray woman named Maren who flickered slightly when she thought no one was looking—had left them a pot of barley soup, half a loaf, and a bottle of plum wine that tasted faintly of candle smoke. Quentin poured two cups without asking whether Serah wanted one. That was the thing about traveling with Quentin Ravensworth: she never asked. She simply decided you were included and let you discover it afterward, like weather.

"You're doing the thing," Quentin said.

"What thing?"

"The face. The mage face. Where you look at the fire like it owes you an explanation."

Serah looked at the fire. It did, frankly, owe several explanations, but that wasn't the point. "I don't have a face for it."

"You have four faces. The court face, which is marble. The working face, which is marble with eyebrows. The tired face, which is marble after a long winter. And this one." Quentin pointed the ladle at her. "This one means you've been composing a sentence since noon and haven't found the courage to say it out loud."

Serah wrapped both hands around the cup. The warmth was real; that much in Hollowmere could be trusted. "You were tutored once," she said. "In small magics. Before Corvane outlawed them."

Quentin went very still. Not dramatically—the stillness of someone whose whole life had trained her to go still. "My mother taught me. Nobody else."

"There was another. A girl, older by two years, who came on festival days. Who taught you how to hold a charm in your palm without letting it sing. You used to get frustrated and the charms would buzz like trapped bees." Serah heard her own voice as if from across the room, level and precise and utterly betraying her. "She told you that magic kept warm was magic wasted."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, the ghost dropped something heavy and, Serah suspected, entirely on purpose.

"That's not possible," Quentin said quietly. "That girl—" Her hands had gone white around the cup. "That girl was called Wren. She stopped coming when I was nine. My mother said she'd gone south. Married a scholar." She looked up, and Serah watched ten years of invisibility crack down the middle like river ice. "Say the rest of it. Say whatever it is you've been carrying up that mountain, because you've been carrying it since the pass road and it's making you walk crooked."

"Wren didn't marry a scholar," Serah said. "Wren took a new name when she entered the Collegium. When she took her oath." She made herself meet those eyes, the eyes of a woman who had spent a decade being unnoticeable and deserved, finally, to be noticed by the worst possible truth. "Wren was me, Quentin. I'm the girl who stopped coming."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the sound of the fire and the snow that wasn't making any sound at all, which somehow made more noise than any storm.

Then Quentin set down the cup with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it, and stood up.

"No," she said.

"Quentin—"

"No. Sit there and be marble a moment longer while I work this through, because either I have gone snow-mad on the pass road, or you are telling me—" She laughed, one sharp note with nothing in it. "You. Serah Vail. Sworn sword of the crown. The woman sent to arrest me. You were *Wren*."

"I took the oath at fifteen. New name, new life, the Collegium's terms."

"You were my sister in everything but blood. You taught me the warming rhyme. You—" Quentin's voice caught, and she hated it, he could see her hating it, hating that her body would do grief in front of the person who had caused it. "You stopped coming the same season my mother was taken."

And there it was. The sentence Serah had been rehearsing since noon, since the pass road, since the first night at the inn when Maren the flickering ghost-keeper had looked at her with dead gray eyes and said, gently, *You knew her mother, didn't you? The dead remember who reported whom.* Serah had been composing and discarding versions of it for six days, and every version was terrible, and she said it anyway, because Quentin had taught her once—if you meant to say the true thing, say it plain, before the fear dresses it up.

"I reported her," Serah said. "Your mother. To the city wardens. I was eleven years old."

* * *

The silence that followed was the kind that has weight and temperature. Quentin didn't move. The fire leaned away from her, or Serah imagined it did.

"She sold charms," Serah went on, because stopping now would be worse. "Quiet ones. For quiet people. And the wardens had started paying children to watch the market stalls—a copper for every name. I'd just taken my first lessons in lawful thaumaturgy, and they'd told us wild magic was dangerous, that unlicensed charm-sellers hurt people, that reporting was *protection*." Her voice did something humiliating on the word protection. She let it. "I saw your mother tuck a remembering-key into a widow's hand and take no coin for it, and I still—I wrote her description on the warden's slate. I gave the street. I gave the door."

"You gave them my mother," Quentin said.

"They took her for questioning. She was released in spring. The records said released." Serah spread her hands, empty, useless. "But she came back changed, and she stopped teaching you, and within the year she was bargaining with things beneath a mountain, because the crown had marked her and the mountain offered what the crown couldn't—cover, shadow, a debt that hid its debtors. Everything that happened to your family after that slate-mark began with me. I was a child, Quentin. I was eleven, and I believed the adults, and it doesn't matter, because belief doesn't shrink the wound. I know. I've been pressing on it for twenty years."

"You *knew*." Quentin's voice rose, and the flames stood up straight to listen. "At the walls. When you offered me your alliance. When you said *help me learn what the crown buried*—you already knew. You knew what buried her!"

"I suspected the shape of it. I didn't know the mountain's part until Maren told us. There's a difference between a wound and the scar over it, and I wanted—I needed—to see the whole of it before I said anything. Which was cowardice dressed as caution, yes. Add it to the account."

"Oh, I will." Quentin began to pace, three steps toward the window, three steps back, the old kitchen rhythm of a woman thinking dangerously. "Let's count the account, shall we? You broke my family. Then you grew up, put on a badge, and hunted charm-sellers—people like her—for the crown that broke her. Then you found *me*, the daughter, and instead of saying 'forgive me, I was the hand,' you arrested my trail, interrogated my customers, cornered me with an inquisitor—"

"I lied in my first report to keep you off the lists. I turned my back on the walls so you could run. I've spent ten years—"

"Don't." The word cracked across the room like a whip, and every candle guttered. Quentin stopped pacing. When she spoke again her voice was low and shaking and more frightening than the shout. "Don't you dare spend your guilt at me like coin. You want absolution? Go to her grave. Go to the mountain and ask the Pale Steward what a mother's confession sounds like, because I've read it, Serah, I read it by lamplight underground, and nowhere in that ledger—nowhere—is there a line about a little girl on a warden's errand. She never blamed you. Do you understand what that does to me? She carried *your* sin for me, protected me even in the confession, and you're standing in front of me asking me to carry it too, and I can't, I *can't*—"

Her voice broke. And then, horribly, beautifully, the fire reached for her.

Serah saw it happen: the hearth leaning toward Quentin like a plant toward sun, the coals brightening, the whole room tilting its small warmth toward a woman with half a soul, because half a soul leaves room, and lonely things pour themselves into the space. The ghost upstairs stopped mid-footstep. Even the snow outside seemed to pause against the windows.

Quentin stared at her own glowing hands. "Get away from me," she whispered—but she was crying now, and the fire dimmed when she did, following her grief downward like a tide.

Serah stood up. Every instinct she owned—the mage's, the officer's, the coward's—told her to give distance. She crossed the room instead.

"Quentin."

"Don't touch me."

"I'm not going to touch you. I'm going to tell you the last of it, and then you can decide whether I walk into that pass tomorrow or into the snow tonight, and I'll accept whichever you choose. That's the only penance I have that's worth anything: your verdict, freely given." She waited until Quentin's breathing slowed from ragged to merely hard. "I didn't come to Hollowmere to confess because it would ease me. I came because we're two days from the Undercourt's gate, and the Steward reads hearts like ledgers, and if you learn this below the mountain—from the dead, in front of the court—it will break you where nothing above ground can reach you to mend it. Truth is a wound, Quentin. But wounds opened clean heal, and wounds opened underground fester. So I opened it here, by a fire, while you still had soup in you and a chair to throw at me."

Quentin wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, furious at the fire for dimming in sympathy, furious most of all, perhaps, at herself for the small treacherous part of her that had known—that had always known—because who else but Wren had taught her to cup a charm so it wouldn't sing?

"Why tell me the warden's-slate part at all?" she asked. "You could have confessed lighter. Everyone confesses lighter. It's practically a civic art in Corvane."

"Because you asked me once, when you were seven, why the truth mattered if nobody ever saw it." Serah smiled, and it hurt her face. "You said, 'Because I'd know.' And I've been knowing for twenty years, and I'm tired, and you deserve the whole weight, not the convenient half."

* * *

They sat again eventually—Quentin by the fire, Serah across from her, the soup cold and forgotten between them. The ghost, having judged the emotional temperature acceptable, resumed its slow nocturnal project of moving furniture one inch at a time, which in Hollowmere passed for tact.

"My mother never blamed you," Quentin said at last. It wasn't a question, but Serah answered it anyway.

"Never. Maren says the dead keep cleaner books than the living. If she'd blamed me, I'd have felt it years ago—guilt draws the dead like smoke draws bees. All I've ever felt from her direction was..." She searched. "...patience. Like she was waiting for me to finish a lesson."

"The warming rhyme," Quentin said. "Lesson three. *What is hidden is not healed.*" She laughed, wet and unwilling. "You insufferable woman. You've been teaching me the same lesson for twenty years and I failed the exam twice."

"Three times, counting the walls."

"Counting the walls." Quentin turned her cup slowly on its base. Outside, the silent snow pressed at the glass. "Here's what I can't give you yet, and maybe ever: forgiveness on schedule. I'm not the king's kitchen; I don't serve things before they're ready."

"I wouldn't trust you if you did."

"But." Quentin looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "You told me the worst of yourself by a fire instead of letting the mountain do it. That took a spine I didn't think the Collegium issued. So tomorrow we walk into the Pass of Teeth together, and you keep your oath-breaker's badge, and I keep my half a soul, and neither of us pretends the ledger between us is balanced. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Serah?" Quentin's mouth curved, tired and real. "If you ever lie to me again—even kindly, even bravely—I'll sell your name to every market stall from here to the sea. Quietly. You'll be famous by Thursday."

"Duly noted." Serah raised her cup of smoky plum wine. After a moment, Quentin touched hers to it. The fire settled back onto its logs like a dog circling down to sleep.

It was Maren who interrupted, flickering into the doorway with a lamp that cast light through her rather than onto her, her face arranged around something urgent.

"Beg pardon," the dead woman said. "But you'll want to see the north window. There are lights on the pass road—and they're not travelers. Travelers," she added, with the mild authority of someone who had watched the road for two hundred years, "make noise."

Serah was at the window in three strides. Far up the white shoulder of the mountain, where the Pass of Teeth began its climb, torches moved in formation. Evenly spaced. Disciplined. Crown-blue flames that burned without heat—the inquisitor's fire, the kind that didn't melt snow so much as *command* it.

Behind her, Quentin exhaled slowly.

"Well," she said. "So much for opening wounds clean."

Serah counted the torches twice, hoping arithmetic would improve out of sympathy. It didn't. Fourteen lights, moving fast, aimed at the gate that only they knew was there—and behind them, faint but unmistakable even at this distance, a sound the silent snow had been holding back all night finally arrived on the wind:

Bells. Watch bells, ringing in the mountains, ringing for them.
