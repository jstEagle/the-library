## 13. The Pass of Teeth

The mountain had teeth, and it was showing them.

Quentin felt the pass before she saw it—a cold that came through her boots and settled in her back teeth like a coin left in the mouth overnight. The wind up here didn't howl so much as *whisper*, which was worse. A howl was honest. A whisper meant the mountain was thinking about you.

"Cheerful place," she said, because Serah Vail had gone quiet an hour ago, and quiet Serah was a Serah building arguments.

"It's called the Pass of Teeth," Serah said, not looking up from the map. "I don't think cheer was intended."

"Everything's better with low expectations. That's practically my life philosophy." Quentin squinted at the notch in the ridge ahead, where the rock jutted up in jagged gray fangs against a sky the color of dishwater. "Though I'll admit whoever named this one wasn't being subtle."

They'd been climbing for three days since leaving Hollowmere, and Quentin's legs had filed a formal complaint. Her hands were another matter—she'd been working small charms into her gloves at night by firelight, warmth-stitches her mother had taught her, the kind of magic so minor it barely counted as magic at all. Just thread that remembered heat. Just wool that forgot cold.

It occurred to her, somewhere around the fourth switchback on the second day, that she'd spent ten years pretending those charms were nothing. And now they were the only thing keeping her alive. There was a lesson in there somewhere. She decided not to look at it directly.

Serah climbed like a woman who'd read extensively about climbing and resented every minute of actual practice. She was competent—of course she was, Serah was competent at everything—but she moved with the tight-jawed dignity of someone refusing to admit that ice was winning.

"Hold there," Quentin said, catching her elbow as the mage's boot skated on a shelf of black stone.

"I had it."

"You absolutely did not."

"I was *assessing* the footing."

"You were assessing the sky. Upside down." Quentin hauled her upright. "For a court mage you're remarkably bad at admitting when you're falling."

"For a fugitive food-taster," Serah said, brushing frost off her cloak with tremendous dignity, "you're remarkably good at catching people. It's becoming a habit. I may have to arrest you for it."

"You'd have to catch me first, and we've established your footing is unreliable."

The ghost of a smile crossed Serah's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice—and Quentin found herself absurdly pleased to have caused it. Ten days ago this woman had been hunting her. Five days ago she'd confessed to the thing she'd done as a child, the report that had set everything burning, and Quentin had nearly walked off a mountainside over it. Now they shared rope and rations and jokes, and Quentin did not know what to call what they shared besides that, and was afraid to name it too precisely in case naming it broke it.

* * *

The frozen river came on the third afternoon, a white ribbon laid across the pass floor, and it looked exactly as innocent as a lie.

"That's the Thawwater?" Quentin asked.

"What's left of it." Serah crouched at the bank and knocked her staff against the ice. The sound came back wrong—too deep, too hollow, like knocking on the door of an empty house. "In spring this runs fast enough to drown horses. In winter it should be solid enough to march an army across." She knocked again. "It isn't."

"Because of the Undercourt."

"Because of whatever's leaking out of the Undercourt." Serah's jaw tightened. "The cold isn't natural cold. Natural cold sits on top of things. This comes up from underneath. Like the mountain is exhaling something."

Quentin knelt beside her and pressed her bare palm flat to the ice—the gloves came off only when necessary; her charm-work lived in them. The cold that met her skin was strange indeed. Kitchen-cold was dry. River-cold was wet. This cold was *hungry*, a cold with appetite, and beneath it—faint as a heartbeat through a wall—she felt something else.

Warmth. Deep down. Far below.

"They're still lit down there," she said quietly. "The lamps. I can feel them."

Serah glanced at her. "Through the ice?"

"Through the ice, through the rock, through half the mountain." Quentin pulled her glove back on, flexing her fingers. "Half my soul's going to be down there soon, apparently. Maybe it's already saying hello."

She'd meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like grief, and Serah's hand found hers over the ice between them—brief, warm, deliberate—before either of them pretended it hadn't happened.

They crossed at dawn the next morning, roped together thirty feet apart, walking soft as thieves. The ice groaned twice, long and low, like an old man turning over in bed, but held. On the far side Quentin permitted herself one whole breath of relief before the snow started falling.

Wrong snow. Too early in the season, too silent—no hiss of wind behind it, just flakes descending straight down like ash from a fire nobody could see. By midday they couldn't see ten feet. By evening they were digging a hollow into a drift with their hands, backs to the wind-that-wasn't-wind, sharing the last of the travel bread and most of the silence.

"We're being followed," Serah said into the dark.

"I know. I've known since yesterday."

"The inquisitor's party. Six riders, maybe eight." Serah fed a twig to their tiny shielded flame. "They lost us at the river crossing, but they'll pick up our trail when this snow stops. And it will stop."

"And we can't outride them to the gate." Quentin pulled her knees to her chest. "So we need a miracle."

As if summoned—which, given her line of work, made her deeply suspicious of coincidence—a light bloomed in the white murk below them. A lantern, swinging steady. And a voice, rough and familiar, calling up the slope:

"If the two of you are planning to freeze to death, kindly do it somewhere I'm not standing!"

* * *

Doss looked terrible, which meant he looked like himself: watchman's coat crusted with ice, beard full of frost, and a pack on his shoulders the size of a modest haystack. He climbed up to their hollow breathing like a bellows and sat down without ceremony, and Quentin had never been so glad to see anyone in her life.

"You're supposed to be in Corvane," she said.

"Good to see you too."

"Doss. The city. You are *from* it. You have a job in it."

"Had." He started unpacking with the methodical calm of a man who'd rehearsed this conversation on the road. Dried meat, hard cheese, a waxed bundle of bread that smelled like the bakery on Tanner's Row, a flask of something that was definitely not water. "Resigned. Effective immediately. Turns out the pay was bad anyway."

"You resigned." Quentin stared at him. "You've wanted to be a watchman since you were nine years old. You told me. Twice. At length."

"And I was a very good watchman, thank you for noticing." He handed her the flask. "But I've recently learned the crown I was watching for has been feeding souls to a hole in a mountain to keep its winters short, and it turns out I have opinions about that. Strong ones. Swearing-level ones. I figured I'd bring my opinions here."

He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but Quentin heard the cost underneath. Ten years of knowing Doss. Ten years of him buying candles against grief he wouldn't explain and keys that remembered locks for doors he wouldn't name. Loyalty had been the spine of him, the same way invisibility had been hers. And he'd snapped it clean to walk into a blizzard after two women the entire kingdom wanted.

"You could have sent supplies with a courier," she managed.

"A courier would've taken the warrant too." From inside his coat he drew a folded paper—she recognized the seal before he even opened it, the black wax of the king's inquisitor—and held it up between two fingers like something dead. "Warrant for the arrest of Quentin Ravensworth, sorceress and fugitive. Signed, sealed, delivered to me personally by the inquisitor's clerk, who assumed I'd leap at the chance."

"Wouldn't you? Once upon a time?"

Doss looked at her for a long moment. Snow hissed—no, didn't hiss. Snow fell silently around the little flame, and the lantern light turned his frost-crusted face gold at the edges.

"Once upon a time," he said, "a woman sold me a candle that burned grief, and she never asked me whose grief it was or why I needed it burned. She just took my coin and gave me quiet, and asked nothing back, and let me keep my shame private." He tucked the warrant away again. "You don't arrest a person like that. You climb a mountain after her. It's not complicated."

Beside Quentin, Serah made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Watchmen," she said, "are constitutionally incapable of following orders. I begin to see why the crown fears the lower districts."

"The crown fears everyone with a spine, Your Mageness. It's why the winters are so cold up here—all the spines fled north."

Despite everything—despite the dead river and the wrong snow and the teeth of the pass waiting ahead—Quentin laughed. It startled out of her, short and cracked, and Doss grinned like he'd planned it, and for one moment the hollow in the snowdrift felt almost warm.

Then Serah, because Serah was Serah, ruined it with arithmetic.

"There's a problem," she said. "Three of us can move fast and light. Three of us cannot outrun eight riders once this weather clears. And the gate at the head of the pass—" she tapped the map, at the notch where the rock fangs met, "—is narrow enough that one person with a lantern and a plausible story can turn a pursuit aside for hours. Maybe days."

Quentin understood before she finished the sentence. She hated how quickly she understood it.

"No," she said.

"One of us has to stay—"

"*No.*"

"—and it can't be you," Serah went on steadily, as if Quentin hadn't spoken, "because you're the one the ledger wants, and it can't be me, because I'm the one the inquisitor wants alive for questioning. Which leaves—"

"Nobody," Quentin said. "It leaves nobody, because I am not trading one friend for a mountain pass. I've had enough of bargains that trade people. That's rather the theme of my entire life, and I'm *done* with it."

Silence. The flame flickered. And then Doss cleared his throat.

"Lucky for both of you," he said, "that I never offered."

* * *

They argued until the bread ran out. Doss won, which surprised no one who knew him; he argued the way he patrolled, patiently, relentlessly, wearing you down one reasonable sentence at a time.

"I know these passes," he said. "Grew up in the foothills, before Corvane swallowed my village. My uncle drove mules through the Teeth for twenty years. I know which ledges hold snow and which shed it, which caves breathe warm and which will kill you, and—" he tapped his chest, where the watchman's badge used to sit and now sat a plain leather cord, "—I know how to talk to soldiers, because I was one of them until four days ago. They'll see a lost ex-watchman with an official warrant, and they'll believe him, because people believe their own paperwork."

"And what happens when they ask why you're alone?" Quentin demanded.

"I'll tell them the truth. Well. Most of it." Doss shrugged. "That the fugitives split up in the storm, that I tracked one set of prints south toward the low pass before losing the light. Every hour they spend following my story is an hour you spend climbing. That's all I need to buy you. Hours."

"And then?"

"Then I go home. Long way around, but I go home." He said it easily, and Quentin caught the lie in it the way she caught poison—in the finish, the faint bitterness underneath the sweetness. Doss was a terrible liar. He always had been. It was one of the reasons she'd trusted him.

She looked at Serah. Serah looked back, and in her eyes Quentin saw the same arithmetic, done and redone, coming out the same way every time.

"He stays," Serah said quietly. "Or none of us reach the gate, and everything we've spent gets spent for nothing."

Quentin stood up so fast she cracked her head on the drift above them, which did nothing for her argument's dignity. "You're both doing it. Both of you. Sacrificing yourselves like it's *polite*, like it's just another supply to distribute—well, I've read the ledger, do you understand? I've seen what happens to people who get traded for other people's safety. They end up keeping lamps lit under a mountain forever. That's the whole *point* of what we're doing—"

"Quentin." Doss's voice was gentle. Gently was worse. "Look at me."

She looked at him.

"I'm not sacrificing anything," he said. "I'm doing my job. One last time. The job I actually had, underneath the badge—not guarding the crown, guarding *people*. You. Her." He nodded at Serah. "Somebody's got to hold the door. It's the best work there is, holding a door. Nobody applauds it. Nobody's supposed to. That's how you know it matters."

Quentin's eyes were stinging, which she blamed on the snow, which was not falling, which made the excuse flimsy even by her standards.

"You come back," she said. "You hear me? You mislead them, and you take the long way around, and you come back down to Corvane, and in the spring—when this is over, however it ends—you find the garden. My mother's garden, outside the walls. I'll leave word with the innkeeper at Hollowmere. You come find me, and I'll make you tea, and you can complain about my cooking for the rest of your life. Those are the terms. Non-negotiable."

Doss smiled—that slow, crooked smile she'd paid three coppers a candle to keep safe all these years without ever letting herself wonder why it mattered so much.

"Those," he said, "are acceptable terms."

* * *

They parted at first light, in the blue-gray hush before the sun cleared the eastern fangs. Doss divided the supplies with a quartermaster's precision—more to Quentin's pack than his own, and he lied about the weights, and everyone let him.

At the fork in the trail, where the pass split toward the high gate and the low southern route, he stopped. Fumbled in his coat. Pressed something small into Quentin's gloved hand.

A candle stub. Half-burned, wax gone soft with age and body heat.

"You kept it," she said, throat tight. "All this time."

"Burned it halfway," Doss said. "Turns out grief doesn't burn all at once. You feed it a little at a time." He closed her fingers around the stub. "Keep the rest. Light it when you get where you're going. Not before."

Then he touched two fingers to his brow—the old watchman's salute, the one he'd probably been given on his first day at nineteen years old—and turned south into the snow, and did not look back, because looking back would have ruined it, and Doss never ruined anything.

Quentin watched until the white swallowed him.

"Come on," Serah said softly, hand at her shoulder. "Hours, he said. Let's not waste them."

They climbed. The Teeth rose around them, jagged and gleaming, and the wrong snow kept falling its silent fall, and somewhere below, growing fainter with every switchback, a man with a warrant he refused to use was telling the truth backward to men with swords.

By late afternoon they reached the shoulder below the gate and saw, far down the southern trail, six points of lantern light moving confidently in the wrong direction. Quentin allowed herself one breath of triumph.

Then the mountain spoke.

Not groaned—*spoke*. A crack like the world's largest bone breaking, somewhere above and behind them, and Quentin's skin prickled with a taster's certainty, the same instinct that had saved a hundred meals and one steward's life: *something in this is wrong.*

"Avalanche," Serah breathed. "Above us. Run."

They ran. The gate stood forty yards ahead—a ragged notch between two stone fangs, rimed with ice, dark as a doorway. Behind them the slope roared awake, and the snow came down like the mountain deciding, finally, to swallow.

Quentin dove through the gate as the world went white behind her—

—and landed, breathless and blinded, on stone that was not stone. Warm stone. Smooth as lamplight.

Ahead, down a stair carved from the mountain's living root, a thousand small flames flickered in the dark.

They were already inside.
