## 14. Beneath the Mountain

The door at the bottom of the stair was made of whalebone, or something that had never been bone at all but wished it were. It was warm to the touch.

Serah Vail put her palm against it and felt a heartbeat on the other side—slow, vast, patient as geology—and understood with a lurch that she wasn't feeling the door. She was feeling what lived behind it, and it was feeling her back.

"Quentin," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I want you to know that everything I've said about thinking this through has been a complete fiction."

"I know." Quentin stood beside her in the dark of the passage, travel-worn, frost still melting out of her hair. Ten days from Corvane, three from the pass, and one whole night spent huddled against Doss's shoulder while he lied to soldiers about which way they'd gone—she looked like a woman who had been dragged through winter by her heels, and yet there was a steadiness in her face Serah hadn't seen above ground. "But we're here anyway."

"Yes," Serah said. "That's rather my point."

The door opened without being touched. It swung inward on air that smelled—not like death, which Serah had expected, and not like rot or grave-dust or any of the hundred things the seminaries taught mages to fear—but like bread baking somewhere far away, and lamp oil, and rain on stone in summer.

Light spilled out. Not torchlight. Lamplight, thousands upon thousands of small flames, receding into a cavern so large that Serah's mage-sense simply declined to measure it and offered her instead a sensation like standing at the edge of sleep.

"Oh," Quentin said softly.

They stepped through together, because neither of them was willing to be the one who went first alone, and the door closed behind them with the gentle finality of a book shutting.

* * *

The Undercourt was a city.

That was the thing no text had ever said plainly, and Serah understood now why: saying it plainly would have made it bearable, and it was not bearable. It was streets of pale stone lit by lamps in windows that belonged to houses built for people who did not breathe. It was a market square where figures moved slowly, browsing stalls of light and memory instead of bread and cloth. It was a river of warm gold running between the lanes, and along its banks sat shapes that might once have been fishermen, casting lines that caught nothing, contentedly, forever.

And everywhere—the lamps. Hanging from arches, floating in the river, carried in hands. Each one burned with a small, steady flame, and each flame, when Serah reached toward it with the careful tendril of her training, felt *occupied*.

"They're souls," she whispered. "Quentin. The lamps are—"

"I can feel mine." Quentin's hand had gone to her own chest, fingers pressing the hollow below her throat as though holding shut a door. Her voice was strange. "It's down here somewhere. It's—" She stopped, swallowed. "It's *warm*, Serah. I've been cold my whole life and I didn't know it until just now."

Something moved in the lamplight ahead of them, and the whole street turned to look.

Not all at once—that would have been horror, and this was worse. The residents turned the way a field of flowers turns toward sun: gradually, sweetly, with unmistakable gladness. An old man setting out lamps paused mid-motion and smiled at them with an expression of such pure welcome that Serah's hand flew to her knife before she remembered that these were the dead, and the knife was an insult, and also probably useless.

"They're happy to see us," she said. "Why are they happy to see us?"

"Because we're new," Quentin said. And then, quieter, with the flat tone she used for truths too heavy to carry any other way: "Because nobody new ever comes."

* * *

They walked because walking was the only option offered. The streets inclined gently downward, always downward, toward a glow at the city's heart greater than any single lamp—a light like a hearth seen through the window of a house the size of the sky.

The dead did not crowd them. They simply appeared alongside, at respectful distances, walking the same direction, and if Serah slowed they slowed, and if she stopped they waited with the courteous patience of hosts who had all the time that had ever been minted.

A little girl in a dress two centuries out of fashion fell into step beside Quentin and held up a paper boat.

"For the river," she explained, when Quentin didn't take it. "So it goes somewhere."

Quentin took the boat. The girl beamed and drifted back into the crowd, satisfied, and Serah watched Quentin hold the small paper thing like it was made of glass.

"I tutored children," Serah said, before she could stop herself. "In the capital. Before my oath was finished. They were that age—they wanted you to hold things. To witness them."

"You told me," Quentin said. "At Hollowmere. You told me everything, remember? That was the deal." But she said it gently, and bumped Serah's shoulder with hers as they walked, and the paper boat stayed in Quentin's pocket for the rest of the journey down.

* * *

The Steward met them at the mouth of the great hall, which meant the Steward had come up the long avenue to meet them, which meant—for Serah, whose whole life was the reading of protocol like other men read weather—that the ruler of the Undercourt had extended them an honor that kings would have gone to war over, and done it casually, as if it were nothing, as if *they* were the ones who mattered.

The Pale Steward was tall the way a candle is tall: all of it flame-shaped, all of it burning quietly upward. Where a face should have been there was a suggestion of one, the way a face is suggested in smoke, and yet Serah found she could read it perfectly—could read kindness in it, and exhaustion, and underneath both, an ache so old and so large that her eyes stung in sympathy before she knew why.

"Quentin Ravensworth," the Steward said. Its voice was many voices, gently braided. It pronounced the name the way one pronounces the name of a song one has loved for a very long time. "You have your mother's walk. She argued with every road she ever took, and lost to none of them."

"My mother's dead," Quentin said.

"Yes." The Steward's flame dimmed, briefly, in something that was unmistakably grief. "She came here. Forty years early, and angry about it, and she bargained with me even at the door—she tried to buy her way back out to finish raising you. I couldn't allow it. But she left instructions." The great smoky face turned toward Serah, and she felt weighed, assessed, and—to her considerable alarm—*approved of*. "And she left hope. Welcome, Serah Vail. Oath-keeper. Your badge weighs four ounces and your conscience weighs considerably more. Both are welcome here. We do not require you to set either down."

"That's—" Serah's voice cracked. She mastered it. Court mage, defender of the realm, and she was being undone by hospitality. "That's very generous of a regent whose court I was sent to arrest my way into."

"Were you?" The Steward sounded genuinely curious. "Sent? Child, look at your feet. Whose road did you choose at the crossroads under the pass?"

Serah looked. She thought of the fork beneath the mountain, the left-hand path marked with the crown's surveyor sigils, and how she had stood before it with the warrant in her hand, and chosen right.

"No one's," she said.

"No one's," the Steward agreed, pleased. "Then you were not sent. You *came*. There is a difference, and it matters more down here than anywhere else in the world." It gestured them forward into the hall, and its voice warmed further, and this was the moment, Serah realized later, when the unease truly took root—because the warmth was real, and that was the problem. "Come. You must be hungry. Quentin—child—we have kept your place. Eleven years, we have kept it lit. Will you come and see it?"

And Quentin—who had crossed avalanches and frozen rivers and ten years of deliberate invisibility to reach this moment, who had every reason to guard herself like a fortress—smiled at the Pale Steward with a tired, wondering, homesick smile, and said:

"Yes. Please."

* * *

The hall at the city's heart was hung with lamps beyond counting, and at its center stood a table laid for a feast that no one ate, surrounded by chairs that no one sat in—or rather, by chairs that everyone sat in, eventually, in their own time, and the table was not a table but a ledger, and the feast was not food but names.

The Steward led them past it, deeper, to a smaller room off the side where a fire burned in a hearth the size of a chapel, and before the fire stood a single chair, worn smooth, angled toward the warmth.

On the seat lay a folded blanket. On the blanket, a sprig of dried lavender.

"Your mother sat there," the Steward said, "the night before she went up the mountain to make her bargain. She said, *when my daughter comes, she'll be tired. Leave the chair for her.*" The flame-face bent close, gentle as a grandmother. "You are tired, Quentin. Sit."

And Quentin sat.

Serah watched her friend settle into the chair that had waited eleven years, watched the firelight move across her face, watched her shoulders drop for the first time since the stranger had bled out on her kitchen floor—and felt the unease bloom in her chest like frost-flower on a window, beautiful and wrong.

Because this was the trap. She saw it now, or thought she did. Not chains, not threats, not the iron logic of debt. This. A warm room. A saved chair. A mother's lavender. The Undercourt didn't eat souls—it *kept* them, the ghost-keeper at Hollowmere had said, it keeps them warm—and it kept them the only way a lonely thing knows how: it made staying feel like coming home.

"Steward," Serah said carefully, "if I may ask. What happens now?"

"Now?" The Steward turned its burning face to her, and behind the kindness, deep down in the braid of voices, Serah heard it: the loneliness. Vast, geological, older than the crown's oldest lie. "Now, dear child, she rests. She eats. She meets the others who wait here—there is a weaver who knew her grandmother, she'll want to say hello. And in a day, or a week, or whenever she is ready"—the flame flickered, and for half a heartbeat the great shape seemed almost frail—"we will talk about her soul. What it means to keep it. What it costs." A pause, delicate as falling ash. "What it might cost *otherwise*."

"Otherwise," Serah repeated.

The Steward's gaze slid back to Quentin, drowsing warm in her mother's chair, and the smoky features did something that on a human face would have been called hope, and would have been called hunger, and would have been impossible to tell apart.

"There is more than one way to fill a chair," the Pale Steward said softly. "We will discuss it when she wakes."

Behind them, unhurried, the great doors of the hall began, very quietly, to close.
