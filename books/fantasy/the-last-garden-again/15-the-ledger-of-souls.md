## 15. The Ledger of Souls

The Ledger was kept in a room with no door.

Not *no entrance*—there was an entrance, a tall archway draped in lamplight and hanging moss that glowed the color of honey—but no door. No hinges, no lock, no wood to bar. You walked in, and you were simply *in*, and something about the absence of a barrier made Quentin's skin prickle. Ten years in Corvane had taught her that doors were where safety lived. A room without one was either a trap or a confession, and she suspected it might be both.

"You'll want to wash your hands first," said the little clerk who had been assigned to her.

The clerk was called Mip. At least, Mip said he was called Mip, in the tone of someone repeating information given to him long ago by someone else who may not have been reliable. He stood perhaps as high as Quentin's elbow, gray as candle smoke, with eyes like two drops of lamp oil—warm at the center, dark at the rim. He wore a robe that might have been fine once, in some century that had since been forgotten on purpose.

"Wash my hands," Quentin repeated. "I'm reading a book."

"You're reading *the* book." Mip's dignity was enormous for his size. "And hands carry oil, and oil carries memory, and memory smudges. We are very careful here about what gets smudged."

Behind him, the archway breathed out a smell Quentin couldn't name—warm stone, beeswax, and underneath it something faintly sweet, like almonds that had decided to be kind instead of dangerous.

She washed her hands in the basin Mip indicated, which sat beside the archway for no reason anyone explained, filled with water so clear it seemed embarrassed to be touched. Then she stepped through.

* * *

The room beyond was larger than the mountain had any right to contain.

Shelves rose in curving rows toward a ceiling lost in soft amber dark, and every shelf held books—not paper books, mostly, though there were those too, but ledgers bound in leather and bark and things that might have been bark's older, stranger cousins. Some volumes were thin as pamphlets. One, chained shut near the entrance, was thick as a bread loaf gone wrong and hummed quietly to itself, the way a kettle hums just before it makes up its mind.

Lamps floated between the shelves at head height, unhurried, drifting like fat golden fish. When Quentin walked, they drifted with her, keeping polite distance. She got the distinct feeling they were curious.

"They like new people," Mip said, catching her looking. "We don't get many visitors who come down on purpose."

"We?" Quentin said. "You work here?"

"Everyone works here," Mip said, with the serene confidence of a statement that explains nothing. "That is rather the point of the place. Your section is this way. Surnames beginning with R are kept near the warmth."

He led her down an aisle where the shelves were close enough to brush both shoulders, and the floating lamps gathered behind them like well-trained ducks. And there, at waist height, in a niche lined with faded red velvet—the kind of velvet that had been red a very long time ago and was now the color of embers—a single slim volume lay open on a stand of pale wood.

Quentin knew it before she read a word.

It was her mother's handwriting.

She hadn't seen it in eleven years, but the hand came back to her whole: the way the letters leaned forward like they were hurrying somewhere, the way the crossbars on the t's flicked upward, hopeful. Her mother had written shopping lists that looked like declarations of war and love letters that looked like recipes, and Quentin had spent her childhood reading both upside down across the kitchen table.

*Quentin Ravensworth,* said the heading, in that hurrying hand. *Born under a waning moon, in the year the river froze twice.*

"Take your time," Mip said. "Time is the one thing we have entirely too much of."

Then he bowed—an odd, folding bow, like a napkin deciding to be smaller—and drifted off between the shelves, leaving Quentin alone with her mother's voice.

She read standing up, because sitting felt like something you did when you'd accepted what a thing said, and she hadn't accepted anything yet.

* * *

The first pages were almost ordinary. That was the worst part.

Her mother had written the bargain down the way she'd written everything: plainly, with small jokes in the margins. Quentin found herself laughing before she could stop, a short ugly laugh that startled a passing lamp into flickering.

*"Terms as agreed with the Steward's office (the Steward himself is taller than expected and very polite; do not be fooled, or rather do be fooled, it costs nothing),"* one entry read. *"One soul, whole and unworn, belonging to my daughter Quentin, age negative three months. Payment: seed grain for the winter, medicine for the fever carts, and the city watch instructed to look elsewhere. Signed under protest, which the clerk says doesn't legally count but he wrote it down anyway, bless him."*

Under protest. Quentin pressed her fingers flat against the page, carefully, the way Mip had taught her without teaching her. Her mother had sold her before she was born and *joked about it in the margins*, and some traitor part of Quentin's chest ached with how exactly that was her mother—how the woman had never once in her life done a terrible thing solemnly when she could do it terribly and funny at the same time.

But it was the third page where the writing changed.

The jokes stopped. The letters still leaned forward, but now they leaned like people lean into wind.

*If you are reading this, then you came down yourself, which means you learned, which means I failed at the only thing I was ever truly good at: keeping you ignorant. Forgive me. Ignorance was the second thing I bought for you, and it turns out it wasn't mine to give.*

*I need you to understand what the soul-bargain actually purchased, because the grain and the medicine were the smallest parts, and if you think the smallest parts were the point, you will hate me, and I would rather you hated me for the truth than loved me for the lie.*

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. In a room full of lamps, she thought absurdly of water, of the taste-tester's trick of sipping between courses to clear the tongue. There was no water here. She kept reading.

*There was another creditor. Above ground. Before the famine winter, before any of it, your father owed a debt in Corvane—to men whose names I will not write even here, because names written can be found, and these men find things. It was not coin he owed. Coin would have been a mercy. He owed them a life, to be named later, and the later they named it the worse the terms grew, the way a wound grows worse the longer you leave it dressed in dirty cloth.*

*When he died, the debt passed to his blood. To me. And through me, to you.*

*They had already begun to look at my belly.*

Quentin stopped. The lamps had drawn close around her without her noticing, hovering at her shoulders like concerned hens. She waved them back, half-heartedly. They didn't go far.

*Understand this, my heart: the Undercourt does not eat souls. Whatever stories they tell upstairs, whoever told them—they are lies, or they are mistakes, or they are lies told by people who needed them to be true. The Court keeps souls the way a hearth keeps fire: fed, tended, warm. It is a lonely place below the mountain, lonelier than you can imagine while your heart still beats inside you, and the bargained-away are its company, its lamps, its children. It is not a kindness that they keep us. But it is not cruelty either. It is hunger of the sort that only wants company, and I have known worse hungers. I married one, briefly, before he died owing money.*

*So when the famine came, I went beneath the mountain and I made a trade: my daughter's soul, after her death, in exchange for her protection until then. Because a soul promised to the Undercourt is marked, and marked things cannot be claimed twice. Their laws are old and absolute about this. Older than Corvane. Older than the crown, though the crown pretends otherwise.*

*No one above ground could touch what was already promised below. Not the creditors. Not the men in gray coats who came asking questions in your father's last year. Not—*

The ink smeared slightly there, as if the page had been held too long, or wept on, or both.

*Not the crown itself. Though I begin to suspect, writing this, that the crown and the men in gray coats drink from the same bottle. Watch the tasters' guild, my heart. Watch who feeds whom. If you ever learn why certain people in that city never sicken, never starve, never pay—you will understand what I understood on the worst night of my life, standing in a lamplit cave trading my unborn child's forever for her Tuesday.*

*A debt is a chain. But sometimes a chain is also a leash, and sometimes a leash keeps a dog from walking off a cliff. I am not saying I was right. I am saying I chose the cliff I could see over the ones I couldn't, and I would do it again, and I will apologize for it every day of whatever remains of me, and neither of those things cancels the other out. That's the secret nobody upstairs wants you to learn: two true things can sit in the same heart at once. It's crowded in there. Make room.*

Quentin sat down.

There was a stool beside the stand—she was nearly certain there hadn't been a moment ago—and she took it, because her legs had made an executive decision without consulting her. The lamps settled around her feet like cats. Somewhere deep in the shelves, the humming chained book changed key, softer, as if listening.

*Protection until then,* her mother had written. *Until then.* As if death were an appointment you dressed up for. As if her daughter's whole life were a waiting room, and the bargain merely ensured the waiting room was heated.

And yet—

Quentin turned the thought over the way she'd turn an unfamiliar spoonful on her tongue, testing for bitterness underneath. Ten years in Corvane. Ten years of being forgettable, invisible, unremarkable—and alive. She had always assumed her survival was skill. Her own cleverness, her own carefulness, her rule of *never be noticed*. She had built a whole identity on it, brick by brick: *I am alive because I am good at not existing.*

But she had been noticed. Of course she had. A food-taster with no family and no history, tasting for a king, for a decade, without ever once being promoted, punished, or questioned? Someone had been watching her the whole time. Someone had been keeping her in the exact center of safe—too useful to discard, too protected to touch—and she had called it invisibility and worn it like a medal.

The immunity. Her slow-built armor against poison. She had thought it was hers, earned drop by drop over years of quiet, deliberate swallowing. But her mother's words sat in her stomach now like a stone swallowed whole: *watch who feeds whom.* The kitchens. The king's table. Every meal she'd ever tasted, every subtle poison she'd survived and catalogued and built herself upon—who had chosen what went into those dishes? Who had arranged for the taster's training to be thorough, the doses to be survivable, the risks to be *educational*?

Even her one great secret might have been issued to her. A tool, provided by the same crown that had known all along what she was: marked goods, promised south, kept warm and useful until collection.

"I'm going to be sick," Quentin informed the lamps conversationally, "or possibly laugh. With me it's often hard to tell."

The lamps, to their credit, did not judge her.

Because underneath the fury—underneath the cold bright anger at her mother, at the crown, at ten years of her own proud delusions—there was the other thing. The crowded-heart thing. Her mother had traded her soul, yes. Had made her daughter's entire existence collateral. Had let her grow up thinking she was nothing but quick hands and a careful tongue.

And had also, with the same signature, wrapped her in the only protection that had ever mattered, against enemies the child had never known existed. Debt as chain. Chain as leash. Leash keeping the dog from the cliff.

*Two true things can sit in the same heart at once.*

"It's crowded in here," Quentin said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she let it.

She read the last lines twice.

*If you've come down before your time, then something has gone wrong, and I am sorry, and I am proud of you, in whichever order you need to hear those. The ledger will show the terms. The terms will show the names. Follow the names, my heart, all the way up. They reach higher than you think. They reach the table you've been tasting for.*

*And Quentin—one more thing. The Steward will offer you something, when the time comes. He offers everyone something. It's how the Court loves. Be careful what you say yes to. Love should not be a transaction, and if you ever catch yourself pricing yours, walk away, even from me. Especially from me.*

*All my crowded heart,*

*Mama*

The page ended. The next leaf was blank except for the clerk's small stamp at the corner: *Account active. Holder present. Terms pending.*

Terms pending.

Quentin sat with that a long while, the lamps breathing softly around her ankles, the humming book humming, the vast amber silence of the Ledger-room holding her like a hand she hadn't asked to hold.

Her soul was here. Somewhere in this mountain, in a lamplit field or a warm drawer or a hearth tended by a lonely regent, there was a piece of her that had been promised before she drew breath—and it had been kept warm, and fed, and *waited for*, for thirty years, the way you keep a bed made for a child who might come home.

And upstairs, past the shelves and the moss and the long cold pass, a crown that had fed her and shielded her and lied to her for a decade was sending an inquisitor down a sealed gate to collect on arrangements older than her mother's handwriting.

*Follow the names all the way up. They reach the table you've been tasting for.*

Quentin closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room.

"Mip," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was, after all, the family specialty. "I need to see the terms. All of them. And then I need to know—" she stood, and the lamps rose with her, gathering close, golden and attentive, like an audience that already suspected what she was going to say next— "who else's name is written in my margins."

Mip reappeared between the shelves, folding his small bow, and his lamp-oil eyes had gone very dark at the centers.

"That," he said, "depends on whether you're asking as the account holder—or as the heir."

Down the aisle, behind them, the chained book stopped humming altogether.
