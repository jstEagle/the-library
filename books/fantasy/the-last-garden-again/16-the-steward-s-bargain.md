## 16. The Steward's Bargain

The lamplight in the Undercourt did not flicker, and Serah Vail had decided, somewhere around her third hour beneath the mountain, that this was the most unsettling thing about it.

She had grown up on candlelight. Candlelight argued with itself, leaned into drafts, guttered when someone wept nearby. It was honest light. This light was patient. It sat in its glass lamps along the cavern streets like a thousand small eyes that had all the time in the world, which, she supposed, they did.

The Pale Steward walked ahead of them through the lamp-lit avenue, robes whispering against stone that had never known rain. He moved the way frost forms—unhurried, inevitable, beautiful if you refused to think too hard about what it meant for everything it touched.

Quentin walked beside him with her arms crossed, which Serah recognized as the taster's posture: *I am evaluating this dish and finding it suspect.* Even now, even here, at the center of the debt that had shaped her whole life, Quentin Ravensworth looked like a woman who had been served something undercooked.

"You will want to see the ledger hall properly," the Steward said. "Not in grief. In daylight—" he paused, caught himself, and made a small sound that might have been a laugh, "—in lamplight, I mean. Grief makes poor reading."

"I read fine in grief," Quentin said. "I've had practice."

Serah kept three steps behind them both, the way she'd kept three steps behind everyone for twenty years. It was a good distance. Close enough to act, far enough to pretend she hadn't yet decided whether acting meant protecting Quentin or arresting her. The badge felt heavier down here than it ever had above ground. Down here, it was just tin. The mountain didn't care about oaths sworn to a crown that, if the burned ledger's ashes were any measure, had been selling souls by the sackful since before Serah's grandmother learned to write.

The Steward led them past houses. That was the thing Serah couldn't stop looking at. Houses. Doors with worn thresholds, windows with shutters half-open, a child's chalk game still scrawled on one step in letters no living hand had drawn. The dead and the bargained-away lived here, or something adjacent to living, and they lived *domestically*. A woman watering a planter of pale, glowing flowers nodded to them as they passed. She had been dead, Serah guessed, for two hundred years, and she watered her flowers anyway, because what else was there?

"They're not suffering," the Steward said, without turning. He had a way of answering thoughts; Serah suspected the whole city heard thoughts, or perhaps simply had nothing better to listen to. "You came expecting torment. Most of you do. The crown tells stories about us because the truth is more embarrassing."

"And what's the truth?" Serah asked, before she could stop herself.

He stopped then. Turned. His face was the color of moonstone and smooth as river glass, but his eyes were old the way wells are old—you could drop a coin down them and wait a long time for the splash.

"The truth," he said, "is that we are cold. Not cruel. Cold. And lonely. And every soul the bargain brings keeps a little corner of the court warm. Your friend's mother knew this. She bargained not out of desperation alone—though it was a desperate winter—but out of understanding." His gaze slid to Quentin, gentle as snowfall. "The court has been waiting for you a long time, Quentin Ravensworth. You were promised. Promises keep us warm."

"Well," Quentin said, "I've always been told I have a warm personality."

It was a joke. Serah heard the fear underneath it, thin as wire, and loved her a little more for the joke, which was a dangerous way to start an hour.

* * *

The ledger hall was vast and quiet, domed like the inside of a bell, and every name in it glowed faintly on shelves of black stone. Serah found herself searching the shelves for names she knew—Vail, Vail—and hating herself for the relief when she found none. Her family had been too poor to bargain with anything but labor. Labor left no ledgers. It just left people bent.

Ravensworth glowed on the third shelf, second row. Two entries. The mother's, sealed and satisfied. And beneath it, fresh ink that shimmered like the surface of a winter pond:

*QUENTIN RAVENSWORTH — HELD — WARMTH OWED — TERM UNBROKEN.*

"There it is," Quentin said quietly. "My whole life, filed under 'held.' Like a coat at a checkroom."

"Like a promise," the Steward corrected. He folded his hands. And Serah, watching him, saw him do something she would think about for years afterward: he hesitated. The inevitable creature hesitated. "There is a form the bargain can take, other than the one you fear."

"Go on," Serah said, because Quentin had gone very still, and stillness from Quentin usually meant she was loading something.

"The ledger requires warmth. It does not require *your* warmth, specifically. A substitution is permitted. One soul freely offered in place of another—the debt transfers, whole and clean. You walk out of this mountain, Quentin Ravensworth, into whatever life you like. Spring, even. We are told spring exists." His voice did not change at all, which somehow made it worse. "Someone need only take your place. Willingly. With full knowledge. Those are the old terms, and I am bound to offer them."

"No," Quentin said immediately.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear the rest. There's only one person standing next to me who'd volunteer, and I already know how she prays." Quentin turned and looked at Serah, and her eyes were furious and frightened and absolutely certain. "*No.*"

And Serah—because she was who she was, because she had spent her whole life mistaking duty for love and was only now, down here in the lamplit dark, learning the difference—heard herself say:

"I'll do it."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. Serah counted them. It was an old habit from interrogations, counting the silence before the blow fell, and she had never once been on this side of the count.

"Say that again," Quentin said, very softly, "and I will find whatever passes for magic down here and use it on you."

"Quentin—"

"You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there with your *guilt* and your *oath* and your tragic childhood confession and volunteer yourself like—like paying off a tavern tab." Quentin's voice cracked in the middle, which she would hate later. "That's not love, Serah. That's just duty wearing a nicer coat. It's the same logic that made me taste poison for ten years. *Someone has to swallow it, so make it the person whose job it is.* Well, I'm quitting my job."

"It isn't duty," Serah said. And here was the terrible part: she meant it. She had lain awake at Hollowmere turning over what she'd done—the girl who had reported a desperate mother to the authorities, believing she was saving that mother's daughter from worse—and she had built the whole architecture of her atonement around exactly this moment. "I put your mother's name in a report when I was nine years old. Everything that followed—the famine terms, the bargain, the shape of your entire life—it grew from a seed I planted. Let me be the one who—"

"*Stop.*" Quentin grabbed her by the front of her travel coat, and the Steward watched them with the perfect stillness of furniture. "Listen to me. If you stay here, I don't get free. I get *purchased*. Do you understand? I'd be walking out of this mountain knowing some clerk above ground traded a mage for a taster and called it balanced books. Every morning of the rest of my life I'd wake up warm and free and know it cost me the only person who ever saw me clearly. That's not freedom. That's just a bigger cage with a view."

The Pale Steward cleared his throat—a strange, mineral sound, like pebbles shifting in a streambed.

"If I may," he said. "The offer stands regardless. Sentiment does not alter the ledger."

"Then sentiment is going to have to alter *you*," Quentin snapped, rounding on him, and Serah saw it happen again—the thing she'd seen in the inquisitor's chamber, the thing that made kings nervous: Quentin Ravensworth refusing, completely and without apology, to be a piece on anyone's board. "Because here's what I know about your court, Steward. I know you don't eat souls. I know you *keep* them. I know you're dying of loneliness up here under your patient little lamps, and I know that everyone who deals with you treats you like a furnace—feed it, bank it, forget it. My mother. The crown. Maybe even me, eventually. Nobody has ever once asked what *you* want."

The Steward went very still. Stillness, down here, seemed to be contagious.

"What I want," he said at last, slowly, as though the words were being unpacked from crates sealed centuries ago, "is not among the permitted answers."

"Well," Quentin said, "it's about time somebody amended the permissions."

Serah looked between them—the ancient regent of the dead and the food-taster with her sleeves pushed back like she was about to scrub a pot—and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since Hollowmere. This was why she'd come. Not for the ledger. For this: the sight of a woman who had spent ten years making herself invisible standing in the heart of the dark, visible as a bonfire, refusing every version of the story where someone else paid her bill.

"Quentin," Serah said carefully. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," Quentin said, "that there's a third option, and it's going to be terrible, and I'm going to need you to not volunteer for anything until I've finished explaining it." She turned back to the Steward. "Tell me about the rite nobody's supposed to perform. The one the ledger doesn't list. The one that splits a soul instead of trading it."

The lamps along the walls dimmed—all of them, at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone says the true thing aloud.

The Pale Steward looked at her for a long moment, and in his well-deep eyes Serah saw something she had not seen in him before: hope, unpracticed and terrified, like a man who had forgotten how to hold it.

"That rite," he said, "requires the one who performs it to remain *half* below. Forever. Half-warm, half-free. No ledger has ever recorded a soul choosing it, because no soul has ever—" his voice caught on something ancient, "—no one has ever *offered*."

"Yeah, well." Quentin rolled her shoulders like a woman preparing to knead bread. "Nobody's ever asked me nicely, either."

"Absolutely not," Serah said. "Quentin. *Absolutely not.* Half of you, buried under a mountain forever—how is that better than me taking your place?"

"Because it's mine to give," Quentin said simply. "That's the whole difference. You'd be giving yourself out of guilt. I'd be giving myself out of—" she stopped, and for a moment the wit failed her entirely, and she just looked at Serah with an expression that undid every wall Serah had built in twenty years of service. "Out of the other thing. The thing we haven't said out loud because we've been too busy almost dying."

Somewhere far above them, muffled by a mile of stone, the mountain shuddered. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. The lamps flickered—truly flickered, for the first time—and the Steward's head turned toward the sound with sudden alarm.

"The gate," he said. "The high gate. Someone is opening it from above."

Serah's hand went to her belt where her badge used to hang before she'd surrendered it to a drawer in Hollowmere, and found nothing, and understood anyway.

The inquisitor had found the sealed gate. And the crown, which had fed this court souls for generations to keep its winters short, was coming down to collect.
