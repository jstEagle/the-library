## 17. The Court Above Moves

The Pale Steward's court had a way of making time feel like syrup. Quentin had been beneath the mountain—she was almost sure of it—for three days, or possibly three weeks, and the lamps never dimmed, the tea never cooled, and Serah Vail still slept with her boots on, which Quentin considered both sensible and deeply funny.

She was in the lamplit fields when the mountain flinched.

There was no other word for it. The ground rolled once, gently, like a dog shifting in its sleep, and every flower in the Undercourt's impossible gardens turned its glowing face toward the far wall of the cavern—the one that shouldn't have had a door.

Quentin stood up out of the soil, dirt on her knees, and felt the taste change in her mouth.

That was the thing about her tongue. Ten years of tasting for a king taught you things no tutor could. You learned to read a dish the way priests read scripture. And what she tasted now, drifting down through the stone like smoke through a keyhole, was *almond*.

Bitter almond. Laced with something worse.

"Serah," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We have company."

* * *

The sealed gate stood in the western wall of the Undercourt—a slab of black stone carved over with royal seals going back four kings, maybe five. Quentin had walked past it twice and thought nothing of it. Sealed doors were everywhere down here; the dead were sentimental about privacy.

Now it was open.

Not broken. Not forced. *Open*, the way a door opens when someone who owns the house comes home, and standing in it, backlit by torchlight from some stairwell that should not have existed, was a man in grey.

He was unremarkable in the specific way that powerful men cultivate: medium height, medium years, a face like a clerk's ledger—accurate and empty. His robes bore the king's double raven, and under it a smaller sigil Quentin had seen ten thousand times without ever *seeing* it: a candle flame inside a crown.

The sigil of the Royal Taster's Office.

Her office. Her livery. Stitched, in tiny silver thread, into the collar of an inquisitor.

"Quentin Ravensworth," the man said, and his voice was warm as fresh bread, which somehow made it worse. "Or should I say—the Ravensworth girl. Your mother's daughter. The crown's investment."

Behind him came six guards in the same grey, and behind *them*, to Quentin's horror, came a familiar stoop-shouldered silhouette dragging his feet with magnificent reluctance.

"Master Hollis?" she said.

The old steward of the royal kitchens looked up at her with eyes gone red-rimmed and terrified. "They said you'd been kidnapped," he croaked. "They said—I'm sorry, girl. They knew I'd come if they said that. They *counted* on it."

"Of course they did." Quentin kept her voice light, because Master Hollis had taught her how to hold a spoon and she would not let him watch her shake. "Hello, Inquisitor. You're a long way from the wine cellar."

"Inquisitor Crane," he said, bowing slightly. "And you're a long way from your post. Though I'd argue you've been at your true post all along." He gestured, almost tenderly, at the lamplit fields, the glowing flowers, the distant figures of the dead moving through their patient half-lives. "Look at it. Generations of tending. Do you know what this place is, Quentin?"

"A debt collector's waiting room?"

"A *larder*." He smiled. "No. Forgive me. That's the word the old kings used, and it's ugly. This is a *hearth*. The winters above used to last eleven months, did you know that? Before Corvane. Before the crown. The mountain ate whole cities. Then someone clever made a bargain: warmth below, obedience above. A soul here and there, freely surrendered—or not so freely—and winter stays short. The harvests come. The king sleeps soundly." He spread his hands. "You've eaten well your whole life off this arrangement. We all have. Every loaf in Corvane is baked in borrowed summer."

Serah had come up silently beside Quentin, hand near her knife. "The crown feeds souls to the Undercourt," she said flatly. It wasn't a question.

"The crown *tends* them," Crane corrected mildly. "Feeding sounds so careless. There is a ledger. There are schedules. There is—" his gaze slid to Quentin, and the warmth in it curdled into something proprietary, "—quality control."

"I don't understand," Quentin said, though a cold part of her, the part that had spent ten years reading poison in soup, was already three steps ahead and wishing it weren't.

"Don't you?" Crane tilted his head. "Think, girl. A taster with no family, no history, no name anyone remembers. A body that shrugs off toxins that kill grown men. Did you truly believe you built that yourself? Ten years of sipping? No." He shook his head, almost pitying. "Your immunity was purchased. Before your birth. Part of your mother's bargain. The crown doesn't leave its investments unprotected—you cost too much to lose to a bad batch of hemlock."

The words landed somewhere under her ribs and sat there, heavy as a swallowed stone.

All those mornings. All those careful, invisible mornings, tasting the king's breakfast, feeling quietly proud of her own cleverness, her own slow-built armor. *I earned this*, she'd thought, watching poison slide off her tongue like rain off wax. *This is mine.*

It had never been hers. It had been *maintenance*. The crown oiling its machinery.

Beside her, Serah said, very quietly, "You knew. The Office knew. When I reported a rogue charm-seller in the kitchens—when I filed my first reports—they redirected me. Twice. I thought it was bureaucracy."

"Bureaucracy is how the truth hides in plain sight," Crane agreed pleasantly. "Mage Vail. Or should I say *former* Mage Vail? Your resignation was noted. Your desertion will be noted louder."

"I prefer 'career change.'"

"Hollis," Quentin said suddenly. "Why did they bring Hollis?"

Crane's smile thinned by a single degree. "Because the Undercourt requires consent, or something shaped like it. The old bargains are strict. But grief makes excellent consent. A man whose favorite apprentice has vanished into the dark—he'll agree to anything to bring her home." He put a hand on Hollis's shoulder, gentle as a trap. "Tell her, Master Hollis. Tell her what you agreed to."

Hollis's face crumpled. "They said—one soul, freely given, and you walk free. They said it wouldn't hurt. They said—" His voice broke. "Girl, I've known you since you were nineteen and burned your first sauce. I wasn't going to let them take you."

"And instead you offered them *yourself*." Quentin's chest went tight. Of all the cruelties she'd imagined from the crown—arrest, torture, a headsman's block—this was the one she hadn't braced for: kindness, weaponized. "That's why the gate opened for you. Consent. Fresh stock."

"The Steward will be pleased," Crane said. "A willing soul of good character. Better quality than most we deliver, frankly. The city poor fight the collectors, and fighting bruises the—" he caught himself, "—the *arrival*."

Something in Quentin went very still and very clear, the way it always did right before she spat out the king's food and lied to everyone's faces about the flavor.

"You've done this before," she said. "Many times."

"For four generations," Crane said, with the mild pride of a man discussing a vineyard. "The winters stay short, the realm stays fed, and nobody above has to think about where the warmth comes from. You of all people understand the value of being unnoticed, Quentin. We simply applied your philosophy at scale."

"My philosophy," she repeated. She looked at Hollis—at the old man who had slipped her extra bread during her first hungry winter, who called her *girl* the way other people said *daughter*—and then at the lamplit fields beyond, where the Pale Steward was even now gliding toward the gate with an expression Quentin couldn't read. Hope, maybe. Or hunger. After Hollowmere, she suspected they had stopped being different things down here.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "I do understand it. Better than you ever did."

"Oh?"

"Being unnoticed isn't the same as being *nothing*." She stepped forward, putting herself between Crane and Hollis, and felt Serah shift to flank her left without a word, without hesitation, and thought: *there's the oath she actually keeps.* "You've been feeding this place scraps for four generations and calling it mercy. Short winters. Full bellies. And underneath, a whole court starving for company, keeping souls like candles because nobody up there would sit with them for free." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "My mother sold me to buy time. Fine. I've made peace with that—mostly, on good days. But the crown sold *me too*, didn't it? Bought the debt, packaged it, called it employment. Ten years I tasted for a king who owned my soul the whole time. Ten years I thought I was invisible. I was just *inventory*."

Crane sighed, and for a moment the clerk's mask flickered, and Quentin saw something tired underneath it. Genuinely tired. "Do you imagine I enjoy this work? Four generations, girl. My grandmother signed the same seals. Someone keeps the winters short or everyone dies—slowly, stupidly, in famines and wars over grain. I am the *reason* there are children in Corvane who've never known hunger. If that requires a soul now and then—" He spread his hands. "You'd make the same trade. You did make it. Every morning you drank for the king."

"No," Quentin said. "I drank *so the king wouldn't have to*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. I took the risk onto myself. You put it on the poor, the desperate, the ones too tired to fight the collectors." She glanced at the Steward, drifting closer, lamplight pooling around him like spilled honey. "And the worst part? It isn't even working anymore. Is it, Steward? The winters are getting longer anyway. Early snows. Charms failing. Villages emptying. You're dying of loneliness, and the crown's answer is to feed you faster."

The Pale Steward's voice, when it came, was soft as ash settling. "The meals stopped filling me long ago. But the court above does not ask whether the fire wants wood. Only whether the house is warm."

"There," Quentin said to Crane, gesturing. "You hear that? Even your larder is telling you the recipe's wrong."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind Crane's eyes. Doubt, maybe, or its fossil record. Then the clerk's mask settled back into place, and he raised one hand, and the six guards leveled crossbows at the three of them, and Quentin realized with detached professional interest that she was about to die in the one place in the world where dying was complicated.

"Master Hollis," Crane said gently, "the consent stands. Take him to the Steward."

"No," said Hollis.

Everyone paused. Crossbows included.

"No," the old man said again, straightening his stooped back with the creak of a man who had bent over ovens for forty years and was done bending. "I gave my word thinking I was saving her. Now I see what I was feeding." He planted himself beside Quentin like a fencepost. "Consent withdrawn. Let's see your precious ledgers untangle *that*."

Crane's jaw tightened. "Consent given under deception is void," said a new voice—the Steward's, arriving at last, and oh, the cold satisfaction in it. "You of all people should know the terms, Inquisitor. Your grandmothers wrote them. A bargain entered falsely binds the *bargainer*." Lamplight rippled. "The gate opens one way for liars."

The mountain shuddered again, harder this time. Somewhere far above, Quentin would have sworn she heard snow beginning to fall.

Crane looked at the Steward. At the guards. At Quentin, standing in front of a soul he'd come to collect, wearing the face of a woman who had spent ten years pretending to be nobody and had apparently gotten extremely good at it at exactly the wrong moment.

Then he did the last thing she expected.

He smiled, reached into his robe, and drew out a small iron box stamped with the candle-in-crown sigil—the sigil from her own collar—and set it on the ground between them like a gambler laying down cards.

"Then we renegotiate," he said. "Steward. Ravensworth. Mage Vail. I carry the crown's original copy of the Ledger terms—including the clause about what happens to the soul-bonds when the Ledger burns." His eyes found Quentin's, and for the first time there was no warmth in them at all, only arithmetic. "Burn it, and every immunity, every protection, every purchased grace the crown ever bought comes undone at once. Including yours, taster. Including everything keeping you alive."

The iron box sat on the stone between them, small and patient as a seed.

Quentin looked at it, and at Serah, and at the lamplit fields where her other half waited, and thought: *well. Here's the flavor. Best find out if I can stomach it.*
