## 18. Winter's Heart

The inquisitor came through the sealed gate the way winter comes through a badly hung door: all at once, and taking the warmth with him.

One moment Quentin stood in the lamplit vastness of the Undercourt, her name still ringing in her ears from the ledger's page; the next, the far end of the cavern-city split along a seam that had no business existing, and cold poured through it like water finding a crack in a dam. The lamps—those soft, patient flames that had burned down here for who knew how long—all leaned toward the breach at once, like flowers turning toward a sun they hated.

"Ah," said Serah, in the tone of someone watching a very bad prediction come true. "Him."

"Him," Quentin agreed.

Master Corvane's inquisitor—Aldous Vane, though nobody above ground said his name aloud unless they wanted their tea to taste of iron—stepped through the seam wearing his grey coat and his greyer expression, and behind him came six of the king's cold-wardens in white hoods, and behind *them* came something worse: the smell of the court above, which was the smell of a bargain kept too long. Almond and ash.

"You've been reading my ledger," Vane said. His voice carried across the cavern without effort, the way a knife carries into bread.

"It's a very good ledger," Quentin called back. Her heart was hammering, but her mouth—the mouth that had spent ten years saying *nothing wrong with this dish, Your Majesty*—had apparently decided that fear could go hang. "Terrible penmanship, though. Whoever wrote 'Ravensworth, Q., one soul, paid in full' ought to be flogged."

"That would be your mother's hand."

"I know." The words landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there. "I recognized the flourishes."

The Pale Steward drifted between them before the silence could curdle. Up close—and this close, there was no help for it—the Steward looked less like a regent and more like a grandfather who had been left out in the rain for three centuries: tall, translucent, kind around the eyes in a way that made Quentin's throat hurt. The Steward's gaze went past Quentin entirely, to the burning seam in the air, and something ancient and tired moved across that face.

"Aldous," the Steward said gently. "You shouldn't have come. The gate was sealed for a reason."

"The gate was sealed so that *we* could keep our arrangement private," Vane said. "Souls for short winters. A tidy exchange, running four hundred years. And now this—" he flicked two fingers toward Quentin, the way a man flicks a crumb off his sleeve, "—a taster's daughter with ideas above her station, reading documents she has no right to."

"They're *my* documents," Quentin said. "My soul is in them. I checked the index."

Serah made a sound beside her that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Quentin didn't look at her. Looking at Serah right now would have been dangerous—not because of the wardens, but because Quentin had discovered, somewhere between the frozen river and the lamplit fields, that when Serah Vail stood at her shoulder she became approximately forty percent braver than was sensible, and sensible had gotten her this far.

Vane raised one hand. The cold-wardens raised theirs in answer, and frost crawled down the cavern walls in white veins, and every lamp in the Undercourt guttered at once.

The mountain shuddered.

Not a small shudder. Not an earthquake's polite cough. The whole mass of stone above them groaned like an old man asked to stand up quickly, and somewhere far overhead—a mile of rock, maybe more—something cracked with a sound like the sky clearing its throat. Dust sifted down through lamplight. In the distance, in the residential quarter of the dead where bargained souls kept little warm houses with little warm windows, a bell began to ring. Nobody had told Quentin the dead had bells. It seemed exactly the sort of thing they would.

"Stop," the Pale Steward said, and the word wasn't loud, but the frost stopped crawling anyway, mid-vein, as if embarrassed. "Child. Aldous. You are unspooling four hundred years of careful stitching. Every soul fed to this court has held a thread of the winter above us. Pull them out—" the Steward gestured, and Quentin saw it then, saw what she should have seen the moment Vane arrived: the lamps were dimming. All of them, everywhere, a slow tide going out. "—and the winter does not stay polite. It comes down the mountain. It comes for everything."

"Then I'll take my chances with a long winter," Vane said, "over a court that lets its stock read its own accounts."

"I'm not *stock*," Quentin said, and pulled the ledger off its stand.

It was heavier than a book that size had any right to be. It weighed like guilt. Four hundred years of names, each one written in a different hand—famine mothers and desperate fathers and kings who'd signed with rings instead of pens—and Quentin held it against her chest and felt it pulse faintly, like a sleeping animal.

"Quentin." Serah's voice, low. "What are you doing."

"What it says on the page," Quentin said. "I'm a taster. I know how this works. You don't fix a poisoned dish by negotiating with the poison."

She meant to burn it carefully. She meant to burn it with dignity, some small precise charm, the kind of quiet magic she'd sold in night markets for coppers. But the ledger, sensing her intent—or perhaps just tired, the way everything down here was tired—caught fire on its own.

Flames the color of candlelight-at-a-funeral ran up the pages, and each name as it burned made a small sound, and the sounds were not screams. They were sighs. Hundreds of years of sighs, released all at once, and the Undercourt breathed them in, and the mountain screamed.

* * *

Afterward, Quentin would remember the collapse in pieces, the way you remember a storm by its worst moments.

The ceiling cracking over the eastern quarter, and the dead pouring out of their little houses into the lamplit streets, looking up with expressions she couldn't read—fear? hope? the particular blankness of people who had already done their dying and hadn't budgeted for a second round?

Vane shouting orders to his wardens and discovering, with visible offense, that his frost-magic had gone strange down here, sputtering like a wet wick, because the Undercourt's warmth was rising to meet the emergency and it did not care whose side anyone was on.

And Serah—Serah grabbing Quentin's arm and hauling her behind a pillar of veined black stone as a slab the size of a market stall came down where they'd been standing, and saying, breathless, hair full of dust, "Next time, *warn me* before you commit arson."

"There wasn't time! It caught fire on its own!"

"Books don't spontaneously combust, Quentin."

"This one did. It's been holding four hundred years of other people's secrets. I'd combust too."

Another slab came down. Another dive. Somewhere in the chaos the Pale Steward's voice rose over the rumble—not panicked, never panicked, just vast and sorrowful, calling the dead to the central square the way a shepherd calls sheep in from weather. And the dead *went*, streaming toward that patient figure, and Quentin understood, with a lurch, that the Steward was trying to shelter them. All of them. Even now. Especially now.

"We have to get to the Steward," she gasped.

"Why?"

"Because if the bargain unravels, the court doesn't just die—it goes out. Like a lamp. And everything it's been keeping warm goes out with it." She met Serah's eyes. "Including, possibly, my half."

Serah's face did something complicated. "You don't even *have* your half yet. That was tomorrow's problem."

"I'm an efficient worrier."

They ran. Through streets where lamplight flickered like a stuttering pulse, past a house where a dead woman stood in her doorway calmly handing blankets to living strangers—Quentin took one without breaking stride; courtesy was courtesy—under archways that rained grit and older dust, until the central square opened up before them and the Pale Steward stood at its heart with hundreds of the gathered dead pressed close, and above them all the great vault of the cavern had begun, visibly, to come apart.

And Vane was already there.

He stood on the square's far side with his six remaining wardens arranged behind him, and he had found his footing in the chaos the way certain men always do, planting himself in catastrophe like a fencepost. Frost radiated off him in slow waves. He looked at the huddled dead, at the failing lamps, at Quentin sprinting toward him with soot on her face, and he smiled the smile of a man about to describe a problem as an opportunity.

"There she is," he said. "The solution to everything. One soul, freely given back into the court's keeping—renewed, re-signed, witnessed—and the balance restores itself. The winter shortens. The city above never knows how close it came. Everyone lives." He spread his hands. "Or everyone freezes. Your choice, Ravensworth. You always were good at choices. That's what tasting is, isn't it? Choosing what dies."

The words were designed to land. They landed. Quentin felt them sink in below the ribs where her mother's flourishes already lived.

But Serah stepped forward, one step, boots crunching on fallen grit, and drew herself up in her court mage's stance—the one Quentin had first seen across an interrogation room, all straight spine and cold authority—and said:

"Master Vane. By the oath I swore to the crown, I am obliged to inform you that your arrangement with this court constitutes trafficking in souls, prohibited under the Concord of Corvane, section nine, and that I have documented evidence of the same going back—" she paused, delicately, "—four hundred years. Would you like to hear my report?"

Vane stared at her. "You would burn your own career. Your own *oath*."

"My oath," Serah said, "is to the crown. Not to the men who feed it." A beat. "Also, I've grown extremely tired of lying in reports. It's remarkably freeing, telling the truth once. You should try it before the ceiling finishes coming down."

The mountain, as if in agreement, dropped another fifty tons of rock onto the eastern arcade.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way endings do.

Vane ordered the wardens to seize Quentin. Three of them obeyed; the other three looked at the dying lamps, at the weeping dead, at the enormous grieving shape of the Pale Steward, and quietly, deliberately, lowered their hands—one of them actually sat down on a rubble chunk, the way soldiers do when a war stops making sense mid-afternoon.

The three who advanced got exactly one step before Serah put a ward in their path—a wall of pale gold light, humming, and Quentin felt it through her boots and thought, absurdly, *she's been saving that*. Then Quentin herself did the thing she'd sworn never to do, the thing that ended invisibility forever: she planted her feet in front of the Pale Steward and the huddled dead, held up both hands, and let ten years of swallowed poisons rise in her like a lit furnace.

She didn't know what the display looked like from outside. From inside, it felt like every almond-laced cup and every tested dish and every quiet morning pretending to be ordinary finally standing up and being counted.

"Here's my choice," she said to Vane. "I'm not giving you my soul. I'm not giving you anyone's soul. This court isn't a pantry. It's—" she glanced back at the dead, at their frightened, patient faces, at the old woman with the blankets, "—it's a household. And households don't run on eaten children. They run on what people give. Freely."

The Pale Steward turned to look at her then, fully, and in that translucent face Quentin saw the thing the ghost-keeper at Hollowmere had tried to tell her: the loneliness. Four hundred years of being fed instead of loved. Four hundred years of warmth delivered like taxes instead of gifts.

"Oh," the Steward said softly. "*Oh.* You understand."

"Half," Quentin said. Her voice shook. Her knees shook. Everything shook, partly from fear and partly because the mountain was actively falling down. "When this is over—when the ceiling stops trying to kill us—I'll give half. Freely. No ledger. No signature. Just—" she shrugged, helpless, soot-faced, terrified, certain, "—half. To keep the lamps lit. Because somebody should be warm down here on purpose."

The Steward wept. It looked like lamplight falling upward.

And Vane—Vane screamed. Actually screamed, a high furious sound, and hurled everything he had left, all his hoarded frost and office and four hundred years of institutional spite, not at Quentin but at the Steward, at the source, at the last lamp—

—and Serah moved.

Later, Quentin could never quite reconstruct it. There was gold light, and there was Serah's voice reciting something old and structural, the mage's equivalent of putting her whole body between a blow and a child, and there was the sound the frost made when it broke—which was, strangely, the sound ice makes on a spring pond, a cracking that means *thaw*—and there was Vane on his knees in the rubble with his grey coat smoking and his power gone out of him like heat from an open door.

The ledger's ashes, which had been drifting through the square all this while in a slow grey snowfall of their own, settled at last over everything. Over the dead. Over the wardens. Over Vane. Over Quentin's hair, which she supposed was fitting.

The mountain gave one final, tremendous groan—and then, impossibly, held.

Quieted. Settled. Like a house after a storm deciding, tentatively, to remain standing.

In the ringing silence, the oldest of the dead—an old, old woman near the front of the crowd, so faded she was mostly outline—looked up at the darkened vault, then at the Steward, then at Quentin, and said in a voice like paper:

"Well. Someone had better teach that girl the rite properly. Before she halves herself *wrong* and leaves us the feet."

A ripple of dry laughter went through the dead, and Quentin laughed too, half-hysterical, and Serah's hand found hers and gripped it hard enough to mean several things at once.

Then the far gate—the one they'd come in by, the one Doss had died-to-buy them time through—shuddered, groaned, and began, with terrible slowness, to seal itself shut. Stone grinding over stone. Winter pressing down from above like a lid.

The Pale Steward turned toward it, and the lamplight in the cavern steadied, just slightly, just barely, like a heartbeat after a fever breaks.

"The way out is closing," the Steward said. "And child—your half-soul must be given *before* it seals, or not for a generation. Choose your footing. Quickly now."

Quentin looked at Serah. Serah looked at Quentin. Above them, the last of the ash-snow fell through the light, and somewhere overhead, muffled by a mile of stone, the long winter howled.
