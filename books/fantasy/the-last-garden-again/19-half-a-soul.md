## 19. Half a Soul

The mountain had stopped trying to kill them. That, Quentin thought, was somehow worse.

For three days it had shaken and roared and dropped the sky on their heads, as if the whole ancient engine of the Undercourt were a kettle left too long on the fire. Now it was still—too still—the way a kitchen goes quiet just before the master of the house walks in. The lamplight of the cavern-city burned steady and low along the walls of the great hall, ten thousand little flames in glass bells, each one a soul that someone above had sold or lost or forgotten. They flickered when she passed. Not menacingly. The way dogs lift their heads when someone kind comes down the street.

Quentin's hands would not stop shaking, so she held them very still against her apron, out of old habit. Aprons steadied her. Ten years of tasting other people's breakfasts had taught her that fear was mostly a matter of where you put your hands.

The Pale Steward stood at the far end of the hall, beside the Ledger of Souls—or what remained of it. Quentin had set it burning an hour ago with a candle stub she'd bought from herself, years ago, at a night market, for a woman who never gave her name. Grief burns slow, she'd promised the woman. It doesn't go out until you're done with it. Apparently the same was true of ledgers. Pages that should have been ash still curled and glowed in the iron basin, names surfacing like fish rising to feed, then sinking into black.

The inquisitor lay crumpled at the Steward's feet. Not dead. Quentin had checked—she checked everything now; it was the one professional habit she intended to keep. Just emptied. Whatever power the crown had fed him through its generations of stolen warmth, whatever cold contract let him walk through sealed gates and drink poison at his leisure and call himself justice—all of it had unraveled when the ledger began to burn. He looked smaller than he had in the throne room. He looked, Quentin thought uncharitably but with some satisfaction, like a man whose breakfast hadn't agreed with him.

"Quentin."

Serah's voice. Serah Vail stood three steps away, close enough to catch her if she fell, far enough not to crowd her, which was exactly the distance Quentin had come to understand meant *I love you* in Serah's particular dialect. Her court mage's badge was gone from her collar. She'd torn it off somewhere back in the Pass of Teeth and hadn't spoken about it, and Quentin hadn't asked, because some things are better left as actions rather than announcements.

"I know what you're going to say," Quentin said.

"You don't."

"You're going to say there has to be another way."

"There is another way," Serah said. "We walk out. Right now. The gate behind the Steward's chair opens onto the high pass. The winter's breaking—you can feel it, even down here. We leave the court its ledger-ash and its lamps and we go home, and we plant something, and we grow old arguing about whose turn it is to weed."

"That's not another way," Quentin said gently. "That's a different book entirely."

"Quentin—"

"The court is dying." She said it quietly, because it deserved quiet, the way a diagnosis does. "Not of hunger. Not of malice. Of loneliness. You heard the innkeeper at Hollowmere. You saw what I saw in the ledger. Every bargain this place ever struck was struck by something so starved for company it would trade warmth for warmth forever, and every soul it kept made it lonelier, because a kept thing can't love you back. My mother didn't sell me to a monster, Serah. She donated me to a hospice. And I—" her voice did something traitorous, wobbled, and she pressed her lips together and continued, "—I am apparently the only person in ten thousand years who's figured out how to leave a place without taking her whole self with her."

"You don't know that the rite will work."

"I know it's forbidden," Quentin said. "Everything forbidden is either wicked or necessary, and I've met me, and I'm not wicked."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of Serah, which had been the entire point. Quentin took one step toward the Steward, and the ten thousand lamps brightened—not all at once, but in a wave, like wind moving through wheat, like the whole cavern-city leaning toward her.

The Pale Steward had not moved in an hour. It stood the way furniture stands, tall and gray and patient, wearing robes the color of the moment just before snowfall. Its face was the kind your memory refuses to hold—kind, you were fairly sure it had been kind—but now, as Quentin approached, its features gathered themselves, the way a host smooths his coat before answering the door.

"You read my confession," the Steward said. Its voice was dry paper and warm hearth at once.

"I read my mother's confession," Quentin corrected. "You just kept it."

"She wrote beautifully. For a frightened woman. Frightened women often do." The Steward folded its long hands. "You burned the ledger."

"Somebody had to. You weren't going to."

"No," the Steward agreed, and there was no argument in it, only an enormous, geological tiredness. "We do not burn our own debts. That is why we are owed so many."

Quentin looked up at it—at him? She decided on *him*, because the lamps seemed to like it better—and thought about all the bargains stacked beneath this mountain like cordwood. The king's grandfather's grandfather trading a winter of short snows for a cellar of souls. The inquisitor's office drinking strength through a straw stuck into this place. Her mother, nineteen years old and starving, signing away the daughter she hadn't met yet because the alternative was watching her whole family go out like candles. And above all of it, holding the whole rotten architecture together, one immortal creature who had never once, in all those centuries, been told *you may put it down now.*

"I'm going to teach you a word," Quentin said. "It's 'no.' You're allowed to say it. I checked. There's no clause anywhere in any of this that forbids it. The forbidding was all upstairs."

The Steward went very still. Even the lamps held their breath.

"What I'm offering isn't charity," she went on, because pity would have insulted them both. "Don't make that face. I've seen the face people make at charity, and it's the worst face there is. This is a trade. Fair terms. I split my soul—I keep half, the living half, the half that tastes almonds and bickers with court mages and intends, someday, to be very bad at gardening. The other half stays. Warm. Wanted. Not kept—*wanted*. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's ever been a guest instead of a hostage."

"And the rest of us?" the Steward said. "The ten thousand?"

"They go free too. The rite doesn't work otherwise—I read the innkeeper's copy of it twice, and it's very clear. A soul given freely warms a place. A soul kept does nothing but smolder. Half of me, freely given, will light this whole city for as long as it wants lighting. But the doors open, Steward. All of them. Whoever wants to climb toward the spring can climb. Whoever wants to stay and tend the lamps can stay. Freely. Both directions. Forever." She shrugged, and her voice cracked slightly on the shrug. "I'm a taster. I know a poisoned dish when I've served one. This one's clean."

The Steward looked at her for a long time. Then it did something she had not seen it do in all their encounters: it sat down. It folded itself onto the cold stone steps of its own dais, robes pooling, and put its face in its long gray hands, and its shoulders shook.

Serah made a small sound. Quentin understood it. It was the sound of watching something too large to cry finally cry anyway.

"We were young once," the Steward said into its hands. "Before the mountain. Before the crowns came asking. We were a court of the small warm things—the hearth-spirits, the threshold-keepers, the ones who blessed bread and remembered birthdays. Then the famine came, and the kings learned our name, and they came down with contracts instead of candles." He lifted his head. His eyes were lamp-colored. "Do you know what it is, child, to be loved by no one freely for four thousand years?"

"Yes," Quentin said. "Scaled down considerably. About a decade's worth." She knelt so they were eye to eye. "Which is why I'm qualified."

She turned. "Serah."

Serah came to her at once—no hesitation, badge-less, oath-less, hers.

"If I get stupid halfway through," Quentin said, "if the splitting takes more than I've got and I start to fade—hold onto the half that's rude. That part's load-bearing."

"I'll hold all of you," Serah said. "The rude parts especially. They're my favorites."

"Gross," said Quentin, whose eyes were stinging. "In front of the immortal regent of the dead, honestly."

The rite, as the innkeeper's ghost had taught it, required almost nothing. No circle, no blood, no incantation in a language with too many consonants. It required only a truth, spoken whole, while standing between two fires—one that wanted you to stay, and one that wanted you to go. Everything else, the ghost had said, is decoration. Souls know how to do this the way rivers know how to fork. They only need permission.

There was no altar fire here, but there were ten thousand glass-belled lamps, and beyond them, far up the throat of the mountain, the crack in the sealed gate through which real daylight was leaking—gray and thin and smelling of thaw.

Quentin stood between them.

She spoke the truth. All of it, out loud, in front of God and the dead and the woman she loved and a weeping regent and an unconscious inquisitor who would wake up eventually and be furious about all of this:

"My name is Quentin Ravensworth. My mother named me after a flower that grows in ruins. She sold my soul before I was born to keep her family fed, and it wasn't wicked, it was desperate, and I forgive her—mostly; I'm working on the rest. I spent ten years being nobody on purpose, and it kept me alive and it hollowed me out, and both of those things are true. I am good at small magics and bad at being thanked. I love Serah Vail, who arrested me in her heart at least forty times before she kissed me. I want to live. I want it so badly my teeth ache. And I want—" her voice broke, and she let it, "—I want this lonely old place to have one warm thing that was *given*, for once in its whole miserable history. So. Take the half I don't need to walk. Keep it well. It likes honey in its tea and it snores."

She breathed in.

The world split.

There was no pain—that was the strange part, the part she would spend years failing to describe. There was a sensation like setting down a basket she'd forgotten she was carrying. Like finishing a shift. Like the last swallow of soup on a cold day, warmth going down and spreading outward, except the warmth was leaving *outward*, pouring down through the stone floor into the roots of the lamplit city, and the ten thousand lamps caught it and flared—

—and the mountain *sighed*.

Every soul in the Undercourt felt it, though only two of them were alive to remember it afterward. The pressure that had lived under the rock since before Corvane had walls—the hunger, the leak, the wrongness that had pushed the winter early across the whole north—it released. Somewhere far above, snowpack that had been strangling the passes for weeks settled into honest, ordinary drifts. Somewhere above that, a king woke from a dream of endless cold and could not remember why he'd been afraid.

And in the great hall, the Pale Steward wept.

Not gracefully. Not the way statues weep in poems. He wept the way a man weeps who has been carrying furniture up stairs for four thousand years and has just been told the house is paid for. He wept lamp-oil and candle-smoke and something older than both, and the lamps wept with him, ten thousand flames bowing and lifting, bowing and lifting, and Quentin—half of her, the walking half—stood in the middle of it laughing and crying at once, saying, "Oh, don't, oh no, if you start I'll start, I *taste things professionally*, I can't be seen crying in front of the staff—"

"You are the staff," Serah said, holding her up, holding her together, holding her, all of her, the whole remaining half.

"Then promote me."

* * *

The inquisitor woke at dawn-down-the-mountain, which is to say when the first true gray light crept through the cracked gate, and found himself lying on cold stone with no power, no office, and a headache like a cathedral falling on him.

He tried to speak the word of binding that had opened mountains for his order for nine generations.

What came out was: "*Please.*"

He tried it again, horrified. "*Please.* Please, what is—someone fetch me my—"

"No one fetches you anything," Serah said. She crouched beside him with the pleasant, terrible smile she usually saved for paperwork errors. "Your order's contracts burned with the ledger. Your authority died with them. As of this morning you're just a man in expensive robes who trafficked with the dead to warm a throne." She patted his shoulder, twice, briskly. "The good news is you'll live. The better news is you'll have to earn it, like everyone else."

The inquisitor opened his mouth—to threaten, to plead, Quentin couldn't tell—and what emerged, wobbling and astonished, was the truth: "It *hurts*. Being empty. How do you all *stand* it?"

"Practice," said Quentin. "And breakfast. Come on. There's a long climb and I hear the spring is unreasonable this year."

They left him his dignity, such as it was, and a share of the supplies, because Quentin Ravensworth had spent a decade feeding people who didn't deserve it and saw no reason to change a winning formula.

At the gate—the real gate, the old one, standing open for the first time in an age—they paused. Behind them the Undercourt glowed like a coal banked for the night: warmer than it had ever been, its lamps steady, its doors open. Some of the dead were already climbing the far passages toward the light, hesitantly, hand over hand, like swimmers testing water. Some stayed, gladly, tending the flames. The Pale Steward stood at the threshold of his own hall, and when Quentin looked back, he raised one long gray hand—not in farewell, she realized. In blessing. Or possibly in thanks. Or possibly, given the look on his lamp-lit face, in the way one waves off a guest who keeps insisting on hugging everyone before leaving.

"Visit," he called, in a voice like a hearth learning to laugh. "The door is open now. Both ways."

"Send word if the flowers need anything!" Quentin called back.

"Half of you lives here, child. The flowers will be *magnificent*."

They climbed.

The passage rose through ice that was losing its argument with the sun, dripping, groaning, letting go. And near the top, where the gray light thickened to gold, Quentin stopped—stopped dead, grabbed Serah's sleeve, and pointed.

Growing straight up through a seam in the ancient stone, impossibly, absurdly, in the freezing throat of a mountain that had been sealed for a generation: a single flower. White, faintly luminous, petals tipped with a glow like the last ember of a lamp.

It smelled like her mother's garden.

"Oh," Quentin said. Her voice came out very small. "Oh, that's mine. That's—she's already growing things."

Serah looked at the flower, then at Quentin, then back at the flower. "You split your soul six hours ago."

"I told you," Quentin said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve, laughing, terrified, alive, half, whole, climbing. "She always did take after her mother."

Above them, past the last of the ice, the sky was doing something it had no business doing in midwinter: turning blue. And far below, carried up through the warming stone, faint as a heartbeat, the Undercourt was humming—a lullaby, maybe, or a thank-you, or simply the sound of ten thousand souls remembering, all at once, what it felt like to be warm on purpose.

The last of the snow gave way under Quentin's boots, and she stepped through into the light.
