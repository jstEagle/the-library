## 20. The Thaw That Shouldn't Come

The last thing the mountain gave them was a shove.

Serah felt it as a tremor through her boots, then through the stone itself—a long exhale from somewhere far below, as if the Undercourt had turned over in its sleep and decided, finally, to let them go. The passage they had climbed for what felt like a year but was probably three days tilted upward, and the air changed.

She noticed it before she could name it. Warmth. Not the lamplit, borrowed warmth of the cavern-city below, which had felt like standing near someone else's hearth, but warmth that belonged to nothing and no one. Warmth that was simply *there*, unowned, unreasonable.

"Quentin," she said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable like ice on a pond.

Ahead of her in the dark, Quentin Ravensworth stopped climbing. Serah could see almost nothing of her—just the shape of her shoulders, the pale smear of her face when she glanced back—but she could feel her listening. Ten days of shared road and shared terror taught you that much about a person: how they listened.

"Do you smell that?" Quentin asked.

Serah breathed in. Her eyes stung. "Grass."

"Dirt," Quentin corrected, with the particular authority of a woman who had spent ten years pretending to be a food-taster while secretly being a gardener's daughter. "Wet dirt. Thawed dirt." A pause. "It shouldn't be thawing. It's midwinter up there."

"I know."

"It's *impossible*."

"I know," Serah said again, and found, to her own surprise, that she was laughing. It came out of her sideways, breathless, slightly unhinged—the laugh of a woman who had watched a ledger of souls burn, confronted a king's inquisitor in a cavern full of the patient dead, and discovered that the crown she'd served for fifteen years had been feeding children to a lonely court beneath the mountain to keep its winters short. "Quentin. Everything we've done since the pass has been impossible. At this point I think impossibility is simply the terrain."

Above them—above being a word she now used loosely, gratefully, the way you might use *home* about a house you'd been evicted from—there was a gray patch in the rock. Then the gray patch became an opening. Then the opening became sky.

Serah climbed out of the mountain into spring.

* * *

It was not the spring of almanacs. There were no primroses, no returning larks, no gentle green suggestion at the field-edges. It was midwinter everywhere else in Corvane; she knew that the way you know your own heartbeat. And yet here, in the high valley below the mountain's southern shoulder, the snow was pulling back from the ground in slow, astonished retreat, like a crowd giving way around royalty. The grass underneath was not green exactly—it was the green of things remembered very hard, a green with intent behind it. Halfway down the slope, a single tree stood in impossible blossom, white petals drifting onto snow.

Quentin stood beside her and said nothing for a long time.

Then: "That half of me," she said quietly, touching her own sternum, where—Serah supposed—you would touch it, if you had left half your soul behind in a lamplit city under a mountain to keep a dying court company forever. "It's doing this. Or the court is. Or—" She shook her head. "I don't have the words yet. I've never needed words for anything this large. The largest thing I ever had words for was almond extract."

"Bitter?" Serah asked.

"Laced with something worse," Quentin said, and smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked and alive, and Serah thought: *I burned my whole life down and this is the first morning I've been glad to be awake in years.*

She did not say that. She was still, in her bones, a woman who filed reports.

The thought arrived and sat down heavily inside her, wearing its official badge.

* * *

They made camp that night—or evening, though the light refused to behave like either—in the shelter of the blossoming tree, because Quentin declared that any tree blooming in defiance of the calendar deserved guests, and Serah had learned on the road that Quentin's declarations, delivered mildly, over a cup of tea made from melted snow, were not to be argued with. They ate hard bread and the last of the cheese Doss had pressed on them at the pass, and the petals came down around them like a slow snowfall that smelled sweet instead of cold.

"You're going back," Quentin said eventually. It wasn't a question. She said it the way she tasted a dish—carefully, waiting for the bitter note.

Serah looked at the fire. In the mountain, she had confessed everything: the tutoring, the mother, the report she had written as a child of eleven, believing with all the terrible clarity of a child that rules were rules and that a woman selling charms in a famine winter must be doing something wrong. She had confessed it expecting Quentin to leave. Quentin had instead been furious for two days and then, somewhere around the frozen river, forgiven her—not absolved her, never that, but folded her back in, the way you keep a cracked cup because it holds tea just fine if you're careful.

So she owed her honesty now. Honesty was the only currency they dealt in anymore.

"The crown will want a report," Serah said. "The inquisitor is dead or worse than dead. The bargain is broken. Winters will run long until the world learns to carry its own warmth again—and there are people in Corvane who will need to understand why, or they'll find someone to blame. Someone small. Someone forgettable." She looked up. "Someone who tastes the king's breakfast."

"They'll want their mage back," Quentin said.

"They'll want their mage *kneeling*," Serah corrected. "Back is optional."

"And?"

And. Serah reached into her coat, where the badge had lived against her heart for fifteen years, a small cold weight of silver and oath. Court Mage, Second Circle, Sworn to the Crown of Corvane. She had polished it every week. She had once believed it was the best thing about her.

She set it on the flat stone between them, where the firelight caught it.

"I climbed out of that mountain," she said, "and the first thing I felt was relief. Do you know what I felt the second thing? Dread. Because relief like that isn't allowed. Relief like that is *evidence*." She turned the badge over with one finger. "I swore an oath to protect the realm. I have just learned the realm has been kept warm by stolen souls—that my king's grandfather signed the first ledger page, that my own training was paid for out of the same account. Every short winter I congratulated the crown for managing, every harvest festival I attended in my good cloak—I was applauding the theft." Her voice did something embarrassing. She let it. Quentin didn't look away, which was its own kindness. "I can't file this report, Quentin. If I go back, I'll have to choose between lying—which I've already done once, and hated—or telling the truth and watching them bury it under me. There's no version where I return and remain anyone I'm willing to be."

"There's a version where you return and fight," Quentin said gently. "From inside. You'd be good at it. You're infuriatingly good at institutions."

"I am *good at* institutions," Serah agreed. "That's the problem. Institutions are how the crown ate souls for six generations without anyone tasting the poison." She paused. "You'd know about tasting poison."

"I'd know about pretending not to notice it," Quentin said. "For ten years. So believe me when I tell you—" she reached across the fire and closed Serah's fingers back over the badge, folding it into her palm like a secret passed hand to hand— "the noticing was never the sin. The pretending was. Don't go back and pretend."

Serah held the badge. It had gone warm from the fire, or from her hand, or from some third thing she refused to speculate about in front of a woman who now technically contained a piece of an underground court.

"If I stay," she said slowly, working it through aloud, the way she worked through everything, "I'm exiled. Formally, eventually, officially. My name goes in a different kind of ledger."

"Mm," said Quentin, examining her tea.

"People will hunt me. Less urgently than they'd have hunted you, but with more paperwork."

"Naturally."

"I have no money beyond what's in this coat, no trade except magic the crown trained me in, and no references whatsoever."

"No," Quentin agreed serenely.

"I am, in short, a catastrophic risk to whatever quiet life you were planning to build."

At that, Quentin looked up, and the firelight and the petal-light and whatever other light was in her these days all caught together in her face. "Serah Vail," she said, "ten days ago you lied to your own superiors for me, broke your oath for me, walked into hell for me, and offered to *stay in hell* for me, which frankly I'm still angry about, and you're sitting there listing your liabilities like I'm going to check your references." She refilled Serah's cup without asking. "I ran a charm stall in a night market for a decade. I know exactly how to assess what a person brings to an enterprise. You bring a mage's education, a conscience with teeth, and the only honest report anyone's written about Corvane in six generations." A pause, and gentler: "And you bring yourself. That was always the item I couldn't price."

Serah drank her tea so she wouldn't have to say anything with her mouth, since her eyes were already saying quite enough.

* * *

In the morning, the badge went into the earth.

It had been Quentin's idea, naturally. Everything sensible was. They dug a small hole at the foot of the blossoming tree—Serah insisted on digging it herself, properly, with her hands—and Serah laid the silver in it, oath-side up, and covered it with thawed black soil that steamed faintly in the cold air, soil that should not have been thawed, soil that smelled like every garden she had ever failed to grow.

"It's not a grave," Quentin said, watching. "Badges don't die. It's a seed."

"That's not how seeds work."

"How would you know? Have you ever planted one?"

"...No."

"Then it's a seed," Quentin said with finality, and patted the earth down flat, and Serah laughed—really laughed, the unguarded kind, the kind that cost nothing because there was no longer anyone nearby to dock her pay for it.

They stood a moment in the falling petals. Somewhere downslope, water was running where water had no business running in midwinter, chuckling over stones like a fool who hadn't heard the news.

"What do we do now?" Serah asked. It was, she realized, the first question in fifteen years she had asked without knowing the approved answer in advance. It felt like taking off boots after a long march. It also felt like falling. Both sensations at once. She was learning that most true things came in pairs.

Quentin shaded her eyes and looked south, toward the lowlands, toward Corvane, toward everything they were not going back to.

"Now," she said, "we walk down into a country that thinks it's winter, and we keep walking until we find a garden somebody let die, and we bring it back. And if anyone asks who we are—" the crooked smile again— "we give our real names. All of them. Even the ones nobody's heard since I was seven years old."

"That's not how exile works."

"Neither is spring in midwinter," said Quentin, shouldering her pack. "Come on, Vail. The thaw won't wait for us, and I've spent ten years being on time for people who didn't deserve it. We're going to be late for something that does."

Serah took one last look at the badge's small mound, at the tree dropping white petals onto the snow like letters from underground—*we are well, we are warm, dream of us kindly*—and turned south.

Behind them, faintly, absurdly, the first bee of an impossible spring began to hum.

And ahead, at the valley's edge where the road bent toward the ruined holdings outside Corvane, Serah saw—she would swear later she saw—a figure on horseback, watching them, wearing a watchman's coat with its badge conspicuously missing.

"Well," said Quentin, squinting. "Isn't that interesting."
