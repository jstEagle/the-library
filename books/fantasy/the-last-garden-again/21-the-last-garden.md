## 21. The Last Garden

The gate had not been opened in eleven years.

Quentin knew this because it took her the better part of an hour to convince it otherwise—first with her shoulder, then with a crowbar Serah found in the inn at Hollowmere's stable, and finally, when both failed, with the small honest charm she had sworn off using for anything but locks and grief. The key that remembers locks was the first charm she'd ever sold, back when she still pretended her magic didn't exist. She'd kept one for herself all these years without ever quite admitting why.

The lock sighed open like an old man settling into a chair.

"Eleven years," Serah said behind her, "and the thing that finally defeats it is sentiment."

"It's not sentiment. It's professional courtesy between a lock and a key."

"You're crying."

"I'm sweating. It's a warm day."

It was, in fact, an absurdly warm day—the first of the thaw-that-shouldn't-come, three weeks past their climb out of the mountain, and the world outside Corvane's walls had gone green with an urgency that bordered on rude. Grass shoved up through old cart ruts. Buds split their casings overnight. The whole valley smelled like wet earth and possibility, and Quentin, who had spent ten years tasting other people's food for hidden poison, kept tasting the air instead and finding nothing in it but spring.

Beyond the gate lay her mother's garden.

She had expected ruin. She got ruin, yes—roses gone feral and strangling each other, a fig tree fallen across the herb beds, the stone bench where her mother used to shell peas cracked clean through by frost—but under the ruin, the garden's bones were still there, patient as handwriting. The raised beds. The trellis her father had built before Quentin was born. The little stone basin where rainwater gathered even in drought years, which her mother had always insisted was luck and which Quentin now suspected had been a very small, very quiet bargain of its own.

Her mother had bargained everything else away. Why not the rain?

*Don't,* Quentin told herself firmly, kneeling to press her palm flat against the soil. *You didn't come here to be bitter. You came here to plant.*

Though God knew there was plenty to be bitter about, if she wanted it. The ledger. The confession in her mother's own hand. *I did not sell my daughter to the dark,* her mother had written, in ink gone brown as old blood. *I bought her from something worse.* And beneath that, the name of the creditor above ground—a name that had made Serah go pale and quiet and then, after a long silence, say: "That's the crown's privy purse. That's the king's *moneylender*, Quentin."

They had agreed, walking down out of the pass with Doss's supplies and Doss's absence like a stone in each of their pockets, that they would deal with that later. That word—*later*—had done more work than any mule on the journey.

But later was not today. Today there were seeds in her pack, wrapped in cloth that still smelled faintly of lamplight and deep earth, carried up out of the Undercourt in the pocket over her heart. Seeds from the fields the dead tended below the mountain, glowing faintly gold-green even through the wrapping, half-alive because she had made them so—or rather, because half of her had stayed below and refused to let go of them entirely.

She unwrapped them carefully, like something sleeping.

"They're warm," Serah said. She had crouched beside Quentin without being asked, which was how Serah did most things—arriving precisely where she was needed while pretending it was coincidence. "Quentin. They're *warm*."

"That would be me." Quentin turned one seed over in her palm. It pulsed gently, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. "Or the half of me I left down there. Turns out you can't give away half your soul to a dying court of lonely dead people and not have it... leak a little. Into things. Into seeds, apparently."

"And you're going to put that in the ground."

"I'm going to put that in my mother's garden, yes."

"Is that wise?"

Quentin laughed—it came out rusty, a hinge unused to swinging. "Serah Vail. Former court mage of Corvane. Sworn hunter of unlicensed magic. Asking me if planting enchanted seeds is wise."

"Former," Serah said, with dignity, "court mage. And I surrendered my badge, so technically anything you do is no longer my problem."

"Oh, wonderful. Then I'm planting them everywhere."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was the best afternoon of Quentin's life, which she recognized even as she lived it, the way you recognize a good vintage mid-sip.

Serah turned the soil with a competence that surprised them both. ("I grew up on a farm," she admitted, not looking up. "Before the Academy. Before—" she gestured vaguely at her whole self, her history, her guilt. "Turns out some things stay in your hands.") Quentin pulled the feral roses off the trellis and found, underneath, a climbing pea vine still alive, stubbornly green, ten years untended and somehow surviving—which felt less like botany and more like a message from her mother, and made her sit down in the dirt for a moment until her eyes stopped stinging.

"You're sweating again," Serah observed.

"Warm day."

"Mm."

They planted the seeds along the south bed, where her mother had grown marigolds. Quentin dug each hole with her hands, no trowel—she couldn't have said why, except that it seemed important the seeds go into the ground touched by skin, by the half of her that remained above. Each one went down warm. Each one settled into the dark like a child into blankets.

When the last one was in and watered from the stone basin, they sat on the broken bench—cracked down the middle, but two halves make two seats—and watched the bed do absolutely nothing, which was somehow the most suspenseful thing Quentin had ever witnessed.

"How long?" Serah asked.

"For normal flowers? A week or two."

"And these?"

"I genuinely don't know. They've never been above ground before. They've never been *mine* before." Quentin chewed her lip. "Down there, they grew in lamplight for the dead. Up here they'll grow in sun for—for whoever wants them, I suppose. For anyone who needs something alive that glows a little in winter."

"A candle that burns grief," Serah said quietly. "You used to sell those."

"I used to sell them to people who never gave me their names." Quentin looked out over the ruined beds, the crooked trellis, the mountains standing guard to the east—the mountain, singular and terrible, wearing its first snow-free cap in a generation. "I think I'd like to try selling things to people who do give their names. Seems like it might be nice. Knowing who's buying."

"You'd be terrible at it. You spent a decade perfecting forgettability."

"I did spend a decade perfecting forgettability." She grinned. "Time for a new specialty. I'm thinking: memorable."

"God help us all."

The first seedling broke the surface at sunset.

They both saw it happen—one moment bare soil, the next a pale green hook of stem pushing up, unfolding two leaves that caught the last light and held it, glowing faintly gold at the edges, like paper held near a flame without burning. Then a second seedling. A third. By full dark, the whole south bed was stipulated with small lights, dozens of them, breathing slowly, and the garden that had been dead for eleven years looked like a piece of the Undercourt's lamplit fields had been cut out and laid gently down in the world above.

Quentin cried. Openly this time, and didn't call it sweat.

"There she is," Serah said softly. "The woman who saved a steward's life and pretended nothing happened."

"She never thanked me properly, you know. Old Marlow. He sends me honey every year and pretends he doesn't know why his tea started tasting different."

"Honey is a perfectly adequate thank-you."

"Honey is a coward's thank-you," Quentin said, wiping her face, laughing. "But I'll take it."

* * *

Doss arrived two days later, at dawn, which was exactly like him—he had spent twenty years on the city watch arriving places either too early or too late, never once on time, on the theory that punctuality attracted attention.

He came up the road from Corvane on foot, watchman's coat gone, wearing a farmer's jacket that fit him like a borrowed apology, and carrying a sack over one shoulder. Quentin saw him coming from the gate and stood very still, hands dirty, heart doing something complicated.

He stopped ten feet away. Looked at the garden. Looked at the glowing flowers. Looked at her.

"Thought I'd find you here," he said.

"Did you. How?"

"Your mother's garden. Where else does a person go after the end of the world?" He shifted the sack. "Also the inquisitor's men checked every road out of the valley except this one, because nobody guards a dead woman's house. So. Luck."

"Luck," Quentin echoed, thinking of the stone basin, and decided to accept the word.

Doss reached into his coat and produced, with great ceremony, a folded paper. Even from ten feet she could see the royal seal, the heavy black script, the word *WARRANT* doing its best to look imposing. He unfolded it, smoothed it out on his knee, and tore it in half. Then in quarters. Then he kept tearing it, methodically, until it was confetti, and scattered it into the wind, where the pieces drifted down among the glowing seedlings like strange, bureaucratic snow.

"The charges were dropped," he said. "Officially. New regency council, first act of business, everyone pretending the last fifty years were someone else's fault. Unofficially—" he shrugged. "Unofficially, the warrant was void the day I decided it was. Been void for weeks. I just liked carrying it around. Felt useful."

"Doss."

"Yeah."

"You hid evidence for me. You lied to your own sergeants. You walked into the Pass of Teeth with a blizzard coming and told us to leave you behind to mislead the pursuit, and you sat in a frozen hut for six days eating wax cheese—"

"It was a *fine* cheese."

"—and you never once asked for anything. Not thanks, not explanation, not—" She stopped. Her voice had gone somewhere unreliable. "Why?"

Doss considered this with the gravity he brought to all questions, including what to have for lunch. He had bought a candle against grief from her once, years ago, in the night market, and lingered too long, and she had never learned what he grieved, and he had never offered it. Some purchases stayed private. That had always been their arrangement.

"My wife," he said now.

Quentin went still.

"Died eight years back. Winter fever. The candle you sold me—I burned it the first year, and then I couldn't burn it anymore, so I kept it. Lit it once a year. Just to remember her voice." He looked at the glowing bed, and his weathered face did something soft. "These ones smell like her kitchen. Bread, almost. I don't know how, but they do."

"The half of me down there keeps things warm," Quentin managed. "Maybe it keeps smells, too."

"Well." He cleared his throat violently, a man re-holstering his feelings. "Anyway. I brought things." He upended the sack: tools, a hoe, seed potatoes, a jar of honey. "Marlow sends his regards. Says honey's a coward's thank-you, whatever that means, so this time there's a letter too, and he says thank you, properly, in writing, and if you tell anyone he cried he'll deny it to his grave."

"He cried?"

"Big baby about it. Whole thing."

Serah appeared at the gate then, farm-dirt to the elbows, and surveyed the scene: the torn warrant scattered among glowing flowers, the sack of gifts, Doss standing awkward as a fence post in his borrowed coat.

"You're staying," she said. Not a question.

"If there's work." Doss rubbed his neck. "Watch doesn't want me back—I'm a man known for losing paperwork. And I figure a garden that grows light out of half a soul might need guarding. Quiet-like. I'm good at quiet."

"He's terrible at quiet," Quentin told Serah.

"He's excellent at quiet," Serah said. "He followed us for three chapters of our lives and we only noticed twice."

"That's not—how long were you—"

"Potatoes need planting," Doss said firmly, and picked up the hoe.

* * *

By midsummer the garden was unrecognizable.

The glowing flowers had spread themselves—no one had asked them to; they simply seeded where they pleased, gold-lit constellations across beds that had held marigolds, then across the herb rows, then, outrageously, up through the cracks in the courtyard stones. Travelers began stopping at the gate. First one or two, drawn by rumor of lights visible from the road; then more, because the flowers did something no one could explain: they grew brighter in the presence of sadness. Grieving folk walked the rows at dusk and left lighter. Nobody could prove why. Quentin had her suspicions. Half of her was down there keeping the lonely court warm, and it occurred to her more than once that warmth, given away, has a way of traveling.

She started selling charms again in the late summer. Honest ones, priced fairly, from a table set up by the gate with a hand-painted sign: *CHARMS & SEEDS — REAL NAMES PREFERRED, BUT NOT REQUIRED.* The key-that-remembers-locks. Candles against grief. A small bell that rang only when someone lied to you kindly, to spare your feelings, so you could pretend not to know.

Customers gave their names now. Most of them, anyway. She wrote them in a ledger—her own ledger, hers alone, with nothing sold or bartered in it but candles and seeds—and there was a particular satisfaction, deep and slow as roots, in keeping a book where every entry was freely given.

Some evenings, Serah taught letters to the miller's children on the broken bench. Some evenings, Doss walked the rows with a lantern he didn't need, humming. And some evenings—the best ones—Quentin knelt in the south bed as the light came up out of the ground, and pressed her hand flat against the warm soil, and listened.

Not hearing, exactly. More like the memory of hearing. Like a heartbeat through a wall.

*Well?* she would think. *How is it, down there?*

And the flowers would brighten, just slightly, all together, like a roomful of people turning toward a window. And whether that was an answer or just the evening breeze or her own half-soul saying hello to itself, she had decided she didn't need to know. Some things you tend without understanding. Gardens. Souls. People.

One evening in early autumn, as she was thinning the seedlings, the flowers nearest the gate flared suddenly bright—all of them, in a wave, rippling toward the road like wind through wheat—and Quentin looked up to see a traveler standing at the gate she'd left unlocked on purpose, a young woman with a worn pack and the look of someone who had walked a long way to ask a question she wasn't sure she wanted answered.

"You're the taster," the young woman said. "The one who split her soul. They tell stories about you in the villages. About the garden." She swallowed. "I need to know if it's true. What they say. That you can help someone get back what was taken from them."

Quentin set down her shears.

She thought of a map pressed into dying hands. Of a debt older than she was. Of the long road from invisible to rooted, and how it had begun exactly like this—with a stranger at her door, and a question, and a world cracking open.

She smiled, wiped her hands on her apron, and gestured toward the bench.

"Sit down," she said. "Tell me your name."
