## 22. Again, From the Beginning

The shop on Wrenlane had been a cobbler's, once, and then a chandler's, and then nothing at all for six years, which was how Quentin could afford it. The sign above the door she painted herself: **RAVENSWORTH'S — SEEDS, SALVES, SMALL HONEST CHARMS**. Beneath it, in smaller letters, because she believed in full disclosure now: *No souls bought, sold, or accepted in trade.*

Serah had argued the disclaimer was unnecessary.

"People asked," Quentin said. "Three times in the first week."

"They were joking."

"The third one wasn't."

It was spring again—the kind of spring that still made people stop in the street and look up at the sky as if checking it for forgery. Seven years since the thaw that shouldn't have come, and Corvane had learned to treat every mild morning like a gift left anonymously on the doorstep: pleasant, suspicious, not to be questioned too closely lest it be taken back.

Quentin stepped out into it with a tray of seedlings and tasted the air the way she once tasted the king's breakfast. Old habits. Bitterness of last night's frost, fading. Green things waking under it, honest green. Smoke from the bakery two doors down, which meant Marta was burning the first batch again, which meant the day was proceeding according to the oldest and most reliable law of the universe.

Nothing poisonous. Nothing wrong. Just morning, offered freely, no bargain attached.

She stood there a moment longer than she needed to, tray balanced on her hip, letting it sit on her tongue. Somewhere below the mountain—half a mountain away and half a world down—a court of lonely lamps was dreaming, warmed by the half of her she'd given away. She liked to think the dreaming went well. Some mornings she could almost feel it, like warmth through a wall shared with a sleeping friend.

"Those are going to wilt," said a voice behind her, "if you stand out here philosophizing at them."

* * *

Doss came by most mornings now, ostensibly to buy vegetables, actually because his route to the watchhouse passed the shop and he had redefined the word *route* with impressive bureaucratic creativity. He was captain of the watch these days. He still looked embarrassed about the warrant.

"I tore it," he'd told her, years ago, standing in her mother's ruined garden with the paper in pieces in his fist like a man who couldn't decide whether to confess or compost. "I should've torn it sooner."

"You should've," Quentin agreed. "But you didn't use it when it mattered, and then you did tear it, so on balance—" She'd handed him a trowel. "You can balance by digging."

He dug. He'd been digging, in one way or another, ever since. The garden outside the walls was his parish now; he went out twice a week and came back with dirt under his nails and an expression of profound contentment that he tried, always, to disguise as grumbling.

This morning he set a basket on the counter and began unpacking it with the ceremony of a man presenting evidence. Turnips. A sack of barley. A jar of honey with a label in his own blunt handwriting: *FROM THE BEES THAT LIKE ME NOW.*

"They like everyone now," Quentin said. "That's what happens when winter stops being a punishment. Everything relaxes."

"The bees never punished me. They just had standards." He glanced around the shop—at the shelves of seeds in labeled jars, the salves, the small rack of charms each tagged with its true effect and its true price. He picked up one of the tags and read it aloud. "'Candle, grief-slowing. Burns four hours. Will not bring anyone back. Will make the four hours survivable.' You know, I remember when you'd have sold that candle without saying a word about what it did."

"I remember when I'd have sold it to you without saying a word," Quentin said, "and charged you double because you lingered."

"I lingered because you were interesting."

"You lingered because you were lonely. Everyone who came to my night stall was lonely. That was the whole customer base. Loneliness, four coppers." She took the tag back and straightened it. "Now it's free information. Turns out honesty is cheaper. Nobody tells you that."

Doss grunted, which from Doss was applause. Then, more quietly: "Any word?"

She knew what he meant. Every spring he asked, and every spring she gave him the same answer, and every spring he asked anyway, because that was Doss—he believed in asking after things the way other men believed in prayer.

"She's well." Quentin touched the windowsill, where a pot of pale flowers sat glowing faintly, the way embers glow after the fire has gone to bed. Undercourt stock, grown from seeds carried up in her pocket, half-alive with the half of her soul she'd left below. In sunlight they dimmed, shy. At dusk they woke. "I felt her this morning. Content. Or whatever counts as content, down there. She's got company now. More every year."

That was true, and it was the part of the story nobody in Corvane fully understood, though the crown's new historians were trying. The old bargain had fed souls *to* the Undercourt to keep winters short—harvested, taken, stolen. Quentin's rite had been different: a gift, freely split, freely given, and gifts multiplied. Every person in the city who lit a lamp for someone gone, every widow who planted flowers over a grave, every child who whispered goodnight to a dead grandmother—the warmth of those small devotions leaked downward, the way water finds roots. The Pale Steward had wept when the ledger burned. Now, Serah reported (she made the descent every few years; the mountain no longer sealed; bargains ended opened roads), the Steward kept a garden of her own.

"Content," Doss repeated skeptically. "Half your soul is living under a mountain with a weeping regent and a bunch of lamps, and you call that content."

"It's the half that always wanted to stay home," Quentin said. "Turns out I was the one who wanted to leave."

* * *

At midmorning the school children came through, as they did every week, herded by their teacher and immediately ungovernable.

The teacher was Serah Vail, formerly court mage of Corvane, formerly the most feared name in three ministries, currently a woman with chalk on her sleeves and a class of eleven-year-olds who called her Mistress Vail when they remembered and Miss Serah when they forgot, and who had surrendered her badge, her tower rooms, and her oath in that order on a bright impossible morning seven springs ago, walking out the gates beside Quentin with nothing but a satchel and an expression of terrified relief.

She taught history now. True history, which was to say: short lessons, long silences, and questions the children weren't supposed to be able to ask yet but always did.

Today's lesson, per the chalkboard visible through the open door, was **THE OLD WINTERS AND WHY THEY ENDED**, and today's question, per tradition, was delivered by the smallest girl in the class, a gap-toothed creature named Pell:

"But if the king *knew*—"

"He knew," Serah said. She had stopped flinching at that sentence somewhere around year three. "Some of them knew. Not all. The inquisitors knew. The treasurers knew. The good kings suspected and chose not to finish the thought, because finishing the thought would have required them to do something, and crowns are very heavy when you're carrying them uphill."

"My gran says the old kings were monsters."

"Your gran is right and also incomplete. They were frightened men who inherited frightened men's bargains and lacked the imagination to break them. That's not an excuse. It's a warning." Serah tapped the chalkboard, where she'd drawn the mountain with its pass and, beneath it, a careful sketch of lamplit caverns. "Here's what I want you to carry out of this room. The Undercourt wasn't a monster either. It was lonely. Loneliness makes kingdoms do terrible things—under mountains and over them both. Somebody eventually has to be brave enough to answer loneliness with something besides fear."

Pell raised her hand again. "Was it you? Were you brave enough?"

Serah paused. Quentin, listening from her doorway across the lane with the particular shamelessness of a woman married to her own curiosity, watched Serah consider lying, reject it, and settle—finally, permanently, the way a bone settles—into truth.

"No," Serah said. "When I was young, I did a cowardly thing believing it was brave. I reported a woman to the authorities, and my report cost her everything. Her name was Maren Ravensworth." A ripple through the class; they all knew the name; it was on the sign across the street. "For years afterward I told myself I'd been doing right. The truth is I was doing obedient. Those aren't the same thing, and learning the difference cost me twenty years and nearly cost me the best friendship of my life. So no, child. The brave one owed me nothing and saved me anyway. That's the lesson. Write it down. There will be a quiz."

There would not be a quiz. There was never a quiz. But the children wrote it down, because when Miss Serah used that voice, even the pigeons took notes.

Afterward, when the class had scattered toward lunch, Serah crossed the lane and leaned in the shop doorway, chalk-dusted and tired in the good way.

"I heard that," Quentin said, not looking up from her potting. "'Obedient versus right.' You've gotten good."

"Gotten?" Serah said. "I was always good. I was just pointed the wrong direction for twenty years." She picked up one of the glowing flowers, turned it gently in her fingers. It leaned toward her like a cat. "They're brighter this year."

"Every year brighter." Quentin wiped her hands. "She's dreaming well, I think. The half of me down there. Or—I don't know how to explain it. It's not like remembering. It's like being remembered. Warmly. From a long way off."

"Do you ever regret it? Splitting?"

Quentin considered the question properly, because Serah deserved proper consideration and always had. "Do you regret leaving the tower?"

"Every second Tuesday."

"And the rest of the time?"

"The rest of the time I teach children that the world can be mended, and then I come home to you, and I think: yes. This. Whatever this cost." Serah set the flower down. "So."

"So," Quentin said, "no. I don't regret it. I gave away half my soul and got back—" she gestured: the shop, the lane, the wife in the doorway, the smell of bread, the whole ridiculous ordinary morning— "everything I'd been pretending I didn't want. Best trade I ever made. And I never even haggled."

"That's because you'd finally met someone worth paying full price to."

"Flatterer. Go wash the chalk off your hands, you look like a ghost."

"A ghost with tenure," Serah said, and kissed her, and went to wash.

* * *

Spring deepened. The flowers in the window grew so bright that customers started asking if they were enchanted, and Quentin gave her standard answer, which was the only kind of charm she sold anymore: "Everything alive is enchanted. The trick is noticing."

On the evening of the year's longest light, they walked out past the walls to the old garden—Maren Ravensworth's garden, unruined now, or ruined in the way gardens are supposed to be, which is to say growing. Doss was already there, of course, pretending to inspect the fence. The glowflowers had spread along the south bed in a river of soft blue-green light, and as the sun went down they woke, all together, the way they did, a held breath released.

Quentin knelt among them and pressed her palm flat to the warm soil.

*Well?* she didn't say, because it wasn't words, it had never been words. It was the taste of a dish offered freely. It was morning air with no bitterness in it. It was the feeling, familiar now as her own heartbeat, of being warmly remembered from a long way off—and tonight the memory felt different. Brighter. Busier. Almost—

Almost like laughter.

She sat back on her heels. Serah crouched beside her. Doss pretended very hard to examine a fence post.

"She's got news," Quentin said slowly. "Down there. Good news. I can't— it doesn't come in words, but it's big, and it's happy, and it's—" She laughed, surprised at herself, at the wetness in her eyes. "Serah. I think she's dreaming us a message."

"What kind of message?"

Quentin looked out over the glowing beds, at the light rising off them like heat-shimmer in reverse, like something climbing patiently toward the surface after a very long time underground. All along the garden row, every flower had turned—not toward the sunset, not toward the moon, but toward the mountain. Toward home.

"I think," Quentin said, "that spring is coming *up* this year. From underneath. And I think we'd better get the garden ready for company."
