## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgets, and I have learned to let it.

That is the first thing you must understand about Hollowmere, if you insist on understanding it at all: it is not that magic cannot come here. It is that magic, upon arriving, forgets why it bothered. Spells unravel at the boundary stones like knitting dropped by a careless grandmother. Enchantments arrive full of purpose and leave as ordinary weather. A witch once flew over the ridge on a broom — this was thirty years ago, back when I still counted such things as insults rather than curiosities — and by the time she touched down in my turnip field she was simply a woman sitting on a stick, looking profoundly embarrassed.

I gave her tea. She left before noon, walking, and did not thank me, which I thought rude until I remembered she'd have to climb the ridge on foot to get her flying back.

My name is Noor Iversen, and for nineteen years I have been the last oath-bound swordspeaker of the Order of the Verdant Blade, which is a very grand way of saying I am a woman who talks to a sword and the sword has stopped answering. Not because it's sulking. Because words are a kind of magic, and the valley eats them the way it eats everything else.

I keep a garden. It is a good garden. It is, in fact, the only thing about me that has ever flourished without argument.

* * *

Mornings begin with frost or the threat of it, depending on the season's mood, and this morning had committed fully. I came out at first light with my breath hanging around me like a scarf I couldn't take off, and I walked the rows the way my mentor Maren used to walk them: heel to toe, slow enough to notice what had changed overnight.

Something always changes overnight in a garden. This is its chief virtue over people.

The winter kale had survived — of course it had; kale has the survival instincts of a debt collector. The late beans were done, their pods gone papery, and I pulled them and set them aside for seed. The rosemary had gone woody at the base but smelled, when I crushed a sprig between my fingers, like every summer I had ever known, all of them here, all of them quiet.

"Good morning," I said to the garden.

I say that every day. Not because I expect an answer, but because Maren said it, and her mentor said it, going back eleven generations of speakers who believed that a tended thing should be greeted, even if — *especially* if — nothing answers. Courtesy, Maren said, is the last spell that works everywhere. Even here, where it means only what it says.

Here is what I do not say to the garden: that there were once ninety-three of us. That we kept a chapterhouse on the coast where the swords sang actual songs at dawn. That when the crown-thing took King Aldric thirteen years ago, it began eating oaths the way a fire eats a dry barn, and our order was made entirely of oaths, and so we burned. Not all at once. Oath by oath, speaker by speaker, until the ones remaining scattered like sparks and the last few of us ran.

I ran furthest. I ran here.

"You're talking to vegetables again," I told myself, which is the kind of conversation one has after two decades alone, and went inside for porridge.

* * *

The house was Maren's before it was mine, and before that it belonged to no one, because the valley belonged to no one, because the valley was where the Order sent things it wanted forgotten — including, in the end, itself.

Inside, above the hearth, hangs my oath-blade.

I should describe it properly, because it deserves description even if it never speaks again. It is a longsword of northern make, older than any kingdom currently pretending to matter. Its name is Vesper. For the first twenty-six years of my life, Vesper spoke to me — not in words exactly, but in the language underneath words, the one speakers hear in the marrow rather than the ear. It told me the weight of coming weather. It hummed when lies were near. On the night the order fell, it screamed loud enough that I heard it three valleys away, and I have never entirely stopped hearing the shape of that scream, the way you never stop hearing the shape of a bell after it cracks.

Now it is rust. Or nearly. There is rust along the fuller like dried blood, and the grip-leather has gone stiff as old bark, and when I oil it — every seventh day, without fail, because ritual is the spine of a person whether or not anyone is watching — it does nothing at all.

I oil it anyway. You may draw whatever conclusions you like about what that says regarding me.

This morning being the fourth day since the last oiling, I left it be, ate my porridge standing up because sitting felt too much like settling, and went out to mend the south fence, which the goats had been testing with the patience of lawyers.

* * *

I should mention the goats. Their names are Turnip, Cabbage, and Second Turnip, and they hate me with the pure uncomplicated hatred that goats reserve for everyone who feeds them. They are also the closest thing I have to a congregation, which tells you something about my current station in life.

The fence needed three new posts and a great deal of muttered profanity, which is another kind of courtesy, if you're generous about definitions. By midday the sun had burned off the frost and the valley lay open under it like a palm turned upward: the terraced fields of the two farmsteads downriver, the smoke from their chimneys standing straight in the windless cold, the boundary stones ringing the whole bowl of it like a necklace of grey teeth.

Nine stones. One at each compass point and five more where the old surveyors judged the valley's edges needed reinforcing. Each stone taller than a man, each carved — though the carving has worn to suggestion now — with interlocking rings. Maren called them the Quiet Knots. When she sealed the valley, nineteen years ago, she walked the circuit of them barefoot in the snow, speaking the binding into each one, and when she finished she could not walk at all for a week, and within the year she could not speak above a whisper, and within three she was dead of it, and her last full sentence to me was:

"Don't mourn the silence, girl. Silence is just a room. What matters is what you build inside it."

I have been building ever since. Some days I even believe it's enough.

* * *

The tremor came in the afternoon.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance I have left, and because for a while afterward I tried to convince myself it hadn't happened, and lying to oneself requires good notes.

I was at the third boundary stone — the western one, the one I pass most often on the way to the high pasture — checking the mortar at its base, which the freeze-thaw cycle chips away at every winter like a patient creditor. My hand was flat against the stone. And the stone moved.

Not much. Not visibly. If you had been standing there you would have seen nothing, heard nothing, noticed nothing, and gone home thinking Noor Iversen is odd but harmless. But under my palm, deep in the grain of it, something *shivered*. The way a sleeping dog shivers. The way the ground shivers when something enormous passes far away, too far to see, its footsteps arriving after it has already gone.

And then — this is the part I keep returning to — the stone forgot.

I felt it happen. The faint warmth of the binding that lives in these stones, the knot Maren tied, the thing I have leaned against metaphorically for nineteen years the way you lean against a wall without noticing the wall — it stuttered. Like a lamp guttering. Like a word someone starts to say and then loses.

For perhaps two heartbeats, the valley was not sealed.

I know how that sounds. Two heartbeats of unsealed valley, out here where nothing magical has managed to survive longer than a picnic. What could possibly get in? What could possibly get *out*? The answer to both questions, in nineteen years, has been nothing. The valley forgets. That is its whole nature and its whole mercy.

But I am a swordspeaker, even a rusted one, and there is a sense in me older than speech, the one that hears the language underneath words, and that sense went off like a struck bell — one clear note, there and gone — and in that note I heard, or imagined I heard, or dreamed I heard, something on the other side of the stone.

Listening.

Then the knot tightened again, the wall of silence closed, and the stone was just a stone, cold and dumb and dependable, and the afternoon went on being an afternoon, and a crow crossed the sky doing whatever crows do instead of working.

I stood there a long time. Long enough that my hand went numb against the rock. Long enough that Turnip found me and began eating my bootlaces, which is the goats' traditional method of expressing concern.

"It's nothing," I told her. "Old stones settle."

Turnip did not believe me either, but she is a goat, and her skepticism costs her nothing.

* * *

That evening I did something I almost never do. I lit the good candles — beeswax, saved for reasons I've never been able to justify — and I took down the box.

The box sits under a floorboard beneath the hearthstone, and it contains everything I own of the Order of the Verdant Blade: Maren's primer, wrapped in oilcloth; a signet ring sized for a thumb I no longer have; a letter I have never opened, addressed in Maren's handwriting to *whoever survives me*, which I have decided counts as a riddle rather than an instruction; and a folded list of names.

Ninety-two names. Mine is not on it. I have never decided whether that is humility or cowardice, and I suspect the answer changes depending on the season.

I sat with the box open on my knees and the good candles burning and I did not read the primer and I did not open the letter. I just sat, the way you sit with a grave. Outside, the light went from gold to iron to nothing, and the stars came out in the terrible clarity they only achieve in places with no magic and no city, and somewhere downriver a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Here is the arithmetic I performed, sitting there, because I perform it every year on this night and this year I performed it early:

Thirteen turns of the moon ago — thirteen years, the villagers say, though they don't know why they count from that winter, they just do, the way you count from a wound — something put on the king's head and stayed there. Thirteen years ago the order died. Nineteen years ago Maren sealed this valley and bought me safety at the price of everything I was trained to be.

And tonight, for the first time, the seal stuttered.

Coincidence, said the sensible voice, the voice I have cultivated like the rosemary, hardy and unsentimental. Old stones settle. Old knots slip. The world outside has been tearing itself apart for thirteen years and it will go on doing so without consulting you, and your job — your sworn job, the last clause of a broken oath — is to stay here and keep the garden and guard the silence and die quietly, the last leaf on a tree nobody remembers planting.

"Quiet loneliness," I said aloud to the empty room, "is not peace. It's just loneliness with better posture."

The house did not disagree. Houses here can't. That's rather the point.

* * *

But I'll confess the rest, since no one is listening: I did not put the box away. I slept with it beside the bed like a dog, and in the night I woke twice, certain I had heard something.

The first time it was the wind. Wind in a valley sounds like many things, and most nights I enjoy the company.

The second time — well past midnight, the fire down to its red arithmetic — the second time it was not the wind. It came from the west, from the direction of the third stone, and it was not a sound so much as the memory of a sound, the bell-note I'd felt under my palm, arriving late like thunder after lightning. One clear note. And underneath the note, so faint I could have invented it, so faint that I have already half-convinced myself I did:

a voice. Small. Young. Speaking a language that died before the first kingdoms had names.

Speaking it clearly. Getting closer.

I lay in the dark with my heart going like a smith's hammer and I told myself: frost on the eaves. A branch. The valley forgets, Noor. Let it.

Then I got up anyway, and took Vesper down from above the hearth — rust and all, mute and heavy and familiar as my own grief — and stood at the window watching the western dark, where the frost was beginning, weeks too early, silvering the garden row by row like something being written.
