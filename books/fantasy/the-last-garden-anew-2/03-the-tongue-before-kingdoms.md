## 3. The Tongue Before Kingdoms

The child slept for two days.

I know because I counted the hours the way you count anything when you're pretending not to worry — badly, and with too much tea involved. I put her in my bed, which meant I spent those two nights in the chair by the hearth with a blanket that smelled of rosemary and forty years of stubbornness, waking every time she sighed to check that she was still breathing. She was. Children are sturdier than they look. It's one of nature's small jokes at the expense of anyone who has ever loved one.

I did not touch her palm again. The scar sat there under the skin like a thing buried alive, and I had no intention of being the fool who dug it up twice.

On the morning of the third day, she woke, looked around my bedroom with the frank appraisal of a cat deciding whether a house is worth conquering, and said something in the old tongue that made me drop the teapot.

Not all of it. I caught maybe one word in three, the way you catch the shape of a song heard through a wall. But *one word in three* was already an obscenity, because there should have been nothing. The valley forgets, as I've said. Magic doesn't follow here; it doesn't even loiter. And the first kingdoms' tongue was not a language so much as it was magic wearing clothes — every word a small working, every sentence a spell with its sleeves rolled up. My order had spoken it the way other people spoke prayers: constantly, carelessly, until the day we couldn't anymore.

The last person who'd said a word of it aloud had been Maera, my mentor, twenty-nine years ago, sealing the boundary stones with her dying breath and her final sentence: *Let this place be deaf.*

So when the girl opened her mouth and the room *leaned toward her* — when the firelight bent a fraction off true and the water in the basin shivered though nothing had touched it — I understood two things very quickly.

First: the valley wasn't deaf anymore.

Second: I was in a great deal of trouble, and most of it had freckles.

* * *

"You're supposed to be dead," I told her, in ordinary speech, because if the world was going to stop making sense I saw no reason to make sense back at it.

She considered this. "You're supposed to be a story," she answered — in my language, accented oddly, each word set down like a stone on frozen ground. So she could speak mine too. Of course she could. Why would anything be simple?

"Stories don't get cold feet."

"Hers got colder," she said, and something crossed her face — quick, dark, gone before I could name it. *Hers.* Someone else's feet. A mother's, maybe. A sister's. I filed it away next to everything else I didn't ask about, and the shelf was getting crowded.

Her name, she told me over breakfast — porridge with honey, because children who nearly freeze on your doorstep deserve sugar whether or not they're omens — was Wren. Just Wren. No family name offered, none asked for. She ate the way children eat when they've known hunger recently enough to remember it: fast, watchful, one hand curled near the bowl as though the spoon might be confiscated.

"And where do you come from, Wren-just-Wren?"

"Far." A pause. "The king let us go."

"The king," I repeated. There is only one king whose capital lies within walking distance of anywhere, and his name is Aldric, and he has ruled for thirteen turns of the moon, which is either a coincidence or a knife waiting to happen. "That was generous of him."

"He didn't mean to," she said, into her porridge.

I set down my own spoon. "Wren. What did the king do?"

And she looked up at me with eyes the color of rainwater over slate and said, in the old tongue this time, words I hadn't heard in thirty years and understood anyway, the understanding arriving in my chest rather than my ears:

*"He wears something that isn't him."*

The fire cracked. The kettle sang. Outside, the frost on the garden beds glittered like the world's most innocent jewelry, and inside, an eight-year-old had just described the exact nightmare my order had been founded to prevent, using grammar older than any kingdom currently on the map.

"Well," I said, when I could speak. "More tea, then."

It was not my finest moment. But you'd be surprised how often more tea is the correct answer.

* * *

Over the following week, three things happened, roughly in order of how much they frightened me.

First, Wren stopped flinching. Not entirely — she still moved through my cottage like someone perpetually ready to apologize for existing — but the flinch softened into something smaller. She learned where the cups lived. She learned that the third floorboard by the door groans and that I refuse to fix it out of sentiment. She learned that when I said *stay out of the herb beds*, I meant the ones with thorns, and that the strawberries were fair game, and that this distinction, delivered gruffly, was apparently the same as being handed a key to the whole property. By the fourth day she was talking to the bees. They talked back, in their way. Bees are terrible gossips and excellent judges of character, and they approved of her, which annoyed me, because I was trying to maintain professional distance from what I had already privately begun to think of as *the child*.

Second, her speech grew clearer. Not just her grasp of my language — that improved daily, the way children's does, absorbing grammar like rain — but the other thing. The old tongue came out of her in fuller sentences now, and when it did, the air around her did things. Candles burned steadier. Cut flowers in a jar refused, politely, to wilt. Once I found her in the garden saying something soft and syllabic to the bare apple tree, and I swear on my oath-blade — rusted, decorative, mostly symbolic these days — that the buds swelled while I watched. In late autumn. On a tree that hadn't fruited properly in a decade.

I stood behind the fence post like a coward and watched her do it, and felt something in my chest crack along an old seam.

Because here is the truth I haven't said plainly yet: I missed it. The speaking. I was seven when Maera began teaching me, and I was good — not gifted, exactly, but *faithful*, which in my order counted for more than brilliance. Swordspeech wasn't waving hands and shouting lightning. It was precision. It was meaning every word so completely that reality had no choice but to agree with you. I spent my childhood learning to mean things absolutely, and then at nineteen I swore an oath to never mean anything absolutely again, and I have spent thirty years being deliberately imprecise in my own head, and do you know what that does to a person? It makes them tend gardens. It makes them talk to bees and pretend they aren't lonely. It makes them wake at dawn to check frost damage on plants nobody will ever see, because tending something is the closest a silenced speaker can come to speech.

Third — and this is the part where I stopped sleeping well — Wren started dreaming aloud.

* * *

They weren't nightmares, exactly. That would have been simpler. Nightmares you can comfort. What she had instead were recitations: she would sit up in the dark, eyes open but unfocused, and speak in a voice that was hers and yet pitched slightly wrong, slightly *older*, delivering long rolling phrases in the first tongue with the cadence of legal documents. Contracts. Oaths. And always, threaded through them, one recurring phrase that my half-remembering ear kept snagging on:

*"...sworn and sworn and sworn again..."*

I took to sitting outside her door at night with the oath-blade across my knees, which was ridiculous — the blade was iron and rust and hadn't tasted a working since before she was born — but a sword in your lap is less about use than about company, and I wanted the company.

One night she came out to me instead. Bare feet on cold flags, blanket around her shoulders, utterly unbothered by the hour. Children have no respect for brooding. It's one of their few flaws.

"You sit there every night," she said.

"I'm guarding you."

"From what?"

"Good question," I said. "Sit down. You'll catch your death."

She wedged herself into the chair beside mine — my chair, the usurper — and pulled the blanket around both of us, which was presumptuous and warm and I allowed it. For a while we watched the moon move behind thin cloud.

"Noor." She'd learned my name by then; she pronounced it with great care, like a word from the old tongue itself, which in a way it was. "What's an oath?"

Ah. There it was. Children ask questions the way archers loose arrows: directly, at whatever moves.

"It's a promise," I said, "that costs something."

"Costs what?"

"Depends on the promise. Some cost effort. Some cost comfort." I turned the blade over in my hands, watching firelight slide along the ruined edge. "Some cost everything, forever, and you pay it gladly at the time, and then you spend the rest of your life finding out exactly how much *forever* weighs."

She absorbed this with the terrifying seriousness of the young. Then: "Did yours cost everything?"

"Yes."

"Are you sorry?"

I should have lied. I was getting practice at lying lately, and needed it. Instead I said, "Ask me again in a year," and she nodded as though that were a perfectly reasonable answer, and maybe it was, because neither of us knew yet what the coming year would take.

Then she held out her hand, palm up, the scar pale in the firelight. "It talks," she said simply. "In dreams. It says it's almost finished counting."

"Counting what?"

"Moons. It's at thirteen." Her brow furrowed. "No. Twelve. Thirteen comes soon, and then—" She stopped, pressed her palm against her sternum as though holding a door shut from inside. "Then it stops counting and starts *doing*. I don't know doing what. It won't say. It laughs."

An eight-year-old describing the whisperings of a thing I had hoped was legend. My tea went cold in my hands, which was fine, because my blood had done the same.

"Wren," I said carefully. "This thing that counts. Does it have a name?"

She shook her head. "Older than names, it says. Older than names, hungrier than names." Then she yawned — a jaw-cracking, boneless, eight-year-old yawn, the kind that undoes all horror in a room — and leaned against my shoulder, and within four minutes was asleep, trusting me completely, this stranger, this last of a dead order, keeper of secrets she couldn't possibly understand.

I sat there a long time after the fire had burned low, with a sleeping child against my arm and a rusted oath across my knees, and I admitted to myself what I had been refusing to admit since the night she arrived at my gate half-frozen and impossible.

I knew what she was. Or I knew what she resembled, which in my trade amounted to the same thing. A living key. A vessel. My order had written about such things the way engineers write about load-bearing walls: with respect, with dread, and with diagrams.

And I knew where the book was.

* * *

Here is what Maera made me swear, on the last night, while the boundary stones drank her final working and the valley sealed around us like a wound closing: that I would bury the primer. Not burn it — burning a primer is its own kind of event, and she didn't trust the valley's new silence to muffle it — but bury it, deep, beneath the roots of the oldest apple tree, and never dig it up, and never teach from it, and never, *never* speak the first tongue inside the stones.

"Why keep it at all?" young-Noor had asked, nineteen and grieving and angry.

"Because forgetting and never-knowing are different things," Maera said. She was already gray around the lips; the sealing was taking her payment in installments. "A buried book is a memory with dirt on it. An ash heap is just an ash heap. If the world ever needs remembering—" she coughed, smiled with half her mouth, "—well. It won't. Go to bed, Noor. Tend your garden. Grow old somewhere quiet."

She died at dawn with her hand on the largest boundary stone, looking, for the first time since I'd known her, at peace. I buried the primer at noon. I did not cry until evening, and then only briefly, because grief was also a kind of noise and the valley seemed to want quiet.

Thirty years. The tree had grown fat and crooked over that spot. Every autumn its apples came in small and hard and sharp as conscience, and every autumn I thought, idly, in passing, the way you think about a splinter you've stopped feeling: *it's still down there.*

I waited until Wren was asleep, and then I went out into the frost with a lantern and a spade, feeling like the villain in every cautionary tale I'd ever heard, and I dug.

The roots fought me. Of course they did — the tree had spent three decades growing *around* its burden, knitting itself into a cage, and I had to cut away more root than I liked, wincing at each stroke as though the tree could feel it, as though I were performing surgery on an old friend. Maybe I was. The whole garden was a friend. That had always been the point of gardens.

Three feet down, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed linen and a layer of lead foil that Maera had insisted upon, the primer waited. Smaller than I remembered. Dark green leather gone black with damp, silver lettering on the spine worn to ghosts. *Vox Prima: Being a True and Complete Account of the First Tongue, Its Works, and Its Perils.* Compiled by hands nine hundred years dead. Banned by three kings, mourned by one order, buried by one tired woman in a valley full of apples.

I carried it inside and set it on the table between the teapot and the salt cellar, and it sat there radiating the specific gravity of forbidden things, and I poured myself a cup of tea I had no intention of drinking, and I stared at it for the better part of an hour.

Every reason not to open it presented itself in orderly formation, like knights drawn up for review. *You swore.* True. *Oaths cost.* Also true; see previous chapter of my life, all thirty volumes of it. *If you open it, you're admitting the world outside has changed, and if the world outside has changed, then Maera died for a seal that failed, and if the seal failed—*

If the seal failed, then everything I had been — gardener, widow-of-an-order, quiet old woman with dirt under her nails — was a costume, and underneath it I was still what I had always been: a swordspeaker with no sword-speech and a war she'd been hiding from since before this child was born.

From the bedroom came a sound. Small. Sleepy. Wren, talking in her dream again, and this time I caught the phrase clean through the door, in the first tongue, in that voice pitched wrong:

*"Sworn and sworn and sworn again — thirteen is the number, thirteen is the door—"*

I broke the wax seal.

The cover fell open to the first page, and there, in ink that had outlasted empires, the opening line my mentor had made me copy a hundred times as a girl, until my hand knew it better than my own name:

*All oaths are doors. Beware what walks through yours — and beware, above all, the first door ever opened, for it was never closed, only dressed in crowns.*

I read that line three times. Then I looked down the hall toward the child sleeping the sleep of the conscripted, her scarred palm curled open by her cheek like a small pale lock waiting for whatever key the universe intended to try next.

Thirteen turns of the moon. A king wearing something that isn't him. And now a primer, breathing dust and doctrine into my kitchen, telling me in a dead scholar's voice that the first oath ever sworn had never actually ended.

Outside, far past the ridge, past the boundary stones, past the whole sleeping world of tithe-villages and bell towers, something laughed in a child's dream.

I turned the page.
