## 4. What the Oath Binds

I was seventeen the first time I lied with my whole heart, and it was to myself, and it went like this: *this is an honor.*

They dressed it up well, I'll grant them that. The Hall of Speaking had been built by people who understood that awe is mostly architecture. Nine pillars of white stone, each carved with a word so old that the carvers had needed permission from the words themselves. The floor was laid in a spiral, so that every oath-taker walked the same path inward, closer and closer to the center, where the Masters waited in their grey robes with their grey faces and their one grey concession to ceremony: a single candle, burning with a flame the color of dawn.

My mentor walked beside me. Maren was not grey. She wore her swordspeaker's red the way other women wore defiance — sleeves rolled, collar open, boots that had outlived three pairs of their owner's patience. She smelled of woodsmoke and the particular iron tang that follows you when you've been handling an oath-blade all morning.

"Stop bouncing," she said.

"I'm not bouncing."

"You're vibrating. It's very distracting. If you're going to be nervous, be nervous quietly, like a professional."

"I'm not nervous."

Maren looked at me then — that long, level look she had, the one that made you feel like a garden she was deciding whether or not to weed. "Noor," she said, "in forty years of speaking, I have never once met a person about to swear the oath who wasn't terrified. The ones who claim otherwise are either lying or stupid, and the oath has a way of finding out which." She paused at the edge of the spiral. "It's allowed to be both, incidentally. Lying and stupid. Half our order managed it for centuries."

"That's very comforting, Master Iversen."

"I'm not your master yet. That's rather the point of today." And then, quieter, because Maren saved her quiet voice for things that mattered: "Last chance to walk away. Nobody thinks less of you. Well — some people would think less of you, but those people are idiots, and you'll never need to see them again."

I should tell you what I knew then, which was almost nothing, though I'd have fought you over the word *almost*. I knew that the order spoke to blades the way farmers speak to weather — familiarly, warily, with respect for what could go wrong. I knew that a swordspeaker's word could unmake an oath, break an enchantment, still a cursed thing mid-hunger. I knew there were perhaps two hundred of us left in the world, down from thousands, and that everyone agreed this was someone else's fault.

And I knew — because every child raised near the order knows, the way children know where the sharp things are — that magic did not enter the valley of Amberdale. That the boundary stones stood in a ring older than the kingdom, older than the first kingdoms, and that nothing crossed them that carried power. Not spell, not spirit, not curse. We were, the elders said, the safest place in the world, which is a thing people only say about places where something once went terribly wrong.

I stepped onto the spiral.

The stones were cold through my sandals, and as I walked, the hall did its trick — the one every initiate whispered about and no elder confirmed. The pillars began to hum. Low, at the edge of hearing, like a struck bell remembered rather than heard. Each pillar held one word of the first tongue, and each word woke as I passed it, and by the time I reached the center I could feel them all behind my teeth, patient as seeds.

Three Masters waited for me. In the middle of them, on a cushion of grey cloth, lay a sword.

Not mine. Not yet. A plain blade, unadorned, its steel dark with age — but even my untrained eyes could see that the metal moved. Slowly. Like water deciding whether to be ice. Oath-blades remember every word ever spoken into them, and this one had heard a great deal.

"Kneel," said the eldest Master, whose name I have honestly forgotten, which tells you something about how much of that day I actually kept.

I knelt. The stone bit my knees. Somewhere above us the candle-flame leaned, though there was no wind.

"Do you come freely?"

"I come freely."

"Do you come whole?"

Here Maren made a small sound behind me, half cough, half warning, because *whole* was doing a great deal of work in that question and we both knew it. My mother had died the winter before. My father had never been in the picture long enough to leave. What remained of me I had brought, and I supposed that counted.

"I come whole enough," I said.

A ripple went through the Masters. For a heartbeat I thought I'd broken something — ruined the rite, disgraced my mentor, ended my life before it started. But the eldest Master's mouth twitched, and Maren later told me it was the closest that woman had come to laughter since the reign of somebody's grandfather. *Whole enough* went into the record. There are worse epitaphs.

Then they gave me the blade, hilt-first, and the eldest Master spoke the old words, and I felt them land on me like snowfall — soft, weightless, accumulating. The oath was not long. That surprised me. I had expected pages; it was nine lines, one for each pillar, and when I repeated them back in the dead tongue, the words came out of my mouth sounding like they belonged there, the way your own name sounds in your own voice.

I will not recite all nine lines here. Some of them I've earned the right to forget. But three of them I have never once stopped carrying, and you should know them, because everything that came after — the valley, the child, the breaking — grew out of these three roots:

*I will speak only truth into steel.* 

*I will carry no magic that magic did not give me.*

*And when the last word fails, I will keep the silence that remains.*

Say them fast and they're nothing. Say them slowly, alone, at night, and you can hear the trap built into the third line, coiled small as a spring. *When the last word fails.* Every order believes itself eternal. Every oath assumes there will always be someone left to keep it. The men and women who wrote those lines could not imagine a world with no speakers in it — and so they built a door with no handle on the inside, and called it faithfulness.

Seventeen-year-old Noor heard only the music. I pressed my lips to the flat of the blade, as instructed, and felt it warm under my mouth like a living thing acknowledging a stranger, and the hall exhaled, and I was a swordspeaker.

Maren embraced me afterward in the antechamber, hard enough to crack a rib, and then held me at arm's length with her hands on my shoulders and gave me the look I would spend thirty years trying to deserve.

"You swore beautifully," she said. "Now forget the ceremony. The oath isn't the words. The words are just the fence. The oath is what you do at three in the morning when nobody's watching and doing the right thing costs you something." She tapped my sternum, right where the words had landed. "It lives here. Feed it well."

"What does it eat?" I asked, because I was seventeen and clever.

"Grief, mostly," said Maren. "But also bread. Come on. They're serving honey-cakes in the refectory and if we don't get there first, Brother Anselm will eat his body weight and blame the novices."

That was the best day of my first life. I want that on the record too. Whatever came after, whatever I lost and whatever I spent — that day was good, all the way through, like fruit without a worm in it. I got to be young and chosen and certain, for exactly one afternoon.

*

The valley sealed itself eleven years later, on a night in late autumn, and I remember every hour of it, which is the cruelest kind of memory.

You have to understand what the world was doing to us in those years. The crown-tithes had begun under Aldric's grandfather and grown teeth under his father, but it was Aldric's coronation that changed the air itself. Thirteen turns of the moon after he took the throne, the bells of the capital rang wrong — every speaker within five hundred miles felt it, a wrongness in the world's rhythm, like a heart skipping. And after that, the oaths started going rotten.

An oath, properly spoken, is a small act of creation. Two people agree, and the agreement becomes real, becomes a thing with weight, and the world holds it. But the crown had learned — whatever the crown was, and none of us knew yet — that it could feed on agreements. Swear loyalty to the king, and something drank the vow and left the shell. Knights who had sworn themselves found their words coming out hollow. Whole garrisons woke up one morning unable to remember why they'd ever loved their lords, and loved only obedience instead, which is love's taxidermied corpse.

They called them the oathless. And the oathless hunted speakers, because speakers could unmake what the crown had made.

We lost the northern houses first. Then the river chapter, all of them, in a single night — I will not describe the letters we received. Then the southern masters stopped answering, and Maren sat up three nights running with maps spread across her table, moving black stones westward, and the stones kept moving toward us like water finding low ground.

On the last night, she came to my cell. I was twenty-eight. I had a scar across my palm from a training accident and a reputation, modest but real, for being able to still a haunted blade faster than anyone in the valley. Maren looked at me the way she'd looked at me on my oath day, that gardener's appraisal, except this time something in her face had already finished grieving and was waiting for me to catch up.

"Pack nothing," she said.

"Maren—"

"Nothing. Listen to me. Tonight I do the working, and after tonight, nothing with power in it crosses those stones. Nothing comes in. Nothing goes out. Not a curse, not a blessing, not a message, not a whisper." She said it flatly, the way you report weather. "The valley becomes deaf and dumb and safe."

"The rest of the order—"

"The rest of the order is dead, scattered, or sworn silent, and the ones who are scattered know the way here if they can reach it, and most of them won't." Her voice didn't crack. Maren's voice never cracked; it just got quieter until silence took over from her. "Two hundred speakers, Noor. When I started teaching, four thousand. Do the arithmetic of a graveyard with me. We are the low ground. Everything is flowing toward us, and when it arrives, it will arrive hungry."

"And your answer is to lock the door?"

"My answer is that a garden survives winter by *stopping*." She was pacing now, three steps, turn, three steps, the way she paced when she was building something large out of words. "Everything green in this valley goes dormant and waits, and the waiting isn't cowardice, it's strategy. Roots don't argue with frost. They hold on. That's all holding on is — waiting done patiently enough."

"For how long?"

She stopped pacing.

That was the moment. I've turned it over ten thousand times since, and it always ends the same way: Maren standing in my doorway with the candle shaking slightly in her hand, and the pause stretching between us, and neither of us filling it, because there was nothing honest left to fill it with. She didn't know how long. Neither did I. The honest answers were *forever* or *until it kills us*, and both were true, and both were unbearable, so we said nothing, and the nothing said it for us.

"It will cost you," she finally said. "You understand that. You're the strongest speaker we have left. Sealing the valley means sealing *you* — your gift sleeps at those stones same as everything else. You'll keep the knowledge. The words will stay in you like a swallowed key. But you won't be able to use them. Not here. Not while the seal holds."

"Then I'll wait," I said, and meant it, and it was the second lie I told with my whole heart, because I believed I meant it.

We walked out together into the cold. The whole surviving order stood among the boundary stones — nineteen of us, nineteen, in a ring built for thousands — and Maren went to the center with her blade driven into the earth, and she spoke the working.

I have heard armies. I have heard avalanches. I have never heard anything like the sound the valley made when it closed. It wasn't loud. That's what I always tell people, and they never believe me. It was the opposite of loud — a vast intake of breath, a hush rolling outward through the grass and the orchards and the sleeping village, and every speaker in that ring felt their gift drain out of them like warmth leaving a body, gently, completely, all the way down to ordinary.

The stones went grey. I mean that precisely: they had been faintly luminous for as long as anyone could remember, the old light of the first kingdoms caught in them like flies in amber, and that light went out, and the dark they left behind was the specific dark of a held breath.

Maren pulled her blade out of the earth and looked at it for a long time. Then she drove it into the ground again, deliberately, at the ring's center, and left it there.

"It stays," she said. "Let it rust. Let it forget. If the day comes that this valley needs a speaker more than it needs to sleep—" and here she looked directly at me, and her eyes were doing something complicated that I was too tired and too gutted to read properly, "—then someone will dig it up. And they'll know why."

"Nineteen of us," I said. "Someone should count them. Someone should write it down."

"Noor." She put her hand on the back of my neck, the way she had when I was seventeen and vibrating. "Go to bed."

I didn't go to bed. I walked the ring instead, all the way around, in the dark, touching each stone as I passed. Nineteen stones. Nineteen of us. I counted twice, because grief makes you distrust arithmetic, and both times the number came out the same, and both times I thought: *this is safety,* and the thought tasted like the skin of an onion — layered, and bitter all the way down, and making your eyes water if you handled it too long.

We had bought ourselves silence. That was the price on the tag, and we paid it gladly, and it was only much later that I read the fine print, which read: *silence, indefinite, non-refundable, redeemable nowhere.*

Maren died nine years later, in her sleep, in autumn, with her boots by the door as always, as if she might be summoned somewhere at any moment. She was buried facing the stones. By then there were eleven of us. Then seven. Then three, and then, on a gray morning thirteen years after the sealing, one.

Me.

I stayed, because staying was the oath's third line wearing a garden's clothes. *When the last word fails, keep the silence that remains.* I kept it. I planted things. I let the blade rust where the rust wanted to go, and I told myself the quiet was peace, and for long stretches — hours, sometimes whole days — I almost believed it.

Then came the frost that arrived early, the year the world outside had begun to shake itself apart, and I woke to find the boundary stones humming.

Nineteen stones, sealed for thirty years, and every one of them singing — low, frightened, urgent, like a struck bell remembered rather than heard.

And on the wind, threading through a gap I had not known existed, came a smell the valley had forgotten how to hold.

Magic. Coming in.
