## 5. Riders on the Ridge

I had always imagined that if my past ever came for me, it would arrive in fire. That was how it had left, after all — in fire, and screaming, and my mentor's hands pushing me through a seam in the world while she stayed behind to close it. Thirteen years of quiet had let me polish that memory into something almost dignified. Fire, at least, is honest. Fire announces itself.

The past came instead on three gray horses, on an afternoon so mild I had spent it arguing with a raspberry cane about which direction it ought to grow.

I heard them before I saw them — hooves on the old ridge road, where nothing had traveled but foxes and weather since before Wren came down from the mountain. The sound went through me like a hook through cloth. My body knew it before my mind did: that particular cadence of shod horses ridden by people who did not love them. Soldiers ride like that. They ride as if the horse is furniture.

I set down my shears. I took off my gloves, finger by finger, because some stubborn animal part of me wanted those last few seconds to be ordinary. Then I walked out into the lane, wiping dirt on my apron, and looked up the road toward the ridge.

Three riders against the pale sky. Armor under gray cloaks, but not the parade kind — this was the working sort, dented and oiled, the kind meant to stop arrows rather than impress peasants. And no tabards. No sigils. No oath-marks.

Oathless knights. The king's own, they said now, though there was a time when *king's own* would have been a contradiction in terms. An oathless knight was a walking wound in the world, a person who had sworn service to the crown without any of the old binding words — which meant, in practice, that they could be ordered to do anything and feel precisely nothing about it afterward. My order had kept records on such things. We had believed, once, that oaths were the bones of the world. The crown-thing, whatever it was, had apparently decided bones were easier without marrow.

They came down the switchbacks at an unhurried pace, and I stood in my lane with dirt on my apron and did the arithmetic of my life. One woman, one garden, one hidden child. Three armed riders who had crossed a boundary that should have turned them back.

That last part was the one that made my mouth go dry. The valley forgets. The valley was made to forget — sealed by a swordspeaker's dying act, warded so that magic could not enter and could not leave, a locked room at the bottom of the world. Ordinary travelers wandered in sometimes and wandered out, never quite remembering why they'd come. But knights with crown-iron at their belts should have hit the boundary stones and felt their purpose leak away like water through cupped hands.

Unless the crown-thing had grown strong enough to reach over its own wards. Unless the seal was thinning. Unless everything my mentor had died to build was already rotting, and I had spent thirteen years watering leeks inside a tomb whose door no longer latched.

The lead rider drew rein at the gate.

She swung down, and I saw at once that she was not like the other two. They were big, blank men, the kind hired for shoulders. She was lean and dark-haired, maybe forty, with a face like a closed ledger, and her eyes did the thing I most feared: they cataloged me. Boots. Apron. Hands — callused, sword-calluses worn soft by years of soil, but still legible to anyone who knew the alphabet. She read me in four seconds and filed me somewhere behind those flat gray eyes.

"Good day," she said. Her voice was level and pleasant and entirely without warmth, the way a well-made knife is pleasant. "You're the gardener."

"I'm the gardener."

"Alone out here?"

"Alone." This much was true, and I gave it freely, because a lie is a currency you should spend only when the exchange rate is worth it. "Most folk don't climb this far unless they've lost something."

Something flickered across her face — too quick to name. "As it happens," she said, "we have. A runaway. A child, eight years or so, dark-haired, small for her age. She went missing near the high passes eleven days ago. There's a reward." She produced a folded paper, and even at ten paces I recognized the heavy cream stock of court writs. "We're asking at every holding within a week's ride."

Eleven days. Eleven days since frost had brought a half-frozen girl stumbling to my gate speaking the tongue before kingdoms. I felt the number settle over the morning like a change in pressure before a storm.

"You've come far for one child," I said.

"The crown pays for far things." She smiled slightly. It did not involve her eyes. "May we water our horses?"

It was a small request, reasonable, domestic — and it was a test. I knew it was a test the way you know a step is rotten: the floor gives a little different under your weight. Let soldiers onto your ground and they see your ground. They'd see the second set of boots by the door, small ones, mud-sized. They'd hear — saints preserve me — they might *hear*.

"My well's gone bitter this season," I said. "There's a stream a mile back, where the road forks."

"Inconvenient," she said pleasantly.

"Valleys are inconvenient. It's why they're empty."

For a moment neither of us moved. Somewhere behind me in the garden a bee worked the borage, industrious and oblivious, and I thought absurdly that I envied it more than I had ever envied anything.

Then she laughed — a short, real bark of laughter that seemed to surprise even her — and swung back into the saddle. "Bitter wells and empty valleys," she said. "You argue like a lawyer, gardener." She passed the writ down to me anyway, an oddly courteous gesture, or perhaps another test; touching the paper put the king's wax into my hand, and I held it the way you hold a snake you're pretending is dead. "If the child comes begging at your gate — and children do drift, I'm told, like seeds — you'll send word to the garrison at Marrow Ford."

"I keep to myself," I said. "But if a child comes to my gate, I'll do what anyone would."

"Yes," Serah Vane said — for she gave her name then, with a small inclination of her head, as though the exchange were a formality between equals — "I imagine you'll do exactly what anyone would."

They wheeled their horses. And that should have been the end of it. That is what I told myself, standing in the lane with the court writ crumpling slowly in my fist: *the end of it, they'll ride on, the valley will swallow them, the stones will do their work.* The other two riders were already moving, bored, their purpose visibly draining — I watched it happen, watched one of them frown at his own hand like he'd forgotten why he was holding reins.

Serah Vane did not drain. She sat her horse at the bend of the road and looked back — not at me, but past me, at the garden wall, at the smoke rising from my chimney, at the whole small architecture of a life. Counting windows. Measuring woodpiles. A child eats. A child burns fuel. A child leaves footprints in frost, and the first frost had come eleven days ago.

Then she rode on, and the hoofbeats faded, and the bee went on working the borage, and I stood in my lane until my legs reminded me they belonged to a woman of fifty-one years and not a girl who had once run from fire.

* * *

Wren knew. Children always know; they are small animals built entirely for the detection of danger, and civilization spends twenty years beating it out of them. I found her not hiding, exactly — she was too proud for hiding — but sitting very straight on the kitchen bench, mending a tear in her sleeve with stitches too careful for an eight-year-old, listening with her whole spine.

"Horses," she said, when I came in.

"Gone now."

"They wanted me." Not a question. She didn't look up from her stitching. In the language before kingdoms, the words came out flat and old, older than her face, and I still marveled and dreaded how easily they sat in her mouth. "The iron men. They smell like the scar smells."

"The scar doesn't smell."

"It does. Like coins left out in rain." She bit her thread, finally looked up. Her eyes were too calm. "You lied to them."

I stopped with my hand on the kettle. "You heard that?"

"I heard everything. You said your well was bitter. Your well is sweet." A pause, and then, with the terrible precision of children: "You never lied before. Your voice did a new thing."

And there it was, laid open on my own kitchen table: fifty-one years of an honest life, and the first crack in it noticed by a child before the mortar had even dried. I had lied to Serah Vane twice — the well, and the smaller lie beneath it, the lie of *I am nobody, I am only a gardener, I have nothing here worth searching.* And the strange thing, the thing I turned over that night while Wren slept and I sat sharpening a blade I hadn't drawn in thirteen years, was that the lies hadn't tasted the way I'd always feared lies would taste. They hadn't tasted like rot. They'd tasted like bread. Like the plain necessary food of someone with something to protect.

My order had held truth above all things. Truth was the spine of speech; a swordspeaker who lied could not speak true at the moment of unbinding; every word we uttered was load-bearing. I had lived thirteen years inside that discipline the way other people live inside a religion — comfortably, without examining the walls.

But my order was ash, and its last lawgiver had sealed me in a valley to keep me safe, and safety, I was beginning to understand, was just another word for a cage with good light.

*What would she have done?* I asked my mentor's ghost, turning the whetstone. *Maren, keeper of the ninth garden, breaker of kings — what would you have done with three riders at the gate and a sleeping child upstairs?*

The ghost said nothing, which was unlike her. Maren had always had opinions.

So I answered myself, and the answer frightened me with how little it cost: *She would have lied better.*

* * *

They came back in nine days.

Not all three — the two big blank men had ridden on toward Marrow Ford and, presumably, forgotten why they had ever climbed a mountain. But Serah Vane returned alone, on foot this time, leading a packhorse loaded with salt, lamp oil, and a sack of apples. She stood at my gate in the late gold light of afternoon holding the apples like an offering, and I understood immediately that this was worse than the first visit. Soldiers search. Captains *return*.

"Bitter-well country," she said, "runs short of stores. Thought you might trade."

"I don't take gifts from strangers."

"We met nine days ago. You argued with me about hydrology." She held up the apple sack. "Trade, then. Apples for news. Have you seen the child?"

"No."

"That's the news?"

"That's the news."

She studied me for a long moment. The evening light did her no favors and me none either; we were two women squinting at each other across a gate, each trying to read the fine print on the other's soul. I had the advantage of knowing what was at stake. She had the advantage of knowing I was lying, I suspect, and simply lacking proof.

"There's a thing I learned in the capital," she said at last, conversationally, passing me two apples through the bars of the gate. "In the courts, before I took the gray cloak. Judges use it. *Qui tacet consentire.* Who is silent consents. It means silence can be entered as evidence. A person who says nothing has agreed to the question underneath." She looked past me again, at the garden, the chimney, the small boots I had burned along with every other trace, thank every saint. "So here's my question underneath, gardener: eleven days of hard riding, and the trail goes cold exactly at your valley. Cold trails mean either nothing or something. Nothing looks like you. Something also looks like you. It's very inconvenient of you."

"Valleys are inconvenient," I said. "I mentioned that."

"So you did." She mounted, gathered the reins, and paused. What she said next, she said quietly, without performance, and somehow that was the worst of it. "I've served the crown six years. In that time I've carried out orders I remember every night and orders I've managed to lose, and I can no longer tell you which list is longer. If that child is in this valley, she is safer with a liar than she will ever be with me. Think on which of us the crown will send next, when I report the trail cold." She touched her brow — a salute, or an apology, or both — and walked her horse up the road toward the fork and the stream and the long way home.

I stood at the gate until dark, holding two apples I couldn't eat.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as a footprint in frost: she knew. Or she was ninety percent sure, which for practical purposes is the same animal wearing a slightly different coat. And she had ridden away anyway, leaving me alive and unreported, telling me — in the flattest possible language, in the grammar of a woman too disciplined to say anything directly — that she was leaving a door open. *A liar is safer than I am.* She had handed me her own confession wrapped in a threat, or a threat wrapped in a confession, and ridden off into the dusk to go write her report with cold trails in it.

I went inside. Wren was awake — of course she was awake; the scarred palm pressed flat against her chest, listening, always listening, to whatever faint coin-and-rain whisper the crown-thing sent down the miles to find its key. She looked at me across the candlelight.

"She knows," Wren said.

"She suspects."

"What's the difference?"

I sat down across from her and took one of the apples and turned it in my fingers, and I thought about my mentor sealing the valley with her last breath, about thirteen turns of the moon, about the primer buried under the floorboards with its forbidden pages, about the word *unbinding* that had begun to live in my mouth like a seed too large to spit out.

"The difference," I said, "is how many heartbeats we have left to get ready."

And outside, far up past the ridge where the world began, the moon was coming round again — thinner each night, patient as debt — counting itself down toward thirteen.
