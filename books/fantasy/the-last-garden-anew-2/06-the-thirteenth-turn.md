## 6. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon in the valley was the same moon as everywhere else, but it behaved differently here. I decided this on my fourth night at the garden, lying under a blanket that smelled of dried lavender and old wool, watching it through the gap in the shutters. Outside the valley — in the places I did not talk about, even inside my own head, because talking about them made the scar itch — the moon had been getting fatter every night like a goose being fed for slaughter. Here it seemed to hesitate. Like it wasn't sure it was allowed.

I counted anyway. Counting was something I could do that nobody could take away from me, not even him.

Nine nights left.

*Eight,* said the voice in my palm, and I pressed my hand flat against my chest to smother it, the way you'd smother a spark in straw.

* * *

Here is what I knew about myself, which was not much:

My name was Wren. I knew this the way I knew my own hands — not because anyone had told me recently, but because it fit. A wren is a small brown bird that hides in hedges and sings louder than anything its size should allow. I liked that about it. I hoped it was true about me too.

I had a scar on my right palm, shaped like a little crown with three points, and it had been there as long as I could remember, which was further back than most people's memories went, or so the gardener-lady said when she saw it and went pale as her own cabbages. The scar didn't hurt. That was the worst part. Hurting things want things from you — salve, sympathy, a bandage. The scar only wanted an audience. It talked, and it wanted me to listen.

And I had run for — I counted this too — eleven days through forest and field and one very rude river before I found the gate with the leaning stones, and the woman behind it who looked at me like I was a ghost she'd been setting an extra place for at supper without admitting it.

Her name was Noor. She grew vegetables. She had a sword hanging on her wall that was rusted orange along half its length, and she looked at it sometimes the way I looked at the door of the house I'd run away from: like it was both the best and worst thing she owned.

She couldn't understand my words. That was what she thought, anyway, in the beginning. But here is the strange thing, the thing I turned over in my head at night like a smooth stone: she always answered the right question.

* * *

On the morning after my fourth night, she set out porridge and bread and honey, and I ate like a small polite wolf, and she watched me with her arms crossed and asked me questions in her flat northern way.

"Where did you come from?"

I understood her fine. Understanding wasn't the problem. The problem was that answering felt like opening a window in a house where something was waiting outside. So I shrugged and pointed north-ish, which was true enough, and shoveled more porridge.

"You crossed the boundary stones alone." Not a question. Her eyes went to the gate, then to the hills beyond it, then back to me with an expression I would later learn meant she was doing arithmetic she hated. "A child of eight winters doesn't cross the wilds alone."

"I'm nearly nine," I said, in my own tongue, the one that came out of my mouth like water and apparently sounded like birdsong or nonsense or something in between, depending on who heard it. "And I wasn't alone exactly."

Noor went very still. Stillness, I learned, was her version of shouting.

"What do you mean, not alone?"

I held up my scarred palm. This was a mistake. I knew it was a mistake even as I did it, because the moment the morning light touched the three-pointed mark, it warmed, and the warmth came with words, and the words were not mine:

*She sees us. Ask her if she remembers the oath. Ask her if she kept it.*

"Noor," I said — her name, one of the few words of her language I'd gathered like dropped coins — and then, because the scar pushed and I was tired of pushing back, I let the rest come out in my own tongue, translating as I went the way you'd carry water in cupped hands, spilling some: "It says — it says to ask if you remember the oath. If you kept it."

The color left her face in a slow tide, like she was a hillside and I'd pulled a plug somewhere at the bottom. She sat down across from me, hard, on the bench, and for a long moment she just looked at my hand.

Then she got up, went into the back room, and started throwing things out of a chest, and I ate the rest of the honey bread, because whatever storm was coming, arriving at it hungry seemed strictly worse.

* * *

That afternoon she brought out a book.

It was a thin book, bound in leather gone dark and soft with age, and she handled it like it might bite, which, given everything, it possibly could have. She set it on the table between us but didn't open it.

"This belonged to my order," she said slowly, pointing at herself, then making a shape with her fingers — many people together, then scattered apart, gone. "Before. There were others. Now there is me."

I nodded. Grown-ups always explained things I already understood; it was one of their chief occupations.

"The tongue you speak," she said, and touched the book. "It is in here. It should be dead. Everyone who spoke it has been dead longer than I've been alive." Her jaw worked. "You shouldn't know it. Children learn tongues from mothers and markets. Who taught you?"

I considered the honest answer. *Something in my hand talks to me while I sleep, and it knows all the old words, and sometimes when I wake up my mouth is full of them.* I considered how that answer would land. I had experience with how answers landed, in the before-place. They landed badly.

"A dream-teacher," I said finally, which was true in the way that mattered least and safest.

Noor looked at me for a long time. She had gray coming in at her temples and eyes the color of the sky in late autumn, and when she looked at you properly it felt like being weighed on scales that were very fair and very unkind.

"Dream-teacher," she repeated, flat.

"The scar does the teaching," I admitted, because lying to her felt like lying to a fence post — pointless, and the post always won eventually. I turned my palm up. "It talks. Mostly at night. Mostly counting."

"Counting what?"

I showed her on my fingers, the way I did every night. Nine. Then eight. Then seven. I didn't know what happened at zero. Nobody had told me. But the scar counted toward it with the patience of a spider, and spiders are patient because they already know how the web ends.

* * *

*Six,* the scar said that night, and I lay in my little bed under the lavender blanket and negotiated.

This was our arrangement, mine and the voice. It talked. I listened, mostly, because listening cost less than fighting, and fighting it gave me headaches like a blacksmith's hammer taken up behind my eyes. In exchange, I set rules. Small ones. Rules were how I kept myself being Wren instead of becoming a glove that something else wore.

Rule one: no talking while Noor was praying. She prayed at dawn, quietly, facing the rusted sword, and whatever she said to it was hers.

Rule two: nothing out loud past sundown unless it was an emergency, and I got to decide what an emergency was.

Rule three — rule three was the important one — *you don't get to use my mouth for your words when other people can hear.*

It broke rule three constantly. Rules, I was learning, were like fences to things like the scar: useful for keeping in sheep, useless for keeping in weather.

*Six nights,* it murmured, sly and warm, like a cat settling onto your chest. *Ask her about the thirteenth turn. Ask her what happens when the count ends.*

"I don't want to ask her that," I whispered into the dark. "It'll scare her."

*Good,* said the crown-thing — I had started calling it that in my head, though I never said it aloud, because names give power and I was not in the business of giving anything more power than it already had. *Fear makes the doors open faster.*

"That's horrible."

*It is efficient,* it said, and I could feel it smiling. Things without faces shouldn't be able to smile. It managed anyway. *Little key. Little bird. You were made for opening. Why do you cling to the hedge?*

"Because I'm a wren," I said. "Wrens live in hedges."

*A wren,* it agreed, silk over stone. *And what do men do to wrens, when they wish to keep them?*

I didn't answer. I pulled the blanket over my head and pretended to sleep, and it counted anyway, patient as a spider, and somewhere far away — so far it might have been the edge of the world — I felt a king turn over in his sleep, and the turning felt like a door testing its hinges.

* * *

Five nights left, and frost came to the garden proper, silvering the cabbage leaves, and Noor let me help with the harvest because idle children ask worse questions than busy ones. My job was pulling carrots, which I took very seriously, and dropping approximately one in three, which she bore with the grim patience of a woman who had clearly raised children once and buried the memory somewhere near the sword.

"Can I ask you something?" I said, around a mouthful of carrot top I was pretending was deliberate.

"You just did."

"Another something."

She straightened, wiped her hands on her apron, and gave me the weighing look. "Ask."

"When the count ends." My stomach did a slow roll. "What happens?"

The silence stretched long enough that I heard a crow arguing with itself two fields over. Then Noor crouched down to my level, which grown-ups almost never did, and looked at me straight.

"What does it tell you happens?"

"It doesn't tell. It counts." I swallowed. "But it's happy. When it counts, it's happy. And it's been happy for eleven days, and things that are that happy for that long are usually happy about something terrible."

For a moment something moved behind her face — grief, maybe, or recognition, the look of someone hearing their own thoughts in a smaller voice. Then she said, "Where did you come from, Wren? Truly. Before the wilds."

And because she'd crouched down, and because the frost made everything honest, I told her some of it. Not the worst parts. The parts I could lift.

"There was a house," I said. "Big. Cold in a wrong way, like cold that's pretending. There were people who called me *little majesty* and bowed, which was stupid, because I was barefoot. There was a man who used to visit my room at night and sit by the window and cry without any noise, and he'd say—" I stopped. The next part lived close to the worst parts. "He'd say *don't let it finish counting.*"

Noor had gone white again. She was getting good practice at it.

"And then?" she asked softly.

"Then the man stopped coming, and the house got colder, and the scar started counting out loud instead of quiet, and I ran." I picked up a carrot and put it in the basket, because hands need jobs when mouths are brave. "Is the crying man bad? He felt sad. Sad isn't bad. But the house was his house, and the house was bad, so maybe—"

"He's not bad," Noor said, with a certainty that startled us both. She looked out past the gate, north, toward everything we'd come from. "I think he may be the saddest man in the world."

*Four,* said the scar, delighted, and Noor flinched like she'd been struck, though of course she hadn't heard it. Only me. Only ever me. I was starting to understand that this was the whole point of me: a door that only opened one way, for one listener, carrying one message.

I was also starting to understand that I hated it.

* * *

Three nights left, and I dreamed of the coronation hall.

I'd never seen it awake, but dreams don't care about such details. Stone pillars going up forever, banners the color of dried blood, and a throne on a dais, and on the throne a man made mostly of shadows wearing a small gold light on his head. In the dream I walked down the hall between rows of kneeling people, and my feet made no sound, and everyone's eyes were closed except theirs — the crown's — and it looked at me with something that was not eyes, and it said:

*Thirteen turns since we were joined, little key. Thirteen turns of feeding. On the fourteenth night we renew, and renewing requires a threshold, and a speaker, and—*

"—and a key," I finished, in the dream, in the old tongue, and my own voice frightened me more than the throne did.

I woke gasping with the blanket on the floor and frost on the inside of the window, and downstairs a lamp was already lit, and Noor was already awake, because of course she was. Some part of me suspected she never really slept at all, just stood watch over the dark in shifts with herself.

She found me sitting on the stairs, knees to my chest.

"Dream?" she asked.

I nodded. Then, because the fences were down and the frost was inside: "It showed me a hall. And a man on a chair. And it said I was a key." I held up my scarred palm between us, and the three points of the little crown caught the lamplight. "Noor. What am I a key *to*?"

She didn't answer right away. She sat down beside me on the step, which creaked under both of us, and she was quiet for so long I thought she wouldn't answer at all. When she spoke, her voice was low and rough, like something dragged a long distance.

"To a cage," she said. "Or out of one. Keys don't choose, Wren. That's the cruelty of them. Hands choose."

"Whose hands, then?"

She looked at her own hands — gardener's hands, swordswoman's hands, an oathkeeper's hands, though I didn't have the words for that last one yet — and then at mine, small and dirt-stained and marked.

"That," she said, "is what I am trying very hard to decide."

*Two,* whispered the scar, warm against my palm, patient as a spider, happy as a wedding bell.

And far to the north, in a city I had never seen but somehow remembered, a bell rang slightly out of time, and a man who was mostly shadow lifted his head in the dark as if he'd heard his name called from very far away — and smiled with someone else's mouth.
