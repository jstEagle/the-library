## 8. Serah Vane's Bargain

I had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things worth learning.

First: never trust a man who polishes his boots more than his blade. Second: the ones who talk about honor the loudest are always the ones selling it. Third — and this is the one they don't teach you at your swearing-in, because it would empty the ranks by sundown — every oath is a cage, and the only question is whether you know you're in it.

I knew. I had known for thirteen years, since the night I knelt in the coronation hall and swore myself to King Aldric of the Nine Gates, and watched his eyes go somewhere else while his mouth said *I accept*.

So when my orders came to ride into a valley that did not exist on any map I'd ever seen, and drag back an eight-year-old girl for reasons no one would put into writing, I knew precisely what kind of cage I was standing in. The question was whether Noor Iversen knew she was standing in hers.

* * *

The knights camped on the ridge for two days after that night, and then they left.

Not all at once, and not convincingly. Dain took six men south along the valley's edge as if the child might have sprouted wings and flown over the boundary stones. Rell and his four rode east to shake down a tithe-village that hadn't paid in a season, which was knight-work I understood and, mostly, despised. And I stayed behind with two men who were too tired and too bored to be dangerous, and told them I wanted to map the ford crossings.

Nobody questioned it. That was the trouble with being competent — people stopped watching you. Competence was a kind of invisibility, and I had spent years making mine thick enough to hide in.

On the third morning I sent both men down to the ford with instructions so tedious they'd be grateful for the excuse not to think, and I rode up alone to the garden gate with my helmet hanging from my saddle and my hands where anyone could see them.

The gate was latched but not locked. Of course it wasn't. A woman like Noor Iversen didn't lock gates; she simply made sure you regretted coming through them.

I dismounted well short of the fence line — old habit, learned hard, from men who shot first at anything that moved with purpose — and stood in the frost with my breath steaming and called out, at a volume suitable for addressing a neighbor rather than storming a keep:

"Gardener. It's the captain again. Alone."

No answer. But the quality of the silence changed, the way it does when someone is listening instead of merely absent, and I waited. Patience is cheap. Impatience gets you buried.

A crow worked at something in the bean rows. Somewhere beyond the house a pump-handle squeaked, once, twice — the sound of a woman deciding to finish her chore before honoring me with her presence. I found I respected that enormously.

Then the door opened and there she was, the oath-blade in her hand, held the way a farmer holds a sickle rather than the way a soldier holds a sword. Loose. Casual. Ready.

"You came back," she said.

"I did."

"The last time you were here, you brought nine armed men and a lie about a runaway child." She came down the path slowly, unhurried as weather. "Which, to be fair, is what she is. But you didn't know that when you said it."

"Didn't I?"

"No," Noor said. "You suspected it. There's a difference. Suspects send riders. Knowers come alone." She stopped at the gate and looked at me through the bars, and her eyes were the flat gray of a winter sky deciding what to do with itself. "Why are you alone, Captain?"

"Because the men I command would follow me into a burning building if I ordered it," I said, "and I am not willing to order this. Whatever this is."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got. You want more, you'll have to let me inside to find it."

She considered me for a long moment. Frost ticked in the hedges. Then she unlatched the gate and stepped back, blade still in hand, and gestured toward the house with the weary courtesy of a woman inviting a debt-collector to supper.

"Mind the beans," she said. "They're the only thing in this valley that hasn't tried to kill me yet."

* * *

Her kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and dried thyme and forty years of one person living carefully. She made tea without asking if I wanted any, which was either hospitality or a way to keep her hands busy near a weapon, and I decided I didn't care which. The oath-blade lay on the table between us, close enough to her side, far enough from mine. Deliberate. Everything in that room was deliberate.

"Your name is Serah Vane," she said. "Sworn shield-captain, Third Oath, the king's own. Your father was a farrier in Lowmarsh. You've buried a sister and nobody else, and you read more than soldiers are supposed to."

I kept my face still, which took some doing. "How."

"There was a man in your column. Tall, gray beard, talks too much. He talked about you while you were out of earshot. Men do that around women they're afraid of — they gossip like washerwomen." She set down the teapot. "Now you know things about me too, or you think you do. So let's skip to it. Why is a sworn captain of the crown sitting in my kitchen without her helmet on?"

I turned the cup in my hands. It was chipped, and the chip had been worn smooth by years of thumbs finding it, and I thought: *this woman has been holding the same cup for decades, in a valley where nothing changes, and I am about to blow the roof off her life.* Some part of me — the part I liked least, the part that had survived nineteen years of service — noted that this gave me leverage, and I told that part to shut its mouth.

"Do you know what happens at the renewal ceremony?" I asked.

"I know what happens at ceremonies generally. Bells. Robes. Someone says words older than sense."

"This one's different. Every thirteenth full moon, the court renews the king's oaths. All of them. Every vow ever sworn in Aldric's name, spoken aloud in the coronation hall, and the king stands under the crown and accepts them again." I looked at her. "Thirteen turns ago, he stopped accepting them himself. His lips move. The words come out. But there's something else saying yes."

The fire popped. Noor didn't blink, didn't breathe, didn't move. Waiting. She was very good at waiting. I began to understand why she'd lasted this long.

"I've served him eleven years," I went on. "Before that I served his father, who was a hard man and an honest one, which is rarer than you'd think. When Aldric took the throne he was twenty-three and he laughed at his own jokes and he once climbed the palace wall drunk on a wager because he refused to lose to his cousin." My voice did something strange on the next sentence and I let it. "I have seen that man eat. I have seen him sleep. I have seen him weep at his mother's funeral, properly weep, like a person. And for thirteen years I have also seen the thing that wears his face sign death warrants with a hand that doesn't tremble, and I am telling you, gardener, those are not the same hand."

"What do you want from me, Captain?"

"I want to know what you saw in that child that made you lie to my face. Because I've been a soldier long enough to know that a lie that costs the liar something is worth more than a hundred truths that cost nothing. You lied badly. You lied like a woman who'd never done it. Which means whatever you were protecting mattered more to you than your own nature." I leaned forward. "And I think you know what she is. And I think you know what's on the king's head. And I think you've known for weeks, and every day you wait is a day the thing on his head gets stronger."

"And if I know all that?" Noor said quietly. "What then? What does a captain of the crown do with knowledge like that? Report it?" Her mouth twisted. "To whom, exactly? The courts? The council? The archivist with the soft voice and the soft hands?"

Something cold walked down my spine. "You know Torvald."

"I knew his teacher. That's enough." She rose, crossed to a chest beneath the window, and lifted out something wrapped in oilcloth — a book, old, its cover scarred with a sigil I half-recognized from the seal on documents I was never permitted to read fully. She set it on the table but kept her hand flat on top of it, like a woman pinning something down. "Before I open this, you're going to answer a question. Truthfully. Because I can hear truth, Captain — it's the one gift the order left me that works even in this dead valley. So."

"Ask."

"When you look at your king — the real one, the one underneath — what do you feel?"

I could have lied. Nineteen years of practice; I could have lied to a bishop mid-blessing. But her eyes were gray and patient and terrible, and the kitchen was warm, and I was so tired of carrying it.

"Pity," I said. "God help me. Pity, and something worse than pity. Recognition." I turned the cup again, harder than I meant to. "Because I swore an oath to him, and oaths are cages, and I know I'm in mine. And every time I see him standing under that crown with his lips moving and his eyes gone, I think: *there but for a different accident of birth, am I.*"

The silence stretched. Then Noor lifted her hand from the book.

"He serves the crown," she said. "Do you understand the difference? The crown isn't jewelry. It was the first oath ever spoken, back when oaths were not words but *things*, and it went rotten, and now it eats vows. Every promise sworn in the king's name feeds it. Every soldier kneeling, every judge sentencing, every farmer tithing — it drinks all of it, and gives back a king who signs and nods and does not live." She opened the book to a page marked with a scrap of ribbon gone brown with age. "The girl is a key. Not metaphorically. She was made — born, shaped, I don't know — to unmake it. And there is a rite, and it requires a speaker, and the speaker has to be willing to spend the gift entire."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning whoever speaks it speaks for the last time." She met my eyes. "That's not your burden. That's mine, if I take it up at all. Yours is simpler and worse. Get us to the capital. Alive. Unannounced. Through nine gates full of men who answer to the thing on his head and one archivist who answers to nothing but his own appetite."

I sat back. Outside, the frost was burning off the bean rows, and a crow complained about something, and I thought about the two men I'd parked at the ford with instructions to count reeds.

"If I do this," I said, "and we're caught — and we will be caught, gardener, because nothing goes unnoticed in that city, especially not a captain traveling light with a pilgrim and a child — then everything I am ends on a scaffold. Slowly, if Torvald has influence over the interrogators, and he will, because Torvald has influence over everything soft."

"Yes."

"And if we succeed, I'm an oath-breaker. Twice over. To the crown and to the company of everyone who ever knelt beside me."

"No," Noor said. "Once over. Think it through, Captain. If the crown breaks, the oath you swore to the man wearing it survives. It's the only piece that survives. You'd be the most faithful traitor in the history of the Nine Gates." For the first time since I'd known her — all four days of knowing her — she almost smiled. It made her look younger and sadder at once. "I broke mine tonight, as it happens. Forty years of keeping it, and it took one book and one child. It turns out an oath is just a wall you build against yourself, and walls are for gardens, not people."

We looked at each other across the table, across the book, across the whole impossible distance between a dead order and a rotten court. Two women bound to broken masters — hers dead and buried, mine alive and wearing a monster — and both of us, apparently, done pretending otherwise.

"Terms," I said, because terms were the only language I trusted. "One: the girl travels as my ward, my sister's daughter, no arguments, no cleverness. Two: we enter the city separately, you and she through the pilgrim gate, me through the officers' postern, and we meet at the shrine of Saint Verren on the third evening — it's neglected, nobody watches it, and the beggars outside it owe me. Three—" I paused, and hated the pause, and pushed through it anyway. "Three. If it comes to choosing between the rite and the child, you choose the child. Swear it. I'll not ferry a little girl into that hall to be spent like coin."

Noor was quiet a long time. The fire settled. And I watched her arrive somewhere I recognized — the place where a person admits the plan has teeth.

"She won't be spent," she said finally. "But I'll swear the ordering. Child first. Always child first." She put out her hand across the table, callused palm up, gardener's hand, oath-breaker's hand. "Well then, Captain. It seems we're both about to betray our masters for love of the men underneath."

I took it. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"One condition of mine," she added, as we shook. "When this is finished — whatever 'finished' means — you come back to the valley, and I teach you to grow beans. You strike me as a woman who's forgotten how to make something live."

"Captain," I said, standing and reaching for my helmet, "I've spent nineteen years keeping things alive that should have died. How hard can beans be?"

She laughed — short, rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and it occurred to me, walking out through her gate into the thin bright morning, that it was the first time in years I'd heard anyone laugh inside the king's reach and mean it.

Behind me, the garden sat quiet under the climbing sun. Ahead, on the ridge, the moon's absence said everything: five nights to full. Five nights to gather two travelers, forge a story, and ride for the City of Nine Gates, where a puppet-king waited under a hungry crown, and a soft-handed archivist was counting days of his own.

I swung up into the saddle, and I did not look back at the valley, because looking back is how soldiers die.

But I thought it. Loudly.
