## 9. Leaving the Garden

I had spent thirteen years telling myself the silence was a choice.

That was the trick of oaths, I think — they don't lock you in so much as they teach you to call the cage a shape you chose. Every morning I woke in my little house above the garden, and every morning the valley lay around me like a held breath: no whispers at the edges of hearing, no prickle of the old words behind my teeth, nothing but wind and water and the honest creak of my own bones. And every morning I told myself, *this is peace*, and every morning some small traitorous part of me answered, *no, this is a room with the door bricked over from the inside.*

On the morning we left, I stopped lying to myself about it.

Wren was already awake. Of course she was. Children wake the way cats do — instantly, completely, with an opinion about breakfast. She sat on the edge of her pallet in my house with the primer open on her knees, the forbidden book that had slept under my floorboards for over a decade until three days ago, when I'd pried up the boards like a woman digging out her own heart.

"It talks more when I hold it," she said.

"The book?"

"No." She touched the scar on her palm, the crown-shaped one, pale as old wax. "The other thing."

I set down the kettle I'd been filling and looked at her properly. Eight years old, thin as a winter branch, hair the color of late straw, and carrying something in her skin that had eaten a king alive for thirteen years. The moon overhead was fat and nearly full. The thirteenth turn was coming whether we liked it or not.

"What did it say?" I asked, because asking was better than not asking, and because Serah Vane's bargain had a clock attached to it, and clocks only run one direction.

Wren frowned, hunting for the words. Her command of our tongue grew by the hour; the old tongue came out of her like breathing, which should have told me everything right there. "It said — *soon*. And then it said something else, and it wasn't words exactly. It was like..." She made a gesture, fingers curling, uncurling. "Like a hand testing a latch."

*Testing a latch.* I had heard Serah Vane say almost the same thing on the ridge two nights ago, standing in my garden with frost on her captain's shoulders: *It knows where the key sleeps. It's only waiting for the door to matter.*

"Well," I said, "then we won't be home when it knocks."

* * *

Packing for a journey you may not survive is a strange arithmetic. I took the primer, obviously. I took dried food enough for two small mouths for perhaps ten days, if neither mouth was honest about its appetite. I took a needle, thread, flint, a knife that was a knife and not anything else.

And then I stood in front of the chest at the foot of my bed, where the oath-blade had rusted in its wrappings for thirteen years, and I had a long argument with myself.

Here is what you must understand about swordspeech, since I will not have the chance to explain it later, and since explaining things is a habit dying people and departing ones share: the order did not fight with steel. Steel is what you use when words fail. We spoke, and the world listened. A true speaker could tell a river to stand aside and the river would apologize for the inconvenience. Could tell a wound to remember being whole. Could tell a wall — well. There are stories about walls, and most of them end with the masons finding other work.

The oath we swore bound that voice. Bound it twice, in fact. Once to service, once to silence — and the second binding was mine alone, sealed the night Maera closed this valley against all magic, in or out, forever, so that the last of us could live out our lives as nothing more dangerous than gardeners. My blade was the anchor of that oath. As long as it stayed wrapped and rusting, so did the words.

I unwrapped it.

The rust had gone deep, but underneath, when I rubbed the flat with my thumb, the metal still remembered how to be bright. Old blades do. They're vain that way. Along the fuller ran the inscription every oath-blade carries, the words I had spoken at seventeen with Maera's hand heavy on my shoulder:

*I speak, therefore the world obeys.*

"You sentimental fool," I said to the blade, or to myself, hard to say. "You absolute sentimental fool."

But I wrapped it in oilcloth and slung it across my back anyway, because I am a sentimental fool, and because somewhere south of here a king was wearing a thing that wasn't him, and someone had to go remind the world whose servant it was supposed to be.

* * *

We walked out through the garden first. That was not sentiment, whatever it looked like. It was practical. If I was going to break the oldest oath of my life, I wanted to do it looking at something I'd grown.

Frost had come in the night, the first hard frost, and the garden wore it like lace. The cabbages were done, the last beans blackened on their poles, but the kale stood green and defiant under its rime, and the rosemary near the gate had gone silver-grey and smelled, even frozen, like memory. I had planted every row of this ground. I had buried three of my fellow survivors in its corners — Hesta by the plums, Oskar by the well, little Bram where the sun came first, because he'd never gotten enough of warmth in his short life. This dirt knew my hands. It was the closest thing I had left to family, besides the child currently eating frozen currants off the bush by the gate despite my explicit instruction otherwise.

"Wren."

"Mm?" said Wren, with her mouth full.

"When we cross the stones, things will change. For both of us. I need you to understand that before we take the step, not after."

She swallowed and wiped her hands on her coat, suddenly serious, because she was a child who had learned early that seriousness kept you fed. "The bad thing gets louder."

"Yes. Outside the valley, the crown-thing can reach you more easily. Feel you. Maybe even pull at you." I crouched to her height, which put my knees in negotiation with the frost. "And I change too. The valley has kept my... my voice asleep. Outside, it wakes. I don't know what that will feel like. I'm telling you honestly: I don't know."

"Is it like the scar?" she asked. "When it wakes up? Like cold water?"

The child had a gift for images that cut to the bone. "Maybe. Yes. Probably like that."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "Cold water isn't so bad. You get used to it. Grandma Inge used to make me wash my face in the stream every morning, even in winter. She said it reminded your body it was alive."

Grandma Inge. The woman who had carried this child half across the kingdom before the knights took her — though Wren didn't know the taking part, only that Grandma hadn't woken up one morning in a barn outside the valley, and I hadn't yet found the courage to explain. Another debt for the ledger.

"All right," I said, standing. "Then let's go remind our bodies."

* * *

The boundary stones stood at the northern lip of the valley, where the land climbed into a saddle between two shoulders of grey mountain. Twelve of them, granite, older than any kingdom — Maera had raised them in a single night of working I still dreamed about, her white hair loose, her voice going out of her in ribbons as she laid the warding into the rock. By morning she couldn't speak above a whisper, and within the season she couldn't speak at all, and she spent her final years writing notes to me on slate until her hands forgot how letters worked.

That is what closing a door costs, when the door is made of language. I had watched it happen. I had promised her I would stay inside the room forever.

The stones looked ordinary in the morning light. Lichen-crusted, sheep-rubbed, utterly unremarkable unless you knew what you were looking at, in which case they looked like twelve teeth clenched around a secret.

I stopped three paces out and made myself breathe.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about breaking an oath: it doesn't feel dramatic from the inside. Or maybe it does for other people. For me it felt like standing at the edge of cold water in childhood, knowing the plunge would steal your breath and knowing also that everyone was watching and you could not climb back down the ladder without shame. Except there was no one watching now except a girl and some sheep, and the shame was all mine either way, and the water was thirteen years deep.

"Maera," I said quietly, to the stones, to the valley, to the dead. "Forgive me. Or don't. But I'm done being safe while a child carries a monster's key in her hand and men ride the ridges looking for her. You taught me better than that. You just also taught me the staying, and I learned the wrong lesson harder. That's on me."

The wind moved through the kale patch below, and I chose to hear it as an answer.

I took Wren's hand. Her palm — the unscarred one — was warm and slightly sticky with currant juice, and I thought, absurdly, that this was the hand I was staking everything on, and that Maera would have found it hilarious.

"Ready?"

"I'm ready," Wren said. "Are you?"

"Absolutely not. Let's go."

We crossed the line together.

* * *

I want to describe what happened next, and I find that language, which I have wielded like a weapon my whole life, fails me precisely here — which is either irony or mercy, depending on the day.

The first sensation was cold. Not the cold of frost or water but the cold of a cellar door opening onto winter — a pouring, rushing cold that went into me through the soles of my feet and up through the roots of my teeth. Magic. Thirteen years of it, dammed up beyond the stones, finding the breach and flooding home into the dry channel of me. It hurt. It hurt like blood returning to a limb gone numb, all pins and burning, and I heard myself make a sound that was half gasp and half laugh and entirely undignified, and my knees buckled, and Wren caught me — eight years old, forty-some pounds of determined child — and held me up with both hands and a scowl of concentration.

Because it wasn't only me. The moment we stepped past the twelfth stone, the valley's protection died behind us — I felt it go out like a snuffed candle, felt the warding unravel from the granite, felt the whole sheltering hush of the place dissolve into the wider world's noise. And into that sudden openness poured everything the boundary had been holding back: the low constant murmur of the living earth, the chatter of water and weather, the vast slow attention of mountains, and threaded through it all, faint and far and unmistakable, the whisper of the crown-thing turning its attention north like a lighthouse beam.

And Wren, standing in the flood of it, caught me and held me, and the scar on her palm glowed faintly gold, and she did not scream. She breathed. In and out, deliberate, the way I would teach a student to breathe before speaking.

"Cold water," she said, through her teeth. "You get used to it."

"You remarkable creature," I managed.

Behind us the valley lay open now, naked to the sky, its stones just stones. Anyone could walk in. Anything. The sanctuary was spent; Maera's great work undone in one stride by the woman she'd saved. I looked back at my garden, small and frosted and beautiful at the bottom of its bowl of hills, and I grieved it the way you grieve a parent — with gratitude and guilt in equal measure, knowing you cannot give it back what it gave you.

Then I turned south, because grief is a poor traveling companion and hope is worse company but better direction, and because somewhere ahead of us waited nine gates, a broken king, a traitor I hadn't met yet, and a rite that would cost me everything I had left.

One thing at a time. That was always Maera's rule.

"Can you still walk?" Wren asked.

"I can walk."

"Good. Because I think we should hurry." She pointed with her chin at the southern horizon, where the road dropped away toward the lowlands, and I followed her gaze, and my stomach performed a slow, cold roll.

Far off, at the very limit of sight, a smudge of dust moved along the king's road. Too organized for weather. Too fast for merchants. And beneath my newly waking senses, faint as a bell heard through a wall, came a sound I knew from nightmares: the rhythm of hooves keeping time, and under the hoofbeats, threading them, something that was almost a voice saying, patiently, hungrily, in the old tongue —

*Found you.*

"Well," I said, and pulled the oilcloth off the oath-blade, and felt the old words stir behind my teeth for the first time in thirteen years, rusty and furious and gloriously alive. "Run."
