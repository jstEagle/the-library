## 10. Road of Small Mercies

The lady Noor snores.

I want to write that down first, before anything else, because everything after it is going to sound very serious, and I think it's important that somebody remembers she snores. Like a big old dog. Like the miller's dog in Harrow's Bend, who used to sleep in the flour dust and snore so hard his whiskers moved. That's her. The last oath-bound swordspeaker. Terror of kings. Snorer.

I keep a list of things in my head now. It started because Noor said, "Pay attention, Wren. Everything you notice might matter later." So I pay attention. But mostly what I notice is that the world outside the valley is both bigger and smaller than I imagined. Bigger because of the mountains. Smaller because most people are just like Marta — tired, and kind when nobody's watching.

Marta was the first one. The first small mercy, I mean. We'd been walking three days when we came down out of the pass and saw the village of Lowmere sitting in its bowl of fields like a dropped button. Noor went stiff the way she does, all sharp edges, and said, "We say nothing about where we're from. You're my niece. Your name is Wren. That part's true enough."

"It's the only part that's true," I said.

"Good," she said. "Start with truth. It makes the lies sit straighter." Which sounds wise until you realize she says things like that on purpose, to make me laugh so I forget to be scared. It works more than half the time.

Lowmere had a tithe-house. I didn't know what that was. Now I know what that is everywhere: a squat stone building with a crown carved over the door, and a line of farmers outside it carrying sacks of grain they could not spare, and inside, men with white sashes and bored faces writing numbers in books. One sack in ten, the sign said. Every tenth egg, every tenth lamb, every tenth coin.

"They call it the King's Share," Noor muttered. "It goes to feed the capital's garrisons. It has fed them for thirteen years."

"Is the king hungry?" I asked. "He looks fat in the pictures on the signs."

Noor made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost not. "No, little one. He isn't hungry. Something else is."

I thought about that for a long time while we walked past the tithe-house and an old woman pressed half a loaf into my hands without stopping her walk or meeting my eyes, like kindness was something you did in the dark so it wouldn't get taxed too.

* * *

Here is the thing I haven't told Noor yet, or maybe I've told her without words, because she watches me the way hawks watch hedgerows.

Flowers follow me.

Not on purpose. They just come up wherever I sleep. In Lowmere we stayed in the barn of a farmer named Col who charged us nothing but asked Noor to look at his lame mule, and in the morning there were crocuses pushing up through the barn floor boards. Crocuses. In late autumn. Under a barn.

Col stood over them with his mouth open and then looked at me and then at Noor, and Noor said, very calmly, "Unusual soil," and gave him two coppers for the trouble, and we left before his wife woke up. But as we went through the gate I heard him behind us saying, "Soil. Aye. Soil," in the voice of a man talking himself off a ledge.

I'm sorry about the barn floor. Truly. But I can't help it any more than I can help dreaming. When I sleep, something in me leaks out, and the ground drinks it, and the ground gets ideas. Ideas with petals.

It got worse in Tallowford. In Tallowford I dreamed about my mother — I don't remember much about her, just hands and humming and the smell of bread, which is probably more than some orphans get — and when I woke up the entire yard of the inn had gone to meadow. Poppies and cornflowers and one enormous sunflower leaning against the well like a drunk uncle. The innkeeper's daughter found me standing in the middle of it and instead of screaming she knelt down and picked a cornflower and put it in my hair and said, "You're a witch's child. My gran said witches' children bloom where they grieve."

"I'm not grieving," I said.

"You are," she said, matter-of-fact, eight-going-on-wise, "you just don't know what about yet." Then her mother called her in and she ran off, and I kept the flower in my hair all day even though it wilted by noon, because wilting flowers still count if someone gave them to you.

That night Noor sat beside me on the inn's narrow bed — one bed between us, because we had four coppers total — and she didn't ask about the yard. She just said, "When you dream, do you hear it? The scar?"

Sometimes I do. There's a whisper that lives in the crown-shaped mark on my palm, thin as thread pulled tight. Mostly it says numbers. Counts. It counts moons. Sometimes it says words in the old tongue, the language I spoke when I fell half-frozen into Noor's garden, the language that made her go white as the frost. And once — only once — it said my name, in a voice like a king being polite to a prisoner.

"No," I told Noor. "It's quiet."

She knew I was lying. She let me lie anyway, which is its own kind of mercy, and put her hand over mine, over the scar, and her hand was warm and sword-callused and steady, and I slept, and in the morning the innkeeper charged us nothing because, he said, he hadn't seen flowers like that since his wedding, and his wife cried and wouldn't say why, and we left Tallowford richer in bread and cheese than we'd arrived.

* * *

The villages blur together after that, but the mercies don't. I keep the list.

A boy at a crossroads shrine who traded me his lucky acorn for one of my buttons, and I think I got the better deal, and I hope he thinks so too.

A ferryman who took us across the Greywater for free because Noor fixed the crack in his hull with a bit of wire and a lot of swearing, and afterward he told us the river used to have spirits in it, before the crown-tithe boats came, and now it just has fish and taxes.

A widow in Ashcombe who fed us stew and talked the whole time and needed no answers, which Noor said afterward was the best conversation she'd had in years.

And in Ashcombe, too, the bad thing, which I should write down because lists that only hold good things start to lie. The tithe-men came while we were eating. Three of them, white-sashed, taking the tenth lamb from a pen while a farm family watched, and the smallest child cried, and the father held her face against his coat so she couldn't watch either. And the tithe-man wrote in his book, unhurried, like he was counting spoons. Nobody argued. Everybody had the look of people who had argued once, long ago, and buried it somewhere under the floorboards.

I felt my scar heat up. I don't know why. Maybe the crown-thing doesn't like seeing its own collection taken. Maybe it likes it. I couldn't tell which, and that frightened me worse than the soldiers ever had — not knowing whose side the thing on my hand was on.

Noor's hand closed over my wrist under the table. "Breathe," she said, barely a breath herself. "Not here. Not for lambs. Save it." And I understood she meant: save yourself. Save the whole terrible purpose you're carrying around in your skin. But I hated her a little bit right then, too, for saying it, and I think she knew, and I think she'd been hated before, by better people than me, and carried that fine.

* * *

One night we slept in a hay barn belonging to no one, halfway between Ashcombe and the hills, and Noor taught me letters by firefly-light, scratching in the dirt with a stick. She said every speaker of the old order learned their letters the same way, kneeling in dirt, because "words written in ink think too highly of themselves."

"That's not true," I said.

"No," she agreed. "But it's a good lie. Lies that teach are half-true."

I asked her, then, because the fireflies were low and the dark was friendly, "What happens to me? After? If we do the unbinding and the crown breaks. What am I then?"

She was quiet a long time. A firefly landed on her knee and she didn't brush it off.

"I don't know," she said finally. "Nobody knows. There's never been a key that lived." And when I must have gone stiff, because she went on quickly, "But I'll tell you what I know about endings, Wren. I've watched an ending. My whole order ended, and I survived it, and do you know what I learned? That what comes after is never what the songs say. It's smaller and stranger and it takes years, and then one morning you're planting onions and you realize you're happy and you don't know when it happened."

"Onions," I said.

"Onions," she said solemnly. "All great happiness is built on onions."

I laughed until the fireflies scattered, and she pretended to be offended, and that night I dreamed of nothing at all — no crown, no whispers, no flowers — just a garden with rows and rows of green coming up, and somebody humming, and I woke up warm.

* * *

We saw the capital before we meant to. We crested a hill at dusk on the ninth day and there it was, far off across a plain full of tithe-wagons crawling toward it like beetles: Nine Gates City, walls pale gold in the last light, and above the tallest tower the palace with its bell spires, and I swear — I swear on the acorn, which is the luckiest thing I own — I heard the bells from that far away. Faint, wrong, ringing slightly out of step with each other, like nine clocks that had stopped agreeing.

My scar burned cold.

"What is it?" Noor said, and her hand went to her rusted blade the way it always did, though there was nothing on the hill but wind and us.

"The bells," I said. "They sound sad."

"Bells can't be sad."

"These ones are. They ring like they're sorry." And the whisper in my palm stirred and said, clear as a spoken word for the first time since the valley:

*Come home.*

I closed my fist. Noor looked at my hand, then at the city, then at me, and I watched her decide something, the decision moving across her face like weather.

"We camp here tonight," she said. "Tomorrow, pilgrims' road. Disguises. Rules." She counted them off on her fingers. "You speak as little as possible. You touch nothing growing. And if anyone asks about your palm—"

"I'm a burn scar from the kitchen," I recited. "I know."

"And Wren." She crouched to my height, which she only does when something matters. The city bells drifted faint and sorrowful across the plain. "Whatever it says to you — however it says it — you tell me. Even if it's frightening. Especially then. Promise me."

Behind us the sun went down. Ahead of us the bells rang their sorry rings, and the whisper curled warm around my wrist like a cat that has decided to live with you whether you agree or not.

"I promise," I said.

And far away in the golden city, something ancient and patient turned its attention toward our little hill, and began, softly, to count.
