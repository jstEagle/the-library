## 11. The City of Nine Gates

The road to the capital was paved, which should have been a mercy after three weeks of mud, but I've never trusted roads that go out of their way to be smooth. Roads like that want something from you. This one wanted pilgrims, and so we became them.

Wren had the idea, or rather Wren had the look — the particular wide-eyed tilt of the head she'd developed somewhere around the second week on the road, when she'd watched a tinker mend a kettle and decided that watching people was a skill worth cultivating. She pointed at a procession of gray-cloaked walkers trudging past us toward the horizon and said, in her careful new common-tongue, "They are going to the bells."

"They're going to be disappointed," I said. "Bells ring once and then they're done."

But the tithe-villages we'd passed had all mentioned it. The thirteenth moon's renewal was coming, and the faithful were walking to the City of Nine Gates to see the king renew his vows. Thirteen turns since his coronation. Thirteen years of the crown-thing feeding, if my arithmetic and my nightmares agreed.

So we bought gray wool off a widow who asked no questions because we overpaid, and Wren cut my hair with the garden shears I'd carried out of the valley like an idiot, because apparently even in flight I couldn't leave a tool behind, and by the time the city walls rose out of the morning haze like the spine of some sleeping animal, we looked exactly like what we weren't: two pilgrims, one old scarred woman and one small quiet child, come to worship at the feet of a king who wasn't there anymore.

* * *

Nine gates, they said, though only seven admitted ordinary folk. We joined the queue at the Pilgrim's Gate behind a family who smelled of onions and devotion in equal measure, and I spent the wait doing what I do best, which is memorizing exits.

It's an old habit. The order trained it into us before we could write our own names: *enter every room knowing how you'll leave it.* In the valley I'd unlearned most things — the forms, the words, the watchfulness — but not that one. So while the onion family argued about whether the king would ride or walk during the renewal, I counted guards, noted the archers on the wall, marked where the gate-wardens' sightlines crossed and where they didn't.

There were more guards than a coronation needed and fewer than a siege required. That middle number worried me more than either extreme would have. It said the crown expected trouble it preferred not to name aloud.

"Your papers?" The gate-warden was a young man with a voice like wet gravel and the bored eyes of someone who'd checked ten thousand pilgrims and found nothing worth remembering in any of them.

"Pilgrim's writ," I said, and produced the scrap Torvald — no. Not Torvald. I still caught myself thinking his name with warmth sometimes, like a tongue probing a missing tooth. A friend in the last village had written it for us, a tithe-broken scribe with ink-stained fingers and a grudge, who'd forged the writ in exchange for bread and my promise to burn the whole court down. I'd promised the bread part honestly.

The warden glanced at it. Glanced at Wren. And here my heart did something acrobatic and unbecoming, because Wren chose that exact moment to speak.

"Why does the king wear a heavy hat?"

The question was perfect — child-shaped, innocent, devastating. Because I knew what she was really asking. *Why does he carry it? Why doesn't he throw it away?* The scar on her palm had been talking all morning; I'd seen her pressing it flat against her skirt as we walked, the way you'd press down a splinter.

"Heavy hats keep thoughts in," I told her gravely, and the warden snorted, waved us through, and went back to being bored at the next pilgrim in line.

We passed under the gate's shadow, and the light changed, and I got my first real look at the City of Nine Gates.

* * *

I'll say this plainly, because everything afterward depends on it: the city was beautiful. Not beautiful the way the valley was beautiful — earned beauty, seasonal beauty, beauty you participated in by weeding — but beautiful the way a jewel is beautiful, all surface and no soil. White stone and copper roofs gone green with age. Canals threading between buildings like silver thread through dark cloth. Statues in every square, and here's where the wrongness started creeping in, because every statue was of the king.

Every single one. Aldric the Lawgiver. Aldric the Merciful. Aldric Crowned in Light, arms spread like a man welcoming rain. All of them carved within the last thirteen years, judging by the sharpness of their chisel-marks, and all of them wearing expressions of serene benevolence that no living man sustains for long. Real faces have weather. Real smiles have effort in them. These faces were smooth as eggs.

"They're all him," Wren whispered, tugging my sleeve. "But none of them are him." Then, quieter, hand going to her palm: "He says they're accurate."

I crouched beside her among the pilgrim traffic and took both her hands in mine. "What else does he say?"

"That the statues don't eat." A pause, her brow furrowing with the effort of translation — the crown spoke in the old tongue, and Wren rendered it into common the way you'd render a fish into poetry, imperfectly and with visible distress. "That eating is for... below. Underneath. For the parts that grow."

"The parts that grow," I repeated.

"People," Wren said simply, the way children say terrible things, and stood up, and asked if we could get honey cakes, because she was eight, and horror and hunger share a stomach at that age.

I bought her honey cakes. What else was I going to do?

* * *

We lodged at a pilgrim-house near the fourth gate — the Widow's Rest, run by a broad, silent woman named Maren whose eyes catalogued everyone who entered and gave nothing back. Serah Vane's letter had gotten us the room; the captain had sent word ahead through channels she hadn't explained, and I'd learned not to ask. An oathless knight who served the king but not the crown had channels everywhere, apparently, like water finding cracks.

Maren set down our supper — bread, stew, small beer — and lingered just long enough to say, without inflection, "Renewal ceremony's in four days. Palace grounds closed from tomorrow. After that, nobody in or out without court writ."

"And before tomorrow?" I asked.

Her eyes flicked to Wren, then back to me. "Before tomorrow, the city belongs to whoever's brave enough to take it."

She left. I ate my stew and thought about the architecture of traps.

* * *

That evening we heard the bells.

Every account of the capital mentions the bells. Nine towers, nine great bells, rung at dawn and dusk and on feast days, and the sound is supposed to be the most lovely thing in the kingdom — the chroniclers go misty about it, all harmony and grace, the voice of the city rising over the rooftops.

The bells rang at dusk, and they were nearly lovely. Nearly.

There was something underneath the harmony, some fraction of a beat off, one bell lagging the others by less than a heartbeat, so that instead of a chord you got a chord with a shadow. Most people wouldn't hear it. Their ears would smooth it over the way eyes smooth over the blind spot. But I had spent thirty years listening for falseness in spoken oaths, and falseness was the one thing I had never lost the ear for, even in the valley, even in silence.

"It's the little bell," Wren said. She was standing at the window, honey cake forgotten, scar-palm pressed flat against the sill. "The ninth one. It keeps trying to say something else."

"What does it try to say?"

She listened, head cocked, and her face went through several small weather systems before settling on puzzlement. "A name. But it's a name that's also a sentence. Like—" she made a frustrated sound, eight years old and asked to translate the apocalypse, "—like a door that's also walking."

The first oath ever spoken, gone rotten. Wren had told me that much on the road, dredged up from wherever the crown leaked into her. I hadn't understood it then. Standing in that room with the bells ringing their almost-chord over the city, I began to understand it the way you begin to understand cold — from the outside in, slowly, dreadfully. A name that was a sentence. A word that was a binding. The first promise anyone ever made, twisted until the promise itself did the promising, wore the wearer, fed on every oath sworn in its master's name.

The bells weren't ringing. They were reciting. And the city below them — the white stone, the green roofs, the ten thousand candles already kindling in windows — knelt inside the recitation without knowing it.

"Wren." I kept my voice level, gardener-calm, the voice I used for transplanting seedlings. "Tomorrow we find the archivist. Tomorrow we learn the rite. Tonight—"

"Tonight I'm tired," she said, with the iron finality of childhood, and climbed into bed, and was asleep between one breath and the next, because children can do that, put down unbearable things like tools at day's end.

I sat up a long while, listening to the ninth bell try to say its name-sentence, and planned how to steal a kingdom's truth from under its own roof.

* * *

We saw the king the next morning, and I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only weapon I have left that doesn't rust.

It was accident, mostly. The palace held public audience on the plaza of the First Gate each renewal-eve — a tradition older than the crown-thing, from when kings had been men who needed seeing to be believed. Crowds packed the plaza, and we were in it, two gray pilgrims among five hundred, close enough to the dais to see clearly and far enough to be nobody.

King Aldric came out onto the balcony at midmorning, and the crowd roared, and I felt the roar move through me like water through reeds and hated myself for joining it.

He was tall. He was composed. He wore the renewal whites and no visible crown at all, which startled me until I remembered — the crown-thing didn't always show itself. It showed itself when it wished to be seen, and to this crowd, presumably, its absence read as humility. *Our king needs no crown. Our king IS the crown.* I could hear the sermon already.

And then he moved.

It was small, at first. He raised his hand to bless the crowd, a gesture surely performed ten thousand times, and the motion was right — wrist, elbow, shoulder, all correct — but the sequence between the parts was wrong. There was a hesitation at each joint, tiny, fractional, like a puppeteer counting under his breath. Raise. Pause. Turn. Pause. Extend. Each piece correct, the music between them missing.

No living man moves like that. Living men flow, even clumsy ones, even dying ones. Movement is the one honest thing about us; you can fake a smile but you cannot fake the way weight travels through a body that wants to travel it.

Aldric's body didn't want anything. Something else wanted, and the body obeyed, and obedience had worn grooves into him so deep that even the wanting-parts had eroded smooth.

Beside me, Wren had gone rigid. Her hand crept into mine, and her palm — the scar-palm — was hot as a fevered forehead. "He's holding still," she whispered. "Inside. He's holding very, very still, so it won't feel him."

"Feel him do what?"

"I don't know." Her eyes were enormous, fixed on the balcony. "But he's been holding still for a long time. He's tired. He's so tired, Noor, he's like a man holding up a ceiling."

On the balcony, the king completed his blessing. His arm descended. And then — I would have missed it if I hadn't spent a lifetime reading bodies, if grief and training hadn't conspired to make me a scholar of the small dishonest motions people make — his head turned. Just slightly. Away from the crowd, toward the interior of the palace, toward the dark doorway behind him.

And for one instant — one — the face changed. Not the serene egg-smooth mask. Something underneath it surfaced, something with weather in it, with effort, with a man's exhausted humanity, and the mouth shaped a word. No sound came. But I read lips the way I read oaths, and the shape of it was unmistakable.

*Hold.*

Then the mask slid back down, serene as a statue, and King Aldric raised his other hand to bless the left side of the crowd, joint by counted joint, and the crowd wept with love for him.

I stood very still in that roaring plaza, a child's hot hand in mine, and understood two things with terrible clarity.

The first: the man inside was not gone. He was holding up a ceiling, and he had been for thirteen years, and he was running out of strength.

The second: the renewal ceremony was in three days, and whatever rite renewed the crown-thing's hold would also, I now guessed, drain what remained of the man holding still beneath it. We hadn't come to the city a season too early or a year too late.

We had come exactly in time to watch a king finish drowning — unless we learned, quickly, how to break the water.

I turned to Wren to tell her we were leaving, that we had an archivist to find and no time to find him politely.

But Wren wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the palace's high eastern tower, where a figure in archivist's gray stood at a window — a thin old man, half-shadowed, watching the crowd with the expression of someone conducting a private inventory.

Even at that distance, I knew him. Thirty years changes a face, but it doesn't change the way a man holds his shoulders when he's pretending not to know you.

Torvald. My mentor's debtor. Our hope with fine print.

And he was looking directly at Wren — not at the crowd, not at the king, but at my child, my key, my burden — with an expression I had last seen on the face of a merchant appraising a horse.

I tightened my grip on Wren's hand and began, quietly, to move us backward into the crowd.

Too late, I noticed that Torvald had not come to his window alone. Behind him in the shadows of the tower room, something glinted — metal, ranks of it, helmets lined up like teeth.

Someone had already told the palace we were coming.
