## 12. The Archivist's Debt

The City of Nine Gates had a way of making you feel like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up.

Wren and I came through the pilgrims' gate at midmorning, in the gray wool of the Order of the Quiet Furrow — a farming order that had never existed, invented by Serah Vane three nights ago over a campfire with the ease of a woman who lied for a living. Pilgrims were tolerated in Aldric's capital the way rain was tolerated: unavoidable, mildly inconvenient, expected to leave eventually.

"Keep your eyes down," Serah murmured, walking half a step behind us with her sword wrapped in burlap like a bolt of cloth. "Pilgrims who gawk get questions. Questions get records. Records get read by people I'd rather not know we're here."

I kept my eyes down. Mostly. It's hard not to look up when you've spent thirteen years in a valley where the tallest thing is an apple tree, and suddenly there are towers — nine of them, one for each gate, each crowned with bells that rang the hours slightly wrong. Not broken-wrong. *Deliberate*-wrong. A quarter-tone flat, so that every hour of your life in this city sounded faintly like a word you almost remembered.

"They're tuned to the crown," I said quietly, when we'd put two streets between us and the gate.

Serah glanced at me. "You know that how?"

"I don't. My hands do." I flexed my fingers inside my gloves. Since crossing the boundary stones, since magic had come back into me like cold water poured down the throat, my body remembered things my oath had made me forget. Swordspeech lived in the hands before it lived in the mouth. And my hands said: those bells are a leash. Every hour, on the hour, they pulled something taut somewhere in the palace, and something at the top of it flinched.

Wren walked between us with her hood up and her scarred palm hidden in her sleeve, which she'd started doing without being told, the way animals hide wounds. She hadn't slept well since we entered sight of the walls. She said the scar "hummed," which was a child's word for it, but children are often more precise than archivists.

"Where first?" Serah asked.

"The archive."

"The royal archive." She said it flatly. "In the palace district."

"Not in the palace itself. The old scriptorium, off the Bell Court. It used to be ours, once." Ours being a word that needed unpacking, and I wasn't ready to unpack it in the middle of a market street where a fruit-seller was watching us with the idle suspicion of a man who charged pilgrims double. "There's a man there named Torvald. He owes my mentor a debt he can never repay, which means he'll either help us or have us arrested, depending on how the interest has compounded."

"That's reassuring," Serah said.

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be honest."

Wren tugged my sleeve. Her voice, when she spoke the common tongue, still had the other language underneath it, like a stone under moss. "Is he kind?"

I looked down at her. Eight years old, frost-bitten ears healing pink, carrying the end of a king on the skin of her hand, asking me if the stranger we were about to beg help from was *kind*.

"I don't know," I said. "He was, once. People change in cities."

"People change in valleys too," she said, with the terrible fairness of children, and I had no answer for that, because she was talking about me, and she was right.

* * *

The old scriptorium sat in the shadow of the Third Gate's bell tower, wedged between a chandler's and a moneylender's like a book nobody wanted shelved. Its windows were tall and grimy and its door was oak gone silver with age. Over the lintel, worn nearly smooth by weather and deliberate neglect, someone had once carved a phrase in the tongue before kingdoms. I could still read it, because apparently I could still read everything now that my oath was cracked open like an egg.

*Words outlive the mouths that make them.*

"Cheerful place," Serah muttered.

Inside smelled of dust, tallow, and vellum — the smell of preserved time. Racks of scrolls rose toward a vaulted ceiling lost in shadow, and among them moved perhaps a dozen figures, scribes and copyists, heads bent over their work like a field of bowed stalks.

And at a desk near the far wall, beneath the one window with light worth having, sat a man I recognized even after twenty years.

Torvald had been young when my mentor sent him away from the last conclave with a sealed case and orders not to stop riding until he reached the capital. He'd been lean then, sharp-faced, all elbows and certainty. Now he was broad and soft and balding, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on his nose, and he was arguing with a pigeon that had gotten into the rafters.

"—for the last time, if you desecrate the Merethic annals I will have you plucked and roasted, I swear it on—" He stopped. Because he'd seen us. Or rather, he'd seen Wren, and then seen me, and whatever his face did next, it did slowly, like a door deciding whether to open or lock.

"Noor Iversen," he said.

"Hello, Torvald."

"You're dead," he said. "Officially. There's a memorial tablet in the west cloister. Very tasteful. I dusted it myself, twice a year, out of guilt."

"The guilt was warranted, then."

"Was it?" He stood, and I watched him take stock of us — the farm-order wool, the wrapped blade, the small hooded girl pressed against my side — and I watched him arrive, visibly, at conclusions. Torvald had always been quick. It was his best quality and, as I would learn, his worst. "Close the door," he said to no one in particular, and a junior scribe scrambled to do it. "My office. All three of you. And you—" this to Serah, whose posture screamed soldier no matter what she wore, "—you'll want to unwrap whatever that is before we talk, because I don't negotiate with parcels."

His office was small, overheated, and crammed floor to ceiling with ledgers, most of them concerning matters that had nothing to do with history and everything to do with grain tariffs, which apparently was what the order's old archives became when the order stopped existing. He poured wine — four cups, unasked — and set one in front of Wren, watered it heavily without comment, and only then sat down and looked at me across the desk like a man preparing to settle accounts twenty years overdue.

"Your mentor," he said, "saved my life. You know that?"

"She never told me why it needed saving."

"No. She wouldn't." He turned his cup a slow circle. "I was nineteen and certain I knew better than the whole order. There was a text I wasn't cleared for. I copied it anyway. Took it out of the deep stacks. Your mistress caught me in the copying room at midnight with wet ink on my hands, and instead of reporting me — which would have meant the oath-quiet, my speech taken, my memory folded shut like a letter — she took the blame herself. Said *she'd* left the seal loose. Paid for it with standing she could ill afford, in the last years, when the order was bleeding members and everyone was looking for someone to suspect." He shrugged, a small, unhappy motion. "Then the end came, and she sent me here with a case I was never to open, and instructions: *if ever a woman named Iversen comes to you wearing the face of the dead, you owe her whatever she asks.*"

"And you've spent twenty years wondering if anyone would collect."

"I've spent twenty years hoping no one would," he said honestly, "and feeling vile about the hoping. So." He spread his ink-stained hands. "Collect. But understand what you're asking, Noor. This city is not a valley. The stones here listen. The bells here report. If you've come for what I think you've come for—"

"A king," I said, "who is being worn."

The words landed in the room and stayed there. Somewhere beyond the door, the pigeon cooed, oblivious.

Torvald took his spectacles off and polished them on his robe, which gave him somewhere to look that wasn't my face. "Thirteen turns," he said quietly. "That's how long the renewal rite has run. Thirteen full moons since the coronation, each one a re-knotting. The court calls it the Vigil of Continuity. The king stands in the coronation hall under the great lantern and swears his oath again, and the bells ring it flat across the city, and everyone goes home grateful and vaguely ill without knowing why." He put the spectacles back on. "I file the ceremony records myself. Do you know what I've noticed, across thirteen filings? The signature changes."

"Kings don't sign differently year to year," Serah said.

"This one does. Year one, the hand is strong. By year four there's a tremor in the descenders. By year eight the letters start leaning — all of them, uniformly, like grass bending under wind. Last moon's renewal charter—" he opened a drawer, hesitated, closed it again. "Last moon's charter was signed in a hand I would swear belonged to a different man entirely. Except the oath-seal burned into the wax is older than any of them. Older than the dynasty. Older than paper, if you follow me."

I followed him. Wren's small hand found mine under the desk, and her scarred palm was warm. Warmer than it should have been.

"There's a thing on his head," I said. "An oath that went rotten. First oath ever spoken, if Wren's right, and I believe her, because she carries its shape in her skin. It eats oaths sworn in the king's name to keep itself fastened. And every full moon it renews the lease."

"Which is why the tithe-villages starve," Serah said, low and hard. "Which is why the granaries empty while the court feasts. It's not greed. Greed has a shape you can fight. This is *feeding*."

Torvald was staring at Wren now, and I saw the archivist in him wrestling the man, and losing. "Child," he said gently, "may I?" He didn't reach for her; he held out his own hand instead, palm up, letting her choose. That cost him something, I thought. Curiosity like his doesn't wait well.

Wren looked at me. I nodded. She laid her hand in his.

The crown-shaped scar had grown. When she'd arrived at my gate on the first frost, it had been the size of a thumbnail. Now it spanned nearly her whole palm, fine lines like filigree, and at its center the skin was pale as candle wax. As Torvald bent close, the lines moved — barely, slowly, the way frost creeps across glass — and Torvald went very still, the way men go still when they realize the thing they're looking at is also looking at them.

"Vashta il morren," Wren whispered. *It sees through the seeing.*

Torvald released her hand and sat back and drank his entire cup of wine in one motion.

"All right," he said, hoarse. "*All right.* You want the rite. The true unbinding — not the court version, not the sanitized liturgy they print for pilgrims. I can give you that. It survives in the deep stacks, misfiled as an agricultural survey from the Second Kingdom, because your mentor's generation hid it and mine forgot to keep hiding it properly." He was already up, moving along the shelves, pulling ledgers, his fear transmuted into motion. "Here's what you need. Three things, no fewer, no substitutes."

He counted them off on ink-stained fingers.

"A **speaker** — someone who can voice the words of the old tongue with authority. Not parroting. Authority. The words have to be *owed*, if you take my meaning. The first oath was spoken freely, and only what was spoken freely can be unspoken freely."

"A **key** — a living vessel the crown-thing has already touched and marked. It must carry the shape of the binding. Without the key, the words have nothing to grip." He glanced at Wren and then, deliberately, at the window. "Poor lamb."

"And a **threshold** — ground where the original oath was spoken, or ground consecrated to the same purpose. Binding-ground. The rite performed anywhere else will either fail or—" he paused, choosing his words with visible care, "—or work sideways. There are stories from the Third Kingdom about rites worked sideways. They involve villages. None of the stories end with villages."

"So we need the coronation hall," Serah said. "During the renewal. On the thirteenth full moon."

"The hall is consecrated ground, yes — the oldest binding-site in the Nine Cities, built over the ruins of the First Court itself. And the renewal is in eleven days." Torvald turned from the shelf, a ledger hugged to his chest, and I saw the fine print coming before he said it, the way you see weather coming. "But hear the rest, Noor. The speaker pays. Unbinding is unmaking in miniature. Whatever faculty the speaker uses to hold the words — for a swordspeaker, that's swordspeech itself — the rite consumes. Permanently. The woman who wrote this account down—" he tapped the ledger, "—described it as *pouring the riverbed away with the flood*. You would speak the unbinding and then never speak powerfully again. Any words. Ever. Your voice would become just sound."

The room got very quiet. Wren's hand tightened in mine.

I found, to my surprise, that some part of me had already known. Had known since the boundary stones, maybe, when magic came back into me and felt less like a gift returned than a loan called due. The oath had always been a bargain. Bargains end.

"Fine," I said.

"*Noor*—" Serah started.

"It's fine. It's a tool. Tools wear out." I heard how flat my voice was and hated it. "Eleven days. We need to be inside that hall during the renewal, and I assume the guest list is short."

"Short," Torvald agreed. "Nobility, priesthood, the knightly orders — Vane's lot included, though I notice you're traveling with exactly one of those and she's supposed to be dead too, or at least disgraced—"

"Flatteringly informed," Serah said dryly.

"—and guarded by the oathless knights, who are not knights in any sense the old order would recognize. They're bound to the crown-thing directly. They feel it when it feels. Which brings me to the largest piece of fine print, and I'll thank you not to shoot the messenger, Captain." Torvald set the ledger down and looked at me, and for a moment I saw the nineteen-year-old under the soft middle-aged man, the boy who'd copied forbidden texts at midnight because certainty made him reckless.

"The key cannot simply be present at the rite. The key must be *willing*. The vessel consents, or the words slide off. And the crown-thing knows this. It's why it marked her instead of killing her, why it hunts her alive instead of dead. A willing vessel is the one ingredient it cannot manufacture." He paused. "And it also knows that the closer the key comes to the threshold, the louder it speaks through her. She's marked, Noor. From tonight onward, every step she takes toward that hall, that thing on the king's head will hear her coming like a drum."

Wren, who had understood perhaps half of this, tugged my sleeve and asked — in the old tongue, softly, so only I could follow — *"Will it hurt the king? When we say the words?"*

And because I could no longer lie to children, I answered in the same tongue: *"Some things hurt going in and hurt coming out. This is coming out."*

She nodded, solemn, and tucked her scarred hand back into her sleeve, and asked nothing else.

Torvald watched us exchange syllables he plainly recognized and couldn't parse, and something in his face folded. "There's one more thing," he said. "One more debt to declare before you decide to spend your mentor's credit. The renewal rite requires the king to stand unguarded for the duration of the swearing — the oathless withdraw, the hall seals, nothing enters or leaves until the crown is re-fastened. Three minutes, perhaps four. If you mean to act, you act inside that window, inside a sealed hall, surrounded by the entire court, against a thing that has eaten thirteen years of oaths and grown fat on them." He spread his hands. "That's the whole ledger. That's everything I owe and everything I know. Help me balance the books, Noor. What do you need from me?"

I should have said *passage*, or *plans*, or *names*. Instead I heard myself ask:

"How much did my mentor pay for your silence, Torvald? All those years ago. What did it cost her, covering for you?"

He blinked. Then, slowly: "Her seat at the high table. Her vote in the last conclave. Possibly—" his voice thinned, "—possibly the margin by which the order fell. She traded her standing for my future, and the order died a little faster for it, and I have done arithmetic on that every night for twenty years."

"Then you understand the price of covering for someone." I stood, and Wren stood with me, and outside the window the bells began to ring the hour — flat, deliberate, a quarter-tone wrong, pulling something taut somewhere above us. "You're going to cover for us, Torvald. Archive access, maps of the palace undercroft, the renewal schedule, and a face that gets us past the Bell Court checkpoint. And when we're done — whatever happens — you'll file our deaths yourself. Tastefully. Twice a year, out of guilt."

For the first time since we'd entered, Torvald smiled. It was not entirely a pleasant smile, but it was an honest one.

"My mentor always said you'd be the death of the order's good name," he said. "I'm gratified to report she was wrong. You'll be the death of something considerably worse."

He was reaching for a fresh sheet of parchment to draw the undercroft maps when the door shuddered — not knocked, *shuddered* — and every bell in the city, all nine towers at once, began to ring. Not the hour. Not any hour. A pattern, rapid and arrhythmic, like a heart stumbling.

Wren cried out and clutched her scarred hand to her chest.

Through the office door came the sound of running feet, and Torvald's junior scribe burst in white-faced, gasping the news from the Bell Court below:

"The palace — they're saying the king — the king has woken from sleep *screaming a name*, and the name is—"

He looked at Wren.

He didn't know her name. But he knew it was hers.
