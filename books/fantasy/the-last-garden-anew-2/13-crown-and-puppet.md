## 13. Crown and Puppet

There is a particular indignity in being a king who cannot trust his own hands.

I discovered it on a Tuesday, thirteen years ago, when my right arm rose without my permission and signed an order I had not written — the conscription of the millers' sons of Harrow Vale, every boy over twelve, to be marched north and sworn into my service. My service. As if the word still meant anything that belonged to me.

The arm did not tremble. That was the worst part. It moved with the calm certainty of a man who has done this ten thousand times, and in a sense it had, because the thing wearing my crown had done everything ten thousand times before I was born, and would do everything ten thousand times after I was gone. If I ever managed to be gone.

I sat at my desk that Tuesday and watched my own signature curl onto parchment like a snake writing its name, and I thought, with the strange clarity available only to men who have been dispossessed inside their own bodies: *so this is what a puppet feels from the inside. The strings are not on your arms. They are under your skin.*

Then the thought was gone. He took it. He takes most of them, the ones that might grow into something. What remains for me are small thoughts, dull hours, and the long grey corridors of memory where I keep the man I used to be, dusting him off each morning like a widower keeping a dead wife's room.

My name is Aldric. Third of that name. I tell you this because he never says it. To him I am *vessel*, or *the house*, or sometimes — when he is pleased, which is worse than when he is not — *my good seat*. Kings get called many things by flatterers. Only one of them has ever made me want to claw my own scalp off, and he wears it.

* * *

Let me describe him properly, since no one else can, since no one else knows he exists.

He looks, from the outside, like a crown. Gold and iron braided together, older than any smith's mark, heavy in the way that things are heavy when they remember being worshipped. Nine points, though courtiers swear there are seven, because two of them exist slightly out of step with the world, the way a bell's echo exists a half-moment behind the bell. When they set him on my head at my coronation — I was twenty-four, proud, freshly grieving my father, and so grateful for anything solid to hold — the weight was wrong. Not too heavy. Too *aware*. A crown should press down. This pressed *in*.

And then he settled against my skull like a cat finding its spot by the fire, and he said — not aloud, not in words, in the way a truth arrives whole and unwanted — *at last. Thirteen hundred years of waiting, and they send me a man with a soft head.*

That was his first joke. He tells jokes. Nobody warns you about that. You imagine possession as horror, all shrieking and contortion, but real possession is mostly administration. He runs the kingdom better than I ever did, I'll grant him that. Granaries full. Borders quiet. Justice swift and impartial, because impartiality costs him nothing — he has no friends to favor, no family to protect, nothing human left to be partial *to*. The realm has never been so orderly. Orderly the way a grave is tidy.

What does he do with it all, you ask? All that power, all those oaths? I'll tell you what I've pieced together across thirteen years of listening through my own ears.

He eats them.

Every oath sworn in my name feeds him. Every knight kneeling in the great hall — *in the king's name, by the king's word, on the king's justice* — every magistrate, every tithe-collector, every frightened village elder promising loyalty to save their fields. Each oath is a thread, and the threads run up through the air, invisible, and they end somewhere just above my scalp, and he drinks from them the way I once drank from a wineskin after a long ride. Slowly. Savoringly. With little sounds of satisfaction I feel rather than hear, like a hum in the bone behind my eyes.

A thousand threads a day, some days. Ten thousand during the tithe-seasons. And I stand at windows and watch the lines come in — I can see them now, I've learned to look sideways at the world, the trick of it — thin gold filaments rising off the bent backs of farmers who will never meet me, and flowing home to the thing on my head, and I think: *they think they're swearing to a king. They're feeding a leech that happens to sit where a king should.*

He grew stronger every year. I grew less. Do you know what it is to feel your memories reorganized? He doesn't take them all at once. He prunes. My mother's face went first — I knew I'd loved her, but the love arrived without the face attached, like a letter with the name torn off. Then my father's voice. Then the smell of rain on the palace gardens where I played as a boy. He kept the useful things — statecraft, languages, swordwork — and he kept the griefs, oddly. Griefs make a man compliant. A king who has forgotten why he smiles remembers perfectly why he weeps, and a weeping man signs whatever stops the weeping.

But here is what he does not know, what he cannot prune because he does not believe it matters: he missed one room in the house. There is a place in me — call it the cellar, call it the last lit window — where I still sit, and watch, and wait. He thinks the flickers he feels are indigestion. Dreams. The vessel creaking.

They are not creaking. They are *me*, and I am patient, and patience is the one weapon they cannot confiscate because it looks exactly like defeat.

* * *

This morning began like every morning: I woke inside my own body the way a tenant wakes in a house whose deed belongs to another, and I lay still and took inventory. Hands present. Voice mine until the first court function. Thoughts — three of my own before noon, a good day coming, I could feel it.

He was restless. That was new. For thirteen years his mornings have been smooth as poured cream; today there was a grain in it, a scratching at the edge of his attention, and through our terrible intimacy I could taste what scratched him.

Fear. He was afraid of something, and he did not know what.

Imagine that. Imagine being older than the kingdoms, older than the oaths, older than the language the oaths were first spoken in — and feeling fear arrive like a stranger at your door, and having no name to give it. His confusion came off him in waves I could surf. I lay in bed beneath silk and stone and let myself enjoy it for a full minute. A full minute! I haven't stolen a full minute of joy since before the millers' boys.

*Something walks,* he murmured, to himself, forgetting I was home. *Something old walks in old clothes.*

And then, sharper, almost hungry: *a tongue. An unbroken tongue. Here? Now?*

I didn't understand it. That's rare enough to record. Usually I understand half of what moves through him, the way you understand half of what your dog smells. But *tongue* — that word rang a bell in the cellar of me, some lesson from some tutor in some year he hasn't let me revisit. Old tongue. First tongue. Words from before the kingdoms, when speech itself was a kind of power and oaths were not requests but *acts*.

I filed it away. Filing things away is my whole life now. I am the greatest archivist in the realm and no one suspects it, because my archive is a skull.

* * *

At midmorning my body was required in the throne hall, because a delegation from the river provinces had traveled nine days to petition me about flood-tithe, and he never misses a tithe. I stood — he stood — while they knelt, forty of them, mud still on their boots from my clean marble, and their spokesman read out their grievances in a voice worn smooth by rehearsal.

And then he did the thing he does, the thing I am forced to attend.

"Rise," my mouth said, warmly. "Swear to me, and the flood-tithe shall be halved."

They swore. Of course they swore. Forty voices, ragged with relief, promising themselves to me in exchange for their livelihoods — what choice is that, what *choice* — and I felt the threads rise off them, forty golden filaments, and flow up, and I felt him drink.

It is always worst right after. The drinking. Because for a moment the hunger recedes and what's left underneath it shows through, the way water shows the stones when the current drops, and what's underneath is not majesty. It is appetite, vast and bottomless and *old*, and it is aimed at everything alive. In that moment I understood something I had never let myself understand: he is not a king-thing, not a spirit of sovereignty gone wrong. He is a mouth. He was the first promise anyone ever made to anyone else, back when words were young and binding, and he has spent thirteen centuries doing the only thing a promise can do if you twist it: taking more than was offered.

Forty threads. He sighed with pleasure through my lungs — my lungs, my breath, turned to a contented animal sound — and the delegation bowed out smiling, thinking they'd met their king, thinking the tall sad-eyed man on the throne had been *mercy itself*.

In the cellar, in the dark, I screamed. I have gotten very good at screaming silently. Thirteen years of practice. It changes nothing, but it keeps the scream from turning into something quieter and worse, which is agreement.

*You felt it too,* he said suddenly, conversationally, the way a jailer chats through the bars. *The tongue. Something spoke today. Or will speak. The city tastes of it.* A pause, and then, with a delight that curdled me: *I had nearly forgotten what I sounded like. Before they bound me into metal. Before the first betrayers hammered me down and called it mercy.*

Metal. Bound into metal. He had said too much — the hunger makes him loose — and I grabbed the fragments and ran with them into my cellar and hid them under the floorboards of my soul: *bound, hammered, first betrayers.* The crown was not always a crown. Someone had put him there. Someone had once defeated him well enough to imprison him, and had chosen imprisonment over destruction — why? Because destruction wasn't possible? Or because destroying him would destroy something else, something tangled with him, some root?

And now something walked in his city that tasted of the old tongue, the tongue from before binding, and he was *afraid*.

Good, I thought, from the dark, with all the dignity I had left, which was not much, but was mine.

* * *

The afternoon brought the bells.

You must understand: the bells of the capital are his instrument as much as anything. He tuned them himself, in my fourth year, through my hands and my decrees — retimed them, rehung them, and now they ring in patterns that settle the populace like a lullaby, that make obedience feel like music. The city loves its bells. The city is being sung to sleep, one peal at a time, and has been for a decade.

Today, for the space of nine strokes, the great bell of the Cathedral of Nine Gates rang *wrong*. Off its pattern. Off its note. A flat, cracked, ancient sound, like a bell remembering an older tower.

I felt him go rigid. I felt it through my own spine, which he inhabits, so that his rigidity is my stiffness and his terror is my cold hands — one more indignity, sharing a nervous system with the parasite. Nine strokes of wrongness, and in those nine strokes something in the city *answered*. Far away, faint, high — a voice or a bell or a voice like a bell, singing a shape he recognized, and the recognition went through him like a spear.

*No,* he whispered, into the marrow of my bones. *No. It was broken. We broke it. I watched them break it.*

*We,* I noted, filing even that. *We.* So he remembered the breaking. He remembered being on the side of the hammer, once, before he became the thing under it.

The bell corrected itself. The pattern resumed. The city sighed back into its lullaby, none the wiser. And he withdrew inward — deep inward, deeper than he usually bothers, down toward the root of me, hunting for whatever flicker of mine might know something, might have heard something, might be hiding knowledge in the cellar.

I showed him nothing. I have become very good at nothing. I arranged my inner rooms the way a peasant arranges a house for a tax collector: bare surfaces, honest poverty, nothing worth seizing. Let him ransack. There's a man sitting quietly in the last lit window with dust on his hands, and dust, if you're not looking, looks exactly like emptiness.

But I will tell you what I saw, in the instant before I closed the shutters. I saw what he reached for first. Not my memories. Not my secrets.

My *oath*. The coronation oath, the one I spoke at twenty-four with the crown already closing around my skull — *I swear by my blood and breath to bear this burden faithfully unto death* — he went to it first, the way a starving man goes to the pantry, and he checked it, and found it intact, and drank a little from it, steadying himself.

My own wedding-vow of servitude. His emergency ration.

* * *

Evening. The council dines with me — with the body of me — and I sit at the head of my own table watching my hands cut meat and my mouth deliver judgments on border levies, and Chancellor Maren leans close at the wine and murmurs, as she has learned to murmur over thirteen careful years:

"The riders returned from the western valleys, Majesty. Empty-handed. Captain Vane reports... complications."

*Complications.* Serah Vane. The one officer he trusts because she swears her oaths with perfect precision and perfect emptiness — I've seen the threads come off her and they're thin as spider-silk, barely a meal. She has found a way to kneel without giving. I would admire it enormously if I weren't fairly sure she'd run me through as readily as him, depending on the arithmetic.

"And the child?" my mouth asks, mild as milk.

"Not found. But found *near*, Majesty. Very near." Her eyes flick up, quick, checking my face for what, I wonder. For the man? She's clever enough to suspect there's a man. Clever officers always suspect it, and then decide they imagined it, because the alternative is unbearable.

The child. A child, near my city, speaking the old tongue, making bells ring wrong and crowns sweat. He was listening through me even now — I felt him lean forward inside my skull like a rider standing in stirrups — and his hunger had changed key. It was no longer the low grazing-hum of daily oaths. It was pointed. Eager. The hunger of a thing that has scented not food but *danger*, and intends to eat the danger before it eats him.

*Bring her to me,* he breathed through my throat, and Chancellor Maren nodded to the king's command, and somewhere in the west a captain I half-trust received orders through channels I don't control, to hunt a child I've never met, for reasons I am only beginning to guess.

Here is what I guessed, alone in the cellar, by the light of my one window, while the body slept and the thing on its head dreamed its slow golden dreams:

Someone had bound him once, with the old tongue, and the binding had held for thirteen hundred years — until someone, some order, some garden of speakers, weakened or died or scattered, and the binding rotted enough to slip. He got free. He found a soft-headed king. And now the last of whoever bound him — a speaker, a true one, maybe the last true one — has come walking into my city in small shoes, eight years old perhaps, carrying in her mouth the very words that were hammered into him along with the metal.

She is coming to kill him. I would stake my kingdom on it — what's left of it, the parts under the floorboards.

And no one will help her. No one even knows to. The courts are his body. The knights are his hands. The bells sing his lullaby, and the archbishop swears his oaths with a full heart, and the city loves its king, who is merciful, who halved the flood-tithe, who has such kind sad eyes.

Except.

Except the captain who kneels empty. Except the bells that cracked today, nine strokes of honest sound, as if the city itself had cleared its throat. Except the flickering man in the throne who screams silently at every oath and files away every secret and has spent thirteen years learning the one discipline a puppet can master: waiting.

He fears the child. He is moving everything he has against her — the riders, the gates, the tithe-rolls, the bells, soon perhaps the archbishop and his holy machinery. Everything he has.

Which means, for the first time in thirteen years, his attention is pointed outward. Away from me. Away from the cellar, away from the lit window, away from the man with the dust on his hands.

I lay in the dark of my own sleeping body and allowed myself, very quietly, to begin planning. Not escape — escape died years ago, along with my mother's face. Planning. The child will need things a child cannot get in this city: a door that opens, a guard who looks away, a moment of stillness in the right hall at the right hour.

I know this city's halls. I have cut meat in all of them. I have stood in the great coronation hall every year of my reign while he renewed himself on my oath, and I know — better than any living soul — the exact three heartbeats, each renewal, when the crown goes utterly still.

Three heartbeats. I have counted them thirteen times.

Whatever comes through my gates, whatever tongue she speaks, whatever desperate swordspeaker or traitor archivist or empty-kneed captain arrives with her — when the hour comes, there is a man inside this puppet who knows precisely where the strings attach.

And he is very, very tired of holding them taut.
