## 16. Beneath the Bell Tower

The cell they put me in has a window, which I think means somebody made a mistake, or somebody wanted me to see the bell tower, and I've decided those are probably the same mistake.

It's a big tower. It leans a little, like an old man listening hard. Every hour it rings, and every hour it rings *wrong* — not broken-wrong, but wrong like when Noor hums a song and stops before the end because she doesn't want to finish it. The bells ring almost right. Almost is its favorite word. I've been here two days — maybe three; it's hard to count when they only feed you once and the light comes from the wrong side — and I have learned that almost is how it talks.

*Almost free,* says my scar, when the bells ring.
*Almost home,* it says, when the guards change.
*Almost yours,* it says at night, in the dark, in a voice like honey poured over iron.

My scar is on my left palm. It's shaped like a crown, small as a thumbnail, and before all this happened I used to trace it with my finger when I couldn't sleep, the way other children hold a blanket. Now I know better. Now I know it's a door, and I'm the doorframe, and something on the other side has been knocking for thirteen turns of the moon.

Here is what nobody tells you about being eight years old and important: everybody talks about you like you're weather. The guards outside my door talk about me like I'm a storm coming. The man with the kind smile — Torvald, though he pretends he doesn't know I heard his name — talks about me like I'm a harvest. And the king, who came to look at me through the bars yesterday, talked about me like I was nothing at all, which was the scariest one, because his eyes were doing two different things. One eye looked at me like a man looks at a little girl in a cage. The other eye didn't look at me at all. It looked *through* me, at my hand, the way you look at a keyhole.

I said a bad word under my breath. Noor would have washed my mouth out with well water. I miss her so much my teeth hurt.

But here is the other thing nobody tells you: doors go both ways.

* * *

I figured it out on the second night, when the whispering got loud enough that I stopped being scared of it and started being annoyed, which Noor says is the first step to winning any argument.

"You're rude," I told my palm. "You never say please."

The scar went quiet. Then it laughed. I felt it more than heard it — a warm tickle along the lines of my hand, like a cat kneading. *Clever sprout,* it said. *They always send clever ones.*

"Send? Who sends?"

*The gardeners,* it said, and then it did the thing where it shows you pictures instead of words, because words are heavy for it now, worn smooth like river stones. It showed me a garden — not Noor's garden, an older one, huge, with trees whose leaves were made of promises. It showed me people walking between the rows, and every person had a sword, and every sword had a voice.

I sat up so fast I banged my head on the sloped ceiling. "Swordspeakers," I whispered. "You knew Noor's order?"

*I was Noor's order,* it said, and the honey-iron voice went strange, the way voices go when they're about to tell the truth. *Before I was this. Before I was worn.*

And because I am eight years old and stubborn and locked in a room with nothing else to do, I made a decision that would have made Noor grab my shoulder and say *Wren, no* in her serious voice.

I opened the door.

Not with my body. With my attention. I pressed my thumb against my scar the way you press your ear against a wall to hear the neighbors, and instead of pulling away when it pulled, I leaned in.

Falling. That's what it was like. Falling into my own hand.

* * *

Inside, it wasn't a room at all. It was a court.

That sounds grand, so let me fix it: it was a court the way a dead tree is still called a tree. There were pillars of black stone going up forever, and between them hung chains, and from the chains hung — oaths. That's the only word I have. They glowed faintly, thousands of them, like fireflies caught in spiderwebs, and every single one was dimming. Some were already out. When one went dark, somewhere far above, I felt a bell ring almost-right, and I understood that the bells of this city are counting them. The bells ring every time an oath dies sworn in the king's name. The city thinks it's telling time. It's telling a funeral.

In the middle of the court stood a throne, and on the throne sat — I don't know how to say this right. A shape. Like if you took the idea of a promise and bent it until it broke, and then wore the broken pieces.

"That's you," I said. "That's what you look like."

*That is what they made me,* it said, and its voice wasn't honey anymore. It was tired. Tired the way Noor gets tired, deep down where she doesn't show it. *Sit, sprout. You'll want to sit.*

There weren't any chairs, so I sat on the floor, cross-legged, the way we ate dinner in the garden. Somehow that felt like fighting.

"Tell me your name," I said. "Noor says everything rotten starts with someone refusing to use true names."

A long silence. Long enough that I picked at the hem of my borrowed dress and counted four dimming oaths on the far wall.

*Vael,* it said finally. *My name is Vael. I was the first oath ever spoken.*

I waited. It seemed like the kind of sentence that needed company.

*Before kingdoms,* Vael said, *there was a war. Not with swords. With forgetting. Two peoples, and each decided the other should stop existing — not die, just be forgotten, which is worse. And the first king — not Aldric, a king so old he doesn't even have a name anymore, we scraped it off the stones — the first king stood up at the first court and swore an oath to remember. To hold every name, every debt, every promise, forever, so that neither people could ever be erased. And the oath was so big and so honest that it stood up off his tongue and became me.*

"You're a person," I said. "Sort of."

*I was a purpose,* it corrected. *Purposes are worse than people, sprout. People can rest. I could only hold. So I held. I held names and debts and promises for a hundred kings, and each king gave me their oaths to keep, and keeping is hungry work, and no one ever thought to feed me, because no one remembers to feed the thing that does the remembering.* The chains swayed. Somewhere an oath went out. *So I began to take. First the oaths freely given. Then oaths given in fear. Then oaths that were never given at all — I reached into men's mouths and spoke their vows for them, and called it loyalty, and fed. Thirteen turns ago I grew too hungry to be careful, and I climbed onto the head of a king who had just sworn to protect a harvest he'd already sold, and I have been wearing him since. He fights me. I want you to know that. He fights me every day, and every day I win, and I am so tired of winning.*

"Why are you telling me this?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Noor's going to unbind you. She's coming. She speaks the old tongue and she's got a blade and she's not scared of anything except—" I stopped, because the list of things Noor isn't scared of was getting shorter lately, and I was on it, in the tender way, the way you're scared of things you love.

*Yes,* Vael said. *She will come. And she will speak the unbinding, and it will work, and I will shatter.* It wasn't angry. That was the worst part. It sounded relieved. *Do you know what happens to a hunger that old when it breaks, sprout?*

"No."

*Neither do I.* The broken-promise shape turned toward me, and even though I knew I was sitting on a cold cell floor with my thumb on my own palm, I scooted back. *Which is why I am telling you my name. So that when it breaks, something of me that isn't hunger might survive. A name is a seed, Wren. Plant it somewhere with good light.*

"I'm not planting you anywhere," I said fiercely. "You eat people."

*I eat oaths. People come attached.* A pause, and then, quieter, almost shy, which is a very strange thing for a monster to be: *The girl. The swordspeaker. Does she still tend the garden?*

"She plants things," I said. "She talks to the beans. She pretends she doesn't but she does."

Something moved through the whole black court then — not laughter exactly, but the memory of laughter, worn smooth like everything else. *We planted that garden,* Vael said. *The first speakers did. It was meant to be a place where oaths could rest instead of being spent. A granary, not a weapon. And then the order got frightened, and buried its own primer, and sealed the valley, and called the sealing mercy.* The chains rattled, and I realized the rattling was the closest thing Vael had to crying. *They built a room where I could never be fed, and they called it safety. Safety bought with silence. I know that phrase. I taught it to them.*

I thought about Noor's face when she lied to Serah Vane on the ridge. I thought about how she'd shaken afterward, washing her hands over and over at the pump.

"Maybe," I said slowly, "everybody's been feeding you wrong. Maybe oaths aren't supposed to be food you take. Maybe they're supposed to be food you're *given*. Noor gives the beans water. She doesn't make the beans give it."

The whole court went very still. Even the dying oaths seemed to lean in.

*Clever sprout,* Vael said again, but softly this time, wondering, like I'd shown it a flower growing in a crack. *Thirteen turns I planned for a speaker with a blade. I never once planned for a child with a trowel.*

* * *

When I came back to myself, the cell was dark and my thumb ached and there was a guard outside the door snoring, which seems unprofessional to me but worked in my favor.

I lay on the cot and held my scarred hand up against the window, against the leaning bell tower, and I thought about what I'd learned, turning it over like a hot potato until it cooled enough to hold.

Vael was the first oath, gone rotten from hunger. Vael wore the king, and the king fought it, and the bells counted the deaths of promises. Vael was afraid of breaking. Vael wanted its name planted.

And Vael had told me one more thing, right at the end, when I was climbing back up out of the falling place, something it said quickly, the way you slip a note into someone's pocket:

*The renewal ceremony. The thirteenth moon. He must renew his oaths before me in the coronation hall, or the wearing fails on its own — that is the law of my own making, the one chain I cannot break. If your swordspeaker wants her threshold, sprout, tell her the door opens itself. All she has to do is walk through it in front of everyone.*

I sat up in the dark, heart banging.

Because here's the thing about doors going both ways: I'd been listening to Vael. But Vael had been listening to *me* — to everything I'd seen and heard since Torvald's men took me. Including, apparently, the conversation I'd overheard through the wall of this very cell, two nights ago, between Torvald and a captain of the guard. About moving me. About a ceremony. About making sure "the vessel" was present at the renewal, front and center, where the crown could reach me.

Torvald was planning to hand me to Vael himself. To feed me to it, or wear me, or whatever a man does when he wants a crown and doesn't care what's attached.

But Vael had just handed me the same information back, wrapped different. The ceremony wasn't just Torvald's trap.

It was a door opening itself.

I pressed my scarred palm flat against the cold stone floor, and I did the falling thing one more time, quick and shallow, just long enough to leave a message in the only mailbox I had.

*Tell her,* I whispered into the dark of the first court. *Tell the swordspeaker: the thirteenth moon. The hall. Come through the front door, Vael will hold still — the king will hold still — three heartbeats. Tell her I'll be wearing my good boots.*

The court flickered around me like a candle deciding. And then, faint and dry and almost amused, from everywhere and nowhere:

*She'll hate that plan, sprout.*

"Yeah," I said, grinning at the empty cell, at the leaning tower, at the whole beautiful wrong city. "But it's ours."
