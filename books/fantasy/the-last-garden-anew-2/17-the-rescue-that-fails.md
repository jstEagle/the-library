## 17. The Rescue That Fails

Serah Vane had a way of saying *no* that sounded like a door closing, and she said it eleven times that afternoon in the cellar beneath the dyer's shop, while the tithe-broken villagers crowded around us smelling of wool and lye and stubbornness.

"Twelve," I counted, when she finished explaining — again — why walking into the palace through the bell tower gate was suicide. "You've said no twelve times. Thirteenth time's the thirteenth moon, Vane. Seems fitting you spend it on yes."

"I hate that you count."

"I hate that you're right about the walls but wrong about the plan. Those are different hatreds. They can coexist."

She glared at me across the barrel of pickling brine we were using as a war table. Around us, the villagers — Maret with her burned tithe-brand, old Hesk who'd carried his son's name on a scrap of paper for six years because the crown taxed even grief — waited the way people wait when they've already decided to follow someone off a cliff and are just polite enough to let the cliff be discussed first.

"The king's dream was real?" Serah asked me, quietly, for the dozenth time.

"He held still for three heartbeats in my sleep, Serah. He told me where to stand. He made a joke about boots." I shrugged. "Dead men don't make jokes about boots. Trapped ones do."

"And if it's the crown baiting us?"

"Then the crown has learned to joke, and honestly, we're doomed anyway, so we might as well die laughing."

Maret snorted. Hesk smiled for the first time in what I suspected was years. Even Serah's mouth twitched — that small, traitorous movement she probably hated more than anything else about me.

"Fine," she said. "Tomorrow night. The renewal rite. But we go in through the bell tower, not the front doors, because unlike the king I don't trust the crown to hold its breath out of courtesy." She looked around at the faces in the cellar. "And nobody dies stupidly. If it goes wrong, you run. All of you. Swear it."

They swore. I didn't, because Serah knew me too well to ask, and I knew her too well to pretend.

* * *

The City of Nine Gates at night is a liar. By day it's all pale stone and bells slightly out of tune, beauty laid over rot like icing over a bad tooth. At night the rot shows honest: lamplighters moving through streets that smell of tallow and fear, the palace rising on its hill with windows lit gold against the dark, every light a small announcement of how much the court could afford while the tithe-villages burned their furniture for warmth.

We went up through the tanners' quarter, twelve of us dressed in the grey of palace laundry-drudges, Wren bundled between me and Maret with her hood up and her scar-hand hidden in her sleeve. She hadn't spoken much since Torvald's betrayal — since the archivist's friendly face had turned out to be a mask over hunger — but that evening she'd pressed her scarred palm flat against mine and said, in the old tongue, the words Noor now knew meant *I am ready.*

Eight years old. Ready. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"The guards change at the third bell," Serah murmured at the foot of the bell tower stair. "Vael's renewal begins at midnight. We have from the third bell to midnight to get down, cross the undercroft, and reach the hall before the crown finishes putting itself back on him." She looked at each of us in turn, captain to soldiers, though half these soldiers had never held anything sharper than a pruning hook. "Noor takes Wren. I take point. Maret holds the stair. Everyone else — you know your work."

"And if something goes wrong?" Hesk asked.

"Then Noor improvises," Serah said, "and we all pray to whatever gods are left that her improvising is better than her planning."

"My planning got us here," I said.

"Yes. Here. In a cellar. About to break into a palace." She shouldered her sword. "Move."

* * *

The bell tower stair smelled of pigeon droppings and old rope. We descended it in single file by feel, the darkness total, Wren's small hand gripping mine so hard I'd have bruises shaped like her fingers for a week. I found I didn't mind.

At the bottom, the undercroft stretched ahead of us: vaulted stone, wine racks gone to dust, and torchlight at the far end marking the corridor to the coronation hall. Too much torchlight. Serah froze, hand up, and I counted what she counted — six guards where there should have been two.

"They know," she breathed. "Someone talked."

Nobody looked at anyone. Nobody had to. We were all thinking the same name, and the name tasted like copper and old friendship's corpse.

"Torvald sold us once," I whispered. "Maybe he sold us twice. Or maybe the crown simply felt its key walking." I squeezed Wren's hand. "Either way, the plan doesn't change. It just gets louder."

"That's not—"

"Serah." I turned to face her in the dark. "There is no quiet version of this anymore. There's only the version where we get to the hall before three heartbeats pass, or the version where Aldric wears that thing for another thirteen years and Wren spends them running. Choose."

For a long moment the only sound was pigeons shifting overhead and Wren breathing fast and small beside me.

"Forward," Serah said finally. "On my mark. And Noor — if it comes to choosing between the child and me, choose the child. That's an order."

"You're not my captain."

"No," she agreed. "But I'm right, and we both know it, and one of us should say it aloud while there's still time."

* * *

It went wrong, of course. Plans always do; the world is built of entropy and bad luck, and palaces doubly so.

The first guard went down to Serah's pommel before he finished turning. The second shouted. The shout became horns, horns became running feet, and suddenly the undercroft was full of noise and torchlight and the ugly arithmetic of twelve against forty.

"Maret!" Serah roared, and the stair behind us bloomed into chaos as the villagers did their work — not soldiers' work, but the desperate, improvised work of people who had nothing left to lose and had finally noticed it. Hesk swung a boat-hook. Maret threw lamp oil and a striker and set a wine rack burning so the smoke would blind the reinforcements. It was ugly and brilliant and bought us ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds to cross a palace.

We ran. Wren's hand in mine, her breath sobbing, my legs remembering things they had no business remembering after twenty years of garden rows. Serah fought backward ahead of us, a wall of steel and profanity, holding a corridor junction alone while we passed through it. I saw her take a cut along the arm and keep fighting. I saw her smile at the man in front of her, that cold captain's smile, and I saw the man step back from it.

"GO," she bellowed. "Front doors, sprout! Three heartbeats!"

We burst into the antechamber of the coronation hall and the sound hit us like weather — the choir, the drone of the old tongue, hundreds of voices chanting the renewal. Through the great doors' grilles I could see the hall blazing with candlelight, the court assembled, Aldric on the dais with the crown descending toward his head on its cushion of black silk, and Torvald standing beside the throne in archivist's grey, smiling like a man watching his harvest come in.

And behind us, footsteps. Many.

Wren pulled her hand free and stepped toward the doors. Her scarred palm came up, glowing faintly, and the old tongue poured out of her — words I knew now, words that made the doors' iron groan.

"Not yet," I said. "Wait for the crown. Wait for the three heartbeats. We speak together or not at all."

"But Serah—"

"Will kill me herself if you open those doors early." I knelt to look into her eyes. "Listen to me, little wren. What happens next happens fast, and it happens loud, and afterward nothing will be the same. Can you be brave for three heartbeats?"

She nodded. Eight years old, and brave for three heartbeats. Children carry what we cannot.

The doors behind us slammed open.

Serah came through them first — backward, bleeding from a dozen cuts, sword broken to half its length, and behind her a tide of palace guard. She took one look at me, at Wren, at the doors to the hall, and did the thing I will see every time I close my eyes for the rest of my life.

She turned around.

"HOLD THE DOOR, VAEL!" she screamed at the king inside the puppet, and planted herself in the gap with her half-sword and her whole ruined body, and held it.

I had time for one heartbeat of horror. Two. On three, Wren's palm blazed and the great doors answered her, iron shrieking, and we were through, into the blaze of the hall, the chanting stumbling to silence as two hundred courtiers turned to stare at a gardener and a child.

Torvald moved first. Of course he did. "Seize them—" 

Aldric rose from the throne.

I felt it happen — felt the moment the man inside seized the crown and *held*, three heartbeats of perfect stillness, the crown-thing's whisper cutting off mid-word like a throat gripped shut. His eyes found mine across the hall. Not the crown's eyes. His. Terrified and resolute and free for exactly as long as he could bear it.

"Now, sprout," he said, through teeth that weren't entirely his own. "Now, while it's blind."

Wren raised both hands. I put mine over hers, scar to scar, and spoke the unbinding through her voice — the old tongue pouring out of both of us, my swordspeech and her living speech braided into one word that had not been said since the first kingdoms:

*Unmake what was first. Return the oath to the air.*

The crown screamed.

Not metal — oath-magic, thirteen years of sworn fealty ripped loose at once, the hall's candles guttering blue-black, and in the middle of it Torvald lunged for the throne, hands outstretched for the shattering light, greedy to the last —

The raw magic took him like a river takes a leaf. One moment he was reaching; the next he was a silhouette of light folding inward, and then he wasn't anything at all except a scorch-mark on the flagstones shaped vaguely like regret.

The crown broke. I heard it break — a sound like the first kingdom ending, which is what it was — and Aldric collapsed, breathing, human, bald-headed and ordinary and weeping without shame.

Behind us, at the doors, the sound of steel stopped.

I turned. Serah was still standing. That was the first mercy. Then she folded, the way a banner folds when the pole is cut, and I was across the hall before I remembered deciding to move.

She was laughing when I reached her. Blood on her teeth, guard scattered around her dead and fled, and she was *laughing*.

"Told you," she wheezed. "Choose the child. You chose the child. Good soldier."

"Don't talk. Maret! HESK!"

"Listen." Her hand caught my wrist — captain's grip, final order incoming. "I serve the king. Not the crown. The crown's dead. So I'm serving... whoever carries the oath forward." Her eyes were going glassy and she forced them wide, furious at them, refusing. "That's you now. Swordspeaker. Heir-in-oath. Say you'll take it. Say it, or I'll haunt your garden forever, and I'm a *terrible* ghost, I'll move all your tools—"

"Serah—"

"*Say it.*"

The hall was silent around us. Two hundred courtiers. A freed king. A child with a cooling scar. And a dying woman ordering me to inherit everything she'd spent her life defending badly and believing in completely.

"I take it," I said. My voice cracked and I let it. "Heir-in-oath. Yours. Now stop giving orders and rest."

"Bad... soldier..." she whispered, almost smiling. "Never rest... on watch..."

And then the watch passed to me, whether I wanted it or not.

She died there in the doorway of the coronation hall, holding it, the way she'd held everything — too tightly, too long, and exactly when it mattered. Wren knelt beside her and closed her eyes with two small fingers, gently, the way you close a book you intend to read again someday.

I stayed kneeling a long time. Grief is a garden too; you don't rush it.

When I finally stood, the court was still staring, and King Aldric — free, diminished, wearing no crown for the first time in thirteen turns — was looking at me with the question every ruler asks and few mean.

"What now?" he said.

Outside, dawn was coming up over the City of Nine Gates, and somewhere in it, the tithe-broken villages were waking to the first morning in thirteen years without the crown's tax-collectors on their roads.

"Now," I said, wiping my face, sheathing my blade, and finding my voice steady in a way that surprised us both, "we talk about justice. And then — I'm going home. I have a garden to replant. And an oath to grow into."

Wren slipped her small scarred hand into mine.

The thirteenth moon was setting. Everything else was rising.
