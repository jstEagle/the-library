## 18. Threshold of the First Court

Torvald's notes smelled of dust and ambition.

I sat in Serah's borrowed rooms above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, three days after Wren had been stolen out from under our noses, and I read the archivist's hand until my eyes burned. He wrote a fine, careful script — the kind of hand that belongs to men who have spent their lives copying other people's truths and calling it work. Between the lines of his research on the unbinding rite, in a cramped cipher my mentor had taught me thirty years ago and Torvald had apparently learned well enough to betray us with, were the notes that mattered.

*The rite requires three things,* he had written. *A speaker. A key. A threshold.*

I had a speaker — me, or what was left of me once this was done. I had a key asleep in a gilded cage somewhere in the Nine Gates, dreaming in a dead tongue. What I did not have was a threshold, and it turned out that thresholds were not the sort of thing one found lying about.

*The unbinding must be spoken at a place where oath-magic pools,* the notes continued. *A site where vows have been made and remade so many times the ground itself remembers them. There are four such places in the Nine Gates: the Oathstone in the Founders' Court, the wedding-crypt beneath Saint Aldessa's, the old gallows square — and the coronation hall.*

I read that last line four times before it stopped reading like a list and started reading like a sentence of death.

*Of these, only the hall will serve for an unbinding of this magnitude. The crown-thing is rooted too deep, has fed too long, to be pried loose anywhere its strength is not matched by older and greater vows. The coronation floor is soaked through with nine hundred years of kings kneeling. It is the single deepest well of sworn words in the world. And it is guarded.*

*Guarded* was generous. The coronation hall stood inside the palace, inside the inner ring, inside every wall and gate and watch-post the crown commanded. You did not walk into the coronation hall. You were crowned into it, or you died trying.

And then there was the matter of timing, which Torvald had noted with what I can only describe as professional enthusiasm:

*The thirteenth full moon falls on the night of the renewal ceremony. The king will kneel before the assembled court and swear his oaths again, feeding the thing anew. On no other night will the crown be so strong — but neither will any other night do, for only while the renewal oaths are being spoken does the crown open itself fully to the floor below it. It drinks. In drinking, it is vulnerable. The window will be small. Three heartbeats, perhaps less, if the speaker can hold the key steady.*

Three heartbeats. I set the page down and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes and thought, with something close to hysteria, *I left a valley where nothing ever happened for this.*

* * *

Serah came back as the chandlers were lighting their evening candles. She moved like she always moved now — carefully, with her weight forward, favoring nothing, though I knew her ribs were still knitting from the rescue that failed. She carried bread, cheese, and the particular expression of a woman who had spent the day listening to people lie.

"Nothing," she said, setting the food down. "No whisper of where they're keeping her. The servants won't talk, the guards won't talk, and the tithe-folk we've recruited are watched too closely to get near the inner ring."

"They're keeping her close," I said. "They have to. The crown needs her near for the ceremony."

"You sound certain."

"I am." I pushed Torvald's pages across the table. "Read this. The part about the thirteenth moon."

She read standing up, the way soldiers read, ready to move. When she finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The renewal ceremony. Of course. He renews his oaths publicly every thirteen years — the whole court attends. It's the one night the hall is opened wide. Ambassadors, lords, the lot of them." Her jaw tightened. "It's also the most heavily guarded room in the world on that night."

"Yes."

"And you're thinking of walking in."

"I'm thinking of how much I hate plans that require miracles," I said. "But yes. Walking in."

Serah sat down across from me. In the candlelight I could see the gray coming into her hair more clearly than I liked; it hadn't been there when we'd met on my garden path, back when I still believed the boundary stones would keep the world out forever. She looked tired in the way that doesn't wash off.

"Noor." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile. "There is no safe plan. I want you to hear me say that plainly, because everything else I'm about to say will sound like there might be one. There isn't. If we go into that hall, some of us are not coming out. Possibly all of us. Possibly the girl least of all, if the crown decides she's more useful as a martyr than a vessel."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been gardening your way toward this for weeks now, and I've let you, because grief makes people patient and I needed you patient. But the ceremony is in six nights. Six. And we have no way in, no way to reach the girl, and an archivist's notes written by the man who sold her to us."

"Those same notes are the reason we know any of this," I said. "That's the ugly arithmetic of Torvald. Everything he gave us was true. He just gave it to the wrong side first."

Serah exhaled through her nose — almost a laugh, if laughter could be bitter enough to strip paint. "So. We need a miracle, a doorway, and three heartbeats."

"I've been working on the first two."

* * *

Here is what I had, gathered over three sleepless days, spread across the table between the bread crumbs and the candle wax:

First, the guest list. Serah had obtained it — bribed, borrowed, or charmed out of a herald who owed her from some old campaign I didn't ask about. Among the names invited to witness the renewal: the delegation from the Salt Marches, arriving late, expected to travel under escort of the crown's own road-wardens. Delegations got waved through gates that stopped everyone else. Delegations got seats in the hall.

"We can't impersonate the Salt Marches," Serah said flatly, when I floated it. "The real delegation will arrive. Half the court knows their ambassador by sight."

"The real delegation arrived early," Serah admitted, when I pointed out the flaw myself. "I checked. They crossed the border four days ago and they're being feted in the eastern districts. Which means the road-wardens expecting to meet them at the west gate on ceremony eve will be waiting for travelers who already came." She rubbed her eyes. "Gods. It might actually hold. For about an hour."

An hour. I wrote it down anyway, because an hour was more than nothing.

Second, the hall itself. Torvald's architectural notes, bless his treacherous thorough little heart, included the service ways — the passages beneath the stands where stewards moved wine and water during long ceremonies. Narrow. Unguarded during the rite itself, because no one attacks a coronation from the wine corridor. No one sane.

Third, and this was the piece I kept turning over like a stone with a warm underside: the crown would be drinking. For those few moments while Aldric knelt and swore, the thing on his head would be open, rooted into the floor, drawing up nine centuries of vows. Open, Torvald's notes said, like a mouth.

I knew something about mouths. I had spent my whole life learning where magic pooled and where it ran dry, because my order's entire art was speaking power into steel. Swordspeech worked by finding the oath in the metal — every blade remembered the vow of its forging — and giving that memory a voice. The crown was an oath gone rotten, yes. But underneath the rot, it was still an oath. And every oath, however deep its corruption, could be *unbound* by a true speaker, if the speaker could reach it in the moment of its opening.

Which meant I didn't need to fight my way to the dais. I needed only to speak loudly enough, clearly enough, at exactly the right heartbeat — and the hall itself, that great soaked sponge of sworn words, would carry my voice the way water carries blood.

"The acoustics," I said to Serah, on the third night. "Torvald's notes say the hall is built to carry a whisper from the door to the throne. Nine hundred years of architects making sure every lord hears every vow. If I stand anywhere in that room — *anywhere* — and speak the unbinding during the renewal, the room will say it with me."

"That's either brilliant or insane."

"In my experience those arrive together."

* * *

What I did not say to Serah, what I wrote instead in my own cipher in the margin of Torvald's page, was the cost. The rite required a speaker to pour herself into the unbinding — voice, will, and the gift itself. Not lent. *Spent.* When I spoke those words through whatever channel the child provided, I would not simply be saying goodbye to swordspeech. I would be emptying the vessel. Whatever part of me had been a speaker since the day I swore my first word of power would go into the unbinding and not come back.

My mentor had told me once, when I was young and arrogant and thought gifts were infinite: *The order does not spend its speakers lightly, Noor. A swordspeaker's words are not breath. They are years.* I had nodded the way the young nod, hearing without listening.

Now I sat in a chandler's rooms with candle smoke in my throat and understood that I had been saving up for this my whole life without knowing it. Every word I'd ever spoken into steel. Every oath. The garden I'd tended with a rusting blade hanging unused on my wall. All of it was coin, and the price of a king's freedom was the whole purse.

I found I minded less than I expected. That frightened me a little, how little I minded. But then, I had been the last of my order for thirty years, hoarding a language with no one left to speak it to. A language dies twice, my mentor said — once when its last speaker goes silent, and once when its last listener forgets. Better mine should die once, loudly, doing something that mattered.

* * *

On the fifth night, Serah brought news that changed the shape of everything, though I didn't yet know how much.

"Aldric's dream-walkers," she said. She dropped onto the bench across from me, armor-less for once, looking ten years older than the woman who'd once held a gate alone. "Rumors in the lower city. The king hasn't slept in a week. His physicians are baffled. And there are stories—" she hesitated, choosing words like a woman crossing ice, "—stories that he's been seen standing at windows at night, staring south. Toward the valley road."

Toward home. My spine went cold.

"Stories from servants," Serah said. "Servants talk, even palace servants. Especially palace servants. And there's one more thing. One of the tithe-villagers we recruited — Marta, the dyer's widow — swears she saw the king at the Founders' fountain three nights ago, alone, no guard. He spoke to her. He asked her—" Serah stopped.

"Asked her what?"

"Whether gardens could grow back after frost."

I sat very still. The candle flame leaned and straightened. Somewhere out in the dark city, a man wearing a monster was asking after gardens, staring south, not sleeping — and I remembered Torvald's notes again, the clinical line I'd skimmed past twice: *the crown opens itself fully during the renewal. It drinks. In drinking, it is vulnerable.*

Open. Like a door.

Like a mouth that could also, perhaps, be spoken *through*.

"He's fighting it," I said slowly. "He's been fighting it for thirteen years, and the renewal is when it feeds deepest, and he knows — somehow he knows we're coming, and he's not raising the walls. He's leaving windows open."

"That's a mad interpretation."

"All the best ones are." I pulled Torvald's pages back toward me and stared at the diagram of the hall, at the dais, at the thin ink lines that stood for walls and guards and nine hundred years of ceremony. Six nights had become one. One night, one hour, one corridor of wine-stewards and one delegation that didn't exist, one whisper that had to cross a throne room and land true.

There was no safe plan. There had never been a safe plan. There was only the plan, and the price, and the three heartbeats between them.

"One more thing," Serah said quietly. She slid a folded paper across the table. "Found pinned to our door tonight. No seal. No signature."

I unfolded it. Inside, in a fine careful hand I now hated and needed in equal measure, was a single line of Torvald's cipher:

*The key sings in her sleep. The crown hears it. Come at the drinking, not before — he cannot hold long, but he will hold.*

I looked at that sentence for a long time. *Come at the drinking.* Either Torvald was playing some deeper game than betrayal — or somewhere behind those traitor's eyes, the debt to my mentor had never entirely been paid off, and a part of him wanted, desperately, to see the crown fail.

Or it was a trap. With Torvald it was always possibly a trap.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we become the Salt Marches."

Serah nodded once, soldier-simple. Outside, the bells of the Nine Gates rang the hour — and for the first time since we'd entered the city, they rang almost, but not quite, in time.

One night remained. Somewhere above the city, behind walls I intended to violate, a king was holding a door open with the last of himself, and a child was singing in her sleep, and the moon was rising fat and full and final over the roofs of the Nine Gates.

I banked the candles, laid my rusting oath-blade across my knees, and began, in a whisper the room carried perfectly, to practice.
