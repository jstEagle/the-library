## 19. The King's Last Request

I dreamed of my father's kitchen again.

That was how I knew it was a true dream, if such a thing can be said to exist — and I have had thirteen years to form opinions on the matter, none of them flattering to the dreamer or the dreamed-of. The crown-thing does not let me keep memories it considers frivolous. It keeps ledgers instead: tithe rolls, troop counts, the precise weight of every oath sworn in my name, each one a small silver thread it winds into itself like a magpie hoarding for a nest I will never be allowed to see finished.

But kitchens. Kitchens it cannot be bothered with. A king who remembers the smell of his mother's bread is, apparently, not a threat. So every few months — when the thing on my skull grows drowsy after one of its great feedings, when the renewal rites leave it fat and slow as a fed tick — I get an hour or two of my father's kitchen, with its crooked chimney and its table scarred by forty years of knives that were never meant for anything grander than onions.

I stood at that table now, and someone else was standing in it with me.

She was older than I expected, which is a foolish thing to think about a stranger in your own dream, but there it is. Grey coming in at her temples like frost finding the edges of a window. Sword-calluses on a hand that had recently been holding something else — a trowel, maybe, or a seed packet. She looked around my mother's kitchen the way people look around churches they don't belong to: politely, and waiting for someone to tell them where to stand.

"You're the gardener," I said. "The swordspeaker. Noor Iversen."

Her hand moved toward a sword that wasn't there, which told me she'd been expecting this meeting even less than I had, and had prepared for it anyway. I liked her immediately. It has been a long time since I liked anyone. The crown discourages it.

"This is a dream," she said. Not a question. She said it the way you'd say *the floor is wet* — assessing the hazard, deciding whether to stand in it.

"It's my dream," I corrected. "Or what's left of it. You're a guest, which makes you the first guest I've had in eleven years who wasn't trying to take something out of me." I gestured at the bench across the table. "Sit? I should warn you the bread isn't real. Nothing here is real except the conversation, and I've been assured by better theologians than me that conversations are the realest things there are."

She didn't sit. Fair enough. I hadn't sat either; I'm not sure this body of memory remembers how.

"You know who I am," she said.

"I know what you are, which is different, and I know both because the thing wearing my head knows them too, and it leaks." I tapped my temple, where beneath the skin and bone and thirteen years of occupation there lived a scar shaped like nothing at all — the crown-thing doesn't mark its host; why would it mark what it intends to wear smooth? "It felt the child cross the ninth gate tonight. It has been purring ever since. Do you know what it sounds like, inside, when it purrs?"

"No."

"Like a millstone turning over wheat. Except the wheat is me." I smiled, because it was either that or say something true and ugly, and I'd decided before she arrived that I would spend my truth carefully, like a man spending his last coin. "It doesn't know you're here. This hour belongs to me. That is the first thing you should understand, and I'll forgive you for not believing it, because I wouldn't believe it either."

* * *

Let me tell you how it happened, since she asked, and since asking is more courtesy than anyone has shown the man under the crown in years. She asked it plainly — *what are you, exactly, you and it* — and I found I wanted to answer plainly, which surprised me. I had rehearsed speeches. Thirteen years is a long time to rehearse.

"The first oath," I said. "That's what Torvald's books call it, when they call it anything at all. The first promise anyone ever made with power behind the words. And every oath since has been a small tribute paid down the line to the original. Kings swear fealty. Knights swear service. Farmers swear their harvests to their lords, and their lords to the crown, and the crown—" I spread my hands. "You see the shape of it. A river of promises, all flowing downhill, all ending in one place. And in that place, after enough centuries, something woke up. Or coalesced. Or simply *noticed*, the way a heap of grain eventually notices it's being eaten."

"And it chose you."

"It chose whoever wore the crown. I was simply the one wearing it when it finished waking up." I heard my own voice go flat and careful and hated it. "My coronation. I was twenty-two. My father had been dead eight months and I remember thinking, as the crown came down, that it was heavier than it looked in the portraits. Everyone laughed afterward when I swayed. They thought it was grief. It was, partly. The other part was something ancient wrapping itself around my thoughts like a hand closing over a candle flame — gently, at first. It doesn't want you dead. Dead kings are useless to it. It wants you *smoothed*. It wants the parts of you that object filed off, year over year, until what remains is a shape it can move."

Noor had gone very still, the way people do around wounds they can't help. "And the oaths it feeds on—"

"Are mine to give. That's the joke of it, if you enjoy jokes. Every knight who kneels to the crown, every magistrate, every tithe-collector — they're swearing to *me*, in law and in magic both, and the law and the magic both route their words through my mouth to the thing above it. I am the funnel. I am the—" I stopped. Started again. "There was a woman in the western provinces, three years ago. Refused the harvest-tithe. They brought her to the capital for judgment, and the court expected me to pronounce some terrible sentence, and for about four seconds I was able to hold my own tongue still. Four seconds. I said: *the crown will decide.* Not I. The crown. And everyone heard mercy in it, or wisdom, or mystery, whatever they needed to hear, and the woman went home with her harvest, because the crown-thing had just fed enormously on the renewal rites and couldn't be bothered with one farmer. They still sing about it, I'm told. The Merciful Judgment of Aldric." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does in dreams. "I have become a legend of compassion through the mechanism of being briefly unavailable."

She almost smiled. I counted it as a victory. In my experience you count victories however small you must.

* * *

"Why am I here?" she asked, eventually, when the kitchen had gone quiet around us. Outside its little window there was no landscape at all, only a soft grey nothing, like fog pressed against glass. "Why now? If it doesn't know — why would you risk it knowing?"

Because I have perhaps two hundred days left, I did not say. Because the smoothing is nearly done. Because last month I caught myself signing an order for the tithe-garrisons to be doubled and felt *nothing*, and I understood that the nothing was the point, that the nothing was the destination, and that whatever part of me could still feel horror at that had best act while it remained a part rather than a remainder.

What I said was: "Because you're going to try to save me. And I need you to stop."

That landed. I watched it land — watched her shoulders come forward slightly, the gardener's posture, someone bracing against weather.

"The unbinding rite," I said. "Yes, I know about it. The thing knows about it — it's the first oath, Noor, it *remembers being spoken*, and everything that might unmake it lives in its memory somewhere, filed away, guarded. When you attempt it, it will know. It will pour everything it has into holding itself whole, and it will use me to do it. My hands. My voice. My knights' oaths, called in all at once like debts at a funeral. You'll be fighting not a crown but a kingdom, and the child will be standing in the middle of it with a door open in her chest."

"She can survive it," Noor said, and there was iron under the words, the kind of iron that comes from having already decided. "Torvald's notes—"

"Torvald." I let the name sit between us for a moment. I watched her face do something complicated. Ah, I thought. She suspects. Good. Suspicion is a tool the crown-thing cannot confiscate, because it never occurred to it that anyone would distrust an archivist. "Whatever Torvald told you, weigh it twice. He has spent twenty years close to the crown, and proximity to the crown changes people. It offers things. Quietly. Through dreams, sometimes, though it prefers ledgers." I paused. "It offered me things once too, at the start. It said that if I stopped struggling, it would let my mother live out her years in peace. She was dying anyway. It knew that. It let me make the bargain so that I would carry the guilt of having made it. That is the shape of the thing you're fighting. Understand it, and you'll stop underestimating it."

"So what do you propose?" she said. "That we walk away?"

"No." And here it was, then — the speech I had rehearsed through a hundred sleepless nights, the one I'd imagined delivering from a throne with dignity and from a dungeon without it. In my father's kitchen, with unreal bread going stale on the table, it came out smaller than I'd planned. Smaller and truer. "I propose you let me help. Not save. Help. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I have thought about little else for eleven years."

"How?"

"Three heartbeats." I held up three fingers, and the gesture felt absurd and holy at once. "When the moment comes — when Wren opens herself and you speak through her — the crown-thing will seize me to fight you with. It always uses the nearest instrument, and I am the nearest instrument there is. But I have been practicing, Noor. Thirteen years of practicing. It thinks the smoothing is nearly complete, and it's right, mostly, but it has made one error. It believes there is nothing left in me worth holding still for." I looked down at my hands, which were my father's hands in this dream, flour in the creases. "There is. There's a kitchen in me it never bothered to burn. And on the night of the renewal rite, when it reaches for me — I will hold. Three heartbeats. Not a muscle. Not a syllable. While it tears at me for control of my own mouth, I will stand inside myself like a door braced shut, and for those three heartbeats, its greatest weapon will be a wall instead of a blade. Three heartbeats is nothing. Three heartbeats is everything. Ask any swordspeaker."

She was staring at me now, and I recognized the look — I'd seen it on generals and priests and once, memorably, on a horse. The look of someone recalibrating what a person is capable of.

"It will cost you," she said quietly.

"It will cost me what's left to cost." I said it lightly, and was grateful the dream kept my voice steady. "Noor, listen to me. I am not asking you to save me. Do you understand? I am past saving — not in the dramatic sense, in the arithmetic sense. Whatever is left of Aldric after three heartbeats of that fight will be very small, and very tired, and possibly not fit to rule a kitchen garden, let alone nine provinces. I am not asking to survive this. I am asking to *matter* in it. For four seconds three years ago, I saved a woman by being absent. Give me three heartbeats to save everything by being present, and whatever happens to me after—" I shrugged, and found I was smiling, and found the smile was real, which in this place was worth more than the crown had ever been. "I have been a puppet so long that the idea of pulling one string of my own, at the end, against the thing holding all the others — that isn't sacrifice. That's a feast."

* * *

She argued with me, of course. I had hoped she would; a person who doesn't argue in dreams is a person who has already decided, and decisions made alone in other people's heads go bad quickly.

She argued that the plan was thin — fair. That three heartbeats was a guess — also fair, though I have been timing them against the palace bells for a decade, counting, always counting, and I am very good at counting now; it's one of the few skills the crown approves of. She argued that Wren was a child and I was asking to put a battle through her — and there I stopped her, and told her what I had felt through the scar when the girl crossed the ninth gate: not fear. Curiosity. The crown-thing had purred, yes, hungry as ever — but underneath its hunger, for the first time in thirteen years, I had felt something in myself rise up to meet it, and I had known it was answering her. Some old part of the first oath recognizing a speaker of the first tongue. The thing wears me, but I am still stitched into it at the root, and through that stitch, I could hear the child singing to herself in a language dead before my grandfather's grandfather, and it sounded like someone opening a window in a sealed room.

"She isn't afraid of it," I told Noor. "She talks to it. Did you know that? Through the scar. I've felt the conversations like weather on the far side of a wall. Whatever you've taught her to fear, unteach half of it — she understands that thing better than any of us, because she's the only one talking *to* it instead of about it."

That silenced her longer than anything else I'd said. Gardeners, I suspect, do not enjoy being told the child they've been protecting has been conducting diplomacy behind their backs.

At last she said: "If I agree — if we do this — how do I know the offer is real? How do I know this isn't the crown using you, using this kitchen, to lure us into the open on the night it's strongest?"

It was the right question. It deserved better than reassurance, and reassurance was all I had, so I gave her the next-best thing, which was the truth arranged honestly.

"You don't," I said. "You can't. Trust is a seed you plant without proof of the harvest — I believe that's in your order's primer, actually, though it's about gardens there. Here's what I can give you instead. If this is a trap, then the trap costs you everything, and I will have been the face of it, and I am content to be hated forever on those terms. But if it's not a trap — if it's a dying man's last honest offer — then consider what refusing it costs. You'll attempt the unbinding with the full weight of the crown-thing behind the king's eyes, fighting you with every thread of every oath in nine provinces. Or you attempt it with those threads slack for three heartbeats, because the man holding them chose to let go. I know which odds I'd plant in."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside the window, the grey nothing pressed closer, and I felt — distantly, like thunder heard through stone — the crown-thing beginning to stir in the world above, the dream-hour draining away the way it always did, sand through a cracked hourglass.

"One condition," Noor said finally. "When it's done — if it's done — if there's anything left of you — you abdicate. You don't rebuild. You don't reform. You put the broken pieces of the crown in a museum and you find out who Aldric is when nobody's wearing him."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time in longer than I could calculate, which in my position was probably a very long time indeed.

"Gardener," I said, extending my flour-dusted hand across the table of my childhood, "you have yourself a deal."

Our hands met. Her grip was strong and earthy and entirely unimpressed by crowns, and it was the finest thing I had touched in thirteen years.

The kitchen began to dissolve around us — the walls going soft, the grey pressing in, my father's table fading like breath off glass. As it went, Noor's figure thinned too, and I heard her voice come from everywhere and nowhere, already halfway back to the waking world:

"The renewal rite. The thirteenth moon. Be ready."

"Count on me," I said. "Counting is what I'm best at."

And then I woke — rose, rather, from the throne-dais where I slept sitting up these days, because lying flat gives the crown too easy an angle — and above me the thing stirred and stretched through my skull, and murmured, in the voice it borrowed from my own throat:

*Nine days, little king.*

*Nine days,* I agreed, inside the silence it thought was empty.

*I have counted.*
