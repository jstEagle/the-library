## 20. Thirteen Heartbeats

There is a particular quality to the light in a hall built for coronations. It comes down through windows tall enough to make you feel like an insect that wandered indoors by mistake, and it gilds everything equally — the throne, the banners, the guards, and the two intruders crouched behind a pillar of imported marble pretending to be pilgrims.

Wren's hand was small and cold in mine. Through her palm I could feel her heartbeat, quick as a bird's, and beneath it something else — a slower pulse, heavy and wrong, like a second heart buried under the first.

*Thirteen turns,* I thought. *Tonight is the thirteenth.*

Below us, the Hall of Nine Gates had been dressed for renewal. Priests in white and gold arranged themselves along the dais steps. Courtiers filled the tiers, glittering and murmuring, and at the center of it all stood King Aldric — tall, straight-backed, moving with that terrible smoothness I'd seen from across the city square. A puppet doesn't jerk. That's what people get wrong about puppets. The good ones move like water, and you only notice the strings when something goes against them.

Tonight, something was going to go very much against them.

"Speaker." Wren tugged my sleeve. She'd taken to calling me that since the bell tower — not Noor, not Freya-of-the-garden, but *speaker*, as if the word were a title she was handing me whether I wanted it or not. "The scar is singing."

"I know." I could hear it too, now that we were this close. A thin whine at the edge of hearing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. The crown-thing knew its key had entered the room. Somewhere under all that gold and ceremony, it was paying attention.

Serah Vane materialized out of the shadow of the next pillar, dressed in the livery she'd put back on three days ago like a woman putting on a corpse's coat. Her face was still bruised from the rescue that failed. Her left arm hung in a sling she refused to acknowledge.

"Torvald's guards are doubled on the west stair," she murmured. "He knows you'll come tonight. He wants you to come tonight. He's positioned himself to catch whatever falls when the crown breaks."

"He thinks he can catch it," I said.

"You think he can't?"

I thought about Torvald's hands, ink-stained and clever, and his voice going soft whenever he spoke of power — the way other men speak of lovers they wronged and still want. "I think a man who spends thirty years filing other people's oaths doesn't understand what one weighs. Let him find out."

Below, a herald's voice rang out, flat and ceremonial: *"On this, the thirteenth renewal of the reign of Aldric, First of His Name, let the crown be brought forth."*

The doors at the far end opened. Two priests carried the crown on a cushion of black velvet, walking slowly, and even from the gallery I could see the courtiers lean toward it — the whole room tilting, subtly, like grass toward sun. The crown itself was smaller than I'd imagined. Old gold, thin as wire in places, worked into shapes that might have been leaves or might have been teeth. It did not gleam. Light went into it and didn't come back out.

And it was whispering. Even here, even through stone and distance, I could feel it brushing against the edges of my mind like a cat rubbing against a leg — proprietary, patient. *Mine,* it seemed to say to the whole room. *All of it mine.*

"Noor." Wren's voice had gone strange. Older. When I looked down, her eyes had gone strange too — the brown shot through with threads of silver, like frost creeping across a window. "It sees me."

"Hold on to me," I said. "Whatever it says, hold on to me."

"It says I'm empty." Her small hand tightened on mine hard enough to hurt. "It says it made a space inside me thirteen turns ago and it's been waiting for me to grow into it."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the hall's high drafty air. Thirteen turns. Wren was eight years old. Do the arithmetic. Whatever had marked her palm, whatever had set her walking toward a valley where magic couldn't follow, had done it before she could tie her own boots.

"Then it waited for nothing," I said, and took out my blade.

Not the rusted oath-blade — that had stayed behind in the garden soil, where it belonged. This was Serah's spare, a plain soldier's sword with no history and no opinions. But when my hand closed around the grip, the old words rose in my throat anyway, unbidden, the way a prayer rises in a burning house.

*"Stand aside."*

I didn't say it loudly. I didn't need to. Swordspeech isn't volume; it's authority compressed into sound until sound can't hold it anymore. The word went through the hall like a stone through a pond, and every guard on the floor below turned — not toward me, but *aside*, their bodies obeying before their minds caught up. Three heartbeats of confusion. That was all we would get. That was all we needed.

We came down the gallery stairs fast, Wren's hand in mine, Serah at our backs with her sword drawn left-handed and her face set in the expression I'd come to think of as her true uniform. The courtiers saw us and began, in the manner of courtiers everywhere, to scream in a way that obstructed nothing.

"On the dais!" Serah shouted. "Noor — the king —"

Aldric had turned. And for the first time, up close, I saw him clearly: a man in his fifties who should have looked older, handsome the way a portrait is handsome, except his eyes. His eyes were fighting. I don't know how else to describe it. Something behind them was straining against something else, the way a horse strains against a bit, and sweat stood on his forehead though the hall was cold.

He looked at Wren. And his mouth — the crown's mouth, the puppet's mouth — curled into something hungry.

But his eyes said: *Now. Please. Now.*

"Torvald!" someone screamed from the tier above. "Torvald, they're here —"

Of course he was there. He'd arranged himself by the throne with six of his personal guard, and he wasn't looking at me or Wren or even the king. He was looking at the crown on its black cushion, being borne up the dais steps toward Aldric's head, and his expression was the expression of a man watching his inheritance arrive at last.

"Kill the woman," he said mildly, as if ordering wine. "Take the child alive. Gently. She's worth more than the kingdom."

His guards came at us across the marble, and there was no more time for anything but the work.

I have spent my life learning that violence is a language, and swordspeech is its grammar. My body remembered what my oath had forbidden: the pivot, the flat of the blade against a temple, the word *"Down"* that dropped two men to their knees mid-stride, the word *"Yield"* that emptied a third man's arms of his weapon and his will in the same breath. Serah fought beside me without speech at all, just steel and fury and fifteen years of knowing exactly where a body is weakest. We were two women carving a corridor through the coronation of a king, and somewhere in it Wren let go of my hand.

She walked forward alone.

The crowd parted for her — not because anyone commanded it, but because the scar on her palm had begun to glow, a thin seam of light like a crack in the world, and no one, not even armed men, wants to stand between a child and a crack in the world.

"Wren!" I lunged after her. A guard caught my arm; I broke his grip with the pommel and nearly lost her in the press of bodies. "Wren, wait — wait for me —"

But she had stopped at the foot of the dais, small and straight-backed in her borrowed pilgrim's cloak, looking up at the king. And Aldric — the crown was almost on him now, the priests lifting it, the whispering rising to a shriek only I and she could hear —

Aldric held still.

That was the bargain. That was the dream he'd sent me, the request he'd made with the last of himself: *not save me — let me help.* Three heartbeats. He would fight the thing wearing him for three heartbeats, hold his own body motionless while the crown settled, give us our opening. Three heartbeats bought with whatever was left of a man who had been erasing himself, drop by drop, for thirteen years.

The crown touched his hair.

And the king of the nine-gated city became a statue of himself.

One heartbeat.

Everything in the hall seemed to inhale. The whispering stopped — cut off, shocked, like a singer losing her breath. Aldric stood rigid, the crown blazing now with a light that cast no shadows, and his face was a battlefield frozen at its worst moment.

Two heartbeats.

Wren raised her scarred palm, and the old tongue came out of her — the tongue before kingdoms, the tongue my order had died rather than surrender, syllables like river-stones worn smooth by ten thousand years. I didn't speak it. I had never been permitted to learn it whole. But I didn't need to know it, because I wasn't the speaker tonight.

She was. And I was the voice she'd chosen.

Three heartbeats.

"*Now,*" I breathed, and stepped up beside her, and put my mouth close to her ear, and spoke the unbinding.

I don't remember choosing the words. They came up through me like water through a well — the swordspeech that was mine, poured into the old tongue that was hers, my authority married to her key. I felt it leave my chest like something tearing loose, roots and all. I felt Wren's scar open its light wide. I felt the crown-thing understand, in the last instant, exactly what was happening to it, and scream — a sound with no sound in it, a scream made of every oath it had ever swallowed, every vow sworn in Aldric's name twisted into its food and its chains.

The unbinding has no translation. But if it had one, I think it would be this: *You were spoken once. Be unspoken now.*

The crown shattered.

Not like glass. Like a promise breaking — a sound that was also a meaning, gold flying apart into light and dust and thirteen years of stolen will coming home to roost. The shockwave took the hall off its feet. Banners fell. Windows burst inward, nine gates' worth of beautiful wrong glass raining down around us, and through the middle of it all King Aldric crumpled like a man whose strings had finally, mercifully, been cut.

And Torvald ran toward the shards.

I saw it clearly, the way you see the important things clearly in disasters. He shoved past his own fallen guards, past screaming priests, arms outstretched, eyes fixed on the largest piece of the crown as it spun glowing through the air — reaching for the power he'd sold a child to obtain, certain, even now, that it could be held.

"Torvald!" I shouted. "*Don't—*"

Raw oath-magic does not want a holder. It wants a keeper of vows, and it found in Torvald a man full of thirty years of small betrayals, each one a door. The shard touched his fingers and the light went *into* him, and for one moment he looked astonished — genuinely astonished, a scholar surprised by his own research — and then every promise he had ever broken called in its debt at once.

What was left when the light finished with him wasn't nothing. That was the worst part. It was a shape in an archivist's robe, kneeling on the dais steps, holding its empty hands up as if it could still read something written there.

Then the bells began.

All over the city, the nine towers' bells — which had rung slightly out of time for thirteen years — swung wildly, hammering, ringing true for the first time in a generation, and the sound came through the broken windows like a tide. In the tiers, courtiers knelt without deciding to. Guards laid down weapons they no longer remembered why they'd held. And at the center of it, on the steps of the dais, Wren swayed, and I caught her, and her eyes were brown again, only brown, an eight-year-old's eyes, exhausted and frightened and unbearably ordinary.

"Did I do it right?" she whispered.

"You did it perfectly," I said, and discovered I was crying, which was inconvenient, because I had always assumed the end of a war would be more dignified than this.

"Noor." Serah limped to us, gray-faced, sword-point down, alive — alive, after the bell tower, after everything, and I decided I would never take that for granted again. Behind her, King Aldric stirred on the marble, groaning, a man waking from the longest nightmare ever recorded. "The king —"

"Let him wake slowly," I said. "He's earned it."

I looked down at my sword-hand then, because some part of me already knew, the way you know a tooth is gone before your tongue confirms it. I flexed my fingers. I reached for the words — *stand, yield, down* — the vocabulary that had lived in my throat since I was younger than Wren, the gift I'd sworn away and spent anyway.

Nothing came. Just air. Just a woman breathing in a ruined hall, holding a child, surrounded by bells.

Ordinary sound. Ordinary hands.

Wren felt me go still and looked up, sharp as any archivist. "Noor? What's wrong?"

"Nothing," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I laughed, because grief and relief sound identical if you're tired enough. "Nothing's wrong. Everything's —"

The king opened his eyes.

They were his own. That was the first thing — the eyes that had been fighting for thirteen years were simply, quietly, his, and they found Wren first, then me, and Aldric of the Nine Gates tried to rise, failed, and managed, in a voice rusty as my old oath-blade:

"The gardener," he said. "The valley. You came."

"We came," I said.

He looked at the golden dust still settling over everything — over the throne, the banners, the kneeling court, the shape that had been Torvald — and something moved through his face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to name. Not joy. Not grief. The look of a man stepping out of prison into daylight and finding the world both exactly as he left it and entirely new.

"Then," King Aldric said, and his voice broke, and he did not trouble to mend it, "we have a great deal to talk about. All of us. Starting with what I owe —"

He stopped. His gaze had gone past my shoulder, to the great doors of the hall, standing open to the night and the ringing bells.

Standing in them, dusty from the road, travel-worn, impossible — a figure I knew better than I knew my own reflection, carrying a staff topped with a boundary stone's twin.

My mentor. Dead thirteen years. Smiling at me like I'd passed an examination I hadn't known I was sitting.

"Well," said Serah faintly, beside me. "That's new."
