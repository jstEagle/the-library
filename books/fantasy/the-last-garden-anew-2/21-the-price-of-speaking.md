## 21. The Price of Speaking

My mentor stood in the doorway of the coronation hall, thirteen years dead, and the first thing I felt — before wonder, before grief, before any of the feelings I would spend the rest of my life sorting through like seeds from chaff — was irritation.

"You're supposed to be buried," I said. "Under an ash tree. I planted it myself."

"Maren," she said warmly, using the name she'd called me when I was nine and had just put a hoe through my own foot. "You always did plant things in the wrong places."

She crossed the hall the way she used to cross the garden at dusk, unhurried, her staff clicking against stone that still glittered with the dust of a shattered crown. Wren was asleep against Serah's shoulder, worn down to the last thread of herself, her scarred palm curled shut like a fist around nothing. Aldric sat on the steps below his empty throne — he refused to sit on it, and I understood why — looking between us with the expression of a man trying to read a letter written in a language he'd been forbidden to learn.

"Explanations," I said. "In order. Starting with how you sealed a valley so well you fooled me for thirteen years, and ending with why you're here now and not then, when I could have used—"

"When you *did* use," she corrected gently. "Everything I gave you. You just didn't know what half of it was." She stopped in front of me and looked me over the way she looked at plants that had survived a hard winter — cataloguing damage, estimating what would come back. "The oath held. That was the point. The valley wasn't a prison for magic, Maren. It was a vault. And you were the lock's keeper without knowing it. As long as a sworn speaker lived inside the boundary and kept faith with the silence, no crown-born thing could reach past those stones to find what slept beneath the garden."

"What sleeps beneath the garden?"

"Ah." Her smile thinned. "That conversation is for spring. When your hands are in the dirt where they belong."

"I hate when you do that."

"I know." She touched my cheek, once, and her hand was warm and real and smelled of loam and old paper, and I let her, because some debts are older than death. "Now. Before anything else. Speak something for me."

"Speak something?"

"Swordspeech. Anything. A binding. A blessing on the door. Humor me, child."

I almost laughed. Almost said *the crown is ash, the work is done, what need have we of blades*. Instead — because she was my mentor and because thirteen years of obedience dies harder than kings fall — I raised my hand toward the great doors, gathered the words the way I'd gathered them ten thousand times, the way I'd gathered them not three hours ago when I spoke the unbinding through a sleeping child's mouth—

And found nothing.

No. Not nothing. That was the horror of it, the thing I didn't understand until I opened my mouth: the words were all still there. Every syllable of the first tongue, every phrase of binding and loosing, arranged in my mind as neat as a seed drawer. I reached for them the way I'd always reached.

What came out was sound. Ordinary sound. Air shaped by a tongue into the shapes of words, and no more. The doors did not stir. The air did not tighten. Somewhere behind my ribs, where power had always lived like a second heartbeat, there was only quiet — the plain, human quiet everyone else had carried their whole lives without complaining about it.

"Oh," I said. Just that. In just my voice.

"The unbinding needed a price," my mentor said softly. "It always does. A crown fed on oaths for thirteen turns; to break it, an oath of equal weight had to be spent entire. Your swordspeech was the oldest oath you carried. It went into the breaking. All of it."

"All of it," I repeated. My voice sounded strange to me. Thin. Mine, but only mine.

"You knew," she said. "Don't tell me you didn't. Noor Iversen does not walk into a coronation hall carrying a living key and a dying king without reading the fine print she wrote herself. You knew, and you went anyway, and you'd go again. So don't stand there looking robbed. Stand there looking paid."

I stood there looking robbed. Because knowing a thing in the moment you choose it is one country, and living in the country after is another one entirely, and nobody — not mentors, not kings, not children who bloom flowers out of season — can draw you a map of the second place until you've walked it.

The first tongue sat in my head like tools in a dead man's shed. I could recite every word of it. It would never again do anything but sound like words.

* * *

They gave us rooms in the palace, because apparently regicide-adjacent heroines get quarters with actual mattresses, and I lay awake in mine listening to bells that finally rang true and mourning a gift I had chosen to spend.

Here is what nobody tells you about grief for a thing you gave up on purpose: it doesn't respect the bargain. You made the trade with clear eyes; you knew the cost; you signed. And then at two in the morning you wake anyway with your hands open and empty, reaching for a weight that isn't coming back, and you lie in the dark being furious at yourself for regretting a choice that saved a king and unmade a monster and set a valley free.

I tried to pray in the old tongue. The words came out sounding like a woman talking to her ceiling.

I got up instead and went to the window, and found I was not the only sleepless soul in the city. Down in the courtyards, torches moved. Word travels fast in a capital when the king collapses mid-ceremony and rises changed, when guards come out of the renewal rite white-faced and silent, when the tithe-wagons that left the villages heavy return light. Serah Vane had wasted no time. By dawn, I'd learn, she'd already drafted three edicts and threatened two ministers, and by the end of the week the first tithe-ledgers would be burning in the palace yard while the villages they'd bled watched from behind barricades built of their own confiscated fences.

But that night it was only torches, and bells ringing true, and me at a window with ordinary hands.

Wren found me there near morning. She padded in barefoot the way she'd padded into my garden on the first frost, and climbed onto the window seat beside me without asking, the way she'd done everything since, and leaned her head against my arm.

"It's gone, isn't it," she said. Not a question. Children always know; it's adults who need things explained.

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

I considered lying. Decided she'd earned better. "It hurts like a missing tooth. Not all the time. But my tongue keeps going to the gap."

She nodded, solemn, eight years old and carrying more understanding than most archivists twice her age. Then she took my hand — the right one, the one that had held a blade and spoken death and mercy both — turned it over, and traced something on my palm with one small finger. Shapes from the first tongue. I knew them instantly, the way you know your own name. A binding. A small one. A gardener's binding, the kind my order used to coax seeds awake.

Nothing happened, of course. I was a locked box with pretty carvings on it now.

"That's so it's not gone," she said. "Even if it doesn't work. Now it lives in me too. You gave me all your words when you spoke through me. Fair trade."

I am not often speechless. That night I managed it twice.

* * *

Aldric received us on the third day — not in the throne room, which stood shuttered, its throne draped in mourning cloth for the man who had died inside his own body thirteen years ago. He received us in a small room with a fire and bad wine and a table scarred by a century of ministers' rings, and he stood when we came in, which kings do not do, and stayed standing until we sat, which kings really do not do.

He was diminished. There was no polite way around it. Thirteen years of the crown-thing feeding on him had taken muscle and memory and whole seasons of himself; he moved carefully, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust, and sometimes he lost words mid-sentence and had to circle back for them like a hawk that had missed its strike. But his eyes were his own. That was the miracle under all the wreckage. When he looked at you, someone looked back.

"Noor Iversen," he said. "Wren." He pronounced the child's name carefully, having learned — Serah told me later — that it came from the first tongue and meant *a bird that returns after storm*. He'd asked. A king had asked a child what her name meant. "I owe you both a debt no treasury can hold. So I won't insult you with a treasury." He folded his ruined hands on the table. "Name it. Land, title, gold, a seat at court, my left arm if you've a use for it. Anything within the reach of a diminished king, and several things beyond it."

I had thought about this. I'd had three days and a sleepless night to think about it, and the answer had been sitting in me the way answers sit in gardeners — quietly, underground, waiting for warmth.

"Just two things," I said. "First: justice. Real justice, not the ceremonial kind. The tithe-villages were bled for thirteen years in your name — oaths sworn on your authority, extracted by men who knew what the crown was and fed it gladly. Some of them are still in power. Torvald's ashes haven't even cooled and half his colleagues are already explaining how they always suspected. I want the ledgers opened. I want the oaths extracted in your name formally voided — every forced vow, every tithe-binding, dissolved the way the crown itself was dissolved. And I want it done by people the villages choose, not by people the court appoints. Otherwise you'll free them from one master and hand them to another wearing a kinder mask."

Aldric listened without interrupting, which I was told he'd never once done as a younger king, and when I finished he said, "Done. Serah has already started. Continue."

"Second." I looked at Wren, who sat very straight beside me, watching the king with the level gaze she'd learned somewhere on the road, from women who didn't look away from powerful things. "Safe passage home. For both of us. To the valley."

Something flickered across the king's face — disappointment, maybe, or relief, or the complicated arithmetic of a man discovering his savior wants nothing he knows how to give.

"The valley," he repeated. "Where magic cannot follow."

"Couldn't," I corrected. "The stones stand open now. By choice or by ruin, I'm honestly not sure which. But it's home. There's a garden that needs replanting, and a girl who needs teaching, and—" I paused, hearing myself. "And a woman who spent thirty years being the last of something, and would like to find out what it's like to be the first of something instead."

The king was quiet a moment. Then he said, in a voice stripped of all ceremony: "When I was a young man, before all this, I wanted to be a farmer. My father found the notion hilarious." He smiled, crooked and human. "Go plant your garden, Noor Iversen. Consider both debts paid, and consider this too: my door, and Serah's sword, and whatever letters of protection a diminished king can write, stand open to you for the rest of my life. If the valley ever needs anything — send a bird. Or better. Send the girl. She seems the sort who arrives exactly when she's needed."

"I'm the sort who gets *sent*," Wren said primly, "when people forget to ask if I want to go. This time somebody will ask."

The king laughed — a rusty sound, a hinge unused for thirteen years — and I saw Serah, waiting by the door, decide to let herself smile about it.

* * *

We left on a grey morning in the first week of thaw, with an escort Serah insisted on providing despite my protests, on grounds that "protection detail" was the least of what she owed a dead friend's heir-in-oath. We traveled slow. Wren collected things — river stones, odd feathers, the friendship of every dog within a mile of any road we walked. Flowers bloomed out of season wherever she slept, and she'd taken to leaving one behind deliberately at each village, a calling card, a promise: *something new grew here.*

On the fourth day, my mentor caught up with us at a crossroads shrine.

"Don't ask," she said, before I could. "Dead is a flexible condition for people who spent their lives tending thresholds. I have things to finish and places to be, and one of them, come spring, is your garden — there's something sleeping under it that you'll need help waking, and no, I'm not telling you what, and yes, I'm enjoying this."

"I hate when you do that," I said, for the second time, meaning it completely differently.

"I know." She squeezed my shoulder, then crouched to Wren's eye-level. "Guard her words, little bird. She'll tell you they're worthless now. Don't believe her. Words keep. Even spent ones. Especially spent ones."

Then she was gone down the south road, clicking her staff, humming something in the first tongue that sounded, to my now-ordinary ears, exactly like a lullaby and exactly like a warning.

We stood at the crossroads a long time. The valley lay north, three days' walk, its boundary stones visible on clear days from the high ridge — standing open now, I knew, though I hadn't seen them since the night I broke my oath and crossed. Wren slipped her hand into mine.

"Are you sad?" she asked. "About your words?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I was tired of lying, even kindly.

"Yes," I said. "And no. I had a gift that could break crowns and kill men and bind doors against armies. And I traded all of it for one sentence spoken through your mouth, and that sentence bought a king his soul back." I looked at my hand, holding hers. Ordinary hand. Enough. "I think maybe I was never meant to be a blade, Wren. Blades end things. Maybe I was always meant to be a garden. Gardens start them."

She considered this with the gravity she gave all my pronouncements, which was considerable, and then delivered her verdict:

"You're going to cry, aren't you."

"A little," I admitted.

"That's okay." She patted my arm, magnanimous as an empress. "I cried lots when I lost my mama. It means the missing part is big. Big missing parts mean big love. That's just how it works."

So I cried a little, at a crossroads in the thaw, with an eight-year-old patting my arm and the road home stretching green and wet ahead of us. And when we crested the ridge at dusk on the seventh day and saw the boundary stones standing open below us — not broken, not crumbled, but *open*, like gates unlatched by a hand deciding to trust — I felt the missing gap in me settle into its final shape. Not a wound anymore. A scar. A crown-shaped scar, almost, though mine was invisible and mine had been given freely.

The garden waited below in the last light, wild and wintered and patient.

"Well," I said to Wren, and heard my voice come out plain and warm and entirely my own. "Let's see what grows."
