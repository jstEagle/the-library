## 22. First Planting

### Noor

The boundary stones stood open.

That was the phrase I kept returning to, that first week of spring, because my mind refused to hold anything simpler. Not broken — the crown had done enough breaking to last the age. Not dead, either, though they had every right to be after what we'd asked of them. Open. The way a door is open when someone has deliberately unbarred it and propped it wide with a chair. The way a mouth is open when it has decided, against all habit, to speak.

I walked out to them on the morning of the first planting, as I had walked out every morning since we came home, and I put my hand on the eastern stone the way you'd check a horse's flank for fever. Warm. Faintly humming, the way a struck bell hums long after the sound has left the room. Once, touching these stones had felt like pressing my palm against glass with an ocean on the other side — all that magic straining at the valley's edge, and me inside like a fish in a very pious aquarium. Now the ocean had come inland, gently, the way water finds its level, and it moved through the grass around my boots in threads of pale light too shy to be called anything so grand as power.

"Still humming?" Wren asked from behind me. She carried the seed basket, which she had insisted upon carrying despite the fact that it weighed more than she did, on the principle that she was eight now and eight was practically grown.

"Humming," I confirmed.

"Good." She set down the basket with a grunt. "Torvald said the stones might go quiet once the crown was gone. He said—" she screwed up her face, doing her impression of the archivist's precise, dust-dry diction, "*'the resonance should decay naturally over a period of months.'*"

"Torvald also said he was our friend," I said, "right up until he sold us to the court."

Wren nodded solemnly, with the gravity of a scholar agreeing that sources must always be checked. Then she picked up the basket again, because there was work to do, and Wren had never in her life let a source-check delay actual dirt under her fingernails.

Here is what nobody tells you about saving a kingdom: afterward, there is still mud. There is still frost heave to clear off the beds and compost that has not turned itself and a fence the winter knocked flat in three places. There is still the whole stubborn business of feeding yourself, which does not pause simply because you have shattered an ancient oath-thing in a coronation hall while guards tried very sincerely to kill you. Aldric had offered me anything. Land, title, a place at his rebuilt court — he'd said it with that careful, newly-light voice of his, a man testing whether his own jaw still worked properly without something else moving it. And I had asked him for tithe-relief for the villages and a wagon home, and I would make the same bargain again every day for the rest of my life, because land and titles are just other kinds of crowns, and I have seen what grows on those.

But some mornings — and this is the confession part, the part I'll only write here, in the garden journal, where no one reads but me — some mornings my hands remember.

Not the fighting. The speaking. Swordspeech wasn't shouting; it was the opposite of shouting. It was reaching into the word beneath the word, the one the first kingdoms built their oaths on, and turning it like a key. When I spoke a blade's name, the blade remembered what it was for, and it became — briefly, terribly — honest. I spent thirty years with that in my throat. It is gone now. Spent, all of it, poured through Wren's small voice into the syllables that unmade the crown, because a speaker can lend the words but the key must carry them, and the price lands on whoever holds the hilt.

Some mornings I wake up reaching for that honesty the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. Some mornings I say *good morning* to the beans and hear nothing underneath it, just air doing its ordinary job, and I grieve. Briefly. The way you grieve a beautiful coat you sold to buy bread: no regrets, but your shoulders remember the weight.

Then I get up, because the beans do not care about my metaphysics, and neither does Wren, who at this moment was attempting to plant radishes in a pattern that could only be described as enthusiastic.

"Noor," she said, "what if we planted them in the shape of a sword?"

"We're eating these."

"What if we planted *some* of them in the shape of a sword?"

"You cannot eat a shape, Wren."

"You could *eventually*," she said, with the flawless logic of the young, and I let her have six radishes' worth of sword, because the truth was I liked seeing it. Let the garden grow a weapon made of food. That seemed like the correct direction for weapons to evolve.

### Wren

Noor doesn't know I read her journal.

Well. She knows I *can* read it. She taught me letters herself, last autumn, sitting by the stove with charcoal and slate, patient as stone, correcting my backwards E's eleven thousand times. But she doesn't know I read the *journal*, which lives on the high shelf behind the seed jars, and which she thinks of as private the way cats think of boxes.

It's not spying. It's homework. She told me once that the old order wrote everything down — every oath, every rite, every failure — because *"a speaker who won't record is a speaker planning to lie."* So reading the journal is practice. Also I wanted to know if she wrote about me. She does. Mostly I'm described as "stubborn" and "promising," sometimes in the same sentence, which I've decided is a compliment.

Today's lesson was names.

"The first thing a speaker learns," Noor said, kneeling across the seed bed from me with her ordinary hands pressed into the dark soil, "is that naming isn't commanding. Everyone gets that wrong. The knights thought swordspeech was a whip. Torvald thought it was a lever. Even the crown—" she stopped, the way she always stops near the crown, giving the wound its space, then went on. "Even the crown thought it was ownership. It wasn't any of those. A true name doesn't bend a thing to you. It reminds the thing of itself. That's all. That's everything."

"So when I speak a bean's name—"

"It remembers being a bean. Which sounds useless until you've watched a seed decide, all on its own, to hurry." She nodded at the bed we'd planted two days past, and I looked, and I felt my stomach do the flip it does now whenever the world shows me it's listening. Green tips. Six of them, pushing up through soil that shouldn't produce anything for another week at least. They hadn't hurried because I'd ordered them. They'd hurried because on planting day I'd crouched down and said their names — all forty-two, because Noor insists I learn each one individually, *this* bean and *that* bean, no shortcuts — and they had heard themselves spoken, maybe for the first time ever, and decided to rise up and be seen.

"That's the whole art," Noor said. "Everything else is grammar."

We practiced on the fence posts next. This was harder. Beans want to grow; that's their own wish already, and a speaker just clears the fog off it. Fence posts don't want anything. My voice found their wood-names anyway, deep slow names like sleeping oxen, and for a moment the nearest post leaned toward me the way a dog leans toward a hand, and Noor said, softly, "That's enough," and I let it go, and the post settled back into being a post, which I understood was the polite thing.

"The old order would have used that," I said. "For war."

"The old order used everything for war, eventually. That's why there's no old order." She brushed soil from her palms and looked at the open boundary stones, far down the slope, standing in their loose half-circle with the morning light coming through between them like guests arriving. "You're going to be the first new speaker, Wren. Not the last of anything. First of something. I need you to understand the difference, because the difference is everything we almost died for."

"Which is?"

"The last of something spends all its strength holding a door shut. The first of something decides which way it opens."

I thought about that all through lunch. I'm still thinking about it now, actually, writing this by candlelight while Noor pretends not to notice that her journal is on the low shelf tonight instead of the high one. She left it there on purpose. I'd bet the radish-sword on it. That's the other thing about Noor: she teaches by pretending not to.

### Noor

Aldric writes. I did not expect that.

The first letter arrived with the thaw, borne by a courier who looked deeply relieved not to find dragons or enchantments at the valley's mouth — the outside world has developed theories about us, most of them wrong, several of them flattering. The letter was written in a strong, careful hand, the hand of a man relearning his own penmanship, and it said, in full:

*I held the crown still for three heartbeats and it was the first honest work I have done in thirteen years. I am told I owe you everything. I am learning to pay debts slowly, so they stick. The tithe-courts are dissolved; the villages have their grain back and their grievances heard. Serah Vane's name is carved in the hall of lawful service, above the door, where everyone entering must pass under it — she would have hated the sentiment and loved the placement.*

*The court asks after you. I tell them you are gardening. They think this is a riddle.*

*Aldric*

He writes twice a season now. He always ends by asking what the garden needs, and I always answer honestly, because he of all people knows the value of an honest answer given freely: *compost, patience, and fewer visitors.* Last month he sent compost. An entire cart of it, royal-sealed, which Wren declared was the funniest thing any king had ever done, and she was right.

Serah's grave is on the ridge, under the rowan, facing east. I did not bury her in the valley — she belonged to the law, even the broken parts of it, and the law buried her properly with honors and weeping clerks. But I planted the rowan here at the boundary line, half-in, half-out, which felt right for a woman who had served two masters and finally got to serve herself. Wren speaks to it sometimes. Says its name. The rowan blooms out of season now, every season, white and stubborn, and I have decided not to examine whether that is magic or botany. Some things are better left as gifts.

The villagers come, these days. That took adjusting. For thirty years the valley's silence had been my whole society — me, the stones, the rusted blade I finally melted down last month to make hinges for the cold-frame, which felt less like sacrilege than I expected and more like a tool becoming a tool again, honest at last. Now there were neighbors walking up the path with cuttings and questions and, in the case of Marta from the mill, opinions about my fence spacing delivered at volume. The valley's protection was gone; anyone could walk in. Anyone could walk *out*. The children of the village had already begun treating Wren like a strange local saint, following her around asking her to make things bloom, and she charged them leaves and interesting rocks, which Noor-approved commerce if I ever saw it.

And once — only once so far — a traveler came up the path with the look I knew too well. Hollow-eyed, hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. A woman with a soldier's shoulders and a scarred voice, who stood at my gate and said, "They say a speaker lives here. They say the words didn't die with the crown."

"They say correctly," I said. "But the words aren't mine anymore. They're hers." I nodded toward the garden, where Wren was teaching three village children the names of carrots, and the carrots — I swear this on the rowan — were visibly preening. "Come back in ten years. She'll teach you. That's what the gate is for now."

The woman looked at the open stones for a long time. "And you? What are you, then, if the speaking's spent?"

I considered the question seriously, because she deserved a serious answer, and because I had been turning it over myself since midwinter.

"A gardener," I said. "First of something new."

She smiled — the first crack in all that hunger — and went back down the road toward whatever life was waiting for her, lighter by one question.

### Wren

Last thing today: Noor gave me the primer.

Not the forbidden one from her order's chest — that one we burned in the coronation aftermath, on purpose, because it was written by people who thought speakers should serve kings, and we agreed its lessons had rotted in the keeping. This was a new book. Plain paper, sewn binding, her own handwriting marching across the first page in letters I recognized from a thousand slates:

*First Principles of Speech, for Wren, who will do it differently.*

Inside were notes. Names of plants and posts and tools, with the true forms written beside them. Warnings — *never name a thing while angry; the thing keeps your anger longer than you keep your temper.* Questions she couldn't answer, marked honestly with gaps: *don't know. ask the beans.* And on the final page, one line alone:

*The last order guarded a door. Your job is to be one.*

I cried a little, which I'm recording here because she says a speaker who won't record is planning to lie.

Tomorrow we plant the west beds, and Noor says this year I do the sowing and she does the naming-of-nothing, just hands in dirt, and we'll see what comes up. The stones are open. The rowan is blooming. Somewhere down the road a soldier with a hungry voice is telling people the words didn't die.

She's right. They didn't.

They're asleep in a seed packet on my shelf, forty-two beans with forty-two names, waiting for spring — and spring, Wren Iversen-Amberdale is pleased to report, is here.
