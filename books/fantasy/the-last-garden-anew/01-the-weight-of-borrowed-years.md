## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The smuggler came over the pass at dusk, and Silas Kavanagh was waiting for him the way winter waits for a traveler—patiently, invisibly, certain of arrival.

She sat on the low stone wall that bordered the old shepherd's road, her hands folded in her lap like a woman resting, her face tilted toward the last copper light on the peaks. To anyone watching—and no one was watching; there was never anyone watching on the Kestrel Pass at dusk—she was just another tired figure taking the air before nightfall. Her pack sat at her feet. Her boots were scuffed to the color of dry earth. Everything about her was arranged to be forgotten five seconds after being seen.

That was the first rule: *Be forgettable.* Not hidden—hidden things invite searching—but forgettable, the way a fencepost is forgettable, the way a mile-marker is. She had spent eleven years perfecting it. Eleven years since she'd learned what lived behind her eyes, coiled and patient as a second heartbeat.

The smuggler's mule clattered up the switchback below her. He was a broad man with frost in his beard and a limp he favored on the left side, and he was humming something tuneless under his breath, which meant his thoughts were easy, unguarded, rolling along like water over smooth stones. Silas could feel him coming before she saw him—not his thoughts exactly, but the texture of him. Every mind had a texture. This one felt like wet wool and woodsmoke.

*Good,* she thought. *Simple. Simple is safest.*

She waited until he drew level with her wall, until his mule's breath fogged the air between them, and then she did the small thing she'd rehearsed ten thousand times: she let her gaze catch his, just for a moment, just long enough for a polite nod between strangers on a lonely road.

And she opened the door inside herself.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing no ballad ever got right, if anyone had ever been fool enough to sing about people like her. There was no flash of light, no gasp, no clutching of the temples. There was only a gentle unlatching, like a window swinging open on a still day, and then the smuggler's memories began to drift across the gap between them the way heat drifts off a hearthstone.

She took carefully. That was the second rule: *Take thin slices, never the loaf.* She skimmed the surface of him—the route he'd traveled, the faces he'd passed at the toll bridge, the weight of the crates strapped beneath the false bottom of his cart. She let his childhood stay where it lay. She left his mother's name alone, and the scar on his palm, and whatever griefs he carried folded small and deep. She took the road, the cargo, the names of his contacts in the valley towns—enough to sell, not enough to hollow.

He blinked. The hum died in his throat.

Silas smiled at him, warm and vacant. "Evening's coming on cold," she said. "You'll want to make the inn at Brindle Ford before dark."

"Aye," he said slowly, and frowned, and rubbed his temple as though a fly had troubled him. "Brindle Ford. Aye." He clicked his tongue at the mule and moved on, and by the time he rounded the next bend he had already decided the ache behind his eyes was the altitude. People were generous that way. They explained away their missing pieces so they wouldn't have to imagine theft.

Silas sat still until his footsteps faded, then drew a slow breath and reviewed what she'd gathered, turning each borrowed memory over like a coin bitten for gold. The route—useless to her, she knew these mountains better than her own hands. The cargo—salt pork and untaxed brandy, worth nothing. But the contacts. Two names in the valley towns who moved goods past the toll-houses without papers, which meant they also moved people, which meant they might pay good silver for a memory of their rivals' routes.

A modest take. A safe take. Enough for bread and lamp-oil and Maren's cut, and not one memory more.

She rose from the wall, shouldered her pack, and started down toward Thornfell as the stars came out hard and bright above the pass.

* * *

Thornfell at night smelled of peat smoke and tallow and the river's cold iron breath, and Silas loved it the way you love a coat that fits—a practical love, unsentimental, but real. It was a town built sideways against the mountain, its streets too narrow for carts, its houses stacked like drunks leaning on one another's shoulders. Nobody in Thornfell asked where your coin came from. Half the town lived on things that had fallen off the backs of smugglers' carts, and the other half made sure things fell.

Maren kept her shop below a chandler's, down a stair that smelled of rendered fat and old wax. The sign above the door read FINE CURIOSITIES BOUGHT & SOLD, which was a lie in the way all the best lies are—technically true and entirely misleading. Inside, the shelves held brass instruments, cracked porcelain, bird skulls, and forty-one glass vials stoppered with red wax, each one faintly warm to the touch, each one holding somebody's yesterday.

Maren herself sat behind the counter grinding pigment with a mortar, a broad woman gone gray at the temples, with forearms like a dockworker and reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She didn't look up when Silas came in.

"Route of a salt-pork runner," Silas said, setting two vials of her own—empty ones—on the counter. "Plus the names of two valley fences who move goods past the Kestrel tolls. Clean lifts. Thin slices."

"Names are worth little unless they're current." Maren ground harder at the pigment. "When did you take them?"

"Dusk today."

"Mm." She set down the mortar, finally looked up, and her eyes behind the smudged lenses were flinty and unreadable. "Current, then. I'll give you nine coppers for the lot."

"Fourteen."

"Ten, and I don't tell you what I heard this morning."

Silas went still. In eleven years of trading with Maren, she had learned that the broker's threats always came dressed as bargains, and that the dangerous ones were always delivered flat, without theater. "What you heard."

Maren took off her glasses, folded them, and laid them on the counter with the deliberate care of a woman buying time. "Hunters," she said. "Crown witch-hunters. They came through the Lowmark villages a fortnight ago. Burned a hedgewitch out of her cottage at Ashford Bridge—her, her cats, and the cottage both. And they're moving up into the mountains now, following the trade roads." She paused. "They've reached Thornfell."

The word landed in Silas's chest like a dropped stone. *Hunters.* Everyone in the trade said the word the same way—quickly, quietly, the way you'd say *wolf* within earshot of sheep. She had never seen one. For eleven years she had told herself that people like her were a story hunters told each other to keep their pay coming, that the burnings happened in far provinces to careless women who danced naked under moons and deserved what they got. She took memories in thin slices. She was careful. Careful people didn't burn.

"That's a week-old rumor from three valleys away," she said, and heard how thin her own voice came out. "Hunters don't climb passes in autumn."

"These did." Maren leaned forward on her forearms. "Girl. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then we'll pretend I never said it. A hunter doesn't need proof. A hunter needs a feeling. You know what a feeling is worth in a market square? Nothing. Worth less than nothing—it costs you everything and pays the crowd in entertainment." She tapped one lacquered nail against the counter, once. "Whatever you've got buried in that clever head of yours, keep the lid on it. No lifts for a while. Live poor. Poor is invisible."

"I can't live poor. I have rent. I have—"

"You have a head full of other people's roads, and none of them lead anywhere good right now." Maren slid ten coppers across the counter anyway, because she was fair even when she was frightened, and Silas understood suddenly that Maren *was* frightened—that the broker who never flinched at anything was grinding pigment with hands that weren't quite steady. "Take it. Go home. Lock your door. And Silas—" the broker's voice dropped, almost gentle, which was worse than any threat, "—if someone knocks and asks you questions with kind eyes, you be nobody. Be a seamstress. Be duller than dishwater. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you." Silas swept the coppers into her pocket. "But it's a rumor, Maren. Rumors are your stock-in-trade; you of all people know how they swell."

"Rumors swell," Maren agreed. "Fires spread. Pick whichever image helps you sleep."

Silas slept badly, which was itself an omen, because she had trained herself years ago to sleep like a paid guard—light, dreamless, ready. That night the dreams came anyway: fire, and a door burning, and her mother's voice calling a name she couldn't quite—

No. Not that. Never that.

She woke before dawn with her heart slamming and the room cold around her, and told herself it was only the mountain wind.

* * *

She spent the next day doing nothing, and discovered that doing nothing was a skill she had never developed. She mended a shirt seam that didn't need mending. She counted her coins twice. She stood at her window above the cooper's yard and watched Thornfell go about its morning—fishwives arguing over the river stalls, the baker's boy hauling his basket uphill, two men re-thatching a roof—and tried to see it the way Maren's warning had taught her to see it: as a thing that could end. It refused. Thornfell looked eternal, shabby and smoky and stubborn, a town that had outlasted every king who'd ever claimed it.

By late afternoon the restlessness won, and Silas broke her own rule for the first time in eleven years.

Not a lift—she kept that promise, barely. She just went walking, down through the tanners' lane and out along the river path, telling herself the fresh air would settle her nerves. The evening was gold and violet, the water loud and bright, and she was halfway to the old millrace when she passed a knot of people outside the Wheelwright's Rest who were talking too quietly for comfort.

"—came up the south road this morning, four of them, grey cloaks—"

"—the smith's boy swears they stopped at the chapel and asked after names—"

"My cousin in Lowmark says they carry iron censers that smoke on their own, no fire under them—"

Silas kept walking. Slowly. Forgettably. Her heart kept its own counsel, hammering.

Grey cloaks. Iron censers. She turned up Cooper's Lane as the light failed, climbed the outer stair to her room above the cooper's yard, and told herself, one more time, with the particular firmness of a woman arguing with a rising flood: *Rumors swell.*

Then she saw her door.

Her door was plain pine, older than she was, swollen with damp like every door in Thornfell. She had painted it green two springs ago to cover the rot. And there, burned into the wood beside the latch at the height of a standing man's hand, was a mark she had never seen before and knew instantly, the way you know a snake among ropes.

It was a circle bisected by a vertical line, with a small flame curling up from the center—and it had been branded there, seared deep, the char still black and fresh enough that when she touched it, ash came away on her fingertip. Around the edges of the burn, the wood had warped and split from heat. Whoever had made it had pressed iron to her door within the last few hours, perhaps the last few minutes, while she walked the river path believing rumors were only rumors.

She looked up and down the stair. Empty. Below, the cooper's yard lay quiet, the barrels ranked like sleeping cattle. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped. The evening had not changed at all, and that was the most frightening thing about it—that the world could hold a brand-new terror and wear the same face it wore yesterday.

Silas stood on her own threshold with ash on her finger and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the mark was not an accusation. An accusation would have come with fists and torches and her name shouted in the square. The mark was something colder: a *record*. A flag planted. We know where you live. We are deciding what you are. We have not yet decided.

Or worse—we already have, and we want you to run. Running gives permission.

She stepped inside, threw the bolt, and lit no lamp. Through the shutter-cracks she watched the lane until full dark, cataloguing every sound: the cooper's dog, the wind, a cart on the far road, footsteps once—only a drunkard, weaving homeward, harmless. Her room filled up with shadows and her own careful breathing.

On the table by the window sat her purse with its ten coppers, and beside it the empty vials she used for lifting, and she found her eyes kept returning to them—to the trade, to the life she had built slice by careful slice, the life that a stranger had marked for burning without ever seeing her face.

*I should leave tonight,* she thought. Pack, slip out the river gate before dawn, be over the Kestrel Pass before the town woke.

But running cost money, and money was thin, and Maren owed her nothing beyond ten coppers, and besides—she pressed her palm flat against the door, against the rough crater of the brand—besides, some stubborn root in her refused to be driven from the only soil she'd ever struck down into. Thornfell was not hers. She had never let it be hers; belonging was a debt and she paid no debts. But it was *hers* the way a hiding place is yours, and she was not ready to give it up to a circle and a line and a curl of flame.

She would wait one day. One day, and if the hunters showed themselves, she would weigh leaving again.

Below in the dark, the cooper's dog began to bark and did not stop, and somewhere down Cooper's Lane, boots rang on stone in a rhythm too measured for drunkards—four sets of them, maybe five, walking in step.

Silas Kavanagh blew out the candle she had never lit, and in the darkness, with her back against the branded door, she began—for the first time in eleven years—to plan how to disappear.
