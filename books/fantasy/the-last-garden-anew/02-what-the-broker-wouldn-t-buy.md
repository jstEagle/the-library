## 2. What the Broker Wouldn't Buy

The courier came into Thornfell at dusk, coughing blood into a red-spotted kerchief, and Silas Kavanagh watched him from the shadow of the cooper's awning with the particular patience of someone who had learned that hunger and opportunity often arrived wearing the same coat.

He was young—no older than she was—but he moved like an old man, one hand pressed to his side as though holding something in, or keeping something from falling out. His boots were good leather, road-worn but well-made, and beneath his travel cloak she caught the glint of a chain. Not gold. Silver, maybe, or something duller that only pretended to be precious.

She fell into step behind him, three paces back, matching her stride to his, letting the rhythm of the cobbled street do its slow work. This was the first rule of the trade: never rush. A memory lifted gently left no scar; a memory torn left a wound that hunters could smell for miles. Maren had taught her that in her first winter, back when Silas had still believed the old broker's gruffness concealed something warmer underneath.

*Three paces. Match his breath. Let him forget you were ever there.*

The courier turned down Tanner's Row, where the evening stink of lye and offal gave way to the sweeter rot of the tallow-chandlers, and Silas felt the first threads of him come loose. People unraveled easily when they didn't know they were being read. His surface thoughts rose to meet hers like fish rising to bread thrown on water: *the inn, the inn, warm bed, the pain in my side is nothing, it's nothing, I'll be paid at the inn.*

Paid. There was the word she wanted. She let it open under her attention like a door left unlatched.

Coins. A purse of them, heavy, sewn into the lining of his coat. And beneath the coins, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, a vial. The knowledge of it surfaced in his mind with a strange, reverent weight—not the casual weight of money but the careful weight of something sacred, or cursed, or both. He had been told not to look at it. He had been told that looking would kill him faster than the sickness already eating his lungs.

Silas had been stealing memories since she was fourteen years old. She knew every flavor of human thought: the salt of grief, the copper of rage, the flat gray taste of ordinary days. But this—this was different. The courier's mind held the vial the way a tongue holds a broken tooth, touching it constantly despite the pain, unable to leave it alone.

*Don't look at it,* his master had said. *Carry it to Thornfell. Give it to the man with the green ring. Don't look.*

And so of course he had looked. Once, on the mountain road, feverish and alone, he had cracked the wax seal just enough to breathe in what slept inside—and what he'd breathed in was still lodged in him now, a splinter of someone else's dying, and Silas could feel it through his thoughts the way you can feel a fire through a wall.

Her mouth went dry.

She should have walked away. That was the second rule, the one Maren repeated until the words wore grooves in Silas's skull: *some things are priced too high, girl. When the fear comes up your spine like cold water, you turn around and you count your losses.* She had heard the rule a hundred times. She had broken it exactly once before, at sixteen, and had spent three days vomiting after lifting a single thread of a plague-nurse's memory.

But the purse was heavy. Winter was coming down off the mountains early this year, and coal cost coin, and Maren's prices had gone up since the hunters reached the lowlands. Silas told herself the lie all thieves tell—that she would take only a little, only what she needed, only the surface.

She followed him to the door of the Drowned Bell and waited while he argued with the innkeep about credit. Then, in the narrow dark between the streetlamp and the stair, she closed her eyes, matched her breath to his ragged one, and took.

* * *

Taking was not like the stories said. It was not a claw or a hook. It was more like standing behind a weaver and drawing out a single thread—one smooth, continuous pull, gentle as unwinding silk—while the pattern above stayed whole and the man below never felt the tug.

She took the memory of the innkeeper's face. She took the route he'd walked from the capital, the tolls he'd paid, the name of the man with the green ring. She took, carefully, the location of the purse in his coat lining, because she was a thief before she was anything else and pride demanded she do the job completely.

Then her fingers—her mind's fingers, the ones no one could see—brushed against the splinter.

It burned.

Not metaphorically. The sensation was physical, a white-hot wire drawn across the inside of her skull, and Silas gasped aloud in the dark of the stairwell. The courier turned, blinking, blood on his lips, and for one terrible moment their eyes met—his clouded with fever, hers wide with something he might have recognized if he'd been less far gone.

"You," he whispered. "You're the one who—"

She ran.

She ran because running was instinct, because the splinter was burning, because the thread she'd touched was unraveling now whether she willed it or not, spooling itself into her mind with the unstoppable momentum of a bucket falling down a well. She burst out onto Tanner's Row and made it four steps before the world dissolved.

* * *

She was in a garden.

Not any garden—a garden the size of a valley, terraced and glowing, where roses the color of embers climbed trellises of white stone and the air tasted of honey and hot metal. And in the center of it, beneath a glass dome older than the kingdom itself, something breathed. She couldn't see it. She could feel it—a vast, slow heat, patient as geology, like a heart buried in the earth that had been beating since before hearts were invented.

A man stood at the dome's edge with pruning shears forgotten in his hand. Gray at the temples, broad-shouldered, with soil worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that it seemed part of him. Edric Hale, royal gardener, though the name came to her the way names come in dreams—from nowhere, from everywhere, from the memory itself.

He was watching the king die.

King Aldric Vane, third of that name, forty-one years on the throne, lay among the ember-roses with his crown askew and his hands open at his sides, and he was smiling. That was the worst part, Silas thought distantly, the part that would stay with her longest. He was smiling up at the gardener as though they were old friends sharing a joke, and he said:

"Don't tell my daughter."

And then the smoke came.

It did not arrive. It *condensed*—out of the shadows between the rosebushes, out of the gaps in the flagstones, out of the spaces between the king's last breaths—as though the garden itself had been holding it, saving it, feeding it for years without anyone knowing. It gathered over the dying man like a shroud being drawn, and Silas felt Edric's horror as her own, felt his legs refuse to move, felt his voice die in his throat.

The smoke bent down. It drank.

There was no other word for it. It lowered itself to the king's parted lips and *drank*, and the king's smile faded by degrees, like a lamp running out of oil, and when it was done the smoke straightened up and began—took shape. Shoulders. A jaw. Hands. The gray became flesh the way frost becomes lace on a window, and when it finished, King Aldric Vane stood among the roses looking down at himself with mild curiosity, flexing his new fingers, testing his new voice.

"Hale," it said, in the king's exact voice, and Edric's terror flooded through Silas so completely that she tasted bile in the waking world, somewhere far away, on her knees in a gutter on Tanner's Row. "You saw nothing tonight. You tended the king in his final hours, and the king recovered, and you are proud of your work. Do you understand?"

Edric understood. Edric nodded. And the thing wearing the king's face smiled with the king's smile and said, "Good. The garden will keep its secrets. They always do."

* * *

Silas came back to herself in an alley off Tanner's Row, cheek pressed to wet cobblestone, blood in her mouth where she'd bitten her tongue. Above her, Thornfell's crooked rooftops cut the twilight into jagged pieces, and somewhere a dog was barking, and somewhere a woman was calling a child home to supper, and none of it mattered, none of it was real anymore, because Silas Kavanagh now carried inside her head the death of a king and the birth of a monster.

She vomited into the gutter. Then she lay still and shook.

*Get rid of it,* she told herself. *Give it to Maren. Sell it. Forget it.*

But even as she thought it, she knew the words were empty. Maren wouldn't buy it—Maren wouldn't touch it with a ten-foot pole, and worse, Maren would know within a heartbeat of seeing Silas's face that something was wrong, and then Maren would be in danger too, and everyone Maren talked to, and everyone they talked to, a web of complicity spreading out from Silas like cracks in ice. The thing in her head wasn't merchandise. It was a brand. It was a lit fuse walking around in the shape of a woman.

Because here was what she understood, lying in the filth of Tanner's Row with the taste of the garden still coating her teeth: the courier hadn't been carrying that vial to Thornfell by accident. Someone wanted it delivered quietly, to a man with a green ring, sealed and unseen. Which meant someone powerful had decided the memory was dangerous. Which meant whoever sent it—the queen's faction, a rival house, some desperate loyalist—had also sent others to make sure it never arrived. Hunters didn't burn hedge-witches for lifting a farmer's wedding day. Hunters burned people who knew how the king died.

And now she knew. She knew the way you know a wound—intimately, permanently, in the body's own language. The smoke-thing had looked at Edric Hale and commanded silence, and Edric had sealed the memory in a vial with his own hands rather than carry it loose in his skull, and somewhere in the capital a man with a green ring was waiting for a delivery that would never come, and somewhere behind him stood whatever power had set the courier on the road, and behind *them*—

Behind them stood the thing on the throne, wearing a dead man's smile, telling its kingdom that the hunt was going splendidly.

Silas got to her feet, slowly, using the alley wall. Her legs held. That surprised her.

The courier was dead. She learned that an hour later, in fragments of tavern talk that drifted to her table in the corner of the Drowned Bell: found at the foot of the stairs, lungs full of blood, poor lad, no family, no one to claim him. No one mentioned the missing purse. No one ever did.

She sat with her untouched ale and turned the purse over in her hands—heavy, real, enough coal to last the winter twice over—and felt nothing but the cold. This was the arithmetic of her life, she thought. Coin on one side, ruin on the other, and her thumb always pressing down on the scale.

She should run tonight. Pack nothing, tell no one, take the smuggler roads south before the hunters' network tightened around Thornfell like a fist. Every hour she stayed was an hour the thing in her head grew heavier, more findable. She had seen what the hunters did to the gifted—they called it cleansing, they built the pyres in the market squares and made the children watch—and she had survived this long precisely by being no one, by taking small memories from small lives and leaving no ripples.

But her feet wouldn't move toward the door. Because there was a name caught in the stolen memory like a fishbone in the throat: *Edric Hale*. Disgraced, dismissed, disappeared—the court gossip in the courier's other recollections had been gleeful about it—last rumored to have crawled away to die somewhere in the hill country west of the capital. A man who had watched a king be eaten and been forced to smile about it. A man who had sealed the truth in glass because he couldn't bear to hold it.

Silas stared into her ale and hated herself for the thought that arrived next, because it was not a thought of survival. It was a thought of *witness*. Somewhere in her, beneath fourteen years of careful invisibility, lived the stubborn conviction that what she had seen deserved more than to die with her in a ditch. The king had said *don't tell my daughter*, and the smoke had made sure no one could—but the smoke had never counted on a thief on Tanner's Row with sticky fingers and a talent for finding the seams in things.

Outside, the first snow of the year began to fall, fat and silent, erasing the streets one flake at a time. Silas pulled her hood up, counted out three coppers for the ale she hadn't drunk, and stepped into the white.

Tomorrow she would go to Maren, say nothing, buy supplies, and vanish west. Tomorrow she would start hunting a ghost of a gardener through the hills, following a dead king's last request into a story much larger and much crueler than any she had ever stolen.

Tonight, she allowed herself one look back at Thornfell—at the crooked roofs, the smoking chimneys, the yellow windows of the homes of people who had never known her name—and then she walked north along the river road, alone, carrying the most dangerous memory in the kingdom locked behind her eyes.

Behind her, at the edge of town, a lantern flared in the window of the broker's shop where Maren kept her ledgers. And nailed to the door, blackened at the edges as though burned there moments ago, hung a strip of parchment marked with a symbol Silas had only ever seen from a distance: a circle bisected by a flame.

The hunters had reached Thornfell.

They had reached it, and they were already marking doors.
