## 3. Hunters on the Thornfell Road

The bells woke her before dawn.

Not the market bells—those rang in threes and stopped. These tolled without rhythm, one hand dragging against the bronze in long, arrhythmic groans, the sound of someone pulling rope who did not care how it sounded, only that it carried. Silas lay in her narrow bed above the cooper's shop and listened, and the cold came into the room before she understood why.

She had slept badly. The vial's contents still moved behind her eyes like sediment in disturbed water—the king's garden at dusk, a smell of crushed fennel, a voice saying *he is not breathing, he is not breathing, but something else is*—and each time she surfaced from sleep, the memory was there waiting, patient as a debt collector. She had begun to think of it as *the bleeding thing*. It soaked through everything now. She could not lift a traveler's memory without the royal garden rising up inside the theft like a drowned body surfacing.

The bells groaned again. Below her floorboards, the cooper's dog began to bark, and then stopped mid-bark, which was worse than barking.

Silas rose, dressed in the dark, and went to the window.

Fog lay along Thornfell's single street like something poured. Through it she could see lanterns—many more lanterns than the hour warranted—and figures moving between them with a purposefulness that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world. They wore grey. Not the grey of labor or poverty, which is a soft grey, a worn grey, but a hard grey, uniform as slate, and over their breasts they bore the mark she had seen last night nailed to Maren's door: a circle bisected by flame, stitched in thread the color of old blood.

Witch-hunters. In Thornfell. Not passing through—*sweeping*. She knew the difference because she had spent nine years learning every kind of danger this road country offered, and the difference was simple: travelers passed through you; sweeps took you apart.

*You should have left last night,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Maren. *You should have run when I told you.*

But she hadn't, because running required coin, and coin required work, and work meant one last lifting, and one last lifting had been her plan for three years running. That was the truth about survival, she thought grimly, lacing her boots: it was always one more job away from being enough.

She gathered what mattered in the time it took to say a prayer she didn't believe. The false-bottomed satchel. Her knife. The little iron box under the third floorboard that held eleven years of savings—not enough, never enough, but all of it hers. The vial itself, wrapped now in wool and oilskin and bound against her ribs where no search would find it, because the hunters would not simply kill her if they found it. They would open her skull for it first. She had seen the instruments in the courier's memory, the ones the crown's inquisitors used on memory-thieves, thin and curved and patient.

She went down the back stairs, past the sleeping cooper and his wife—she pressed two silver bits into the wife's sewing basket, not out of kindness exactly, but because she might need them to remember her fondly later, and because guilt was cheaper paid in advance—and slipped out through the wood-yard into the fog.

* * *

She told herself she was heading for the smuggler paths immediately. She told herself that any delay was stupidity.

She went toward the market square anyway.

It was not courage. She would be honest with herself about that much, at least; honesty was the one discipline her trade demanded. It was that Thornfell was the closest thing to a shape her life had ever had, and some part of her—a part she had never fed, never watered, never once acknowledged—needed to see what was happening to it before she cut herself loose. Nine years of winter mornings buying coffee at Doss's cart. Nine years of Maren's ledgers and Maren's sour tea and Maren pretending, year after year, that she wasn't the nearest thing to family Silas had permitted herself. You could tell yourself you belonged nowhere, that belonging was a trap for people who couldn't count, and believe it right down to the marrow. But the marrow apparently kept its own ledger.

The square was full.

They had lined people up against the fountain—not the accused, not yet, just the ones who had been slow to open their doors, the ones whose faces were known to someone whose face was also known. A hunter with a ledger walked the line, touching each person's temple with two fingers, and where he touched, a faint heat-shimmer rose, like breath on a cold window. Testing. Reading the surface noise of ordinary minds for the wrong kind of silence, the wrong kind of song.

Silas knew the technique from the other side of it. Every thief of memory learned to flatten herself, to make her mind a room with nothing in it, a corridor with no doors. She did it now, standing in the shadow of the chandler's awning, and felt the hunter's attention slide across her like water across oil.

Then it stopped. Came back.

Stopped.

Her heart slammed once, hard, and she made herself breathe the way Edric-whose-name-she-didn't-know-yet breathed in the dying man's memory, slow and even, a man watching his king die and keeping his face empty. *Nothing here,* she pushed outward, the oldest lie in her craft. *Nothing here but a woman late for work.*

The hunter frowned. Looked at her a moment longer. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they held the particular blankness of men who had burned the part of themselves that might have objected to the work.

He moved on.

Silas exhaled so slowly it felt like bleeding. And then she saw what was happening by the fountain's edge, and the relief curdled instantly into something heavier.

Maren stood between two hunters, and her hands were bound.

Not roughly—they were professionals, and professionals are gentle with merchandise—but bound, wrists crossed and cinched with grey cord, and Maren's ledgers were stacked in a wet pile at her feet where someone had thrown them into the fountain trough. Silas could see the ink bleeding out of them into the water, years of transactions dissolving into grey bloom. Names of sellers. Names of buyers. Names of every memory that had passed through Thornfell's quiet underground for twenty years, including, somewhere in those ruined pages, her own name written in Maren's tight, slanted hand.

Maren was sixty-one years old and had a limp from a fall off the Kestrel Pass in '89, and she was arguing with the hunters in the flat, unimpressed voice she used on sellers who tried to inflate their wares. Silas read her lips across the square: *...you have no authority... this is licensed trade... write to the magistrate in Corvane, he will tell you—*

The lead hunter backhanded her without changing expression, the way a man brushes a fly from his sleeve.

The sound carried across the square, and the whole line of townspeople flinched as one body, and nobody moved. Silas did not move. She stood in the shadow of the chandler's awning with her flattened mind and her hidden vial and she did not move, and she understood, in that moment, that this was what she had built her life to do: to watch, to weigh, to survive. She had never let Thornfell belong to her precisely so that losing it would cost nothing.

It cost something. It turned out to cost a great deal.

They dragged Maren toward the wagon at the square's far end—the wagon with the cage, iron-barred, already half-full of people from the outlying farms, and Silas saw the miller's daughter among them, a girl of sixteen who had once sold Maren the memory of her first snowfall because her family needed bread. The girl caught sight of the crowd and mouthed *help me* at no one in general, at everyone, and Silas watched the words pass over the faces of her neighbors like wind over wheat, bending nothing.

Maren, being shoved up into the wagon, did not look at the crowd. She looked, for one moment, directly at the chandler's awning—at the shadows beneath it, at the exact spot where Silas stood—as though sixty-one years of reading desperate people had taught her precisely where a clever girl would hide. Her lips moved. No sound reached across the square. But Silas had spent nine years learning to read mouths across dark rooms, and there was no mistaking it:

*Run, fool.*

And then the wagon lurched forward, and the cage bars swung shut, and the bells began again, and Silas turned and ran.

* * *

She did not take the north road. The north road was the road anyone would watch, and besides, it descended toward the lowlands where the hunters' wagons rolled, and she had no intention of traveling the same direction as caged neighbors. She took the goat track behind the tannery, the one that switchbacked up through scree toward the ruins of the old watchtower, and from there—

There were only four ways off the mountain massif that held Thornfell, if you counted the way most people counted: the north road, the river gorge, the Kestrel Pass, and the long southern traverse to Vess. The crown patrolled all four. What the crown did not know—what only smugglers knew, and only a handful of smugglers at that—was that the mountain was hollow in places. Old mining runs from before the war, sealed officially, unofficially connected by the kind of shortcuts men build when the alternative is hanging. Silas had bought the map of them six years ago from a dying contraband-runner, paying him in the only currency he wanted: she had taken the pain of his last illness out of his head and left him clean and light and grateful, and he had wept while he drew her the routes in charcoal on a strip of sailcloth.

She still had that sailcloth. She had never sold it, though she'd been offered good money twice. Some things, she had reasoned at the time, were worth more than coin. She had not examined too closely why she'd thought so.

By midmorning she was above the tree line, and Thornfell lay below her like a thing seen through smoked glass: the smoke of its chimneys, the grey wagons crawling its street, the tiny bright sparks where things were burning. They had started early with the pyres. From this height she could not see what burned—records, perhaps. Furniture from marked houses. Or perhaps not furniture. The witch-hunters had come to the mountains with new methods, the courier's memory had told her that much: they burned the gifted alive less often now, preferring to take them to the capital for *examination*, but they burned the houses of anyone who sheltered them, and they burned the belongings, and sometimes, when the queue grew too long for the wagons, they simplified matters.

A plume rose from the direction of Maren's street, and Silas made herself stop looking.

*You never belonged to them,* she told herself, walking, the wind tearing the words away as fast as she thought them. *You took from them and gave nothing back. You kept your own door locked. This grief is counterfeit. Grieve properly or don't grieve at all.*

But her feet knew the smuggler paths, and her feet had learned them hoping, though she'd never admitted it, that one day she might walk them in the opposite direction—might come home to Thornfell instead of fleeing it. The word arrived unbidden and unwelcome and true: *home.* She spat into the wind and kept walking.

* * *

The first mine-run opened behind a curtain of loose shale below the old watchtower, a crack barely wide enough for her shoulders, breathing cold air that smelled of iron and deep stone. She squeezed through with her satchel scraping, and darkness closed around her, and she allowed herself, for the first time since dawn, to stop flattening her mind—and immediately regretted it, because the bleeding thing surged up at once, eager, delighted to have her attention.

*The garden at dusk. Fennel crushed underfoot. The king kneeling among the white roses, and his breath going out of him, and something leaning over him drinking, drinking, patient as winter—*

"Stop," she said aloud, and her voice fell dead in the tunnel. "I know. I know what you are."

The memory did not stop, of course. Memories never obeyed. But she built walls around it the way she'd been taught—compartmentalization, Maren called it, the broker's term for locking the dangerous stock away—and staggered onward through the dark by feel and by the small flame of her candlestub, following a route she had walked only twice, both times with a guide.

Twice, she heard things in the tunnels ahead of her. Once, dripping water. Once, something else—scratching, deliberate, then silence when she raised her light. She did not investigate. The mountain had its own tenants and none of them paid rent.

She emerged near nightfall on the southern face, in the saddle of rock above the Vess road, and the view nearly put her back on her knees.

Below, spread through the valley like spilled mercury, were fires. Dozens of them. Campfires ringed with grey tents, and beyond them, on the road itself, a checkpoint: barricades, lanterns, mounted riders. The hunters had not merely swept Thornfell. They had sealed the mountain. Every pass watched. Every road choked.

Except, perhaps, the ones drawn in charcoal on a strip of sailcloth by a dying man who owed her his peace.

Silas ate cold bread in the lee of a boulder and studied the valley until the light failed, marking the gaps. There—one ridge east of the road, where the trees grew thick and the terrain broke into ravines, the hunters' fires thinned to nothing. The sailcloth showed a route there: the Drover's Scar, a shelf-path cut by smugglers along the cliff face, invisible from below, dropping into the forest south of the checkpoint. Three days' walking to Vess. Two, if the weather held and her nerve did.

She was folding the sailcloth away when she felt it.

Not heard—felt: a pressure against the edges of her mind, delicate and probing, like fingers testing a lock. She froze. Flattened herself instantly, the corridor-with-no-doors, the empty room. The pressure withdrew a fraction. Returned. Withdrew again.

Across the valley, on the checkpoint road, a rider had dismounted and was standing very still, facing the mountain. Facing, as far as Silas could judge from the distance and the failing light, her exact stretch of slope.

The hunters could sense it. Of course they could. The courier's memory had warned her, buried in its final moments: *they track the stolen things, the crown's hounds, they smell a lifted memory like a wolf smells blood—* She had thought it a figure of speech, the fevered embellishment of a dying man. It was not. Something in her—the stolen knowledge coiled around her own gift like two snakes swallowing each other—rang out across whatever sense these men used, loud as a struck bell. She was carrying a memory of the king's death. To them she must blaze like a signal fire.

The rider turned and spoke to his companions. Lanterns began to move. Up the slope, unhurried, the way men move when they are certain and merely wish to confirm it.

Silas ran.

Not up—the open scree would show her silhouette against the last sky-light. Down and sideways, into the ravines where the trees stood thick, letting the darkness take her piece by piece, branches raking her arms, the vial's wool wrapping slick with sweat against her ribs. Behind her, above her, she heard the jingle of harness and voices calling to one another in the clipped cadence of organized pursuit, and once, clearly, cutting through the wind:

"She's on the mountain! The marked one—the reader says she's still on the mountain!"

The marked one. So they had a name for what she was now. She had spent nine years being no one, invisible, a rumor with quick hands, and in a single day she had become a marked thing hunted across a burning mountain—for a memory she had never wanted, about a death she had never witnessed, carrying a secret that would kill her slower and worse than any pyre.

She ran until the voices faded behind her and the trees closed overhead and the Drover's Scar opened at her feet like a promise: a ledge no wider than a coffin, running along the cliff into the dark, the only road left in the world that was hers alone.

At the mouth of the path she stopped, chest heaving, and looked back once at Thornfell's distant glow—Maren in a cage, Doss's cold coffee, the bells, the bleedyards of her whole careful life.

"I'm sorry," she said, and did not know entirely whom she was saying it to, and suspected that was the point.

Then Silas Kavanagh stepped onto the smuggler's road, and the mountain swallowed her, and far below in the valley a lantern began, patiently, to climb.
