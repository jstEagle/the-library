## 4. The Gardener of Ashes

Edric Hale had learned to measure his days in seedlings.

It was a small life, the one he had made at the edge of Ashfen—a cottage with a leaning chimney, a plot of black soil that grew nothing the crown would want, and a view of the marsh where the reeds went gold each autumn and no one came to look. Ten years of it. Ten years since the palace gates closed behind him and the king's own voice—*that thing's* voice, he corrected himself, though the correction never stuck—had pronounced him mad before the assembled court. *The gardener has lost his wits, poor man. Grief does strange things. See him safely out of the city.*

He had not lost his wits. That was the cruelty of it. He remembered everything with the terrible clarity of a man who had watched something impossible and been told, gently, by everyone he loved, that he was broken.

So he grew things. Beans that no one ate but him. A row of junipers he was training into shapes he would never explain to anyone, because explaining would mean speaking of the royal garden, and speaking of the garden meant speaking of the king's death, and speaking of the king's death meant the pyre.

He was on his knees in the bean rows when he heard the gate.

Ashfen had no thieves worth the name and fewer visitors. Edric rose slowly, brushing loam from his knees, and saw her standing at the sagging wooden gate: a young woman, thin as a winter branch, hood thrown back to show dark hair cropped short and practical, eyes that did the thing eyes were not supposed to do—they seemed to be looking *through* him, cataloguing him, the way he might assess a sick tree for the rot beneath the bark.

"You're Edric Hale," she said. Not a question.

"No," he said, which was true in every way that mattered anymore.

She didn't smile. "I know what you saw."

The words went through him like frost through late blossoms. He set down his trowel with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not give them the satisfaction. "You'll want to leave," he said. "This is private ground."

"Your memory says the garden gate opens from the inside only if you sing to it." Her voice was flat, unhurried, almost bored, and that boredom was worse than any urgency. "It says you kept the key-verse in your left boot because your master trusted no lockbox. It says the king smelled of lilies the last week of his life, and you thought it was the new compost, and you hated yourself for three days before you understood what it meant."

"Stop."

"It says—"

"*Stop.*"

His voice cracked across the garden like a whip over oxen, and somewhere a marsh bird went up shrieking into the grey sky. The woman stood unmoved, arms loose at her sides, watching him the way one watches weather come in.

"How," Edric said, and found his throat had gone tight as rootbound clay. "How do you have those things. Those are mine. No one knows those. I told no one."

"I took them," she said simply. "From a courier. He was dying. He'd carried them sealed in a vial—your confession, or whatever the brokers call it—and I needed coin, and I opened it, and it—" She paused, and for the first time something flickered behind the flatness, something almost like apology. "It bled into me. I've been living inside your last day at the palace for two weeks now. I watched the king die. I watched the other thing drink him. I watched you kneel in the lily bed and be too afraid to scream."

Edric sat down. It wasn't a decision; his legs simply ended their employment. He landed hard on the cold soil among the beans and put his face in his hands, and ten years of careful silence came apart in his chest like wet paper.

* * *

He made tea because his hands needed work or they would do worse than shake. The woman—Silas, she said, when he demanded a name—sat at his rough table with the wariness of someone accustomed to being thrown out of places, and drank nothing until he nodded at her cup twice.

"Say it plainly," he said. "All of it. From the beginning."

She gave him the shape of it: Thornfell, the mountain passes, the lifting of memories from travelers' heads and the selling of them to men like the courier who had died carrying Edric's own past in glass. The witch-hunters sweeping the villages. The mark burned onto her doorframe. And underneath all of it, threaded through her recital like wire through wax, the thing she carried without wanting to: the knowledge of what wore the crown.

"The fire under the garden," Edric said, when she finished. His voice sounded far away to himself. "You felt it. In my memory. Tell me what you felt."

"Heat," Silas said. "Old heat. Like standing near an oven door. And something breathing underneath the roots—slow, so slow. And the creature feeding on it. Every night, kneeling where the king knelt, drinking from the ember through the roots of the oldest pear tree. It's why the garden blooms out of season. Why the roses never fully die in winter." Her eyes lifted to his. "And it's why the fire is dying. It's been eating for years. There isn't much left."

Edric looked at his hands. Gardener's hands, scarred and split, nails rimmed with permanent shadow. Hands that had once tended the most beautiful ground in the kingdom and had run from it, run from the truth of it, let a madman's exile be stitched over his mouth like burlap over a seedling.

"I screamed that night," he said quietly. "Inside. Where no one could hear. I knelt in the lilies holding His Majesty's hand while the light went out of him, and I watched the smoke rise off his breath and take his shape the way water takes the shape of a cup, and I did not scream. I thought: if I scream, I die, and if I die, no one will ever know. So I buried it. I wrote it down and sealed it and paid a courier more than a year's wages to carry it to Thornfell, and I let them call me mad, and I came here, and I grew *beans*."

"Bitter," Silas observed. "But honest."

"Do not presume to—"

"Somebody has to say it. You've had ten years alone; I've had two weeks of your head. Trust me when I tell you the self-pity gets old fast." She said it without malice, which somehow made it land harder, like a hoe turning soil he'd left fallow too long. Then she leaned forward. "Hale. Listen to me. The hunters can sense the memory in me. They're coming for me, and if they find me, they find the vial's origin, and they find you. But that's not why I'm here. I'm here because there's a princess—Isolde—in Vess, hiding in a wine cellar, who already suspects what her father is. And because the fire under your garden is the only thing in the world that can unmake that creature. And because it's going out."

"And you want the gardener to walk back through the gates he was thrown out of, and show three fugitives the way down to a dragon."

"Yes."

"No," said Edric Hale.

Silas stood, unhurried, and settled her hood. "Then I'll find another way. Someone else remembers something. The groundskeeper's boy, maybe. The under-almoner. Someone always remembers."

"There is no one else. The verse dies with me. The paths under the roots—I mapped half of them myself; they exist nowhere but in my skull." He heard his own voice rise and forced it down. "Which means you're asking me to burn. Don't you understand? If the hunters are sensing *you*, they'll sense me the moment we move together. Two marked people walking toward the capital during a festival celebrating the hunt. We won't reach the garden. We'll reach the pyre, and the creature will go on wearing the king forever, and everything I buried will have been for nothing anyway."

"Yes," Silas said. "That's roughly the shape of it."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside the window the marsh wind moved through the reeds, and Edric thought about how many thousands of evenings he had listened to that sound and called it peace, when it was really just silence with the volume turned up.

"Why do you care?" he asked suddenly. "Truly. You steal memories for coin. You've admitted it. What is the false king to you?"

Silas was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't answer.

"When I opened your vial," she said at last, "I expected to feel rich. Stolen royal memories fetch a fortune. Instead I spent two weeks being a man who loved a garden more than his own safety, and who failed it, and knew it, every hour of every day." Her jaw tightened. "I don't care about kings. I care that you have to live in this hovel growing beans because telling the truth got you destroyed. And I care that whatever is under that garden—it's *alive*, Hale. It's been down there in the dark for centuries and that thing up top is drinking it to death. Somebody should stand between them. Nobody ever stands between them."

Edric looked at her then, really looked, and saw what he had missed at the gate: not a thief assessing him, but a young woman holding a stranger's grief inside her chest like swallowed river-water, unable to spit it out.

"You felt all of it," he said. "The shame."

"All of it."

"Then you know I can't refuse you. You've been wearing my conscience for a fortnight." He laughed—an awful sound, rusted from disuse—and pushed back his chair. "God help us both. Wait there."

He crossed to the hearth and pried up the flagstone he had never told anyone about, not even the marsh birds. Beneath it lay an oilcloth bundle, and within the bundle: a spade with the royal cipher filed off but still ghosting the handle, a leather roll of pressed seeds from the garden's rarest beds—he had taken them the day he left, a theft he had never regretted—and a strip of parchment soft as cloth, inked with the key-verse in his dead master's hand.

He hadn't sung it in ten years. He found, touching the parchment, that he still remembered the tune. Of course he did. Some things grow deeper than forgetting.

"What are the odds?" Silas asked from the doorway, watching him with those cataloguing eyes. "Honestly. Us reaching the capital alive."

"Poor," Edric said. He wrapped the bundle and slung it across his back, and felt something shift in his chest—not hope exactly, hope was too grand a word for it—something smaller and tougher. The way a seed feels when it finally decides the frost is done. "But the garden taught me one lesson above all others, Kavanagh. Things that look dead very often aren't. They're waiting."

He banked the fire, took one last look at the bean rows he would never harvest, and stepped through his own gate for the second time in ten years—this time toward the place that had cast him out.

Behind them, the marsh reeds bowed and whispered, and ahead, three weeks' hard road away, an old fire guttered in the dark, waiting for its gardener.
