## 5. A Princess in a Wine Cellar

Isolde had learned to count her days by the sound of boots.

Not the soft, shuffling tread of Vess's market folk, who wore their soles thin on cobblestone and thought nothing of it, but the hard, deliberate strike of soldiers' heels—the kind of walking that expected the world to move out of its way. Three days ago she'd heard them pass above the wine cellar and held her breath until her lungs burned. Two nights ago, they'd stopped. She'd listened to their voices through the floorboards, muffled into a low river-sound, and heard one of them say *the princess was seen at the ferry crossing*, which was a lie, because Isolde hadn't been near a ferry in five weeks. Someone else had been seen instead. Some other girl with dark hair and a tired face, dragged off toward the capital in chains while Isolde sat in the dark beneath a tavern called the Drowned Bell, counting bottles she would never drink and listening for boots.

She had been in the cellar eleven days. She knew it was eleven because she scratched a mark into the barrel-stave beside her each dawn, when the light came thin through the grate at street level and turned the dust to drifting gold. Eleven marks. The stave already bore older scratches—tallies from some long-dead cellar-keeper, or perhaps just the teeth of time—and she liked to imagine that whoever had made them had also been hiding, also been counting, also been afraid. It made the dark feel less like a punishment and more like a tradition.

Her father's men were hunting her. That sentence still refused to sit properly in her mind, no matter how many times she turned it over. *My father's men are hunting me.* She kept reaching for the man who used to fill that word—*father*—and finding him gone, the way a room is gone after a fire: the shape of it still there, the walls, the windows, but everything warm burned out of it.

The king who sat the throne now looked exactly like her father. He walked like him, laughed like him, signed documents with the same impatient flourish on the final letter of his name. But he did not *smell* like him. That was the thing no one would believe, the thing Isolde had never managed to put into words without sounding mad: her father had smelled of beeswax and cedar and the particular green sweetness of the royal garden that clung to him no matter how often the laundresses washed his coats. The king smelled of nothing at all. Of cold hearths and empty rooms. As if someone had built a man out of description and forgotten that living things have scent.

And there were other wrongnesses, small ones she collected like a miser collects coins. Her father had been left-handed; the king wrote with his right. Her father had flinched at thunder—a boyhood thing, a memory of a storm that had killed his favorite horse—and the king watched lightning with the flat, curious attention of a cat watching rain slide down glass. Her father had loved her. However distant he'd grown in her last years at court, however buried under councils and wars and the slow grey weight of rule, he had *loved* her, and love leaves fingerprints all over a person. The king touched her shoulder once, at midwinter court, and his hand had been gentle and perfectly placed and completely empty, the way a portrait is gentle, the way a statue's hand on a child's shoulder means nothing except that the sculptor thought it would look right.

So she had run. Not all at once—she was not brave in the way songs describe bravery, all at once and shouting—but in the coward's way, in increments, in excuses. A visit to an aunt in the river country. A pilgrimage to her mother's shrine. A trading errand that grew legs and kept walking until it walked her across three borders and down into a wine cellar in Vess, where the last of her mother's jewels bought her silence from a tavern-keeper named Odile who asked no questions because Odile had spent forty years learning exactly which questions paid best left unasked.

Eleven days. And tonight, finally, the sound of boots again—but different this time. Two sets only, uneven, one of them dragging slightly, and they came down the alley behind the Drowned Bell and stopped at the cellar door, which was supposed to be hidden behind a stack of empty casks.

Isolde pressed herself into the gap between two brandy barrels, hand closing around the little knife she'd taken to sleeping with, and waited for the door to open and her life to end or change, having learned by now that those were usually the same event.

* * *

The door opened the way old doors do, with complaint. Light spilled down the stairs—lamplight, not torchlight, which meant travelers rather than hunters, hunters traveled in packs and carried fire like a threat—and then footsteps, and voices, low and arguing.

"—I'm telling you, Hale, this is the town. I read it off the courier before he died. *Vess, the Drowned Bell, ask for the woman who keeps the back cellar.* If your princess isn't here, then we've crossed half the kingdom for a rumor."

"And if she *is* here," said the other voice—older, roughened, with the clipped vowels of someone who'd once spoken softer words in finer halls, "then we're leading witch-hunters straight to the king's daughter, and we should both be hanged for it. Slowly."

Isolde's breath caught. *Hale.* Edric Hale. The name surfaced from years down, from her childhood, from the garden. Old Edric, who had taught her the names of flowers when the tutors weren't looking, who had let her plant a crooked row of beans behind the glasshouses and praised them as though they were roses. Edric Hale, who had vanished the same season her father died—the season everything went wrong—and whose disgrace had been announced in a single dry line of court record that no one ever explained to her.

She stepped out of the shadows before she could talk herself out of it.

"Master Hale?"

The lamplight swung toward her and she saw them clearly for the first time: Edric Hale, grey now, gaunt, his gardener's hands scarred and knotted, wearing the face of a man who had been angry for a very long time. And beside him a young woman, slight and sharp-eyed, with dark hair cut short in the practical manner of someone who couldn't afford to be grabbed by it, dressed in a smuggler's leathers that fit her the way borrowed clothes always do—not quite, but well enough to pass.

The young woman's knife was out before Isolde finished speaking. Fast. Faster than any courtier moved.

"You know him?" the woman said. Not a question so much as a demand wearing a question's clothes.

"I know him." Isolde kept her hands visible and her voice level, the way you spoke to spooked horses and armed strangers. "Edric Hale. Royal gardener. He taught me to graft apple stock when I was seven, and he told me never to tell my father, because the king believed gardening should be left to professionals, and Master Hale said—" she found herself smiling despite everything, despite eleven days of dark and fear, "—he said the king could prune a hedge but he couldn't grow anything, and that this was the difference between ruling and tending."

For a long moment Edric Hale simply stared at her. Then something moved through his face like weather—recognition first, disbelief, and then a grief so sudden and naked that it hurt to watch.

"Princess Isolde." His voice cracked on the second word. "Gods below. You were *small*. You were this high." He put his hand at his own chest. "You used to follow me around the herb beds asking why things died in winter."

"Because I wanted to know if they came back."

"They mostly don't," he said. "That's the lesson nobody wants."

The young woman had not lowered her knife. She stood with her weight balanced, her eyes moving between Isolde and the stairwell behind her, cataloguing exits, and Isolde recognized the posture for what it was: not hostility, exactly. Arithmetic. This was a person calculating whether Isolde was worth more alive than dead, and Isolde had the sudden vertiginous understanding that the answer might come out either way.

"And you are?" Isolde asked her.

"Silas." No surname offered. The knife stayed where it was. "Odile sent us down. She said there was a guest in the cellar who paid too well and asked too few questions, and that if anyone ever came asking about the palace, we should meet." Her eyes narrowed. "Though she didn't say the guest would be the *princess*. That changes the price of everything."

"Does it?" Isolde lifted her chin, falling back into a tone she hated herself for using, the court voice, the cold one. "Then let me save you the trouble of negotiation. You want to reach the false king. I want to reach the false king. Our interests align, and I have knowledge neither of you has—every servant's passage, every forgotten stair, every sealed door in that palace. My grandmother had them mapped when she was queen, and she taught them to me because she trusted me more than she trusted locks."

Silence. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, the tavern noise murmured on—laughter, a fiddle badly played, the ordinary music of people who were not hiding from anything.

"The false king," Edric said quietly. He set the lamp down on a barrel-head, and the light steadied, and his face in it was terrible and tired. "You said it. Just now. *False king.*"

"Yes."

"You believe it." He took a step toward her, and Silas's hand twitched but Edric didn't seem to notice, or didn't care. "You've *seen* it? You know what sits on the throne? Because I have carried what happened in that garden for six years, girl, six years, and every soul I've ever tried to tell has looked at me the way you look at a madman—with pity and a little fear and a hand drifting toward the door. So before I spend another hour of what's left of my life on this, I need to hear you say it plainly. What do you think your father is?"

Isolde realized she was shaking. Not from cold, though the cellar was cold enough, but from something older and hungrier than cold. Six years. Six years of watching the thing that wore her father walk through her childhood home and smile at servants and sign death warrants, six years of knowing and being unable to say it, because to say it aloud was treason and madness in the same breath, and she had swallowed it and swallowed it until the swallowing itself became a kind of illness.

"I think," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted, so she started again. "I think my father died six years ago, in the royal garden, in the spring. I think whatever is wearing his face drank something out of him—I don't know what, his breath, his life, his *name*, something—and I think it has been ruling in his shape ever since. And I think it is getting worse. Colder. Hungrier. Every year the hunts get larger and the burnings more frequent, and everyone says the king has grown zealous, the king has grown stern, the king grieves and so burns witches—but kings don't grieve like that. Kings don't burn hundreds. Something else does that. Something that is *afraid*."

The last word surprised her as it left her mouth. She had never framed it that way before, but the moment she heard it she knew it was true. All of it—the hunts, the purges, the terror spreading outward from the capital like frost—it was not the behavior of a tyrant confident in power. It was the behavior of a creature terrified of being discovered.

Edric Hale sat down slowly on a brandy barrel, as though his legs had given notice.

"She knows," he said, to no one, to the dark, to God. "Six years and she worked it out on her own, from across the whole cursed kingdom, and no one believed her either." He looked up at Isolde, and his eyes were wet and furious. "They dragged me out of that garden in chains, Princess. They made me watch them burn the head gardener's records—forty years of planting books, my whole life's work—and they called me traitor for grieving at the wrong grave. And you. You were a *child*, and you saw it, and they hunted you for seeing it."

"Not for seeing it." Isolde shook her head. "For saying it. There's a difference, and it's the difference that matters. Half the court sees it, Master Hale. They see and they bow lower every year."

"That's the horror of it," Edric said. "Yes."

It was then that Isolde noticed the other one—Silas—had gone very still. Stillness of a particular quality, Isolde thought; not the stillness of caution but of recognition, the way a person goes quiet when a conversation walks unexpectedly over ground they know intimately. She filed that away. In the wine-dark of the cellar, with the lamp between them, the smuggler girl watched Isolde with an expression that was almost painful to look at—as though Isolde were proof of something Silas had desperately hoped was true and had not dared believe.

"You said the courier," Isolde said to her. "Before. On the stairs. *I read it off the courier before he died.* Read what off him?"

Silas hesitated. Glanced at Edric. Something passed between them, some silent negotiation, and Isolde watched it happen with the practiced eye of a girl raised at a court where every conversation had three layers and the real one was rarely spoken.

"She's a memory-thief," Edric said at last, heavily, as though confessing on Silas's behalf. "One of the lifters. She carries my memories of the king's death. Carries them in her head like—like cargo." He rubbed his scarred hands together. "I gave them to her freely, if you're wondering whether to judge. After I got past wanting to kill her for taking them."

"I didn't take yours," Silas said, and there was something raw underneath the flatness of it. "Yours bled into me. It's different."

"It is not different," Edric said. "But go on."

Silas turned to Isolde fully, and up close her eyes were strange—not the flat black of ordinary dark eyes but something deeper, layered, like looking down a well and seeing lights at the bottom that shouldn't be there. "I carry a dead man's last hours," she said. "A royal courier named Tam Wess, who died on the Thornfell road with a sealed vial in his coat. Inside the vial was a memory—your gardener's memory, the night King Aldous died. I broke it open without meaning to. Now I know what Edric saw. I know about the smoke. I know about the thing that knelt over your father in the garden and *drank* him." Her voice stayed steady, but her hand, Isolde noticed, had drifted to her own collarbone, pressing there as if holding something in. "And I know why the hunters can feel me coming. What's inside me is a death sentence with legs. Which is why we need you, Highness. We need the ways in."

*Highness.* No one had called her that in six years. It should have sounded absurd—a fugitive princess in a stolen cloak standing in a stranger's wine cellar—and instead it landed in her chest like a struck bell.

"You want the servant passages," Isolde said.

"I want every door in that palace that isn't a door," Silas said. "Edric says the garden is the key—that there's something under the garden, something old, and that whatever wears your father is feeding on it or fighting it or both. But the garden is guarded now, ringed with hunters, and the front gates are suicide. So. Forgotten stairs. Sealed doors. Ways your grandmother mapped and the creature doesn't know exist, because it wasn't born in that palace. It moved in." A ghost of grim humor touched her mouth. "New tenants never learn where the house keeps its secrets."

Isolde laughed—a short, startled sound, entirely inappropriate, the laugh of someone surfacing too fast from deep water. Six years of silence, six years of swallowing, and here in a cellar that smelled of oak and dust and other people's secrets, two strangers were saying the unsayable back to her as easily as discussing the weather.

"There's the Rose Stair," she heard herself say. "Behind the chapel of the winter court. My grandmother had it bricked up during the succession wars, but the brickwork is hollow—you can hear it, tap it with a knuckle. It runs from the chapel crypt down past the orangery and comes up inside the glasshouse ruins at the garden's north wall. The hunters don't watch the ruins because the roof fell in twenty years ago and everyone thinks it's dead space." She was talking faster now, the knowledge pouring out of her like water from a jar too long stoppered. "There are the coal-chutes under the east wing. The queen's private bathhouse connects to the garden's irrigation tunnels—if the sluice gates haven't rusted shut, and Edric would know better than I—"

"The sluice gates are bronze," Edric said faintly. "Bronze doesn't rust. I oil them myself, every autumn, I've done it since—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. "Since before. Gods. Twenty years of habit, and it turns out I was maintaining our escape route the whole time."

Something loosened in Isolde's chest, some knot she hadn't known the size of until it began to untie. She had imagined this meeting a hundred times in eleven days of dark, and in none of those imaginings had it felt like this—like *coming home to strangers*. Like finding out the conspiracy of the sane was larger than one.

But Silas was watching her with that well-deep gaze, and the humor had drained out of the thief's face, leaving something more careful behind.

"There's a cost to this, Highness. You should understand it before you commit. Hunters can sense what's in me—the stolen memory, the king's death. Traveling with me is traveling with a lit torch through dry grass. Odile will sell your location within the week if the price gets high enough; that's not an insult, it's arithmetic, and you should treat everyone including me accordingly. And if we fail—" Silas paused, and for a moment the smuggler's armor slipped and Isolde saw the girl beneath it, young and frightened and carrying far too much of other people's dying. "If we fail, the creature won't just kill us. It'll wear the failure. It'll stand at your father's window and look out at the garden and keep being him, forever, and no one will ever know there was ever another ending."

Isolde thought of her father's study. The chair by the window. The way the light used to come through it in the late afternoon and turn the dust to gold, and how the king who sat there now never opened the curtains, because light meant nothing to a thing that had no blood to warm.

"My father used to say," she said slowly, "that a garden is a bet you make against despair. That every seed is a wager that the future is worth reaching. He said it to annoy the priests, mostly." She smiled, and found to her surprise that it was a real smile, tired but real. "I'm making the wager. Whatever the odds."

Edric made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and covered it by pretending to adjust the lamp.

Silas studied her a moment longer, then nodded once—sharp, decisive, a merchant closing a deal—and slid her knife away. "Then we leave before dawn. Odile's cousin runs grain barges down the Lethe as far as Marrow Ford, and after that we're on foot through hill country the hunters don't bother with." She paused at the foot of the stairs, lamplight catching the edge of her jaw, and added, almost as an afterthought:

"One thing more. Before we go—there's a caravan of brokers camped outside Vess. Memory-brokers. And yesterday evening, one of their runners came through the market asking questions about a girl traveling alone." Silas's voice was carefully, deliberately calm. "He described me. He also mentioned that the caravan master had received word from the capital. Word that the king himself has taken an interest in a certain thief." She met Isolde's eyes. "Your father—or the thing that eats with his mouth—knows I'm carrying his secret, Highness. And he's not sending hunters anymore. He's sending something faster."

Above them, muffled by floor and earth and the ordinary world, the fiddle in the tavern stumbled, recovered, played on—and Isolde thought: *dawn is coming*, and for the first time in six years the thought felt less like dread than like a promise kept.
