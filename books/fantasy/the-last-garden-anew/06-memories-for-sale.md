## 6. Memories for Sale

The broker caravan found them on the third morning out of Vess, or perhaps they found it—a spill of painted wagons and paper lanterns camped in the crook of a river bend, all gaudy as a festival and twice as temporary. Silas smelled it before she saw it: ink, beeswax, and that faint copper tang of vials that had been opened too often. Somewhere behind her, Isolde made a small sound of distaste.

"This is how you live?" the princess said. "This is *how you live?*"

Silas didn't answer. She was watching the caravan's front wagon, where an old woman with silver rings on every finger sat sorting memories into lacquered boxes the way a grocer sorts fruit—by ripeness, by bruise, by what would spoil first. Maren had done the same work for eleven years in Thornfell, though Maren had never worn silk and never smiled at customers like they were meat.

"You'll want to let me talk," Silas said instead. "Brokers can smell desperation. It sours the stock."

Edric Hale grunted from under his hood. The gardener had not spoken much since Vess, since the princess had stepped out of that wine cellar with a knife in her boot and her father's eyes in her face, and Silas suspected he was doing arithmetic in his head—the kind where the sums were miles and the remainders were graves. He looked at the caravan now the way a man looks at a fire that once burned down his house.

"They'll know what we are," he said. "The moment you open your mind to trade, they'll know."

"That's the price," Silas said. "Everything is."

* * *

The old woman's name was Odile, and she received them in a wagon hung with curtains so heavy they swallowed sound. Inside, shelves climbed to the canvas roof, every jar and vial labeled in a hand so fine it looked printed. Silas counted exits out of habit: back flap, side window, the gap beneath the driver's bench. Three ways out. She'd need one if this went wrong.

Odile poured tea no one drank. Her rings clicked against the cups like small teeth.

"Three travelers," she said. "One smells of soil, one smells of court soap, and one—" her gaze settled on Silas with the pleasant weight of a hand on a scale, "—one smells of nothing at all. Which is its own signature, girl. I've bought from empties before."

"I'm selling, not selling myself." Silas set six vials on the table between them. She'd chosen them the night before, by lantern light, holding each one up and reading the memory through the glass the way other people read letters. A fisherman's dawn on the Irenmere, all silver water and patient joy. A wedding in Thornfell, the bride's laughter still warm in the wax seal. A child's first snowfall. A soldier's last letter home, unsent. A baker's hands kneading dough at midnight while his daughter slept upstairs. And one more, unlabeled, which she did not explain.

Odile lifted each vial to the lamp in turn, and Silas watched her own stolen life appraised.

"The Irenmere piece is clean," Odile murmured. "Little decay. The wedding has frayed at the edges—you held it too long without tending. Still, there are buyers who like the fray. Grief-adjacent markets pay well for things that feel *used*." She glanced up. "You tend your stock. Most lifters don't bother."

"My mother taught me to close jars properly."

It was a lie. Silas had no memory of her mother's voice, only her face—and even that lived in a vial inside her own skull these days, taken out and looked at so rarely that some mornings she woke unsure whether what she remembered was the woman or the remembering. But brokers liked lies with craft in them. Odile smiled as if she knew it was a lie and approved of the stitching.

"Passage to the capital costs more than coin these days," Odile said. "The roads are crawling with white hoods, and my drivers want hazard pay. Your six vials buy you three seats and my discretion until the Lethe crossing." She paused, rings clicking. "After that, discretion is sold separately."

"Done."

"Done," Odile echoed, and then, softly, almost kindly: "You're running from something with teeth in it. I can tell because you didn't haggle."

* * *

The caravan moved at dusk, lamps lit early, because brokers traveled like theater troupes—loud enough to seem harmless, fast enough to be gone by morning. Silas rode on the tail wagon beside a boy of maybe fourteen whose job was sealing fresh stock, and it was there, in the swaying dark among crates of other people's lives, that she began to truly watch what a broker caravan *was*.

A farmer's wife came to the night market they made at a crossroads hamlet, counting out coins with shaking hands. She bought a single vial: her son's memory of her, taken by a lifter years ago when he'd passed through desperate, and now sold back to her at triple the take-price because grief has no ceiling. She pressed the vial to her lips like a relic before she drank it. Silas watched the woman's face change as the memory took hold—watched her become, for a few shining minutes, the way her son had once seen her. When it faded, she wept, thanked Odile, and asked when they'd come again.

"She'll be back next month," the sealing boy said to Silas, matter-of-fact. "They always come back. First time's a gift. After that it's hunger."

Silas said nothing. Her hands were very still in her lap.

Farther down the row, a young man tried on a soldier's courage the way another man might try on a coat—drank a stranger's bravery at a border skirmish, straightened his shoulders, walked taller for an hour. A widow bought a decade of marriage compressed into a quarter hour and came out of it looking older and younger at once. Two children pooled their pennies for a shared taste of flight—one of Edric's colleagues' stocks, maybe, some royal gardener long dead who had once ridden a hawk-tamed falcon over the palace towers. The children screamed with joy and immediately begged their mother for more.

And Silas sat among it, the architect of all of it, and felt—for the first time in eleven years of careful, invisible, rule-following theft—something turn over in her chest like a stone in a plowed field.

She had always told herself the rules made it clean. Take only surplus; never take what holds a person upright. Pay fair prices to Maren, who paid fair prices onward. Never leave a mind hollowed. She had recited the rules so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and she had mistaken the smoothness for goodness.

But the farmer's wife at the crossroads had not looked fed. She had looked *starving*, and paying, and coming back.

* * *

Isolde found her at the wagon's rear past midnight, when the camp had gone quiet except for the creak of rope and the mutter of dreaming mules. The princess lowered herself onto the crate beside Silas without asking, which was, Silas was learning, simply how Isolde occupied space—as though the world were furniture arranged for her convenience and occasionally rude about it.

"You watched them tonight," Isolde said. "All evening. Like a hawk on a wire."

"Somebody should."

"Yes," Isolde said. "Somebody should have. For years, apparently." She turned the ring on her thumb—her one concession to disguise, a plain iron band over the signet she'd hidden in her boot. "I grew up on stories about people like you. Lifters. My nurse swore one stole her sister's whole childhood, left the woman staring at walls, not knowing why she cried at lullabies. I used to think they were bogeymen. Stories to keep children from talking to strangers."

"And now?"

"Now I've spent two weeks on the road with one. Now I've watched her sell a dead soldier's unsent letter to a broker who will mark it up four hundred percent." Isolde's voice stayed level, but there was heat underneath it, banked coals. "That letter wasn't surplus, Silas. That letter was the last true thing that man ever made. You took it because you could, and you sold it because you needed passage, and somewhere in between you decided those two facts balanced."

"They don't balance," Silas said. "Nothing balances. That's the first thing you learn." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, the trader's voice, and hated it a little. "You think I don't know what I am? I've known since I was nine years old. I chose the rules because the alternative was becoming the thing my—because the alternative was worse. I never took what holds a person up. Never. Not once."

"The farmer's wife," Isolde said quietly. "Buying her son's love of her back, one vial at a time. Did that hold her up, do you think? Or did your rules just decide her son's memory of his mother was inventory?"

The stone in Silas's chest turned again, grinding.

"You don't get to judge from a wine cellar," she said. "You hid from your father's hunters in comfort. I hid in gutters. The gift doesn't ask whether it's convenient. People starve politely where you're from. Where I'm from, they starve loudly, and lifting kept me fed when stealing bread would have gotten me hanged. You want to burn me as a monster, do it knowing I'm the reason you have a guide who knows every smuggler road between here and the capital. I'm the reason you're not in chains. My *filthy little thefts* bought those seats."

"I know," Isolde said. "That's what makes it hard."

Silas blinked. She had been braced for fury, for the cold imperial disappointment she imagined princesses were raised on. She had not braced for this—Isolde looking at her with something that wasn't contempt at all, but recognition, which was infinitely worse.

"I'm not saying you're wicked," Isolde went on. "I'm saying I watched you tonight, watching them, and something in your face was breaking. I know that look. I wore it for three years in that cellar, telling myself my father was ill, that the thing wearing him was a sickness someone could cure. Self-examination is agony, Silas. But it's also the only door out of the cellar." She stood, brushing straw from her cloak. "You carry other people's deaths in your head. You told us. The courier's vial. You know exactly what waits for anyone the false king fears. Maybe ask yourself why you keep every memory sealed tight, even the ones that hurt you. Even the ones that hurt *others*. Sealed things can't accuse you."

She walked away toward the fire, leaving Silas alone with the crates and the dark and the soft glassy clink of a thousand other lives settling in their boxes.

* * *

Silas did not sleep.

Instead, near the coldest hour, she crept to the stock wagon, picked its simple lock out of habit, and stood among the shelves with a shuttered lantern. Odile's inventory stretched around her: joy, grief, courage, terror, sorted and priced and waiting for mouths. She found the soldier's letter, newly cataloged in Odile's fine hand. *Unsent. Author deceased. Strong residual sentiment. Reserve for anniversary markets.*

Anniversary markets. The soldier's mother might walk into a caravan someday, hunting anything that smelled of her boy, and buy her dead son's final words at a markup, never knowing the lifter who took them had needed coin for boots.

Silas unsealed the vial and let it pour back into her own mind—not to keep, but to hold. She would return it. Somehow, someday, to whoever the letter was meant for, if such a person still breathed. It was a drop in an ocean she had helped fill. It was embarrassingly little. It was the first thing she had ever given back, and her hands shook doing it, and she could not have said whether the shaking was shame or relief or both wearing the same skin.

Behind her, a floorboard creaked.

Edric stood in the wagon doorway, hood back, moonlight in his gray hair. He looked at the open vial, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, the way he'd nodded at frost-bitten roses in another life—something damaged, something worth saving anyway.

"First one's the hardest," he said. "The rest get easier. That's not a comfort, mind. Just a warning."

He handed her a second empty vial from his pocket—he'd brought it for her, she realized; he'd been waiting—and together, in silence, the thief and the gardener began opening jars.

They were still at it when the sentry shouted from the north road—lanterns, many lanterns, moving fast—and across the dark field Silas felt it like a hook behind her ribs: the cold, patient pull of hunters who had caught the scent of a stolen king's death.

White hoods. Coming straight for the caravan.
