## 7. The Thing That Wears the Crown

Edric Hale had not slept in three days, and the memory would not let him.

It came the way it always came—unbidden, patient as root-rot—rising through him in the hour before dawn when the fire burned low and Silas and the princess slept in their separate corners of the ruined mill. He had learned, in the years since, to stop fighting it. Fighting only gave it teeth. Better to let it come whole, let it wash over him like cold water, and endure.

So he sat by the hearth with his scarred hands open on his knees, palms up, as if he might catch the memory as it fell out of him, and he went back to that last morning in the royal garden.

* * *

The garden had been at its most beautiful. That was the cruelty of it—the thing had chosen beauty for its cradle.

Edric remembered the light: early, honeyed, slanting through the larches along the eastern wall so that every dewdrop on the climbing roses burned like a struck match. He remembered the smell of turned earth from the beds he'd forked over himself two days before, black and sweet, and the sound of bees working the lavender in numbers he had never seen before or since—as if they too knew something was ending. He had been forty-one years old then, and his knees ached, and he was happy. He wanted the remembering-self to know that. He had been *happy*, kneeling in the mulch with dirt under his nails, when the king came down the gravel path alone.

King Aldous Verrin had never walked anywhere alone in Edric's twenty-two years of service. There were always attendants, always guards, always the rustle of a court that clung to royalty like burrs to wool. But that morning the king came down the path by himself, dressed plainly, in boots scuffed at the heels, and his face—

His face was already wrong. Edric hadn't understood it then. He understood it now, turning it over in the dark of the mill like a stone found in bread. The king's face was wrong the way a portrait is wrong after water damage: all the features present, correctly placed, but the *likeness* draining out of them, running somewhere below the frame. He looked like a man who had been described to someone who had never seen him.

"Edric." The king stopped at the gate to the sunken rose terrace, hands clasped behind his back in the old familiar way. "Walk with me."

"Your Majesty honors my mud," Edric had said, wiping his hands uselessly on his apron, falling into step. It was an old joke between them. Aldous Verrin had liked old jokes. He had been, in the years Edric served him, a good king—not a great one, not a wise one always, but decent, and tired, and kind to gardeners.

The king did not laugh. They walked the length of the terrace in silence, past the roses he had bred himself—*Verrin's Gold*, pale as candlewax—and down the steps to the lowest garden, the one no one visited because there was nothing in it worth seeing. Old yew. Flagstones going green with moss. A dry fountain shaped like a coiled serpent, cracked down the middle, dry for longer than Edric had been alive.

The king stood before the fountain and said, "I am dying, Edric."

"I'll fetch the physicians—"

"The physicians have done all they can. It's in the blood. My father's father passed it down like the crown itself." The king smiled, and the smile fit badly, like a borrowed coat. "No. I didn't bring you here for that. I brought you here because I want to be buried in this garden. Not in the crypt under the chapel with the marble and the lies about glory. Here. Under the yew. Where things grow."

Edric's throat had closed. "The council will never permit it."

"No," the king agreed. "They won't." He looked up at the yew then—at the tree, Edric thought, at the great dark canopy of it—but later, so much later, Edric would understand that he had not been looking at the tree at all. He had been looking *through* it. At what slept beneath it. "There is something you should see. And something I must ask. And I'm sorry, Edric. I am sorrier for this than for any of it. But I have no one else I trust, and less time than I thought I had."

And the king knelt—he knelt, King Aldous Verrin, on the wet moss before the cracked serpent-fountain—and pressed both palms flat against the flagstones, and spoke words in a language Edric did not know but which his bones recognized, the way livestock recognize weather.

The ground opened.

Not violently. There was no earthquake, no roar. The flagstones simply grew apart, the way lips part before speech, and beneath them was a stair of living rock spiraling down into a warmth that came up like breath from a sleeping animal's mouth. And the warmth smelled of—Edric had spent thirty years among growing things and had no better word for it than *summer*. Deep summer. The heat at the center of a hayfield at noon. Something older than summer.

"What is down there?" Edric whispered.

"The reason this kingdom exists," the king said. "The reason there was ever a kingdom here at all, and not just wolves and winter. Come. You should meet her while I still can make the introductions."

He remembered the descent. He would remember it until whatever end waited for him. The stair went down farther than the palace had foundations, farther than sense, the walls sweating warm mineral light that came from nowhere and everywhere, and the air thickened until breathing felt like drinking. And then the stair opened out, and Edric Hale, royal gardener, son of a hedgerow-thief, stood in the cavern at the bottom of the world and saw the dragon.

She was dying too.

That was his first thought, absurdly—that she and the king had caught the same sickness, that perhaps they had caught it from each other. She lay coiled in the vast dark like a hill that had learned to breathe, scales gone dull as old pewter, wings folded tight against a body grown thin. Her eye, when it opened, was the size of a cartwheel and dim as a lamp running out of oil, and it fixed on Edric with an attention so total that his knees simply gave, and he found himself on the stone floor with his forehead nearly touching it.

"She is the last," the king said softly, beside him. "The others died in the wars my grandfather fought, or fled east beyond the maps. Her fire has kept this land green for six hundred years. Every harvest. Every spring. The rivers don't freeze because she warms the roots of the world, Edric. We built our gardens on her dreaming, though we forgot it." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had frayed to thread. "And now her fire is guttering. And mine is going out. And something has smelled both."

"Something," Edric repeated. His voice sounded very small under the earth.

"It comes when a fire dies. It has always come. It waits outside the world the way a fox waits outside a henhouse—not evil, perhaps. Only hungry. Only made of hunger." The king looked down at his own hands, and Edric saw that they trembled. "When my breath goes, it will try to drink the last of hers. And if it drinks—if it takes the dragon-fire into itself—it will wear whatever shape pleases it. Forever. Nothing born can kill it then. Nothing grown. It will sit on my throne until the mountains wear down, wearing me, feeding on this country one soul at a time."

The dragon's eye had closed again. Her breathing filled the cavern, slow as tide.

"So what do we do?" Edric asked. "What do I do?"

"You keep the garden," the king said. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see. You keep the garden, and you remember, and someday—maybe not you, maybe someone after you—you tell the truth to whoever can carry it." He put his hand on Edric's shoulder, and it was heavy, heavier than a hand should be, the weight of a man leaning on the last thing left upright. "The garden remembers every hand that ever tended it. It's older than the crown. Older than her. If it can be reached—if someone can get down to her while there's still an ember left—" He didn't finish. He pressed his lips together and shook his head, and Edric understood that the king had run out of plans and was handing him the only piece he had.

They climbed back up into the morning. The bees were still working the lavender. The light was still honeyed. The king walked Edric to the garden gate and gripped his forearm once, gardener to gardener, and said, "Thank you," and walked back toward the palace alone.

He died four days later. Officially, in his bed, attended by physicians, mourned by a nation.

Edric knew better, because he had gone back to the sunken garden on the third night, unable to sleep, drawn by something he could not name—and he had seen.

* * *

He had seen the smoke come up out of the ground.

That was how his mind held it, in the years after: *smoke*. Though smoke was too gentle a word, too domestic, too much the stuff of hearths and chimneys. What rose between the roots of the yew in the moonless dark was more like the shadow of smoke, smoke's hungry twin—a column of moving absence that drank the moonlight rather than reflecting it, that made the night around it somehow *less*, the way a wound makes flesh less. It had no face. It had no edges. It had appetite, and appetite had a direction, and the direction was the king, who knelt where he had knelt before, at the edge of the opened ground, waiting for it.

Edric hid behind the dry fountain with his heart hammering and told himself afterward, forever after, that he had hidden because intervening would have doomed everything—the king's plan, the garden, the world. That was true. It was also true that he hid because he was afraid, bone-deep, animal afraid, and he had let his king die alone within fifty feet of him, and no amount of necessity had ever quite washed the taste of that out of his mouth.

The thing reached the king. It did not touch him. It *inclined* toward him, the way a drinker inclines toward the cup, and the king tipped his head back, baring his throat like a man offering a blade or begging one, and exhaled.

And the creature drank his last breath.

Edric watched the life leave Aldous Verrin—not gradually, not the way illness takes a man, but all at once, a silver thread pulled out of his mouth like yarn off a spool, and the thread went into the smoke, and the smoke *shuddered*. That was the worst of it, worse than the death. The shudder of satisfaction. The way the column of darkness gathered itself, contracted, and began—there was no other word—to *knit*. Smoke becoming shape. Shape becoming substance. And the substance was the king: his shoulders, his gray-streaked hair, his long gardener's stride, assembling out of hunger the way frost assembles ferns on glass.

In less time than it takes to tell it, King Aldous Verrin stood up from beside his own body.

Because the body was still there. It had fallen gently sideways onto the moss, an old man asleep in a garden, and the new thing stepped over it with the dead man's feet and looked down at it with the dead man's eyes and smiled with the dead man's mouth, practicing, and the smile fit perfectly this time. It had taken the likeness whole. It had drunk the shape along with the breath.

Then it raised its head and looked directly at the fountain. Directly at Edric.

For a moment that lasted a season, the gardener and the thing regarded each other across the moonless garden, and Edric understood that it knew exactly where he was, and what he had seen, and that it was deciding. Deciding whether to drink him too. He felt its attention the way you feel a hand hovering over your nape—not touching, but *about* to.

Then, distantly, a bell rang in the palace—the watch changing, ordinary bells, mortal bells—and the creature turned away, and dragged the king's body down into the opened ground, and sealed it under the yew, and walked back toward the lit windows of the palace wearing the kingdom's grief like a newly tailored coat.

Edric stayed behind the fountain until sunrise. Then he packed one bag, told no one, and ran, and had been running for eleven years.

* * *

"—Edric. *Edric.*"

He surfaced. The mill. The low fire. Princess Isolde crouched beside him with her hand on his arm, her face pale in the ember-light. Behind her, Silas stood in the doorway with her travel cloak half-fastened, watching him with those flat, careful eyes that missed nothing and offered nothing back.

"You were saying his name," Isolde said quietly. "My father's name. Over and over."

"Was I." His voice came out sanded raw. He realized his hands were shaking and closed them, and the scars on his palms went white. "Sit down, both of you. There are things you need to hear, and I'd rather say them once."

Isolde sat. Silas did not; she stayed by the door, weight on her back foot, always halfway to leaving. He had stopped taking it personally.

"I was there," Edric said. "The night your father died, Highness. Not at his bedside. In the garden. I saw what killed him, and I saw what wore him afterward, and I've kept it behind my teeth for eleven years because I was a coward and because no one would have believed me and because—" His throat worked. "Because I ran. I've told myself a hundred stories about why I ran. That staying would have gotten me killed uselessly. That I needed to survive to testify. All true, probably. None of it the truth. The truth is I hid behind a fountain and let him die and then I ran, and I have carried that like a stone in my chest every day since, and tonight it's coming out, because tomorrow we may all die on the road and I will not arrive at whatever's waiting with it still inside me."

Silence. The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the dead cornfields around the mill with a sound like whispering courtiers.

"He told me," Edric said. "Days before. He showed me the dragon—the last dragon—sleeping under the garden, her fire keeping the whole kingdom alive without knowing it. He told me something would come for that fire when his breath went. He asked me to keep the garden and remember and wait for someone who could carry the truth." He looked at Silas. "I think I've been waiting for you, though I didn't know your face. The memory you carry—I felt it leave me that night on the courier's road, or felt something like it. What you're holding is the last true account of the last true king. And what sits on his throne now is a hunger that learned his smile."

Isolde's face had gone through shock and out the other side into something harder. "The festival," she said abruptly. "Three days ago, a proclamation came through Vess. I read it twice because something in it itched. He's declared a grand festival—*the Festival of Cleansing Flame*—to celebrate the hunt's success. Public burnings in the capital square. He wants the whole country to come and watch." Her hands had curled into fists in her lap. "My father hated burnings. Hated the witch-hunts his own grandfather started. He ended them in his second year—pardoned everyone, apologized publicly. Whoever rules now has restarted them tenfold and calls it mercy."

"A hunger doesn't hate," Edric said. "It only eats. And a country busy burning its gifted is a country that won't notice what else is being eaten." He leaned forward. "But hear the rest, because this is why the festival matters to us. The proclamation named the date. The first day of the harvest moon. And the false king chose that date deliberately, whether he knows it or not, because it's the night of the old garden rite—when the kings of this line used to walk the sunken terrace and renew the covenant with the fire beneath. He's drawn to it. The hunger wants to finish its meal. Which means on that night, the gates to the deep garden will be openable—perhaps only on that night—and the creature will be *below*, close to the ember it's been starving."

Silas spoke for the first time, quiet as a knife laid on a table. "And the hunters?"

"Worse every day." Isolde's jaw tightened. "The roads out of Vess are strung with patrols. They took a family out of the miller's row yesterday—a grandmother and two children. The children screamed." She looked at her fists. "We cannot stay here. And we cannot go around the capital forever. Everything leads through the Lethe crossing eventually."

Edric nodded slowly. Eleven years of hiding, and here was the strange peace of it at last: the secret spoken, the stone set down, the purpose ahead of him plain as a straight furrow. He was still a coward. But a coward walking toward the thing he feared was a different animal entirely.

"Then we cross the Lethe," he said. "Tomorrow. Before the patrols thicken. There's a ferryman at Greyford who owes me a debt older than the hunt." He looked around the little circle of firelight—the thief, the princess, himself—and felt, beneath the dread, the first green shoot of something he had not dared feel in eleven years.

Hope. Fragile, and rooted in terrible soil, but growing.

Outside, far off in the dark toward the river, a horn sounded—once, twice, a third time. Patrol-signals. Closer than they should be.

Silas was already fastening her cloak.
