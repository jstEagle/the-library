## 8. River of Forgetting

The Lethe announced itself long before they saw it—a cold breath rising off the water, threading through the pines like a whisper of something older than the road. Silas felt it in her teeth first, that particular chill of deep water moving slow over stone, and then in her gift, which stirred uneasily the way a cat stirs when a storm is still hours off.

She said nothing about it. She had learned, in three weeks of traveling with Edric Hale and Princess Isolde of Vess, that saying things aloud gave them weight, and weight was the one currency she could not afford to spend.

The river appeared at last through a break in the trees: wide and slate-gray, moving with the unhurried patience of something that had been forgetting things for ten thousand years. The Lethe did not freeze in winter. The Lethe did not flood in spring. It simply carried what was given to it—silt, leaves, boats, bodies—and let the current take the edges off everything until even grief came downriver smooth as river glass.

"There," Edric said, pointing with his walking staff toward a shape downstream. "The ferry crossing. Old Tam's barge, if he's still alive."

"You knew him?" Isolde asked.

"I knew everyone who worked the palace roads, once." Edric's voice had that flat quality it took when he spoke of his old life—the voice of a man handling broken glass. "He ferried gardeners' wagons across when I was an apprentice. Charged double if you were carrying roses."

Silas hung back as they descended toward the bank, letting the other two lead. This was habit, not strategy—or so she told herself. In truth she had begun to watch them both the way she watched all people: cataloguing, weighing, noting the small tells of thought that flickered across a face like fish under ice. Edric rubbed his left thumb against his forefinger when he was calculating distances. Isolde went very still before she lied, stillness being her version of a blush.

Three weeks. Three weeks of shared fires and shared watch and shared silence, and still Silas caught herself doing it. Reading them. Lifting the surface-thoughts that drifted loose from any mind within arm's reach—not stealing, never stealing, just *catching*, the way a gutter catches rainwater—and telling herself that this was different from what she'd done to travelers on mountain passes.

It wasn't different. She knew it wasn't. But knowing and stopping were separated by a distance no map covered.

*They'll find out,* whispered a voice in her that sounded like Maren. *They always find out. And then they look at you the way everyone looks at a thief—with their hands over their pockets.*

"Silas." Isolde had turned, waiting. The princess wore a wool cloak now, dyed the brown-gray of wet bark, and without her court silks she looked less like royalty than like a sharp-eyed falconer's daughter—but the bearing remained, spine straight as a lance, chin lifted as though the whole world were a hall she intended to walk through without bowing. "You're drifting again. Where do you go?"

"Nowhere," Silas said, and heard how easily the lie came, and hated it a little.

Isolde studied her a moment too long. Then she turned back to the river, and Silas exhaled, and the moment passed, the way moments passed, the way everything passed if you didn't hold it too tightly.

* * *

Old Tam was alive, or something shaped like him was: a broad, gray-bearded man mending net by the ferry landing who squinted at Edric for a long moment and then laughed from somewhere deep in his chest.

"Hale! They told me you were dead. Or worse—*promoted*."

"Both rumors had their appeal." Edric clasped the ferryman's arm, and for a heartbeat Silas saw him almost smile, the ghost of the man he'd been before the garden burned him out. "We need the crossing. Tonight, before light."

Tam's laughter died by degrees. He looked at the three of them—at Edric's exile's clothes, at Isolde's hidden face, at Silas standing apart with her hood up—and something in his expression closed like a door. "There are men asking questions on the south road," he said quietly. "Gray cloaks. Iron pins shaped like sunbursts. They paid me silver yesterday to report anyone crossing north."

"And will you?" Isolde asked.

"I'm deciding right now, my lady, though I don't think I need to say which way I'm leaning." Tam spat into the water. "Get aboard. And pray your god and mine that the fog holds till midmorning, because the Lethe gives no cover once the sun finds it."

The barge was flat-bottomed and broad, built for wagons and livestock, with a single sweep-oar mounted astern and a rope strung bank to bank through an iron ring anchored mid-river. Tam poled them out into the current while Edric hauled the guide-rope hand over hand, and the fog closed around them like a held breath. The water beneath the hull was black and utterly still, and looking down into it, Silas felt the strange vertigo of depth without detail—as though the river were not deep but *empty*, a hole in the world filled with forgetting.

Her grandmother—no. Not her grandmother. That memory was sold years ago; she knew only that there had been a woman, and that the woman had said something about rivers. *Some waters drink what you give them.* The thought arrived with no face attached, no voice, just the words floating loose in the architecture of a childhood she had dismantled herself, board by board, to buy bread.

Silas pressed her palms flat against the barge deck and breathed.

That was when she felt them.

It started as a pressure behind her eyes, the same pressure she'd felt on the Thornfell Road the day the hunters took Maren—a wrongness in the near distance, a cluster of minds moving together with intent, and threaded through each mind a bright, disciplined thread of *belief* that hunters carried like a lit candle carried in wind. Witch-hunters did not doubt. Doubt would have saved them, and the Order burned doubt out of its recruits along with everything else human.

"How many?" Isolde murmured. She had seen Silas go rigid.

Silas opened her mouth to lie—to say she didn't know, to protect the secret one more hour, one more day—and then the fog ahead of them tore open like rotten cloth, and lying stopped being an option.

Four skiffs slid out of the mist, low and dark, each carrying three men in gray. At their center stood a figure whose mind Silas had already touched without meaning to, because it blazed: tall, gaunt, iron sunburst pinned at his throat, a brand-scarred jaw and eyes of such pale blue they seemed lit from inside. He raised one hand, and the skiffs spread into a line across the river with the practiced economy of wolves cutting a herd from the flock.

"By authority of the Crown and the Order of the Radiant Hand," the lead hunter called, his voice carrying flat and clear across the water, "stand to. No soul crosses the Lethe unexamined."

Edric's hand found his staff. Isolde drew a knife from her boot with the smooth speed of someone who'd been drawing knives since childhood. And Tam froze at his oar, caught between two loyalties, both of them fatal.

"We're pilgrims," Isolde called back, her voice pitched low and rough, peasant-voiced. Nothing of the throne room in it. "Bound for the shrines at Corvane."

"Pilgrims." The hunter smiled. It was not a kind arrangement of features. "Then you won't mind examination. The gifted leave traces, friend. Like oil on water. We need only come close enough to read the shine."

The skiffs closed. Ten yards. Eight.

And Silas understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only at the last possible moment, that there was no more hiding. The stolen memory in her head—the gardener's dying recollection of the king's death, the thing she had carried across half a kingdom—was a bonfire to these men's senses. They hadn't stopped them at random. They had been following *her*, the way dogs follow blood.

If she surrendered, they would burn her. If she ran, they would run faster. If she did nothing, they would take Isolde and Edric too, and the false king would keep wearing his dead face until the last dragon-fire guttered out and nothing in the world could kill him.

Eight yards. Six. She could see the hunters' hands now—gloved, holding iron chains coiled loose, the links etched with scripture that made her skin crawl where her gift brushed against it.

*They always find out,* said Maren's voice. *And then they look at you—*

No. Not like everyone else. Not with hands over pockets.

With fire.

"Get down," Silas said, and her own voice sounded foreign to her, calm and cold, a stranger speaking through her mouth. "All of you. Get down and do not move, whatever you hear."

"Silas—" Isolde began.

"*Down.*"

The lead hunter's skiff ground against the barge's flank. He stepped aboard lightly, boots ringing on wet planks, chain swinging from his fist, and up close his mind hit Silas like a wall of heat—the certainty, the zeal, and underneath it, buried deep where he likely didn't know it lived, a boy on a farm kneeling beside his sister's pyre, weeping, while men in gray told him his tears were the devil working in him.

She could have used that. Another time, another life, she might have—found the crack and widened it, turned his grief against him gently, the artisan's touch.

But Isolde was crouched at her feet with a knife, and Edric was raising his staff, and there was no time for craft.

Silas reached out with her gift and *tore*.

She had stolen memories for eleven years. She had lifted faces and first loves and the names of dead children; she had taken a smuggler's route-map whole and left him standing in the snow trying to remember why he'd crossed the pass. She had always been careful, always surgical, always leaving enough of the person intact that they'd never know what was missing—only a vague sense of loss, like waking from a dream you couldn't hold.

This was not that. This was putting her hands into the architecture of a mind and *pulling*.

The lead hunter's scream started human and ended somewhere else. Silas ripped the discipline out first—the trained walls of the Order, the catechisms, the iron certainties—and beneath them the belief itself, the radiant thread, tearing it loose like ivy stripped from a wall, taking plaster and mortar and chunks of the man himself with it. His memories scattered like a dropped deck: the farm, the sister, the pyres he'd lit, the faces of the burned, his mother's kitchen, the words of every prayer he'd ever spoken, all of it spinning loose into the fog between them, and Silas *caught* it—couldn't help catching it, her gift drinking the spill like parched earth drinking rain—

—and for three endless seconds Silas Kavanagh was a boy named Aldous Vey kneeling beside his sister's burning body, and the smoke smelled like pork roasting, and his mother's hands were pulling him away, away, away—

She screamed too. She couldn't tell if it was out loud.

The hunter collapsed to his knees on the barge deck, chain sliding from fingers that had forgotten what fingers were for. His eyes, those terrible lamp-blue eyes, went soft and vacant and *young*, and he looked up at Silas with the face of a man waking from a fever into a world he had no name for.

"Where—" he whispered. "Where is she? My sister. Where did they take—"

The other skiffs had fallen into chaos. Two of the hunters were staring at their leader as though watching a man be devoured; the third was shouting scripture, voice cracking. Edric moved like a much younger man, staff sweeping the legs of the first hunter who tried to leap the gap between boats. Isolde's knife flashed, not killing but persuading—a slash across a forearm, a shout of pain, an oar knocked into the water. Tam recovered his wits and put the barge's sweep hard over, and the current, the patient, hungry, forgetting current, took hold.

The last thing Silas saw of the ambush, as the fog swallowed it, was the lead hunter kneeling on the shrinking deck of his own skiff, cradling his empty head in his hands, weeping for a sister whose name he no longer knew.

* * *

They made the north bank in silence. Tam beached the barge, refused their coin with a shake of his head, and pushed off again into the fog without a word, though his eyes lingered on Silas a long, complicated moment—fear in them, yes, but something else underneath, something that might have been awe, or pity, or the beginning of a story he would tell for the rest of his life.

Then it was the three of them, alone on a pebbled shore with the great gray river sliding past, and Silas sitting on a fallen log with her hands shaking so badly she had to press them between her knees.

What she had taken was still in her. That was the horror of it—that her gift did not destroy what it seized but *kept* it, hoarded it, and now the inside of her skull was a battlefield littered with a stranger's life. A farmhouse kitchen. The smell of lye soap. Scripture in a child's voice. The specific weight of a younger sister asleep against his shoulder on the wagon ride to market. Firelight on twenty-three faces she had condemned, and the way each of them had looked at him at the end—some cursing, some praying, one singing.

And under all of it, the boy. Just the boy, kneeling in the ash, learning that love was a disease and tears were proof of infection.

"Silas." Isolde crouched in front of her. The princess's face was pale but steady, and there was blood on her knife-hand that she had not bothered to wipe. "Look at me. Are you hurt?"

"He's in my head." Her voice came out scraped raw. "I took him apart and now he's—I can feel his *kitchen*, Isolde. I can smell his mother's bread. I know the hymn they sang when they burned the cooper's daughter and I will know it until I die and I didn't *want*—" Her breath hitched. Eleven years of careful rules, careful distance, careful theft, and she heard her own voice cracking like a girl's. "I never wanted to be this."

"What did you do to him?" Edric asked quietly. Not accusation. Worse—curiosity, the honest question of a man who had watched a weapon demonstrated and needed to understand its range.

"I emptied him." She made herself say it plainly, because dressing it in softer language would be the last theft, the theft from herself. "Not all of him. Enough. He won't hunt anyone again. He doesn't remember why he was on the river." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I gave him mercy, Edric. Do you understand? The worst thing I've ever done to another person, and it was *mercy*. Because the alternative was watching them burn you both."

The silence stretched. The river forgot them, yard by yard, carrying the morning downstream.

Then Isolde did something Silas would turn over in her mind for years afterward. She sat down on the log beside her—close, shoulder to shoulder, in the manner of equals—and said, "I've known since Vess. Since the first week. What you are. What you can do."

Silas went very still. *Caught. Seen. The end of everything.*

"I watched you watch us," Isolde continued, her gaze fixed on the water. "For weeks. The way you'd go quiet and then suddenly know things. Which road was watched. Which innkeeper was lying. I grew up at court, Silas—I know spies, and I know liars, and you were neither. You were something else. Something frightened and useful and terribly alone." She turned, and her dark eyes were fierce. "I waited for you to betray us. Every night I waited. And instead you tore a witch-hunter's soul open to save a woman you've known three weeks. So here is what I believe: whatever you've done to survive, whatever you've stolen—you are not what they say the gifted are. And I will swear that to my dying day."

"You don't know what I am," Silas whispered.

"No," Isolde agreed. "But I know what you chose today. And choices are the only inheritance worth anything."

Edric said nothing at all. He simply picked up Silas's pack from where it had fallen, dusted it off, and held it out to her—an ordinary gesture, a servant's gesture, a *gardener's* gesture, tending something growing—and when she took it, he met her eyes and nodded once, the nod of a man revising a judgment he was not proud of having made.

They should have kept moving. Hunters traveled in companies, and where four skiffs failed, forty soldiers might follow. But for a long moment none of them moved, the three of them standing on the forgotten shore of the forgetting river, bound now by something that could not be unstolen, unburned, or given back.

It was Edric who finally broke the silence. He was looking east along the north road, where the fog was thinning into a pale gold morning, and his weathered face had gone strange.

"That mark," he said slowly. "On the lead hunter's collar. The sunburst pin. Did you see it clearly?"

"Iron and gilt," Silas said. "Why?"

"Because the last time I saw that design," Edric said, "it was stamped into the wax seal of a letter on the king's desk—a letter written in a hand I'd have sworn belonged to a dead man. And the date on it was three days *after* I watched the king die in the garden." He shouldered his pack and turned north, toward the distant smudge of hills. "Come on. There's a shrine two days from here, abandoned these thirty years. We'll be safe there. And Silas—" he paused, not turning around, "—whatever's in your head now that wasn't there this morning? When we make camp tonight, I want you to show me. All of it. Because I think that man's memories may hold more than a farm and a sister. I think the Order has been showing its hunters things. And I think one of those things is what's sitting on the throne."

Silas looked down at her trembling hands, full of a stranger's life, and understood that the hardest crossing was still ahead of her—not the river, but the opening of her own skull in front of people who had every reason to flinch.

She followed them north, into the thinning fog, carrying a hunter's stolen faith like a stone she could not yet set down.
