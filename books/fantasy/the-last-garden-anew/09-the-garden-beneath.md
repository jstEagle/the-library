## 9. The Garden Beneath

Edric Hale had not spoken the garden's name aloud in nine years.

He had thought it, of course—thought it in the grey hour before dawn when the dreams came, thought it with his hands buried in the poor soil of his exile plot behind the tanner's cottage in Vess, coaxing beans from ground that smelled of ash and offal instead of loam and rain. He had thought it every time he passed a hedgerow gone wild, every time he saw a rose cankered with blight that no honest pruning could save. But speaking it was different. Speaking it was a door opening, and doors, once opened, let things out as well as in.

Now he stood at the window of the safehouse above the chandler's shop, watching the last light fail over Vess's crooked rooftops, and he heard himself say it anyway.

"The King's Garden."

Behind him, Silas Kavanagh looked up from the map she'd been studying—a smuggler's chart of the capital's underways, bought with stolen memories and paid for in coin Edric didn't want to think about. Isolde sat near the cold hearth, her princess's posture softened by weeks of hard road into something almost ordinary, though nothing about her was ordinary. She had her father's eyes. That was the cruelty of it. Every time Edric looked at her, he saw the thing that wore those same eyes on a throne of black oak, smiling its borrowed smile.

"You've been avoiding this," Silas said. Not accusing. Observing. She had a thief's talent for seeing what people hid, which Edric supposed was only natural, given what she carried. Given what she *took*.

"I have." He turned from the window. The candle guttered between them, throwing his shadow long against the wall. "Because once I tell you what I know, there's no walking away from it. There's no *surviving* walking away from it, if the hunters catch us. What I'm about to say is worth more than my life. It may be worth more than all of ours."

"Then say it," Isolde said quietly. "Please, Edric. I've spent three years suspecting what sits on my father's throne. I've had nothing but suspicion. Suspicion doesn't kill monsters."

"No," Edric agreed. "It doesn't." He crossed to the hearth and crouched beside it, because he couldn't say what he was about to say standing up, looking at her face. He needed to look at the fire instead. Fire was where this story began, and fire was where it would end, one way or another. "You know the memory Silas carries. My last day in the garden. The king dying among the roses."

"I know it bled into me," Silas said. "I know what I saw through your eyes. The king on the grass. The smoke-thing drinking his last breath. And then—" Her voice caught, just slightly. "And then you running. Leaving him there."

"Yes." He let the word sit. He would not defend himself; he had stopped defending himself years ago. "You've asked me, both of you, why we should believe the creature can be killed at all. You've asked what weapon exists against something that wears a king's face and commands a kingdom's swords. And I told you I'd answer when I trusted you enough to spend the knowledge. Well." He laughed, short and bitter. "I watched you tear a hunter's mind apart on the Lethe rather than let him take Isolde. I suppose trust comes late to me, but it does come."

He reached into the hearth and moved a log. Sparks rose. He watched them climb.

"When I was a boy," he said, "before I ever held shears or spade, my grandmother told me stories about the old kings. Not the dynasty that rules now—the ones before, the ones the histories call the Root-Kings. She said their power wasn't in armies. It was in a covenant. The land fed the crown, and the crown kept the land's oldest resident safe. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Isolde had gone very still. "There's something under the palace."

"There is something under the *garden*," Edric corrected. "The garden isn't decoration. It never was. Four hundred years ago, the Root-Kings built their palace around it like a fist closing around something precious. Every gardener who served the crown learned part of the truth, and each generation learned a little more than the last, passed hand to hand in the quiet hours, the way you pass down a cutting from an old rose. My master told me. His master told him. We are the keepers of the deepest beds." He looked up at them at last. "Beneath the royal garden, beneath six levels of root cellar and cistern and forgotten stair, sleeps the last dragon in the world."

Silas made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite disbelief. "A dragon."

"A dragon," Edric said. "Not the beasts of story, wings blackening the sky. Those died centuries ago, hunted for sport and fear. This one is older than all of them. Older than the kingdom. It slept beneath the earth before the first stone of the palace was laid, and the Root-Kings built around it the way a tree builds around a stone. Its fire never went out. And that fire—" he leaned forward, and the heat of the hearth pressed against his face, "—that fire is the only thing in creation that can unmake the creature wearing the king's face. Because they were born of the same darkness, the old texts said. Smoke and fire. Siblings. Only fire can judge smoke."

For a long moment, no one spoke. Somewhere below them, the chandler's boy was singing to himself as he trimmed wicks, a small bright sound in the gathering dark.

"That's why it took the garden," Isolde said slowly. Edric could see her mind working, the way it always did, turning a thing over until every facet caught light. "When it drank my father's breath—it didn't stay in the throne room. It went to the garden. Everyone said it was grief. The new king ordered the gardens closed. Sealed. No one tends them anymore."

"No one is *allowed* to tend them," Edric said. "I was dismissed nine years ago. Officially, for negligence—the blight that took the western beds. Unofficially, because I asked too many questions about why the deep wells had gone dry, why the soil above the old cisterns was warm to the touch in winter, why the roses nearest the sealed stair bloomed out of season, pale as bone." His hands curled into fists. "I knew what was happening. I'd known since my master's deathbed confession. The creature was feeding."

"Feeding," Silas repeated.

"On the fire." Edric stood, and his knees ached, and he barely felt it. "A dragon's fire renews itself, given time and sleep. But the creature has been sipping at it for nine years, the way a leech feeds—slowly, so slowly the wound never shows. Why do you think the false king closed the garden? Not grief. *Husbandry*. It keeps its food source close, and it starves the flame by inches so the fire can never gather strength enough to burn it. Every year, the ember dims. Every year, the creature grows more certain, more solid, more *real*. There are nights now when I dream of it and the dream feels less like a nightmare than a coronation."

"So we go to the dragon," Silas said. "We reach the fire before it dies, and we—what? Wake it? Aim it?"

"I don't know," Edric admitted. "My master's knowledge ended at the door. He told me how to find the way down—the gardener's path, the signs only our line knows—but no keeper in four hundred years has walked it, because no keeper ever needed to. The covenant held. Until it broke." He met Silas's eyes. "What I do know is this: whatever happens next must happen soon. My master said the fire speaks to the garden. That the plants above feel its warmth the way roots feel water. Nine years ago, the whole garden still hummed with it, faint as a heartbeat under a floor. When I left, the heartbeat was slowing. If it stops—if the last ember gutters out—then the creature becomes exactly what it has been pretending to be. Untouchable. Eternal. A king forever, and no fire left in the world to say otherwise."

Isolde rose. In the candlelight, with her father's eyes burning, she looked suddenly very much like the queen she might have become in a kinder world. "Then we leave for the capital tomorrow. Tonight, if the roads allow."

"The roads don't allow," Silas said dryly. "But tomorrow will do."

Edric almost smiled. Almost. Then the weight of what remained unsaid settled back onto him, and he sat heavily on the bench beside the hearth.

"There's more you should hear," he said. "The worst of it. Before you commit yourselves."

"We're past worst," Silas said. "Tell us anyway."

He nodded slowly. "The way down runs through the heart of the garden itself. The gardener's path begins at the sundial—there's a mechanism in the base, a lock keyed to a living plant, a rose my line has kept alive for four hundred years in secret cuttings. I have one. I've carried it in a clay pot through nine years of exile like a fool carrying a candle through a storm." He touched his chest, where, wrapped in oilcloth inside his pack, the little cutting waited, two leaves and a stubborn green stem. "But the path opens only for a keeper's hands. Mine. Which means everything depends on me—on a disgraced old man whose hands shake in cold weather—and on a rose the size of a thumb. And even if we reach the dragon, even if the fire still burns, I don't know what waking it requires. I don't know if it will see us as heirs of the covenant or as trespassers come to finish what the creature started."

"Wonderful," Silas murmured. But she didn't look afraid. She looked, Edric thought, like someone doing arithmetic—weighing debts, counting exits. A thief to the bone.

"And the city," Isolde added grimly. "You haven't mentioned the city. My—the false king announced a festival. The Festival of the Hunt's Triumph. Celebrating the purging of the gifted. It begins in eleven days. The capital will be full to bursting. Hunters on every corner. Bonfires built in the squares." Her jaw tightened. "Bonfires, Edric. They'll be burning people while we sneak beneath their feet."

"Then we'd best not sneak," Edric said. "We'd best *move*."

* * *

They left Vess before dawn, three travelers among dozens on the north road, hooded and unremarkable. Edric carried his pack with the clay pot cradled inside like an infant, and every jolt of the cart he'd hired made him wince.

It was on the third evening, camped in a hollow of birches with rain drumming the leaves, that the doubt finally found him alone. Silas had gone to scout the road ahead; Isolde slept fitfully by the banked fire. Edric sat with the pot in his lap, unwrapped the oilcloth, and looked at the cutting.

Two leaves. One stem. Four hundred years of covenant balanced on something he could crush between finger and thumb.

"Talk to it," his master had told him, forty years ago, kneeling in the warm dark of the deep cellars. "The garden remembers every hand that ever tended it, lad. Roots longer than memory. You think you serve plants. You serve the *land*, and the land serves the fire, and the fire—" the old man had smiled then, gap-toothed and certain, "—the fire remembers everything."

Edric had knelt in the dirt of a hundred gardens since, and never once felt anything but soil. But tonight, in the rain, with the ember of the world guttering somewhere far beneath a sealed gate, he cupped his palm around the little rose and whispered to it.

"Hold on," he said. "Just hold on. We're coming."

The leaves trembled in the wind. Or perhaps they didn't. He could no longer tell the difference between faith and foolishness, and he suspected that was the condition of every keeper who had ever lived.

Silas returned near midnight, dropping silently into the hollow. She studied him for a moment—the pot, his face—with those eyes that saw too much.

"It's real, isn't it," she said quietly. "All of it. You're not certain, but you *believe*. I can tell. Belief looks different in a person than certainty. It looks like someone standing on ice and listening for cracking."

"Poetic, for a memory-thief."

"I steal other people's poetry along with everything else." She crouched by the fire, feeding it a stick. "Here's what I can't stop thinking about. The creature has been feeding on that fire for nine years. Slowly. Carefully. You said yourself—it's husbandry. So ask yourself: why hasn't it finished? Why sip for nine years when it could drink the ember dry in a night?"

Edric frowned. "Caution. Fear of waking it."

"Maybe." She stared into the flames. "Or maybe it *can't*. Maybe the fire can't be taken—only given. Maybe that's why it wears the king's face and holds festivals and burns the gifted. It's not just hunting magic. It's hunting for something. A key. A way to make the fire surrender willingly." She looked up at him, and in the firelight her eyes were old and tired and sharp as a spade's edge. "And if that's true, Edric, then every day we travel toward the garden, we're also traveling toward the thing that wants the garden most. We're not sneaking up on it. We're delivering ourselves."

The rain filled the silence between them, patient and gray.

"Then we go faster," Edric said at last.

Silas huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You sound like Isolde."

"One of us has to." He wrapped the oilcloth back around the pot, gentle as bandaging a wound. "Get some sleep, Kavanagh. Tomorrow we cross the Marrow Hills, and after that, the capital. After that—" he lay back, staring up at the birch canopy swaying against the stars, "—after that, we find out whether an old man, a thief, a runaway princess, and a rose the size of a thumb can wake a dying god of fire before the world's last ember goes out."

From across the hollow, Isolde's voice drifted, thick with sleep but steady: "We will."

Neither of them answered her. Neither of them believed in promises anymore. But Edric found, to his surprise, that he slept without dreaming of smoke—and woke before dawn to Silas shaking his shoulder, her face pale in the pre-light.

"Riders on the ridge," she whispered. "Six of them. Hunter's cloaks. And Edric—" her voice dropped lower, urgent, "—they're not searching the road. They're searching *us*. They rode straight past two caravans without stopping." She swallowed. "They know we're here. Something's leading them."

In Edric's pack, wrapped in oilcloth, the little rose cutting had opened a single bud overnight—pale as bone, blooming impossibly out of season, pointing north like a compass needle toward the garden and everything waiting there.
