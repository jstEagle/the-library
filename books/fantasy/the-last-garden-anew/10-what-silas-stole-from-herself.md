## 10. What Silas Stole from Herself

Isolde found the hollow in the third hour of the watch, when she had given up pretending to sleep and begun, instead, to study her companions the way her tutors had once taught her to study courtiers: by what they guarded.

Edric slept like a man who had made peace with his own failures, curled around his pack with the strange white rose cutting cradled against his chest as though it were an infant. He snored softly. His hands, even in dreams, kept moving—pruning some invisible hedge, tending some invisible soil.

Silas did not sleep at all.

She sat apart from the fire's reach, at the mouth of the ruined shepherd's hut they had claimed for the night, and she was doing something with her hands that Isolde could not quite make sense of in the dark. Small movements. Precise. The motion of a woman counting coins, or shelling nuts, or—

Or opening something that should not be opened.

Isolde rose without sound. She had learned to move quietly in a palace full of listening walls, and the habit had saved her life more than once since Vess. She crossed the frost-brittle grass, keeping to the shadow of the hut's broken wall, and it was only when she was close enough to see the firelight catch on Silas's fingers that she understood what she was looking at.

Vials. A dozen of them, maybe more, each no longer than a thumb, each stoppered with wax and sealed with a twist of thread. They lay spread across a square of oilcloth like a merchant's wares, and they caught the moonlight and held it, glowing faintly from within—not with reflected light but with their own, a soft luminescence like foxfire or deep water. And inside each one moved something that was not liquid. Something that *coiled*. That pressed against the glass like a moth against a window.

Memories. Isolde had heard of them the way one hears of anything forbidden—rumors, half-truths, the kind of stories nursemaids told to frighten children away from the old magic. She had never believed they could be *held*, that a life could be poured out of a skull and stoppered like wine.

"You're awake," Silas said, without turning. Of course she had heard. She heard everything; Isolde had noticed that about her weeks ago, though she'd attributed it to a thief's caution rather than whatever this was. "You were dreaming about your father's library. You do that most nights. You always stop at the same shelf."

The words landed like cold water. "How do you know what I dream?"

"Because you talk in your sleep." Silas's voice was flat, careful, a door pulled shut. "Everyone does." She began sweeping the vials back into their cloth with quick, practiced motions—the motions of concealment, of a lifetime's concealment—and something in the gesture, in its very fluency, made Isolde angrier than any confession could have.

"No," Isolde said. "That's not it. You *took* it. From me. While I slept, some night on the road, you reached into my head and—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things demanded response, and every response available to her frightened her. "What else? What else have you taken from me?"

"Nothing that matters."

"Nothing that—" Isolde laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and brittle in the dark. "My dreams don't matter? My *father* doesn't matter? Do you know what I've been doing these past weeks, Silas? Trusting you. Sleeping beside you. Telling Edric things I haven't told anyone in three years, while you sat there with a head full of my—my *insides*—"

"Keep your voice down."

"Don't tell me to keep my voice down!"

Edric stirred, muttered, turned over. Neither of them breathed until he settled again. The white bud on the rose cutting seemed to glow faintly in the darkness between them.

Silas looked up then, and Isolde saw her face properly for the first time—saw that beneath the anger there was something worse than guilt. There was *weariness*. The bone-deep exhaustion of someone who has been caught at last, after so long running, that being caught is almost a relief.

"You want to know what I've taken?" Silas said quietly. "Fine. Sit down, princess. Because if we're going to do this, we're going to do it properly, and you're going to wish to every god your false father prays to that you hadn't asked."

* * *

She laid them out one by one, and she named them, and her voice never shook, which somehow made it all worse.

"The ferryman at Coldbridge. Three memories—a drowned son, a first love, a debt he couldn't pay. Sold them to Maren for eleven silver. He cried for two days afterward and didn't know why." A vial, lifted, set aside. "A soldier at Thornfell gate. The taste of his mother's bread. Just that. Just the taste. He sold it to buy passage south, and I bought it from Maren later because I was hungry for something warm, and I carried it for a year before I sold it again." Another vial. "A midwife in Harrow Vale. Her whole apprenticeship—every birth she ever attended. She wanted to forget the ones that went wrong. I took the good ones too, because the good ones paid better."

"Stop," Isolde said.

"You asked."

"I asked what you took from *me*."

"And I'm telling you what I am," Silas said, "so you understand what it means when I answer that question. Which is this:" She picked up the last three vials—all of them older than the others; Isolde could see it in the wax, yellowed and cracked, in the thread gone gray—and held them in her palm like something holy and horrible. "These are mine. These are the ones I took from myself."

The wind moved through the ruined hut. Somewhere downslope, a fox screamed and was silenced.

"My mother's face," Silas said. "I don't remember my mother's face. I remember that I had a mother. I remember that people say I have her eyes, her hands, the shape of her chin—but I can't see her. When I try, there's just—smoothness. A wall where a face should be." Her thumb traced the oldest vial, the one whose seal had been broken and rewaxed so many times the wax had built up in layers, like tree rings. "I was fourteen. We were starving. A broker in Thornfell paid thirty silver for it, and I told myself I could always take it back someday, and then I discovered that taking it back costs five times what selling it did, and then I stopped thinking about it at all, because thinking about it felt like pressing on a bruise."

"Why would you sell your own mother's face?"

"Because grief is heavy," Silas said simply, "and hunger is heavier. Because she died badly, and remembering how she died badly was killing me faster than the hunger was. Because I was fourteen and clever and alone, and I had just learned what my gift was, and the first thing I learned is that you can cut pieces out of yourself and other people will pay you for them." She set the vial down with terrible gentleness. "This one is my childhood home. The street, the door, the smell of the stairwell. I sold that the following winter. And this—" the last vial, the smallest, "—I don't actually know what this one is anymore. I sealed it during a fever, years ago. I've never dared open it. It might be nothing. It might be everything."

Isolde stared at her. At the little glowing shapes arranged on the oilcloth, each one a piece of a person, each one traded away like turnips. And she waited—for revulsion to come, for fear, for the cold clarity of judgment that had sustained her through three years of hiding, the certainty that the world divided cleanly into those who took and those who were taken from.

It didn't come. Instead she heard herself ask: "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you deserve to know what's sleeping next to you. And because—" Silas's composure flickered, the first true crack, "—because you asked what I'd taken from you, and the honest answer is that I don't fully know. Fragments, when you dream. Nothing whole, nothing you'd miss. But I've been carrying your father's court in my head since Vess, and your nightmares, and the way you said your mother's name once in your sleep, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't take things because I'm cruel, Isolde. I take things because taking is the only thing I've ever been good at, and because every time I lift a memory out of someone's mind, I get to feel what it's like to be someone whose worst day isn't today. That's the truth of it. That's the ugly, small, pathetic truth. I steal other lives because I can't stand inside my own."

"And now?"

"Now?" Silas looked at the vials. All of them. The stolen years of strangers, and the stolen years of herself, glowing together like a handful of captured stars. "Now I'm running from witch-hunters with a dead king's death rattling around in my skull, and I realized somewhere on the Lethe road that I couldn't tell you why. Not the surface reasons—those are easy, the hunters, the memory, the reward on our heads. I mean the *real* why. The reason underneath. I sat down tonight to look for it, and I went digging through everything I am, and do you know what I found?" Her laugh was terrible and quiet. "Holes. Holes everywhere. I've sold off so much of myself that I don't remember what I'm running *from* anymore. I just remember that I'm running. I just know that stopping has always meant dying, the way you know fire is hot—not because you remember learning it, but because it's written into you deeper than memory."

The confession hung in the air between them, and Isolde understood suddenly, with a lurch, that she was looking at a mirror. Here was a woman who had carved away her own past to survive, piece by piece, price by price—and Isolde had done the same thing with a knife of a different temper. She had cut the palace out of herself. She had cut her father out of herself, or tried to. She had spent three years becoming a hole shaped like a princess, and she had told herself it was wisdom.

"I judged you," Isolde said slowly. "In the caravan, when we sold those memories to the broker. I stood there and judged you, and I want you to know—" She knelt, so that they were level, so that Silas could not retreat behind the height difference or the darkness. "I grew up watching my father feed people to a lie. Every day. Smiling. And I told myself the creature wearing him is the monster, and it is, it truly is—but Silas, I watched *courtiers* do what you do. Trade in pieces of people. Flatter and extract and consume. They just used words instead of gifts. They were worse, because they enjoyed it."

"And I don't enjoy it?"

"Do you?"

Silas was silent for a long moment. The fire had burned down to coals; the light moved on her face like water. "No," she said finally, and it came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. "Gods help me. No. I hate it. I hate every single time. I just don't know how to be anything else."

"Then learn," Isolde said, and heard the fierceness rise in her own voice, unexpected and certain. "Listen to me. Whatever you think you are—whatever you sold yourself into believing—you carried Edric's memory to him when you could have sold it for more coin than either of us will see in a lifetime. You tore a hunter's mind apart on the river to save me, and you've flinched from it every day since, because it *cost* you, because you felt what you took. Thieves don't flinch, Silas. Thieves don't lose sleep over strangers' mothers' bread." She reached out and closed Silas's fingers over the oldest vial, the mother's face, pressing it back into her palm. "You keep this. You carry it across the whole cursed kingdom, through hunters and festivals and gods-know-what waits under that garden. You don't sell it. And when this is over—if we live, if there's an *after*—you buy it back. Whatever it costs. Whatever you have to become to afford it. Promise me."

"Why?" Silas whispered. "Why does it matter to you?"

"Because I lost a father to something that consumes," Isolde said. "And I will not stand by and watch a friend feed herself to the same hunger, one memory at a time."

The word *friend* fell between them, and neither of them picked it up, and neither of them brushed it away. It simply stayed there, glowing faintly, like the vials.

Silas's hand closed tight around the sealed memory of her mother's face. "There's something else," she said at last, so quietly Isolde had to lean close. "Something I found tonight, digging through the holes. The courier's memory—the king's death—I've been carrying it wrong. Or it's been carrying me. There are layers in it I didn't put there. Layers I don't remember *taking*." Her eyes came up, and in the coal-light Isolde saw genuine fear in them for the first time. "Isolde, some of these memories aren't all mine. Someone has been putting things *into* me. Things I was meant to find only when it was too late to run."

Before Isolde could answer, a sound broke across the hillside—the snap of a branch, deliberate, too heavy for a fox. Edric sat bolt upright from his sleep, the white rose clutched to his chest, its single pale bud now open wide and trembling.

Down the slope, at the edge of the firelight, stood six figures in hunter's cloaks. And the one at their center lowered her hood, and Isolde's blood turned to ice, for she knew that face—it was the face of the king's chief huntsman, the man who had taught her to ride, smiling now with teeth that did not smile the way teeth should.

"Lady Isolde," he said pleasantly. "Your father sends his love."
