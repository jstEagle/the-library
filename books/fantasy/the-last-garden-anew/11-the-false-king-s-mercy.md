## 11. The False King's Mercy

They took her memories first.

Not all of them—the guards were not gifted, only cruel and thorough—but the small ones, the practical ones. The route from Vess to the capital. The name of the innkeeper in Lowmarsh who had looked the other way for coin. The face of the hunter captain whose men had dragged her from the Lethe crossing with a hood over her head and iron cuffs that bit cold as river water. She felt each one lifted like a stone pulled from a wall, leaving gaps she could trace with a fingertip pressed against the inside of her skull.

That was the worst of it—not the loss, but the *familiarity*. She knew exactly what they were doing to her because she had done it to others, gently, a hundred times. There was no gentleness here. They dug with hooks.

The palace cells smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and she was left alone long enough to count the drips along the ceiling—forty-one between echoes of her own breathing—and to understand that the waiting was itself a weapon. Whoever sat on the throne above her did not need to torture her. He only needed to let her imagination do the work, and hers was well-fed.

She thought of Isolde's face when they'd taken her—spitting fury, chin high, hair torn loose from its braid. She thought of Edric going quietly, so quietly it frightened her more than any struggle would have, an old man folding into himself like a coat put away for winter. And beneath both thoughts, quieter than either, she thought: *I should never have let them close enough to be taken.*

That was the rule. That had always been the rule. Take what you need, keep your hands clean of hearts, walk away before anyone can become a weight you carry. She had broken every clause of her own contract in the space of one river crossing, when she'd knelt in the shallows with a hunter's mind open under her fingers and torn it apart to save a princess she'd known for three weeks.

And now here she was, in the dark, carrying the debt.

* * *

They came for her at what she guessed was dawn, though the palace kept no honest hours. Two guards, a blindfold, a corridor of marble that rang differently under her shoes—vast, then narrow, then vast again—and finally warmth on her face like sun through glass, which made no sense until they pulled the cloth away and she saw where she stood.

A conservatory. Of all things, a conservatory.

It crowned the highest tower of the royal palace, a dome of white iron ribs and glass panes, filled with plants she half-recognized from Edric's memory and half-recognized from nowhere at all—flowers with petals like folded paper lanterns, vines that climbed trellises of silver, a central bed of black soil so rich it seemed to breathe. Through the glass, the capital spread below in morning haze, rooftops and spires and the distant glitter of the festival preparations, banners already hung for a celebration of victory over people like her.

And at the center of it all, standing with his back to her among the lantern-flowers, was the king.

He was smaller than his portraits. Everyone was smaller than their portraits; she had learned that much from stolen memories of coronations and market-day parades. But there was something else wrong with him that portraits could not account for—a stillness, a quality of being *placed* rather than standing, as if he were a very good rendering of a king rendered by someone who had seen one only briefly, from a distance, through fog.

"You are the thief," he said, without turning. His voice was pleasant. That was the second wrongness. It was pleasant the way a fire is warm—it gave nothing away about what burned inside it. "Silas Kavanagh. Born in Harrow Fen, though you don't remember it. Sold your first memory at eleven. Your gift is old blood—older than my hunters know how to name." He turned, and his face was the king's face, weathered and kind and lined at the eyes with the suggestion of humor. "Do you know what I admire most about you, child?"

"I imagine you'll tell me."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes—not anger. Interest. A collector finding a specimen in better condition than advertised. "You never took a memory you didn't need," he said. "You could have been rich beyond counting. You could have lived in Thornfell like a little queen, buying loyalty by the vial. Instead you took scraps. Passages. Names of safe roads. Enough to eat, enough to hide. Do you know why?"

"Because greedy thieves get caught."

"Because some part of you understood what I understand," the king said, drifting closer, and she made herself hold still as he circled her, slow, studying her profile the way Maren used to study a doubtful vial held up to candlelight. "That a memory is the truest thing a person owns. More true than land. More true than gold. More true than love, which fades, or flesh, which fails. And that to take one wrongly—to take one carelessly—is a desecration." He stopped before her. Up close, she could smell him: smoke and honey and underneath it something cold, mineral, like the air in a cave that has been sealed for centuries. "You have committed many desecrations, Silas Kavanagh. But never out of greed. Only out of fear. I find that almost forgivable."

"You've read my file."

"I've read everything the hunters wrote, and everything they failed to write." He smiled, and the smile fit his face perfectly and meant absolutely nothing. "But we are not here to discuss your crimes. We're here to discuss your *fortune*."

* * *

He led her along the glass aisle, and she noticed—because noticing was the one skill no one could confiscate—that the garden was dying.

Subtly. Beautifully. The lantern-flowers still bloomed, but their light had gone the color of old ivory. The silver trellises were tarnished green. The black soil at the center was cracked in patterns like dried mud, and around those cracks the gardeners' mulch lay thick and fresh, hiding something. Edric's memory stirred in her unbidden: *the roots go down further than any root has a right to go, girl, and what they drink isn't rain.*

"The false king keeps a garden," she said. "Isn't that ironic? Or do you not know the word anymore?"

The creature wearing the king's face laughed. It was a good laugh. Warm. It raised the hairs on her arms anyway. "Edric Hale told you stories. Of course he did. The gardener always loved his fables—dragons under the flagstones, monsters in the nursery. A man who spends forty years talking to plants will believe anything breathes." He plucked a dead lantern-flower and turned it between his fingers. "Here is what is true, Silas. Here is what I offer freely, as a courtesy between professionals."

He held the dead flower up between them like a lens.

"You have spent your life stealing fragments. A road here. A face there. And all this time, you have been robbing yourself to pay for the journey. You took your mother's face from yourself when you were fourteen years old, because grief is heavy and you wanted to travel light. You took your childhood home. You took the reason you ran from Harrow Fen—you carry the running, but not the cause. You are a woman built entirely of exits, and you cannot remember a single door you fled." His voice softened, and the softness was worse than any threat. "You woke yesterday in chains wondering why you fight. Wondering who these people are to you, this gardener and this girl, and why your chest hurts when you think of them in cells. Because you have amputated the parts of yourself that might have answered."

Her throat was dry. "You've been reading my file again."

"I can give them back."

The words dropped into the conservatory's hush like a coin into a well, and she heard them falling for a long time.

"All of them," the king said. "Every memory you ever stole from another person—I will draw them out of you whole and return them to their owners, restored, as an act of royal mercy. And every memory you stole from *yourself*—" he leaned closer, and his breath smelled of honey and cold stone, "—I will give back to you. Your mother's face, Silas. Her voice. Her hands kneading bread in the kitchen at Harrow Fen while the snow came down past the windows. The way she said your name. You have carried the shape of the wound for ten years without remembering the knife. I can hand you the knife. I can hand you the whole morning, the last morning, the one you cut out of your own head because living without it hurt less than living with it."

"Don't," Silas said. Her voice came out strange. Small. "Don't describe her. You haven't earned her."

"I don't need to earn her. I only need to find her." He straightened. "Your gift works by touch, yes? Mind to mind. Mine is older, and larger, and it does not require consent. Every memory you have ever taken still lives in you like a splinter—I can feel them from here, a constellation of other people's lives wrapped in your skin. I can take them all in an hour. I can leave you *clean*."

"And what does it cost."

"Ah." He beamed at her, and for a moment—for one horrible, vertiginous moment—he looked genuinely delighted, a scholar watching a student ask precisely the right question. "There she is. The professional. Yes. There is a price, and it is modest." He gestured toward the center of the conservatory, toward the cracked black soil and the fresh mulch laid over it like bandages. "Somewhere beneath us sleeps the last ember of the old world. The hunters call it dragon-fire. My predecessor called it a curse. I call it *dinner*—or I would, if I could reach it. It hides behind locks of growing things, behind roots that remember every loyal hand that ever tended them, and it will open for no one alive." His eyes came back to hers, and now the pleasantness fell away entirely, and what looked out of the king's face was old, and patient, and starving. "Except perhaps for a thief with a gardener's memory in her head and a gardener's friendship in her pocket. Bring me to it, Silas. Open the way. In exchange: your freedom, your friends' freedom, and everything you ever lost, returned with interest."

* * *

She made herself look away from him. She made herself look at the dying garden instead, because the garden was telling the truth even when the king was not.

Cracked soil. Tarnished silver. Lanterns burning down to ivory. Whatever fed this place was guttering, just as Edric had said—just as the courier's stolen memory had whispered with his dying breath—and the thing in the king's shape had been drinking it for years, sip by sip, keeping the flame low enough to control and bright enough to live on. If she opened the way, she wouldn't be handing him a meal. She'd be handing him the last meal, and when it was gone, nothing in the world would be able to kill him. Not dragon-fire. Not anything.

But her mother's face.

Gods help her, her mother's face.

She had built her whole self around that absence the way a tree builds around a fence wire swallowed by bark. She knew the absence the way you know a missing tooth—with the tongue of the mind, constantly, helplessly returning to the gap. Ten years of thieving, and the one thing she had never sold, never traded, never shown to a broker, was the memory she'd cut out of her own head at fourteen, sobbing in a hedgerow with her own hands shaking against her temples, because her mother was dying and the grief was going to kill her too and she could not—could *not*—

"What was her name?" the king asked softly.

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Almost nothing that matters is." He moved closer, and his shadow fell over her, and it was wrong, the shadow, longer than his body and thinner, like smoke stretched across the glass floor. "Think of Isolde. Think of the gardener. Think honestly, thief—they are strangers you met on a road. Three weeks. Less. You are prepared to die in a cell for three weeks of acquaintance, when for a single touch I will return to you the woman whose face you mourned without ever being able to picture. Whose death you have been running from since you were a child. Who loved you." His voice dropped to a whisper that was almost tender. "Who said your name. Don't you want to hear it again? Just once? Just once more?"

Silas closed her eyes.

And there it was—the seduction working on her exactly as designed, because the terrible truth was that the creature wasn't lying. She could feel it, the way she felt the seams in other people's minds: this thing *could* do what it promised. It drank memories the way she sipped them, and it had drunk kings and kingdoms and centuries, and somewhere in that vast cold hoard it could surely find—or fashion, or restore—her mother. The gap in her would fill. The tooth would regrow. She would stand in a kitchen in Harrow Fen with snow coming down past the windows and hear her name said in a voice she had spent ten years forgetting how much she missed.

And Isolde would rot in a cell below her feet. And Edric, who had looked at his own stolen memories in her eyes and chosen, after all his rage, to help her—Edric would die in the dark believing the garden had been for nothing.

"Here is what I know about taking," Silas said, opening her eyes. "Ten years of it. And here is what I learned, late, badly, on a riverbank with a man's mind screaming under my hands." Her heart was pounding. Her voice was steady. "The thing that takes never gets to keep. It fills itself and stays empty. You've eaten a king, and you're hungry. You've eaten his kingdom, and you're hungry. You'll eat the last fire in the world and still be hungry, because hunger is all you are." She stepped back from him, out of his shadow. "My mother's face isn't for sale. It isn't for *trade*. It's mine—even the losing of it is mine. I paid for that gap in myself, and I'm not selling the scar."

The king regarded her for a long moment.

Then he sighed, and shook his head, and the disappointment on his borrowed face was so human, so genuinely sorrowful, that it chilled her worse than rage would have. "I hoped you'd be wise," he said. "Wisdom is so rare in my kingdom these days. My hunters burn it weekly." He turned away from her, plucked another dead lantern-flower, and spoke to it rather than to her. "Take her down. Give her time to reconsider. Grief makes people flexible, given enough hours in the dark—and I have so many hours, Silas. I have had years of hours." Over his shoulder, almost kindly: "The offer stands until the ember dies. After that, there will be nothing left to bargain with. For any of us."

The guards took her arms. She didn't resist. Resistance was for people with a plan, and right now all she had was a refusal and a hollow chest, and neither of those would open a single lock.

But as they led her toward the stair, past the cracked black soil and the fresh-laid mulch, she let her fingertips brush the railing beside the central bed—one light, careless touch, the touch of a woman steadying herself.

And through the iron, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, she felt it.

Warmth. A thread of it, deep down, banked and buried and nearly gone—but *alive*, and aware, and turning its attention to her fingers with the slow, immense curiosity of something that had slept for centuries beneath a liar's garden.

It recognized what she was.

Down in the dark, under the dungeons where her friends waited, the last fire in the world remembered thieves fondly.

The stairs swallowed her, and above her the false king stood alone among his dying flowers, smiling at nothing, and Silas went down into the stone carrying two secrets now instead of one—and the second one was burning a hole straight through her like a coal cupped in bare hands.
