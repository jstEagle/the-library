## 12. Chains in the Dark

Edric Hale had spent thirty years learning the language of roots—the slow grammar of things that grew in darkness and pushed toward light without ever having seen it. He had thought this made him patient. He had thought it made him strong.

Three days in the palace dungeons taught him otherwise.

The cell was cut from the same grey granite as the rest of the old keep, but the masons who built the lower levels had worked without windows in mind, without any thought of green or growing things, and Edric felt the absence like a missing limb. No moss in the mortar seams. No lichen. Not even the pale, stubborn fungus that found its way into every cellar he'd ever known. The walls were dead stone, scrubbed clean of life, and he understood now that this was deliberate—that whatever ruled above them could not abide even the smallest thing that remembered how to grow.

He sat with his back against that dead stone and his hands open in his lap, palms up, the way he used to sit in the seed-house on winter nights when the work was done and the waiting was all there was. His wrists were raw where the iron had rubbed them. The chains were long enough to let him reach the water bucket and the slop pail and nothing else—a cruelty of exact measurement, a warden's arithmetic.

Across the corridor, Isolde's cell was dark. He heard her sometimes, a low murmur, pacing her four paces like a wolf counting fence-lines. And beyond her, at the far end where the torchlight failed entirely, Silas was silent in a way that worried him more than any sound.

*She's negotiating,* Isolde had whispered through the bars on the first night. *The creature wants what's in her head. She's making it think she might give.*

But Silas had said nothing to Edric. She had been dragged past his cell in manacles, and she had looked at him—just looked, one long gray glance—and something in that look had told him: *wait*. So he waited, and hated himself for how easily waiting came back to him, how naturally his body folded itself into endurance, as if thirty years of exile had been nothing but rehearsal for this.

The problem was not the stone. The problem was that he had come home.

He closed his eyes and the palace rose around him anyway, complete and merciless. He knew these dungeons. He had walked this corridor a hundred times, carrying cuttings down to the king—not the false king, the true one, the good one, Aldous the Gardener-King they'd called him behind his back with love instead of mockery—because Aldous believed that growing things belonged everywhere in his house, even in the dark, even among prisoners. There had been a garden down here once. A small one. Edric had built it himself, in the old days, in the years before the ash.

A mushroom bed in the second chamber. A trough of medicinal herbs by the infirmary grate—comfrey and yarrow and feverfew, tended by lamplight for prisoners whose wounds festered in this damp. He had planted a single rowan sapling beneath the one grating that opened to the sky, a slot of blue no wider than a hand, because an old gardener had once told him that rowans kept off evil, and the king had laughed and said, "Then plant three."

Edric opened his eyes. He turned his head slowly, stiffly, until his cheek rested against the cold wall, and he looked down the corridor toward the place where the herb trough had stood.

It was gone. Of course it was gone. But the bracket that had held the watering can was still bolted to the stone, rusted orange, exactly where he had hung it every evening for eleven years.

They hadn't taken everything. That was the thought that crept into him, small as a seed, unwelcome as a weed, and would not be pulled out again. They had not taken everything.

* * *

On the fourth day, the jailer came.

Edric heard him before he saw him—the particular heavy tread, the jingle of keys worn smooth by decades of the same hand. Brannock. Even after twenty years, Edric knew the man's walk the way he knew the creak of his own garden gate. Brannock had served under Aldous too; he had been young then, a broad-shouldered farm boy from the Weald with a laugh you could hear through three floors of stone. He had once carried a sack of Edric's spring bulbs down these stairs on his back, complaining magnificently the whole way, and then planted half of them crooked just to spite the gardener's fussing, and the two of them had argued about tulip depth while the king watched from the stairs and laughed until he wept.

The man who rounded the corner was not that man. He moved like furniture. His face had gone slack and gray, and his eyes—when they passed over Edric—did not stop. They slid across him the way rain slides off oiled leather, touching nothing, holding nothing.

"Prisoner," Brannock said, and set down the food tray through the slot without looking at it.

Edric rose. The chains dragged. He came as close to the bars as the iron allowed and gripped them, and the cold of them bit his ruined wrists, and he did not care.

"Brannock."

Nothing. The jailer turned away.

"You planted my tulips crooked," Edric said loudly, "in the year of the great frost, and I told you they'd never bloom facing north, and every one of them came up blind, and you swore me to secrecy so the king wouldn't hear of it."

Brannock stopped.

For a heartbeat—one long, terrible heartbeat—he simply stood there in the torchlight with his back to the cell, a mountain of flesh that had forgotten it was ever anything else. Then his head turned, slowly, the way a door turns on a hinge no one has oiled in years, and he looked at Edric Hale, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish moving under ice. Far down. Briefly visible. Gone.

"I don't know you," Brannock said. His voice was flat as slate. But it took him too long to say it, and his hand had risen halfway toward his own chest before falling, as if it remembered some gesture the mind had mislaid.

"No," Edric said softly. "I don't suppose you do."

The jailer went. Edric sank back down against the wall, and his heart was hammering, and he pressed both palms flat against the dead stone as if he could take its pulse.

Something had been done to Brannock. Something had reached into that honest farmer's skull and scooped out the years—the bulbs, the laughter, the king on the stairs—and left only duty behind, hollow duty, duty as a shell. It was what the creature did. It was what Silas had described, in her flat thief's cadence, on the road: *it eats what people are and wears what's left.*

And yet. The fish under the ice. Halfway to his own heart.

Things buried were not always dead. Any gardener knew that. Seeds slept seven years, ten, forty, in ground everyone had given up as barren—and then a fire came, and cracked their casings, and they woke.

* * *

That night—for night was a guess here, marked by the dimming of the corridor torches and the change in the distant festival-drums above—Silas spoke to him for the first time since the cells.

Her voice came low along the stone, barely more than breath shaped into words. She had worked herself to the corner of her cell nearest his, and though he could see nothing in the dark, he could feel her presence the way he felt weather coming.

"Gardener." She never called him Edric. "You knew the jailer."

"Twenty years ago I knew him."

"He doesn't know himself. They've stripped him. Mostly." A pause. "There's a girl in what's left of him. A daughter. He holds onto her like a drowning man holds a plank—it's the last thing they couldn't pry loose, or didn't bother to. Everything else is rotted through. I've been reading him for two days whenever he comes near. He's full of holes." Her voice tightened with something that might have been guilt, though she would have denied it under torture. "I know the guards' rotations. I know the gate mechanisms. And I know how to get into the garden."

Edric's breath caught. "The garden. The true garden?"

"The way down. Under the greenhouse foundations. There's a stair the jailer has sealed and half-forgotten—his father sealed it before him. It opens from the old cistern gallery, two levels up, past the flood-gate wheel." She recited it like a litany she'd stolen fair and square, which, he reflected, she had. "The wheel's seized. Rusted. Nobody's touched it in twenty years."

"It'll move," Edric said.

Silence. Then, dry as winter straw: "You sound sure."

"I oiled that wheel myself every autumn for eleven years. Before—" His voice betrayed him, cracked down the middle. He mastered it. "Before the ash. If the rust is surface-deep, and it will be, because I taught Brannock's father to grease the housing properly, it will move for a strong arm and a long lever. And I am," he added, with a bitterness that surprised even himself, "very well acquainted with long levers."

Another silence. When Silas spoke again, something in her tone had shifted—wariness giving way, grudgingly, to something almost like respect.

"They broke you when they brought you in. Both of us saw it. You looked at these walls like a man looking at his own grave."

"I did."

"What changed?"

Edric leaned his head back against the granite and looked up into blackness, and told her the truth, because in the dark there was no one to perform for and no strength left for lying.

"There was a garden down here once. My garden. Herbs for the sick, mushrooms for the kitchens, a rowan tree under the sky-slot. The king wanted it—he said a prisoner who could smell growing things stayed human longer. I built it with my own hands. And when the creature came, when everything above burned, I assumed this died too. Dead stone, dead air, dead memories." His hand found the wall seam beside him, the old mason's joint, familiar as his mother's voice. "But tonight I found the bracket where my watering can hung. Still bolted. Still rusted in the shape of the ring. Nothing in this palace dies all the way, Silas. Not the gardens. Not the jailer. Maybe not even the thing we're trying to kill—though that's a different lesson, and a darker one." He exhaled slowly. "I spent twenty years telling myself I ran from the palace because I was a coward. That I abandoned my king, my work, everything, and deserved the exile I got. Sitting here, I finally understand I didn't run from failure. I ran from grief. And grief keeps, Silas. Like a root. All this time underground, in the dark, doing nothing but waiting—and it's still alive. Which means so am I."

From the corner of the darkness came a long quiet. When Silas answered, her voice was strange, thinned by something she was trying to keep out of it.

"My mother had a saying about seeds like that. I don't remember her face anymore—I sold it, gave it away, don't ask—but I remember her sayings. Isn't that a mad thing? The face goes and the words stay." A breath. "She said: *what you bury grieving grows deepest.*"

Edric smiled in the dark, and it hurt his face, because his face had nearly forgotten how.

"She sounds like she'd have made a fine gardener."

"She'd have made a terrible one. She had no patience for things that took longer than a season." But there was warmth under the words, guarded and sudden, like sun through a shutter. Then it was gone, banked, and the thief's pragmatism returned. "Listen. Tomorrow at the changing of the mid-watch, Brannock does his rounds alone. The other guards go up for the count. I'm going to talk to him—really talk to him, get close enough to read the seal-mechanism out of what's left of his memory, maybe take the keys' hiding place while I'm at it. He won't know. He never knows. But Edric—" and here she paused, and he heard her choosing her words with a pickpocket's care, "when the moment comes, I may need you to hold his attention. Talk to him. About the old days. About anything. The deeper he digs into what's buried, the easier it is for me to reach the parts underneath."

"You want me to make him remember."

"I want you to make him *feel*. Can you do that? Without breaking him? Because if he breaks, he shouts, and if he shouts we hang from the festival scaffolds by sundown."

Edric was silent a long time. In the blackness he saw the young jailer's face, sun-brown and laughing, a sack of bulbs on his back; he saw the older face, gray and hollowed, the fish flickering under the ice. To stir those waters gently, gently, knowing what storms might wake—to tend a mind the way he'd once tended beds, pruning without wounding, coaxing without forcing—

It was, he realized, the only kind of gardening left to him. And perhaps the most important.

"Yes," he said. "I can do that."

* * *

The mid-watch came, and with it the receding tramp of boots on the stair, and then the great hush of the dungeons, deep as the bottom of a well.

Brannock came down the corridor with his lamp and his ring of keys, and Edric stood at his bars with his hands wrapped around the iron, and when the jailer passed, Edric said, quietly, not demanding anything, only offering:

"The rowan tree is still alive, Brannock."

The lamp stopped swinging.

"You wouldn't know it," Edric went on, conversational, gentle, a man remarking on weather. "It's up under the sky-slot in the third chamber, the one they use for storage now. Twenty years untended. Should be dead—rowans are short-lived things, everyone says so. But I went up and looked, the day they arrested me, on the way past. Its bark's gone silver. Its roots have found a crack in the cistern wall and gone down to the water." He smiled, and his voice dropped lower, warmer, the voice he'd used over seedlings in late frost. "It's flowering. Out of season. Out of reason. White blossoms in the dark, where no one was ever meant to see them. Do you remember planting it? You dug the hole crooked, and I scolded you, and the king said a crooked hole makes a humble tree."

Brannock had turned. He stood in the middle of the corridor with the lamp trembling slightly in his fist, and his face was doing something complicated and painful, like a field thawing after a hard winter—all at once, mud and water and things heaving up out of the ground that had been frozen solid for years.

"I don't—" he started, and stopped. His free hand had risen again to his chest, pressing flat, as though holding a door shut from the inside. Or holding something in.

"There was a girl," Edric said very softly. "Your daughter. You brought her here once, in summer. She wore ribbons the color of marigolds, and she picked a poppy from my cutting bed and you apologized a hundred times and the king laughed and said the flower had gone to a better prison than any of ours." He watched the big man sway. "What was her name, Brannock? Say it. Just the name. Nothing bad will happen. Just the name."

The jailer's lips parted. His eyes had gone somewhere far away and terribly close at once. And Edric understood, with a lurch, that Silas was already working—that somewhere in the shadow of the corridor her gift was moving through the ruined country of this man's mind like a careful hand through a burned wood, gathering what she needed—and that his task was only this: to keep the light burning, to keep the ground soft, to keep a dying man's heart open wide enough for the theft to pass through without waking the watchdogs of grief.

"Lily," Brannock whispered. "Her name was Lily. Where—" His voice cracked like river ice. "Where did she go? Why can't I find—"

"You'll find her," Edric said, steady and sure, a lie and a promise braided together. "I swear it on the rowan tree. Now tell me about the cistern gate, old friend. Tell me about your father's wheel."

And Brannock, weeping silently in the torchlight, began to talk.

At the far end of the corridor, in the dark beyond the last lamp, Silas rose to her feet inside her cell, and Edric heard the whisper of chain-link going taut, and heard her breathe one word that carried along the stone like a spark down a fuse:

"Now."
