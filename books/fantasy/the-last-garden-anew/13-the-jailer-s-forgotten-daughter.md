## 13. The Jailer's Forgotten Daughter

The dark beneath the palace was not the honest dark of a mountain night. It had texture. It had history. Silas could feel it pressing against her skin like wet velvet, thick with the residue of every soul that had ever rotted down here waiting for the hunters to come collect them.

She sat on the stone floor of her cell with her knees drawn up and her palms flat against the cold, and she listened.

Not with her ears. Ears were nearly useless in the dungeons of Vhalen Keep; the walls swallowed sound and gave back only drips and the distant groan of settling stone. She listened with the other sense, the one she had spent her whole life pretending she didn't have—the sense that reached out like fingers made of smoke and touched the minds walking past her door.

There were two guards on rotation in the cell block below. One was bored, his thoughts a gray sludge of dice-games and an unpaid debt to a woman named Cora who ran the tannery. The other—

Silas went very still.

The other was a wound.

She had felt wounds before. Everyone carried some scar or splinter in their memory; most people simply didn't know it was there. But this was different. This man's mind had a hole in it, clean-edged and deliberate, the kind of absence that wasn't grief but *surgery*. Someone had cut something out of him. And around that hole, his whole self had begun to collapse inward like a house with a missing load-bearing wall.

He walked the corridor above her cell—she tracked him by that strange hollow ache—and stopped, and stood there for a long moment without moving, as though he had forgotten what he'd come down here to do.

*That,* thought Silas, with a chill that had nothing to do with the dungeon damp, *is my work.*

* * *

She had been stealing memories since she was nine years old. She knew the shape of a lifted thing better than she knew her own reflection: the smooth-walled absence, the way the mind grew scar tissue over it like flesh over a pulled tooth. Most people never noticed. That was the mercy of the trade, if you could call it mercy—that the robbed rarely knew they'd been robbed. They woke with a faint unease, a sense of a room in their house they couldn't find the door to, and then life filled the space with ordinary things and the door stayed lost forever.

But Silas had also spent three years selling to Maren, and Maren had taught her the second rule of the trade, the rule written under the first one in letters no broker liked to say aloud:

*What is taken does not vanish. It goes somewhere.*

Into a vial. Into a buyer's head. Into a stranger's dreams.

And sometimes—this was the part that had kept her awake at night more than once, though she'd always managed to drink herself back to sleep—it went into Silas herself.

She closed her eyes now, in the dark of her cell, and did the thing she almost never did deliberately: she opened her own inner hoard.

It spread out before her mind's eye like a market stall at dusk. Memories she had lifted over fifteen years, each one wrapped in its own particular feel—a smuggler's first kill, tasting of iron and pine; a bride's wedding morning, all honey-light and terror; an old woman's last sight of the sea. Some she'd sold long ago and remembered only as transactions. Some she'd kept because keeping them was how she'd built herself, brick by stolen brick, into someone who could survive anything.

And there, near the center, where the oldest things lived—

A girl. A girl of perhaps seven, with mud on her boots and a crooked crown of dandelions on her head, laughing as she ran through an orchard toward a man in a jailer's uniform who swung her up onto his shoulders. *Papa! Papa, look what I found!* And the man laughing too, his voice rough as gravel and warm as bread, saying, *What did you find, little wren? What treasure have you got there?*

Silas's breath left her slowly.

She knew this memory. Not from the outside. From the inside. She remembered the day she'd taken it—six years ago, maybe seven, in a tavern in Lowmark, when she'd been hungry and reckless and fresh off her first real success. A jailer passing through on escort duty, drinking alone, easy prey for a girl who looked harmless. She'd slipped under his thoughts while he fumbled with his cup and found the brightest thing in him and *pulled*, and it had come away so easily, so sweetly, and he hadn't even flinched, just blinked and stared into his ale and after a while paid his tab and left, and she'd run into the night with a father's love for his daughter burning in her hands like a coal.

She had sold it within the week. To a merchant whose own child had died, Maren said; the man had wept with gratitude. Good coin. Good outcome, Silas had told herself. Everyone gets what they need. Nobody gets hurt.

But the girl in the orchard—the real girl, the one with the mud on her boots—had not been dead. She had simply been *forgotten*. Erased from the one mind that held her. Somewhere out there was a daughter who had gone home to a father who no longer knew her name, who had looked at her with a stranger's polite blankness while she screamed and cried and finally, maybe, stopped coming to the door at all.

And that father, Silas realized, her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to escape, that father was standing twenty feet above her cell right now, guarding prisoners he could barely remember why he'd agreed to guard, with a hole in his chest shaped exactly like a small laughing girl.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Edric whispered through the gap between the stones. "The thinking-so-hard-I-can-hear-it thing."

"Shut up, Edric."

"That's gardener Hale to you." But there was no bite in it. He'd been broken when they'd thrown him in here—she'd watched the palace do it to him, watched the man walk back into the place that had ruined him and fold up like a plant in frost—but something had been rekindling in him these past two days, slow and stubborn, the way embers rekindle if you don't stir them wrong. "Isolde's asking if you've found anything."

"Tell her I'm working."

"She'll take that as a yes and start planning an escape route through the cesspool again."

"Tell her the cesspool route remains my last resort."

A dry breath that might have been a laugh. Then silence, blessed silence, and Silas turned back inward to the thing she had been avoiding since the moment she'd recognized it.

Because here was the truth she kept circling like a moth around a lamp she knew would burn her: Isolde had been right. Back on the broker caravan, in the argument Silas still replayed when sleep wouldn't come—the princess had looked at her with those steady gray eyes and said, *You take things that aren't yours and call it survival, but survival for whom? You're alive. They're less than they were.* And Silas had thrown back every justification she owned, every hard-luck story, every *you don't know what hunger does to a person*, and Isolde had let her talk herself out and then said quietly, *I know. But that doesn't make it clean,* and Silas had hated her for a full week for being correct.

Now she sat in a cell her thefts had helped put her in, and the debt had come due in the cruelest possible currency: the man guarding her door was missing his daughter because Silas had sold her.

Restitution. The word surfaced from somewhere deep—from the dragon-memory, from the garden beneath the earth, from whatever ancient patient thing had been speaking to her in borrowed voices. *What is taken must be given back. Freely. Or nothing balances.*

She could give it back.

Her gift had always been a thief's gift—reach in, pull out, carry away. But every thief's tool could be turned around. She had proven that on the Lethe River, tearing the hunter's memories apart in front of witnesses, feeling for the first time what it was to use her power in the open. Tearing apart was easy. Destruction always was.

Putting something *back*—that she had never tried. It might not work. It might kill her. She genuinely did not know, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Above her, boots on stone. The wounded man, making his rounds.

Silas pressed her palm flat against the floor and began, carefully, to unwrap the memory of the girl.

* * *

It took her until the changing of the watch—hours of delicate, exhausting work, holding the memory open without letting it bleed, the way you'd hold a bird with cupped hands: firmly enough that it couldn't fly, gently enough that it wouldn't crush. By the time the new shift's footsteps faded and only the wounded man remained on the block, sweat had soaked through her shirt despite the dungeon cold, and her hands were trembling.

*I can't do this from in here,* she thought. And then, with the recklessness that had gotten her into every catastrophe of her adult life: *Watch me.*

She rose. She put her eye to the gap between the stones. The corridor beyond was dim, lit by a single oil lamp, and the jailer stood at the far end of it, facing the wall, his keys hanging motionless at his belt. Big man. Gray at the temples. Shoulders like a doorframe. Even from behind, she could see it—the way he stood slightly hunched, as if carrying weight no one else could see. As if carrying an absence.

"Jailer," she said softly.

He turned. His face was weathered and tired, and when his eyes found hers through the grate there was nothing in them—not cruelty, not kindness. Just that terrible polite blankness. The look of a man who has forgotten something important and has stopped trying to remember what.

"Back from your bars, witch," he said, but the words had no heat. "The king's mercy doesn't extend to your kind. Save your silver tongue."

"I'm not here to bargain." Her mouth was dry. "I want to ask you something. Do you have a family?"

Something flickered across his face—pain, quick as a fish under ice, then gone. "No."

"No wife? No children?"

"I told you. No." But his hand had risen, without his seeming to notice, and pressed flat against his own chest, over the sternum, over the hole.

"Do you dream?" Silas asked. "Do you dream about an orchard?"

His whole body went rigid. "What—"

"A green orchard in late summer. Apples heavy on the branches. A little girl running through the grass with mud on her boots and dandelions in her hair, calling for her papa."

"*Stop.*" His voice cracked on the word. He took a step backward, then another, and his hand was shaking against his chest now, pressing harder, as though he could hold himself together by force. "What sorcery—what are you doing to me—"

"I'm not doing anything to you." She kept her voice low and even, the way she'd speak to a spooked horse. "I'm asking if you remember her."

"I don't—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice was barely a whisper. "I don't have a daughter."

"Then why are you crying?"

He touched his cheek. Looked at his wet fingertips with genuine bewilderment, a man discovering evidence of a crime committed against him in a country he couldn't recall visiting. And Silas felt something break open in her—not painfully, or not only painfully. It broke open the way soil breaks open under a plowblade: destruction in service of planting.

"My name is Silas," she said. "Six years ago, in a tavern in Lowmark, I stole something from you. I was starving and I told myself it didn't matter, that you'd never miss it, that everyone walks away from these trades a little lighter and no one bleeds. I was wrong. I have been wrong for six years, and I am so sorry." The apology came out ragged; she hadn't meant it to. "I still have her. I never let her go. And I want to give her back."

"That's impossible." But he had drifted closer to the bars, drawn against his own fear, the way the drowning drift toward voices. "You can't—you can't just *give back* a person—"

"Not a person. A remembering." She reached her hands through the bars, palms up, empty. An offering posture. She'd seen beggars use it; she'd used it herself, once or twice, before the gift came. "I need you to trust me. Just for a moment. Let me touch your temples, and I will stop, the instant you say stop, I swear it on whatever you have left that's holy."

He stared at her hands. At her face. And Silas watched him weigh it—watched the arithmetic of a desperate man, the calculation of *what do I have to lose* answered by the hollow in his own chest. Guards did not take counsel with witches. Guards reported witches, beat witches, handed witches to the pyre-masters.

This guard stepped forward instead.

His skin was hot under her fingers, fever-hot, though the dungeons were freezing. Up close she could see the gray stubble he'd missed shaving, the ink stain on his knuckles—he wrote letters, then, or had once, to someone—and the fine trembling in his jaw. "If this is a trick," he whispered, "I'll—"

"You won't," Silas said gently. "You'll thank me. Close your eyes."

He closed his eyes.

She reached in.

Going into a willing mind was nothing like going into a sleeping drunk's. It was like being invited into a house rather than breaking through a window—every room lit, every door unlocked, the whole architecture of the man laid open before her and *watching her pass through it*, aware, afraid, permitting. She moved carefully, reverently, past his soldiering years, past his marriage to a woman named Brenna who died of lung-fever eight years back—grief there, real grief, worn smooth by time—and down, down, to the hole.

Up close, the absence was worse than she'd imagined. The edges weren't scarred; they were raw. Six years and the wound had never healed, because the mind kept reaching for what was missing the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Around the hole floated fragments, orphaned shreds that had once attached to her: a small shoe by a doorway, unexplained. A habit of buying candied ginger every market day with no idea why. A name he almost remembered, a name that lived on the tip of his tongue like a splinter—*Wren*, the memory called her, his little wren, because she'd found a fledgling once, fallen from its nest, and carried it home in her cupped hands, and begged him to help her save it, and they had, they'd fed it mashed apple with a wooden spoon for three weeks until it flew—

Silas took the girl in both hands, this memory she had carried for six years, that had been hers to sell and spend and hoard, and she did the hardest thing she had ever done.

She gave it back.

It resisted at first—the way water resists being poured uphill, the way any stolen thing resists return—and for one vertiginous moment she felt the whole transaction teeter, her grip slipping, the memory shredding at the edges, and she heard herself make a small involuntary sound of effort and loss. Because some part of her, the starved nine-year-old part, the part that had built herself out of other people's bright moments, did not *want* to let go. The girl in the orchard was sunlight. She was the best thing in Silas's collection, the treasure she took out on bad nights, the proof that love existed somewhere even if it had never once been aimed at Silas Kavanagh.

*Let go,* she told herself. *It was never yours. Nothing was ever yours.*

She let go.

The memory slid from her like a splinter leaving flesh—pain, relief, and then a strange lightness, a hollowness that was somehow also healing. It flowed through her palms into the jailer's mind and she felt it settle home, felt the edges knit, felt the hole close over it the way water closes over a dropped stone, seamless, complete, as though it had never been anywhere else.

The man gasped.

His eyes flew open—not blank anymore, not anymore, blazing, flooded—and his knees buckled, and he caught himself on the bars of her cell with a crash that echoed down the whole block. "*Wren,*" he sobbed, and the name came out of him like an arrow coming out of a wound. "Wren—my girl—her birthday's the—she was seven, she lost a tooth that summer, she made me keep it in a box—Brenna, Brenna, our girl, oh gods, oh gods, where is she, where—" He looked up at Silas with tears streaming down his weathered face, and his expression was the most complicated thing she had ever seen: joy and horror and gratitude and grief all fighting for the same ground. "Where is my daughter?"

"I don't know," Silas whispered. Truthfully. It was almost worse than lying. "I took her from you. Everything after—I don't know what happened to her. I'm sorry. I am so sorry."

"Why?" He gripped the bars. "Why would you give her back? You could have kept her. Kept her forever. What witch gives anything back?"

Silas knelt, slowly, on the cold stone, and bowed her head. Not performance. For the first time in her life, the gesture was exactly what it appeared to be.

"Because I owe you," she said. "And because I'm done taking."

For a long moment the only sounds were the lamp flame and the man's ragged breathing. Then, somewhere above, a door boomed open—footsteps, voices, the night shift coming early—and the jailer straightened, wiping his face with the back of his hand, and when he looked at Silas again the blankness was gone and something fierce had replaced it.

"They're moving you," he said quickly, low. "All three of you. Tomorrow. The king wants you at the festival—wants the gifted burned publicly, wants the crowd to see." His jaw tightened. "I spent six years as half a man and called it duty. I guarded the cells of children while forgetting I had one." He was already pulling the ring of keys from his belt, hands shaking. "I'm not spending whatever time I have left doing it again. Stand back from the door, witch."

Metal scraped against metal. The lock, old and stubborn, fought him—and then gave with a click that sounded, to Silas, louder than any bell in Vhalen Keep.

The cell door swung open.

"Get your friends," the jailer said. "We have until dawn, and I know a way down that the king's creatures cannot follow—through the old root-cellars, under the garden wall. My Wren used to play there."

*Under the garden wall.* Silas's heart lurched. The garden. The fire. The way down.

Somewhere above them, muffled by tons of stone, the festival drums had already begun to beat.
