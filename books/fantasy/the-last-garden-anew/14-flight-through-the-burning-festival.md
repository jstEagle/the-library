## 14. Flight Through the Burning Festival

The bells began at dusk.

Silas heard them first as a tremor in the soles of her feet, a low bronze moan rolling up through the cobbles of the Undercroft where the three of them crouched between barrels of salted fish. Then the sound climbed into the air above and multiplied—every bell in the capital ringing at once, a chaos of peals that had no rhythm to it, no order, only urgency.

"They're not celebrating," Isolde whispered. Her hand found Silas's wrist in the dark. "Festival bells are rung in sequence. This is an alarm dressed as joy."

Edric said nothing. He had said very little since they'd come up out of the dungeon's dark, since the jailer with his restored daughter's face had pressed the garden keys into his palm and turned away weeping. He moved like a man carrying something fragile through a storm—hunched, careful, listening for it to crack.

Above them, through a grate that opened onto a delivery yard, the sky was the wrong color. Not the deep blue of a festival evening, not the gold of lantern-light. It was orange, flickering, alive—and it was moving.

Smoke threaded down through the grate and coiled around them, thick with a smell Silas knew from her worst stolen memories: burning hair, burning wool, burning flesh.

"They've started early," Edric said. His voice came out sanded flat. "The pyres. They always light them at moonrise."

The false king had promised a festival to celebrate the hunt's success, and the hunt had been successful indeed. The hunters had filled their cages all autumn—the hedge-witch from the Tanner's Quarter who could coax milk from barren goats, the ferryman on the Lethe whose touch could calm drowning men, the twin girls from the weavers' row who shared dreams between them the way other children shared sweets. Silas knew some of their faces from memory-broker stalls, from vials she'd handled and sold. She had never wondered what became of the people after she'd lifted what was valuable out of them. She was wondering now, and the wondering was a knife.

"We go now," she said. "While every hunter in the city is watching the fire instead of watching us."

She reached up and pulled the grate open. Heat poured down like water.

* * *

They came up into a city gone mad with light.

The great avenue that led to the palace gates had been transformed. Braziers lined both sides, taller than a man, and between them rose the pyres—seven of them, Silas counted before she made herself stop counting—each one already crowned with flame. Crowds pressed against the barriers, thousands of faces lit from below in that terrible festive orange, and many of those faces were *smiling*. Children sat on their fathers' shoulders for a better view. Vendors moved through the crowd selling roasted chestnuts and honey-wine, and the smell of the sweet things tangled with the smell of the smoke until Silas thought she would never again be able to taste honey without gagging.

On the third pyre, someone was singing.

It took Silas a moment to understand what she was hearing—a high, wavering thread of melody cutting through the crackle and roar. An old woman, tied at the stake, singing a lullaby in a language Silas didn't know but recognized anyway, because memories were memories no matter the tongue, and this one carried the unmistakable shape of a song sung to a child. The woman sang it steadily, without haste, as though the flames were merely weather and she had somewhere gentle to be.

Isolde stopped walking. Silas saw the princess's face and understood: the woman on the pyre had been singing that song in the nursery once, decades ago, to a small dark-haired girl who would grow up to hide in wine cellars and plot against her father.

"Don't," Silas said quietly. "Don't look at her. Look at me."

"I know her." Isolde's voice cracked like river ice. "Her name is Wrenna. She used to let me steal honey-cakes from the kitchens when I was six. My father—" She swallowed. "The thing wearing my father gave the order for this. I can see the royal box from here. He's *waving*, Silas. He's smiling and waving like it's a harvest fair."

"Then he isn't looking for us yet." Silas caught her arm and steered her left, away from the avenue, into the mouth of a cooper's lane where the shadows were deep enough to drink. "Grief later. Movement now. That's the bargain, remember? We made it in the dungeon and we don't renegotiate in the street."

Edric brought up the rear, and Silas heard him murmur something under his breath—not a prayer, or not quite. A gardener's words, old ones, the kind you said over seeds in early spring. *Grow back. Grow back where they cut you.* She pretended not to hear, and loved him a little for it.

* * *

The problem was the trail.

Silas felt it before she could name it—a tugging sensation at the base of her skull, faint as a fishhook set in wool. The stolen memory inside her, Edric's dying king and the smoke-thing that drank his last breath, had a weight of its own, and somewhere behind them, something with a hook made of the same stuff was pulling on the line.

Hunters. Sensing her. The way she'd sensed them on the Thornfell road, a cold prickle of attention, except now the cold was aimed *at* her and gaining.

"They're tracking the memory," she said under her breath, hurrying the three of them along the cooper's lane toward the tanners' district, where the reek would cover everything else. "I can feel it. Like a hand on my collar."

"How far?" Edric asked.

"Close. Two streets. Maybe three." She did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: the garden gate was on the far side of the palace grounds; the palace grounds were across a city full of festival crowds and festival hunters; and she was trailing her own scent behind her like a spilled sack of flour. "I can outrun the pull if I'm alone. You two can't. And I can't reach the gate without you."

"Then stop spilling the flour," Isolde said.

Silas looked at her.

"You take memories," Isolde said. There was no accusation in it anymore—that war had been fought somewhere on a broker caravan's road, and something in Silas had shifted ground since, though neither of them named it. "You've spent your whole life taking them carefully, invisibly. So be invisible. But take *loudly* this time. Fill the streets with so much stolen noise that whatever's following us drowns in it."

Silas stared at her, and then she laughed—a short, startled bark of sound with nothing merry in it, only admiration. "You want me to rob an entire fleeing crowd."

"I want you to make them lose our scent in everyone else's."

"It will hurt people. Confusion, lost minutes, lost hours. Some of them will wander."

"Some of them," Isolde said, nodding toward the avenue, where Wrenna's lullaby had finally, terribly, stopped, "are going to die tonight anyway. Choose the smaller wound."

* * *

The tanners' district emptied toward the river when the fires started, and that was where Silas found her crowd.

They came pouring down Tanner's Row in a frightened, jostling flood—dyers and fleshers and knife-boys, families with bundles on their backs, a man dragging a cart of hides he couldn't bear to abandon. They wanted nothing but away, and away was exactly what Silas needed them to want loudly enough to blind a hunter two streets back.

She planted herself against a wall, closed her eyes, and opened herself the way she never allowed herself to do—wide, greedy, indiscriminate.

Memories came to her like water finding a breach.

A boy's terror of the smoke, and beneath it the smell of his mother's bread. A dyer's last sight of her workshop, forty years of color going up in imagination if not in fact. A knife-boy's single afternoon by the sea, hoarded like a jewel, given up without knowing he'd given it. Silas didn't dig; she skimmed. She skimmed a hundred minds in as many heartbeats, lifting only the top layer, the bright surface moments, leaving the deep structures intact—her old craft performed at ten times her old speed and a hundred times her old scale. In her mind she built a bonfire of borrowed moments, and then she did the thing she had never done, the thing Maren had told her on her first day in the trade was impossible:

She threw them outward.

Not into any single mind. Into the air between minds, into the thin place where gifted senses lived, she scattered the stolen fragments like chaff before wind. Terror-of-smoke and smell-of-bread and forty-years-of-color and one-afternoon-by-the-sea, flung wide, smeared across the whole district, until the psychic air itself was thick with human residue, a hundred false trails braided into a rope with no end.

And underneath it, wrapped in the noise, she tucked the true trail—the memory of the dying king—folded small and hidden inside a butcher's memory of slaughtering hogs, buried in blood-smell and steam.

"There," she gasped, sagging against the wall. Sweat ran cold down her spine. "Let them sort through a thousand lives to find mine."

For a long moment there was only the roar of the distant pyres and the shuffle of the fleeing crowd around them, parting and reforming like water around stones.

Then the tug at the base of her skull stuttered—wavered—slid sideways, snagging on a dyer's grief, a knife-boy's sea, and slid off entirely.

Gone. Or at least blinded.

Edric caught her elbow before her knees found the cobbles. "How much did you take?" he asked, and his voice was gentle in a way that told her he already suspected.

"Scraps. Surface things. Nothing anyone will miss." It was mostly true. Mostly. One of the fragments sat in her chest like a swallowed coal—a man's memory of teaching his small daughter to skip stones, the exact weight of the girl's hand in his—and Silas knew, with a sick lurch, that she hadn't needed that one. Her gift had simply been hungry, the way it got when she opened it wide, and hunger didn't ask permission. She filed it away with all the others she'd have to return someday, if there was a someday, if the guild she dreamed of ever became more than a desperate woman's fantasy.

"The river gate," she said aloud, pushing off the wall. "We cross at the tanners' bridge while the hunters are sniffing hog-blood and sea-air. Move."

* * *

They almost made the bridge.

They were fifty yards from it—close enough to see the black water sliding beneath, close enough to smell the river mud that would carry them clean to the palace's forgotten postern—when the crowd ahead of them stopped moving.

Stopped, and turned, and went silent in a spreading wave, the way wheat goes still before hail.

At the far end of Tanner's Row stood a line of hunters in ash-grey cloaks, and in front of them, held aloft on a pole like a lantern, burned a brand. Not wood-fire. The mark itself was aflame—a circle bisected by a blade, the witch-hunter's sigil—and it cast a light that wasn't light, a pale wrongness that made Silas's stolen memories shiver in her skull like glass in a quake.

The lead hunter stepped forward. Even at distance, Silas felt his attention sweep the crowd like a hand through drawers, and she felt it catch—catch on *her*, on the folded, hidden, butcher-scented bundle in her mind—as though the whole ocean of noise she'd raised had parted for him alone.

"Well," the hunter called out, and his voice carried with unnatural ease across the panicked quiet. "The festival's been lovely. But the king asks for a thief."

Beside her, Isolde drew a breath. Behind her, Edric's hand closed around the garden keys in his pocket, and Silas heard the small metal music of them, and understood that whatever happened next, it happened now.

She stepped forward, put herself between the hunters and the bridge, and smiled the smile of a woman with nothing left to lose.
