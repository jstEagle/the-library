## 15. Roots That Remember

The garden had grown wild in his absence, and Edric Hale found that he could not hate it for that.

He stood at the rusted gate where the festival's chaos had spat them out—the three of them running through smoke and screaming, hunters' torches blooming in the streets behind like fever-blisters—and he pressed his palm flat against the iron scrollwork and felt, beneath the corrosion, the old sigil of the royal groundskeepers. His own initials were still there, worked into the vine pattern by a smith who'd owed him a favor twenty years ago. *E.H.*, curled like a tendril.

Behind him, Silas Kavanagh was watching the street. Her hood was back—there was no point now, not after what she'd done at the river, not after the whole city had seen her tear a hunter's mind apart like wet paper—and her face held the particular stillness she wore when she was listening to something no one else could hear. Isolde crouched beside her, breath ragged, a smear of soot across her cheek where she'd pulled a burning banner down off a doorway to cover their flight.

"They're not following yet," Silas said. "The crowd's confusion is holding. I took enough from them—enough wrong directions, enough faces that aren't ours. It'll buy us minutes. Not more."

"Minutes are all I need." Edric heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt. His hands, though. His hands would not stop shaking, and they hadn't stopped since he'd walked back through the palace district and seen what the thing wearing Aldric's face had made of the king's gardens—or rather, what it had let them become. "Open the gate, Silas."

"It's locked."

"It's mine." He closed his eyes. Twenty years of muscle memory lived in his fingers still: the third scroll from the left, pressed twice, then turned as if opening a very small door. The lock was meant to be worked blind, by feel, by someone who had tended these gates every morning before dawn while the rest of the court slept. A test disguised as a mechanism. He had designed half of it himself, in another life, when he had been a man the garden trusted.

The lock clicked. The gate swung inward on hinges that groaned but held.

* * *

Inside, the world changed.

The noise of the burning festival fell away as though a curtain had been drawn across it. The air turned cool and green-smelling, thick with loam and crushed mint and the sweet rot of fallen fruit no one had harvested. Moonlight came down through a canopy of chestnuts that Edric himself had planted as saplings, and it fell in coins on paths gone soft with moss.

But the garden was dying. He saw it immediately, the way a physician sees death in a face. Whole beds had gone to bramble and bindweed. The rose walk was a tangle of thorned ghosts. The great glasshouse had lost half its panes, and frost-blackened fronds drooped from within like drowned things. And everywhere—everywhere—there was the grayness. A pallor in the leaves. A brittleness in the stems. As if the color itself were being drunk out of the soil, siphoned away root by root into some deep and patient thirst below.

"He's been feeding," Edric said quietly. He knelt and pushed his fingers into a bed that should have been black with humus; it came up pale and powdery, like ash. "All these years. Everything the fire gave, everything the roots drew up—he drank it. The garden has been dying from underneath for twenty years, and no one above ground knew why the roses wouldn't bloom."

Isolde stood very still among the ruined beds. She was looking at a stone bench—just a bench, lichened and cracked—and yet her hand went to her mouth.

"I used to hide here," she said. "When I was small. Before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before my father changed. There was a gardener who never told on me. He'd leave fresh bread on this bench and pretend not to see me. He taught me the names of things. He said—" Her voice caught. "He said everything growing remembers who was kind to it."

Edric did not trust himself to answer. He looked at the bench instead, at the bread crumbs twenty years gone, and thought: *she remembers me as kind*. He had carried his disgrace like a stone so long he had forgotten anyone might carry something else of him.

"Which way?" Silas asked. She had moved to his shoulder, silent as falling leaf-mold. Her eyes—those unsettling, memory-crowded eyes—were scanning the dark paths. "You said beneath. Beneath what? Where?"

"The heart of it." Edric straightened. "The oldest planting. Come. And stay close to the middle of the path. The garden will know you're strangers." He paused. "It may not like it."

* * *

They walked deeper, and the garden began, slowly, to wake.

Edric felt it first in his boots—a tremor in the ground too regular to be footsteps, too slow. Then in the air: the scent shifting, sharpening, green-sap and crushed thyme rising where there had been only rot. The moonlight seemed to thicken. And along both sides of the path, the plants began to move.

Not wind. Not animals. Movement with intention—the deliberate lean of a foxglove turning its bells toward them as they passed, tracking them like faces in a crowd. Ivy uncurling from a dead trellis, finger by finger, tasting the air. A hedge of yew rippling without wind, its darkness folding and refolding like something breathing in its sleep.

Silas had gone rigid. "Edric."

"They're remembering," he said. His voice came out hushed. "I told you. Every plant in this garden was raised by a human hand. Every cutting grafted, every seed pressed into soil by fingers. Plants remember touch the way stone remembers heat. Long after the hand is gone, long after the gardener is dead or disgraced or dragged out of the city in chains—" his throat tightened around the word, because that had nearly been his ending, once; it had been Wren's ending, and Tam's, and a dozen others' on the night the false king purged the groundskeepers— "the roots keep the shape of it. Keep us. All of us, everyone who ever knelt in this dirt."

"And they know you," Isolde breathed. She was staring past him. "Look."

He looked.

The path ahead had changed. The bindweed that choked it was withdrawing—not dying, not retreating in fear, but *withdrawing*, curling back from the center of the way like a congregation making room. The moss beneath his feet had brightened from gray-green to a deep, living emerald, and it brightened further with each step he took, a ribbon of color unrolling ahead of him and fading again behind, as if the garden were laying down a carpet it remembered how to make and could only hold together in one place at a time.

And the foxgloves—all along the borders, hundreds of them, tall spires of dusty purple that should have been dead in the cold—had turned. Every bell faced him. Every stem leaned, faintly, the way wheat leans toward sun.

"Oh," Edric said. It was all he could manage. Something enormous was happening inside his chest, something that hurt worse than grief and was not grief.

Twenty years he had told himself the story of his disgrace: that he had failed, that he had run, that he had abandoned the garden and the king both to the thing in the smoke. Twenty years he had believed the garden must have forgotten him, or worse, cursed him. And here it was, remembering him instead. Remembering the twenty-two years before the ruin. The dawn feedings. The winter mulching with his bare hands. The thousand small kindnesses done in the dark to things that could not thank him.

"They kept me," he whispered. "All this time, they kept my shape in the roots."

"Edric." Silas's voice was strange. When he glanced at her, her eyes were shining, overbright. "I can feel it. The memories—it's like standing in a room full of voices. They're all yours. Thousands of them. Little ones. Watering. Pruning. Talking to them—" she laughed, wetly, incredulously— "you talked to them. You talked to them every single day."

"A gardener who doesn't talk to his plants is only a laborer," he said automatically, and then had to stop walking, because his eyes had flooded and the path had begun to swim.

Isolde put her hand on his arm. She said nothing. She didn't need to. The princess who had hidden on benches and eaten stolen bread had learned, somewhere in her exile, when silence was the kinder gift.

* * *

The heart of the garden lay beyond the ruins of the old orangery, and Edric had almost forgotten how much the way there would cost him.

Because the orangery was where it had happened. Where Aldric Vane—king, friend, the man who had once knelt in the mud beside him for an entire afternoon replanting a storm-flattened orchard, laughing, mud to his elbows, a king no better than any common grower—had come to Edric in the last hour of his true life and said, *I don't feel well, old friend. Walk with me among the trees.*

Where the smoke had poured up between the citrus trunks like floodwater finding a cellar. Where the king had fallen. Where Edric had run—not fought, not stayed, *run*—and lived, and been disgraced for living, and deserved it.

His feet slowed at the threshold. The others noticed. Neither of them spoke. That, too, was a kindness.

Inside, the orangery was a cathedral of bones. The great glass ribs stood open to the sky where the roof had fallen in. The orange and lemon trees were dead—truly dead, silver-gray, their branches like coral—but they still stood, and standing among them Edric felt the old memories rise in him like water into a wick: Aldric's laughter, the smell of bruised citrus leaf, the terrible last weight of the king's hand on his shoulder saying *go, Edric, go*—

No. Not *go*. That was the version he'd told himself for two decades. The truth, which he had finally spoken aloud to Silas on the river road, was simpler and far worse: the king had said nothing at all. The smoke had taken his breath mid-word, and Edric had simply fled, and the king's last sight had been his oldest friend's back.

"He died here," Isolde said softly. Not a question.

"Here." Edric's voice was gravel. "And I have spent twenty years being ashamed to come back. Look at me now, walking in like the garden owes me something."

"The garden doesn't owe you," Silas said. She had stopped beside one of the dead lemons, her fingers hovering an inch from the bark, not touching. "It's the other way around, isn't it? That's what all this is." She gestured—at the leaning foxgloves, the withdrawn weeds, the carpet of moss that still glowed faintly green where he'd walked. "It's not forgiving you. It never needed to. It just... kept your place."

Edric looked at his hands. The same hands that had shaken all night, that had shaken for twenty years. They were steady now. He flexed them, and found them steady, and did not fully understand it, and accepted it anyway.

"Down," he said. "The entrance is under the central planter. Come."

* * *

The central planter of the orangery was a circle of ancient stone wide as a wagon wheel, and in twenty years the garden had swallowed it whole. Bramble sealed it like a lid. But when Edric knelt at its edge and set both palms against the earth—properly against it, the way he used to, the way he had taught apprentices to greet soil each morning, *like greeting an elder*—the brambles shuddered.

Then they parted.

Thorns folded back upon thorns, a slow unfurling, careful even in its wildness, the way a hand opens. Beneath them the stone was clean. In its center, a carved rosette—twelve petals, each etched with the sigil of an old royal house—and at its heart, a keyhole shaped like nothing so much as a seed.

"There's no key," Isolde said.

"There was never a key." Edric settled himself cross-legged at the rim, the posture of a man about to do the hardest work of his life, which was not lifting but yielding. "The door opens for the gardener. Only ever for the gardener. It reads you through your hands—through what they've done. Through what they remember." He swallowed. "I haven't touched this soil in twenty years. I've spent them in exile hating myself. If the garden finds that in me—if it finds only shame—"

"Then it also finds this." Silas knelt opposite him. For a moment, her eyes did their unsettling work, flickering with borrowed light, and when she spoke again her voice was softer than he'd ever heard it. "I'm standing in your memories right now, Edric. All of them. Do you know what the deepest one is? Not the king. Not the night he died. It's a morning in March. Rain coming. You moving seedlings under glass, alone, before anyone else woke. And the feeling underneath it—" she pressed her fist briefly against her sternum, translating, struggling— "it's not duty. It's love. Plain love. The garden knows that's in your hands. Shame is just weather. Love is what's planted."

For a long moment, Edric Hale could not speak at all.

Then he laid his hands flat on the soil, and greeted it like an elder, and told it the truth the way he'd told no living soul: *I ran. I left him. I left you. I am sorry, and I am here, and if you'll have me I will spend what's left of my life making it right.*

The rosette grew warm beneath his palms.

Warmth spread outward through the stone, petal by petal, each sigil kindling with a faint gold light like sun through honey. Somewhere far beneath them—far, far beneath, deeper than cellars, deeper than the palace foundations, down where the oldest roots of the oldest trees had wound themselves for centuries around something vast and sleeping—a sound answered.

A breath. Slow and immense and labored, like a bellows full of embers rather than air. The ground trembled with it. Dead leaves shivered in the orangery. And threaded through the exhalation, unmistakable, came heat—the first true warmth Edric had felt in this garden in twenty years, rising up through the opened stone, carrying the smell of sulfur and cinnamon and something older than either.

The seed-shaped keyhole filled with light.

With a grinding sigh, the twelve petals of the rosette split apart and folded downward, unfolding into steps—stone steps, descending into a glow that pulsed slow and red-gold, like the inside of a banked hearth.

Heat washed up over the three of them. Isolde made a small sound, half awe, half fear. Silas stood motionless, her face lit from below, and Edric saw her expression and recognized it with a chill: recognition. As if the fire rising from the deep knew her somehow—as if it had already reached into her crowded, stolen, secret-filled mind and found something there it wanted.

Or something it needed.

"Edric," she said quietly, without looking away from the light. "There's something down there. It's... speaking. Not in words. In memories. Hundreds of them. Voices." Her brow furrowed. "One of them sounds like—"

She stopped. Went pale.

"What?" Edric gripped her shoulder. "Silas. What does it sound like?"

Her eyes came up to his, wide and lit from beneath by the ember-glow, and when she answered, her voice was barely more than breath.

"Like my mother."
