## 16. The Last Dragon

The stair went down farther than the palace had ever gone up.

Silas counted the steps out of habit—counting was how she kept herself whole when the world grew strange—and lost her place somewhere past four hundred. The walls had changed by then. The dressed stone of the garden's underways gave way to older work, cyclopean blocks furred with moss that glowed a faint, patient green, and the air grew warm in a way that had nothing to do with furnaces or summer. It was the warmth of a body. Of blood beneath skin. She could feel it against her palms when she trailed her fingers along the stone, a pulse so slow she might have imagined it: one beat, then a silence long enough to doubt, then another.

Edric walked ahead of them with his lantern held high, and though his shoulders were bent with age and shame and three days of hard travel, there was something in his gait now that Silas had not seen before. He moved like a man returning to a house he had built himself. His free hand brushed the moss as he passed, and where it touched, the glow brightened briefly, as if greeting him.

"They planted the first beds above before the palace was a thought," he said, his voice low—not from fear, Silas realized, but reverence. "The founders knew what slept below. Every gardener since has known, in the way you know a thing without knowing it. Why do you think the royal gardens have never once gone fallow? Not through plague, not through war, not through the worst winters in living memory. Something below keeps the roots warm."

Isolde came last, close enough that Silas could hear her breathing, quick and shallow. The princess had said almost nothing since they'd slipped beneath the burning festival, since they'd waded through smoke and screaming to find the hidden gate Edric remembered with a gardener's hands if not a gardener's mind. Now she spoke only once, barely above a whisper.

"I used to dream about this," Isolde said. "When I was small. A warmth under my feet when I walked in the garden. I told my father, and he—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He looked at me like I'd caught him at something."

"He would have," Edric said grimly. "It's been drinking from this warmth for years. Every year a little more. You felt it thinning, child. Even a babe feels a fire going out."

The stair ended.

Not abruptly—the way simply opened, the walls falling back on either side until Silas stood at the mouth of a cavern so vast her eyes refused its edges. The green-glowing moss climbed the far walls in slow rivers, and stalactites hung like the roots of some inverted forest, and everywhere, everywhere, was that heartbeat-warmth, thick as water now, pressing against her skin. In the center of the cavern floor lay a hollow, a basin of smooth black stone worn into being over ages, and in the basin—

A candle-flame.

That was her first thought, and her second, and even her third, because nothing else fit. A single flame no larger than the ones she'd lit in a hundred rented rooms, guttering gold-and-red atop a bed of ash, casting light no farther than a few arm-spans. All that vastness, all that ancient dark, and the last fire of the last dragon burned small enough to cup in two hands.

"Oh," Isolde breathed beside her. "Oh, it's *dying*."

"It's been dying for two hundred years," Edric said. "Slowly. And faster lately." He set his lantern down at the edge of the hollow with the carefulness of a man laying flowers on a grave, and knelt.

Silas did not kneel. She could not have said why, except that kneeling felt like something one did for the dead, and the flame was not dead. As she watched, it leaned—actually leaned, the way a person leans toward a door when they hear footsteps—toward Edric. And it spoke.

"Edric Hale." The voice was a woman's voice, warm and rusted, an old nurse's voice, the kind that has sung ten thousand lullabies. "You cut back the winter roses your first year and wept over the clippings. You were twenty-two and ashamed of weeping. We remember."

Edric made a sound low in his throat. "You remember everything," he managed.

"We were made to remember." The flame swayed upright again, and the voice changed—a man's voice now, deeper, courtly, faintly amused. "Two centuries of gardeners, of kings and kitchen girls who crept down to warm their hands, of children's secrets whispered into the dark because children know a fire will keep them. Every voice that ever spoke in this room lives in us still. Would you like to hear your mother? She came here the night before your wedding, Edric Hale. She was afraid you were marrying wrong."

"*Don't*," Edric said, sharp and cracked, and Silas saw him press the heel of his hand against his eyes.

But Silas had stopped listening to the words. She was watching the flame itself, and understanding was arriving in her the way cold arrives—not all at once but degree by degree, until suddenly it is everywhere and has always been. The dragon did not merely speak in borrowed voices. It *was* borrowed voices. It had fed on memory the way she fed on memory, for centuries, taking in every word spoken in its presence, holding them, keeping them alive when their owners had crumbled to dust. It was not a creature that hoarded gold. It was a creature made entirely of what others had given up without knowing.

*I am looking at what I would become*, she thought, and the thought did not frighten her so much as it steadied her, which frightened her more.

"You are the thief," the dragon said.

The voice was new—or not new. Silas knew it. Her whole body knew it before her mind caught up, and her knees nearly buckled, because it was her mother's voice, the exact timbre of it, the slight roughness at the end of sentences, and she had not heard it in eleven years, and the last time she'd heard it she'd been running away from a burning house with her own gift tearing holes in everything she loved.

"How do you have her voice?" The words came out of Silas ragged. "She never came here. She died on the other side of the country."

"Voices travel," the dragon said gently, in that stolen cadence, and Silas wanted to scream and weep in equal measure. "Travelers speak of what they love. For two hundred years, everyone who descended these stairs brought someone with them in their hearts, and their hearts said those names aloud. Your name is not unknown to us, Silas Kavanagh. Three travelers in the past year spoke of a memory-thief in Thornfell. One of them spoke of you kindly. He owed you money and forgave you the debt the moment he learned what you could do. He thought you were remarkable." A pause, and the flame flickered. "We think you are remarkable."

"Don't flatter me," Silas said. "Don't use her voice to flatter me."

"Then we will use another." The voice shifted—Maren's now, her broker's dry rasp—and somehow that was worse. "Or none." And then it spoke in a register that was no one's voice at all, or perhaps everyone's, a sound like wind through a canyon, like the resonance of the cavern itself. "Listen, thief. We have watched the thing above for years. We felt it the night it was born, feeding on a dying king's last breath, and we have felt it every day since, sipping at our fire through the roots of the garden, growing fat while we grow thin. You have come to kill it. Good. But you cannot kill it with steel. Steel passes through smoke. You cannot kill it with fire alone; it is made of hunger, and hunger eats fire. There is one thing it cannot abide."

"Tell me," Silas said.

"A memory freely given." The great voice softened, and the candle-flame bowed low over its ash. "Understand what we say. Not taken. Not stolen, not sold, not bartered, not pried loose by need or desperation. Given. Freely, wholly, with the giver's whole heart consenting to lose it forever. Such a memory is the opposite of everything the creature is. It is a thing surrendered instead of seized. Fed into our fire, it burns hotter than any fuel, because it carries love in it, and the creature cannot digest love that was given away. It can only take. What is freely relinquished, it can never hold. Do you understand?"

Silas understood. That was the terrible part. She understood instantly, completely, the way she understood the mechanics of her own gift—some knowledge simply arrived whole, and you carried it whether you wanted it or not.

Behind her, Isolde's breath caught. "Freely given," the princess repeated. "What does that mean, practically? What kind of memory?"

"A true one," the dragon said. "And the truest memories are the ones we love most. Anything less and the fire will swallow it and give back nothing. It must be a feast, not a scrap."

"No," Isolde said, and Silas heard her moving forward, heard the rustle of skirts as she dropped to her knees at the edge of the hollow. "No, there has to be another way. Take mine—I have hundreds. My tutors, my dancing lessons, my—"

"The princess's memories are rich," the dragon said, and its voice gentled into something almost sorrowful, "but they are not yet ripe. Love ripens with loss, child. Yours have not yet been tested against absence. Perhaps someday. But the ember will not last to your someday." The flame shrank as it said it, as if to demonstrate, dwindling to scarcely a thumbnail of light. The darkness pressed in. The warmth thinned.

Silas stood very still.

She was thinking about her mother's face. She had been thinking about it since the false king's throne room, since the dungeons, since the moment on the road when Isolde had found her crying in her sleep without knowing why—but now she thought about it deliberately, turning it over the way she turned stolen memories when she weighed their price. Eleven years she had kept it. Through hunger and cold and the hunters' fires, through every bargain and betrayal, she had guarded that single recollection the way misers guard their last coin: her mother's face, seen across a sunlit kitchen, laughing at something Silas had said when she was six. She did not remember the joke. She did not remember the day, or the season, or what came after. Only the face, and the laughter, and the light. It was the smallest memory she owned and the largest thing in her life, and she had told herself a thousand times that it was the last piece of the person she had been before she became a thief—that as long as she kept it, she could be found again.

*If I give it away*, she thought, *there is nothing left to find.*

"Thief," the dragon said, and now it used no one's voice at all, only that vast resonant hollowness. "We will not ask it of you. Hear us clearly. We have absorbed ten thousand confessions in this dark, and we know what a heart is worth, and we know that a thing demanded is worth nothing, and a thing offered in despair curdles in the fire. If you give, you must choose it as a bride chooses, freely, on an open road, with your eyes open to the cost. And you may refuse. You may turn around and climb those stairs and die with your mother's face intact, and no one—no one—will have the right to call you coward. Do you hear us?"

"I hear you," Silas said.

Her voice sounded strange to her. Calm. Far away. Somewhere behind her Isolde was saying her name, saying it again, grabbing her sleeve, but the words reached Silas as if through deep water. She was looking at the little flame, and the little flame was looking back—it had eyes; she hadn't noticed before; two points of darker gold within the fire, ancient and exhausted and unbearably kind—and she felt the same vertigo she'd felt the first time she'd ever lifted a memory, fourteen years old and desperate, realizing that the world was full of doors other people didn't know they carried.

Except this time she was standing in front of her own door.

"There's something else," Silas said slowly. "Isn't there? You're telling me the rules, but you haven't told me why you called me the thief. Not Edric, not the princess. Me. You've been speaking to me this whole time."

The fire flickered. For a long moment the cavern held only the slow pulse of its dying warmth, one beat, silence, beat. Then:

"Because the memory must be given to the fire," the dragon said, "and there is only one pair of hands in this world that knows how to move a memory from a mind to elsewhere." A pause. "Ours is old craft, thief, older than your gift, and nearly forgotten. But the giving must pass through a taker. The memory must be lifted—freely, willingly, with the owner's whole consent—and poured into the ember by the only one among you with the hands to pour it. You are not merely the sacrifice, Silas Kavanagh. You are the altar."

The words settled over her like snowfall, soft and total.

She thought of every memory she had ever taken. The smuggler's mountain dawn. The courier's sealed vial. The jailer's daughter, whom she had erased and then restored. Hundreds of small lives, lifted and sold and scattered. And now the last theft of her life would be the one theft that was not a theft at all—would be a gift passing through a thief's hands, transmuted by the journey, made clean or destroyed by it. She would feel her mother's face leave her. She would be the one to carry it to the fire. She would know, in the final instant, exactly what she was losing, because feeling it go was part of the giving.

"That's why it has to be me," she said. Not a question.

"That is why it could only ever be you," the dragon answered, and the flame leaned toward her, and in its borrowed voices—in her mother's voice, in Maren's, in a hundred dead gardeners' voices murmuring beneath—they all seemed to say it together: "We are sorry."

* * *

They camped in the cavern, because none of them could bear to climb back into the burning world that same night, and because the dragon asked them—with Edric's mother's voice, apologetically—to stay a while and talk, so that it could fill its last hours with living voices instead of remembered ones. So they talked. Edric told it about the gardens he'd tended in exile, the window boxes and grave-plots and one stubborn rooftop in Vess. Isolde told it about her father—not the false king, but the man from before, the one who had taught her the names of trees—and the dragon listened and said, in the old king's own voice, "He practiced that speech about your birthday for a week. He was terrified you'd laugh." Isolde put her face in her hands and laughed and cried at once.

Silas sat apart, near the hollow, and watched the flame breathe.

Once, deep in the night-watch, the dragon spoke to her alone, quietly, in a voice she didn't recognize—an old woman's, cracked and sweet.

"In the morning," it said, "when you choose, we want you to know what we see. We see a girl who took and took because the world took from her first. We see that every memory you kept of others you kept better than they kept it themselves. You are not a thief, Silas Kavanagh. You are a keeper who never learned she was allowed to keep anything of her own. Whatever you decide—we will hold your mother's face as carefully as you have. We promise you that. Fires keep things. It is what we are for."

Silas looked at the little golden light, at its tired ancient eyes, and found that she could not answer. She nodded instead. And the dragon, in her mother's voice, so softly it was almost not a sound at all, sang four bars of a lullaby Silas had not heard in eleven years and had believed forgotten—and the song was not stolen, and it was not sold, and it sat in Silas's chest like a coal as the long night wore down toward the hour when she would have to decide what she was willing to burn.

Above them, muffled through a hundred yards of stone, the festival fires still crackled, and the false king still wore his borrowed face, and somewhere in the palace dark the creature had begun, at last, to notice that its dinner had slipped out of its hands—and was coming down.
