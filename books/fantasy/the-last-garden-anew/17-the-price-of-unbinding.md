## 17. The Price of Unbinding

Isolde had imagined the last dragon a hundred times in her childhood—in the fever-dreams that followed her father's death-in-all-but-name, in the stories Edric told when he thought she was asleep among the topiary. She had imagined it vast and terrible, wings like storm-fronts, a voice that could crack mountains.

She had not imagined something so small.

The cavern beneath the royal gardens opened around them like the cupped hands of the earth itself, ribbed with pale stone that might once have been bone. Roots hung from the ceiling in slow, breathing curtains, and every one of them glowed faintly—not green, but gold, the color of banked coals, the color of the light Isolde remembered from her earliest years, before everything went wrong. And there at the center, curled into a hollow worn smooth by centuries of its own sleeping, lay the dragon.

It was no larger than a plow horse. Its scales had gone dull as old pewter, cracked along the flanks, and between two of those scales, just above where a heart would beat, burned an ember no bigger than a thumbnail. It pulsed. It dimmed. It pulsed again, slower each time, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

The dragon raised its head with an effort that seemed to cost it a year of life, and when it spoke, it did not speak with one voice.

It spoke with her mother's.

*Come closer, little one,* said Queen Marenne, dead these eleven years, her voice pouring out of that ruined throat warm as summer. *You've grown so tall.*

Isolde's knees nearly gave. She felt Edric's hand close on her shoulder—steady, calloused, real—and she gripped it like a drowning woman grips driftwood.

"It does that," Silas said quietly. Her face was gray in the ember-light. "Borrowed voices. I should have warned you."

"You *should* have," Isolde managed, and hated how her voice shook. "How many? How many voices does it carry?"

"Centuries' worth," Silas said. "Maybe more. Every memory that ever came near this fire." She paused. "I think—I think that's what it *is*. A creature made of remembering."

The dragon's eyes were the only living thing about it: deep amber wells, and within them, images moved. Isolde caught glimpses—a harvest festival, a coronation, a woman's laughing face, a boy running through wheat—memories swimming in the ancient gaze like fish beneath ice.

*I know why you've come,* said the dragon, and now the voice was Edric's, young Edric, the gardener he had been before disgrace bent him. The old man flinched as if struck. *You've come because the fire is dying, and you know what dies with it.*

"The creature above," Edric said hoarsely. "The thing wearing the king's face. If your fire goes out—"

*Then nothing in this world can unmake it.* The dragon lowered its great head onto its forelegs with a sound like stones settling. *I have held that thing back for eleven years. Do you understand what I am telling you, gardener? Eleven years I have burned against it through the roots of this garden, through every rose and rowan planted over my sleep, and every year it has drunk deeper. It fed on the king while I slept too soundly. It feeds on my fire even now, slowly, patiently, the way rot feeds on a tree from inside. When the last ember dies, it will be beyond all flame forever. It will wear its stolen shape until the world ends, and nothing you love will ever be safe from it again.*

The words fell into the cavern and lay there heavy as the hanging roots.

Silas stepped forward. Of course she did—she always did, that reckless thief with her careful rules, walking toward danger the way other people walked away from it. "Then tell us how to feed you. How to make the fire blaze. There has to be a way—you were fed once, weren't you? Someone tended you."

*Tended,* the dragon repeated, and laughed—a strange, layered sound, a dozen voices laughing slightly out of time with one another. *Yes. Tended. Fed. You have already guessed, haven't you, memory-thief. You looked into me the moment we met, and you already know the answer, and you are praying I will give you a different one.*

"I don't pray," Silas said. But her hand had drifted to her chest, unconsciously, the way Isolde had seen her do whenever something touched the hollow places inside her—the places where memories used to be.

*Then listen anyway,* said the dragon, and its voice changed again, became something older, deeper, a voice like tectonic plates grinding—its true voice, perhaps, or the closest it could come. *I was born of memory and fire both. The first dragons were the world's rememberers. What mortals forgot, we kept; what they loved, we burned bright with. My fire is not ordinary flame. It is the flame of what matters. It cannot be fed wood or coal or flesh. It can be fed only one thing.*

The ember above its heart pulsed once, twice.

*A memory of true love. Freely given. Surrendered wholly—not shared, not copied, not half-held with one hand while the other clutches. Given away so completely that the giver wakes tomorrow and does not know what they lost, only that something is missing. Only such a gift burns clean enough to drive that creature out of existence. Anything less, and it merely sips, and grows stronger, and waits.*

The silence afterward was absolute. Even the dripping water somewhere in the dark seemed to hold itself still.

Isolde watched Silas's face, and watched the blood leave it.

"No," Isolde said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, flat and final, a door slamming. "No. There's another way. There's always another way."

*Princess,* the dragon said gently, in her mother's voice again, which felt like a cruelty, though Isolde suspected it was the opposite—an offering of comfort, badly aimed. *There is not.*

"There must be. Memories of true love—" Isolde turned, desperate, grasping. "Edric. Your garden. You loved this place, didn't you? Thirty years you—"

Edric shook his head slowly. His face in the ember-light was carved from grief and granite. "I loved it, aye. But it isn't what he means, is it?" He looked at the dragon. "True love. Not craft. Not devotion. Not duty dressed up pretty."

*The fire knows the difference,* the dragon said. *Believe me. In the long centuries I have been offered much. Kings have offered me their crowns. Lovers have offered me their vows. Soldiers have offered me their honors. All of it ash before it touched the ember. Love freely given is rarer than you dream, child, and rarer still is the person who holds one and can bear to let it go.*

Isolde's eyes went back to Silas.

Silas was very still. Stillness, Isolde had learned, was what Silas did instead of screaming. She had seen it on the Lethe riverbank, after she tore the hunter's mind open—had seen the moment the exhilaration curdled and the stillness came down like frost. She saw it now, total and terrible.

"There's another way," Isolde said again, and crossed the distance between them, and took Silas's cold hands in hers. "Listen to me. We'll find something else. Some other memory—"

"You heard it." Silas's voice was steady. That was somehow worse than tears. "Love freely given. Nothing else burns clean enough. And Isolde—" a ghost of her crooked, defensive smile, there and gone, "—look at what I am. Look at the inventory of my heart. I have spent my whole life taking. I've got smugglers' first kisses and farmers' last harvests and a jailer's daughter rattling around in my skull. I've got a dying courier's terror and half a hundred strangers' sundays. Do you know what I don't have?"

"Don't," Isolde whispered.

"One thing." Silas's hands tightened on hers, brief and fierce. "One true thing. One memory I didn't steal. My mother's face. I stole it myself—from myself—years ago, because remembering her hurt too much to carry and forgetting her hurt just enough to survive. It's the only memory I own outright. The only thing in me that was ever truly *mine*, freely kept." Her breath shuddered, just once. "And apparently the only thing I have that's worth anything to a dying god of fire."

"That's exactly why you can't," Isolde said fiercely. "Don't you see? It's all you have. It's *you*. You told me—you told me you don't even remember why you run, only that you must. That memory is the last thread connecting you to whoever you were before all this. Give it up and—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated being the princess who cried, but she couldn't stop. "Give it up and there's nothing left of Silas Kavanagh but the taking."

For a moment something moved behind Silas's eyes—something raw and young and frightened, the girl under all the armor of cleverness. Then it was gone, shuttered away.

"Maybe," Silas said, "that's not the worst trade I've ever made."

"It *is*. It is the worst—"

"Isolde." Silas said her name like a hand laid flat on a table. "Look at me. Really look. What is my mother's face doing for me right now? Can I picture it? Can I tell you the color of her eyes? Whether she smiled with her teeth? Whether she sang?" Silas's jaw worked. "I can't. I've been carrying a locked box for years and telling myself the box mattered more than what was in it. At least if I give it to the fire, it stops being locked. It becomes light. It becomes the thing that saves everyone I—" She stopped herself. Isolde watched her stop herself, and understood what word had been coming, and felt her heart turn over.

"Don't you dare decide for me," Isolde said, "that I'd rather have a world than have *you* whole."

Silas stared at her. The ember-light caught the wet on her lashes before she blinked it away. "That's the trouble with loving people," she said softly. "They start thinking their wholeness belongs to them."

*She is wiser than she knows,* the dragon murmured, in a voice Isolde didn't recognize—a man's voice, warm, unfamiliar, some farmer or king from five hundred years dead. *And so are you, daughter of the false king.*

Isolde turned on it. "I'm not his daughter."

*Are you certain?* The amber eyes swam with moving images. *You carry his blood. You carry his nose, his temper, his stubbornness. And yet you would burn him from the world. Tell me—that is not also a kind of love? To love what someone should have been, hard enough to destroy what they are?*

"He's not my father. He's a *thing* wearing my father."

*Yes,* the dragon agreed, simply. *And still you grieve him every day. I taste it in you. Grief is love with nowhere to go, little princess, and yours has been going nowhere for eleven years.* The great head shifted; scales whispered against stone. *But hear me now, all three of you, because the hour is short and I will not say this twice.*

The cavern seemed to lean in. Even the glowing roots stilled.

*When the gift is given—if it is given—it cannot be taken back. No power under heaven restores a memory surrendered to my fire. The creature will promise otherwise; it lies as it breathes. Understand also this: the fire will blaze for a season, a year, perhaps two, and then it will begin to fade again unless fed again. This is the covenant the first gardeners accepted. This is the price that has been paid before.* The ember flickered. *Once. Long ago. By a woman who gave me her wedding day, and walked out of this cave smiling, and lived a long and happy life without knowing what she had lost. It can be borne. I have seen it borne.*

"But never undone," Edric said quietly.

*Never undone.*

Edric came to stand beside them, and Isolde realized the old man was crying—silent, unembarrassed tears cutting tracks through the dust on his face. "I brought you here," he said to Silas. "Both of you. I dug up my shame and my guilt and marched you across half a kingdom to this, and now I find I'd take it all back if I could. Every step."

"You wouldn't," Silas said.

"No," Edric admitted. "I wouldn't. Damn me, I wouldn't." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a laborer clearing sweat. "But hear an old man's counsel, girl. The dragon says freely given. Freely means freely. If anyone pushes you down that slope—if you leap because we're watching, because it seems noble, because you think it's the price of belonging—then the fire won't take it clean. Half-given gifts sour. So whatever you choose, choose it alone, and choose it true, and let no one—including yourself—call it owed."

Isolde wanted to argue. Wanted to seize Silas by the shoulders and shake her, wanted to offer her own memories in exchange—her mother's lullabies, her father's true face from before, anything, everything—but the dragon's warning sat in her chest like swallowed stone. *Freely means freely.* She could not give what was not asked for. She could only stand there and watch the person she— 

The word arrived unbidden and landed like a bell struck in an empty church.

The person she loved.

She loved Silas Kavanagh. Thief, liar, keeper of a hundred borrowed lives. She loved her crooked smile and her terrible ethics and the way she went still instead of screaming, and she loved her most of all right now, standing in a dying god's cradle, calmly pricing her own soul against the world.

And love, the dragon said, demanded sacrifice it could not bear to ask for. Well. Here it stood, asking anyway, by saying nothing at all.

Above them, muffled through fathoms of earth and root, the festival music began. Drums. Pipes. The false king's celebration of the hunt, of the burning, of a kingdom's fear wearing festival colors. The sound reached even here, thin and obscene, and the ember above the dragon's heart stuttered—dimmed—as if the creature's hunger had found a new vein to drink.

The dragon groaned, a sound of stone and sorrow.

*It feels me weakening,* it said. *It will come soon. Tonight, perhaps. Tomorrow. It wants to be present at the death, to drink the last ember as it gutters, and finish its work forever.* The amber eyes fixed on Silas. *So decide quickly, memory-thief. Decide honestly. And know that whichever way you choose, I will not judge you. I have watched this world a long time. I know how much it asks, and how little it gives back.*

Silas closed her eyes.

Isolde watched her—watched the play of thought behind the shut lids, watched her fingers curl and uncurl at her sides like a woman feeling for handholds on a cliff face. And in that watching Isolde made herself a silent vow, fierce and probably foolish: whatever Silas chose, Isolde would stay. Whole or hollowed, remembering or bereft, Isolde would plant herself beside this woman like a tree, and be the memory she could not keep. If Silas surrendered her mother's face, then Isolde would carry it for her—would ask her, tonight, every question, learn every detail and hold them safe, so that something of the gift survived somewhere outside the fire.

It wasn't enough. It wasn't fair. But it was what love could do, when it was forbidden to do more.

Silas opened her eyes.

They were clear. Terribly, terribly clear.

"All right," she said. "Tell me how it's done."

"No—" Isolde's protest tore out of her—

And then the drums above stopped. All at once, mid-beat, the way a heart stops.

In the silence that followed, far above, faint as a whisper through stone, they heard the gates of the royal garden begin to open.
