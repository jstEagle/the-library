## 18. The Creature Ascendant

The ember was dying.

Silas could feel it the way she felt a memory slipping through her fingers—not with her ears or eyes but somewhere deeper, in the hollow behind her ribs where she kept everything she'd ever stolen. The dragon's fire had burned down to something smaller than a candle now. It sat in the center of the vast underground cavern like a coal in a dead hearth, and around it the last dragon lay coiled, its scales gone gray as old ash, its breathing so slow that minutes passed between each rise of its enormous ribs.

Edric had knelt beside it for an hour, one weathered hand resting on its flank, murmuring things Silas couldn't hear—perhaps names, perhaps apologies. Isolde stood at the cavern's edge, where the garden's roots hung through the ceiling like pale veins, her sword drawn though there was nothing yet to fight.

It was Isolde who heard it first.

"Footsteps," she said. "Above. Many."

And then the light came—not lamplight, not torchlight, but a cold white radiance pouring down through the roots and the ancient stone stairwell Edric had opened for them, radiance without warmth, the light of something that remembered what light looked like but had never once been lit from within.

The false king descended.

He came slowly, unhurried, in the manner of someone entering their own home. His robes were white shot with gold thread, immaculate despite the climb, and his face—the king's face, the face Silas had seen die in Edric's stolen memories—wore an expression of gentle sorrow, as if he were a father arriving too late to stop his children from breaking something precious.

Behind him came hunters. A dozen at least, filling the stairwell, their iron brands glowing, their faces blank in that particular way Silas knew too well: the emptiness of people whose fear had been harvested down to nothing.

Isolde moved to stand between them and her father—or the thing that wore him—and raised her blade. "Stay back."

"Isolde." The creature said her name the way a collector says the name of a piece he has finally tracked down. "You have your mother's stubbornness. I told you that once, when you were small. Do you remember? In the orchard, when you wouldn't come down from the tree."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the word. "Don't use his voice for that."

"I have all of his voice," the creature said mildly. "All of his memories. Every walk in every garden, every lullaby, every disappointment. I wore them so long I sometimes forget they were ever anyone else's." He spread his hands, and the gesture was so perfectly, heartbreakingly human that Silas felt her stomach turn. "I am not here for violence. Put the sword down, child. I am here to make an offer."

* * *

The offer was made to Silas, not to Isolde—that was the first surprise. The creature's gaze slid past the princess and the old gardener as though they were furniture, and settled on her with an attention that felt like cold water poured down the back of her neck.

"You are the thief," it said. "The one who lifts what others carry. I know your kind. I *am* your kind, if we are honest—only I take whole lives where you take hours."

"I'm nothing like you."

"No?" It smiled, and the smile was the king's smile, warm and rueful, the smile of a man who had once forgiven his gardener a ruined rosebed. That was the cruelest thing about it, Silas thought. It didn't wear the king's face badly. It wore it perfectly. "You sell memories to brokers. You've emptied frightened people to fill your purse. You took a jailer's daughter from him and sold her like a trinket." It watched her flinch. "Yes. I see everything that happens beneath my gardens. I have watched you since Thornfell, little thief. You interest me."

Isolde stepped closer to Silas, shoulder almost touching hers. "Whatever you're offering, she won't take it."

"I haven't offered it yet." The creature turned its head—a smooth, fluid motion, a hair too fluid—and looked at the dying dragon. At the ember. Its expression shifted then, and for just a moment the mask slipped: beneath the king's kindly features Silas saw something move, something like smoke behind glass, something hungry pressing outward against the shape it wore. "Do you know how long I have sustained myself on this fire? Centuries. I found this dragon when it was already old, already sleeping, already burning low. And I learned that if I fed slowly enough—if I drank the fire the way a tick drinks blood—the flame never quite died, and neither did I. But slow feeding is thin living. I have been hungry for three hundred years." The king's voice dropped lower, and now it was not the king's voice at all but many voices braided together, a choir of everyone it had ever consumed. "This is the last night of my hunger. One way or another."

"The fire dies," Edric said quietly. He hadn't risen from the dragon's side. His hand stayed on its flank. "Or you do."

"The fire dies," the creature agreed, "unless it is fed. And here is my offer, thief, and I will make it only once, because unlike you I keep no ledger and tell no lies I cannot keep." It raised one hand, and in its palm—impossible, obscene—light gathered. Not fire-light. Memory-light. The soft gold glow that Silas had seen a thousand times rising from open vials in Maren's shop, from the minds of travelers on mountain passes, from her own hands when she worked.

Her breath stopped.

She knew that light. She knew its color, its weight, its particular shimmer, the way a musician knows one voice in a crowd. She had carried it for fifteen years in the deepest vault of herself, wrapped in layers of forgetting, the one theft she had committed against her own life because losing it entirely would have meant losing the reason she'd become what she was.

"You cannot," she whispered.

"I hold more memories than any broker who ever lived. I hold a kingdom's worth. Somewhere in me is a young woman kneeling in a garden in spring, laughing, tucking a sprig of some flowering weed behind her daughter's ear." The creature spoke gently now, terribly gently. "Somewhere in me is her face."

The cavern tilted. Or perhaps Silas tilted. Isolde's hand closed over her arm—warm, real, anchoring—but even that warmth seemed far away, across a great distance, because the creature had spoken the words she had spent fifteen years not letting herself think.

She couldn't remember her mother's face.

That was the truth she had built her entire self around avoiding. She knew she had loved her. She knew her mother had died when Silas was nine, and that grief had been so vast, so unbearable, that Silas—who was already learning, even then, what her gift could do—had reached into her own mind and lifted the memory out. All of it. The face, the voice, the smell of her, the weight of her arms. She had sealed it away because she could not bear it, and in doing so she had saved herself and unmade herself in the same motion. Everything since—all the stealing, all the rules, all the careful invisibility—had been the architecture of a girl trying to build a life around a hole shaped like her mother.

And the thing wearing the king's face was telling her it could give the hole its shape back.

"You want the dragon," Silas said. Her voice sounded strange to her, scraped thin. "Say it plainly."

"Plainly, then." The creature gestured at the dying fire with something close to reverence. "Let me feed. Let me take what remains of this old flame into myself, and let me take its shape—dragon-fire instead of borrowed flesh, forever, unkillable, beyond your hunters and your princesses and your little rebellions. Stand aside. Hold your friends' hands and look away if you must. Do that, and I will reach into myself and pull out your mother entire. Every laugh. Every morning. Her face, restored to you, yours again for the rest of your life." It extended the handful of golden light toward her. "Freely given. No theft. The one thing I can offer that no broker ever will."

* * *

Silas looked at the light in the creature's palm, and she wanted it.

There was no point pretending otherwise, not even inside her own head. She wanted it the way the drowning want air, the way the starving want bread. Fifteen years of hollow mornings. Fifteen years of seeing other people's mothers in market squares and having to look away before the ache showed on her face. Fifteen years of carrying other people's most treasured memories—weddings, first loves, children's first steps—and knowing that somewhere in her own skull, locked away, was the single treasure that outweighed them all, unreachable even to her.

The creature knew exactly what it was offering. That was the horror of it. It had watched her, it said, since Thornfell. It had studied her the way she studied travelers. It had found the vault, found the seal, found the wound, and it pressed its thumb directly onto the center of the hurt.

"Silas." Isolde's voice, low, urgent. "Look at me. Don't listen to it."

"She is listening to me," the creature said. "That is only honest. Let her listen. I am not afraid of the truth, princess. Are you?"

"Silas." Edric had risen now, ash-gray and steady, standing between her and the dragon. "Whatever it shows you—it's showing you what it holds. And everything it holds, it *took*. Think, girl. You know the trade better than any of us. What does a taken memory cost to give back?"

And that—

That was the question.

Silas felt it land in her like a stone dropping through still water, and she made herself think it through, made herself be the professional she had been for fifteen years, because her craft was the one clear thing left in the roaring fog of longing.

Memories did not survive theft unchanged. She had known this since her earliest days with Maren. A memory pulled out of the mind that lived it lost something vital—the warmth of context, the web of connections to everything around it. Sold memories played flat, like music heard through a wall. The brokers' customers paid for the experience, but the customers always came back faintly disappointed, always chasing something the vials never quite delivered. Because a memory wasn't a picture. It was a room, and the picture was only the window.

And the creature held her mother's memory the way brokers held vials. Taken. Sealed. Carried for centuries in the dark belly of a thing that consumed everything it touched. Even if it gave the memory back—even if it truly meant to, even if such a thing could keep a promise—what would she receive?

A face. Flat and cold. A window without a room.

She would look at her mother's face and feel the way the buyers felt: *almost*. Almost love. Almost her. A stranger wearing the most beloved face in the world, and the terrible knowledge, after, that she had traded away the last ember of the last dragon for it.

"It can't give it back to me," Silas said slowly. "Not really. Can it."

"Not really," Edric agreed.

"It doesn't understand that, does it? It thinks memories are *things*. Coins. It's been eating them for three hundred years and it still thinks they're coins." Something was rising in her—not hope exactly, but clarity, hard and bright as frost. She turned to face the creature fully. "You can't give what you only take."

The king's face went very still.

"That's the difference between us," Silas said, and her voice grew stronger with each word, finding ground beneath itself. "When I steal, I know I'm taking something alive out of a life. I've hidden from that truth my whole career, but I've always *known* it. You don't know it. You've eaten a king and you think you're a king. You've eaten ten thousand lives and you think you're full. But you're not full—you're the hungriest thing under this mountain, and you'll be hungry after tonight too, whatever shape you take, because hunger is all you are. A shape with nothing inside it. A face with nobody home."

"Careful, thief."

"And your gift—" she pointed at the golden light in its palm, and her hand did not shake, which surprised her— "is worthless. You're offering me a corpse. My mother's memory, pickled in your hunger, drained of everything that made it hers. You could pour it into my hands and I'd feel nothing, because everything I'd feel would be mine, not hers, and the whole point—the whole *point*—" Her voice broke, and she let it. "The whole point was *her*. Not the picture of her. Her."

For a long moment, silence. The ember pulsed faintly, dimmer than before. The dragon's breathing had slowed further; Silas could barely see its ribs move.

Then the false king laughed.

It was the king's laugh—deep, warm, rolling—and hearing it come out of the thing was worse than anything it had said. "Oh, well argued. Well argued indeed, little thief. But you misunderstand the nature of my offer. I did not say I would give you the memory I hold." The golden light in its palm began to grow. "I said I would give you *her face*. And I can make a new memory. I have made thousands. I have worn a king so long I remember being crowned, and I was never crowned. I can craft your mother entire—every laugh, every morning, every detail polished brighter than the original ever was. Would you like that? A perfect mother, better than real? You'll never know the difference. Isn't that what your whole life has been—trading true things for comfortable ones?"

And there it was. The knife beneath the silk.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question she had run from across half a kingdom. If the counterfeit was perfect, what was the true thing worth? If she couldn't remember why the original mattered, why refuse the copy?

Silas looked at the light. She let herself want it, wholly, honestly—one last time. She let herself imagine the garden in spring, the laugh, the sprig of flowering weed tucked behind her ear by hands she could not picture. She imagined having it back and weeping and being, finally, finished.

Then she looked at the dragon.

Gray, ancient, nearly gone. Three hundred years it had endured this parasite drinking its fire, holding the last flame of its kind alive through sheer stubbornness, waiting—for what, Silas wondered, for whom?—in the dark beneath a garden built by hands that had loved it. Edric's hand still rested on its flank. Isolde stood with her sword up and her jaw set, ready to die on this dirt floor for a woman she'd known for weeks, because that was what Isolde did: she gave things freely. Trust, loyalty, her name, her claim. Everything the creature could only steal, Isolde gave away like water.

And Silas understood.

Not reasoned—understood, the way you understand fire is hot or winter is long. The thing could not give, because giving requires having something you are willing to lose. It held ten thousand memories and would surrender none, not even to buy the dragon's death cheaply; it offered her mother's face the way a gambler offers chips, certain it could win them back. Everything it possessed, it possessed as a theft. And a life made entirely of thefts was a life with nothing inside it—no wonder it was hungry. No wonder it had been hungry for three hundred years and would be hungry forever. It had mistaken consumption for living so long ago that it no longer knew there was a difference.

"You can't give what you only take," Silas said again, softly now. "And I'm done taking."

She reached up—not toward the creature's offered light, but to the chain at her own throat, where, hidden against her skin for fifteen years, she had kept the one vial she had never sold. Not her mother's. She had drunk that memory into herself long ago; there was no vial for it. This was older. Smaller. A splinter of memory she'd lifted from her own mind the same terrible night, the fragment she'd judged too dangerous to keep and too sacred to destroy: the sound of her mother's voice saying her name. Just that. Four syllables. She had carried it unopened because some part of her had always known that the day she finally listened to it would be the day she chose what kind of person she was.

The false king's smile faded. Behind the king's eyes, the smoke pressed hard against the glass. "*Thief.*"

"My name," Silas said, uncorking the vial, "is Silas Kavanagh. My mother gave it to me freely, and I'm going to need it for what comes next."

The ember flickered. The dragon stirred—just slightly, just one vast eyelid sliding open, one ancient eye reflecting a girl with a vial in her hands—and somewhere above them, muffled by earth and root and stone, the festival bells of the city began to ring.

The creature descended the final steps.

"*No,*" it said, in ten thousand stolen voices at once, and lunged.
