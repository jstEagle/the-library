## 19. Feed the Fire

The dragon's chamber was not a chamber at all. It was a wound in the world, a hollow beneath the royal gardens where the roots of ancient oaks had grown down through stone for centuries, braiding themselves into ribs around the sleeping shape within. The air tasted of cinders and rain and something older than either—something like the smell of a hearth remembered from childhood, from a house that no longer existed.

Silas stood at the edge of the last light.

It was barely a light at all. In the center of the hollow, coiled among the root-ribs like a dying star fallen into a nest, the dragon breathed. Each breath was a flicker: an ember no larger than a candle-flame, guttering in a hollow of blackened scales the size of shields. The fire that had once been visible from every window of the capital—the fire that kings had sworn oaths beside and gardeners had tended with offerings of rosemary and rainwater—was now this. A spark. A held breath. A thing that could end between one heartbeat and the next.

*You have come,* said the dragon.

Its voice did not come from its throat. It came from everywhere—from the roots, from the stone, from the ember itself—and it was never the same voice twice. This time it spoke with the cadence of an old woman selling apples in a Thornfell market; the next breath it was a soldier reciting drill-orders; then a child laughing; then Edric, young, saying *the roses need pruning before the frost.* The dragon had spent centuries absorbing what drifted down to it—memories shed like leaves, prayers whispered into garden soil, the last words of the dying—and it wore them all the way Silas wore stolen faces.

Behind her, far above, the sounds of the burning festival still reached them even here, muffled by earth and stone: bells rung wrong, screams thinned by distance, the roar of fires lit for joy or for murder—it was impossible to say which. Somewhere up there, Isolde was holding the gate-line against hunters who had followed them into the garden. Somewhere up there, Edric stood before the garden doors with a pruning knife and forty years of grief, refusing to move.

And somewhere above all of that, coming down, unhurried as rot, was the thing that wore her king's face.

*You carry something for me,* the dragon said—or perhaps it was the apple-seller who said it, or the child. *No. Not for me. For the fire.*

"I know," Silas said. Her voice sounded small in the vast dark. "The creature can only be unbound by a memory freely given. You told me that already."

*I told you the shape of it. Not the weight.* The ember pulsed, dimmed, pulsed again. *Come closer, thief. Let me see what you would burn.*

Silas crossed the hollow. Her boots crunched on scales that had gone grey and cold as slag, and each step felt like walking on the bones of something that had once been magnificent and knew it. When she reached the sleeping head—a head large enough that she could have stood inside its open mouth, when there had been a mouth worth opening—she knelt.

The eye opened. It was the size of a cartwheel and nearly without color now, a milky white shot through with one dying thread of gold, like the last vein of ore in a mined-out mountain. In it she saw her own reflection, small and smudged, and behind her reflection she saw everything else the eye had ever seen: processions, coronations, the first king planting an oak with his bare hands, smoke, chains, centuries.

*Ah,* the dragon said, and its voice was suddenly, terribly gentle. It spoke with her mother's voice.

Silas flinched as if struck.

*Forgive me. I forget my manners after so long alone. But you brought it with you, pressed so close to your heart that it warms the whole of you. How could I not recognize it?*

"Don't," Silas whispered. "Don't use that voice."

*It is not using, child. It is keeping. There is a difference, though I know you will not believe that tonight.* The great eye regarded her. *You have carried this memory for eleven years. You took it yourself—not from another mind, but from your own. Do you know how rare that is? To steal from oneself? Most people would rather die than forget their mother's face.*

"She died badly." Silas heard her own voice go flat, the flatness she used at markets when she haggled over memories she didn't want to feel. "She burned. In the first purges, before the hunters got organized, before anyone thought to make a trade of it. I watched. I was nine years old. And afterward I couldn't sleep, couldn't eat, couldn't do anything except see her face in the fire, so I—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I took it out. Folded it small. Put it away where I wouldn't find it unless I went looking."

*And you went looking,* the dragon said. *Every night. Every night for eleven years you went looking, because the pain of holding it was smaller than the pain of losing it.*

Silas didn't answer. There was nothing to answer. It was true, and they both knew it, and the truth sat between them on the cold scales like a third presence.

Above them, faintly, a bell stopped ringing. Then another. The silence that followed was worse than the noise.

"They're here," Silas said. "The creature. It's coming down."

*Yes. I can taste it. Smoke and hunger.* The ember shuddered. *Listen to me now, thief, because we have little time, and I am old, and old things ramble. You think you understand the bargain. You give me the memory; the fire feeds; the fire burns the creature. That is true, as far as it goes. But you should understand what you are giving. Not the memory itself—I will take that whether you hand it gently or clutch it, though only a free hand opens the door. What you are giving is the version of yourself that kept it.*

"What does that mean?"

*It means,* said the dragon, in a voice that might have been her own at nine years old, thin and terrified and watching, *that you have built a house around that wound. Eleven years of rooms. Every memory you ever stole, every life you ever sold, every wall you put between yourself and other people—all of it was masonry around this one warm stone. Take the stone out and the house does not fall. But it changes shape. You will be someone new standing in the same bones.*

"Good," Silas said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much she meant it. "I'd like to be someone new."

*Would you?* The dragon's laugh was a crackle, a spark jumping a gap. *Then why do your hands shake?*

Silas looked down. Her hands were shaking. She folded them together and held them still by force.

Because it wasn't only fear. It was the other thing, the thing she had admitted to Isolde two nights ago in the dark of the garden paths while the festival-fires painted the clouds orange above them: that her mother's face was the last true thing she owned. Everything else she had ever possessed had been taken from someone else. Even her name was half-borrowed. But her mother's face—that was hers, earned in blood and fire, paid for at nine years old, hoarded through eleven years of hunger and flight and loneliness. It was the proof that she had once been loved by someone who owed her nothing. If she gave it up, what remained?

A woman made entirely of other people's leavings.

*That is not what remains,* the dragon said, reading her as easily as she read smugglers. *But you will not believe me until you do it. No one ever believes me until they do it. Come. Kneel closer. The fire is polite, but it is also dying, and dying things cannot wait on ceremony.*

Silas leaned forward until she was close enough to feel the heat—or what remained of heat, a warmth like a hand held near a candle rather than over it. The ember lay in the hollow of the dragon's breast, between two plates of scale, and it was so small. She thought of the stories: the fire that had lit the coronation of the first queen, the fire that had melted the chains of the Bonded Wars, the fire that kings had claimed and courts had coveted and hunters had dreamed of extinguishing. All of it reduced to this. A spark in a dying chest, fed for years upon by a creature that drank without ever warming anything.

She understood, suddenly, with terrible clarity, why the false king had let it live. Why not snuff it out? Because the creature could not make fire. It could only take shapes, wear faces, drink what others made. The dragon-fire was its larder, its wine-cellar, its slow meal stretched across decades. Kill the dragon outright and the meal ended. Keep it starving, keep it guttering, and the feast lasted forever.

"You've been letting it feed on you," she said aloud. "All these years. You could have burned it any time. You chose to starve instead."

*Chose,* the dragon echoed, amused. *Or feared. Or hoped someone would come. Old things are not wiser than young things, thief. We are only more patient with our mistakes.* The milky eye turned fully toward her. *But you came. So. Will you give it freely? Say it plainly. The fire must hear the words. It is simple-minded, fire. It understands only yes and no and love.*

Silas closed her eyes.

And there it was, exactly where it had always been, folded small behind a door she had built herself: her mother's face. Not the face from the fire—she had been careful about that, even in her desperation, careful to keep the burning separate—but the face from before. Sunday mornings. Flour on her hands. The way she hummed when she braided Silas's hair, off-key, unashamed. The exact angle of the smile she saved only for her daughter, crooked on the left side because a fever in her own childhood had left the muscles uneven, and how Silas had loved that crookedness more than any symmetrical beauty in the world. The voice saying her name—not *girl*, not *thief*, but *Silas*, in a tone that made the name mean *mine, mine, my heart.*

Eleven years she had rationed it. One look a night, sometimes less, hoarding it like bread in a famine winter. And now—

Now she opened the door all the way, and let herself look, one last time, for as long as she wanted.

She looked until the tears came, and past that, until the tears stopped being about loss and became something else, something like gratitude. *Thank you,* she told the face silently. *Thank you for the flour and the humming and the crooked smile. Thank you for loving me badly and well and always. I'm sorry I hid you. I thought hiding you was keeping you safe. I think maybe I was just keeping myself too frightened to live.*

The face smiled its crooked smile. And in her mind, her mother—who had been dead eleven years and could not speak, who was only memory and had never been anything else—seemed to say, in the tone that meant *mine, mine, my heart*: *Give it to them, little thief. It was never meant to be buried.*

Silas opened her eyes. They were wet, and she did not wipe them.

"Yes," she said aloud, to the fire, to the dragon, to whatever listened beneath the world. "Freely given. My mother's face. My mother's love. Take it. Warm yourself. Burn what needs burning."

She reached into the place where the memory lived, and instead of folding it small, she unfolded it entirely—every detail, every morning, every note of the off-key humming—and she held it out the way you hold out your hands to show they're empty, the way you hold out bread to a stranger, the way her mother had once held out a honey-cake to a beggar and gotten flour on his sleeve.

For one endless moment, nothing happened.

Then the ember moved.

It did not leap. It *reached*—a thread of gold lifting from the dragon's breast like a filament of dawn, crossing the space between them, touching her forehead, her lips, her hands. And the memory left her.

She felt it go. That was the cruel mercy of it: she felt every instant of its going, the face dissolving into warmth, the crooked smile becoming light, the humming becoming a sound like wind through a furnace. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. It felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying, like exhaling after eleven years of held breath. The warmth spread down through her arms, her chest, her knees against the cold scales, and where it passed it left not emptiness exactly but *openness*—rooms with the windows thrown wide, air moving through a house at last.

The ember drank.

And then it *blazed*.

Light detonated through the hollow. The grey scales flushed red, then orange, then white-hot; the root-ribs overhead caught the glow and threw it back tenfold; the cold air itself seemed to ignite. The dragon drew a breath—the first full breath in years, Silas could feel the displacement of it, a wind rushing inward from every tunnel and vent in the garden above—and the flame roared up through its throat and filled the chamber with a sound like the world's largest hearth catching after a long, long winter.

*OH,* said the dragon, in every voice it had ever kept, all at once. *OH. OH, THAT IS—THAT IS THE SUN. THAT IS MY MOTHER'S HEARTH. THAT IS EVERY FIRE I EVER WAS.*

It laughed. Dragons, it turned out, laughed like avalanches.

And in the same moment, from the tunnel mouth behind her, came a different sound: a shriek that was not a shriek, a sound like tearing silk and grinding stone and a man screaming through a mask that no longer fit him.

Silas turned.

The false king stood at the entrance to the hollow, framed by the sudden blaze, and the firelight was killing him. She could see it happening. The shape of him—the beloved heavy shoulders, the silver beard, the kind seamed face from every coin and proclamation—was *sliding*. Like wax. Like paint in rain. Beneath it, something else writhed: a suggestion of too many joints, of a body like poured smoke trying to remember how to be solid, of a hunger given anatomy. The creature had come down expecting a dying spark and a frightened thief. It had come down into a sunrise.

"No," it said, in her father's voice, in the king's voice, in a hundred voices it had drunk over the years. "No, no—you were supposed to *starve*—"

*You fed me for thirty years,* the dragon said, rising. Gods, it was *rising*, unfolding coil after coil, wings cracking open like sails, like storm-fronts, like the doors of heaven. Its shadow filled the hollow and then left the hollow entirely, thrown up through the garden vents, and Silas knew that everyone above—Isolde at the gate-line, Edric at the doors, the hunters, the crowds, the burning city—had just seen a dragon's shadow pass over them for the first time in three hundred years. *Small sips. Cruel sips. Now I drink deep, and I drink what you are.*

The creature fled upward. Of course it did. Things that only take have nowhere to stand when the taking stops.

What happened next, Silas saw in fragments, because she was crying again and because some things are too large for a single gaze. She saw the fire pour up the tunnels like water running backward. She saw, through the garden grates far above, a column of clean golden flame rise against the night sky, and the festival-fires below it look suddenly small and dirty, tallow beside the sun. She heard the creature scream from somewhere in the upper garden—a long, unraveling scream, the king's voice peeling away first, then the courtiers' voices it had borrowed, then, last of all, a thin raw sound that belonged to nothing but itself, hungry and small and terribly lonely—and then the sound simply stopped, the way a lie stops when the teller dies.

The silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

* * *

Silas did not remember climbing the stairs. She found herself standing in the royal gardens with ash drifting down around her like grey snow, and the great oak at the garden's heart scorched but alive, and the sky above the walls still glowing where the pillar of fire had risen and faded.

Isolde was on her knees by the fountain, alive, her sword arm bleeding, three hunters disarmed and sitting stunned against the wall with the particular blankness of people whose memories have recently been rearranged. She looked up at Silas, and her face did something complicated—relief and horror and awe fighting for space—and then she started laughing, or sobbing, or both.

"It flew," Isolde said. "Silas. It *flew*. Did you see—"

"I saw."

Edric came around the hedge at a dead run, pruning knife still in his fist, soot to the elbows, a burn along one forearm he clearly hadn't noticed. He skidded to a stop, took inventory of them both with a gardener's quick eye—alive, upright, breathing—and then turned to look at the open grate in the ground, the one pouring out heat like an opened oven, the one the dragon had already gone through, up, and away.

"Thirty years I tended those beds," he said unsteadily, "and there was a *dragon* under them."

"There was," Silas said.

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he had that first day in his exile cottage when he'd recognized his own memories in her eyes. Whatever he saw there made him set down the knife and cross the distance between them and hold her, briefly, hard, the way you hold someone who has just done something enormous and irreversible. He smelled of smoke and rosemary.

"It's gone, isn't it," he said quietly. "Whatever you gave it."

Silas reached inward, to the place behind the door.

The door was open. The room was empty. Clean, swept, sunlit, empty. She searched it the way you search a house the morning after you've sold it, touching the walls, checking corners, finding nothing—no face, no crooked smile, no humming, no name spoken in a mother's voice. She knew the facts still: her mother had existed, had loved her, had died in the purges. The knowledge sat in her like a fact in a ledger. But the face itself, the living warmth of it, was ash on the wind, food for a fire that was now somewhere over the mountains, flying.

Empty. She was empty. Hollowed to the walls.

And underneath the hollowness, where the grief should have been crushing her flat, there was something else. Something she had no practice at recognizing, because she had spent her whole life filling every vacant space the moment it appeared, stealing a memory for every silence, a stranger's joy for every private sorrow.

It was lightness.

Not happiness—she wasn't sure she'd know happiness if it bit her. Lightness. The specific, terrifying freedom of having nothing left to lose and nothing left to hide. She had walked into the dragon's chamber carrying eleven years of hoarded pain and a lifetime of stolen goods, and she had walked out owning nothing at all, and the nothing weighed less than air.

"Silas?" Isolde had gotten to her feet, was watching her with that fierce, perceptive gaze that missed nothing. "Are you—can I—"

"I don't remember her face anymore," Silas said. It came out steady, which surprised her. "I want you to know that. So that later, if I seem strange about it, you'll understand why. I gave it away. All of it. And I'm not sorry." She paused. Tested the truth of it. Found it held. "I'm not sorry."

Isolde crossed to her and took her hands—her bloodied sword-hand and all—and held them the way you hold something that might blow away.

Around them, the gardens were waking. Steam rose from the vents where the dragon-fire had passed; frost-blackened beds were already, impossibly, putting out green shoots, as if the flame had been a season unto itself, a spring compressed into a single night. Somewhere beyond the wall, the bells had begun again—ringing right this time, ringing wild, the sound of a city realizing its nightmare was over. Somewhere in the palace, servants were surely finding the dungeons unlocked, the jailer weeping over a daughter's name he'd remembered that morning, prisoners stumbling blinking into corridors that smelled of smoke and freedom.

Edric picked his pruning knife back up off the flagstones, considered it, and slid it into his belt.

"Well," he said. "Garden's burnt. Half the hedges are gone. The rose walk will need replanting from stock." He looked around at the scorched paradise he had served for thirty years, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling. "Best day's work it's ever seen."

Silas laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startling her own ears.

Then, from the direction of the gates, footsteps. Many footsteps, slowing, uncertain—the first of the freed prisoners, the first of the fleeing crowds, the first citizens of a kingdom with no king, drawn toward the gardens by the light they'd seen in the sky. They came through the broken gate in ones and twos, ash-smeared and staring, and stopped when they saw the three of them standing amid the steaming beds.

A woman at the front—an old woman, a market-woman, her face familiar from a thousand Thornfell mornings—looked at Silas, at the empty-handed thief standing in the middle of the miracle, and asked the question that would shape everything that came after:

"What happens now?"

Silas looked at the scorched earth. At the green shoots already breaking through the ash. At Isolde, born to rule and learning it. At Edric, already kneeling, already turning the soil with his fingers, checking what could be saved.

She looked down at her own empty hands, and found, to her astonishment, that they itched to be full—to plant, to build, to grow something that had never belonged to anyone else.

"Now," Silas said, "we start the garden over."
