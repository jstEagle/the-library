## 20. Ashes and Acorns

Edric Hale had spent forty years learning the language of soil, and he had never once heard it speak the way it spoke that morning.

He knelt in the ruined garden with his bare hands buried to the wrist in earth that was still warm—not from fire, though there had been plenty of fire, but from something older and deeper. The dragon's passage through the roots of the world had left the ground itself humming, a low vibration he felt in his palms, in his knees, in the old ache of his spine. The soil was singing. He would have called himself mad for thinking it, except that he had watched the roses open out of season three days ago, all at once, like an audience rising to its feet.

"Edric." Isolde's voice came from behind him, careful, the way one speaks to a man kneeling in a graveyard. "The council is assembled. They're waiting."

"Tell them the garden isn't finished dying yet," he said, not turning. "It needs another hour. Maybe two."

"They've been told that twice already."

"Then tell them a third time." He lifted a clump of scorched loam, crumbled it between his fingers, smelled it. Underneath the char there was life—there always was, if you knew where to look for it. Nitrogen in the ash. Worms already working the lower strata. The garden had been burned before; gardens always were. That was half of what gardening meant: being present for the burning, and staying for after. "They can wait. Kings waited on me for thirty years. A council of frightened clerks can wait on me for an afternoon."

He heard her smile more than saw it. "You know I'm not a queen yet. Only regent."

"You're whatever you decide to be. That's rather the point of everything we just did."

* * *

Three days after the false king burned, they had carried Edric's old tools up from the gardener's cottage at the garden's edge, where they had gathered dust and spider-silk for seven years. Seven years since the creature wearing the king's face had looked at him across a bed of trampled lavender and said, *You saw nothing, gardener,* in a voice like smoke drawn through a keyhole—and Edric had said nothing, because saying something meant the pyre, and he had been a coward in the specific way that men who love living things are often cowards. He had taken his shame into exile like a stone sewn into his coat.

Now the tools were back in his hands. The spade's handle had swelled slightly with damp years, and it fit his grip differently than memory insisted, but fit it did, the way an old song fits the mouth even when the voice has changed.

Silas found him there on the first morning, standing among the beds he'd once kept as meticulously as a monk keeps his hours. She stood at the edge of the boxwood walk with her hands in her pockets, watching him with those eyes that had held his own memories—the king's death, the creature's feeding, the last breath Edric had witnessed and done nothing to stop.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm deciding whether to say something kind or something true."

"Those were ever the same thing with you. Say both."

She came down the walk. Up close she looked thinner than she had in the dungeons, hollowed in the way of people who have given away something enormous and are still listening for the echo of it. He knew what she had fed the fire. They all knew. Isolde had wept for her, and Silas had let her, standing very straight, like a woman letting rain fall on her face without flinching.

"The beds along the south wall," Silas said. "Are those salvaged?"

"Some. The roots run deep enough. Roses are stubborn creatures—they die above ground and keep their opinions below it." He glanced at her. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I want to help. And I don't know how. I've never grown anything in my life. I've only ever—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened around whatever the sentence had been going to be. *Taken*, probably. *Lifted*. *Sold*.

"Grown things is learned work," Edric said gruffly. "Same as anything. Here." He pressed the hand trowel into her palm and closed her fingers over it. "Start there. Turn the bed by the sundial. Cut in six inches, lift, break the clods, don't fight the stones. The soil will tell you what it wants if you shut up long enough to listen."

"That sounds suspiciously like patience."

"It usually does, the first time."

She snorted—a small, surprised sound, as if laughter were a stolen good she hadn't expected to find in her own pockets—and went to kneel by the sundial. Edric watched her set the trowel against the earth, hesitate, and cut in. Six inches. Lift. Break. She worked badly and earnestly, and by midmorning there was a stripe of dark turned soil behind her like a sentence being written.

He felt something in his chest loosen that had been clenched for seven years. Grief, he thought. Or its opposite. With him lately it was hard to tell them apart; they seemed to grow from the same root.

* * *

On the fifth day, the dragon flew.

Edric was pruning the hedge roses—he could not stop pruning; his hands demanded occupation the way other men's hands demanded drink—when the light over the central lawn changed. It went gold, then bronze, then the deep ember-red of a forge seen through a doorway. He straightened, and there it was, rising out of the garden's broken heart on wings that threw shadows like cathedral sails.

It was not large. That was the first thing that undid him. In the stories dragons were mountains with teeth, but this one was old and starved and newly fed, and it rose off the scorched stone of its sleeping place no bigger than a hay wagon, its scales dull copper gone green at the joints, its wings membrane-thin and patched like mended sails. It rose anyway. It rose the way the roses had opened, the way the shoots broke ash—all the stubborn rising things of the world doing what they had always done, together, at last.

The dragon circled the garden once. Twice. Its shadow swept across Edric, across the beds, across Silas standing frozen at the sundial with dirt to her elbows and her mouth open, across Isolde on the terrace with the wind pulling at her mourning-black sleeves. Then the great head swung toward them, and the dragon spoke.

Not in its own voice. Edric had learned that in the depths, in the terrible wonderful dark beneath the roots: the dragon had no voice of its own anymore. Centuries of sleeping under a garden full of human voices had filled it with borrowed ones. It spoke now in a chorus—in the king's dead timbre, in a child's laugh shaped into words, in Edric's own mother's lilt, in voices he didn't recognize and some, he suspected, no one alive would ever recognize again.

*"Seven hundred years,"* it said, in all those voices at once, *"I have not seen the sky. It is smaller than I remembered. It is better than I remembered."*

And then it climbed, climbing, climbing, until it was a spark of bronze against the morning blue, and then it banked eastward over the city walls, and the whole capital stopped—market-stalls, church bells, the grinding of the harbor cranes—to watch the impossible thing pass overhead, and a sound went up from ten thousand throats that Edric had never heard a crowd make before. Not fear. Not quite joy. Something between a cheer and a prayer.

Isolde found him by the hedge afterward. Her face was wet and she made no apology for it.

"My father used to say the dragons died out before his grandfather's time," she said. "He told it like a lesson. *Everything wild ends, Isolde. Best to make your peace.*" She wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand, an unqueenly gesture that Edric decided he liked enormously. "He was wrong about nearly everything, wasn't he. Even that."

"He was wrong about everything except the roses," Edric said. "He did love the roses. Whatever wore his face couldn't be bothered to learn which end of the shears was which, but he—" His throat closed. Forty years of service, and the truest eulogy he could give the dead king was that he had known how to prune. "He loved the garden. That part was real. I'll keep that part."

"Yes," Isolde said softly. "Keep that part."

* * *

The reinstatement ceremony was small, which suited him. Isolde had wanted grandeur—her advisors had certainly wanted grandeur—but she had walked the burned garden with Edric one evening and watched him wince at the thought of hundreds of feet on the soft new growth, and she had settled the matter with a wave of her hand: *We'll do it here, among the beds, and anyone who complains can weed.*

So it was that Edric Hale, disgraced seven years ago in a corridor smelling of lavender and smoke, stood in the same corridor—rebuilt, re-planted, the lavender young and pale as new linen—and took back the title of Royal Gardener from a princess who bowed to *him*, which was backwards and improper and which he could do nothing about except bow lower, so that for an absurd moment the two of them were folded nearly double over each other's hands while the household staff tried valiantly not to laugh.

"There's a condition," Edric said, when they had righted themselves.

Isolde raised an eyebrow. "Name it."

"The guild. The broker woman's restitution guild. When it needs a charter—when she comes asking—you'll grant it." He had heard Silas talking in the nights since the burning, low-voiced, planning: ledgers of every memory sold in Thornfell and beyond, brokers sworn to return what was taken, a trade built backward, unwinding itself thread by thread. "What she means to build is worth more to this kingdom than anything I'll ever prune."

"She hasn't asked me for anything."

"She won't. That's why you'll offer."

Isolde studied him a moment, then smiled the smile he was beginning to understand was her father's only in shape and nothing else. "You've known her four months and you're already managing her affairs."

"I'm a gardener. Managing things that don't know they need managing is the whole profession."

* * *

Autumn came early that year, or perhaps the garden simply decided to have one, and Edric spent it teaching.

He taught the household staff, who drifted into the garden in twos and threes, drawn by something they couldn't name—the hum in the soil, maybe, or the stories of the dragon, or just the strange new peace that hung over the grounds like weather. He taught them turning and mulching and the difference between pruning for shape and pruning for mercy. He taught the kitchen girls to save seed, and the stable boys to compost, and one solemn chamberlain's son to identify every oak in the park by its bark alone.

And he taught Silas.

She was a frustrating student and a devoted one. She wanted mastery immediately; the soil declined to cooperate; she took this personally. But she came every morning, and she kept the little notebook Isolde had given her filled with cramped observations—*roses hate wet feet, beans fix the soil, do NOT let Edric near the espalier pears when he's sulking*—and somewhere in the middle weeks of autumn, Edric noticed that the beds she tended had begun to look, quietly, like the best beds in the garden.

"They're showing off for you," he told her one gray morning, nodding at a row of winter greens so vigorous it was almost rude.

"They're showing off because I threatened them."

"Threats work too. Half of horticulture is threats."

She laughed—easily now, these days, the laugh of someone practicing—and knelt beside him at the cold frame where the acorn trays sat. Acorns from the park oaks, gathered by children, planted in rows. Some of them, in the spring, would go into the ground over the place where the dragon had slept for centuries, filling the empty dark with roots.

"What happens to a thing like that," Silas asked, nodding at the trays, "when you plant it over a grave?"

"Depends whose grave." Edric turned a tray gently, checking the white threads of root emerging from the drainage holes. "Over most graves, a tree is a monument—it stands where something ended. Over the right grave, it's something else. An inheritance. The tree doesn't remember the grave. It just grows out of what the grave left behind." He looked at her sidelong. "That's the trick of grief, if you're asking. You don't get to keep what you lost. You get to grow out of it. Whether you mean to or not."

Silas was quiet a long moment. He had learned not to interrupt her quiet moments; they were load-bearing.

"I don't remember her face," she said at last, simply, the way you'd report weather. "I keep reaching for it and there's just... warmth. Like a hearth in an empty room. I know the room was warm. I can't see the fire."

"No," Edric said. "But the room remembers." He tapped the tray of acorns. "These don't remember the oak either. Not one ring of it. And come spring, every one of them will try its level best to become one."

* * *

Winter's first frost silvered the garden on the morning Isolde was formally proclaimed Regent before the assembled court, and Edric skipped the ceremony entirely, on the grounds that someone responsible ought to protect the citrus from the cold. He was wrapping burlap around the young orange trees when he heard footsteps on the frost-stiffened gravel, unhurried, and turned to find Silas standing at the gate with a traveling cloak on and a pack at her shoulder.

His chest did something complicated.

"When?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. First light. Maren's meeting me at Thornfell—half the brokers are terrified of us and the other half want to join, which Maren says is the correct ratio for any honest enterprise." She smiled, but it was a stretched thing, pulled between leaving and arriving. "There are eleven thousand entries in the first ledger alone. People walking around missing pieces of themselves. Children erased. Whole marriages built on gaps." Her hand drifted to her collarbone, where the memory-vial had once hung. "I put some of those gaps there, Edric. Some of them I didn't, but I profited from every one. I'm going to spend a long time putting things back."

"And after?"

"After," she said, "I'm coming back. If the invitation holds."

"The invitation," Edric said, "is a bed in the gardener's cottage and first pick of the spring planting, and it holds until the walls fall down."

She crossed the frost-bright garden and hugged him—briefly, fiercely, the embrace of someone who had spent her life taking things from people and was only now learning what it cost to be given something instead. Then she stepped back, and her eyes were bright and dry.

"Keep the south beds for me," she said. "The roses. I want to see what they do next year."

"Roses are stubborn creatures," Edric said. "They'll wait."

She went out through the gate, and the frost took her footprints almost immediately, so that it was as if the garden had accepted her passing and moved on, keeping her the way it kept everything: not in memory, exactly, but in the soil, in the roots, in the slow chemistry of becoming.

Edric stood alone among the wrapped trees a while. Then he picked up his shears, and knelt at the nearest rose bed, and began the winter pruning—which is not really cutting at all, but a promise made in the cold to something sleeping, that it will wake, that it will bloom, that the hands that failed it once will be there, faithfully, when it does.

Far to the north, a bronze spark wheeled over the mountains. Far to the south, a girl with empty hands walked toward a city full of debts to repay.

And in the garden, under the frost, under the ash-turned-soil, the acorns waited, holding in their small hard hearts everything they had never seen, ready to grow it all.
