## 21. A Memory Freely Given

The fire in the garden did not flicker the way ordinary flames did. It breathed.

Silas had learned that in the weeks since the burning of the false king—learned it the way she'd once learned the tells of a mind about to be opened, watching for the small signs. The dragon's flame rose and fell in a slow rhythm beneath the iron grate at the garden's heart, and when it exhaled, the whole garden seemed to lean toward it, every leaf turning like a face toward a hearth on a winter night.

She came to it each morning now, though she could not have said why. Habit, perhaps. Or penance. Or something she was not yet ready to name.

The morning Isolde found her there, frost still clung to the rose canes and the sky above the palace walls was the color of old pewter. The princess—regent, now; the coronation was set for spring, though Isolde spoke of it as one speaks of a tooth that must be pulled—came down the gravel path with her hands clasped behind her back and her jaw set in the way Silas had come to recognize as *preparation*.

"You've been avoiding the council sessions," Isolde said, without greeting.

"I've been avoiding the council," Silas corrected, not looking up from the grate. She was feeding the fire small things: a dried sprig of rosemary Edric had given her, a curl of birch bark, a single amber bead she'd carried since Thornfell without knowing why until now. Each offering went up in a breath of gold. "Sessions are just the council with better chairs."

"The council wants to honor you."

"The council wants to bury what happened under honors so no one has to look at it directly." She brushed ash from her knees and stood. "I've seen how memory works, Isolde. Given enough ceremony, people will forget anything they want to."

Isolde's mouth tightened. For a moment she looked very much like the girl in the wine cellar at Vess—all sharp edges held together by stubbornness—and then she looked like what she was becoming: a woman who would wear a crown over a wound. "That's why I'm here," she said. "Not the council. Me."

Silas waited.

"There's a place for you at court." Isolde said it quickly, the way one rips out a splinter. "A position. Keeper of the royal archive, if you want it—or something less formal. An advisor. A friend of the crown, whatever title makes it bearable. You'd have rooms in the east wing. You'd have—" She stopped, started again. "You'd have a home. A real one. Not a rented room above a broker's shop with a hunter's mark burned into the doorframe."

The words landed where Isolde had clearly aimed them, deep beneath Silas's ribs where all her old hungers lived. A room that was hers. A door no one would mark. A name spoken aloud in halls where names were power.

She thought of her mother's face—or the space where it had been, the smooth blank wall where the most precious thing she owned had stood. She had fed that face to the fire. Some mornings she woke reaching for it and found only the shape of its absence, an ache like a tongue probing a missing tooth. And some mornings—the rare ones, the unbearable ones—she woke almost light.

"You're offering me a cage with gilded bars," Silas said quietly. "And I know you don't mean it as one. But I spent my whole life taking things I hadn't earned because I was afraid of what would happen if I stood still and asked for them. If I say yes to this because it's offered—if I take another thing I didn't build—I'll be the same thief I always was. Just wearing cleaner clothes."

"Silas—"

"Let me finish." She turned to face the regent fully. Frost melted off the rose canes between them; the fire's breath reached even here. "Ask me what I'm going to do instead."

Isolde studied her, wary. "What are you going to do instead?"

Silas smiled, and it felt strange on her face, unpracticed, like a key fitting a lock it had never been meant for. "I'm going to give everything back."

---

They sat on the low wall edging the fire-garden while Silas laid it out, and by the time she finished, the sun had burned through the pewter and the frost had gone to shining water on the flagstones.

"Maren kept ledgers," she said. "Every broker in Thornfell did. Names of sellers, names of buyers, descriptions of every vial that ever passed through their hands. Maren thought it was protection—leverage against the day the hunters came. It'll be evidence now. Every stolen memory in Thornfell is traceable. Every person who sold their grief for bread money, every family whose quarrels were bought and bottled, every child who traded away a bad night and lost the good ones tangled inside it." She flexed her fingers, feeling the ghost-tingle of her gift, duller now, changed by what she'd done in the garden. "I know how to read those ledgers. Better than anyone alive. And I know how to carry a memory back to the mind it came from. That's not theft. That's the other direction."

"You'd spend years on the roads," Isolde said.

"I'd spend my life on the roads. There's a difference, and the difference matters." Silas looked at her hands. They were a thief's hands, narrow and quick, scarred at the knuckles from vial-glass and lockpicks. But they had also cradled a jailer's daughter back into her father's mind. They had held the last ember steady while a king's screaming shadow came apart. Hands were just hands until you decided what they were for. "Maren dragged chains through Thornfell for twenty years and called it trade. The hunters burned half of it and called it cleansing. I'm not interested in calling things by pretty names anymore. Restitution. That's the honest word. Ugly work, honest word."

"And the guild?"

"A guild of restitution. Brokers who return instead of take. Trained by me, bound by oaths written in ink I'll mix myself, answerable to the people they serve rather than the coin they chase." She laughed softly, without much humor. "The brokers will hate it. Half of them will call me a traitor and the other half will try to buy me. Let them. I've been bought before. I know all the prices."

Isolde was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the fire breathed, and a bed of night-blooming jasmine opened early in its warmth, white stars against dark soil.

"You could stay," Isolde said at last, and her voice had lost its regent's polish. It was just a voice now, young and stubborn and afraid of losing something. "You could be happy here. We won the war, Silas. You're allowed to stop running."

"That's just it." Silas reached over and took the regent's hand—the first time she had ever initiated touch with anyone, and they both knew it, and neither remarked on it. "I'm not running. Running looks exactly like this but feels different inside. This is the first thing I've ever walked toward."

---

Leaving took eleven days to arrange and one hour to accomplish, which Silas supposed was the way of all true departures.

Edric saw her off at the garden gate, his hands black with loam as always, his gray beard trimmed now that he was royal gardener again and someone at court insisted on propriety. He pressed a packet of seeds into her palm—acorns from the great oak at the garden's center, the one that had grown over the dragon's sleeping place for three hundred years.

"For planting wherever you stop," he said gruffly. "A guild needs roots. Plant one at every house you establish. In thirty years, someone will sit under them and wonder who was fool enough to carry acorns across the mountains." His voice caught. "It was an honor to walk beside you, memory-thief."

"It was an honor to be remembered by you," she answered, and meant more than courtesy by it, and saw that he knew.

Isolde did not come to the gate. She sent a rider instead, and the rider delivered a small case of white oak, and inside the case lay a single vial of clear glass, empty, sealed with wax bearing the regent's own signet.

"She says," the rider recited, awkwardly, "that a guild of restitution should have a founding vessel. And that the first memory returned should be one freely given, so the guild begins as it means to continue. And—" the rider squinted at the seal, "—that Her Grace says the vial is already commissioned. She's written a memory of her own to travel with you. Her father's funeral, the real one, the one nobody attended because everyone believed him still alive. She says it deserves to be witnessed by someone who will understand."

Silas closed her hand around the empty vial and felt the weight of it settle somewhere deeper than her palm.

"Tell Her Grace," she said, "that I'll carry it to Thornfell first. And that I'll stand witness. And that when the guild's ledger opens, its first entry will be in her hand, freely given, asking nothing."

The rider bowed and was gone. Silas stood at the mountain road's first bend, the palace shrinking behind her, the acorns in her pocket, the vial against her heart, and the fire's warmth still faintly in her skin like a blessing she hadn't asked for.

Below, in the valley, the lights of Thornfell were beginning to kindle.

She walked toward them.

Somewhere in those streets was a ledger with ten thousand names in it, and somewhere behind every name was a hollow where something loved used to live. She would find them. All of them, or as many as her one life allowed, and when her life ran out, others would take up the road after her, planting acorns as they went.

The wind came down off the pass carrying woodsmoke and the far-off scent of snow, and Silas Kavanagh—who had spent her whole life lifting what was never hers—began, at last, the long walk home to give it all back.
