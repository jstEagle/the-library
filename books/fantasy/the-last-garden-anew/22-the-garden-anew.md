## 22. The Garden Anew

The hole Silas dug was deeper than any grave.

She had measured it by feel, kneeling in the dark soil above the dragon's old sleeping place with a spade Edric had sharpened for her himself, and when the blade finally rang against stone she kept digging with her hands, clearing away a century of fallen grit until the hollow beneath was wide enough to cradle roots. The earth down there was warm. It was always warm now—the fire below never slept, only breathed—and Silas sat back on her heels and let that warmth soak through her palms like a hand pressed over hers.

A year ago this ground had been a secret. A year ago she had descended into it half-dead of fear, carrying nothing but a memory she was about to surrender, and the thing wearing the king's face had followed her down into the dark expecting an easy meal. Now the garden above grew wild and generous over the whole hidden length of it, roses rioting through the broken trellises Edric hadn't gotten around to mending, bees drunk in the lavender, and nobody who walked these paths suspected that beneath their feet an ancient heart burned steady as a hearth.

That was how it should be, Silas thought. The best fires were the ones you didn't have to see to feel.

She lifted the sapling from its wrappings of wet burlap—a rowan, barely taller than her forearm, its leaves shivering silver-green in the morning light. Isolde had brought it from the northern hills, from the valley where Silas's mother had been born, though Silas knew that valley now only by description. She had gone looking, once, in the first raw weeks after everything. Stood in a village square where a woman who shared her jawline might once have bought bread, and felt nothing rise to meet her. No face. No voice. Not even the shape of an absence anymore—just a clean, smooth wall where a life used to be.

It should have devastated her. For a long time she had expected it to, waited for the grief to ambush her the way grief was supposed to, and when it didn't come she'd worried something in her had been broken past repair.

But standing in that square, she had found instead that she could *imagine*. A woman with flour on her hands. A laugh like a door swinging open. A way of humming while she worked, off-key and unbothered. None of it true, probably—or all of it true, somewhere, in some version of the story. It wasn't remembering. It was better than remembering, maybe, because it was hers. Chosen. Grown from seed instead of stolen from someone else's keeping.

She lowered the rowan into the warm dark and began, gently, to fill the hole.

* * *

Edric came at mid-morning, as he always did, with his pruning shears hanging from his belt and dirt already under his nails before he'd touched anything, because Edric Hale had never in his life entered a garden without immediately finding some fault in his own hands.

"You've planted it crooked," he said, squatting beside her, squinting at the sapling with the severity he reserved for seedlings and royalty alike.

"It's straight."

"It leans east."

"Toward the sun," Silas said. "Like everything else that wants to live."

He made a sound in his throat that was not quite agreement but had stopped being argument years ago, if two people could manage years in twelve months. They could, apparently, when they'd held a gate together against hunters while the world burned. He settled onto the ground beside her, joints cracking, and began absently pulling weeds from around the new planting—not because it needed it, but because his hands required occupation the way other men's required speech.

"Isolde's coming today," he said. "The herald came up from the palace at dawn. She dismissed her escort at the garden wall again."

"She doesn't need an escort here."

"No." He snapped a weed stem cleanly between his fingers. "She doesn't. That's rather the point of what we built, isn't it."

What they built. Silas looked out across the terraces. A year ago the royal gardens had been a gilded cage, all order and display, every hedge clipped into obedience. Now half of it ran to vegetables because the palace servants had been hungry during the interregnum and Edric had torn out two ornamental parterres without asking anyone's permission. The other half had gone deliberately, gloriously feral—left to reseed itself, to remember its own old purposes. Bees everywhere. Foxes in the foxgloves. And threaded through all of it, the guild-house: the long low building near the eastern wall where the brokers worked now, where people came every day carrying memories they wanted returned.

Restitution, they called it. Silas had fought over the name for a week before conceding it was right. Not charity. Not mercy. Restitution—because so much of what passed through those doors had passed through *her* hands first, or hands like hers, and the debt was older than any single thief.

"The girl from Thornfell came again yesterday," Edric said quietly. "The one whose father sold her wedding day. She sat with it for an hour before she could bear to take it back."

"And?"

"She laughed when it settled. Cried after. Laughed again before she left." He glanced sideways at Silas. "You do good work, Kavanagh. I know you don't like hearing it said plainly, so I'll say it crookedly: the garden's lucky to have a gardener who tends more than plants."

Silas didn't answer. Her hands kept moving in the soil, firming it around the rowan's roots, and she let him believe she hadn't heard, which was a kindness they extended each other regularly and without comment.

* * *

Isolde arrived in the afternoon, riding her own horse, dressed like a merchant's daughter, which fooled no one and was meant to fool no one. The regent of the realm did not need disguises in her own gardens; she simply preferred them, the same way she preferred arriving through the servant's gate and complaining about council meetings and eating bread and cheese standing up in the kitchen like a person instead of a portrait.

She found Silas by the new tree and stood looking at it for a long moment before saying anything.

"It's grown."

"Trees do that."

"Don't be clever at me, I've had a terrible week. The northern lords want to reopen the witch-hunt trials under their own authority, and I've spent three days explaining, slowly, with diagrams, why that will end with their provinces on fire." She sat down beside Silas on the warm earth without ceremony, skirts and all, and pulled off her riding gloves finger by finger. "I won, obviously. But I'm tired."

"Obviously," Silas said.

They sat in the kind of silence that had taken a year to build and now needed no maintenance whatsoever. Below them, faint as a heartbeat through bedrock, the fire breathed. Silas felt Isolde feel it too—she saw the small shift in the princess's shoulders, the unconscious settling, the way everyone who spent time here eventually learned to lean on the dragon's warmth without noticing they did it.

"I brought something," Isolde said at last. She drew a flat package from her satchel, wrapped in linen, and set it between them without ceremony. "From the north. From the valley."

Silas went very still.

"I know you went," Isolde said. "Last spring. I know you came back and said it was nothing and then didn't speak for two days. And I know—" her voice gentled, "—that you can't remember her. And I'm not going to apologize for bringing this, because I decided a long time ago that I would rather you be angry at me than have you spend your whole life afraid to look."

Inside the linen was a painting. Small, cracked with age, the varnish gone amber. A young woman stood in a kitchen doorway with flour on her hands and an expression caught mid-laugh, head tipped, eyes bright with some joke the painter hadn't recorded.

Silas looked at it for a long time.

The woman in the painting did not match the mother in her imagination. The imagined one had been softer, rounder-faced, with hair she'd worn loose. This woman was sharp-boned and laughing, with her hair pinned severely and escaping anyway, and flour dusting her to the elbows, and—

And it didn't matter. That was the thing Silas discovered, sitting there with her heart doing something complicated and quiet in her chest. It didn't matter that the real face and the imagined face were different women, or perhaps weren't different at all, or perhaps the painter had flattered her. What mattered was that Silas looked at this stranger and felt recognition anyway—not memory, not the ghost of something stolen, but something sturdier. Like meeting a relative in a crowd. Like a tree leaning toward the sun regardless of where the gardener put it.

"That's her laugh," Silas said finally. Her voice came out rough. "The tilt of her head. That's exactly—I imagined that exact—" She stopped, laughed once, wiped her eyes with the back of a dirt-caked wrist. "I got it wrong and right at the same time."

"Is that allowed?" Isolde asked. There was a careful lightness in it, but underneath, Silas heard the real question: *is it enough, what you lost? Can you live with the shape of it?*

"It's better than allowed." Silas turned the little painting over. On the back, in faded ink, a single line in a hand she didn't recognize: *For my daughter, when she is grown.* "Someone gave this away to keep it safe. Someone carried it north all this time hoping—" She stopped again. Breathed. "People keep giving me things I don't remember earning. You. Edric. The jailer's daughter, who sends me honey every autumn and calls me the woman who gave her father back. At some point I'm going to have to accept that inheritance isn't a vault, Isolde. It isn't what you lock away. It's what gets handed forward whether you're watching or not."

Isolde leaned her shoulder against Silas's shoulder, the way she'd done in a dungeon cell a year ago when neither of them knew yet whether they'd live out the week.

"So plant your tree," she said, "and stop acting surprised that things grow."

* * *

Evening came gold and slow. Edric lit the lamps along the terrace path, and the three of them ended up—as they always ended up—at the low stone table near the eastern wall, where the guild-house windows glowed warm behind them and the smell of bread drifted out from the communal kitchen where the brokers took their meals together.

Maren had come up from Thornfell last month, gray-haired and sharp-tongued as ever, and had inspected the guild with the air of a woman searching for fraud and finding, to her visible disgust, none. She stayed three days, argued with Silas about ledger practices, taught two of the younger restitutors her old tricks for handling volatile memories, and left without saying goodbye—though Silas had found, tucked into the guild's account book, a page in Maren's handwriting recording a donation large enough to fund the winter stores. Some people blessed you the only way they knew how: grudgingly, and in ink.

Below the table where they sat, the ground held its gentle heat. Above them, the first stars. Somewhere in the dark of the garden a nightingale had started up, and Edric, half-asleep in his chair with his pruning shears still on his belt, muttered something about netting the cherry trees before the birds stripped them, which was his version of a lullaby.

"Do you ever miss it?" Isolde asked quietly, when the bird had paused. "Stealing. The old life."

Silas considered it honestly, because Isolde deserved honesty and because lying to yourself was just theft with worse bookkeeping.

"I miss being invisible," she said. "There was a safety in it. Nobody looks twice at a shadow." She rolled a fallen rowan leaf between her fingers. "But I was never safe, Isolde. I was just alone, and told myself it was the same thing. Every memory I lifted, I told myself it didn't count because I didn't *keep* anything. But I did keep. I kept a whole life in pieces and called it survival." She looked at the little painting, propped now against the candle-lamp where the young laughing woman with flour on her hands could watch the garden. "Turns out you can't build a self out of other people's leavings. You have to grow one. It's slower. It hurts more. But it's actually yours."

The nightingale started again. Down in the deep places, the fire breathed on—steady, patient, fed now not by sacrifice but by tending, by the daily small devotions of people who came to sit near it and leave a memory freely offered on the ember-stones, not because the dragon needed feeding anymore but because giving turned out to be a habit worth keeping.

Silas thought about the girl she had been on the mountain pass, lifting a smuggler's memories in the fog, so proud of her rules, so certain that distance was the same as strength. She wished she could tell that girl what she knew now. She couldn't, of course. Nothing traveled backward. Everything only grew forward—from seed, from scar, from whatever poor soil you were given.

She pressed her palm flat against the warm earth beside the young rowan and felt, unmistakably, the answering pulse below.

Tomorrow there would be ledgers. Tomorrow the northern lords would try something, and Isolde would outmaneuver them, and Edric would complain about aphids, and someone would arrive at the guild-house door carrying a memory they'd lost and a courage they'd only just found. Tomorrow the work would go on, the slow work, the growing work.

But tonight the tree stood straight in its warm dark—straight, whatever Edric claimed—and the fire burned steady, and Silas Kavanagh, who had once survived by taking, sat in the middle of everything she had given away and found herself, at last, impossibly, full.
