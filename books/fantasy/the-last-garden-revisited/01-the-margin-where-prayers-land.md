## 1. The Margin Where Prayers Land

The pass didn't exist on any map but mine, which was the point of it.

I'd been climbing since before dawn, boots crunching over frost that the sun hadn't reached yet, following a rumor I'd bought from a smuggler with wine on his breath and three silver in his pocket. *There's a way through Thornfell's shoulder*, he'd said, *narrow enough you could shake hands with yourself.* Smugglers lie for sport, but they lie around a spine of truth, and my trade was finding it. I'd charted eleven passes in nine years. Every one of them had gone into the king's surveyors' office under someone else's name—*discovered by order of His Majesty*—because a woman alone in the mountains is a rumor, and rumors get hunted.

So I mapped in secret. I kept my name folded away like a letter no one should read. It was safer that way. Safer for everyone.

The smuggler hadn't lied. The pass opened at midmorning like a wound in the mountain's flank, barely a body wide, walled in gray stone streaked white with old snowmelt. I ate cold bread against the rock and worked. Altitude. Bearing. A spring seeping from a crack that would freeze by autumn and run again in spring—I noted it all in my careful hand, the small precise letters I'd invented so no one could trace them back to any scriptorium or school. My father's maps had been beautiful. Mine were useful. I'd decided long ago that useful was better than beautiful, because beautiful things got remembered, and remembered things got asked about.

I was halfway down the far side when the shrine lit up.

It sat in a niche where the path bent—a thing I'd have walked past without a glance, one more weathered stone among thousands, its carved face worn to a suggestion. Old gods' work, pre-Imperial, the kind the surveyors catalogued as *waymarker, pagan origin, no significance*. Pilgrims had left wax stubs at its feet once; the wax had gone gray as the stone itself. Centuries dead. I gave it exactly the attention it deserved, which was none.

Then the air above it caught fire without burning.

Light rose off the shrine like heat-shimmer given color, gold shot through with something deeper, a red like the inside of a closed eyelid. The frost on the surrounding rocks hissed into steam. And every hair on my arms lifted, because I knew what this was. Everyone knew what this was, the way everyone knew the shape of their own name even if they never spoke it. Shrines flared when gods woke. Gods hadn't woken in four hundred years. The priests said the old ones were spent, burnt out like stars, and the new faith's quiet god needed no shrines at all, only silence and submission.

The light found me anyway.

It crossed the pass like a hand reaching across a table—not fast, not slow, just *certain*—and settled on me where I stood with my charcoal frozen above the page. Warmth poured through me, down through my boots into the stone, as if the mountain had been waiting for my weight specifically. And in my skull, behind my eyes, in a voice that wasn't sound at all:

*Vesna.*

I dropped the charcoal. My knees hit rock. Because Vesna was not my name. Vesna was the name my mother whispered over my cradle once, once only, before she taught me Ansel, taught me silence, taught me how to be nobody—and then went north and did not come back. Vesna was a word I had buried so deep I'd almost convinced myself I'd dreamed it.

Almost.

"Stop," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the empty pass. "Stop it. I'm not—" *Not what?* Not yours. Not hers. Not anything. "You've got the wrong person."

The shrine burned on, patient as sunrise. Wax stubs that had been gray for centuries softened and ran like tears.

That was when I heard the footsteps—or rather the stumble, boot-heel catching stone, a body failing forward along the path below me. I did the sensible thing, the survival thing: I pressed into the shadow of an outcrop and made myself small, a trick I'd practiced until it wasn't a trick anymore. People looked straight through me. Nine years of practice.

A pilgrim came around the bend. That was the only word for him, though he wore no pilgrim's badge—he was gaunt as a winter branch, robes crusted with road-dirt, and he walked like a man carrying something heavy in his chest. His eyes were wrong. Not blind-wrong. *Burned*-wrong, the look of someone who had seen a great deal of light recently and none of it kind.

He stopped at the shrine. He didn't seem surprised that it blazed. He knelt, winced, and said, in a voice scraped raw: "Still burning. Good. Then there's still time." He fumbled at his neck and drew out a pendant—a crown worked in black iron, tiny, ugly, the kind of devotional object sold cheap in capital markets. He stared at it the way you'd stare at a snake you'd found in your bed.

Then he turned his head and looked directly at the shadow where I hid.

"I can't see you," he said. "But She can. And if She can, you're who I've walked six hundred miles to find."

Every instinct screamed at me to stay still, to let the invisibility do its work, to wait him out. But my legs, traitors, carried me out into the light.

"I'm a mapmaker," I said. "Passing through. I don't know anything, and I'm not going anywhere with strangers."

"You're going to have to be braver than that." He tried to rise and couldn't. He sagged against the shrine's base, and up close I could see the damage the distance had done him—lips cracked, hands trembling, a stain spreading dark beneath the robes over his stomach. Wounded, then, and hiding it badly. "My name is Brother Emeric. Was. They took the brother part when I started asking questions." He laughed, which cost him. "Do you know how many royal confessors the palace has buried in the last ten years? Eleven. Eleven confessors in a decade, all hale men, all dead of 'fevers.' I asked why the eleventh's letters home stopped mid-sentence. That's all I did. Ask."

"Tragic," I said. "Not mine."

"It became mine when I saw the king pray." His burned eyes fixed on mine, and I understood that whatever he'd walked six hundred miles toward, he'd already accepted he wouldn't walk back. "I served at the winter chapel. King Aldric prays four hours a day now—did you know that? Four hours, kneeling before the iron crown reliquary. He goes in hollow-eyed and comes out *shining*, and each time there's a little less of him behind the shine. Like a lamp burning oil that isn't his." His breath hitched. "I watched him give it something. In the prayer. Something inside himself, drawn out through the mouth like thread. And something in the reliquary drank it."

"The reliquary," I repeated. "A crown."

"A crown older than the dynasty. Older than the faith. They keep it because it can't be destroyed and can't be moved, so they worship it instead and call it devotion." His hand shot out and caught my wrist—stronger than he had any right to be, dying men spending their last coin of strength. "Something is wearing Aldric's crown, girl. Something ancient and starving, and it has been eating our king alive one prayer at a time. The shrines are waking because the balance is breaking. And the old ones—" his gaze flicked up to the blazing stone above us, then back to my face, and I watched him see me, truly see me, invisibility and all—"the old ones sent me to find their daughter."

"I'm not—" The word *daughter* went through me like a blade finding a gap in armor. "I don't know what you think I am. I'm nobody. I make maps."

"Then you're the only nobody the mountain ever called by name." He slumped, the strength gone out of him all at once, and pressed something into my hand—the little iron crown, warm as a held bird. "Save him. Please. He was a good man before it found him. Save him from the thing wearing his crown."

"No." I heard my own voice, flat and frightened and final. "No. Find a priest. Find an army. I'm not anyone's daughter and I'm not anyone's savior, and if you tell anyone you met me—"

He smiled, and there was pity in it, which was worse than anger. "Too late for warnings. Look at your hand."

I looked.

Across my palm, rising through the skin like veins of light under ice, a mark was burning itself into me: a branching shape, root and river and mountain-path all at once, gold fading to a dull ember-red. It did not blister. It did not bleed. It simply *was*, the way my name was, the way the buried word Vesna was—part of me, surfacing at last, refusing to stay put.

"No," I said again, uselessly, to the shrine, to the mountain, to whatever patient thing had waited four hundred years for me to walk this particular pass on this particular morning.

Brother Emeric's breathing slowed. I knelt beside him, all my refusals suddenly very small, and stayed while the light in the shrine dimmed from blaze to ember to a faint, contented glow—satisfied, somehow. Fed. He died just past sunset, quietly, holding nothing, wanting nothing, his last word half a syllable of a name that might have been *Vesna* or might have been the wind.

I buried him under cairn-stones because it was right, and because I needed the hour to decide what I already knew.

By full dark I sat by a small fire I'd normally never risk, looking south toward the distant smudge of the capital's lights, the iron crown pendant in one fist and the burning mark across my other palm. The mark pulsed gently, in rhythm, I realized, with my own heartbeat. Or perhaps my heartbeat had agreed to follow it.

Eleven confessors dead. A king being eaten alive. A god's daughter—whoever that was supposed to be—refusing the job.

I told myself I would go north in the morning. Deeper into Thornfell. Chart new ground. Disappear, the way I'd always disappeared.

Then, in the coals, I saw the mark reflected back at me, glowing faintly gold, and understood with cold, absolute certainty that I had just finished mapping the last place in these mountains where I could hide.

South, then. Toward the crown.

Toward the thing wearing it.
