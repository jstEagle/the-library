## 2. A Crown That Devours

Ansel had sold maps in three border towns without anyone asking why a woman with burned hands drew mountains she'd never climbed. The capital was different. The capital watched.

She set up her stall in the Mapwrights' Row on a grey morning, her charts weighted with river stones, and within an hour a customs officer had turned two of them over looking for smugglers' marks. Ansel kept her face bored and her answers dull. *Surveyor's apprentice out of Thornfell. Master died. Selling what he left.* The mark on her palm she kept wrapped in linen, bound tight as a secret, though it throbbed against the cloth like a second pulse.

The palace was visible from half the city — white walls, green roofs, the Tower of Devotion rising at its center like a needle. Pilgrims clogged the streets around it, kneeling in the mud to pray toward a king most of them would never see. Ansel counted eleven shrines between her stall and the Row's end. All of them lit. All of them burning with that same hungry gold she'd seen flare in the pass.

*Prayers are being answered,* Corin had written in the margin of a stolen ledger she'd later find. But that was ahead of her. On this first morning, all she knew was that the whole city glowed like a wound, and no one else seemed to see it.

She saw him on the third day.

King Aldric rode in procession down the Processional Way, and the crowd surged around Ansel like water around a stone. She got one clean look through a gap in the bodies: a broad man in his forties, golden crown, handsome still, but the eyes —

The eyes were wrong. Not mad. Worse than mad. *Empty*, the way a shrine is empty after the god has eaten everything offered and left the shell standing. He smiled at the crowds with his mouth while his gaze slid past them like they weren't there, like they were only fuel. And when he passed the Tower of Devotion he bowed his head to it — not a king's nod to a monument, but a supplicant's bow, deep and practiced and *loving*.

The mark under Ansel's bandage flared so hot she bit her tongue to keep from crying out.

*Save him,* the dying pilgrim had begged. She hadn't promised anything. She was here to confirm a rumor, nothing more. Kings could burn on their own thrones; it was no business of a mapmaker who'd spent thirty years making sure no crown ever learned her name.

But she stayed through the procession's end, and she came back the next day, and the next, learning the guards' rotations, the servants' door rhythms, the hour when the Tower of Devotion's bell tolled thirteen instead of twelve — always thirteen, always unnoticed, as if the extra toll fell into some gap in the world where only she stood.

On the sixth evening, following the thirteenth toll, she slipped into the cloister behind the Tower through a drainage arch she'd charted on a map nobody alive had drawn.

* * *

Corin had been stripped of orders four years ago, and he had learned in that time that exile had a texture: dust in the archive stairwells, the taste of cheap wine, the particular silence of a man whom everyone has agreed to forget. They'd let him keep his little room above the seminary records because forgetting was easier than evicting. He catalogued dead men's correspondence and told himself it was peace.

He noticed the map-seller because she looked at the Tower wrong.

Everyone looked at the Tower of Devotion with love or hunger or the numb habit of the faithful. She looked at it the way he once had, before they took his stole away — like an examiner, like something there needed counting. She came back six days running. On the sixth night, when the tower's shadow lay long across the cloister and the bell rang its hidden thirteenth toll, he followed the flicker of movement by the drainage arch and put his hand on the shoulder of a woman crouched in the dark with charcoal-stained fingers and a bandaged right hand pressed flat against the stones as if listening to them.

"Don't," she said, before he spoke. "Whatever you're about to shout, don't."

"I'm not going to shout."

"Then you're either a fool or—"

"A priest." He heard her laugh — short, bitter. "Formerly. Which makes me exactly the fool you're hoping for, I suspect, since you've spent six days finding a way into a place that will kill you if they catch you, and I'm the only man in this city who won't turn you in."

"Why wouldn't you?"

He considered the question honestly. "Because they took everything from me for asking questions, and you look like someone who knows the answers."

Her hood came back. Sharp face, mountain-grey eyes, a scar through one brow. Hands that had held rope and pen and probably worse. "Ansel Vane," she said finally, like the name cost her something. "Mapmaker."

"Corin. Archivist, formerly of the Order of the Quiet Flame." He hesitated. "And if your hand is doing what I think it's doing to those stones, we should talk somewhere the walls don't listen."

* * *

They talked in the archive, among shelves of confessors' records going back two hundred years, by the light of one shielded lamp. Corin pulled ledgers down with the ease of long habit and laid them open before her: the royal confessors of Aldric's reign, listed year by year.

"Father Ambrose. Father Kellam. Sister Ondrey. Father Maren." His finger moved down the column. "Every confessor the king has had in nineteen years is recorded here as *retired*, *recalled to mother house*, or *died in grace*. Now look at the mother house rolls."

Ansel looked. None of the names appeared. Not one.

"Nineteen years," Corin said quietly. "Nineteen confessors, give or take. Every one of them close enough to the king to hear what he prays, to feel what he feeds that thing in the Tower. Every one of them gone. No body, no farewell letter, no record anywhere but this room — which no one visits, because I am no one."

"You found the gap."

"I found gaps everywhere once I started looking. The thirteenth bell. The shrines relighting after three hundred years dark. The way devotion in this city doesn't comfort people anymore — it drains them. You've felt it too." It wasn't a question. Her bandaged hand twitched. "I was cast out for preaching that the old gods weren't dead, only starved, and that starvation makes things cruel. Everyone called it heresy. Then the shrines started burning again, and the ones who called it heresy knelt down and thanked whatever answered them."

"The king," Ansel said. Her voice came out rough. "I saw him today. There's nothing behind his eyes. Whatever wears that crown, it isn't him anymore."

"It's wearing his love." Corin closed the ledger gently, like covering a face. "Aldric was devout even as a boy. Genuinely good. Something found the purest devotion in the realm and made a meal of it, and now he can't stop offering because part of him — the real him — is still in there, still loving, and it uses his own love as a leash. You don't rescue a man like that by killing the beast. You sever the binding. And the records say—" He stopped.

"Say what?"

"That only blood of the old line can unmake a god-binding." He looked at her bandage then, really looked. "What happened to your hand?"

For a long moment she said nothing. Thirty years of hiding sat in her throat like a stone. But the mark pulsed under the linen, warm, patient, and Corin waited without pressing, the way a man waits who has already lost everything and so cannot be threatened with loss.

"My mother's line goes back further than any kingdom in these mountains," she said at last. "Further than the crown. Further than your church." She unwound the bandage.

The sigil shone on her palm — a branching sign like a tree grown inside a star — and the lamp flames in the archive leaned toward it, every one.

Corin didn't recoil. He went very still, the way men go still before a precipice. When he spoke, his voice was almost a whisper, and almost reverent.

"The Thornfell maps," he said. "The ones with the blank spaces in the heart of the range. Half my order believed those blanks were ignorance." His eyes lifted to hers. "They were warnings, weren't they? You've been there."

"No one's been there," Ansel said. "That's rather the point."

"And you're the only blood that can walk in."

"I never said I'd go."

"You didn't have to." He gathered the ledgers, squared their edges, set them back in their places with the care of a man putting the dead to rest. "The king's confession falls at midwinter, when he'll be alone with the thing in the Tower for three nights. After that, whatever holds him will be strong enough to reach past the city walls. I've done the arithmetic, Ansel Vane. We have until midwinter, and I have nothing left to lose, and you—" he paused, chose the words carefully, "—you have a name you've spent your whole life refusing to say."

The lamp flames leaned. Under her skin, the mark burned like a compass needle finding north — pointing not at the Tower, but east, toward the mountains, toward the blank spaces on her own unfinished maps.

"Midwinter," Ansel said, hating how easily the word became a promise.
