## 3. The Unmapped Heart

My maps had always ended in a lie.

Every chart I'd ever drawn of the Thornfells carried the same honest falsehood: a faded line of ink trailing off past the third ridge, annotated in my own careful hand—*terrain unknown, passes unreliable.* It was the safest sentence in cartography. It had kept me alive for twenty years, because no one asks a mapmaker to walk into the blank space.

Now the blank space was asking for me.

The sigil on my left hand had not dimmed since the shrine. If anything it had grown more insistent, a brand of interlocking lines that glowed faint gold whenever we climbed higher, and pulsed like a heartbeat when we stopped. By the fourth day it had begun to itch. By the sixth, it had begun to *speak*—not in words, exactly, but in pulls. A tug east when we should have gone west. A warmth that said *yes* to one canyon and went cold at another.

"It's steering us," Corin said, watching me flex my fingers by the fire. "That thing on your hand. You know that, don't you?"

"I know."

"And you're letting it."

I fed a branch to the flames. "It's the only thing up here that knows where it's going. That's either a comfort or a trap, and I haven't decided which."

Corin sat across from me with his stolen priest's collar stuffed in his pack and his questions worn openly on his face, which was, I'd learned, how he did everything. He'd been stripped of his orders for asking why the royal confessors kept vanishing. He'd been exiled from the only belonging he'd ever known for refusing to stop counting the dead. And he'd still chosen to follow me into mountains that ate pilgrims, because the alternative was a warm room and a clean conscience, and Corin had never once in his life managed to have both.

"Ansel." He said my name carefully, like it might be another buried thing I'd flinch at. "When this is done—whatever's waiting up there—what happens to you? Have you thought about that? The god-touched places answer you. What if they don't want to let go?"

"Then I'll draw them a map out," I said. "I'm very good at exits."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I.

* * *

We crossed the third ridge at dawn and found my own unfinished work waiting for us.

There, scratched into the rock face where no surveyor could ever have reached, were lines. My lines. The precise double-stroke contour marks I used, the tiny triangles for scree, the dotted trail notation I'd invented myself because no guild would teach a woman their symbols. Someone—or something—had copied my private shorthand onto the mountain itself, a hundred feet of stone turned into an extension of the charts rotting in my satchel.

Corin touched one of the carved contours and pulled his hand back fast. "Cold," he breathed. "It's cold like a grave."

"It's an invitation," I said. My voice came out flat. I made it come out flat; underneath it, everything was shaking. "It's been reading my maps. All these years, while I told myself I was hiding, something down there was studying me right back."

The pass beyond the carvings didn't behave like stone. The air warmed as we descended, which was wrong—the deeps of the Thornfells should have been frozen—and a smell rose to meet us, green and loamy, the smell of turned earth and growing things. In the black heart of a mountain range where nothing grew, we walked into the smell of a garden.

The sigil burned so bright it lit our way.

* * *

We came out of the tunnel into light.

Not sunlight. Something older and greener, a glow rising out of the ground itself, and the ground was *alive*. A cavern vast enough to hold a cathedral opened around us, and every inch of its floor was growth—vines thick as my waist, trees with silver bark growing toward a ceiling of stone, flowers blooming in colors I had no names for and therefore no power over. At the center, ringed by all of it, stood a shape.

It had once perhaps been worshipped. Now it was mostly hunger. The figure was tall, wrong-angled, its outline flickering like a candle in wind—sometimes a robed majesty, sometimes a starved animal, sometimes nothing but a hollow where devotion should have been. Its eyes found us across the garden, and they found *me*, and it smiled with something that wasn't a mouth.

*Daughter,* it said, without sound. The word landed in my bones. *Daughter of the buried ones. Come to feed me at last.*

"You're the thing in the crown," I said.

*I am older than crowns.* It drifted closer, and the vines bent away from it, ashamed. *I was a god before your kings learned to write. They loved me once. They built me gardens and sang me names, and then they built better gods, louder ones, and forgot mine. Do you know what forgetting does to a thing like me, daughter? It starves you slowly. For six hundred years I have been starving in the dark. So I grew clever. I found a man who needed something to worship more than he needed his own soul, and I climbed into his crown, and I drank.*

"Aldric," Corin whispered beside me. His face was grey. "All those confessors. It ate them when they got too close to the truth."

*I ate their faith. The truth they brought with them was gristle.* The god's attention never left me. *But you—you are the old blood. The last of it. Your veins carry what my starving body lacks. Give yourself to me freely, daughter, and I will release the king. Refuse, and I will take you anyway, and him too, and wear this kingdom until nothing is left but my name.*

"There's another option," I said quietly. "Unmake the binding. My blood can do that too. Can't it?"

The garden went still. Every leaf, every vine—even the light seemed to hold its breath.

*Yes,* the god said at last, and the hunger in its voice turned cunning. *Or inherit. Those are the doors your blood opens, little mapmaker. Severance or succession. Unmake me and walk free—or take my place, take the crown, take the feeding, and be a god forever. Choose.*

And that was when Corin stepped forward.

"No," I said, sharp enough to startle both of them.

"She's not choosing alone." Corin planted himself between me and the god, arms slightly out, ridiculous and magnificent, a disgraced priest with no orders and no weapon standing against a starving deity. "You need blood of the old line—I heard you say it. Mine isn't old. But I've spent thirty years learning how devotion works. How it binds. How it feeds. Let me offer what I have instead. Take a willing heart over a reluctant one. Even you must see the arithmetic."

"That's not how it—" My voice cracked. I started again. "That is not how any of this works, you absolute fool, it needs *my blood*, it said—"

"Then I'll carry the knife," he said. "You shouldn't have to cut yourself open alone. That's all I'm saying. Whatever door opens next, you don't walk through it by yourself." He looked at me then, and all the careful questions were gone from his face, and what replaced them was worse. Simpler. "I followed you into the dark, Ansel. Did you think that was duty?"

The garden waited. The god waited, patient as erosion, because it knew—it had surely known for centuries—that love offered at a threshold was the sweetest prayer of all.

And I understood, standing there between a starving god and a man who would die for me without hesitating, that I had already answered the question I'd refused to ask. Somewhere between the capital's rooftops and the third ridge, between stolen records and shared fires, I had fallen in love with him, and I had hidden that too, because hiding was the only skill I'd ever trusted.

No longer.

"I love you," I said, aloud, in the hearing of a god, and the sigil on my hand flared white. "So put your knife away, Corin, because tomorrow I'm going to stop being invisible—and I'd rather do it with you watching."

Beyond him, the god smiled its mouthless smile, and the garden began to bloom.

* * *

That night we slept in the silver-barked grove, close enough to touch, far enough to breathe, while the light under the earth pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. And in my dreams I saw the garden again—but changed. Crowned. Waiting for a queen.

The sigil burned through my bandages, and this time, when it pulled, it pulled downward, into the roots, into the place where the god kept whatever remained of its true name.

One night left. One choice. Sever—or succeed.

I lay awake until dawn, memorizing the sound of Corin breathing, and drew my final map on the inside of my own eyelids.
