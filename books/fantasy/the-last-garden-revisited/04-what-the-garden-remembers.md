## 4. What the Garden Remembers

The tunnel opened like a throat, and we fell into light.

Not sunlight—nothing so honest—but a pale gold glow rising from everywhere at once, from the moss on the walls and the water running in silver threads down the stone. Ansel Vane had mapped four hundred miles of the Thornfells, and she had never once drawn this.

A garden. Grown wild inside the mountain's ribs.

Trees that had never known weather leaned against the cavern ceiling, their branches fused with rock, their leaves translucent as stained glass. Flowers I had no names for crowded the ground in drifts, blooming and browning and blooming again in fast-forward centuries. At the center, where my burning hand pointed without mercy, stood something that had once been an orchard wall—and was now a throne of woven roots, and on it sat the god.

It looked like King Aldric. Of course it did. It wore him the way a leech wears blood.

The king knelt before his own likeness—or its likeness of him—a hollow man in a hollow crown, lips moving through prayers he no longer understood. His eyes were open but nobody lived behind them. Between each whispered devotion, the thing on the throne drank. I could see it happening: thin golden threads running from the king's mouth to the entity's chest, threads of light being swallowed, and with every swallow the king grew grayer, thinner, more transparent, like a map left too long in the rain.

"There you are," the god said, and its voice was Aldric's voice, and then it wasn't—it was a hundred voices layered over each other, priests long dead, confessors vanished, all of them praying in chorus forever. "The last daughter comes home."

"I don't come home," I said. "I came to take something back."

"Take?" It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was terrible because it was gentle. "Child. I only ever wanted what they wanted to give. They prayed. I answered. Is that devouring? Is love devouring?"

"Yes," said Corin, beside me, his stolen priest's medallion clenched in his fist. "When it's a lie. When they pray to be held and you hold them so hard they stop breathing." He stepped forward, and I grabbed his arm—the same arm I'd wanted to grab for weeks now and never let myself—and pulled him back.

"You offered," I said to Corin quietly. On the threshold, hours ago, he'd offered to take my place, and I'd realized with a lurch that I loved him, which was the most inconvenient discovery of my life. "You don't get to unoffer and then die anyway. That's not how this works."

"And how does it work?"

I looked at my hand. The sigil the shrine had burned into me glowed through my glove now, through skin, through everything—my mother's mark, my grandmother's, all the way back to women who had spoken with gods as easily as with neighbors. The old line. My line. The one I'd spent thirty years erasing from every record, every introduction, every mirror.

Only blood of the old line can unmake the binding. Or inherit it.

The starving god watched me work it out, patient as stone. It had waited three hundred years inside this garden; it could wait while a coward finished her arithmetic.

* * *

Here is what hiding costs, that no one tells you: not safety. You pay in something else. You pay in name.

My mother died calling me by a word I refused to answer to. I buried her alone because burying her meant standing in a village square where people might see me weep, and weeping would raise questions, and questions would lead to the truth under my gloves, and the truth would lead here—to torches, or worse, to worship. So I chose the margin. I drew maps of places I'd been and called myself a surveyor, a traveler, a woman with no history worth recording. I made myself a footnote in my own life.

And the mountain had heard every prayer I'd ever pretended not to pray. *Let me belong somewhere. Let someone know me whole.* I'd whispered them into campfires for decades, and they'd landed nowhere—except, apparently, here, in this garden, in the ears of a thing that fed on longing.

That was how I understood it, finally. Not as a monster. As a mirror.

"You've been answering prayers," I said aloud, walking forward. "But you've never had one answered yourself. Three hundred years of taking worship and none of it fills you, because it isn't given freely. It's taken from a man who doesn't even know your name."

The god's borrowed face flickered—for one heartbeat it was not Aldric at all but something vast and starved and terribly young, a child-god abandoned by its people when the old gods went silent. "They forgot me."

"They forgot everyone," I said. "My family included. We hid instead of grieving. Maybe if we hadn't—" My voice caught. "Maybe there would have been someone left to remember you."

Behind me, Corin made a sound—not words, just breath, the sound of a man watching someone decide.

I stopped before the root-throne. The king knelt between us, gray as ash, still whispering. Close enough now to touch the threads of light pouring out of him.

There was a way to do this that kept my secret. I could feel it, the way you feel a door in a dark room: I could bleed just enough, offer just enough, stay half-hidden even now, trade a little divinity for a little anonymity, walk away diminished but safe. The coward's door. I had walked through doors like it my entire life.

Instead, I took off my glove.

Then the other one. Then I pulled back my hood, and stood in the god's garden with my marked hands open at my sides, and said the name I had not said since I was nine years old, since the night my mother pressed her signet into my palm and told me never, ever to speak it:

"My name is Ansel Vane. And before that—before the margins, before the maps—I am Ansel of the Old Line. Daughter of Maren. Granddaughter of Ysolde. Last daughter of the gods who planted this garden." My voice didn't shake. Thirty years of silence had saved up all its force for this moment. "I am not invisible. I am not a footnote. I remember you."

The sigil on my hand opened like a flower.

Light poured out of me—not taken, *given*, freely, the thing the starving god had never once received—and the garden turned toward me. Every translucent leaf. Every impossible bloom. The roots of the throne unwove themselves, reaching, and I let them reach, and the old gods answered.

Not voices. Presence. A warmth moving up through the stone floor and into my bones, ancient and enormous and unmistakably *family*, and I heard—felt—knew, in the marrow-way that needed no language: **Our daughter. Our daughter. Ours.**

The binding snapped.

I felt it go—a thread parting somewhere far above us, in a palace chapel, around a crowned head—and the golden threads feeding the entity simply dissolved, and King Aldric crumpled forward onto the moss like a puppet with cut strings.

The starving god looked at me with its own face at last—young, exhausted, relieved—and began, gently, to fall apart. Not dying. *Planted.* It came apart into motes of gold that sank into the garden soil, and where each mote landed, a new flower opened, and the flowers stayed open this time, unhurried, real.

"Thank you," it said, in a voice like the last note of a hymn, and was earth.

* * *

King Aldric wept for a long time.

He knelt in the moss of a garden no map had ever shown and sobbed like a boy, and when he finally found words they were small ones: "How long. How long has it been." And Corin, kneeling beside him with his hand on the king's shoulder, said, "Eleven years, Majesty. But you're out now. That's the only arithmetic that matters."

The king looked up at me—at my bare marked hands, my uncovered face—and whatever he saw there, he bowed his head. "Daughter of the old line," he said hoarsely. "Name your price."

"Maps," I said. "Access. The right to chart every god-touched place in these mountains, openly, under my own name. And a pardon for a disgraced priest who asked too many questions."

Corin made a noise that was mostly a laugh and mostly not.

We walked out of the mountain the next morning, the three of us, following my own unfinished lines to the pass where the first shrine had flared alive. Behind us, sealed by the old gods' will, the garden slept and grew. Ahead, the world.

Corin fell into step beside me, our shoulders touching, neither of us moving away. "So," he said. "Ansel of the Old Line. Sounds strange?"

"Sounds true," I said, and found that it did.

At the pass's mouth, the shrine was waiting—and it was not alone. Down the valley, shrine after shrine kindled along the ridgelines, one after another, like watchfires answering each other, all the way toward the capital. The old gods were waking everywhere at once. And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came bells—Aldric's capital ringing, though no feast day explained it—as if the whole kingdom had somehow already begun to hear that its king was coming home human, and that the last daughter of the old gods was bringing him.
