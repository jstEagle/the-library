## 1. Salt on the Mountain Road

There are three rules to running moon-herb over the Thornfells, and I had learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never carry more than you can bury in an hour. Two: never sleep below the treeline on a waning moon, because that's when the Ministry's lantern-ships come over the pass like fat pale moths. Three — and this is the one that separates the smugglers who retire from the ones who end up as cautionary tales told in low voices over bad beer — never let anyone learn your face well enough to miss you when you're gone.

Six years of being no one. It sounds like a tragedy when I say it like that, and most nights it isn't. Most nights it's a trade like any other. You give up your name, your history, the shape of your own laugh, and in exchange nobody comes for you in the dark. In this empire, that is generally considered a fair rate. People have sold more for less. People sell their mothers' faces every day at the Ministry of Continuity's little kiosks, trading a memory of a wedding for a ration chit, and they walk out lighter and grateful and unable to say why the corners of their eyes are wet.

I don't trade. I just don't have anything left to trade. That's different. I've checked.

The pass was called the Shepherd's Tooth, though no shepherd living remembered why, which in Thornfell meant either that the Ministry had been through or that the mountain had simply outlasted everyone who cared. It was a narrow throat of grey stone with a stream down the middle that froze by dusk and thawed by lies, and I came up it in the blue hour before dawn with forty weight of dried moon-herb sewn into the lining of a coat that had once belonged to someone braver about buttons.

Moon-herb is not illegal, technically. Nothing is illegal anymore; that would require the things it replaced to be legal first, and the Ministry does not legislate against what does not exist. Moon-herb is simply *unlicensed*, which is worse. It grows only above the snowline, silver-leaved, and when you brew it people dream — vividly, stubbornly, in color — and dreams are contraband of a kind. The Ministry prefers its citizens' nights as blank as their histories. A population that dreams starts asking what the dreams are *of*, and questions are how countries come back from the dead.

So: forty weight of herb, one coat, one woman-shaped person climbing a frozen creek bed in the dark, humming nothing, thinking nothing, being nobody with great professionalism.

That was the plan, anyway. The mountains had other ideas.

* * *

I smelled them before I saw them. That was the first wrong thing.

Wildflowers have a smell like any other living thing — green and faintly sweet and entirely unremarkable — but these smelled *loud*, if that makes sense, like a chord struck in a quiet room. Sweet rocket and something like clover and something else underneath, older, that I had no word for and that made the back of my teeth ache the way a struck tuning fork aches in your jawbone.

I stopped. In my line of work, stopping is death, but I stopped, because the second wrong thing was the light. The sky was still the bruised purple of false dawn, and yet ahead of me, around the bend where the stream cut through a shelf of rock, there was a glow. Not firelight. Firelight flickers and breathes. This light *stood*. Pale gold, steady, patient, like lamplight in a window of a house that has been waiting a long time for someone to come home.

Smugglers do not investigate mysterious lights. Smugglers go around, and if they cannot go around, they go back, and if they cannot go back, they lie down and die quietly so as not to inconvenience anyone. I want that on the record. I am, by every measure that matters, a coward with good boots.

I went toward it.

In my defense — and I have thought about my defense for six years, polishing it like a stone in my pocket — the smell did something to me. The clover-and-old-things smell got into my head the way water gets into a crack in rock, finding some fault line I didn't know was there, and it pushed, gently, the way you'd push open a door you've been told a thousand times is locked, and behind the door was a feeling I can only describe as *homesick*. Homesick for a place I had never been. For a kitchen I couldn't picture. For a word I couldn't remember, sitting right at the tip of my tongue like a loose tooth.

I rounded the bend, and there was the shrine.

Or rather, there was where a shrine had been, because shrines don't survive the Ministry. I knew the shape of it the way you know the shape of a scar under clothing — a flat shelf of stone jutting over the stream, its surface too smooth to be natural, a socket worn into the rock where an idol or an offering-stone must once have stood before somebody with a ledger and a chisel unmade it. The empire had been thorough here, as everywhere. Two hundred years thorough. The stone was bare. The god was gone. Everyone said gone. Gone the way Velthmere was gone, which was to say: there had never been anything here at all, and if you thought otherwise, there were gentle men in grey coats with ledgers who could help you think otherwise.

And growing out of the socket, out of the bare stone, out of the cracks between the stones, were wildflowers. Hundreds of them. Rocket and clover and the old unnamed thing, blooming in a fist of frost and late autumn, in a place where nothing had bloomed in living memory, glowing faintly gold like they'd swallowed a piece of dawn each and were saving it.

I stood there with my illegal coat full of illegal herbs and I did the arithmetic. Flowers don't grow in stone. Flowers especially don't grow in stone in the killing season. And they absolutely, categorically, do not glow.

Which meant one of three things. Either I was dreaming — possible; moon-herb dust gets into everything, including, occasionally, the smuggler — or the Ministry was running some kind of lure, which didn't fit because lures are for catching people who matter, and I had spent six years making myself the least catchable kind of person there is, the kind whose own reflection would struggle to pick her out of a lineup.

Or the third thing. The thing the oldest smugglers muttered about when the beer was bad enough and the night long enough. That the old gods weren't dead, only sleeping, and that the sleeping was ending, and that the mountains remembered even if we were not permitted to.

I crouched down — carefully, coat held away from the wet — and looked at the flowers properly. Up close the glow was stranger still. It wasn't coming off them like heat off a stove. It was coming *out* of them, the way meaning comes out of a word, and when I looked directly at the brightest bloom I felt that homesickness again, that fault-line push, that door easing open —

— and something said my name.

Not *Rosalind*. I need to be clear about that, because the difference is the whole story. Nobody has said *Rosalind* in six years. Out here I'm Moss, when I'm anything, and half the time not even that. What the flowers said — what the mountain said through the flowers, in a voice that was small and cracked and rusty as a gate unused for two centuries — was my name. The real one. The whole one. The one I gave away or lost or buried, the one with all its syllables intact, the one that apparently had edges I'd forgotten, because hearing it was like being gripped firmly by the back of the neck by a hand made of summer.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then the wind shifted, the glow guttered down to almost nothing, and the flowers were just flowers again — impossible, out-of-season, glowing faintly, but just flowers, minding their business in a socket where a god used to live.

I ran. I want that on the record too. Whatever ballads eventually get sung about this — and something tells me, looking back from here, that ballads get sung — let it be known that my first response to the divine was to run downhill like a rabbit with a price on it, which, to be fair, I also was.

* * *

I made the drop by noon, which was sloppy of me, because I wasn't paying attention. The buyer was a flat-faced woman called Dagny who ran a wool shed two valleys over and paid in coin and never asked questions, which I had always assumed was professional discretion and now suspected was simple mercy.

"You're white," she said, counting coins. "Altitude?"

"Something like that."

"See something on the Tooth?" Her eyes came up, quick and flat. "Lights? Growing things?"

So she'd heard. Of course she'd heard; the passes talk to each other faster than any post-road ever managed. I shrugged with the practiced laziness of a person to whom nothing has ever happened. "Frost and rocks. Same as always."

"Mm." She counted the last coin, then didn't hand it over. "Old Halversen swears he saw a flowering up by the Widow's Ledge. Swears the stone was warm." She turned the coin over her knuckles. "Halversen also swears his wife comes back to him in dreams, so draw your own line."

"What line would you draw?"

Dagny looked at me a moment too long, the way people look at a door they've decided not to open. Then she flipped me the coin. "I'd draw the line that keeps me paid," she said. "But I'll tell you for free: if the mountains start saying names again, best hope yours isn't in the list. Grey coats come fast when the dead start talking."

She went back to her wool. I went back to my coat and my silence and the long walk down into the valley, doing what I do best, which is being no one very thoroughly while inside my skull a single word rang like a bell that wouldn't stop.

My name. My whole name, with its roots and branches, the one I hadn't heard since — since when? That was the horror of it. I reached for the memory of losing it and found smooth walls, the particular seamless blankness that means work was done, that means somebody with a ledger sat across from you and ate. Six years ago, then. Or eight. There had been a cottage, hadn't there? A teacher? A woman with ink-stained fingers and a temper like a struck match — Maren, something said, Maren — and a night, and firelight, and running. But when I pressed on the memory it went soft as wet paper, and underneath the softness was nothing, and underneath the nothing was that same homesick ache, the fault line, the door.

You can live a long time as a hole wearing a person's shape. I know because I'd done it. You eat, you walk, you count your goods, you memorize the patrol schedules, and the hole doesn't hurt exactly, it's just *there*, like a tooth gone missing — your tongue keeps going back to it, keeps checking, keeps finding absence where presence should be, until checking becomes a habit and the habit becomes the person.

Except now something had put a finger in the hole. Something small and starving and two hundred years patient, sitting in a flowerpot of a shrine in a pass where nothing grew, and it had said my name like a key turning, and the bell was ringing, and I could feel it echoing outward. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee.

Grey coats come fast when the dead start talking, Dagny had said. She was right, and she was wrong, and before the week was out I'd understand both halves of it. Because the dead hadn't just talked. They'd talked to *me*. Out of all the smugglers and shepherds and wool-traders crossing those passes, out of all the hollowed-out nobodies the empire had manufactured by the thousands, the flowers had chosen the one person carrying a name she couldn't hear.

I camped that night above the treeline, breaking rule two on purpose, because the alternative was sleeping in the valley among people, and I suddenly couldn't bear the thought of anyone seeing my face while it was busy not being no one's. I built a small fire, brewed a pinch of the herb — my own pay, technically theft, technically whatever — and drank it down, and lay back against my pack, and waited to dream.

What I dreamed, insofar as it was a dream, was a garden. A gate of white wood. A wall covered in roses that had no business growing anywhere, and beyond it a country I did not recognize and recognized completely: green hills, stone bridges, banners with a sigil like a seed split open, a language rising off the fields like heat shimmer. Someone was singing in the garden, far away, a song I almost knew, and the song had words, and the words were—

I woke at dawn with frost on my eyelashes and my cheeks wet and no memory of the words, only the shape they'd left, which was the exact shape of my own name.

I sat up. I looked east, where the valley fell away toward the lowlands, toward roads and markets and grey coats and the enormous machinery of forgetting. Behind me, north, the high passes glittered, and somewhere up there a shrine stood warm and blooming and waiting.

Being no one had kept me alive for six years. That was the arithmetic, clean and cold. No name, no trail, no grave.

But the mountains had said my name, and the bell wouldn't stop ringing, and for the first time since I'd run — run from *what*, from *whom*? — I caught myself wanting to know the answer more than I wanted to be safe.

"Damn you," I said aloud, to the flowers, to the mountain, to whoever was listening. My breath smoked in the cold. "Whatever you are. Damn you for knowing."

Far above me, at the top of the pass, something answered. Not in words. Just a warmth on the wind, faint and gold, like lamplight in a window — and beneath it, unmistakable now that I'd heard it once, the sound of a gate beginning, after two hundred years, to unlock.

I shouldered my pack and started walking. North. Toward the shrine. Toward the name.

Toward everything I'd spent six years making sure I'd never find.
