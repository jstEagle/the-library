## 2. The Apprentice Who Ran

The trouble with running away from a teacher is that you carry her with you. Every herb I cut on the Thornfell passes, I cut the way Maren taught me—thumb and forefinger, never the blade against the stem—and every time my hands did it without asking me first, I hated her a little, and missed her more than was convenient.

Six years is a long time to be no one. It's also, as it turns out, not long enough.

I was three days out from the shrine with the impossible flowers when the weather turned, and the weather turning gave me an excuse I didn't need: the high road past Coldmoss was closed by sleet, but there was a lower path, an old one, that wound down through the birches toward the valley where I'd spent the only childhood anyone had bothered to give me. Smugglers don't take that path. Smugglers have no business there, no debts, no history. Which is exactly why, when the sleet came hissing sideways off the peaks, I told myself it was the sensible route, and took it.

My feet knew better. My feet walked it like a psalm.

* * *

Maren Osgood found me the winter I was nine, stealing her onions.

I want to be clear about this, because everyone in the valley told it differently afterward—I wasn't stealing much. Three onions. Maybe four. The Hollowbrook winters were lean, and my mother had died the autumn before, and my father's hands had been crushed in the quarry the spring before *that*, and there are only so many ways a child can be hungry before hunger starts making decisions for her. So: onions. I had them inside my coat, warm against my ribs, and I was halfway over Maren's wall when she said, from the doorway, "You're holding your breath. Thieves who hold their breath trip."

I tripped.

She came down the garden path with the unhurried gait of a woman who has never once been chased by anything, took the onions out of my coat one by one, and looked at me the way I'd later learn she looked at everything—with that flat grey appraisal, like she was pricing me at market and finding the seller optimistic.

"You're Dunmore's girl," she said. "The quarry man."

"Rosalind," I said, because even starving, I wasn't going to be *Dunmore's girl*. I was nobody's girl. That was rather the whole problem.

"Rosalind." She tried it out like a coin bitten between the teeth. "Hm." And then, instead of beating me or turning me over to the reeve or any of the sensible things, she said, "Come inside. You'll weed the feverfew bed while you eat. If you're going to steal from a hedge-witch, girl, you may as well learn what's worth stealing."

That was the apprenticeship. There was no contract, no ceremony. There was a bowl of barley soup and a bed of weeds and a cottage that smelled of camphor and woodsmoke and something underneath both, something older, like the smell of a room that has been locked a long time. I weeded the feverfew. Then I stayed for the evening. Then I stayed for the winter. Nobody in Hollowbrook asked questions about a parentless girl taken in by the witch on the hill, because the valley had learned, in the way valleys do, that some things are safer unexamined.

She taught me the craft the way water teaches stone—slowly, patiently, wearing grooves into me I didn't notice until years later, when everything I thought and felt ran along channels she'd carved. How to read the health of a hive by its hum. How to set a bone and how to tell when a bone should be left alone because the body has already decided to die around it. How to brew willow-bark bitter enough that even stubborn men would rest. And then—the part she taught last, reluctantly, in a voice dropped low enough that the fire itself seemed to lean in—how to work memory.

Not the empire's kind of memory-work. She was very clear on that. "What they do in the capital," she said, "is eating. They take a thing whole and leave a hole, and the hole grows edges, and the edges grow teeth until the person eats *themselves* trying to find what's missing. What I'm teaching you is gardening. You can prune a recollection. You can graft one onto another. You can bury a thing so deep it sleeps. But you don't take, Rosalind. Promise me. Whatever you become, you don't take."

I promised. I was twelve. I promised everything at twelve.

And I was good at it. That's the shameful part, looking back—not that I learned, but how *easily*. Other apprentices (she'd had two before me; both left, both ordinary) struggled for a year just to touch the surface of a client's grief. I could slip under a memory like a hand under a door. The first time I eased a nightmare out of a shepherd's sleeping mind—a simple pruning, a dead branch removed—he woke weeping with gratitude and couldn't say why, and Maren watched me across the hearth with an expression I mistook, at the time, for pride.

It wasn't pride. I know that now. It was the look of a woman watching a lock turn smoothly in a door she'd hoped was rusted shut.

* * *

Here is what I remembered of the night I ran, for six years, and what I clung to like a raft: Maren went strange. Maren began locking her workroom. Maren started saying odd things—"Do you dream in colors, girl? Do you ever taste words?"—and writing letters she burned unread, and standing at her window at night looking north toward the capital with her arms folded like a fortress under siege. And then one night in my fifteenth year I woke to smoke and found her crouched over her hearth, feeding a page into the flames, a page covered edge to edge in handwriting I somehow knew was mine though I had never written it, and when I cried out she turned with the page half-consumed and said the words I built my entire exile upon:

"It isn't safe for you here anymore. They've found the thread. You have to go and you mustn't remember why."

So I went. Out the window, down the hill, through the birches, taking nothing but the clothes I wore and a hatred that fit neatly in the space where the burned page used to be. Six years of smuggler roads and false names and moon-herb runs through imperial checkpoints, six years of being no one with tremendous skill. Six years of being safe.

Safe. Ha. Let me put that word down gently, the way you put down something you've just realized is broken.

* * *

Because here is what I remembered on the lower path, walking down through the sleet into the valley with my feet reciting a psalm and the impossible flowers still a bruise-colored smudge behind my eyes: I remembered that I had gone back. Once. Two days after I fled, cold and hungry and furious, certain I'd misjudged her, certain the old woman had simply lost her nerve or her mind. I had stood in the birches outside her window at dusk and watched her through the glass, sitting motionless at her table with the hearth burned down to coals, and I had been too proud to knock.

And I remembered—or the remembering rose up out of some sealed place and hit me like a landslide—that she hadn't been alone at the table.

There had been a second figure. Tall, grey-cloaked, faceless in the way of things seen once and immediately forbidden. And Maren had been speaking to it, hands flat on the table, and the posture of her was not surrender. It was negotiation. It was *stalling*.

The page she burned hadn't been betrayal. It had been triage.

I stopped in the middle of the path and let the sleet hit me, because I needed something colder than my own thoughts. Six years. Six years I'd carried that night like a knife between my shoulder blades, aimed inward, and the whole time the knife had been pointing the other direction. She hadn't tried to erase me. She'd tried to protect whatever that page held—from them, from the tall grey thing, from the empire whose Inquisitors I'd spent six years learning to dodge by instinct, the way you learn to flinch from a whip before you remember being beaten.

*They've found the thread.*

Found it leading where? Found it leading to whom?

The valley opened below me, grey and smoking with woodsmoke, and there on its hill sat the cottage, small and dark against the failing light, exactly where six years of my anger had kept it.

I went down to it. What else does guilt know how to do?

* * *

The door was open. Not unlocked—open, hanging by its lower hinge, splintered where something had forced it, and the snow of two days' sleet lay drifted across the threshold like the house had been holding its breath since then.

Inside, the ransacking had been thorough and wrong. Thieves wreck a place for value and leave the worthless; whoever had done this had wrecked it for *meaning*. Jars of dried herbs swept and ground underfoot—not the valuable ones especially, all of them equally, chamomile and hemlock alike, as if the crime were the knowing. Her worktable split down the middle with an axe or something shaped like an axe. The floorboards pried up in every corner. Even the feverfew bed outside, I saw through the shattered window, had been dug over, every root exposed to the frost.

They hadn't found what they wanted. You could read it in the wreckage the way Maren taught me to read a sickroom: the violence of a search that failed, the specific fury of men who break a house open and find it empty of the one thing they came for.

"Maren?" I said it anyway, to the empty room, because hope is stupid and immortal. "Maren, it's—"

I almost said my name. I caught it at the teeth. Six years of discipline, and it nearly died in a ruined kitchen.

No answer. Only the drip of sleet through the broken roof, and the smell—camphor and woodsmoke and that older smell beneath, the locked-room smell, fainter now, fading. A house losing its memory of its owner.

But she'd left things. Of course she had. This was Maren Osgood, who taught me that a hedge-witch's first tool is not the knife or the kettle but the assumption that someone, someday, will kick the door in. I searched the ruin the way she'd taught me to search a patient—gently, systematically, reading absence as carefully as presence. And in the cold hearth, under a fallen beam, wedged into the chimney's throat where fire would never reach unless the whole house burned:

A tin. Inside the tin, wrapped in oilcloth, a key. And tied to the key with waxed thread, a scrap of paper in her crabbed hand, and I knew the handwriting the way you know your own pulse, and it said only:

*"If you are reading this, you remembered. Good. That means they were wrong about you. Cellar. Third stone. Don't come home again after — R."*

R. Not Rosalind. Just the letter, like the ledger-entry Sera Vane would one day show me, the half-eaten name, *R—— D——*, though I didn't know that yet. All I knew was that my teacher had known, six years ago or sixty minutes ago, that the people who broke her house might come back, and had spent her last safe hour not fleeing but leaving me a message addressed to the one person who would understand it: the apprentice who ran.

I knelt in the wreckage of her kitchen with the tin in my hands and let myself feel all of it at once—the guilt, hot as a coal, and under the guilt something harder taking shape, something with edges. She had stalled for me. She had burned a piece of me to buy me time, and paid for it with her house and very likely her freedom, and I had spent six years hating her for it.

Resolve is just guilt that's found a job.

I got up. I took the crowbar from among her scattered tools, because sentiment keeps terrible hours and I intended to open a cellar, and I went to the third stone.

Behind it, in a space no bigger than a bread oven, a bundle of oilcloth, and inside the bundle: papers. Dozens of them, pages dense with her handwriting, diagrams, pressed flowers gone brown as old blood, and on the topmost sheet, underlined twice, a single line that made the ruined cottage seem suddenly, enormously quiet:

*"The empire did not conquer Velthmere. Velthmere was eaten. Read all of it before you pray to anything."*

Before you pray to anything.

As if she knew. As if somewhere in those pages was an explanation for wildflowers blooming out of season above a drowned shrine, for the mountain that had spoken my name in a voice I almost recognized.

Outside, far off down the valley, a dog began to bark, and did not stop.
