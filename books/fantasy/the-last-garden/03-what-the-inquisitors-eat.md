## 3. What the Inquisitors Eat

The valley called itself Harrowmere, though the sign at its mouth said only HARROWMERE and beneath it, in smaller imperial script: *A Prosperous Community of the Protectorate.* Both statements were true the way official things are true—technically, and with an odor of varnish.

I arrived at dusk on a Ministry mule named Deacon, because the Ministry names its mules after saints it has also unmade. There is a certain economy in that. The animal was gray, stubborn, and had eaten my travel rations somewhere outside of Fetter's Cross, so by the time I rode down into Harrowmere I was hungry, saddle-sore, and carrying a ledger that weighed more than my regrets.

Which is saying something. My regrets are substantial.

The Ministry of Continuity does not burn books. That's the first thing people get wrong about us. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. We don't even eat memories ourselves—that's the second thing they get wrong. We *record* them. We write them down, seal them in ledgers, carry them to the capital, and file them in vaults deeper than the roots of mountains, where no one will ever weep over them again. Then we take the memory out of the person who held it, the way a surgeon takes out a bad tooth, and the patient goes home lighter, happier, and entirely unable to tell you what hurt.

Continuity, the Ministry calls it. As if history were a river you could dam from the middle and still expect the downstream villages to believe in rain.

My orders came sealed in black wax with the Ministry's sigil—an open eye inside an open book, which I have always thought should be closed, one or the other, but never both. The paper said:

*Anomalous recollections reported in the Thornfell valleys. Wildflower bloom out of season at site of former shrine (shrine: unrecorded; no such structure exists). Investigate. Identify holders. Unmake as necessary.*

*Unmake as necessary.* Four words doing the work of forty. I have written those words myself. I have watched other Inquisitors write them and go home to their suppers. There is no necessary. There is only what we decide we can live with, and then there is the ledger.

The ledger rode against my ribs in oilcloth, and it was thin this trip—eleven entries, most of them shepherds who'd been seen leaving flowers at a place where flowers shouldn't be. Eleven names. I read them all on the road, the way you check your knives before a job, and then I read the twelfth entry, the one that wasn't supposed to be legible.

Halfway down the last page, in ink that had been struck through twice and then, strangely, half-restored—as if someone had begun the erasure and stopped, or been stopped—the entry read:

*R—— D——*

That was all. A first initial, a surname initial, and between them a gap like a missing tooth. Below it, in the margin, in a hand I did not recognize and which disturbed me more than the entry itself, someone had written: *keystone?*

I sat with that for a long while in the dark of my room at the Waystone Inn, candle guttering, mule complaining in the stable below. An entry half-eaten is not unusual. What is unusual is when the eating stops midway, because the Ministry does not stop midway. The Ministry finishes its sentences.

Unless someone refused.

I put the thought away the way you'd put away a live coal—wrapped, careful, aware it would keep burning through whatever you buried it under—and went down to do my work.

* * *

Inquisitorial interviews are conducted gently. That is doctrine. You sit across from the subject, you pour the tea yourself, you ask small questions with large doors behind them, and you watch the face while the memory surfaces. Fear closes people. Kindness opens them. And once they're open, you reach in and take what you were sent for, and they thank you for it. They always thank you. That's the part I've never gotten used to—not the taking, but the gratitude afterward, warm and confused as a dog licking the hand that pulled its teeth.

The first name on my list was Tam Broadhurst, a shepherd of perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of his own fences. His wife poured me cider before I could refuse it, and he sat across from me twisting his cap and asked, before I'd said anything at all:

"Is it about the flowers?"

There it was. Not even ten seconds off the mule.

"What flowers?" I said, which is doctrine too. Never confirm what you haven't verified.

"The ones up past the split rock." He glanced at his wife, and something passed between them—the look of people who have agreed not to say a thing for so long the agreement has grown bark. "Blooming now. Out of season. Out of *reason*, if you follow me. White ones, and a kind of blue I don't have a word for." He frowned, working at something behind his eyes. "Funny thing is, I know the place they're growing. Know it like I know my own yard. But I couldn't tell you what's *there*, miss. Just—it's a place I've always known. Empty place."

"An empty place you've always known," I repeated.

"That's the size of it." He laughed, embarrassed. "Listen to me. Sounds like a madman."

"It sounds like a man remembering accurately," I said, and watched him go still.

Here is what I did not write in my field notes: that my own chest had tightened when he said *a kind of blue I don't have a word for.* Because I knew a blue like that. Or—I didn't know it, that was the whole point of me, the whole architecture of my training, twenty years of discipline built over a hole shaped like a childhood—but some part of me that predated all of it leaned forward at that blue like a starving thing at a window.

"Tam," I said gently. "Have you been praying?"

He blinked. "Not since I was a boy."

"And when you were a boy—who did you pray to?"

And here is where the interview turned, because Tam Broadhurst opened his mouth to say the answer every soul in the Protectorate gives without thinking—*the Radiant Concord, praise the ninefold light*—and instead his face simply *broke*. Quietly, the way ice breaks, all at once and without sound. Tears ran down into the creases of his cheeks and he didn't wipe them and he didn't seem to know they were happening.

"I don't know," he said, and his voice was a boy's voice for a moment. "Miss, I don't know why I'm crying. I went up there last week and I took my hat off and I don't know *why* I took my hat off, and I stood there and something in me wanted to say a name and there isn't any name, there's just the—" his hand made a shape in the air, cupping nothing, "—the *shape* of a name, like a footprint in snow, and the snow's melted and I keep going back to look at the empty spot—"

His wife had her arms around him. She was crying too. Neither of them knew why. Neither of them could stop.

I gave Tam Broadhurst tea laced with tincture of forget-root, standard procedure, and I told myself it was mercy, and I wrote in the ledger: *Subject retains affective residue without content. Recommend full extraction.* And my pen hesitated over *recommend*, because I realized I had just recommended hollowing out the only honest grief in the valley, and my hand was steady, and that frightened me more than anything Tam had said.

Because the tears weren't anomalous. That was the finding I could not put on paper. The tears were the *correct response* to a correct memory, and the anomaly was everything else—the two hundred years of smooth imperial surface over the wound. These people hadn't misremembered. They had been *misremembered*. Something had reached into them and left the grief and taken the beloved, and grief without an object doesn't fade. It just waits.

* * *

The second interview was a widow named Cass, third on the ledger, who kept bees and spoke to me in a whisper as though the hives might overhear.

"They say the empire conquered us fair," she whispered. "Two hundred years back. Battles, treaties, all proper-like. But my grandmother—" she stopped. Started again. "My grandmother used to hum a lullaby. I know the tune. I could sing you the tune right now, note for note. But there aren't any words anymore. I open my mouth to sing it and the words fall through, like coins through rotten cloth. And I have spent sixty years thinking I was a foolish old woman for mourning a lullaby I couldn't remember."

She looked at me then, directly, with the flat courage of the very old.

"You're one of the eaters, aren't you."

"We don't eat anything, madam. We preserve—"

"You preserve it *somewhere else*," she said. "Out of *us*." She folded her hands. "Well. Take your notes, girl. But I'll tell you what my grandmother's tune sounds like, underneath. It sounds like it's *waiting*. Like the words are standing outside the door of me, knocking, and somebody locked it from the inside."

I extracted her recollections of the shrine site at dusk. Standard procedure. She thanked me. She always would.

I walked out into the street afterward and was sick behind the woodpile, quietly, like a lady, and then I straightened my coat and went back to work, because that is also doctrine, and doctrine is what holds an Inquisitor together when the contents want out.

* * *

By the fourth day I had worked through eight of eleven names, and the pattern was undeniable and unpublishable: every subject retained intact emotion around absent content. Every one of them wept without knowing why. And every one of them, when I pressed on the shape of the absence, produced the same impossible geometry—a *name-shaped hole*. Not a god-shaped hole. A specific one. Singular. As if the valley remembered one particular listener, one particular door that prayer used to knock on.

And the wildflowers kept blooming.

I went up to see them myself on the fifth morning, alone, because there are things you cannot order a witness to. The split rock stood above the tree line in a fold of the mountain, and around its base, in defiance of frost and season and two hundred years of imperial botany, grew drifts of white flowers and that blue—the blue I didn't have a word for, the blue that made something behind my sternum lean against its cage.

No shrine remained. I checked. Stone remembers longer than flesh, and there was nothing—not a footing, not a chip, not a stain of mortar. The ground where the shrine should have been was bare, and the flowers grew in a perfect ring around the bareness, the way grass grows around a grave.

I knelt. Not praying—investigating. Doctrine has a word for everything.

The air above the empty circle was warm. Five degrees, maybe. Noticeably warm, in a wind that should have cut. And in that warmth I heard—or didn't hear; I want to be precise about this—I *almost* heard something that felt like my own voice saying a name I didn't know.

It felt like being recognized by a stranger.

I stood up fast. I am a professional. I have stood in rooms where men begged me with their mothers' voices, and my hands have never shaken. My hands shook now, and I hated them for it, and I got out my ledger to write the observation down and make it small and manageable and filed.

The ledger fell open to the last page on its own weight, the way it had been falling open for five days, the way I had been not-looking at it for five days.

*R—— D——*

*keystone?*

Eight names done. Three to go. And the twelfth entry, the half-eaten one, the one that broke every rule of how the Ministry finishes its sentences—its holder was somewhere in these mountains. Maybe in this valley. Maybe I'd already bought bread from them, or shared a fire, or passed them on the road with a nod between professionals, two nobodies acknowledging each other.

And here is the thought that followed me down the mountain, that sat behind me on Deacon like a second rider, that I carried into the inn that evening and set down on the table beside my supper like a dish I had no appetite for:

Someone had begun to erase R—— D—— and stopped halfway. And the Ministry never stops halfway.

So either the erasure had failed—

Or someone had refused to finish it. Someone who had held the ledger, and held the knife, and looked at the name and the face behind the name, and put the knife down.

I knew exactly what that refusal cost. I was starting to suspect I might know what it felt like from the inside, though I couldn't have said why—couldn't have said why the missing initials itched like a scar of my own, why *R—— D——* sounded almost like a rhythm I'd once known, a hand holding mine, a garden gate—

I closed the ledger.

Tomorrow I would find the holder of the twelfth entry. Tonight I sat in the taproom of the Waystone Inn among smugglers and shepherds, drinking slowly, watching the door, telling myself I was hunting a fugitive of the Ministry.

And every time the taproom door opened, some traitor part of me—some part older than my training, older than my name, planted in me like a seed in a vault—looked up hoping.
