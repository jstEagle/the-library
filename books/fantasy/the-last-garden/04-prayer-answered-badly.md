## 4. Prayer Answered Badly

There are three rules to surviving as a smuggler in the Thornfells, and I had learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

One: never carry more than you can outrun. Two: never sleep in the same hollow twice in a season. Three: never, ever pray.

The third rule wasn't mine originally. It was Maren's, and she'd said it the way she said most important things—offhand, while her hands were busy with something else, so that you almost missed it. *"Gods are like creditors, Rosalind. The moment they know your name, they come collecting. Best to owe them nothing."* She'd been grinding yarrow at the time. She didn't look up. That was how I knew she meant it.

Six years I'd kept that rule without even trying, because praying requires believing someone's listening, and for two hundred years nobody had been. The empire saw to that. Whatever gods the Thornfells used to have were scraped off the mountains along with everything else the Ministry of Continuity decided was inconvenient—which, from what I could gather, was most things. History, mostly. Names. The color of certain doors.

So when I climbed back up to the shrine on the third morning after finding Maren's cottage gutted, it wasn't praying. It was reconnaissance. There's a difference. One is an act of faith; the other is an act of a person with no better ideas.

* * *

Let me tell you about the shrine, since apparently it was going to be important.

It sat in a fold of the mountain where the smuggler's road dipped below the ridgeline—a circle of standing stones, waist-high and lichen-eaten, arranged around a flat slab that might once have been an altar and was now mostly a table for marmots. Smugglers used it as a landmark. *Turn at the stones, count two hundred paces north-northwest, mind the scree.* Nobody knelt there. Nobody left offerings. You don't leave flowers at a grave when you've been taught the person buried in it never existed.

Except.

On my way down into the valley four days ago, the fold of the mountain had been bare. Late autumn, first frost already silvering the shadows. Dead season. Nothing blooms in the Thornfells past harvest except stubbornness.

Now the ground between the stones was a riot of white flowers.

They came up through frost-killed grass like the grass had merely been a suggestion. Moon-pale, five-petaled, nodding in a wind that I could feel against my face—and yet every single bloom leaned toward me, all together, like a room full of people turning when you say something too loud.

I stood very still and did the arithmetic smugglers do. Flowers out of season: strange but survivable. Flowers all facing one direction: also survivable, if the direction is away. Flowers all facing *me*, in a place where a god used to live before the empire ate him:

That was the kind of arithmetic that ended with running.

But Maren was gone. Maren's cottage had been turned inside out by people who knew exactly what they were looking for, and Maren—who had survived forty years of empire attention by being aggressively unremarkable—had not been careful enough, or had been caught anyway, or had run, and I had no way to know which because I hadn't been there, because I'd spent six years making sure I would never be anywhere, and now the one person whose door had ever opened for me had walked through hers and vanished.

Desperate people do stupid things. I'd like to tell you I hesitated longer than I did. I'd like to tell you I remembered rule three.

I crouched at the edge of the flower ring instead.

"All right," I said to the stones, quietly, the way you talk to a horse you don't trust. "I'm not asking for much. I'm not even sure I'm asking. But if something out here knows where Maren Colver went—if she passed this road, if she left word, if she's lying hurt somewhere in these mountains—" My voice cracked on *hurt*, which annoyed me. "Tell me. Please."

Please. Six years without saying it to anything bigger than weather.

The wind stopped.

Not gentled. Not shifted. Stopped, all at once, the way a conversation stops when someone important walks in. The nodding flowers froze mid-sway. The frost on the northern stones glittered without melting, and the light coming down through the pass went the particular gold-gray color of late afternoon even though it was barely midmorning.

And then something spoke.

It said: *"Rosalind."*

My own name. Fine. Expected, even—I'd said Maren's name aloud; names travel. But it said *Rosalind* in a child's voice. A small, high, certain voice, the kind of voice that belongs to someone who has never once been ignored, and it was coming from behind me, and I had checked behind me, I always check behind me, and there had been nothing there but scree and sky.

I turned anyway. Slowly. The way you turn toward a sound you're afraid will stop being friendly if you startle it.

A girl stood among the flowers.

She looked about eight years old. She wore a smock the color of undyed wool and no shoes, and her hair was dark and tangled and her feet didn't quite touch the ground—or maybe they did; the flowers bent under them, or maybe they didn't bend at all, maybe I wanted them to. Her face was—

Her face was mine.

Not similar. Not *resemblance*. Mine. The face I'd worn before I learned to keep it blank, round-cheeked and gap-toothed and lit up with the uncomplicated delight of a child who has found something wonderful, which was, presumably, me.

"Hello," the girl said, with my mouth, in my voice from fifteen years ago, and beamed.

Every animal instinct I owned voted unanimously to leave. I stayed crouched where I was, which tells you something about how badly I needed news of Maren, or possibly about how broken I'd become. Probably both.

"You're not real," I said.

"I'm *very* real," she said, wounded. "I'm just not *here*. There's a difference. There used to be a whole word for it." Her brow furrowed—the same furrow I make when I lose track of a ledger line. "I can't find the word. Someone took it. Someone took lots of things." She looked down at her hands, turned them over, as if checking they were still loaded. Then she brightened again, terribly, the way children do, grief passing through them like weather. "You prayed!"

"I asked a question."

"You *prayed*." She said it with enormous satisfaction, tasting it. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone prayed? Two hundred years and eleven days. I counted. Counting is nearly all I have left." She began walking toward me across the flowers, and the flowers leaned after her the way they'd leaned after me, and I noticed that where she stepped, the frost melted and refroze in patterns too regular to be accidental. Spirals. Sigils. Writing in a script I almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a language from a dream.

"Stop," I said. "Stay there."

She stopped. Immediately, obediently, which frightened me more than anything else, because things that obey you are usually waiting for something.

"You're a god," I said. It sounded absurd. It sounded like the beginning of a story told drunk. "Or you're wearing a god. Or I'm having a stroke on a mountainside."

"I'm what's left of one," she agreed cheerfully. "Like soup after the meat's been eaten. Like a song where someone's hummed over the words." She tilted her head, studying me with my own eyes. "You're the one who has the words, actually. Isn't that funny? They hid them inside you. And then they hid *you* inside yourself, and everyone forgot to look, including you. Very tidy. Empire work. They love tidy."

My mouth had gone dry. "Who are you?"

"Ah." She spread her arms, presenting herself, and for a heartbeat the air around her rippled like heat over a forge, and I glimpsed—just glimpsed—something vast moving underneath the child-shape, something with too many joints and a patience like stone, and every flower in the ring turned black for the length of a breath and then white again. "That," she said, and her voice layered, briefly, into a hundred voices, into a chorus, into wind through a doorway, "is the question. That's the whole question. That's the question the empire spent two hundred years making sure no one could ask."

She dropped her arms. The ripple stilled. Just a girl again, gap-toothed, waiting.

"They took my name," she said simply. "Names are how we hold our shape. Without mine I'm—" she gestured at herself, at the flowers, at the frozen wind, "—this. Borrowing. Wearing whatever memory drifts close enough. Right now I'm wearing yours, because yours are the closest and the loudest and—" her face did something complicated, my face doing something complicated, "—the saddest. Yours are the saddest memories on this mountain, Rosalind Dunmore."

"That's not my name."

"It isn't, is it?" She smiled, gentle now, and somehow that was worse. "Funny thing about a stolen name. You can't miss it properly, because missing it means remembering it. So you just feel like a coat that doesn't fit. All the time. Your whole life." She crouched down to my level, and her knees made a child's popping sound, which was such a mundane, human detail that my eyes burned. "I felt yours anyway. Through the flowers. Through the stones. You walked past four days ago and something in you *ached*, and ache leaves a shape, and your shape fits me."

"I want news of Maren Colver," I said, holding on to that like a rope over a drop. "That's all I came for. Tell me where she is, or what happened to her cottage, or—"

"Maren Colver." The girl rolled the name around like a marble. "Hedge-witch. Keeper. One of the ones who remembered on purpose, quietly, for years, feeding little crumbs of us to the soil so we wouldn't starve completely." Something moved across her borrowed face—not anger. Grief, old and enormous, compressed down small enough to fit in a child. "Men came. Gray coats. One carried a book that eats. They took her *upward*, toward the capital road, and they took other things too. Things she was keeping." She looked at me steadily. "She kept you best of all. They didn't find that part. It's still hidden. Even from you."

Gray coats. The Ministry. An Inquisitor in the valley, hunting anomalous recollections—that was the rumor in the low-town inn, whispered, half-disbelieved. And Maren taken up the capital road in their custody, alive, presumably, because the Ministry didn't kill keepers. Killing wasted material. They'd want to know where she'd hidden what she was keeping.

Where she'd hidden me.

"Can I get her back?"

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Wind held its breath. Somewhere far below, a hawk cried and got no echo.

"I can open a door," she said at last. "Small ones. Thresholds. Cracks in the world's attention. I could put you a day ahead of them on the capital road, or a mile behind, whichever serves. I could hide your scent from their ledgers—for a while." She held up one small hand before hope could finish forming in me. "But every door costs. And there's a price I want more than any of that."

"Which is?"

"My name back."

"I don't have it."

"No," she agreed. "You have something better. You have the *shape* of it. Buried deep, wrapped in the hiding-spell Maren wove around you, tucked inside the six empty years you built to be invisible in." She crawled closer on her knees, flowers bowing beneath her, and took my hand, and her grip was cold and small and absolutely solid, and I let her, God help me, I let her. "Here's what nobody told you, Rosalind-who-isn't-Rosalind. Invisibility isn't free either. You've been paying for it your whole life, in installments. Every year a little less of you. You thought you were being careful. You were being *eaten*, slowly, politely, by your own hiding."

"I'm safe," I said, and heard how thin it sounded. "Safe matters. Safe is the whole point."

"Were you safe in Maren's cottage? Were the flowers safe? Was Velthmere safe?" At the word *Velthmere* the whole shrine shuddered, stones humming like struck bells, and I felt the word go through me too, landing somewhere I didn't have, waking something I couldn't name. "Nothing hidden stays safe forever. The gray-coats have a new hunter in this valley, and hunters smell waking things. The moment those flowers bloomed, the clock started. You have two choices, and I won't dress them up."

She raised one finger. "Stay hidden. Run deeper, hide harder, spend yourself thinner, until one day an Inquisitor opens a ledger and finds the last crumb of you and eats it, and that's the end, quiet, unwitnessed, a footnote that erases itself."

A second finger. "Or give me back my name. Wake me properly. Let me teach you what you're carrying and why the empire fears it—and yes, the moment you do, you'll blaze like a signal fire on every ledger in the Ministry. Every power in this land will know your face. You will never be invisible again, not for one day, not for one hour, as long as you live."

The wind resumed, gently, as if embarrassed. The flowers swayed. Somewhere in my chest my heart was going like a smuggler's heels on wet slate.

"And if I say no?" I asked. "If I walk away right now?"

The girl looked at me with my own childhood eyes, and for a moment the vast thing underneath surfaced fully, and I saw—not horror, worse—*weariness*. Two hundred years of weariness, worn like a coat that didn't fit.

"Then I'll wait," she said. "I'm good at waiting. It's nearly all I am." She released my hand and sat back among the flowers, small and patient and starving. "But I'll tell you a secret, since you gave me a please. The hiding-spell Maren cast on you is unraveling. She's the one who tied it, and they've taken her, and knots know their maker. Whether you choose or not, chosen is coming for you. The question isn't whether you end up visible, Rosalind. The question is whether you're visible as *nothing*—a husk, an empty archive they drain and discard—or as *yourself*, with your name in your own mouth and a god at your shoulder who owes you everything."

She smiled then, gap-toothed and terrible and hopeful, and held out her small hand, palm up, the way children ask, the way beggars ask, the way the whole erased country of Velthmere must have asked, once, before the eating began.

"Just say my name," she whispered. "Whatever rises in your throat when you reach for it—that one. It's been waiting in you longer than you've been alive."

And the awful, unbearable truth, the thing I will carry to whatever grave the Ministry leaves me, is that I could feel it. Rising. Sitting on my tongue like a word from a dream, warm, familiar, *mine to give*, and behind it, faint as flowers under frost, a whole country clearing its throat.

I opened my mouth—

—and far down the pass, where the smuggler's road bent toward the valley, a bell rang. Once. Clean and cold and official.

The girl's head snapped toward the sound, and every flower in the ring turned to face it, and her face—my face—went grim in a way I'd never managed to make it look.

"They're early," she said. "Run, or speak. Choose *now*."
