## 5. The Girl with the Ledger

**Sera**

The Ministry trains you to notice everything and care about nothing. Six years of that, and I can tell you the weight of a man's guilt by how he holds his cup, the exact moment a lie is born in someone's mouth—it happens just before the lips part, a little hitch in the breath, like a door deciding whether to open.

What the Ministry does not train you for is the feeling of recognizing a stranger.

The inn was called the Drowned Lamb, which told me either the owner had a sense of humor or the smugglers had burned down two previous signs and this one was meant as a warning. It sat at the crook of the pass where the Thornfell roads went from merely illegal to genuinely fictional—roads that existed on no imperial survey, that appeared only when you'd paid the right person and forgotten to look too closely at the map.

Rosalind Dunmore had come through here. I was sure of it the way I was sure of weather: the innkeeper's eyes had flicked upstairs when I said *traveler, alone, young*, and there was mud on the stair rail with white flecks in it, moon-herb pollen, still fresh enough to smell like cold honey and something older underneath. Something I didn't have a word for yet.

I had a ledger in my coat. Forty-one names, each one a life scheduled for subtraction. The Ministry of Continuity called them *corrections*. We were very good at euphemisms; it was practically our national product.

Entry thirty-seven read:

*R—— D——. Anomalous recollection, class three. Keystone-adjacent. Deliver intact.*

Half a name. Someone above my pay grade had started the file and then—stopped. That never happened. Files didn't stop. Files were rivers; they ran downhill into erasure, always. But this one had been dammed up mid-word, and when I touched the ink of it I felt something I had no protocol for.

I felt *homesick*. In an inn I'd never entered, over a name I'd never spoken.

I climbed the stairs anyway.

* * *

**Rosalind**

Six years of being no one teaches you certain skills. You learn to eat slowly in public places, because people who eat fast are leaving. You learn to sit facing doors. You learn that the most dangerous person in any room is not the loudest but the quietest one who ordered nothing and keeps looking at the stairs.

I was eating slowly, facing the door, when she came up.

Grey coat cut for travel rather than ceremony, but the cut was too good to be anything but Ministry. No visible weapons, which meant at least two hidden ones—I'd learned that trick from Maren, back when Maren's lessons were about herbs and not about burning pages out of my head. She had dark hair going silver early at the temples, and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they landed on.

And she looked at me the way no one had looked at me in six years.

Not through me. Not past me, the way you look at furniture, at weather, at the invisible. She looked *at* me, and her face did something complicated—a flinch folded inside a recognition folded inside a grief she clearly hadn't authorized.

Every instinct I owned stood up and said *run*.

But running confirms things. And besides—and this is the part I'm not proud of—I wanted to know why she looked at me like that. Six years without anyone seeing you does strange things to your hunger. So I stayed seated, moved my hand casually off the knife under my jacket, and smiled the smile I used on customs men: pleasant, forgettable, a door painted on a wall.

She sat down across from me without being invited. Bold. Or desperate. Those often wear the same coat.

"Cards?" she said, and produced a worn deck from her pocket like a duelist producing a glove.

I should mention: I am an excellent liar and a mediocre card player, which is a dangerous combination, because liars overestimate their control of chance.

"Depends," I said. "On what?"

"On whether you're the person I think you might be."

"Who do you think I might be?"

"Someone whose name starts with a letter," she said, "and ends before it should."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, into whatever the mountain keeps beneath its cellars. Nobody knew that name. I barely knew it myself anymore; I'd fed it to the dark piece by piece until only the shape remained, a tooth socket in my own mouth where a word used to be.

"Names cost extra here," I said. "House rule."

"Then we'll play for it." She shuffled. Her hands were steady, scarred across the knuckles in ways that said *training*, not *accident*. "One hand. If you win, I walk out of here and report a dead end. If I win—" she paused, and for a moment the Inquisitor mask slipped and something younger looked out of it, frightened and wanting, "—if I win, you tell me why your face is familiar."

"That's not much of a prize."

"It is to me," she said quietly, and dealt.

* * *

**Sera**

Here is what they don't tell you at the Academy: every Inquisitor carries a hole. The training requires it—you cannot unmake memory if you treasure your own—so the first year, they take something. A summer. A face. They don't tell you what they took. You just live the rest of your life around the absence, the way a tongue returns again and again to a missing tooth.

Mine was childhood-shaped. I knew this because other Inquisitors occasionally mentioned their holes—drunk, once a year, at the winter audit—and theirs had edges. A mother's voice. A brother's funeral. Mine had none. My earliest memory was standing in a corridor at the Academy at age nine, already filed, already numbered, as if I'd been manufactured whole from grey wool and doctrine.

So when I opened the inn room door and saw the person behind the half-name—saw that face, that particular tilt of the jaw, that way of watching a room while pretending not to—the hole in me didn't ache.

It *answered*.

I don't know how else to describe it. Like a bell struck somewhere far away, resonating in a cavity I'd stopped noticing was there. I knew this person. Not from the file. From *before the file*. From wherever I had been before I was Vane.

And the worst part—the genuinely unforgivable part—was that my hand, trained by six years of duty, reached for the restraint cuffs in my left pocket.

And my other hand, which belonged apparently to someone else, some earlier girl, laid the cards on the table instead.

* * *

**Rosalind**

We played a game neither of us named, with rules that shifted like the mountain fog. She cheated twice; I caught her both times and let her keep the second one, because the first was a test and the second was a tell. I cheated once. She caught it and laughed—one short, surprised syllable, quickly strangled, like a sound escaping prison.

"You're terrible at that," she said.

"I'm excellent at lying. Cards are different. Cards want you to be honest about being dishonest."

"What do cards want," she repeated, and studied me over her hand. Up close her eyes were dark brown with a ring of amber near the pupil, and there was a small scar through her left eyebrow that made her look permanently, faintly skeptical of the world. "You talk like someone who learned words secondhand."

"I had a teacher," I said. "She believed vocabulary was survival gear."

"And where is she now?"

Gone. Ransacked cottage, empty bed, a scorch mark on the floor shaped like a page. I kept my face pleasant and my heart hammering against its cage. "Around."

"Mm." She played a card. Won the trick. Didn't gloat, which unsettled me more than gloating would have. Outside, wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere below us the innkeeper dropped something glass and swore with real artistry.

"Your turn to answer something," I said. "Why does my face matter? You came up those stairs knowing. The mud on the railing gave me away—don't look surprised, I saw you check it. So the question isn't whether I'm who you think. The question is why you sat down instead of calling your friends."

Something crossed her face like a cloud crossing a valley. "I don't have friends."

"The grey coats downstairs. Three of them. Boots too clean for drovers."

"They're not mine." She set her cards down. "They're the Ministry's. And in about twenty minutes they're coming up these stairs because I was supposed to signal them by now, and I haven't, and Corporal Ilves counts seconds like a moneylender counts interest."

I was on my feet before I'd decided to be. Knife out, low, against my thigh where Maren taught me—not threatening, just *available*. "Then this was a trap."

"No." She said it fast, and then slower, like she was hearing the truth of it as she spoke. "No, it was supposed to be an arrest. That's what I told myself on the stairs. That's what I've been telling myself since the valley." She looked up at me, and her hands were flat and open on the table, and she looked—gods help me—she looked *terrified*, and not of my knife. "And then I saw you, and there is a face in my head that shouldn't exist, because everything before age nine was taken out of me professionally, and your face is standing in the middle of that nothing like a lit window."

The knife point drifted downward. Not lowered. Drifted.

"That's not possible," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. And yet."

Boots on the stairs. Two pairs, heavy, unhurried—the walk of men who believe the world is already theirs. She heard them half a beat before I did; I'll give her that. She rose smoothly, turned toward the door, and became someone else entirely: shoulders squared, chin level, the grey coat suddenly fitting her like armor fits a knight, which is to say like a sentence fits a prisoner.

"Gentlemen," she said as they filled the doorway.

"Inquisitor Vane." The taller one—Ilves, presumably, with a face like a closed ledger—glanced past her at me with the flat appraisal of a butcher pricing meat. "Signal's late."

"There's a complication." She didn't look at me. Her voice had gone smooth as poured wax. "The target isn't alone. There's a second anomalous signature—stronger. Moving east through the pass tonight, if my reading's right. If we take the small fish now, we spook the big one."

Ilves chewed on that. "General Corvane wants delivery."

"The General wants results. Delivery of one keystone means nothing if the archive it's keyed to slips away." She said *keystone* the way you'd say *bread*—blandly, and my blood went cold, because I didn't know what the word meant yet and I already feared it. "I'll stay on the target. Alone. You take the eastern road."

A long silence. Somewhere below, the innkeeper dropped something else. At this rate the Drowned Lamb would be out of glass by midnight.

"If you're wrong," Ilves said finally, "it's your name in the ledger."

"My name's already in plenty of ledgers," she said lightly. "One more won't ruin the binding."

They went. The boots descended. The front door opened and shut, and the night swallowed three sets of footsteps, and then it was just the two of us and the candle guttering between us like it couldn't decide which side to betray.

I sat back down because my legs suggested it.

"You lied to them," I said. "Flawlessly. For me. Why?"

"I don't know." She sat too, heavily, like a woman setting down a load she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. She turned her cards face up on the table—she'd lost the hand, I realized, she'd lost it deliberately somewhere in the middle—and looked at me across them. "Ask me tomorrow. Maybe I'll know by then. Maybe I'll have remembered something I was never allowed to keep."

Outside, far off down the valley, thunder rolled—except the sky was clear, I could see stars through the shutter gap, and the thunder had a cadence to it almost like speech.

Almost like a name.

Both of us heard it. Both of us pretended, for one more hour, that we hadn't.

* * *

**Sera**

Later—much later, when the candle was a puddle and Rosalind slept in the chair with a knife still in their hand even in dreams, which told me more about their six years than any confession would have—I took out the ledger and read entry thirty-seven again.

*R—— D——.*

And beneath the ink, faint as breath on glass, letters that hadn't been there when I left the capital. Writing itself. Writing in a hand I almost recognized, though I had no memory of ever learning it.

Two letters restored. *Ro*—— D*——*.

The file was remembering. Or something was remembering it *for* me, patient as water working at stone, giving the name back one letter at a time like a ransom paid in installments.

I looked at the sleeping stranger who was somehow also the oldest acquaintance of my soul, and I understood that I had already chosen. Somewhere on the stairs, somewhere in the dealing of the cards, I had walked off the edge of the Ministry's world and was simply falling, elegantly, in grey wool.

The question that kept me awake wasn't whether I'd hang for treason.

It was why, looking at that face, I felt like I was finally coming *home*—to a home I had never once been allowed to remember.
