## 6. Maren's Last Instruction

Maren had always said that if anything happened to her, I should look under the hearthstone. She said it the way she said everything—flatly, over her shoulder, while stirring something that smelled like boiled socks and would cure me of something I didn't yet have.

*"If anything happens to me,"* she'd say, *"look under the hearthstone. Not before. If you look before, you'll find a cache of dried nettle and my emergency brandy, and you'll drink the brandy, and then where will we both be?"*

I was crouched in the ruins of her cottage three days after finding it ransacked, and the hearthstone was already up, and there was no brandy.

There was a box.

I want to say I felt something momentous when I lifted it out—harp music, destiny, the weight of history settling on my shoulders like a well-tailored coat. What I actually felt was cold. My knees had been on that floor long enough to go numb, and the draft coming through the broken window kept finding the back of my neck like it had a grudge. Six years of smuggling had taught me to read a room, though, and this room read wrong. The Inquisitors—or whoever they'd been—had torn the place apart looking for something. Bedsplit gutted, pantry emptied, every jar of herbs smashed and sniffed. But the hearthstone sat undisturbed under a fine crust of ash, because nobody thinks to look at the thing that looks most like itself.

That was Maren all over. Hide the treasure in the obvious place and trust that people were too clever to be simple.

The box was cedar, small enough to hold bread for two, bound in iron that had gone black with age. Inside: a bundle of letters tied with green thread, a key made of antler, and a single pressed flower I recognized from the pass above Thornfell—a moon-petal, white as bone, blooming out of season in a book that had to be forty years old.

Which meant Maren had known about the flowers. Had known, probably, about everything I was only just beginning to suspect, back when she was teaching me to grind feverfew and lie to tax collectors. Back before she tried to burn a page out of my head.

I sat down on the wrecked bed—because if your whole life is about to rearrange itself, you might as well be comfortable—and untied the thread.

* * *

The first letter was dated the year I was born.

*Rosalind—if you are reading this, then I am dead or taken, and either way you have finally learned to follow instructions, which took long enough. I am going to tell you things I swore an oath never to tell you, and I want you to understand that I am not sorry. I am many things tonight, writing this by a candle I should save, but I am not sorry.*

You have to understand what those words did to me. Maren apologizing. Maren admitting fault. In six years of apprenticeship I heard her concede exactly once—when she salted a stew twice and even then she blamed the salt.

*I found you in the spring of the year the empire finished its roads through Thornfell. You were not abandoned. That is the first thing, and it is the hardest, so take it now while your hands are steady. Someone left you at my door on purpose, wrapped in wool that had no business being in this country, with a sigil stitched into the hem that I spent four months and two bribed priests learning to read.*

*It is the keystone mark. And Rosalind, if you are old enough to read this, then you are old enough to know what that means, which means I have failed to keep it from you, which means it doesn't matter anymore.*

Keystone.

I knew the word. Masons used it—the stone at the top of an arch that holds all the others up, that the whole structure leans its weight against. Pull the keystone and the arch comes down.

I did not like the direction my thoughts were walking, so I kept reading.

*Here is what the Ministry of Continuity will tell you, if it ever tells you anything: Velthmere fell. Conquered. Annexed. A backward mountain nation absorbed into the empire's bright future, its gods retired, its language folded away like winter clothes. That is the story in every book, and it is a lie so complete that the people who lived through it cannot remember it is a lie.*

*The empire does not conquer, child. It eats. It has eaten eleven countries that I can name, and Velthmere was the twelfth, and it was the hardest, because our gods were small and stubborn and hid well. A god of thresholds. A god of hearths. A god of rain that knew every farmer's name. You could not burn them out, so the empire did something worse. It unmade the memory of them, country-wide, god by god, until no one remembered there had ever been anything to forget.*

*But memory, properly made, cannot be destroyed. Only moved.*

I stopped there. Read the sentence again. *Only moved.*

Outside, wind came down off the pass and rattled the shutters, and somewhere below in the valley a dog started barking at nothing, which is what dogs do when the world has seams in it. I pulled my cloak tighter and kept going, because there is a kind of fear that feels exactly like momentum, and I have learned not to trust my legs when that feeling starts.

*A keystone is a person who carries what cannot otherwise survive. A vessel, loaded ritually, with the last intact memory of a nation—not fragments, not stories, but the whole of it, dense as a seed. There were twelve children chosen in the final days of Velthmere. Eleven were caught. You are the twelfth.*

*You were six months old. Your parents carried you through the passes with the empire's eaters behind them, and at my door your mother made a choice I hope to every waking god I never have to make: she gave you up so that you could stay hidden. Because a keystone who doesn't know what they carry is safe. Invisible. The Ministry's instruments slide right past you, because there is nothing anomalous to find—you are just an orphan with a hedge-witch for a guardian and a talent for lying.*

*Every year of your life, I have watched you grow around that buried country like a tree grows around a fence post. And every year I have hated myself a little more for what we did to you, and thanked your mother a little more for doing it.*

*Six years ago, you began to dream in a language you were never taught. I know because I heard you talking in your sleep. That is the seal thinning. That is Velthmere pressing at the inside of your skull like a seed pressing at its shell. I tried to cut the page of it out of you—I know you remember that night, whatever else you've lost—and I failed, and thank the gods I failed, because what I would have taken out was more than dreams. It was everything. You wouldn't have been hurt, Rosalind. You would have been *less.* A person-shaped hole where an archive used to be.*

So there it was. The night I ran, laid flat in ink.

I had spent six years telling myself the story of that night: Maren went mad, or Maren was always crueler than she let on, or I did something unforgivable and she snapped. I built my whole self on the foundation of having escaped something. Runaway apprentice. Self-made. No one's archive, no one's daughter, no one's keystone.

And the truth was she'd been trying to protect me, badly, the way she protected everything—with fire and bad timing—and I had run straight out of the one safe place in the world because I mistook surgery for butchery.

I laughed. It came out wrong, more like the dog barking downstairs, and I put my hand over my mouth and laughed again anyway, because the alternative was the other thing, and I wasn't ready for the other thing.

* * *

The second letter was shorter. Newer. The ink hadn't faded; some of it still smudged when I tilted the page toward the window-light. She'd written it recently. Maybe weeks ago. Maybe days before whoever tore apart her cottage came for her.

*They've noticed. I don't know how—the flowers, I think. The shrine on the high pass bloomed, and things that bloom out of season are prayers wearing coats, and the Ministry reads prayers the way bailiffs read ledgers. An Inquisitor has been assigned to this valley. One Inquisitor, which means they think it's small, which means we have time, but not much.*

*Rosalind. Listen to me. The seal on your memory isn't failing by accident. The gods are waking, and when the old gods stir, everything they touched stirs with them, and they touched you more than anyone alive. You are going to start remembering whether you choose to or not. So choose to. Do it on your own terms, somewhere they can't reach you, because the difference between remembering freely and being remembered *at* is the difference between opening a door and having it kicked down.*

*Go to Lowmark. Find a printer named Odile Vasseur—her shop is behind the chandlery, ask for maps. Her father drew charts of a country that officially never existed, and he hid them inside maps of places that did. She'll know what the sigil means. She'll know where the Garden goes when it moves.*

Yes, I thought. Of course. *When* it moves. Naturally. Why would the last door out of a dead country hold still like an honest door?

*And Rosalind—one more thing, and then I'll let you be angry with me, which you have every right to be.*

*Your name is real. Whatever else is buried in you, whatever the ritual packed you with—that part is yours. Rosalind Dunmore. I named you myself, from nothing, out of thin air, the way mothers do, and no empire can eat a name given freely. When you find out who you were, don't let it swallow who you are.*

I sat with that for a long time.

Then I read the last line, which was just: *Under the hearthstone. Not before. —M*

She'd written the joke into the actual letter. Into her last testament. I pressed the page flat against my knee and discovered that my eyes had gotten wet without consulting me, which was inconvenient, because the paper was old and I needed it.

* * *

I burned nothing and took everything. The letters went inside my shirt, next to my skin, where I keep the things I can't afford to lose: needle, flint, the antler key. The pressed moon-petal I held a moment longer than necessary. Forty years old and still faintly fragrant, still white, still impossibly itself. Proof that things could last. Proof that Maren had been waiting half her life for a spring that finally came, and wasn't here to see it.

Before I left, I stood in the middle of her ruined kitchen and did something I hadn't done since I was a child hiding from a thunderstorm. I prayed. Not to anything in particular—there wasn't anything in particular left to pray to, that was rather the empire's whole project—but to the shape of the absence. To the hearth-god whose name nobody remembered, if such a god existed anywhere outside my own skull. To the threshold I was about to cross.

*If you're in there,* I thought, at the inside of my head, at the seed of a dead country curled up behind my eyes, *I'm sorry I didn't know you were there. I'm sorry I complained about you. The headaches, the dreams, the night terrors—all of it, I'm sorry. You were carrying a whole nation and I was mad about bad weather.*

And something answered.

Not words. Not yet. Just a warmth behind my sternum, brief as a struck match, and a feeling I can only describe as *orientation*—the sensation of a compass needle swinging, of a map redrawing itself, of something vast turning over in its sleep and becoming briefly aware of the flea sleeping on its ear.

It knew I knew. That was the message. The seal wasn't thinning anymore. It was open on one side, and everything on the other side was leaning its weight against it, patient as masonry, waiting for me to decide whether I was going to walk through or be pushed.

"Well," I said aloud, to the empty cottage, to the hearth with its lifted stone, to the ghost of a woman who had hidden an entire country inside an orphan and then had the nerve to apologize for it. "Lowmark, then."

Three days' walk south, if the smuggler roads were kind. They're never kind. But they're mine, every switchback and false trail, and for the first time in six years I wasn't running along them.

I was walking toward something. Which, as any experienced runner will tell you, is far more dangerous.

I stepped over the threshold of Maren's cottage, and beneath my feet—so faint I'd have missed it if I hadn't just been told what to listen for—something small and hungry stirred, like a god of doorways clearing its throat.
