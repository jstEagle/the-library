## 7. The Road That Isn't There

The Ministry of Continuity teaches its Inquisitors that hesitation is a wound. Left untended, it festers. I had been carrying mine for six days now—since the inn at Thornfell's foot, since a card game and a face I had no right to recognize—and the infection had spread to places I preferred not to examine.

So when the courier pigeon arrived at the waystation above Hollowmere, I read the orders twice, burned them in the stove, and told myself the fever had simply broken.

*Subject R—— D—— to be delivered intact to the capital archive by month's end. Delivery of subject supersedes all prior instruction. Failure will be understood as treason.*

Understood. Yes. Corvane had a gift for clarity. He wrote orders the way surgeons cut: no wasted motion, nothing left ambiguous enough to hide behind. I stood there watching my career curl into ash in the grate and felt—this is the part they don't put in the training manuals—relief.

Six years I'd served the Ministry. Six years of ledgers and interviews and villages where people wept without knowing why, and I had told myself it was necessary arithmetic. Some memories are dangerous. Some histories have to be edited for the health of the whole. I'd believed it the way you believe the floor will hold your weight: without thinking, because thinking about it is how you fall through.

Then a smuggler with careful hands and a laugh like something breaking quietly had dealt me a hand of cards and lost on purpose so I wouldn't leave, and the floor had opened.

I took out the second document—the one I wasn't supposed to have, the contingency copy sewn into my coat lining—and fed it to the stove too. Two sets of orders. One fire. The arithmetic was getting simpler all the time.

"Rough day?"

The waystation keeper, an old woman with a face like a dried apple, watched me from the doorway. She'd seen couriers come and go for forty years. She knew what burning documents meant.

"It's going to get rougher," I said, and paid her triple for the mule.

* * *

There are roads, and then there are roads. The King's Highway runs through Thornfell on the map the Ministry issues—wide, surveyed, honest. But smugglers don't travel on maps the Ministry issues, because maps issued by empires are confessions of what the empire watches. What the empire watches, the smugglers avoid. Which means the real roads of Thornfell appear nowhere official at all.

Maren's notes—I'd copied them from Rosalind's pack while they slept, a theft I still hadn't confessed to—mentioned a pass called the Shepherd's Tooth. It exists on perhaps four documents in the world, drawn by hand, passed palm to palm among people who move things the Ministry prefers stayed still. The pass goes over the shoulder of the mountain instead of around it. It takes three days off the journey to the free towns. It also cannot be followed by anyone who hasn't been shown the way.

Which was why, when I crested the first ridge with Rosalind's borrowed mule and looked back down the valley, I saw what I saw.

Lanterns. A dozen, maybe more, moving up the King's Highway in formation—not the loose stagger of travelers but the disciplined spacing of Ministry field teams. And at the head of them, riding a gray horse that moved like it disapproved of everything, a figure whose silhouette I would have known anywhere, the way you know the shape of a knife held behind someone's back.

Corvane hadn't sent orders hoping I'd obey. He'd sent orders knowing I wouldn't. The delivery instruction had been a test with only one wrong answer, and I had just given it, in writing, in ash.

"He knows," I said to the mule. The mule, being a mule, declined to comment on the strategic situation.

I turned uphill and did not look back again, because looking back is how you learn whether you're being followed, and I already knew the answer. The only question left was the one I'd been avoiding for six days, and it caught up with me somewhere around the second switchback, breathless and insistent:

Why? Why burn the orders, Sera? You could have delivered them. You could have handed Rosalind Dunmore to the archive team at the capital gates, collected whatever absolution Corvane dangles over the ambitious, and gone back to being a person who sleeps well because she has arranged not to remember what she does.

I had an answer. I'd had it since the inn, actually. I just hadn't said it aloud yet, even inside my own skull, because some things become true the moment you name them, and I wasn't ready for this one to be true.

The mule didn't need me to say it. The mule just needed the path, and the path—the Shepherd's Tooth—was doing its best to kill us both.

* * *

The smuggler maps describe the Tooth as "follow the water until the water lies." I'd assumed this was smuggler poetry. It is smuggler poetry, but it is also literal. The stream that runs up the pass—for two hours it genuinely runs uphill, a trick of the terrain that makes your body insist you're descending while your boots tell you otherwise—forks at a deadfall, and the fork that looks real ends at a cliff face. The fork that looks like nothing, a seep barely dampening the stone, drops into a cleft you can walk through sideways.

I found the cleft at dusk, wedged the mule through with a great deal of profanity, and came out the far side into a hanging valley that no surveyor had ever sullied with ink.

And that was where I stopped feeling clever.

Because the hanging valley was full of flowers.

Moon-herb, silver-pale, should have been months from blooming—it was the crop Rosalind had been smuggling when I first tracked them, out-of-season even then. But here it covered the valley floor in drifts, waist-high, glowing faintly the way moon-herb does when it's happy, which is never, because moon-herb is a weed with delusions and is never happy anywhere. Except here. Here it was rioting. And beneath it, half-buried under centuries of moss, I could see the corner of something carved—a lintel, maybe, or the top of an archway—stone worked in a style the Ministry had taught me to classify, automatically, as *pre-Imperial: do not engage.*

I stood in an illegal valley full of illegally blooming flowers beside an unclassified ruin, and I thought: *This is what we erase.* Not abstractions. This. Valleys that glow. Roads that lie. A country that had gardens once.

"Sera."

I had my knife out before the sound finished registering—which was stupid, because I knew the voice, had spent six days learning its every inflection—but stupidity seemed to be the theme of the week. Rosalind stepped out of the moon-herb drifts ten feet away, hood back, hands visible, wearing the expression of someone who has been following me for an hour and is trying to decide whether to be flattered or furious.

"You copied my notes," they said.

"I—" There was no version of denial that survived those eyes. "Yes."

"You stole pages out of my pack while I slept, copied Maren's handwriting, and then burned the originals?" They held up a charred scrap between two fingers. "I found this in your stove ash at the waystation. I've been tracking your stove ash across half a mountain, Sera Vane, and I want you to know that I have *opinions* about it."

"I can explain."

"Please. I'm exhausted. I'd love to hear how an Inquisitor of the Ministry of Continuity comes to be fleeing her own colleagues through a pass that officially doesn't exist, in possession of my dead mentor's stolen letters." They weren't shouting. Somehow that was worse. Their voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice of someone who learned early that loudness gets you found. "Start with why I shouldn't leave you here."

"The Ministry is coming," I said. "Corvane himself. A dozen field operatives on the King's Highway, and he'll have the other passes sealed within a day. He anticipated the burn—he *wanted* me to run, because I know these mountains better than his teams do, and a traitor leads you straight to what she's protecting." I swallowed. "He wanted me to lead him to you. I'm doing exactly what he planned. I just picked a road he can't follow."

Rosalind was silent for a long moment. The moon-herb glowed between us like a bad conscience.

"You're telling me," they said slowly, "that your grand act of treason is playing directly into the enemy's hands."

"When you say it like that—"

"Because that's what it sounds like. I want to be clear. I've been running from your Ministry for six years, and the first time one of you defects, the defection is *part of their plan.*"

"Yes," I said. "That's accurate."

"And you're telling me this instead of lying to me. Why?"

Here it was, then. The question underneath the question, the wound under the wound. I could give the tactical answer: *because you'd catch me lying, you're better at this than I am.* I could give the self-flagellating answer: *because you deserve honesty after what I've done.* Both were true. Neither was the truth.

"Because I burned the orders before I let myself decide anything else," I said. "Because whatever Corvane planned, the choice was still mine, and I made it, and I'd make it again. Because—" and here the thing I hadn't named rose up my throat like floodwater, and I named it, or most of it, "—because there's a page in my ledger with your name half-eaten on it, and I've carried that page for three years, and I never asked myself why I kept looking at it. Until you dealt me cards in a smuggler's inn and I recognized your face from somewhere I have no memory of. Someone took something out of my head, Rosalind. Whatever it was, I think it had you in it. And I am done letting the Ministry decide what I'm allowed to remember and who I'm allowed to be."

The valley held its breath. Somewhere upslope, water ran the wrong direction.

"That's the most dangerous thing you could have said," Rosalind said finally. "Do you understand that? If you're telling the truth—if there's something missing in you that connects to me—then you're not just a traitor anymore. You're anomalous. They don't arrest anomalies, Sera. They *eat* them." They stepped closer, and up close their exhaustion was visible, the hollows under their eyes, the six years of it. "You could still go back. Ride down, tell Corvane you got lost, hand me over, and sleep soundly forever. He might even give you whatever he promised."

"He offered me my childhood once," I said. "In escrow. A memory at a time, for good service." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I used to think about what I'd buy back first. Now I think: what if what he'd sell me isn't mine at all? What if it's edited? What if my missing years aren't missing—they're *filed*, and the filing says whatever serves the Ministry?"

"So you'd rather not know."

"I'd rather choose what I know." I met their eyes. "Starting with choosing you. Whatever that means. Whatever it costs. I'm done pretending it's tactics."

Rosalind looked at me for a long time. Behind them, the buried archway caught the moonlight, and for half a second I could have sworn the carvings on it were deeper than they'd been a moment ago—fresher, as if something underneath the moss had shifted in its sleep.

"Fine," Rosalind said at last. "You walk with me. But we take the long way to the free towns, the way that isn't on any map, including Maren's. And Sera?" They turned back at the edge of the flower-drift, and the moon-herb light made their face strange and young and old all at once. "When we stop running—when we finally stand still long enough to find out what's missing from both of us—you don't get to flinch. Whatever we find. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, and discovered I meant it, and discovered further that meaning it terrified me more than Corvane's entire column of lanterns winding up the dark highway below.

We walked north through the glowing valley, side by side, two liars with nothing left to lie about except the one thing that mattered. Behind us, far below, I heard—or imagined I heard—the gray horse's rider pause at the mouth of a stream that ran uphill, and consider, and smile.

Corvane always smiled when the game got interesting. That was the worst thing about him.

Ahead of us, at the valley's far end, where no path should have been, a path was.
