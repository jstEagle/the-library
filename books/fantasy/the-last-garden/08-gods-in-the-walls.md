## 8. Gods in the Walls

The reservoir was called Lake Merrow on every imperial map, which was the empire's way of apologizing for drowning a valley without naming it twice.

I'd known that since my second year on the smuggler's roads, when old Hesk told me over a cup of something that tasted like pine tar and regret: *Don't drink deep near Merrow, girl. That water's got a floor, and the floor's got streets.* I'd laughed then. Six years of being no one teaches you to laugh at most things, because the alternative is asking questions you can't afford answered.

Now I was wading into it up to my thighs with Maren's last note folded against my heart and three Inquisitor lanterns flickering somewhere on the ridge above me, so I suppose the laughing years were over.

*Follow the drowned road down,* Maren had written, in her crabbed hand that always looked like it resented being legible. *When the water forgets to be cold, you'll know you've crossed into what they couldn't eat. Knock politely.*

That last instruction was pure Maren. The woman once made me apologize to a jar of pickled newts for opening it out of turn.

The water did forget to be cold. It happened gradually, the way forgetting usually does—one step the chill bit through my boots, the next it was merely cool, and by the third step it was nothing at all, like walking through evening air that happened to be wet. Around me the lake went dark as poured ink, but not empty dark. There were shapes below me. Straight lines. Right angles.

Streets.

I kept walking because stopping felt worse than going, and because the lanterns on the ridge had started moving in a pattern I recognized from six years of running: the sweep-and-close, the way Inquisitors worked a valley like hands working dough. They'd find the shrine where I'd prayed—where something had *answered*—and they'd follow the trail of wrongness I'd left across half of Thornfell like spilled honey follows a bear.

So. Down.

The water rose past my waist, my chest, and here's the thing about drowning in a lake that's forgotten to be cold: your body objects anyway. My lungs objected loudly. I took one last breath and put my head under, expecting blackness and terror.

Instead there was light. Faint, greenish, sourceless—the light of a place remembering sunlight rather than having any. A cobbled street stretched below me, sloping gently down between walls with doorways in them, doors still hanging on their hinges after two hundred years underwater, and every single door stood open.

I walked in. Air met me on the other side of some boundary I didn't see, dry air, smelling of stone dust and, absurdly, of baking bread. My clothes dried in maybe ten steps. Behind me the reservoir hung like a wall of dark glass, fish drifting through it, unbothered.

"Well," I said, to no one. "Knocked politely."

The street ahead ran on farther than any street should. Doors lined both sides, all of them open, all of them showing rooms that were just—rooms. Kitchens with tables set for meals two centuries cold. A nursery with a wooden horse mid-gallop on its wheels. A workshop full of half-carved chairs, waiting for hands that had been somewhere else for a long time.

I knew this place. That was the horrible part. Not from memory—I didn't have memories of Velthmere, whatever Maren's notes claimed—but from the *shape* of knowing. Like hearing a song through a wall and finding your foot tapping before your brain catches up.

"You're late."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere and, when I turned, from directly behind my left knee, which is where it was standing.

It was small. That was my first coherent thought: the god of this place was *small*, hardly taller than the wooden horse, thin as a candle stub burned down to nothing. Its shape wouldn't hold still. One moment it wore my face—not my face now, but younger, rounder, the face from before—and the next it wore Maren's scowl, then the innkeeper's wife from Colder's Ford, then Sera Vane's sharp gray eyes blinking out of a body made of doorway-shadow, then back to child-me again, cycling through borrowed features like a beggar cycling through coats.

"Late?" I managed. "For what?"

"For everything." It sat down in the middle of the street, cross-legged, in the posture I used to sit in when Maren lectured me about herb-drying, wearing Maren's impatient expression while doing it. "Two hundred years I've held the doors. Do you know how long two hundred years is when you're made of thresholds? Every door in a nation, and every nation has so many doors, and each one is a little mouth that needs feeding with someone coming home. And nobody came home. Nobody came home for two hundred years." It looked up at me with my own childhood eyes. "You came home. Eventually. Late."

"I'm not—" I stopped, because arguing with a starving god about whether I was from here seemed like a fight I'd already lost. "What are you?"

"The god of thresholds. Of limens. Of *between*." It said the words carefully, like coins counted out one at a time. "Doorways and crossings and the moment on a bridge when you're neither on one bank nor the other. I was the smallest god of Velthmere. That's why I'm the one left. You can't eat what lives in the gaps. The Ministry's teeth are very fine, but gaps slip through them." A pause, and the face cycling stopped on Maren's, holding it, which felt either like courtesy or cruelty. "Mostly."

"Maren sent me."

"Yes. Maren who was born Aldith Vael of the Weavers' Quarter, who carried a page of you out of the burning archive in her stomach, who spent sixty years teaching you to be small enough to hide." The god said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "She was good at hiding things. She hid herself so well she forgot her own name for eleven years. I watched her do it. I could have reminded her. Thresholds don't reach; we only open."

My throat hurt. "Where is she now?"

"Gone where the taken go. The Inquisitors came for her in autumn. She left you the note instead of leaving herself a way out." The god shrugged with my shoulders, my exact shrug, and watching myself do it from outside was like seeing my reflection disagree with me. "But that's grief, and grief keeps. Urgency doesn't. Sit down, R—— D——."

"Don't call me that."

"It isn't your name yet. But it will be, if you live long enough to claim it." It patted the cobblestones beside it. "Sit. Listen. Then run, if you still want to, though I think you won't."

I sat. The stones were warm. Underwater streets shouldn't be warm, and I filed that away under *things about tonight I'll scream about later.*

"The Last Garden," the god said. "Maren's notes told you it's real?"

"They told me it might be. A story she half-believed."

"It's real. I held its gate myself, once, before the fall. It's not a place, exactly. It's the country's memory of itself, given roots. Every garden remembers being Eden; this one never forgot anything else." The god's face settled, finally, on a version of me aged maybe thirty—a face I'd never worn, might never wear. "And it has a key."

"No," I said, because I could feel where this was going the way you feel rain coming.

"A keystone," it continued mercilessly. "A living archive. When the empire came—not conquered, *ate*, you understand, they ate Velthmere god by god and name by name and year by year until even our ghosts forgot us—the last priests did one thing with the time their deaths bought them. They loaded a child. Poured the whole intact memory of the nation into one small skull, sealed it, hid it, and scattered. The empire hunted that child for a generation and found nothing, because the child's guardians understood hiding better than the empire understood hunting."

"And then?"

"And then time passed. Guardians died or forgot. The keystone grew up believing itself an orphan apprentice named Rosalind Dunmore, and the memory slept in her like a seed in frozen ground." The god leaned close. Its breath smelled of rain on hot stone. "Six years ago you ran from Maren. You thought it was fear. Partly it was. Mostly, the thing inside you felt the Ministry's net tightening around Thornfell and moved you itself, the way a plant moves toward light. You have been running along the seams of this country your whole life, R—— D——. You never chose a direction in your life. Your cargo did."

"That's—" My voice cracked and I let it. "That's not a life. That's a delivery route."

"Now you understand why Maren taught you to laugh at things."

I laughed. It came out wrong, more like the sound a door makes when it sticks.

* * *

We sat on the warm drowned street for a while, the small starving god and I, while above us fish drifted through two hundred years of quiet. Somewhere in that silence I noticed I was crying, and did it quietly, the way you do when you learned young that tears are evidence.

"Why show me this?" I finally asked. "Why not let me keep running? I was safe. Being no one works. Six years, it worked."

"Because it stopped working the night you prayed at the shrine." The god's borrowed face was gentle, which was almost worse than mockery. "You gave the mountains back a word, and the whole country heard it. Every sleeping thing under every hill heard *its own name spoken aloud for the first time in two centuries*. Do you know what happens to seeds when the ground warms? They don't ask permission. They germinate."

"So the gods are waking."

"Waking. Starving. Answering prayers badly, because prayer is a language we're relearning through a mouthful of dust." It gestured at itself—at the flicker, the thinness, the way its edges kept trying to become doorways and failing. "This is what two hundred years of being uneaten-but-unfed looks like. And here is the urgency I promised you." It took my hand. Its grip felt like a latch clicking shut. "Corvane knows what you are."

"Inquisitor-General Corvane." The name came out flat. Everyone on the smuggler's roads said it flatly, the way you say *landslide* or *winter*.

"He has spent thirty years assembling the Ministry's great project—not erasing anymore, they finished erasing, there's nothing left to erase. Now he wants the opposite. He wants to *eat whole*. Consume the keystone memory entire, in one swallowing, with himself as the vessel. Not to destroy it—to *own* it. To be the last man who remembers Velthmere, the sole heir of a murdered nation, the treasury of everything, forever." The god smiled with my mouth, and it was not a kind sight. "And here is the part you must carry out of this valley at a dead run: a keystone eaten whole doesn't preserve the memory. It *ends* it. Memory needs many minds to live in, the way fire needs many logs. Gathered into one, it burns down to ash and one very fat, very hollow man. If Corvane eats you, the gods don't sleep again. The gods die. All of us. Even the ones in the gaps."

The warmth went out of the street right then—or I imagined it did—and the open doors lining the drowned road suddenly looked less like hospitality and more like mouths.

"How does he know?" I whispered. "Six years, the god says I've been invisible—"

"Because you prayed." The god released my hand. "Because you gave the mountains a word. Because last month wildflowers bloomed out of season over a dead shrine, and blooming is a signature, and Corvane has spent thirty years learning to read signatures. He doesn't know *where* you are. He knows *what* you are, and he knows you've started waking things, and he has perhaps—being generous—two weeks before your trail becomes a bonfire visible from the capital."

"Two weeks."

"Less, if you keep being compassionate in public. The woman at the shrine whose daughter's name you gave back—that ripple reached three valleys. Every name you restore is a bell. Every memory you reclaim is a signal fire. You cannot be gentle and hidden anymore, keystone. Those days died with the old world, and the old world is what you're carrying." The god stood, and standing, it seemed briefly taller—all the doorways in the street straightened with it, hinges sighing. "Which brings me to the choice Maren never got to explain to you, because Inquisitors interrupted her explanation with iron."

"There's a choice? I was promised a delivery route with no exits."

"There's always an exit. I'm the god of them." It began walking down the street, and the lamps that had never existed lit themselves ahead of it, grudgingly, like tired servants. "The Garden opens for the keystone alone. You walk in carrying the complete memory, and you are changed irreversibly—what enters is not what comes out, and I won't lie to you about the odds of anything coming out at all. Or—" it glanced back, and for one moment its face was no one's, just a smooth blank like a locked door, honest in its emptiness, "—you refuse. Bury yourself deeper. Find some hole where even Corvane's hounds lose the scent, spend fifty years being no one, and die quietly with a nation asleep in your skull. The country stays dead. The gods starve out. But you get a life. Small, hidden, yours."

The word *yours* landed like a knife between ribs.

"That's not fair," I said.

"Nothing about you has been fair since the day two priests decided their daughter was furniture for a resurrection." The god said it plainly, without softening, and I loved it a little for that. "Fairness is for countries that exist. You have to choose between a self and a people, and everyone who arranged your life already chose *for* you, badly, and you're the only one left with standing to choose differently."

I looked down the drowned street, at the open doors, at the table set for a meal no one ever came home to. Two hundred years of held breath. And I thought about the mourners I hadn't met yet, and the villages that sang songs with holes in them, and Sera Vane's ledger with my name half-eaten in it—*R—— D——*—and how even the empire couldn't finish the job, because something in the ink itself had refused.

"What do I have to do?" I asked.

The god grinned, and for the first time the grin was entirely its own—wide, cracked, luminous, a threshold flung open onto morning.

"Walk," it said. "West, past the Glass Lake, into the pass that exists only on smuggler maps. There's an inn at the far end called the Halfway House, and in its cellar is a mapmaker's daughter who owes Maren a debt. She'll draw you the road that moves." It pressed something into my palm—small, cold, a key, iron, older than the empire. "And take this. Every threshold in this drowned valley answers to it, and you'll be needing doors that open fast, keystone. The hounds are already on the ridge, and one of them used to love you."

My hand closed around the key. Above us, through two hundred feet of dark water, I saw the lantern-sweep of Inquisitors crossing the shore.

"Used to love me?"

"Oh yes." The god was already fading, folding itself back into the gaps between the doors, becoming the space where a wall ends and a doorway begins. "Ask her yourself. She's camped on the west road. She burned her orders an hour ago, and she's been sitting by that fire ever since, trying to decide whether to run to you or away from you, and I'm the god of thresholds, not hearts, so I couldn't tell you which way she'll choose."

"But you could guess."

"I could." The last of it was nearly gone, just a voice and a shimmer shaped like an open door. "Guessing is free. Go, R—— D——. The Garden is real. Corvane is coming. And somewhere under that ridge, a woman who was sent to erase you is discovering that she can't."

The street went dark. The water-wall waited behind me, patient, full of fish.

I put the key around my neck, took a breath of bread-smelling air, and stepped back into the cold—because the cold, at least, had never lied to me about what it was.

Behind me, faintly, from every open door in the drowned city at once, something that might have been two hundred years of held breath finally let itself out.

It sounded almost like a cheer.

Almost like a warning.

I swam.
