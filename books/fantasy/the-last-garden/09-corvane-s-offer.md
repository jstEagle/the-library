## 9. Corvane's Offer

They took me at the ford, which was humiliating on several levels.

First, because I had chosen the ford specifically because it wasn't on any Ministry map—I'd learned its location from a smuggler named Doss in exchange for two silver pieces and a compliment about his dog—and yet there they were, six of them in grey oilcloth, standing in the shallows like herons who had decided to get into politics. Second, because I didn't even put up a fight worth the name. My hand went to my knife and then stopped, and I'd like to tell you that was strategy, but it wasn't. It was the sigil on the lead rider's glove. The open eye, lidless.

You don't draw steel on the Inquisitor-General's personal seal. You just don't. It's like spitting into a cathedral. The architecture makes you feel small before anything even happens.

"Vane," said Corvane, and his voice was exactly as I remembered it from the briefing halls: dry, patient, the voice of a man reading aloud from a ledger in which you are a line item. "You're a long way from your circuit."

"I'm pursuing an anomaly," I said.

"Mm." He looked past me, up the valley, at nothing. "The anomaly has a face now, doesn't she. Or a not-face. Tell me, Sera—may I call you Sera? He never waited for permission; he simply deployed the name like a man laying a card down and watching to see if you flinched—" When did you last sleep under a roof?"

I did the arithmetic honestly, because lying to Corvane was a sport for people with better nerves than mine. "Nine days."

"And when did you last report?"

"Fifteen."

"Fifteen days," he repeated, gently, as if I were a child reciting scripture slightly wrong. "Fifteen days of silence from one of my best. Do you know what most people would conclude?"

"That I've gone rogue."

"No," he said. "That you've gone *human*. Which is worse, but more fixable."

* * *

They didn't bind my hands. That was the thing I kept turning over later, the detail that snagged like a splinter: no ropes, no hood, no manacles. They rode me up out of the valley to a hunting lodge I'd never known existed despite eleven years in the Ministry—a low stone house tucked under a shoulder of Thornfell, smoke rising straight from its chimney in the windless cold—and sat me at a table laid with actual food, actual wine, a fire actually lit, and left me alone with it for the better part of an hour.

It was a message. Everything Corvane did was a message. The message was: *we could have taken you violently, and chose not to, and notice how much that unsettles you more.*

It unsettled me considerably. I ate anyway. I want to be honest about that. Eleven years in the Ministry teaches you that fear and appetite are neighbors who share a wall, and that refusing good stew out of pride is a luxury for people whose names aren't currently written in anyone's ledger.

When he finally came in, Corvane moved like a man entering a room he owned down to the stones. He was smaller than legend suggested—the stories made him seven feet tall with eyes like banked coals, and the reality was a neat, greying man with a clerk's hands—but the stories had gotten the voice right. He poured two cups of wine without asking and sat opposite me, and for a long moment he simply looked at me with an expression I recognized, horribly, from funerals. Pity, professionally applied.

"Do you know why you became an Inquisitor?" he asked.

"Aptitude scores. Recommendation. A regrettable shortage of other prospects."

"You were empty," he said. "Do you understand what I mean? Most recruits arrive carrying their childhoods like luggage—they drag it through every training exercise, every discipline, every unmaking. It leaks. It sentimentalizes. But you—" he tapped the table once, softly, "—you came to us hollow, and hollow things hold their shape. I have always valued that in you."

"Thank you," I said, because eleven years of conditioning will make you thank a man for insulting you correctly.

"It occurs to me," Corvane went on, turning his cup slowly, "that I have never asked whether you wanted to be filled."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. I kept my face where it was, which is to say nowhere.

"There is a girl," he said. "Rosalind Dunmore. Though of course the file says R—— D——, half-eaten, a curiosity we've carried for years. She is a keystone, Sera. A vessel loaded with the last intact memory of Velthmere. Every prayer she answers, every memory she reclaims, every wildflower that blooms out of season along those ridgelines—that is a dead nation knocking at the door of the living one. And if she reaches the Garden, everything the Empire spent two hundred years digesting comes back up. Borders. Gods. Wars we ended by forgetting why we fought them." He smiled thinly. "Chaos. Beautiful, honest chaos, but chaos all the same."

"And you want me to deliver her."

"I want you to do what you were always going to do," he said, "before you fell in love with her."

I want it noted, by whatever record survives this, that I did not flinch. What I did was reach for the wine, which is the same gesture wearing a different coat.

"You've been misinformed," I said.

"Sera." He set his cup down and leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "I have read your reports since you were nineteen years old. I have watched you interview weeping villagers and felt nothing and written flawless summaries. And then, three weeks ago, your field notes changed. They got *worse*—looser, more lyrical, full of details about mountain light and smuggler's banter that serve no operational purpose. Do you know what that is? That is a woman describing the world again. Hollow things don't describe the light."

* * *

Here is what I had never told anyone, including myself, including especially myself: I had no childhood.

Not a tragic one. Not a hidden one. None. My earliest memory was a white room and a woman's voice telling me my name, aged perhaps ten or eleven, and everything before that was smooth dark glass. The Ministry called recruits like me *clean vessels*, and I had always assumed the term meant what it said—that I'd been orphaned young, that grief or fever had taken the early years. It was only much later, holding Rosalind's half-erased file in my hands and seeing the shape of the redaction, that I understood clean vessels weren't found.

They were made. And someone, somewhere, held the contents of my first decade in escrow.

Corvane opened a case he had brought in with him. Not a weapon case. A reader's case—black lacquer, brass fittings, the little cradle that held a memory-slate the way a reliquary holds a saint's fingerbone.

"I could offer you absolution," he said. "A clean slate, reinstatement, your pick of postings. But you'd see through that, so instead I'll offer you something honest." He set the slate in the cradle. "This is fragment 4471-C. Recovered during the Continuity audits, provenance unknown, until three weeks ago—when our anomalous keystone began answering prayers in the Thornfells, and old vaults started... rattling. Things we sealed are loosening. Memories we thought devoured are surfacing. This one surfaced attached to a name." He looked up at me. "*Sera.*"

My heart was doing something undignified. "Lots of girls are named Sera."

"Not with your biometric resonance." He turned the slate toward me, and there on its surface, faint as breath on glass, was a shimmer—the visual signature of a stored memory, and beside it the verification glyph, the one that couldn't be forged, the one that matched the pattern in my own skull the way a key matches the particular ruin of a particular lock. "It's yours, Vane. Whatever else happens tonight, know that. We found it. It exists. Your childhood was taken, not lost, and it has been sitting in a Ministry vault in the capital for eleven years while you walked around being conveniently empty."

The fire was very loud. Or maybe that was me.

"Why show me this," I said, when I could trust my voice, "instead of just using it? Threaten to destroy it. Leverage. That's the textbook play."

"Because destruction isn't on the table anymore," he said, "and because I want you to *see* it first. I need you to understand what you'd be trading away, so that when you deliver the girl, you do it with clear eyes rather than resentment. Resentment makes operatives sloppy." He gestured to the cradle. "One fragment. One minute, perhaps less. A taste. Play it, refuse the rest, walk out of here tomorrow, and I'll consider the debt between us paid. Play it, accept the terms, and the whole archive is yours—every year, restored whole. All I ask is that R—— D—— arrives in the capital alive, and that she arrives *soon*."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll be very disappointed," said Corvane, "and the fragment goes back in the vault, and someday—when the Unremembering rolls through these mountains and takes everyone you've come to care about—you can think about it in the dark."

He was good. I want to be fair to my enemy: he was very, very good. He hadn't raised his voice once. He hadn't lied—not really, not verifiably. He had simply arranged the truth into a corridor with one door, and stood politely aside, and let me walk toward it myself.

I reached for the slate.

* * *

A garden gate.

That was all. That was everything.

White paint gone grey at the edges, peeling in long curls. A latch of black iron worn smooth by decades of hands. Beyond it, a garden gone slightly wild—roses climbing where they shouldn't, a stone path swallowed by thyme—and above it all a sky the color of late afternoon in a country I had never seen but knew instantly, bone-deep, the way you know a song from before language.

And a hand in mine.

Small. A child's hand, sticky at the palm, gripping two of my fingers in that way children grip—possessive, absolute, certain the hand they're holding will never be withdrawn. I couldn't see the child's face. The memory was too short for faces; it caught the gate, the roses, the smell of warm thyme crushed underfoot, and a voice—high, laughing, saying something I couldn't parse, some joke between us, some word that was ours alone—

And then it ended. The slate dimmed. The lodge came back: fire, wine, the neat grey man watching me with a craftsman's satisfaction.

I was crying. I note this without shame because I genuinely did not notice it happening; there was simply a warmth on my face, and when I touched my cheek my fingers came away wet, and somewhere far away Corvane was speaking.

"—the rest is like that. Forty-one fragments. A whole decade. There's a garden, Sera. There's a *someone*. The resonance suggests a sibling, possibly a twin—we won't know until restoration. Imagine knowing. Imagine waking up one morning and remembering who used to hold your hand."

I imagined it. God help me, I imagined it with the full strength of nine days of hard ground and fifteen days of silence and six years—no, eleven years—of being a well-made empty thing walking through a world full of people who had somewhere to come from.

And underneath the imagining, quieter, colder, the other thing: a different small hand. Chapped knuckles, ink stains, the hand of a runaway who slept sitting up against stable doors and gave half their bread to strangers' dogs. A hand that had gripped mine across a smuggler's inn table while we cheated each other badly at cards, both of us grinning like idiots, both of us lying with every second sentence and neither of us fooled for a moment.

Two hands. Two grips. And only enough soul in me to hold one.

"You should know," I heard myself say, from a great distance, "that the girl will fight you. And that I will have to lie to her to do it. And that I am apparently still capable of lying, which is useful, and also—"

"Also?" said Corvane, softly, the fisherman feeling the line go taut.

"—also that you made one mistake."

His expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts over a submerged rock. "Enlighten me."

"You showed me the fragment *first*," I said. "Before the terms. Before the contract. You let me taste it free, because you wanted my hunger honest." I stood. My legs were steadier than I deserved. "But a person who has remembered one minute of a garden is no longer empty, General. And you told me yourself—hollow things hold their shape." I picked up my knife from the table where they'd left it, all along, because they'd never truly feared I'd use it. "What's left of me now has a crack in it. Cracks remember."

For a moment—one heartbeat, perhaps two—I believed he would order the six grey riders in from the next room and end this the efficient way. His hand rested flat on the table near the reader's case, and his eyes did the arithmetic I'd seen him do over the bodies of lesser failures.

Then he smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man moving a piece on a board I couldn't see the edges of.

"Take the night," he said. "Ride out in the morning, whichever direction you choose. Consider it an experiment." He slid the slate back into its case and closed the brass fittings with a click that sounded obscenely final. "Either you bring me the keystone—or you run to warn her, and lead me straight to her, which is the same delivery with extra steps. I win either way, Sera. That is what it means to hold someone's heart in escrow."

* * *

I rode out before dawn, which was not the morning, and therefore technically not disobedience. I have decided to treasure that distinction forever.

The pass east of the lodge was white with frost, and my horse's breath plumed, and I rode hard with the fragment replaying behind my eyes on a loop I couldn't shut off: the gate, the thyme, the small sticky hand. Corvane thought he'd baited a hook. Maybe he had. Maybe I was swimming toward the boat with my mouth already bleeding, and every league east was a league closer to the trap he'd built around the only person I—

Around Rosalind. Say the name, Vane. You broke your own conditioning in a hunting lodge at midnight; the least you can do is stop hiding behind pronouns.

There was just one problem, and it sat in my chest like a swallowed coal: he was right about the mechanics. If I ran to her, I led the Ministry to her. If I ran anywhere, I led them everywhere, because I no longer knew which of my instincts were mine and which had been installed. The only move that saved her was the one move I couldn't make—disappearing, cutting loose, becoming nobody again—and I had spent eleven years being nobody and I would sooner eat the ledger itself than go back.

Unless.

There was a third option. Ugly, thin, desperate, the kind of plan you build at four in the morning on a frozen horse. It required a fireline, a liar's confession, and a woman stubborn enough to hear the worst thing I'd ever done and not walk into the snow.

I crested the ridge as the sun came up over the Thornfells, and below me, in a fold of the valley where no smoke should have been rising at that hour, there was smoke.

And around it, impossibly, out of the frost-hard ground, wildflowers.

Blooming in a perfect ring, in winter, like candles lit for someone expected home.
