## 10. Confession at the Fireline

The pass behind us was still screaming.

Not the wind—wind doesn't scream in words. This was Corvane's hounds, the Ministry's leash-hounds made of other people's forgotten griefs, and they were saying my name in a dozen voices I almost recognized. We'd come down the smuggler's road at a dead run with Sera's hand locked around my wrist like she was afraid I'd dissolve into the dark if she let go, and now we were three miles past the wardline with our lungs full of blood and pine resin, and I was starting to think we might actually live through the night.

That was when Sera stopped walking and said, "I have to tell you something, and you're going to want to kill me."

"Later," I gasped.

"No. Before the fire. Because once we sit down and you look warm and human, I'll lose my nerve."

I should have listened to that tone of voice. I've heard it before—from Maren, the night before everything went wrong. It's the tone people use when they've already decided something terrible and are only negotiating the hour of delivery.

We walked another mile anyway. Some cowardice is mutual.

* * *

The fireline was a shelf of granite above a frozen stream, sheltered by an overhang that some long-dead smuggler had carved with initials and crude jokes. Sera built the fire small and shielded it with her cloak the way Ministry-trained hands do everything: precisely, efficiently, as though even flame could be filed into a ledger. She fed it for a while without speaking. I watched the light move on her face and thought, not for the first time, that she had the kind of face that looked honest from every angle except the one where the truth was standing.

"So," I said. "Tell me."

She didn't look up. "You know what a Continuity assignment is. The file comes to your desk. A name, a threat assessment, a recommended procedure. Unremembering, usually. Sometimes just... trimming." Her thumb moved along the edge of her knife, back and forth, a motion I'd seen her make a hundred times without thinking anything of it. "Six years ago I was given a file."

My stomach did something slow and cold, like water finding a crack in a wall.

"The name in it had been half-eaten already," she went on. "Corrupted by whoever archived it first. R—— D——. Apprentice to a hedge-witch in the Thornfells. Flagged for keystone resonance." She finally raised her eyes. "Flagged, Rosalind. Six years ago. Before you ever ran. They knew what you were before you knew yourself."

"That's not possible," I said, which is what I say instead of *oh no*.

"I read the file twice. Standard procedure. And then—" Her hand stopped moving on the knife. "And then I requested reassignment. And then I filed a memory writ against myself."

The fire popped. Somewhere below us, the stream cracked and settled under its ice.

"You what?"

"There's a procedure. For Inquisitors who see something they shouldn't act on. You don't report it upward—you can't, the file goes into the general ledger and then everyone who reads it is compromised. So you eat it yourself. You write down what you know, you sign the writ, and the Ministry takes the memory out of you and files it away in the vaults, and you go on being useful." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "It's considered a mark of discipline. They gave me a commendation. I have a little pin somewhere. I threw it in a privy in Harrowmere two years ago, but officially I earned it."

I stood up. My legs decided this without consulting me.

"Let me make sure I understand," I said, and my voice came out very flat and very quiet, the way Maren's used to right before she hit me with a lesson I hadn't asked for. "Six years ago, the Ministry put my name on a list of things to be erased. You read it. You refused. And instead of warning anyone—instead of finding me, or finding Maren, or burning the Ministry's archive to the ground—you made yourself forget you'd refused. You forgot me. On purpose. And then you went on hunting people like me for six years with a hole in your head shaped exactly like my name."

"Yes," Sera said.

"Maren died for that file. Whatever happened to her—it happened because nobody stopped it. Because the one person who saw it coming chose to *forget*—"

"I chose the only thing that kept you alive!" She was on her feet too now, and the firelight jumped between us. "Do you understand how it works? If an Inquisitor refuses an assignment openly, the file escalates. Another Inquisitor takes it. Then a senior one. Then a retrieval team. Refusal isn't mercy, Rosalind, refusal is *escalation*. The only way to bury a name in that building is to bury it so deep even you can't dig it up again. So yes. I ate my own conscience and filed it, and I have spent six years waking up at night with my heart going like a trapped bird and no idea why, and last month, when I opened your case ledger and saw R—— D—— written in my own handwriting from six years ago, do you know what I felt?" Her voice broke on it. "Nothing. I felt *nothing*. I had to reconstruct my own treason from paperwork."

"Congratulations," I said. "You're very thorough."

"Don't." 

"You sat across from me at that inn. You dealt cards with me. You watched me lie about my own name and you said—" I had to stop, because my throat had closed around something. "You said you were glad you'd found me before someone else did."

"And I meant it."

"You didn't *know* what you meant! That's the whole point! You don't get credit for feelings you deleted!"

The stream muttered under its ice. The hounds' voices had faded hours ago, but I could still hear them somehow, or maybe that was just my blood.

Sera sat down slowly, the way people sit when their legs have stopped taking orders. "No," she said quietly. "I don't. You're right. But Rosalind—the writ didn't delete the choice. Only the knowing of it. The choice is still mine. It's been mine the whole time, running underneath everything like a river under ice. Every time I was sent after you and found reasons to be slow—every wrong turn, every misplaced warrant—I told myself I was tired, or careless, or that the trail had gone cold. I wasn't. I was keeping a promise I couldn't remember making." She looked at her hands. "I chose you over myself six years ago. Badly. Clumsily. In the stupidest way available to me. But I chose."

"And it cost you nothing," I said, "because you made sure you'd never feel the cost."

"It cost me everything. I just wasn't allowed to be there when it was paid."

That landed harder than any shout would have. I hated it. I wanted it to be a lie, and the worst part—the part that made me turn away and walk to the edge of the overhang and stare out at the black shoulder of the mountain—was that I recognized it. Running and forgetting are the same trick performed in different directions. I'd spent six years being no one, telling myself invisibility was safety when really it was just a slower way of doing to myself what the Ministry does with a writ.

"You could have told me at the inn," I said to the darkness. "Before the pass. Before I started trusting you."

"If I'd told you at the inn, you'd have run, and Corvane's team would have taken you at the ford. I needed you to trust me enough to follow me out." She paused. "Which is exactly what an Inquisitor would say, I know. I've had this argument with myself four times since yesterday."

"Who won?"

"Nobody. That's rather the problem with arguing with yourself."

Despite everything—despite the fury sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal—a breath came out of me that was nearly a laugh. I turned around. She was still sitting with her elbows on her knees, watching me with the expression of someone waiting to find out whether they still have a home.

"Why are you telling me now?" I asked. "What changed?"

"The hounds," she said. "They called your name in my mother's voice."

I went cold all over. "Your mother's voice."

"She died when I was nine. I don't remember her—I remember *that* I had her, the way you remember a room you slept in once. But the hound used her voice, and I knew it instantly, the way you know your own front door in the dark. Which means the Ministry has her memory. Filed. Eaten out of me and stored somewhere in the capital, probably in the same vault as my writ." Her jaw tightened. "Corvane showed me a restored memory once, back in chapter—back at the reservoir. A garden gate. He thinks he owns me because he holds my childhood in escrow. But tonight those hounds sang your name in a dead woman's throat, and I understood something." She stood. "He doesn't hold memories. He holds *hostages*. And hostages are only leverage until you decide they're already lost."

"Sera—"

"I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight. I'm asking you to keep walking with me tomorrow. There's a difference, and I know exactly how wide it is." She picked up the kettle and poured hot water over the last of the moon-herb, and the smell rose between us, bitter and green and familiar as my whole criminal career. "Drink something. Sleep if you can. In the morning you can hate me with better posture."

I took the cup. Our fingers touched around it, briefly, and neither of us pretended it meant nothing, and neither of us pretended it fixed anything.

I sat with my back against the warm granite and drank, and thought about rivers under ice, and promises that survive their own forgetting, and whether love counts if the lover can't remember loving. I thought about a page Maren tried to burn out of me, and how I'd fled rather than let her take even one memory—and here was Sera, who had taken a whole future and handed herself a commendation for it.

But she'd handed it *back*. At a fireline, in the cold, with hounds still singing her mother's voice in the dark. That counted for something. Maybe not forgiveness. Maybe just arithmetic.

"Tomorrow," I said finally, "you're going to tell me everything else. Every file, every order, every name in that ledger of yours. Starting with why Corvane wants me alive instead of eaten."

Something crossed her face then—quick, involuntary, gone before I could name it. Not guilt exactly. Something more like fear wearing guilt's coat.

"That," Sera said, stirring the fire with a stick until the sparks climbed, "is a longer story. And it starts with the reason your name was flagged six years ago in the first place." She looked up at me through the smoke. "Rosalind. Do you know what a consecration is?"

Below us, far off in the mountains, something answered—not a hound this time, but a low bell-note that came up through the rock itself, through the soles of my boots, through the old scar tissue of my missing years. And every hair on my arms stood up, because some part of me, some part deeper than the parts they'd stolen, remembered that sound.

Remembered answering it.
