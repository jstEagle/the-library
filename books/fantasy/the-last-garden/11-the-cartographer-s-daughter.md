## 11. The Cartographer's Daughter

The free-town of Ashkettle smelled like ink and onions, which is the smell I have come to associate with places that pretend they don't belong to anybody.

Officially, Ashkettle belonged to nobody. Officially, it was a market town that paid its taxes to three different provinces and none of them, a place where smugglers drank next to tax collectors and everyone agreed not to mention it. Unofficially—and by unofficially I mean according to Sera, who had spent nine years learning where all the bodies were buried, sometimes literally—the Ministry kept a man here. Not an Inquisitor. A listener. Someone who drank tea in the right houses and wrote letters in invisible ink and got paid in memories he'd never miss because he'd never had them.

"We should be careful," Sera said, for the fourth time since the bridge.

"You said that at the bridge."

"The bridge was also true."

I adjusted the pack on my shoulders and tried to look like a person with nothing inside her head worth eating. This had been my entire strategy for six years: be dull, be forgettable, be a smudge on the world's window. It had worked beautifully right up until wildflowers started blooming out of season and gods began whispering in my own childhood voice.

Now the strategy had changed. Now, per Maren's notes and the starving little god under the reservoir, I had to do the opposite of everything I'd trained myself to do. I had to remember. On purpose. Loudly.

And every Inquisitor in the mountains could feel remembering the way you feel someone walking on your grave.

"Careful," I agreed. "That's why we're going straight into town to find the one shop that deals in forbidden maps."

Sera made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "When you put it like that—"

"I know."

"—it sounds like a suicide note with extra steps."

"I *know*, Sera." I stopped at the edge of the market square, where the morning crowd was thickening around carts of winter apples and cheap ironware. Somewhere a fiddler was playing badly. It was so ordinary it hurt. "But the mapmaker's daughter was Maren's friend before I was born, and Maren trusted exactly two people in her whole life, and I'm fairly sure I'm not the other one."

"And if this mapmaker's daughter reports us?"

"Then you can arrest me," I said. "You've still got the shackles, presumably. Very official."

She gave me a look—the look, the one that had been developing steadily since the fireline confession, equal parts exasperation and something warmer she refused to name out loud. "I burned those shackles in Corvane's pass."

"That was very dramatic of you."

"It felt appropriate." She touched my elbow, brief, steering me left past a fishmonger who was shouting about prices as though the fish had personally wronged him. "Left. And Rosalind—if anything feels wrong in there, we leave. No heroics. You're not a weapon, you're a—"

"A seed vault," I said. "With legs. I've read the notes."

"*A person*," she said, with a sharpness that surprised both of us. Then, quieter: "You're a person. Who happens to be carrying a country. Try to remember which part matters more."

I didn't answer, because I genuinely didn't know anymore, and lying to her had gotten harder lately, which was its own kind of terrifying.

*

The print shop was wedged between a cooper's yard and a shuttered chapel—itself an act of quiet rebellion, given whose god had been worshipped there before the empire redrew the borders and the hymnals. The sign above the door showed a compass rose with one arm snapped off. Beneath it, in small neat lettering: **QUILLS & QUADRANTS — MAPS, CHARTS, SURVEYS. NOTHING FORBIDDEN SOLD HERE.**

"Nothing forbidden sold here," Sera read aloud. "That's either honest or the best lie I've seen all year."

"Only one way to find out."

The bell over the door announced us to a shop that smelled of lamp oil and dust and, underneath it, faintly but unmistakably, of mountain sage—the same smell that had clung to Maren's cottage, the same smell the blooming shrine exhaled like breath. My skin prickled. The old gods left traces now, everywhere, like a dog that had finally come home and was marking every wall.

A woman sat behind the counter, sixty-odd, gray braid coiled at her neck, mending a roller with a cobbler's needle. She looked up with the flat professional gaze of someone who has sold things to dangerous people for forty years and regrets none of them.

"Closed," she said.

"There's smoke from your chimney," Sera pointed out.

"For warmth. Not commerce."

"We're looking for Aldith Vey," I said. "Cartographer. Late of Velthmere."

The needle stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sound was the fiddler outside, working his way through a tune that had no business existing—a modal thing, minor-keyed, the kind of melody that doesn't belong to any province because it belongs to a country that officially never did. My chest went tight. Somebody in Ashkettle was playing Velthmere without knowing what they were playing, the way a scar remembers a wound.

The woman set down the needle. "Nobody's used that name in thirty years. Where did you hear it?"

"From a hedge-witch named Maren," I said. "Who learned it from your mother."

Something moved behind her eyes—grief and wariness fighting for the same ground. She stood, crossed to the door, turned the lock, and flipped the sign. Then she pulled aside a shelf of survey charts, revealing a narrow stair going down into lamplight.

"Down," she said. "And if either of you is Ministry, know that my mother built the cellar door to drop anyone heavier than her onto spikes, and I've kept it oiled out of sentiment."

"She sounds delightful," Sera murmured.

"She was," the woman said simply, and led us down.

* * *

The cellar was not a cellar. Or rather, it was a cellar the way a cathedral is a room.

Shelves climbed the stone walls to the ceiling, crammed with rolled maps, brass instruments, jars of ink ground from minerals I didn't recognize. But the center of the room held a table the size of a hay wagon, and on the table lay a map of the Thornfell mountains so detailed it looked like the mountains themselves had lain down and died there—every ridge, every ravine, every smuggler's path drawn in fine brown lines, and threaded through them, in colors that seemed almost to shift when I blinked, other paths. Paths that weren't on any map I'd ever stolen or carried.

Paths that hummed.

Not literally. Probably. But standing near that table was like standing near the shrine, near the reservoir ruins—that low pressure behind the ears, the sense of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Aldith," I said.

"Tam." She offered no hand. Her eyes were doing something I recognized: cataloguing me. Face, hands, the way I stood, the sigil-shaped pendant Maren had left me that I still hadn't decided whether to wear openly or bury in a ditch. "Maren sent word once a year, then once every three years, then nothing. Six years ago, nothing. What happened to her?"

"That's why we're here. Her cottage was ransacked in early autumn. She's gone. Taken or fled, we don't know which."

Tam closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the grief had been filed away somewhere, neatly, the way people file things they intend to use later as fuel. "Then you'd better tell me the rest, and tell it fast, because if Maren's gone, whatever she was protecting has come looking for a new keeper. And I'm too old and too stubborn to be it." Her gaze dropped to my pack. "Show me what she left you."

I hesitated. Six years of instinct screamed at me: *don't show anyone, don't be visible, don't exist.* But six years of instinct had also gotten Maren's cottage ransacked while I hid in the hills smelling moon-herb and feeling sorry for myself.

I set the pack on the great table and unwrapped Maren's notes—the pages in her cramped hand, the pressed sprig of white bloom, and the keystone sigil itself, drawn on vellum so old it had gone the color of tea. A circle of nine points. At its heart, a gate.

Tam went very still.

She didn't touch it. She leaned close instead, the way you lean toward a sleeping animal, and read the sigil the way Sera read ledgers—with total, professional attention. Then she laughed. It wasn't a happy laugh. It was the laugh of someone confirming a diagnosis.

"My mother drew that," she said. "Forty-one years ago. From memory. She was one of the last surveyors licensed under the old crown—before the Continuity Act, before they burned the archives and called it peace. She mapped Velthmere's last border. And when the Ministry came for her records, she hid the true ones—" Tam tapped the table, the humming paths, the shifting colors, "—inside the trade routes. Every false road on every smuggler's chart in these mountains hides a true one underneath. The Ministry thinks the smugglers' roads are lies. They're the most honest documents in the country. Nobody audits a crime."

Sera made a low whistle. "That's why the passes never appear on Ministry surveys. We assumed corruption."

"Corruption helps," Tam agreed. "But mostly it's that the roads are *anchored*. To things the Ministry can't see, because seeing requires remembering, and remembering—" she spread her hands, "—well. You two would know better than I what happens to people who remember."

"What are the anchors?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, the way you know a tune before the words arrive.

"Shrines. Wells. Threshold stones. The drowned city under the reservoir." Tam looked at me directly. "And one moving thing. One door."

My mouth had gone dry. "The Last Garden."

"So it does have a name now. In my mother's day it was just 'the nursery.'" Tam walked slowly around the table, trailing her fingers above the map without touching it, following one of the colored threads. "Here's what she told me, and she told me drunk, once, the night before the Ministry men came, so forgive the poetry. The Garden isn't a place. Places hold still. The Garden *walks*. It was planted as a refuge during the first purges, and it moves along the old ley-lines like—" she searched for the word, "—like a snail carries its shell. Its door appears where it wants to appear. Most people walk past it every day of their lives and see a rock face, a hedgerow, a locked garden wall, nothing. Because the door isn't locked. It's *hidden*. Hidden in plain sight requires a key, and the key isn't metal."

Her eyes came up to mine, and I felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it always did just before my life rearranged itself again.

"The key is memory. Whole, unbroken, living memory of the country the Garden was planted to preserve. The Ministry ate everything else. There's exactly one intact copy left in the world." She nodded at my pack. At me. At the thing I had spent six years pretending I wasn't. "It's walking around wearing a person."

* * *

I sat down on a crate because my legs had opinions about standing.

This wasn't new information, exactly. Maren's notes had said *keystone*, had said *archive*, had laid out the ritual and the betrayal of my parents in her tight furious handwriting. But there is a difference between reading that you are a vault and hearing a stranger say it across a table, in a voice full of pity and awe in equal measure, while the actual vault in question sits in your chest taking notes.

"Say the rest," I managed.

Tam's expression softened by a degree. "There's a price. There's always a price; my mother said magic is just accounting that bites. The door shows itself to the bearer. Only the bearer. Which means to find the Garden, you have to stop suppressing it." She let that land. "Everything Maren taught you—to hide it, to keep the walls up, to be nobody—I need you to understand that every one of those habits is now pointing you away from the thing you're looking for. The Garden won't show itself to a smudge. It'll show itself to Rosalind Dunmore of Velthmere, last consecrated child of the nursery, in full."

"In full," Sera repeated quietly. She was standing very straight, arms crossed, and I knew that posture. It was the posture she wore while calculating how many ways a plan could kill us. "Which means opening the archive. Deliberately. Widely."

"Widely enough to be heard," Tam said. "Yes. The Ministry hunts anomalous recollection—they feel it like heat through a wall. The moment she starts truly remembering, every Inquisitor within fifty miles will know where she is and roughly what she is. Including—" a glance at Sera, frank, unafraid, "—your former colleagues. Including the General himself, if the stories are true."

"They're true," Sera said. "Worse, actually."

"Then I'll say it plainly." Tam braced her hands on the table, over the humming map, and addressed me like a surgeon addressing a patient who deserves honesty more than comfort. "You have spent six years being safe by being empty. To open the Garden, you must become the opposite of empty, loudly, in a country that eats people for exactly that. There is no version of this where you stay hidden and also reach the door. Choose."

* * *

We stood afterward in the yard behind the shop, among stacked cordwood and a rain barrel catching the gray afternoon light, because Tam had work to do and a living to protect and had made it clear, kindly, that our continued presence endangered both.

Sera paced. I watched her pace. It was almost meditative.

"You could go back," I said, when she'd worn a groove in the mud. "Turn me in. Tell Corvane where I am, get your childhood back out of escrow. He'd honor it—he keeps his bargains, it's the one decent thing about him."

She stopped pacing. "Do you want me to?"

"No."

"Then stop offering it like it's on the menu." She came and stood in front of me, close, and the wry armor slipped just enough to show the fear underneath. "Rosalind. If you open yourself up the way she described, I can't shield you alone. Shielding works through shared moments—we proved that on the road—but one person anchoring another against the full weight of the Ministry's attention?" She shook her head. "I've seen Inquisitors burn out trying to suppress a single village's grief. You'd be asking me to hold back a tide with a teacup."

"Then we find more teacups."

"Such as?"

I looked back at the shop. Through its grimy front window, lamplight fell on the counter where Tam Vey sat bent over her mending, and beyond her the great humming map of a dead country threaded with live roads. Somewhere out in the square, the fiddler was playing the modal tune again, and this time I caught words under it—half-remembered, dialect words, a lullaby from a nation that officially never existed, sung by a man who couldn't possibly know what he was singing.

The erased gods were waking. The villagers were dreaming in a dead tongue. Prayers were igniting across the mountains like signal fires, and each one, Maren's notes said, was a thread of the same fabric I carried.

"They remember," I said slowly, feeling the idea take shape the way ice takes shape on a pond, spreading outward from the first cold point. "Not fully. Not cleanly. But it's in them—in songs they can't source, in griefs they can't name. If I'm not the only vessel, I'm the clearest one, but I don't have to broadcast alone. I anchor to the land itself. To the prayers. To—" I laughed, a bit wildly, "—to a god of thresholds who owes me a favor."

Sera followed my gaze to the window, to the map. Understanding moved across her face like weather. "You're talking about making yourself a beacon on purpose."

"I'm talking about stopping being a fugitive," I said. "Fugitives run and hide and hope. I'm done hoping to remain unseen. I want to be *seen*, Sera—by the right things first. By the Garden. By the country that's starting to dream again. Let Corvane come. Let him feel me remembering from fifty miles off and come running." My hand found the sigil pendant through my shirt, warm against my sternum, warmer than body temperature had any right to explain. "Because a beacon doesn't just call hunters. It calls everything. And there are older things than the Ministry listening on these frequencies now."

She was quiet for a long moment. Rain began, fine and cold, spotting the barrel's surface.

"When do we start?" she asked finally, which was Sera for *yes, obviously, and I hate that I love you*, and we were both, by unspoken treaty, not examining that last clause yet.

"Now," I said. "Tonight. Tam's going to draw me the approach routes—all of them, true and false—and tomorrow we walk the line the map hums loudest along. And tomorrow night, somewhere high and defensible, I open the archive." I swallowed. "All the way."

Sera took my hand. Hers was steady. Mine wasn't, and she didn't pretend otherwise, and somehow that helped more than steadiness would have.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"Whatever comes out of you when you open it—the child, the parents, the fall of Velthmere, all of it—you don't carry it alone for even one hour. You wake me. Every time. Even if it's just to sit by the fire while you shake." Her thumb moved once over my knuckles. "Deal?"

Behind us, inside the shop, the great map's colored threads pulsed—once, slow, unmistakable this time, like something beneath the mountains rolling over and opening one eye.

"Deal," I said, and the pendant against my chest answered with a warmth like a struck match.
