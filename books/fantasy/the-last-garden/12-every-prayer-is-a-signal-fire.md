## 12. Every Prayer Is a Signal Fire

The first prayer went up the night Rosalind remembered their mother's hands.

I know this because I was awake, and because I felt it — a warmth in the air like a struck match three rooms away, except we only had one room, and it came from inside Rosalind's chest. They sat bolt upright on the inn's narrow bed with their eyes open and wet, whispering something over and over, and outside, in the frozen yard of the Waystone Inn, every lamp lit itself at once.

Not the oil lamps. The old ones. The stone lanterns set into the gateposts that nobody had tended since before the Empire drew its borders, carved with faces so worn smooth that everyone had stopped seeing them as faces at all. They flared green-white, steady as held breath, and stayed lit.

"Rosalind," I said carefully, "what did you do?"

"Nothing." Their voice cracked on it. "That's the point. I just — *remembered*. Her thumb had a scar, from a harvest knife. She used to rub it against my palm when I couldn't sleep. That's all I did. I just let myself have her back for ten seconds, and—"

The second lantern lit itself. This one was down the road, half a mile off, past where either of us could see. We heard it anyway: a soundless bloom of pressure, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"That," I said, "was not nothing."

* * *

Here is what they don't put in Ministry training, or rather what they put in and then make you unlearn: prayer is not a petition. Prayer is a signal.

Every Inquisitor learns to read recollection the way a falconer reads the sky. A strong memory radiates. A grief held long enough develops weather. And when enough people remember the same thing at the same time — a god, say, a name, a face carved into a gatepost two hundred years ago — the resonance climbs until even a mediocre sensor can find it across a valley.

Rosalind Dunmore was not a mediocre memory. Rosalind was a keystone, an archive wearing a person's skin, and every time they reached deliberately into themselves and pulled out one true thing, they rang like a cathedral bell dropped down a mineshaft.

"You're lighting beacons," I told them, the next morning, while they ate bread like a person who hadn't spent six years being hunted. "Every memory you take back, somewhere out there, Corvane feels it land. You might as well stand on the highest peak in Thornfell with a mirror and flash him your location in Morse."

"Then teach me Morse," they said. "Teach me how to do it quietly."

And that was the problem, wasn't it. Because there was no quiet way. Not anymore. The mapmaker's shop in Harrowgate had given us the truth and the price in the same sentence: *stop running, start remembering.* The Garden would show its door only to someone carrying Velthmere whole. Which meant Rosalind had to walk toward Corvane with their soul wide open, gathering everything he wanted to eat, and hope to arrive first.

Hope. The Ministry had a file on hope too. It was filed under *tactical error, recurring.*

"All right," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and professional, which was the only way I could say what I said next without it meaning more than it meant. "There's a technique. It isn't taught. I've seen it used once."

"By whom?"

"By me." I turned my cup in my hands. "When I refused your file. When I decided I'd rather forget refusing than live knowing I'd betrayed the Ministry. I hid the decision somewhere Corvane couldn't reach it. Inside someone else."

Rosalind looked up. They had flour on their knuckles from helping Marta the innkeeper bake, because apparently even fugitives couldn't resist Marta's stare until they worked for their breakfast. "Inside who?"

"Inside a moment we shared." I made myself keep eye contact, because if I looked away it became a confession instead of a lesson. "Memories you hold alone are yours. Corvane can take them; he's built his whole career on taking them. But a memory that lives between two people — a moment neither of you owns outright — doesn't come out clean. He'd have to pull the whole shared thing, both sides of it, and shared things tear. Badly. For him, mostly, but not pleasantly for us either."

"So we share everything," Rosalind said slowly. "We braid it together. My memories, your memories, wound around each other until no Inquisitor can tell whose is whose."

"It's not a perfect defense. It's—" 

"A signal fire we build under a roof," they said, and smiled, and I hated them a little for making it sound easy, and loved them considerably more for not asking why I already knew how.

* * *

We started small, because intimacy, like arson, is best practiced away from witnesses.

The technique has no official name. In the field, ex-Inquisitors — a population currently consisting of me — call it *twining*. You take a moment. Any moment will do, but it should be real, specific, textured with details that belong equally to both participants. Then each person holds the same instant from their own side of it, and you press the two versions together until they fuse — not blended, fused, the way two streams merge and you can't afterwards say which water was which.

The first moment we twined was an accident, technically. The night before, in the yard, after the lanterns lit — Rosalind had grabbed my wrist. I'd felt their pulse hammering, felt my own answer it, and for about four seconds we had stood there sharing exactly one sensation: fear, bright and mutual, with a current underneath neither of us named.

"That one," I said. "Hold that one. Every detail."

"The cold," Rosalind said, eyes shut. "Your sleeve was rough where the cuff was frayed. Your pulse was faster than mine."

"It was not."

"It absolutely was, Sera. I counted."

"Fascinating. Hold it anyway." I pressed my hand over theirs, the same wrist, the same grip, and rebuilt the moment around us: the frost coming up through our boot soles, the green light on our faces, the smell of snow and lamp-oil and the particular iron tang of magic waking up after two centuries of starvation. Rosalind's version slid against mine like a key finding its ward.

And then it locked.

I don't know how else to describe it. One moment, two owners, zero edges. When I reached inward afterward to check it was still mine, I found Rosalind's heartbeat in it, their cold-numbed fingers, the exact pitch of the breath they'd taken — and none of it felt borrowed. It felt *shared*, the way a hearth is shared by everyone warming their hands at it.

"Oh," Rosalind said softly, opening their eyes. "Oh, I can feel it from your side. Sera, that's — you were terrified too. You were terrified and you didn't run."

"I'm an excellent runner. Ask anyone I've arrested."

They laughed, and the laugh twined too, and somewhere in the mountains a third lantern lit itself, gentler this time, almost embarrassed, like a candle rather than a beacon. Progress. Sort of.

* * *

Corvane answered on the fourth day.

We knew because the birds left. All of them, in one morning, streaming south over the pass in silence, which any smuggler will tell you is worse than wolves. And then, at dusk, we saw it from the ridge above the Waystone: far to the east, beyond three ridgelines, a wave moving through the valleys.

It didn't look like much. That's the horror of the Unremembering — it looks like weather. A grey shimmer rolling slow along a river valley, bending slightly around hills, pooling in low ground. Villages stood in its path with their windows lit and their smoke rising, ordinary as supper.

Then it passed through them.

The lights didn't go out. The smoke kept rising. Nothing burned, nothing screamed. But as the shimmer crossed each village, I watched through Rosalind's spyglass — their hands, my training — and saw the people stop. Mid-stride, mid-gesture, mid-laugh. Each one stood still for perhaps five heartbeats, swaying like wheat, and then moved on.

Moving on lighter. Moving on *emptier.*

"They're taking everything," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "Not targeted extraction. Corvane's done it before, once, in the border provinces — a saturation sweep. Everything loose. Faces, names, last week, last year. What survives is whatever's nailed down by habit. People keep their skills and lose their reasons."

Rosalind lowered the glass. Below us, in the nearest valley, a shepherd stood in a field staring at his own flock with the polite bafflement of a man trying to remember why sheep mattered to him.

"One of those villages," Rosalind said. "Fenlow. We came through Fenlow." Their face did something complicated. "Marta's sister lives in Fenlow."

"Marta's sister lives in Fenlow," I confirmed, because lying to them had become physically difficult somewhere around the second twined memory, which was either love or a security vulnerability, possibly both.

"We can't outrun that wave."

"No."

"And every time I remember anything, I'm ringing a bell that tells him exactly where to send the next one." They sat down hard on the cold rock, and for a moment they were just a tired person on a mountain, and I understood with terrible clarity that I would burn down the entire Ministry of Continuity for that tired person, and that this understanding was now stored somewhere Corvane could never reach it, twined into a moment in a frozen yard, and thank every starving god for that.

"So we get better at the roof," I said.

"What?"

"The signal fire. We build it under a roof. If your memories are twined — if everything you reclaim gets braided into moments we share — then when Corvane sweeps, he hits knots. Walls. He can take what floats free, but the load-bearing parts are joint property now." I sat beside them. Our shoulders touched. Deliberately, on my side; I'll admit that. "It means you can't do this alone anymore. Every memory you take back, you bring to me first. We hold it together. Both sides. No exceptions."

Rosalind turned to look at me, and the last light was doing something unfair to their face. "You understand what you're offering. You're offering to be inside my head for every single—"

"Yes."

"—and some of these memories aren't going to be pretty. Maren. The night I ran. Whatever my parents—" Their voice caught on the word and they pushed through it, jaw tight. "You'll be holding those too. From the inside."

"I've held worse. I held a career in the Ministry."

That earned me half a smile, gone quickly. "Why?" they asked, and it wasn't rhetorical, and we both knew it. "Why would you do this?"

Because you lit lanterns when you grieved. Because you gave a stranger's daughter her name back in a hollowed village and called it a mistake and would do it again. Because I was sent to erase you and instead I memorized you, and memorization, it turns out, was just erasure practiced in reverse.

"There's a moment," I said instead, "from two nights ago. You fell asleep at the table over Maren's notes. I carried you to bed. You said my name without waking up. Like it was a word you'd always known."

"I remember that."

"Hold it," I said. "From your side. I'll hold it from mine. And then we'll nail it down where no one can ever take it from either of us."

* * *

We got good at it, is the thing nobody warns you about.

Twining takes trust the way drowning takes water, and we had plenty of both. Within a week Rosalind was reclaiming memories at a rate that would have gotten them killed solo — Maren's hedge-lessons, the taste of Velthmere honey cake from a country that officially never existed, the consecration hymn they'd been made to sing as a child without understanding it — and each one came to me first, and we built it into the weave.

The weave grew. That's the only word. Some nights I lay awake cataloguing what we'd become: two people with a shared architecture in their heads, corridors running between us that hadn't existed in October. I'd reach for a memory of the Glass Lake crossing and find Rosalind's terror-of-the-cracking-ice threaded through my focus. They'd reach for their mother's scarred thumb and find my memory of watching them cry, holding it gently, keeping it warm for them.

Intimacy as armor. Love as a survival technique. The Ministry had files on weaponized memory, on memetic warfare, on emotional compromise of assets. Nowhere in any of them was there a line item for *two people who decided that belonging to each other was safer than belonging to themselves,* and I thought, with a certain grim satisfaction, that Corvane's ledgers had finally met something they couldn't index.

The prayers proved it.

As the weave thickened, the mountains woke faster. Lanterns we'd never touched lit along roads we'd never walked. Shepherds in the high passes began leaving offerings again — a twist of wool here, a cup of milk there — without knowing to whom, saying words that predated the Empire and tasting them like a language half-remembered from infancy. Once, crossing a ford, an old woman grabbed Rosalind's sleeve, stared at them with tears running freely down her face, and said, "You. I dreamed about you before you were born," and then wandered off, entirely serene, having no idea what she'd meant.

Rosalind took to sleeping less. The memories crested in them like a fever breaking and re-breaking. Some nights I woke to find them standing at the window, lips moving, and the whole valley below flickering faintly green as forgotten shrines tested their voices.

And Corvane swept. Again and again. We watched the grey waves cross the valleys, and the villages came through them thinner every time — and here was the proof, written in other people's suffering: the sweep hit Rosalind's region of the mountains harder wherever the weave was thin. Where we'd been careless, where a memory floated free, the Unremembering found purchase and tore. Where we'd built well, the wave broke and slid past, frustrated, like surf on sea-walls.

"He knows," Rosalind said one night, grimly satisfied, watching another failed sweep pool and drain away below us. "He knows something's blocking him. He just can't see what."

"He thinks it's a technique." I passed them the tea Marta had sent up, blessed woman. "Let him. Let him waste divisions hunting a countermeasure that doesn't exist."

"And when he figures out it's not a technique? When he figures out it's *us?*"

I drank my tea and didn't answer, and Rosalind, who could now literally feel my non-answer from the inside, sighed and leaned their head against my shoulder, which was also becoming part of the weave, God help us.

"Then we'd better reach the Garden first," they said.

* * *

The message came on the morning the sky turned the wrong colour.

Not storm-grey. Not dawn-pink. A bruised violet, high and eastward, spreading like ink in water, and beneath it the pressure-silence of every bird within fifty miles going still at once. I'd seen that colour once before, from the safe side of a containment operation in the Karst. Saturation event, Class Five. Corvane had stopped sending waves.

He was bringing the tide himself.

It came with a herald, too — that was new, that was theatrical, that was Corvane all over. A rider on the mountain road below the Waystone, black-liveried, who didn't approach, didn't dismount, simply unrolled a scroll and spoke in a voice pitched to carry unnaturally far, the way Ministry-trained voices do:

"*To the keystone, and to the traitor Vane. Every memory you plant, I will harvest. Every name you return, I will eat twice. Come to the Glass Lake by the dark of the moon, or I will unmake every village between here and the coast, starting with the one called Fenlow, where a woman named Odette Marta's-sister keeps a loom she will no longer remember how to use.*"

The rider rolled the scroll. Turned the horse. Rode on.

Silence, except for the wind, and Rosalind breathing beside me in short, controlled pulls, and the green light of forty lanterns kindling along every ridge at once — the mountains themselves, answering the threat, glowing like a fuse burning toward something.

"He's done waiting," I said.

"No." Rosalind's voice came out strange, doubled almost, as though something older were speaking through the same throat. They were looking east at the violet sky, and their hand found mine without either of us deciding it, and I felt the weave shiver all along its length. "No, Sera. He's done *hunting*. Look at the route he named. The Glass Lake. By the dark of the moon."

I ran it through my head, the old maps, the smuggler routes, the drowned-country geography the mapmaker had scratched for us. The Glass Lake. Frozen. Flat. A place where a man with an army could see anyone coming from miles.

Or — my stomach dropped — a place where a door that moves might choose to stand still.

"He's not threatening us away from it," I breathed. "He's herding us toward it."

Below us, in the yard of the Waystone, every lantern flared at once, green-white, urgent, unmistakable — and in the space between one heartbeat and the next, I heard what they were saying, or Rosalind heard it and I heard through them, which by now amounted to the same thing:

*Come. The door is open. And you are not the only ones who know.*
