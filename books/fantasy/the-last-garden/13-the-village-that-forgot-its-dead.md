## 13. The Village That Forgot Its Dead

The Unremembering had come through Lowmere three days before we did, and you could tell by the way the dogs looked at us.

Dogs remember. That's the thing nobody tells you about memory-eating. It takes names first, then faces, then the shape of a life, and it works from the top down—priests before farmers, mothers before children—because the Ministry believes history is a hierarchy and grief is just a filing error. But dogs don't have names to lose in the way people do, or they do, and they don't care, so when the wave passed through Lowmere it slid off them like rain off slate. Every dog in the village watched us walk in with the flat, patient attention of creatures who knew exactly what had happened here and were waiting for someone else to figure it out.

"They're going to follow us," Sera said quietly.

"Everything follows us lately."

"I mean specifically." She nodded at a brindled bitch sitting in the middle of the road like a toll gate. "That one's been behind us since the pass. I thought it was coincidence."

"Nothing's been coincidence since the shrine," I said. "You know that."

Sera didn't answer. She'd taken to wearing her Inquisitor's coat inside out, the grey lining showing, which was either a disguise or a confession, and she carried Corvane's burned orders folded into a shape that fit her palm like a stone she kept forgetting to put down. Three days since the lake. Two nights since she'd stopped flinching at the sound of her own name. Progress, of a kind.

Lowmere sat in a fold of the hills where the smuggler's roads crossed an honest one, which meant it had always had two economies and no illusions about either. It should have been loud. Market day, or near enough—the stalls were still standing, awnings strung, a cart of late apples parked crooked in the square as though its owner had simply stepped away to argue with someone. But there were no voices. There was the sound of a broom somewhere, sweeping nothing in particular, and under it a hum like a struck bell that had decided against ringing.

The hum was the prayers. I could hear them now. That was new, that was mine, that was the price of everything Maren had loaded into me coming up like water into a well: every prayer said in these mountains arrived somewhere, and apparently that somewhere was me. Most of them were small. *Let the bread rise. Let him write. Let me sleep past dawn.* A few were enormous and wordless, whole lives compressed into a single ache. And underneath all of them, threaded through the village like root-rot through a floorbeam, was a silence shaped exactly like the missing parts of two hundred people.

*Don't stop,* Sera murmured, which was what we'd agreed. *Don't look at anyone too long. We pass through, we resupply, we go.*

We passed the apple cart. A woman stood beside it, arranging fruit that needed no arranging, turning each apple over in hands that moved with the muscle-memory of a market she could no longer describe. She smiled at us. It was a good smile, warm and automatic, the smile of a woman who had always been friendly and now had nothing left to be friendly *about*. Her eyes went over my face, over Sera's, and found nothing to hold.

"Fine morning," she said.

"It is," I said, because lying to the hollowed seemed crueler than lying to anyone else.

She went back to her apples. Behind her, chalked on the cart's rail in a hand that shook, someone had written: *I HAD A STALL HERE. I THINK IT WAS MY STALL.*

Sera saw it too. Her jaw did the thing it did instead of speaking.

The church—or what had been a church; the empire had re-roofed it with imperial slate and called it a Continuity Hall, and the villagers had gone on calling it the church because villagers always win eventually—stood open at the end of the square. Its bell rope hung cut. Inside, we would learn, the registers were ash, the baptismal rolls scraped to vellum blankness, the memorial plaques unscrewed and stacked by the door like firewood that hadn't yet been told it was sacred.

But the graveyard was behind it, and the graveyard was where they'd all gone.

* * *

There must have been sixty of them, standing among the stones in the thin mountain light, and not one of them was making a sound.

They weren't arranged. That was the worst of it. Grief organizes itself—mourners cluster at the fresh grave, the family stands at the head, the priest takes his place—and this was nothing like that. They were scattered across the whole yard like people who had wandered out of their houses following something only half-remembered, and then forgotten why they'd come, and then forgotten they'd forgotten, and stayed anyway because their feet refused to carry them home. An old man stood with his hat pressed to his chest before a stone so weathered its inscription predated the empire. Two sisters—I decided they were sisters, the way they leaned together—stood at a grave with a child's lamb carved on it. A young man knelt in wet grass he didn't seem to feel, facing a marker whose name had been chiseled out so recently the granite still showed pale in the scar.

Nobody was crying. That was the detail that finally got under my skin and made a nest there. They had all the posture of mourning and none of its motion. Tears need a reason attached, and the Ministry had eaten the reasons and left the bodies standing at attention like soldiers dismissed without the order to fall out.

"We should go around," Sera said. Her voice had gone flat, professional, the voice she used when feeling was a liability. "Rosalind. We should go around."

"I know."

"You're not going around."

"No," I said. "I'm really not."

Because I could hear them. Not the mourners—the graves. Every stone in that yard was humming, faintly, the way the shrine had hummed, the way the threshold god had hummed in the drowned dark of Velthmere. Names don't vanish when they're eaten. I'd learned that much. They go *somewhere*, the way smoke goes somewhere, and the ground under Lowmere was full of smoke, pressing up against the underside of the world, knocking.

And I was the door. Maren had made sure of that six years ago, loading me like a mule and running me into the mountains, and every mile since had been the empire trying to decide whether I was treasure or contraband.

"Rosalind." Sera caught my sleeve. Up close her eyes had the lake in them still, some cold green thing that hadn't thawed. "If you give even one name back, Corvane will feel it. He felt the woman on the glass lake. He'll triangulate within a day. Maybe hours. Everything we've done to stay invisible—"

"I know the strategy," I said. "I wrote half of it."

"Then—"

"And the strategy says sixty people stand in a graveyard until they die of standing, and nobody ever knows who they buried, and the Ministry wins another quiet little round." I turned my hand over and took hers off my sleeve—not pushing it away. Holding it. She let me, which after the lake meant more than I could afford to think about. "Sera. You were Ministry. Tell me what happens to a village like this if nobody intervenes. Officially."

Her mouth twisted. "Nothing happens. That's the point. No unrest, no anomalies, no file. The village continues. Children are born who are never told there was anything to forget. In forty years Lowmere is a place that has always been slightly sad for reasons no one can name, and the ledger stays clean." She paused. "It's efficient."

"It's obscene."

"Yes," she said. "I know. I signed off on eleven of these." She said it the way you'd report a wound—factual, already infected. "So I'm not telling you it's right. I'm telling you what it costs. If you open your mouth in that yard, you become the loudest thing in these mountains, and everything hunting us stops pretending to hunt and starts arriving."

Somewhere above us, on the ridge, a crow laughed like it understood the terms.

I looked at the kneeling man. He'd begun, very slowly, to smooth the grass in front of the defaced stone, patting it flat, the way you'd settle a blanket over someone sleeping. He didn't know whose grave it was. He only knew it was *someone's*, and that the someone deserved better than pale granite and a hole in the world, and that knowledge had been enough to bring him to his knees and keep him there for three days.

My whole life I'd been careful. Six years of being no one, of salt-smuggling and false names and sleeping in ditches with my memories packed down tight as gunpowder. Careful had kept me alive. Careful had also kept sixty people standing in a graveyard like furniture.

Maren burned a page of me once, trying to save my life. I'd hated her for it. I understood it better every day. But understanding wasn't the same as agreeing, and there came a point where survival was just a slower way of being eaten.

"All right," I said. "One name. One. And then we run."

* * *

Choosing which mourner was its own kind of horror. I walked the yard slowly, Sera at my shoulder radiating disapproval like a stove, and listened. Under each stone the eaten names pressed upward, and most of them were just weight—grief with the words boiled out. But near the yew tree at the yard's corner there was a grave where the hum had a shape to it, almost a melody, and standing before it was a woman perhaps fifty years old with flour still dusted on one forearm, as though she'd walked out of her kitchen mid-loaf and simply never returned to it.

She was holding something. A wooden bird, roughly carved, painted blue in a child's idea of blue. She turned it over and over. Every few turns she'd stop, look at it like it might explain itself, and start again.

"That's hers," she said, when I stopped beside her. Not looking up. Talking to me the way you talk to yourself in an empty house. "I know it's hers. I don't know whose hers is." She laughed, one dry note with nothing under it. "Listen to me. That's not even a sentence."

"It's a true one," I said. "Those are rarer."

"She made it. I know she made it because I know I helped her hold the knife, and I know—" Her hands tightened on the bird. "I know I loved her. That's the part they couldn't take, do you understand? They took her name and her face and her voice and the year she was born and the fever she died of, they took all of it, and I'm still standing here loving her like a hole loves the shape of what used to fill it. What kind of mercy is that? What kind of *butchery* is that?"

"A thorough one," I said softly.

She finally looked at me. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, three days past tears with nothing to show for it. "You're not from the Hall."

"No."

"You're not from anywhere. I can see it. You've got that look, like a person written in pencil." She held up the blue bird between us. "Can you fix this?"

And there it was. The whole question, held out in a child's carved hand.

I want to say I weighed it longer than I did. I want to say I thought of Sera, of the lake, of Corvane's reach, of the Garden and the plan and the hundred good reasons to shake my head and walk on. I did think of them—all of them, in the space of a breath, the way you think of everything when you're already falling. But the truth is simpler and less flattering: a woman asked me for her daughter's name, and I had it, because I had everything, because that was what I was *for*, and there has never been a version of me that could look at that bird and say no.

"Hold still," I said. "This will feel like a stitch being pulled. It hurts for a moment and then it doesn't, and then it does again, differently. Don't fight it."

"What are you—" she started.

I reached into myself, into the archive Maren had died protecting, and found the thread. Velthmere's last intact memory, and woven through it, small as a knot in wool: *Lowmere. The baker's house. A girl called—*

I pulled.

The sound she made wasn't a word. It was the noise a lock makes when it's been rusted shut for years and finally gives—a crack, a shudder, and then air moving through a space that had been sealed. She staggered; I caught her elbow. The blue bird trembled in her grip.

"Brenna," she whispered. Then louder, appalled, delighted, destroyed: "*Brenna.* My daughter's name was Brenna. She was nine. She liked—she used to line her shoes up under the bed like little soldiers, she was scared of deep water and she jumped in anyway last summer, she jumped in and I nearly died of fright and she came up laughing—" The tears came now, all three days of them at once, and she laughed through them, and the sound went across the graveyard like a spark going through dry grass.

Because the other mourners had heard. And whatever the Ministry had left in them recognized it—the shape of a name returning, the specific frequency of grief getting its words back—and they began to turn, sixty faces, sixty hollowed silences, all looking at me.

"Oh no," Sera said, very quietly, behind me. "Rosalind. Oh *no*."

The old man with the hat came first. Then the sisters. Then the kneeling boy, rising from the grass with granite dust on his knees, and they didn't grab, they didn't beg, they just *gathered*, the way people gather toward a fire in a cold room, and every one of them was looking at me with the same expression the woman had worn: hope with nowhere left to put itself.

I looked at Sera.

She looked back at me for a long moment. On the ridge, I'd swear later, the crow went silent. And then she exhaled through her nose, closed her eyes briefly like a gambler watching her last coin leave her hand, and said, "If we're doing this, we're doing it fast, and we're doing it in order of freshest graves, and then we are *gone*, and when Corvane comes—and he will come, Rosalind, he is already coming—you hide behind me and you let me do the talking, because I am very good at talking to Inquisitors and you are catastrophically bad at it."

"Sera Vane," I said, "that is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It wasn't romantic. It was triage." But her hand found mine in the crowd, and it stayed.

We worked until the light went long and gold. Name by name, stitch by stitch, I gave Lowmere back its dead: Marta and Old Tam and the twins, Joss and Jem, who'd drowned together in the reservoir and been buried in one grave so neither would be lonely. The baker's daughter Brenna, who by dusk had a grave covered in wildflowers that had no business blooming in that season, in that soil, in that country—and I noticed, and I filed it away next to the shrine on the pass, next to every impossible blossom since, because the old gods were waking under our feet and the land itself was starting to remember ahead of schedule.

The woman with the flour on her arm was the last. When it was done she pressed the blue bird into my hand and wouldn't take it back. "Brenna made two," she said. "One for her, one for whoever brought her home." And then, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise: "They'll come for you for this. The grey men. I've seen them before, years ago, taking things. Run fast, pencil-girl."

"Always do," I said.

We ran. Out the churchyard gate, through the square past the apple cart—the stall-woman was standing very still, staring at her own chalked sentence, and as we passed she read it aloud, once, wonderingly, like a spell breaking—and up the east road into the trees with the last light dying behind us.

It was full dark when Sera stopped so abruptly I walked into her back.

Ahead, down the valley, spread across the whole horizon like a city deciding to exist, lights. Dozens of them. Lanterns in a column miles long, winding along the imperial road toward Lowmere, orderly as a rosary.

"Not a patrol," Sera said. Her voice had gone very calm, which I'd learned was her most frightened register. "Patrols don't travel in processions. That's a *convocation*. That's the full Ministry, Rosalind. That's him."

The lanterns began, all together, to move faster.

"He felt the flowers," I said.

"He felt the *names*." Sera turned, and in the dark her face was pale and set and, beneath everything, lit with something I was almost afraid to recognize. "Every prayer in those mountains is a signal fire, and you just lit sixty of them at once. So we have until dawn, maybe. Which means the Garden door had better be where the mapmaker said, and you—" she took my hand, hard, certain, "—had better be ready to stop hiding."

Above the treeline, far off toward the drowned ruins of Velthmere, something answered her. A light rose over the reservoir—not lantern-light, not moonlight, but a slow green-gold bloom like spring seen from underground—and every dog in the valley began to sing.
