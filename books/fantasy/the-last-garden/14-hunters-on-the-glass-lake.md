## 14. Hunters on the Glass Lake

There are three things every Inquisitor learns in their first year at the Ministry of Continuity, and I learned all three before my voice finished changing.

One: a memory, once eaten, does not vanish. It goes somewhere. Nobody in the Ministry liked to say where, which should have told us everything.

Two: the fastest way to catch a fugitive is not to chase them. It's to think like them until your thoughts fit inside theirs like a hand in a glove, and then close your fingers.

Three: never cross frozen water with a keystone. Ice remembers being water, and keystones remind it.

I had been out of the Ministry for exactly nine days, and I had already violated the third rule, was in the process of violating the second, and had burned the paperwork on the first so thoroughly that even the ash had ash. Which is to say: we were halfway across Lake Verren with Corvane's elite closing behind us, and I had no one to blame but the girl who'd written my training manuals.

The lake was the color of a held breath. Not white — glass. The kind of ice that forms when the cold comes down hard and fast and honest, freezing a lake mid-ripple so that the waves are still there under your feet, suspended, like the mountain took a photograph of the water and forgot to let it move again. Rosalind walked ahead of me, arms wrapped around themselves, breath tearing out in little flags, and every few strides they glanced down through the clear black ice at the drowned world beneath — weeds standing straight as candlewicks, a sunken rowboat, pale stones that might once have been someone's offering.

"You're staring," I said.

"I'm *listening*," Rosalind said, which was the answer of someone who had spent two weeks learning to hear memories the way you'd hear music through a wall. Since chapter — since the print shop, since they'd stopped running and started *remembering*, deliberately, on purpose, like a person striking matches one after another to see how much dark they could hold off — they'd been doing this. Pausing. Pressing a palm flat to things. Doors, stones, the rail of a ferry, now this. "The ice is loud."

"Good loud or bad loud?"

"Sera." They turned and looked at me, wind-whipped, chapped-lipped, absolutely certain. "It's all loud now. That's rather the point of me."

I wanted to kiss them. I also wanted to throw them over my shoulder and run back down the pass and put them on a boat to somewhere the Ministry's writ didn't run, and both desires were, I note for the record, equally impractical. So instead I said, "Keep moving," in the tone of a woman who has decided that feelings are a problem for people with dry socks.

That was when I heard the bells.

* * *

Ministry elite don't wear armor. Armor says *I expect to be hit*, and the Ministry preferred its hunters to be the kind of thing you don't expect at all. They wore grey traveling wool, unremarkable boots, faces like clerks. The only ornament any of them carried was a small silver bell on a chain, worn under the collar, and it made no sound to anyone but another Inquisitor — a resonance in the teeth, a taste like coins, a certainty arriving at the base of the skull that said: *one of ours is near.*

Six bells, coming up the eastern shore. Moving fast. Spreading into a crescent, because Corvane taught his elites to hunt the way falcons hunt, wide and patient and converging, and because Corvane, above all people alive, knew that I would recognize the shape of it.

"He sent Veyl," Rosalind said. They'd gone still on the ice, and their face had done the thing it did lately — that flicker of borrowed knowledge crossing over their own expression like weather. "Your mentor. He's — I can feel him remembering. Loudly. On purpose. He wants me to feel it."

Master Halric Veyl. Twenty-two years in the field. The man who'd taught me to walk a room and read what had happened in it from the dust. The man who'd held my hair back the night after my first Unremembering, when I was nineteen and had just helped hollow out a smuggler who reminded me, horribly, of my brother — except I couldn't have told you why he reminded me of anyone, because I'd eaten the reason myself an hour earlier. Veyl had sat with me on the steps of the Chapterhouse and said, *The sickness passes, Sera. What stays is the work.* And I'd believed him for eleven years.

"How many?" Rosalind asked.

"Six visible. Probably two holding the shore we came from." I was doing the arithmetic automatically, the way you count exits in a room you've just entered; eleven years of habit doesn't burn off in nine days, it just changes employers. "They'll want us in the middle of the lake. Open ground, no cover, and—" I looked down through the glass at the still, waiting water. "—and they'll want the keystone frightened. Fear makes memories loose. Easy to pull."

"So don't be frightened," Rosalind said.

"Wonderful. I'll get right on that."

But then Rosalind did something I will think about for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be. They knelt. Right there, on the middle of a frozen lake, with six Ministry hunters fanning out around us like a closing hand, they knelt and pressed both palms flat against the ice, and closed their eyes, and *listened*.

And the ice listened back.

I felt it through my boots first — a shiver that wasn't cold, a deep slow pulse like something enormous turning over in sleep. The light changed. It came up through the ice instead of down onto it, green-gold, the color of a summer afternoon reflected upward, and for one absurd moment I was standing on a lake in June, feeling the warmth of shallows around my ankles, smelling reeds and mud and the particular sweetness of water weed, hearing dragonflies stitch the air — a memory of this lake in some year before the Empire redrew its maps, rising through the frozen present like heat through floorboards.

"What did you do?" I whispered.

"Reminded it," Rosalind said. Their eyes opened and there was green in them that hadn't been there a month ago. "It's still summer underneath, Sera. It never stopped being summer. It just agreed to pretend." They stood, and the wind gusted across the lake and bent around them instead of through them, and they smiled at me with a wildness I found — God help me, in the teeth of everything — deeply attractive. "Now. Your mentors. Do we talk our way past them or do I do something dramatic?"

The bells rang closer. Six of them, and beneath the bells, faint and deliberate as a knock, the particular pressure of Master Veyl *thinking at me*. *Sera. Come home. Bring it in.*

"No talking," I said, and drew my knife.

* * *

Here is a thing I have never admitted in any report, confession, or quiet hour: dueling is the only prayer I know.

I was raised by the Ministry from the age of eight, which means I was raised by procedure, and procedure doesn't leave room for kneeling. But a duel — a real one, blade to blade, the whole world narrowed to distance and timing and the other person's eyes — that's a form of attention so complete it borders on worship. For the length of a fight you believe in exactly one thing, absolutely, and it is the person in front of you. Everything else falls away. It's the closest I ever came to grace, before.

So when Veyl came out onto the ice — walking easily, unhurried, his bell silent against his chest, his knife already drawn, his face wearing the mild disappointed expression of a tutor whose best student has failed her examinations — some traitorous part of me settled. Some part of me thought: *finally, something simple.*

"Sera." His voice carried flat across the glass. Behind him, the other five spread wide, flanking toward Rosalind, and I moved left to cut the angle without deciding to. "You're ill. We've all seen it. Corvane is prepared to be generous — rest, treatment, a clean file. You were compromised during the Dunmore assignment. None of this will follow you."

"That's very kind," I called back. "Did you rehearse it on the walk?"

"Every word." And there — a flicker at the corner of his mouth, the old dry humor, the ghost of a hundred shared watches. He'd always been funny. That was the trap of Veyl; everyone forgot, because of the ledger and the bell and the hollowed villages, that he was genuinely, warmly funny, and by the time you remembered, you were already laughing your way into custody. "Sera. Look at them." He nodded past me, at Rosalind, standing on summer-colored ice with their chin up. "Do you know what that is? Not a person. A *file*. Two hundred years of statecraft folded into one walking error. You're not betraying a person. You're losing an argument with an archive."

"You're right," I said, and meant it, and watched his eyebrows rise. "They're not only a person. They're a whole country's worth of person. That's rather the point of me too."

He sighed, and the humor went out of him the way light goes out of a window, and I understood that the conversation was over, and that it had been over before it began, and that he had come out onto the ice alone for one reason only: to give the other five a clear line at Rosalind while I was busy loving him.

"Last chance, girl."

"I stopped taking last chances from the Ministry when I read my own file," I said. "Page forty. My handwriting, dated four years ago. *Refused. Recommend reassignment.* And then the order underneath it, in yours: *Subject to forget refusal. Reassign anyway.*" I let that sit between us on the bright air. "You ate my no, Master Veyl. This is what grew in the hole."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt; I'd have known guilt; I'd worn it myself long enough — but recalculation. The falcon adjusting to prey that had read the sky.

"Take them," he said quietly, to his people, and came at me.

* * *

Fighting on Rosalind's ice was like fighting inside a held note.

Every step landed true. The surface gave nothing, slid nothing, held each boot-plant as if the lake itself had opinions about footwork. I felt the memory of summer humming up through my bones, warm and stubborn, and Veyl felt it too — I saw it in the half-second hitch in his rhythm, the hunter's instinct recoiling from ground that refused to be anonymous. Ministry training teaches you to treat terrain as neutral. Rosalind's presence made terrain *loyal*, and there is no drill for that.

We fought the old pattern, the one he'd drilled into me across a thousand practice yards: press high, punish low, trade the lead twice, finish on the bind. I knew every beat of it. He knew I knew. Somewhere in the middle exchanges we were less fighting than conversing, blades talking over each other in a language only the two of us had ever spoken fluently, and I want to be honest about this because honesty is the only currency I have left: I was glad. In the middle of treason, on a haunted lake, with five grey shapes converging on the person I — fine. The person I loved. In the middle of all that, some starved corner of me was simply happy to spar with my teacher one more time, clean and true, before everything.

Then I saw, over his shoulder, one of the five break from the crescent and drift toward Rosalind with a drawn bell-chain, not a knife — and I understood the whole design of the morning. I was never the target. I was the *distraction*. The fight was the leash. Every second I traded with Veyl was a second the others spent closing on a keystone standing alone on the ice with their palms full of summer.

Veyl saw me see it. And he did the cruelest thing he could have done: he slowed. Deliberately, expertly, a half-beat of mercy dressed as fatigue, giving me the choice he knew I couldn't make fast enough — finish the exchange and abandon Rosalind, or break the pattern and take his blade in some unguarded place.

I broke the pattern.

I won't dress it up. There was no clever flourish, no duel-winner's geometry. I stepped *inside* his next cut, into the range where knives stop being elegant, and I put mine through the soft place under his ribs, angled up, the way he taught me to kill a man quickly when the mission allowed kindness.

He made a sound I had heard him make once before — laughing, years ago, at some joke in a border tavern, a short surprised bark — and then his knees went, and I caught him, because of course I caught him, and lowered him onto the ice of a lake that was, underneath, still having its summer.

"Sera," he said. Blood at his teeth. His hand found my wrist with a grip that had trained half the Ministry's field service. "The file. Page forty." A wet breath. "I wrote that order. But the refusal—" His eyes were going distant, the way eyes do. "I kept it. Off-ledger. Couldn't—"

He died before he could tell me why, which I suspect was not an accident of timing but the last professional decision of a very thorough man. Even dying, he rationed information like a quartermaster. Or — darker thought, which I carry now whether I want it or not — even dying, he was protecting something. Maybe me. Maybe the version of himself that once wrote *refusal* in a margin and couldn't quite bring himself to eat it.

Around us the other five had stopped. Bells silent. Knives lowered, uncertain, because their doctrine had no page for *mentor down, keystone glowing, agent compromised beyond recovery*. Doctrine rarely does. That's its whole weakness, if you're asking — and nobody was asking, so I stood up over my dead teacher, wiped my knife on my coat the way he'd taught me, and looked at them one by one, memorizing their faces the way he'd taught me, filing them the way he'd taught me, everything I am built out of his lessons, and I said:

"The next one of you who takes a step toward them joins him. And I promise you" — and here Rosalind chose that exact moment to lift their hands from the ice, and the whole lake *shone*, green-gold light pouring up through the glass like dawn happening in the wrong direction, and every one of those hardened Ministry elites took an involuntary step backward onto ground that suddenly, unmistakably, *remembered being alive* — "you'll remember it forever. That's rather our whole business now."

They withdrew. All five, backing off the lake in good order, because they were professionals, and professionals know when a story has stopped being theirs.

I knelt back down beside Veyl. I closed his eyes. I took his bell — he'd have hated leaving it for strangers — and I put it in my pocket, where it lay against my ribs making no sound at all, which somehow was the loudest thing on the lake.

* * *

Afterward I was sick. Twice, violently, into a snowdrift at the lake's edge while Rosalind held my hair back with hands that smelled of herbs and cold, and I thought, distantly, through the heaving: *this is the same, this is exactly the same, the steps of the Chapterhouse, nineteen years old, Veyl's hand in my hair* — and the symmetry of it broke something open in me that I suspect needed breaking.

"It passes," Rosalind said softly, when I could speak. Echoing him. Choosing to echo him, but meaning the opposite.

"No," I said. I wiped my mouth. My voice came out scraped and steady, and I found I meant every word. "It doesn't. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, Rosalind. He told me the sickness passes and what stays is the work, and I built eleven years on that sentence, and it's backwards. The work passes. The sick stays. And the only thing to do about it is—" I looked back at the lake, at the small dark shape on the ice that had been my teacher, my maker, my keeper. "—is decide what you're willing to be sick over."

Rosalind crouched beside me in the trampled snow. Wind off the lake. Somewhere behind us, the bells were retreating east, back toward a capital that no longer had any claim on me that I recognized.

"For what it's worth," they said, "he was trying to tell you something. At the end. About your refusal. I could feel it — there's a memory of his that isn't in the Ministry's keeping. It's *hidden*, Sera. Buried deep, off-ledger, warded like a grave. Someone hid it inside him so thoroughly that even he had to die reaching for it." Their eyes were very green. "And it's about the Garden. I'm nearly sure of it. It's about the door."

I stared at them. The dead man on the ice. The bell in my pocket, silent as a held tongue.

Of course. Of course the thorough old bastard had hidden his one act of conscience where the Ministry could never audit it — inside himself, in the one vault they never searched, because they believed their own people were empty. He'd carried my no for four years, buried past his own knowing, and he'd come out onto the ice to try to hand it back to me, and I'd killed him mid-sentence.

"Well," I said, when I could. "Then we know what he was protecting."

"We do?"

"The location of his grave," I said, standing, pulling Rosalind up with me, turning us west toward the high passes and away from everything I had ever been. "Because I intend to dig it up. Whatever he buried in himself — his body's in the Ministry's records, but his *memory* isn't, and if there's a map to the Garden hidden in a dead Inquisitor's skull, then somebody owes me a resurrection." I shouldered my pack. My hands had stopped shaking. I noticed it the way you notice weather clearing. "Come on. We're going to need a god who does thresholds and a very patient corpse."

Rosalind looked at me — blood-flecked, wrecked, lighter than I had ever been — and started to laugh, helplessly, appalled, the laugh of a person falling in love with something terrible, and together we walked off the lake that remembered summer, leaving my old life lying face-up on the ice behind us.

I did not look back.

Well. Once.
