## 15. What Rosalind Was Before

The memories didn't come back all at once. I want to say that up front, because the songs—if there are ever songs about this, and knowing Velthmere there will be, probably with a lot of harp in them—will tell you otherwise. They'll say the keystone child opened her eyes and remembered everything in a single shining moment. That's a lie of convenience. The truth is that remembering is like bleeding: it happens in pulses, and you can't decide when to stop.

We were camped in the high hollow above the drowned ruins, three days after the lake, two days after Sera stopped being sick every time she looked at her own hands. She'd built the fire small and shielded, the way she'd taught me—*a memory kept between two people burns slower*, she said, and then she'd held my hand and we'd thought about nothing at all for an hour, which was either the most practical thing we'd ever done or the least, depending on how honest you wanted to be about it.

I was on watch. Sera was asleep with her ledger under her head like a pillow, which was its own kind of joke—the Ministry's book of names to be unmade, serving as bedding for the woman who'd burned hers out of it.

And then the crest came.

It started as warmth behind my eyes, the way the recovered memories always did since the village where I'd given the grieving woman her daughter's name. Every name I gave away seemed to loosen something else, like pulling one thread from a knot. But this time the thread didn't come alone. It came with weight. It came with *weather*.

I was standing in a garden.

Not the smuggler's-hollow kind of garden, not Maren's ragged herb beds with their ambitious weeds. A garden the size of a valley, hedges grown into architecture, roses the color of old blood and new dawn, and everywhere the smell of turned earth and bees and something sweeter underneath that I had no word for yet but would later learn was the smell of a country dreaming itself.

And a woman's voice, saying: *She has to be consecrated before the fall reaches us. You understand that. There's no other vessel.*

A man's voice answering, low and wrecked: *There's always another way.*

*Name it,* the woman said, not cruelly, which was worse. *Name one other way, Aldric, and I will take it. Name one way we keep our daughter and our country both.*

Silence. The particular silence of parents who have already decided and are only waiting for someone to make them say it.

I came back to myself sitting down hard in the frost, fire spatting, Sera already up with a knife she'd apparently sleep-held, because of course she did.

"Rosalind." My name in her mouth, careful. "You went somewhere."

"I went home," I said, before I could stop myself, and then laughed, because it wasn't true, and it was more true than anything I'd ever said.

* * *

Here is what I'd known, going into that night: I was a keystone. An archive. A child loaded, ritual by ritual, with the last intact memory of Velthmere so that when the empire finished eating the country, someone would still be carrying it, somewhere the Inquisitors couldn't find, in a body too well-hidden even to know what it hid. Maren had told me that much. The threshold god had confirmed it. I had made my peace with being a walking library, more or less. Libraries are useful. Libraries don't have to feel things about their own shelves.

Here is what I learned at the crest:

My name wasn't Rosalind Dunmore.

It was Rosalind *Velthmere*. Not named for the country. Named *by* it. The last consecration at the Last Garden happened the night before the empire's memory-eaters crossed the border, and the rite required a child born under the Garden's hedge-line, given up whole—not fostered, not sent away, *given*, selfhood and all—to hold what no adult mind could carry without shattering. And my parents hadn't been forced. That was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed river stone. There were protocols, there were priests, there was a god of thresholds standing witness with its borrowed face and its starving patience—and at every step, some official would have accepted refusal. My mother was offered the choice eleven times. I counted. The memory counted for me. Eleven times she said yes. Eleven times my father stood beside her and let her.

They chose a nation over a daughter. And the nation lost anyway, and the daughter got scattered across six years of running, and everyone involved called it survival.

I told Sera all of this at dawn, in the flat voice I use for inventory lists, because if I used any other voice I was going to do something dramatic with the fire iron and she'd have to tackle me, and neither of us had slept.

"So they're alive," she said. She'd learned, in these weeks together, to find the question under my monologue. "Your parents. The memories would know."

"I don't know." I pulled my knees in. "The archive ends at the consecration. Everything after that was cut loose—that was the design. I'm carrying Velthmere's memory, not mine. My own childhood, the real one, the one with their faces in it—" I gestured at the fog. "Eaten. Or hidden. Or maybe it never existed, maybe they handed me to the priests at birth and everything I remember before Maren is scaffolding somebody built."

"You remember a mother," Sera said quietly. "Before Maren. You've mentioned her. Bread, and singing off-key."

"I remember *a* mother. Whether she's mine or a patch job—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "Do you understand what the worst part is? It's not that they gave me up. People give children up. Bad harvests, bad years, bad luck. It's that they were *right*. If they hadn't done it, Velthmere wouldn't just be conquered, it would be *unhappened*. Every god dead forever, every grave empty of names, every—everything I've been giving back, village by village, gone. They traded me for a country and the trade worked. What am I supposed to do with that? Rage requires somewhere to put it, and every direction I aim it, I hit the truth."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You could aim it at the ones who made the choice necessary."

"The empire?"

"The rite," she said. "The priests who designed a magic that needed a child. The god who witnessed it. Corvane, who's spent thirty years making sure no one could ever undo it except through you. There's plenty of blame in this story, Rosalind. Your parents are the only ones in it who paid the full price up front."

"That's very generous of you, considering your Ministry ate the country they were protecting me for."

"Yes," she said, without flinching, which was one of the things I—well. Which was one of the things. "It is. I'm learning generosity late and secondhand. Sue me."

I almost smiled. Almost. The frost was coming off the grass in gray curls and somewhere below us the reservoir held a drowned city full of starving gods, and I was so tired of being an object in other people's ceremonies.

* * *

The second pulse came at midday, while we walked.

That was the pattern now—the memories cresting in waves, each one bigger, the way the sea tests a seawall before it commits. This one took me mid-stride, and Sera caught my elbow without breaking pace, steered me off the trail, and stood watch over my body while I stood watch over a garden gate.

Because that's what it was. Another memory of the Garden—but this time from inside the rite itself. From the child's side.

I was small. Six, maybe seven, in the way you're small when you haven't yet learned that adults lie. Someone had braided my hair with rowan. There were candles that burned without heat. And the god of thresholds—it wore my own face then, I realized, it had always worn the faces of whoever stood nearest, and I had been the nearest thing to it my whole life without knowing why—crouched down to my eye level and asked me, in a voice like a door opening:

*Do you consent?*

And six-year-old me, wanting desperately to be brave, wanting the nice candle people to be proud of me, said yes. Said it clearly, loudly, the way you say a lesson you've rehearsed.

And the god—this is the part I will carry until I die, whatever else gets planted or poured or eaten—the god paused. Looked at my parents. Looked at the priests. And said, softly, so only the child heard:

*I will ask again someday. When you are old enough to mean it. Remember that I asked.*

Then it took everything. The name first. The future next. The little ordinary selfhood, the girl who would have grown up crooked and specific and loved, folded up and stored beneath the keystone memory like a letter under a floorboard.

The rite needed a vessel. It didn't need to be *empty*. But empty vessels keep better.

I surfaced gasping, and Sera was kneeling in front of me, and I grabbed her collar because my hands needed to hold something that wasn't rage.

"It asked," I said. "Sera, it *asked*. Consent. It knew—I was six, I couldn't have meant it, and it *knew*, and it promised to ask again. That's why the prayers work, that's why the gods answer me specifically, that's why the shrine said my name on the mountain. It's not gratitude. It's a *debt*. Two hundred years, and the god's been waiting to hear whether I still say yes."

"Will you?" she asked. No judgment in it. Just the question, laid flat between us like a card face-up.

"I don't know!" It came out louder than I meant. A crow went up off the scree. "That's the point, don't you see? Everyone keeps deciding for me. My parents. The priests. Maren, who found me and raised me and never once told me what I was, and I can't even blame her because telling me would have lit me up like a beacon and gotten us both unmade—everyone in my life has treated me as a thing to be protected or a thing to be used, and the one creature that bothered to ask permission was a starving god wearing my own face, and I said yes to *it* before I could talk properly!"

"And now?"

"Now—" I let go of her collar. Smoothed it, absurdly, because my hands wanted a task. "Now I'm old enough to mean it. That's what it said. Old enough to mean it."

* * *

We made camp early, in a fold of the hills where the smuggler maps showed nothing at all, which lately meant the old country was thickest. Sera built the fire and I sat with my palms open on my knees and let the third wave come on purpose, which felt like leaning into a wave instead of being knocked down by it, and was only marginally less drowning.

This one was the parents. Properly, finally, the parents.

My mother's name was Ilsette. She sang off-key—so the bread-and-singing memory *was* real, or at least a splinter of the real, salvaged from before the consecration, and I sat with that fact and shook. My father was Aldric, a hedge-cartographer who drew maps of places that didn't exist yet, because in Velthmere cartography was half divination, and he'd mapped the Garden's moving door seventeen times and never once been able to find it again on purpose. He laughed with his whole chest. He cried exactly once in front of me, the night before the rite, and pretended it was smoke in his eyes, and I had believed him, because I was six and because love makes you gullible.

They visited. After. That was in the memory too—they were allowed visits, supervised, the child-vessel kept in the Garden's outer precincts under the priests' care, and once a season they'd walk up the hedge-road and I'd run to them and they'd hold me and never explain, because explaining was forbidden, and I'd go back to my candle-keepers wondering why my mother's hugs lasted exactly as long as the supervising priest's patience and not one breath longer.

The last visit was the worst. The empire's eaters were weeks from the walls. My mother held my face in her hands and said, *Whatever happens, whatever you become, you were ours first. Before the country. Before the god. Ours.* And my father said nothing at all, just pressed his forehead to mine, and I felt his tears on my skin and this time nobody blamed the smoke.

Then the eating came, and the Garden closed, and the memory cuts off there—my memory of them ends at a hedge-gate swinging shut, because everything after was stored, not lived.

I opened my eyes. Sera was watching me with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether to touch me, which she was getting braver about, but never presumptuous.

"They loved me," I said. My voice cracked straight down the middle. "That's the filthy trick of it. I wanted monsters. Monsters would be easier. But they loved me and they did it anyway and they were *right* and I hate that I can't hate them, I hate that the angriest thing I can honestly say is that they should have fought harder for a way to save both, and they'd say—Ilsette would say—we did fight, love, we fought for eleven rounds and lost every one—"

"Rosalind."

"—and the god asked, it *asked*, and I said yes, and now the whole rotten beautiful machine of my life turns on a six-year-old's yes, and I am so *tired* of being the answer to other people's questions—"

"Rosalind." She took my face in her hands, the way Ilsette had, and I flinched and then didn't. "Listen to me. I spent nine years in the Ministry. I know what it is to have your selfhood written by other people—orders, conditioning, files. And the only way out I ever found was the one you already know." Her thumbs moved once across my cheekbones. "You stop being what they made. You start being what you choose. The memories aren't you. They're cargo. You're the one carrying."

"Cargo," I repeated. "That's very romantic."

"I'm an ex-Inquisitor. Romance is a foreign language and I'm learning it badly." But she didn't let go. "Say it, though. Say what you choose. Out loud. The god's waiting to hear it eventually—might as well practice."

So I sat there in the firelight with Velthmere's whole dead country stacked behind my eyes and my mother's tears still phantom-warm on my face, and I said:

"I choose to plant it. All of it. Not keep it, not hoard it, not be the last library of a murdered nation—I choose to give it back, to the land, to the gods, to every mourner at every unnamed grave. And then—" my voice wobbled and I let it, "—and then whatever's left of me, the part that was never anyone's vessel or archive or keystone, that part gets to exist. For itself. Even if it's small. Even if it's new. Even if it doesn't have a name yet."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat through stone, I felt the threshold god turn toward me in its drowned dark, attentive as a held breath.

And then the fourth wave hit—and this one wasn't memory. This one was *present tense*.

It came up through the ground like a shout through a floor: prayer, dozens of prayers, hundreds, igniting all across the mountains at once, the signal-fires of every name I'd returned and every seed the gods had managed to sprout—and threaded through all of it, cold and deliberate as a fishhook, a presence I recognized from Sera's nightmares.

Corvane. Feeling me remember. Feeling the beacon I'd become. And not coming to kill it.

Coming to *harvest*.

Sera was on her feet, knife drawn, reading my face the way she read ledgers. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"He knows," I said. "He's known since the lake. And Sera—he's not waiting anymore. The Unremembering's moving. Tonight."

Above the tree line, far to the south, the stars began to go out—one constellation, then another—as if something vast and patient were eating the sky on its way toward us.
