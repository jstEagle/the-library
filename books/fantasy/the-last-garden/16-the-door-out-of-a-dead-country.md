## 16. The Door Out of a Dead Country

*Rosalind*

The threshold god led us through the mountains the way a child leads you to a secret: badly, joyfully, and with far too many detours.

It had taken to wearing my mother's face again. I'd asked it not to. It had agreed, and then done it anyway, because gods are like cats that way—they agree with you in the same tone you'd agree with weather.

"Left here," it said, in Maren's voice this time, which was worse. "No—sorry—right. The door moved."

"The door *moved*?"

"Gardens drift," Sera said behind me, not even winded, which was frankly offensive given the grade we'd been climbing since dawn. "Everything the Ministry couldn't pin down learned to drift. It's basic survival."

"Thank you for that fascinating lecture on metaphysical cowardice," I said. "Very helpful while my legs are filing a formal complaint."

Sera smiled. Six days ago she'd killed a man on a frozen lake and been sick afterward, and she'd let me hold her hair back at the edge of the ice like we were two farmwives after bad brandy, and something between us had gone quiet and settled, the way a house settles when the storm finally passes. Not calm. Just—load-bearing. She walked half a step behind me now instead of three steps behind me, and if you don't think that's a distance worth measuring, you have never spent six years being no one.

"Here," the god said suddenly, stopping so fast I nearly went over its borrowed shoulder.

There was nothing there. A shelf of granite, some wind-bent pines, snow going gray at the edges. And then—

I don't know how else to say it. The nothing had an edge to it. Like a page turned slightly wrong, like a word you can feel is misspelled before you find the letter. My eyes kept trying to slide off the space between two boulders, and every time they slid, something under my ribs tugged the other way.

"It only shows itself to someone carrying Velthmere," the god said. Its voice had gone strange—layered, like several people saying one sentence out of phase. "You see it because you *are* it. Or it is you. The grammar of keystones is terrible; I apologize."

"How do I open it?" I asked.

"You don't." The god turned to look at me with my mother's eyes, and I hated it, and I understood why it did it—it wanted me to listen the way you only listen to family—and it said, gently, "*It* opens. You knock by remembering. But Rosalind. Before you knock. There is something you should see first, and I would rather you saw it standing on this side than kneeling on that one."

"That's not ominous at all," Sera muttered.

The god raised one hand—my grandmother's hand, work-rough, flour in the knuckle creases—and touched the wrongness between the rocks.

* * *

*Sera*

I have stood in rooms where memories were eaten. I have watched the Ministry of Continuity unmake a man's wedding day while he sat across the desk from them, smiling pleasantly at nothing, his whole face smooth as poured wax. I thought I knew what a memory looked like from the outside.

I was wrong. What the Ministry does isn't showing a memory. It's showing the hole where one was.

What opened between those boulders was the other thing entirely.

A garden gate. Iron, green-painted, peeling. Beyond it, hedges, and beyond the hedges—a country. Velthmere, whole. Terraced orchards going gold down a valley. A river with boats on it. Smoke from chimneys rising straight up in still air. People. Hundreds of people, moving through their ordinary lives, and every single one of them sharp-edged and present in a way that made my chest hurt, because I had spent my entire career learning what absence looked like and had never once seen presence at this volume.

And then the gate shuddered, and everything burned.

Not metaphorically. The orchards caught light like paper. The smoke bent sideways all at once, every chimney in the country, as though a vast hand had inhaled. The people stopped mid-gesture—a woman lifting bread from an oven, a boy running with a dog beside him—and began, very slowly, to come apart. Not into blood. Into blankness. Their edges softened first, like wet ink. Then their faces. The woman set down her bread and looked directly out of the vision at us, and her mouth formed a word, and the word was a name, and I could not hear it, and I understood that no one ever would again unless—

The scene ended. Reset. The gate, the gold orchards, the bread, the burning.

Then it happened again.

"Oh gods," Rosalind whispered beside me. "It's *looping*. It's the same moment, over and over—"

"The moment of the fall," the threshold god said. "The Garden's door opens onto it because the Garden was built in it. Every garden remembers the frost. This one simply never got permission to stop."

"How long has it been replaying?" I asked. My voice came out flat, Inquisitor-flat, the voice I used for autopsy reports. It was the only voice I had left.

"Two hundred years," the god said. "Give or take the years the empire ate."

Rosalind stepped forward. I caught their sleeve without deciding to. They looked down at my hand on their arm, then at me, and there was something in their face I'd only seen once before—at the fireline, the night they almost walked away from me for good. Grief with its sleeves rolled up. Ready to work.

"Someone has to go through carrying all of it," Rosalind said. Not asking. Telling themselves.

"Through the door of a dead country, into the moment it died, holding the complete memory of everything that was lost." The god said it plainly, kindly, the way you tell someone the price of bread. "Yes. And the carrier does not come through unchanged. Memory that size doesn't sit quietly in a person. It will remake them around itself. Whoever carries it will be—" it paused, searching among my stolen childhoods for a word big enough, "—*redistributed*."

"That's not a word," I said.

"It is where I'm from."

* * *

*Rosalind*

We made camp in the lee of the boulders, close enough to the door that its wrongness hummed against my teeth like a struck tuning fork. Sera built the fire small, out of habit—smoke signals were how Corvane had found us twice now—but neither of us suggested moving farther away. You don't walk away from a thing like that. You just pretend you're staying put for tactical reasons.

The fall played on behind us. I could hear it, faintly: the roar of the burn, then silence, then the small domestic sounds of the beginning again—the creak of the oven door, the dog's bark, a church bell I didn't recognize and recognized instantly, the way you know your own heartbeat in someone else's chest.

Velthmere's bell. I had never heard Velthmere's bell. Except I had, hadn't I, in the place Maren had tried to burn out of me, in the sealed room behind my eyes where a nation lived folded up small.

"Ask it," Sera said.

"I was getting there."

"No, you weren't. You were doing the thing where you stare at the fire and compose a noble speech about sacrifice." She fed a stick to the flames. "I've read your file, Rosalind. Well. Half of it. You always rehearse before you're brave."

"That's slander."

"It's in the margin notes. 'Subject exhibits pre-narrative behavior.'"

"Corvane wrote margin notes about my *narrative behavior*?"

"He writes margin notes about everyone. Mine say I over-season my food." She said it lightly, but her hand wasn't light on the stick, and the firelight showed me the bruise-dark circles under her eyes, and I understood she was doing the same thing I was: rehearsing.

So I skipped ahead past both our rehearsals.

"If I go through," I said, "will I still be me?"

The god sat down across the fire. It had finally, mercifully, stopped wearing my family, and settled into something neutral—a shape like a doorway wearing a coat, all edges and hinges. Somehow it was more unsettling than my mother's face. At least my mother's face meant something.

"I don't know," it said.

"You're a god."

"I'm a very small god who has been starving in a wall for two centuries. My domain is thresholds, not outcomes. I know what doors do. I don't know what's on the other side of this one, because no one has ever walked through it carrying what you carry." It leaned forward, or gave the impression of leaning, the way fog leans. "But I will tell you what I believe, and you may weigh it as belief. I believe the keystone was built to survive this. I believe whoever consecrated you intended, from the beginning, for a carrier to reach this gate eventually. They would not design a seed vault that destroyed the seed."

"And the gardener?"

"The gardener comes through changed. Changed is not erased." It tilted its hinge-edge head toward the looping vision, where the woman with the bread was setting it down for the ten-thousandth time. "She was erased. You would be *translated*. There is a difference, and it is the entire difference, and it is also, I grant you, cold comfort when the translation might leave very little of the original text legible."

I laughed. It surprised all three of us.

"What?" Sera demanded.

"Nothing. It's just—I ran away from Maren's cottage six years ago because she tried to burn one page out of me, and I've spent every day since being proud of how thoroughly I stayed myself. Nobody knows me. Nothing touches me. I'm a ghost with excellent smuggling contacts." I turned the stick in the fire. "And now the choice is to stay a ghost forever or walk through that door and possibly come out as a footnote."

"Not a footnote," Sera said.

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't." She shifted closer, until our shoulders touched, and I let the contact happen because it was armor, actual armor—we'd learned that on the road, shielding each other's memories inside shared moments, and old habits die hard. "But I know what erasure looks like from the professional side. And whatever happens through that door, it isn't that. That woman in there"—she nodded at the loop—"she gets to stop. Whatever you become, you get to keep becoming. Those aren't the same fate. I've spent my life confusing them, and I won't sit here and watch you confuse them too."

"When did you get wise?"

"About four days after I committed treason. It comes on suddenly. Like measles."

* * *

*Sera*

I woke in the grey hour before dawn and Rosalind was gone from the bedroll.

My heart did the thing it had done at the glass lake, at the reservoir, at every checkpoint since I burned my orders—the specific cardiac event of loving a person the Ministry has designated as cargo. I was up with a knife in my hand before I'd finished waking, and then I heard voices, low, at the door.

They were standing in front of the gate. Not touching it. The loop played behind the iron—burn, reset, burn—and Rosalind watched it with the stillness I'd learned to read during our weeks together. Stillness like that wasn't peace. It was a person reading the last page of a book to decide whether to finish it.

"—and if I don't come back as anything recognizable," Rosalind was saying to the god, "you'll make sure she knows it wasn't her fault. She'll decide it was anyway. But say it."

"Rosalind." My voice cracked on the second syllable, undignified as ever.

They turned. In the light off the looping fires, their face was calm in a way that terrified me more than any panic could have, because I knew that calm. I wore it to work for eleven years. It's the face you build when you've already signed the paperwork.

"You were going to leave a note," I said.

"I was going to leave a really good note."

"You were going to leave a *terrible* note. Your handwriting slants uphill when you lie." I crossed the frozen grass and took their hands, and they let me, and their fingers were ice. "You don't get to do this alone. That was our whole—everything we figured out at the fireline, everything since the lake. Alone is how the Ministry wins. Alone is the *design*, Rosalind. Consecrated as a baby, hidden for twenty years, guided here by ghosts and starving gods, and oh, what a coincidence, the keystone arrives at the door with no one standing beside it. Corvane couldn't have planned it better."

The flinch told me I'd landed it.

"So what's your proposal?" Rosalind asked quietly. "We both walk through? We both get redistributed? You'd give up—" they gestured at the world generally, at the mountains, at the sky going pale, "—all of this, on a maybe?"

"I'd give up all of this on a maybe, yes."

"That's insane."

"Says the person about to knock on the door of a dead country."

That got a laugh out of them, wet and unwilling, and I held onto their hands through it because I am, whatever my margin notes say, a professional.

"Here's what I actually propose," I said. "We knock together. We find out exactly what the crossing demands—not what the god guesses, not what the dead gardeners intended. What the door says. And then we decide. Both of us. With full information. That's the deal. That's the only deal I'm offering, and I'm the one with the knife, so."

"You're the one with the knife," Rosalind agreed solemnly. "Hard to argue with that."

Behind us, the threshold god made a sound like a latch settling.

"There is one more thing you should know before you knock," it said. "The door has noticed you. It has been noticing you for some time. And it is not waiting patiently."

The gate, which had loomed inert between the boulders all night, shivered. The loop behind it stuttered—the burn, the reset, the burn—and then, for the first time, did *not* reset. The image held: the woman with the bread, mid-fall, turning her softening face toward the gate. Toward Rosalind. Her mouth shaping that unhearable name.

And this time, faintly, terribly, I heard it.

Not a name. A word. One word, in a language the empire had eaten, and yet I understood it the way you understand a shout in a dream:

*Come.*

The gate began, very slowly, to open.
