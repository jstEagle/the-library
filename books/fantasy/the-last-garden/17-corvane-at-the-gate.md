## 17. Corvane at the Gate

I had always assumed that when the Ministry finally caught up with me, it would feel like something. Relief, maybe. Or shame. The particular cold satisfaction of a ledger balancing itself.

What it actually felt like was a headache.

They came up the mountain road at dawn, forty of them in dove-grey coats, and the light went strange around them the way it always did when that many Unrememberers walked in step—like the world couldn't quite decide whether to keep its details. Birds stopped singing. Not fled. Stopped, mid-note, as if someone had lifted the concept of birdsong out from under them and hadn't yet put it back.

Rosalind felt it too. I watched them go still over the campfire, one hand hovering above the kettle, and I watched them do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I'd done for eleven years in dove-grey—and arrive at the answer they'd been dreading since the drowned city.

"They're here," Rosalind said.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Enough." I kicked dirt over the fire. "It's time you saw what I used to be. All of it at once, unfortunately. No tasteful reveal. No dramatic single combat. Just—" I gestured at the treeline, where the dawn was curdling grey, "—that."

Rosalind looked at me for a long moment. Six weeks ago that look would have been fear. Three weeks ago, after the confession at the fireline, it had been fury. This morning it was something worse, or better, or both: it was trust, offered by someone who knew exactly how little I deserved it and was offering it anyway.

"Then stand where I can see you," Rosalind said, "and don't get clever."

"Clever is the only thing I've ever gotten," I said, "but fine."

The threshold god chose that moment to ooze up through the flagstones beside us, small and starving, wearing my mother's face—which is to say, wearing a face it had borrowed from a memory of mine, because I have no mother's face of my own to give it. It had been doing that a lot lately. I'd stopped flinching. Mostly.

"They come to eat," it said, in my own stolen voice, "and you come to plant. Go to the hedges. The hedges remember how to fight."

"Do they?" Rosalind asked.

"No," said the god cheerfully. "But neither does he. That's the point of him."

* * *

Corvane did not march at the head of his column. Of course he didn't. He arrived the way rot arrives in fruit—from the inside, quietly, already there when you cut it open. One moment the path before the Garden's outer hedge was empty; the next, Inquisitor-General Corvane was standing on it, hands folded behind his back, looking up at the moving door with an expression I had seen him wear exactly once before, over a burning archive in Vhal, and never wanted to see again.

Reverence. The bastard actually revered this place. That was the horror of Corvane, the thing they never put in the training manuals: he wasn't a butcher who hated memory. He was a curator who loved it so much he couldn't bear to let anyone else hold it.

"Sera Vane." My name in his mouth sounded like a summons. It always had. "You're standing on consecrated ground in the company of a Class-One anomaly, in direct violation of Continuity Directive Seven. I want you to notice that I'm alone."

"You brought forty people."

"I sent forty people home last night," he said, "because what happens next requires no witnesses, and because I am about to offer your friend a mercy, and mercy should not be delivered by crowd." His eyes moved past me, found Rosalind, and softened—and that softness frightened me more than any weapon in the Ministry arsenal. "Rosalind Dunmore. Do you know what you are?"

"A person," Rosalind said.

"You are a book," Corvane said gently. "A very old book, bound in living skin, that has spent twenty-three years trying to learn to read itself. Every prayer answered in these mountains, every wildflower blooming out of season, every villager weeping over a name they never learned—they are all symptoms of you. And every day you walk free, the sickness spreads." He spread his hands. "I am not here to punish you. I am here to close the cover. Quietly. Painlessly. You will not even know you were ever anything at all. Most people would call that a gift."

"Most people," Rosalind said, "haven't seen what's inside the gift box."

"No," Corvane agreed. "That's rather the point of boxes."

I stepped sideways. One step, deliberate, until I stood squarely between them, between the man who had trained me and the person I had—well. We could come back to what I had.

"General," I said. "Move along."

He regarded me with something almost like fondness. "Sera. Eleven years ago I pulled a half-feral child out of a Ministry holding cell and taught her that forgetting is not loss—it is kindness, administered at scale. I gave you a ledger instead of a childhood because a ledger is better company. And now you're going to tell me that this—" he gestured at Rosalind, at the hedges, at the thin gold light beginning to leak from under the Garden's door like blood under a bandage, "—is worth throwing away everything I built for you."

"It's not worth anything," I said. "That's the whole point. It isn't a transaction. You never understood that. None of us understood that. We thought memory was inventory." My voice cracked, and I let it; I was done sanding the edges off myself for the Ministry's comfort. "You ate a country, Corvane. You ate it and called it peace, and then you handed us brooms and told us we were janitors of history. And the worst part—the part I will burn out of my own skull if I survive today—is that I believed you. For years. I read names off that ledger and told myself each one was a kindness."

"I remember," Corvane said softly, "when you were assigned the Dunmore file."

The words landed like ice water. Beside me, Rosalind went very still.

"You refused it," Corvane continued, conversationally, terribly. "Did you know refusal leaves a scar? A shape in the memory where a decision used to be? You carved the decision out yourself rather than carry it. Very tidy work, incidentally. Almost professional." He tilted his head. "But scars remember, Sera. Even when the wound forgets. Why do you think you've been dreaming about garden gates for eleven years?"

I hadn't known. God help me, I hadn't known—I'd filed those dreams under anxiety, under overwork, under the hundred harmless diagnoses a good Inquisitor reaches for before the true one. And all that time the shape of my own refusal had been sitting in me like a splinter, working its way toward the surface, toward her, toward the smuggler's inn where I'd sat down across from a stranger with half a name and felt my whole chest open like a door.

"You came here to collect me," I said.

"I came here to save you," Corvane said. "From her. From this. From becoming a footnote in someone else's restoration." He sighed, and for one naked moment he looked tired—old, even, though nobody knew how old he was; the Ministry's records on Corvane began, fittingly, with nothing. "Last chance, Vane. Step aside. Take the absolution. I'll even return what's in escrow."

"My childhood."

"Every year of it. The garden gate. The girl's hand in yours. All of it, intact, yours again—one word from you." He smiled without warmth. "Or refuse, and I take it with the rest. I'm told the extraction is painless. I've never had the courage to test that claim personally."

And there it was. My heart, held in escrow by the enemy, priced at one betrayal.

I thought about the garden gate. I thought about the girl's hand—small, warm, sticky with some sweetness I couldn't name, because the name was the thing that had been taken. I thought about how I had built an entire self out of that absence and called the building a career. Eleven years of dove-grey competence, of names crossed off ledgers, of villagers weeping without knowing why, and underneath it all a child's hand reaching for mine across a gap I couldn't see.

I thought about Rosalind's hand, six days ago on the frozen shore of the glass lake, pulling me up off the ice after I'd killed the only other teacher I'd ever loved, and saying—not *it's all right*, because it wasn't, but *you're still here*, which was somehow both crueler and kinder.

"The gate can wait," I said.

Corvane raised an eyebrow.

"I mean it." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Whatever's behind that gate—my mother, my name, the girl whose hand I held—I'll find it the long way. Earned. Remembered honestly. Not dispensed by you like a wage for good behavior. Because here's what I finally understand, General, eleven years late and forty miles past the point of usefulness:" I turned my head, just enough to see Rosalind over my shoulder. "Memory isn't something you're given. It's something you keep. And I keep this."

I took Rosalind's hand. In front of the Inquisitor-General of the Ministry of Continuity, in front of the moving door of the Last Garden, in front of whatever gods were watching through the hedges with their starved, hopeful faces—I took their hand and laced our fingers together and said, aloud, in words that no conditioning had ever authorized:

"This one. I choose this one. Let every power in the land hear it and come collecting."

The world rang like a struck bell.

I felt it happen—the Ministry conditioning, the lattice of compliance they'd woven through my mind since I was nine years old, the reflexes of obedience dressed up as duty—it didn't shatter. It *unraveled*. Thread by thread, quietly, almost politely, like a knot deciding it had held long enough. And underneath it, in the space the lattice had occupied, something else surfaced: not my stolen childhood, not yet, but its *shape*. A warmth. A hand in mine. The certainty, absolute and unaccountable, that I had been loved once, thoroughly, by someone whose face I would know again someday—and that love, unlike duty, had never once asked me to forget anything.

"Oh," I said, mostly to myself. "Oh, that's what it feels like."

"Sera," Rosalind breathed.

"Not now," I said, because if I turned around I was going to cry, and I refuse to cry in front of Corvane even under optimal circumstances. "In a minute. Possibly several minutes. There's a man here who needs killing first."

"There doesn't have to be," Corvane said, and drew his knife—a thin grey blade that drank the morning light, the tool they called the Pen, because Ministry humor died young and stayed dead. Around him, the air began to fill with his senior Unrememberers, the forty he'd sworn he'd sent away, folding out of concealment like ink dropped in water. So much for mercy without witnesses. So much, in fact, for mercy. "Stand away from the keystone, Vane. What comes next will not distinguish between you."

"Then aim carefully," I said, and pulled my own blade.

* * *

The hedges fought the way the god had promised: badly, and with tremendous enthusiasm.

I will say this for the outer garden of Velthmere—whatever gardener had shaped those thorn-walls two centuries ago had done it with an empire's fall in mind. When the first three Unrememberers waded in swinging their Pens, the hedge simply *closed*, the way a fist closes, and I heard sounds I won't repeat and saw a grey coat emerge alone on the far side minus its occupant and plus a great deal of red. The fourth Unrememberer got smart and started cutting; the hedge grew faster than she cut, and somewhere in the tangle I heard her start screaming a name over and over—her own, I realized. Her own name. The Garden was giving back everything she'd eaten, all at once, unfiltered.

I made a note to avoid the hedges personally and went for Corvane.

He met me in the open ground before the door, and fighting Corvane was like fighting weather. He didn't press attacks; he arranged them. He didn't defend; he permitted. Twice he disarmed me without appearing to move, and twice I recovered the blade because Rosalind was behind me, and something in me refused—flatly, structurally, the way stone refuses to be water—to fall down while they were watching.

"You're fighting above your weight," Corvane observed, parrying a thrust that should have opened his throat.

"I'm fighting for something," I said. "You're fighting against. It turns out that matters."

"Sentiment." He turned my blade aside and drove me back three steps. "The Ministry's first lesson, Vane. Sentiment is just memory with ambition."

"And you're just hunger with a title." I feinted left, went right, felt the Pen kiss my ribs—a line of cold, a patch of fog where a memory of a rainy day in Vhal used to live. He'd marked me. Small cut, small theft. He was eating me in appetizers, patient as erosion. "You want to know the difference between us? You collect. I keep."

Behind him, the battle among the hedges was turning ugly. The Unrememberers were learning—falling back into formation, linking shields of enforced blankness that made the thorns wilt where they touched. The Garden's defenses were magnificent and mortal, and there were forty of them, and two of us, and one small starving god who had taken the shape of a border collie and was biting ankles with theological commitment.

And Corvane saw me see it, and smiled.

"Here is my final offer," he said, backing me toward the hedge-line, the Pen weaving lazy figure-eights. "Not for you—for the keystone. Tell Rosalind Dunmore that surrender means sleep. That oblivion is rest. That the pain of being a vessel ends the moment the vessel empties. Say it convincingly, and I'll let you both walk away. You can spend your exile together. Twenty years, thirty, in some country too dull to matter. Growing old. Isn't that what you want?" His voice gentled into something hideously sincere. "Isn't this all just a very elaborate way of asking permission to be happy?"

And that—that was the cruelest cut of the entire morning, because it was aimed at something true.

I wanted it. God, I wanted it. A cottage somewhere. Rosalind arguing with the kettle. Mornings that belonged to no empire and no ledger. I wanted it so badly that for one full second my blade dipped.

Which is, of course, when Rosalind stepped out from behind me.

"Corvane," they said, walking forward into the space between our blades like it was a garden party, "you have it backwards. Again. As usual." They stopped an arm's length from the most dangerous man in the empire and looked up at him without a flicker of fear. "We're not running from you. We never were. We're running *toward* something, and you've been chasing us with your little knife because you can't stand that some things can't be collected." They glanced at me—one heartbeat, warm as a coal—and then back at him. "And no, I won't sleep. I spent twenty-three years asleep. I'm done. Whatever's in me, whatever I was made to carry—I'm planting it. Right here. Right through you, if you insist on standing in the field."

Something moved beneath Corvane's composure. Not anger. Hunger—the vast, bottomless, curatorial hunger of a man who has eaten a country and is still hungry, hearing the last course announce itself.

"Then I'll take it now," he said, "at the source," and the Pen rose, and the grey coats converged, and the hedges screamed—

—and under the door of the Last Garden, the golden light went suddenly, enormously bright, and every god in every wall between here and the drowned city opened its eyes at once.

Corvane turned toward the light. Toward the door that was opening. Toward the memory of a nation rising out of its vault like floodwater.

"What," he said, "is it *doing*—"

"That," I said, and put my shoulder into the charge that knocked him sprawling, "would be the harvest, General. Try to keep up."

The door swung wide. Rosalind walked toward it. And the complete memory of Velthmere—every name, every grave, every god, every garden—began, at last, to pour.
