## 18. The Eating of Names

I have been chased through mountains, lied to by a witch I loved like a mother, hunted across a lake of glass by men with ledgers where their hearts should be, and none of it—not one hour of it—prepared me for the sound a god makes when it is eaten.

It is small. That's the horror of it. Not a scream, not thunder. A *pop*, like a soap bubble breaking, and then a hole in the world shaped exactly like the thing that used to be there.

The threshold god had told us once that the Garden's hedges remembered every path ever walked through them. Now the paths were forgetting themselves as we ran them.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last few minutes deserve better than my panic.

When Corvane came through the outer hedge with the full weight of the Ministry behind him—forty Inquisitors in grey, moving in step, their boots leaving no prints because they had unremembered the ground beneath them as they crossed it—I did what any sensible person does. I ran deeper into the Garden.

Sera stood her ground. Of course she did. She'd already said it aloud, in front of all of them: *"I choose this. I choose them."* Words that should have cost her everything, spoken like she was reporting the weather. I loved her for it and I was furious about loving her for it, because love is a terrible thing to be carrying while you flee.

The Garden is not a garden the way you're imagining it. No neat rows, no gardener with dirty knees. It's older than gardens. The hedges grow in shapes that hurt to look at sideways, and the paths rearrange themselves around your intentions, which means that running from something gets you exactly nowhere—you have to walk toward what you want and trust the green to meet you halfway. The threshold god walked beside me, wearing the shape of my mother today, which was either comfort or cruelty; with gods it's usually both.

Behind us, Corvane stopped chasing and started *eating*.

I heard Sera shout his name—a warning, or maybe just defiance—and then I heard the first pop.

A god died. Somewhere off to my left, past a hedge that bent like a bow, one of the old gods of Velthmere went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw: an absence with edges.

"He's not hunting us," the threshold god said, in my mother's voice gone thin. "He never was."

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing in the heart of a dying paradise while forty grey-robed professionals ate my country's soul course by course:

Corvane hadn't come for me. Or rather—he'd come for me the way a man comes for the cellar key when what he wants is the cellar. The keystone memory inside me was a copy. A backup. But the Garden itself? The Garden was the original archive. Every prayer ever answered here, every name the Ministry had devoured over two hundred years—all of it had been carried here, hidden, tended by starving gods who had nothing left to tend but each other.

And Corvane was going to eat the whole thing.

Another pop, closer now. The air where it happened smelled like rain on hot stone—the smell of the shrine in the pass where all this began, the smell of a prayer answered after two hundred years of silence. Some god of wellsprings, maybe. Some god who had waited centuries for someone to say its name and gotten thirty more minutes instead.

"They can't fight," I said stupidly.

"We are not made for fighting," the threshold god agreed. "We are made for *between*. For doorways and dusk and the moment before you speak. When the empire took the temples, we retreated to the between-places. There is very little to defend in a between-place." It looked up at me with my mother's eyes, and my mother has been dead or erased for years, and somehow that was the thing that made my chest crack open. "You could still run, little key. The paths will hide you. It is what we are for."

"Running is what got us here," I said. Six years of running. Six years of being nobody so hard that even I started to believe it. And here was the punchline: being nobody hadn't kept me safe. It had just kept me *small*. The Ministry found me anyway. They always find you eventually, if what they want is small enough to fit in a ledger.

Somewhere behind us, Sera's voice rose—sharp, commanding, the old Inquisitor cadence deployed like a weapon against her own people. Buying time. Spending herself to buy time for me.

Another pop. Then another, close together, like popcorn in a pan. Like the whole sky going out star by star.

And that's when it happened. Not a vision, not a revelation handed down by trumpeting angels. Just a memory, surfacing—Maren's kitchen, six years ago, the night before I fled. She was planting onions in a cracked clay pot on the sill, and she said—I thought idly, I thought meaninglessly—

*"Everything worth keeping, you give away, girl. Hoard a thing and it rots. Seed's the same. Memory's the same. Love, most of all."*

I had run because she tried to burn a page out of my head. I had hated her for it. And all along she'd been trying to teach me the only lesson that mattered: I was never meant to *carry* Velthmere. Carrying was the empire's whole crime—hoarding history in vaults, hoarding names in ledgers, hoarding childhoods in escrow. You don't save a country by locking it in a box. You save it by putting it in the ground.

The keystone memory wasn't a weapon. It wasn't even an escape hatch.

It was a seed.

"Oh," I said aloud. "Oh, you absolute fools. All of us."

The threshold god turned. "Little key?"

"It's not supposed to stay inside me." My hands were shaking. My whole body was shaking, but underneath the fear was something bright and terrible, the way dawn is terrible if you've been hiding in the dark long enough. "That's why it was put in a *child*. Not a vault. Children grow. Things planted in children are meant to come *out* again—" I stopped. Because the rest arrived all at once, the way floodwater arrives, and the rest was this: coming out meant letting go. All of it. Maren's kitchen. Sera's hand at the fireline. The pass, the shrine, my own name. Everything I had clawed back from the dark at such cost, poured out into soil that might not take it, might not sprout, might just be dirt afterward with one dead girl's ghost mixed in.

Seeds don't get to watch themselves bloom.

* * *

I found Sera near the outer hedge, and I want to remember this part properly, because if I do what I think I'm going to do, proper remembering is about to become a scarce commodity.

She was bleeding from a cut above her eyebrow and holding her former mentor's attention the way you hold a wolf's—absolutely, deliberately, with nothing spare. Three Inquisitors lay folded among the hedge roots, asleep or worse. Her ledger was open in her left hand, and I realized with a lurch that she wasn't reading it. She was *feeding pages to the hedge*. The Garden was drinking the Ministry's own records—every name they'd unmade, pouring back into the roots where names go to wait.

"You look terrible," I told her, because we are neither of us good at tender moments under fire.

"You look like you've had an idea," she said. "I hate your ideas. Your ideas have a body count."

"This one's mine alone." I took her hand. Her knuckles were split. Even now, some traitor part of me catalogued that—*split second knuckle, left hand, she fights southpaw when she's tired*—because apparently even at the end I am a person who notices things instead of feeling them. "Corvane's eating the Garden. The gods can't stop him. Nothing here can stop him, because he's stronger than anything that's left, and he'll get stronger with every bite."

"So we run. Deeper. There are paths he doesn't know—"

"Sera." I said her name and watched her stop, really stop, the way she only does for me. "There's no deeper. This is the deepest place in the world. That's the entire point of it." I pressed her hand flat against my sternum, over the keystone, over the humming pressure that had lived under my ribs since before I could talk. "It was never cargo. It was never a treasure to smuggle over a border. It's a seed, and seeds don't survive in ledgers or lockboxes or frightened girls. Seeds go in the ground."

Understanding moved across her face slowly, and then all at once, like ice going out on a lake. "No," she said.

"Yes."

"*No.* You let it pour out of you, you won't—" She couldn't finish it. Sera Vane, who had faced down Inquisitor-Generals and her own conditioning and a frozen lake full of hunters, could not finish a sentence about losing me. "There has to be another way. There's always—"

"There isn't, and you know it better than anyone. You spent ten years inside the machine that makes sure there isn't." I cupped her face. Blood on her brow, fury in her eyes, and underneath both, the thing she'd chosen over her own stolen childhood without ever saying it plainly: me. Us. Whatever word fits for two people who taught each other how to hold onto memories by holding onto each other. "Hey. Listen. If it works—if it takes—then everyone gets it back. Maren. The village that forgot its dead. Your garden gate. Your girl's hand in yours. Everyone. Including you. *Especially* you."

"And you?"

I smiled, and I'm told it was a real smile, though God knows where I found it. "I'll be someone new. Someone with room to grow. That's not dying, Sera. That's the opposite of everything the Ministry ever did."

Behind her, down the long green aisle between hedges, Corvane appeared. He didn't run. He walked, unhurried, and the hedge behind him was grey. Not shadowed—*grey*, colorless, the leaves remembering nothing of green. He had eaten his way through the outer garden and he was coming for the heart, and the gods were guttering out around him like a city losing power district by district.

Pop. Gone: some god of hearths, and every home in Velthmere got colder in a place no map recorded.

Pop. Gone: the god of debts forgiven, and somewhere an old quarrel hardened forever.

"Rosalind," Sera whispered. My name. My true name, the one that made me visible to every power in the land, the one I'd reclaimed knowing the price. She said it like a plea and a promise at once.

"I know," I said. "Walk me to the middle."

* * *

The heart of the Last Garden is a clearing with no trees, which sounds simple until you understand that the absence of trees here is itself ancient and deliberate—a wound the Garden keeps as a reminder, or a bed prepared long ago for exactly this planting, I'll never know which. The threshold god walked with us as far as the edge of it and would go no further. Between-places, it said. This is not a between. This is a beginning.

Corvane reached the far side of the clearing at the same moment we did, because of course he did. He had eaten enough of the Garden by now to know its shortcuts. Grey crept up his sleeves like frost.

"The keystone comes with me," he said. His voice was gentle. That was always the worst thing about him—the gentleness, the sincere belief that unmaking people was a form of mercy. "Child, I am offering you peace. Two hundred years of noise, all of it silent. You'll finally rest."

"You're offering me a grave," I said. "I've lived in one for six years. The rent's terrible."

Something flickered in his face—annoyance, maybe, or the ghost of humor, though his kind buried that long ago. He raised one hand, and the grey came with it, and I felt the keystone shudder under my ribs like a bird testing cage bars.

But here is the thing Corvane never understood, the thing the whole empire never understood, the thing you'd think two hundred years of eating memory would have taught them:

You cannot devour a seed and kill what's in it. Eating is how seeds travel.

I closed my eyes. Under my ribs, the complete memory of Velthmere—the nation, the gods, the gardens, the griefs, two hundred years of a country that officially never existed—turned over like something waking in warm soil.

And I opened my hands.

"Rosalind—" Sera's voice, breaking.

"Hold onto me," I told her. "Whatever happens. Hold onto me, and I'll know the way home."

The keystone began to sing.

Corvane lunged, grey flooding forward across the clearing like a tide with teeth—but he was too late, and he was too late because he had never once, in all his long career of taking, learned the difference between a vault and a womb.

The last seed of Velthmere broke open in my hands, and I let it.
