## 20. Aftermath of a Returned Nation

I have stood in the Ministry's great hall and watched a man's entire life get folded into an envelope. I have eaten names off a ledger with my own hands—well, not *eaten*, exactly; the Ministry preferred the word *archived*, which is what a vulture would say if it had a bureaucracy. I thought I knew what it looked like when a country died.

I was wrong. I'd only ever seen the corpse. What I saw on that morning, standing in the smoking hedges of the Last Garden while Velthmere poured back into the world like water into a dry riverbed—that was something else entirely.

It started small. A bird. Some gray little hedge-bird landed on a blackened trellis beside me and sang four notes that no bird in the empire knows, because the song belongs to a country that officially never existed. Then the wind came through the broken hedges carrying the smell of bread baking somewhere, though there was no village near enough to bake anything. Then the mountains themselves seemed to sit up straighter, the way a sleeping man does when someone says his name.

And then the people.

They came up out of the valley in ones and twos at first, then in streams—farmers, shepherds, smugglers, children—walking toward the Garden with their faces wet and their hands out in front of them like men feeling for furniture in a dark room. I heard a woman near the outer wall say, "Thornfell," over and over, but not the way you say a mountain's name. The way you say your mother's.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about erasure: it doesn't kill the memory. It just orphans it. Two hundred years of orphaned memories, all of them waiting, and Rosalind Dunmore walked into the heart of the Garden and opened every door at once.

I stood there with my sword still drawn and ash in my hair and did the only sensible thing available to me.

I cried like a child who'd been handed back her own name.

* * *

You should understand something about Inquisitors before I go further. We are trained from childhood—or I was; more on that later—to feel nothing when we work. There's a technique they teach you, a little trick of breathing and self-division: you put the part of you that feels in one room and lock it, and the part that works walks through the house alone. It works beautifully. It works so well that after eleven years I genuinely did not know where I'd left the key.

The Ministry fell apart in about six hours.

Not with fire, though there was some of that—the archive vaults in the capital burned hot and bright as everything inside them came home to roost, and I do not mourn those flames. No, mostly it fell apart the way ice falls apart: quietly, structurally, all at once. Every Inquisitor in the field woke up holding two hundred years of stolen lives in their heads. Some of them sat down in roads and didn't get up. Some ran. A few—I'm told three, which I consider three too many—tried to keep working, tried to eat the memories coming back, and were simply overwhelmed, drowned in other people's weddings and funerals and first kisses until there was no room left for the Ministry.

The Continuity Office had always claimed it was load-bearing. That without it, the empire would collapse under the weight of its own crimes.

We were right. That's the joke. That's the whole rotten joke, and I laughed until I sobbed, sitting on a garden wall in a country that didn't exist yesterday.

By noon, garrisons were flying white banners made of bedsheets. By evening, soldiers were asking villagers—in dialects they hadn't known they knew—which road led to towns they couldn't remember living in. An empire doesn't die when its armies lose. It dies when its soldiers stop knowing why they're holding the spear.

I watched a young corporal by the reservoir take off his helmet, look at the drowned ruins beneath the water, and say, very quietly, "I used to swim there." He'd been stationed in Thornfell for two years. He had never seen the ruins before this morning. But he had, once. Everybody had, once.

* * *

Corvane was still there.

I don't know how long he'd been standing among the ruined hedges when I found him—an hour, half a day. He wasn't guarding anything. There was nothing left to guard. He just stood where the fighting had been thickest, in a trampled circle of blackened yew, with his hands hanging at his sides and his face turned up toward the sky like a man listening for rain.

Inquisitor-General Aldous Corvane. Forty years in the Ministry. The man who ate nations and called it mercy. When I was nineteen and full of purpose, I wanted to be him. When I was twenty-five and starting to crack, I feared him. Standing in front of him now, I felt neither. I felt what you feel looking at an empty house.

"Sera Vane," he said, without turning. His voice was steady. That frightened me more than shouting would have. "You're not arresting me."

"No, sir."

"Good. Because I'm not sure there's anything left of me to arrest." He turned then, and I saw it. All of it. You have to understand—he had spent forty years eating memories, archiving them in himself, sealed away behind the same locked doors they train us to build. And now the doors were open. Not because anyone broke them. Because the memories had remembered themselves, and what remembers cannot be held.

He was carrying every life he had ever taken. Every farmer whose name went into the ledger. Every god he'd starved. Every child loaded down with a nation's past and told it was a gift.

"I can hear them," he said. "That's the punishment, isn't it? They built me to be a vault, and now the vault is full, and the vault has to *feel* it all." He laughed—a small, awful sound, like a hinge. "Do you know what I've learned since dawn, Vane? Do you know what forty years of work amounts to?"

"Tell me."

"Grief." He said it simply, almost gratefully, like a diagnosis finally delivered. "Just grief. All of it. Every archive, every unmade name—it was all grief, and I carried it for them because they paid me to, and I called it order." His eyes found mine, and they were clear in a way I had never seen them. Corvane had always had eyes like a locked drawer. Now they were just eyes, tired and wet and human, and it was the worst thing I'd ever seen on his face. "Tell the girl—if she lived—tell her I understand now. Tell her that's not forgiveness. I know it isn't. But tell her someone understands what she gave."

He walked past me then. Out through the broken hedge. Toward the mountains, away from the capital, away from everything he'd built. I watched him go and I did not stop him, and I want it noted for whatever history gets written now that histories are possible again—I did not stop him because stopping him would have been a kindness, and I had decided I was finished doing the Ministry's kindnesses.

They say he wanders still. Village to village, and when people ask who he is, he tells them the truth, the whole ugly ledger of it, and lets them decide. Most decide nothing. Grief recognizes grief. It doesn't need a verdict.

* * *

Rosalind was gone.

I want to be precise about that sentence, because precision was the last thing the Ministry ever taught me that turned out to be worth keeping. Gone is not dead. Gone is not alive. Gone is a word that means *the search has not ended*, and I held onto it the way a drowning woman holds a plank—with both hands, and with no intention whatsoever of letting go.

The center of the Garden was a courtyard I had never seen, though I'd fought through its outer hedges. Old stone, older than the empire, older maybe than stone ought to be, ringed by beds that had been empty for two centuries and were now—impossibly, gloriously—green. Seedlings everywhere. Shoots pushing up through ash. Whatever Rosalind had done in there, they had done it the way you plant a field, not the way you win a war.

And in the middle of it, lying in the soil like a dropped coat, was everything they'd been.

I knelt beside it. I'll admit that freely. My knees went first and the rest of me followed, and I gathered up an armful of earth and memory-shape that had been a person I loved, and I said their name out loud—"Rosalind"—and the name just sat there in the air, ordinary, unanswered.

For six years they had been no one. Half a name on a half-eaten page. And in the end they'd chosen it again—not the safety of being no one, but the giving-away of being someone, every recovered memory of Velthmere poured out into the land like seed from a torn sack. The threshold god had warned us. Seeds must be planted, not hoarded. Rosalind had understood it hours before I did, the way they always did, the way that made loving them feel like following someone through a door in the dark and trusting there was floor on the other side.

There was floor. There just wasn't Rosalind standing on it.

I searched. Of course I searched. I am very good at searching; it was most of my job description, and unlike the rest of the job description, searching translates. I walked the Garden's green rows calling every name they'd ever carried—Rosalind, and the half-name from Sera's ledger, R—— D——, and even the name Maren had whispered to me once, drunk on plum brandy and guilt, the true one, the consecrated one. Nothing answered. The wind moved the seedlings. A hedge-bird sang its four impossible notes.

Toward dusk I found the threshold god sitting on the courtyard wall, small and starving-looking as ever, wearing a borrowed shape—this time a young shepherd's face I didn't recognize, though somebody in this country must have.

"They're not dead," it said, before I could speak. Gods get impatient with human questions; they answer the question underneath.

"Then where—"

"Everywhere." It spread its thin hands at the green. "Look around you, ex-Inquisitor. What do you see?"

"Velthmere. Coming back."

"Mm. And what did they plant it with?" It hopped down off the wall, light as a debt. "You keep looking for a body. There isn't one, quite. There's a person distributed across several thousand square miles of returning country, and whether any of her gathers herself back together—" it shrugged, a gesture it had stolen from me, I realized, from some interview years ago, "—is not the sort of thing gods can promise. We are grateful. Gratitude is not omnipotence. Ask anyone who's prayed badly."

"It worked," I said. "Their prayer worked. At the shrine. So prayers work again, which means—"

"Which means if there is anything left of Rosalind Dunmore anywhere in this land, and anything in her that wants to come home, then yes." The god's borrowed face did something complicated. "But wanting to come home requires a home to want. Build her one."

Then it stepped backward through a doorway that wasn't there, because thresholds will be thresholds.

* * *

So I stayed.

I stayed through the first night, when the returned villagers lit fires in the Garden's outer court and sang songs in Velthmere's old tongue, haltingly, getting the words wrong and laughing at each other for it. I stayed through the week after, when the first harvest festival in two hundred years got planned by people who remembered planning it and couldn't remember why that memory made them cry. I stayed through the month, helping rebuild the shrine on the mountain pass, learning the old prayers myself—badly, with an accent, the way you learn any language of a country that raised you without telling you.

People asked, sometimes, who I was. Ex-Inquisitor, I'd say, and watch faces close like shop shutters. Then I'd add: the one who switched sides. And eventually—slower than they deserved, faster than I expected—faces opened again. Velthmere, it turns out, knows something about being forgiven for things you did while not fully yourself. It's practically the national art form.

I rebuilt Maren's cottage, too. Found the hedge-witch's notes, Rosalind's old training exercises, a page half-burned that I read by candlelight and wept over and then tucked into a book on the shelf, where it belonged. If Rosalind came back, they should find their things where they left them. That's what a home is, partly. Proof that someone expected you.

And every morning, I walked the Garden rows.

Green shoots. Green rows. Somewhere in all that living green, a person, scattered or sleeping or simply choosing—for the god had said *wants to come home*, and Rosalind had always been maddeningly free with their wants—and every morning I said their name into the soil like a prayer, because prayers worked again, and I had decided to be shameless about it.

On the thirty-first morning, I noticed the seedlings in the third row had grown overnight. All of them. Uniformly, unnaturally, the way things grow when a god leans close.

And in the middle of the row, pressed into the soft new earth, was a footprint.

Small. Bare. Facing toward the cottage on the hill.

I stood very still for a long moment, heart going like a smuggler's hammer on a locked door. Then I turned uphill, toward Maren's rebuilt roof and the candle I'd left burning in the window, and started to run.
