## 21. Someone New Under an Old Sky

I woke up in the ruins of a garden with no memories that weren't mine.

You'd think that would feel like losing something. Everyone tells you it should—the priests of the new temples, the villagers who wept for three days as two centuries came home to them like floodwater, even the gods themselves, who keep looking at me the way you look at an empty bowl that once held something precious. *Poor thing*, their faces say. *It gave away its whole inheritance.*

But here is what nobody warned me about: I don't miss them. The archived memories. Velthmere's whole swallowed country, every harvest festival and funeral rite and lullaby in a language that hadn't been spoken aloud since before the Ministry had a name for itself. It poured out of me at the heart of the Last Garden and went where it needed to go—into the soil, into the gods, into Corvane's hollowed skull and Sera's healing hands and ten thousand strangers waking up weeping—and what was left behind was just... me. Rosalind Dunmore. Six years of being no one, one season of being everyone's vault, and now this: a person with exactly one life's worth of past and no idea what to do with it.

It turns out one life is plenty. It turns out it's almost too much.

* * *

The first morning after, I lay in the grass among the Garden's ruined hedges and took inventory, the way Maren taught me when I was small enough to count on my fingers. One body, bruised from collarbone to knee where the memory had torn loose going through me. One set of clothes, ruined beyond salvage—I smelled like burnt rosemary and old paper. One friend, missing.

That last item sat in my chest like a stone all through the inventory, so I put the list down and went looking for her instead.

What I found were the gods.

They were everywhere, now that Velthmere remembered itself. Not the towering presences the old stories promised—no mountain-crowned sovereigns striding over the passes—but small things, hungry things, made newly fat on returned prayer. The threshold god I'd met beneath the reservoir was there, sitting on a broken garden wall, wearing my own face again because apparently that was still its favorite shape. Around it clustered others: a river-thing made of cold light, a hearth-god the size of a cat with embers for eyes, something tall and gray that might have been the patron of masons or of grief or of both.

"You're awake," said the threshold god, with my mouth, which never stopped being unsettling. "Good. We owe you thanks, and thanks from us is not a small thing."

"I noticed," I said. My voice cracked. I cleared my throat. "You've been following me."

"We've been *accompanying* you," it corrected gently. "There's a difference. Accompaniment implies consent."

"Fine. Thank you for your accompaniment." I looked past it at the others—at the ember-cat washing one paw, at the tall gray figure watching me with what I chose to interpret as kindness. "And thank you for whatever part you played in me surviving this. I assume there was a part. I don't remember most of the last day."

"There was a part," the hearth-god said, in a voice like a banked fire settling. "Small ones. You'll not remember them, and that's proper. What matters is this: the Garden stands open now, Rosalind of nowhere and everywhere. It will not close again. And there is a place in it for you—for the hand that carried the seed. A house by the gate, if you want it. Work worth doing. Worship, if worship doesn't offend you. Belonging."

Belonging. Spoken aloud, in my own face, by a creature older than the empire. I felt the offer go through me like warm water through cold hands, and I understood, with a kind of sick clarity, how easy it would be to say yes. To be *Rosalind, Keeper of the Garden*, some title with two hundred years of weight behind it. To trade the archive they'd loaded into me for a role they'd hand me now, freely, gratefully. To stop being no one by becoming someone very specific, chosen and named by powers greater than myself.

I'd spent my whole life being assigned a self. First Maren's apprentice. Then the keystone, the vessel, the vault. Then the fugitive, the file, the half-erased name in an Inquisitor's ledger. Every version of me had been written by somebody else's need.

"No," I said.

The ember-cat stopped washing its paw. The gray figure tilted its head.

"No?" said the threshold god, and for the first time it sounded less like me and more like itself—something puzzled and old and a little hurt.

"It's a generous offer. I mean that." I sat down on the wall across from it, because my legs had opinions about standing. "But I've been a container my entire existence. A page in someone's book. If I stay here—if I take the house by the gate—I'll just become a different kind of reliquary. *Come see the hero who saved the gods.* People would make pilgrimages. There'd probably be a shrine. I'd have to start saying wise things."

"Would that be so terrible?"

"I'm terrible at wise things. Ask anyone." I rubbed my face with both hands. "Look. You got everything back. The country, the names, the dead, the festivals—all of it poured out of me and into the world, and I'm *glad*, do you understand? Glad down to the bone. But what's left over is mine. Just mine. One small unremarkable life that nobody planned and nobody prophesied, and I'd like to find out what it does next without an audience."

The gods were quiet. Wind moved through the hedges, carrying the smell of roses that had bloomed out of season for six years and were only now blooming *on time*.

"The road, then," the threshold god said at last. "You always did look at the horizon like it owed you money."

"That's not—" I started, and then thought about it. "Okay, that's fair."

"It is not worship we can give you instead," it went on, "but we can give you this: wherever you walk in Velthmere, thresholds will know you. Doors will open easier than they should. Roads will be kinder than maps promise. Consider it interest on a debt we cannot repay."

"And if I ever want to come home?"

The god smiled with my smile, and it looked strange and soft on a face that wasn't mine anymore than mine had ever really been mine. "Home is a door, Rosalind. You of all people should know that doors open from both sides."

* * *

I spent three days in the Garden before I left, and I won't pretend those days were nothing.

I walked the rows where Velthmere's memory had come up out of the ground like spring. Whole orchards stood where there had been ruin a week before—apple trees heavy with fruit that shouldn't exist in this season, because the land remembered autumn differently than the calendar did, and the land, these days, was winning arguments. Villagers came up the mountain road in a slow procession, day after day, staring at the open gate, weeping, laughing, arguing with each other about streets they suddenly remembered walking as children. An old woman kissed the ground at the threshold. A boy of maybe eight ran straight past her, through the gate, shouting names of grandparents he'd never known he'd lost.

I watched all of it from the edges, keeping to the shadows the way six years of smuggling teaches you, and nobody minded. That was the strangest grace of it. The returned country didn't need me to stand center stage. Velthmere had gotten itself back, and I was allowed to be a bystander at my own miracle, which is, I've decided, the best possible seat.

On the second day I found the grave markers. Small ones, in a row behind the hedges—the consecrated children. Not just me. Eleven stones before mine, each with a name worn nearly smooth, each a child some desperate family had given up so a nation could sleep safely in their skull. I stood there a long while. I didn't cry, or I did, briefly, privately, and I'm not writing it down either way. I put my hand on the eleventh stone and said, "You're free too, whatever your name was," and meant it, and walked away lighter than I had any right to feel.

On the third day, Sera still hadn't come back, and I stopped pretending I wasn't waiting.

She'd been there. After. I knew that the way you know things with your skin instead of your head—waking once, near dawn, to hands checking my pulse, a voice low and wrecked saying my actual full name, first time anyone had said it whole in years, then gone again when I surfaced toward consciousness. Since then: nothing. The gods wouldn't say. *She has her own reckoning,* the hearth-god told me, insufferably cryptic for a cat-sized pile of embers. *Some debts are paid standing up.*

So I packed. Because that's what you do when waiting turns into drowning—you pick a direction and let the walking save you. I took food from the Garden's impossible orchards, a knife, the one thing I'd kept from Maren's cottage: a stub of charcoal pencil, chewed on one end, useless and precious. I stood at the great gate as the sun came up behind the mountains, and I looked back once at the house they'd offered me, small and golden in the morning light, already sprouting ivy that hadn't existed yesterday.

"Not yet," I told it. Maybe I told myself.

Then I walked north, out of the Garden, out of the story, onto the open road with nothing but my own single, unremarkable, entirely self-owned life ahead of me.

It fit strangely, like boots bought for someone else. I expected that to pass. It hasn't yet. I'm starting to suspect it isn't supposed to.

* * *

Here's what being only yourself actually feels like, in case the gods ever ask you to write the sequel:

It feels like hunger with no particular flavor attached. For six years I ate because bodies eat, slept because bodies fail, and underneath it all ran this low constant hum of borrowed weight—Velthmere's griefs, its joys, its two hundred years pressing against the inside of my ribs like weather. I thought the hum was *me*. Turns out it was cargo. Now the cargo's gone and what remains is startlingly light and occasionally terrifying, the way a boat feels wrong the first hour after you've unloaded it.

I cried at a stranger's wedding in the first village I passed through. Full, ugly, kneeling-in-the-ditch crying, because the bride turned around after her vows and smiled at somebody in the crowd, and the smile hit some part of me that used to hold four hundred archived weddings and now held exactly zero, and apparently the absence itself could ache. The bride's mother brought me cake afterward and asked if I'd known the groom's family. "No," I said, wiping my face. "I'm just new."

"Just new." She laughed like it was a blessing. Maybe it was.

Other things: I dreamed for the first time in years—actual dreams, nonsense dreams, a horse made of bread, a door that argued with me about rent—instead of replaying other people's memories in my sleep like a cursed theater. I got lost twice and enjoyed it, which I mention mostly because getting lost used to be a death sentence for someone whose head was a national treasure. I argued with a ferryman about his prices and won, and the victory felt *earned*, mine alone, no dead country whispering bargaining tricks into my ear.

And every night I caught myself doing the same thing: watching the road behind me. Not afraid, for once. Just watching. Waiting for a figure in travel-worn Inquisitor blacks, except the coat would be gone now, burned or buried or traded somewhere, and she'd be walking like a person instead of a weapon, and she'd have some dry remark ready about my campfire technique—

Which is when I'd make myself stop. Because hoping that loudly is a way of demanding, and I'd done quite enough of being demanded from, and if Sera Vane found her way to the crossroads out of these mountains it would be because she chose it. Freely. The way I'd chosen the road. Anything else would just be another assignment, and we were both, finally, gloriously unemployed from our assignments.

So I watched the road anyway. Sue me. Some habits are love.

* * *

The crossroads came on the ninth morning, where the mountain track spills down into the green valley that used to be a war and is now, improbably, just a valley. Someone had raised a waystone there—not a Ministry marker, not an imperial obelisk, but a rough-hewn stone in the old style, carved with the sigil I'd carried in my blood my whole life without knowing it: the seed-and-gate mark of the Last Garden. Fresh-cut. Days old, at most.

Someone was mapping what returns. Good. Somebody should.

I stood at the stone with my pack on and my future unwritten, and I took stock one last time, Maren-style, counting on fingers. One body, healing. One name, mine, chosen as much as anything can be. One road, going anywhere. One blank space where a person used to carry a country, and the space not empty anymore but *open*—the way a doorway is open, the way a question is open, the way a morning is open when nobody's told you yet what it's for.

For the first time in my entire existence, I was no one's vessel, no one's vault, no one's heir, no one's ghost. Just Rosalind. Under an old sky that finally, finally belonged to the people under it.

"Alright," I said aloud, to the stone, to the valley, to whoever was listening—including, I suspected, several small hungry gods taking notes. "Let's see who I am."

Behind me, gravel crunched under a boot with a very particular stride, and a voice I'd have known blindfolded, drunk, and dead said:

"You left without saying goodbye, and I have *feelings* about that."

I didn't turn around right away. I wanted to memorize the moment first—the light, the waystone, the whole ridiculous reborn world holding still for it.

Then I turned, and there she was, and whatever I said next, I already knew how the story would end before it began: together, walking, mapping what returns.

But that, as they say, is another chapter.
