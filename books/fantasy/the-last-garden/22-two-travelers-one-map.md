## 22. Two Travelers, One Map

### Sera

I had been told, in my years with the Ministry of Continuity, that the hardest part of any erasure was the paperwork.

This was a lie. The paperwork was nothing—a ledger, a signature, a stamp that smelled of iron and old decisions. The hard part was afterward, standing in a village where everyone smiled at you and no one remembered why they had ever wept, knowing you were the reason the well ran dry. I had done it forty-one times before I did it once too often. And then I had done the unforgivable thing: I had fallen in love with the file.

Now here I was, six weeks after the end of the world as I'd known it, walking out of the Thornfell mountains with an empty ledger under my arm like a penitent carrying her own head, looking for one person at a crossroads that had no business existing on any map.

The crossroads found me first.

That's not quite fair. Rosalind Dunmore—though the name meant something different now, something older and stranger and still being argued about by scholars who would never admit how much of their scholarship was guesswork—was sitting on a milestone at the junction where the smuggler's road spilled down out of the passes into the green throat of the valley. They were eating an apple. They were doing it with the particular concentration of someone who has recently discovered that apples exist, which, given everything, they may genuinely have.

"You're late," they said, without looking up.

"I'm punctual," I said. "You're early. There's a difference, and it's the whole reason I survived the Ministry's training."

"Six weeks." They looked up then, and I made myself hold the look, because I had spent six weeks making myself hold other things—the silence of the house we'd shared for three days outside Velthmere's drowned walls, the shape of the door where they'd walked through it while I slept, the note that said only *Don't follow me yet. Please.* "You could have written."

"To whom? You gave away your name to a country. I'd have had to address it 'To Whom It May Concern, Who Used to Sleep With Their Back Against Mine.'"

Rosalind laughed—that startled, unpracticed laugh, the one of someone hearing their own voice do something new—and tossed me the apple core, which I caught out of pure reflex and then felt foolish holding.

"That's your breakfast now," they said. "Penalty for lateness."

"The Ministry taught me to eat worse things with better manners."

"And how is the ex-Ministry treating you?"

"It isn't. That's rather the point."

They studied me the way they'd learned to study things during our flight—like reading weather off a ridge line. I let them. I had brought nothing but the ledger, and a person carrying only a blank book has very little to hide behind.

"You came anyway," Rosalind said finally. "After the note said don't."

"The note said *don't follow me yet.*" I sat down on the milestone beside them, close enough that our shoulders touched, because I am a coward in all matters except the ones that count. "I have never in my life been good at *yet.*"

* * *

### Rosalind

Here is what nobody tells you about giving away every memory you were never supposed to have: you expect to feel empty. What you actually feel is light. Not the lightness of having nothing—the lightness of having put down something enormous you'd been carrying so long you'd mistaken it for your spine.

The gods had offered me gratitude and a home. A shrine with my face on it, probably. Hymns. Very serious people asking me questions about the consecration I couldn't answer anymore, because I'd poured the answers into the soil of a resurrected nation like a farmer sowing barley. I thanked them kindly and took the road instead, because there are only so many times a person can be called *the Vessel Returned* before they start wanting to be called *pass the bread.*

Sera, though. Sera I hadn't counted on.

She came down out of the mountains with an empty ledger and dust on her boots and that expression she wore when she was pretending not to hope—which, in fairness, was also the expression she wore when interrogating smugglers, so I'd had to gamble. Six weeks I'd sat at this crossroads, or near it; I'd worked a harvest in the valley below, learned the names of forty-one people who had gotten their dead back and didn't know what to do with them. Forty-one. I remember that number for reasons Sera will understand if she ever tells me the size of her list.

"So," I said, when she'd finished pretending the apple core was fascinating. "The ledger. Walk me through it. Is it a threat? A confession? A very ambitious grocery list?"

She turned it over in her hands. Ministry-issue binding, iron-stamped, the kind of object that used to make villages go quiet. Now it was just a book with nothing in it, which might be the most subversive thing the Ministry ever produced.

"When Corvane fell," she said—she always said *fell,* never *was hollowed out by his own appetite until he wandered weeping into the hills holding every stolen grief at once,* which was technically more accurate—"when Corvane fell, the Continuity archives opened. Every file. Every name. I went in thinking I'd burn it all."

"And?"

"And I read mine. All of it." Her voice did the flat thing it did when she was holding herself together with procedure. "Every village. Every person. I sat in that room for two days, and then I understood why you walked into the Garden instead of running. Because you can't carry it. You can't even *hold* it without it eating you from the inside. So I copied out the last page—my last page, the one with your half-erased entry, R—— D——, the file that started all of this—and I burned the rest."

I waited. She was building toward something; I knew her rhythms by now, the way a river knows its stones.

"And then I kept walking," she said. "Out of the capital. Past the checkpoints where the guards didn't stop me because there's no more Ministry to give them orders. Down through the lowlands, where the roads have started going places they didn't used to go. Did you know that? The mapmakers are losing their minds. Roads remembering older routes. Rivers bending back toward channels that were filled in a century ago. The country is *unfolding,* Rosalind, and nobody has a map for it."

"And you thought," I said slowly, "a blank ledger—"

"—is exactly the right tool for mapping a country that keeps changing its mind." She looked at me, and beneath all that Inquisitor composure there was the girl from the garden gate memory, the one Corvane had shown her in escrow, waiting with her hand held out. "I thought: someone should walk these roads and write down what returns. Someone who knows what it costs when nobody writes it down. And I thought maybe that someone shouldn't do it alone."

* * *

### Sera

I want it noted, for whatever record survives us, that I rehearsed that speech for eleven miles. I want it noted that it still came out sideways, because I have faced down Inquisitor-Generals and threshold gods and my own former mentor on a frozen lake, and none of those frightened me as thoroughly as asking Rosalind Dunmore to travel with me.

They were quiet long enough that I started composing retreats. Then they reached over and put their hand flat on the ledger's cover, over the blank space where a title would go.

"What will you call it?" they asked.

"I hadn't got that far."

"Liar. You've had six weeks. You had titles by mile three."

"*An Account of Roads That Remember,*" I admitted. "*Working Title.*"

"That's terrible."

"It's honest. The Ministry taught me many things, but not humility."

Rosalind grinned—that crooked, unguarded grin that had survived the loss of everything else, that seemed in fact to be the one thing they'd kept out of the Garden on purpose—and stood up, brushing milestone dust off their trousers.

"All right, Vane. Terms."

"Terms?"

"If we're mapping what comes back, we do it properly. We stop in every village, even the unfriendly ones. We ask names, and we write them down, and if anyone's lost one, we find it or we plant a new one. No skipping the hard places because they're sad." Their voice roughened slightly. "I met a woman standing at a grave she couldn't name, once. Before all this. I gave her her daughter back, one word of it, and it cost me—but it was the best thing I ever did, and I did it *against* strategy. So we lead with that. Compassion first, cartography second."

"Compassion first, cartography second," I agreed. "And third?"

"Third—" They hesitated, and I saw them reach for something that wasn't there anymore, some memory or instinct from the archive they'd emptied, and come up instead with something brand-new, minted on the spot, entirely their own. "Third, we figure out who we are when nobody assigned us to each other. No files. No orders. No bargain either of us remembers making. Just—" They gestured vaguely between us, at the crossroads, at the morning coming gold down the valley where a dead country was pushing up green like grass through pavement. "Just two travelers."

"One map," I said.

"Blank."

"For now."

We stood there a moment longer, two people at a junction, neither moving, both smiling like idiots, and I thought: *this is what duty never bought me. This is what I chose.*

Then Rosalind picked up their pack—light, so light, everything they owned fitting in something smaller than a prayer book—and nodded down the road where it ran out of the mountains and into the reborn world, toward villages getting their festivals wrong and rivers finding old beds and gods learning, shyly, to be small again.

"Come on, then," they said. "The country's not going to remember itself."

It already was, of course. But somebody had to write it down.

* * *

### Rosalind

We walked.

That's the whole of it, really, and I suspect it always will be. We walked out of the Thornfells and the road unspooled ahead of us like a sentence someone had just remembered how to finish. By noon we passed a shrine—new, or newly remembered—where an old woman was leaving bread for the god of thresholds, who had apparently taken up residence in a culvert and was accepting offerings in the form of buttons. Sera wrote it down. Her handwriting was beautiful; I told her so, and she said the Ministry drilled penmanship into recruits so their lies would look official, and I said well, now it could make truths look official instead, and she looked at me for a long moment with something I decided not to name yet, because naming things is sacred work and I wanted to get it right.

By evening we reached a village that had gotten its festival wrong—celebrating a harvest homecoming three months early, because the memory had come back before the calendar did, and nobody minded. They fed us. They asked our names.

"Sera Vane," she said, easy as breathing, and I watched a former Inquisitor hand her true name to strangers voluntarily and saw what it cost her and what it paid.

And then they asked mine, and I opened my mouth, and found—I had known this was coming, I'd had six weeks at a crossroads to prepare—I found nothing. No name. The archive gone, the keystone emptied, and whatever name my parents gave me before they gave me away buried somewhere in the soil of a resurrected nation, planted along with everything else.

So I did what I'd done since I was seventeen years old and ran from Maren's cottage with smoke behind me. I chose.

"Rosalind," I said. "Just Rosalind. It's not the one I was born with, but I'm keeping it. It's the first thing I ever picked for myself."

The villagers nodded, satisfied, and moved on to arguing about whether the threshing dance went left or right, and Sera leaned close and murmured, "First entry in the new ledger," and wrote it: *Rosalind. Chosen. Keeping it.*

"That's not a map," I said.

"Everything's a map," she said, "if you're walking in the right direction."

Far off down the valley, where the drowned city of Velthmere was lifting itself out of its reservoir stone by remembered stone, I thought I saw lights—not lamps, not fires, but the slow green-gold shimmer of a garden gate opening somewhere it had no business opening, in a season with no business blooming. Something out there was growing. Something out there had my name in its roots, or the roots of whoever I'd been, and someday—maybe soon, maybe years from now—we'd walk that way and see what came up.

But not today. Today there was a road, and a blank book filling slowly with the names of the returned, and Sera Vane's shoulder warm against mine as the dark came down gentle over a country learning its own name again.

"Where tomorrow?" she asked.

"Wherever the road remembers," I said. "We'll follow it, and write it down, and plant what we can carry."

She laughed—really laughed, the unpracticed sound of a woman doing something new with her voice—and clicked open the ledger to a fresh page.

The ink was still wet when the first star came out over the mountains, and under it, faint but unmistakable, a wildflower bloomed out of season in the crack of the road ahead—as if the ground itself were checking that we were watching.

We were.
