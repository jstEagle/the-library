## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The last stroke of the pen took Wendell Oyelaran longer than any other, and it was, by every measure, the smallest: a single dot of ink at the eastern edge of the map, marking a wellspring that no living traveler had confirmed in four hundred years.

He sat back and blew gently across the page, the way his mother had taught him—not to dry the ink, though it needed drying, but because *a map should never be rushed into being*, she'd said, *any more than a promise should*. The lamplight caught the wet lines and made them gleam like veins under skin. The realm spread out before him in careful miniature: the Vellwood with its nine named clearings, the salt terraces of Ambrun descending toward a sea he had smelled once, from very far away, on a day when the wind came over the mountains wrong. He had drawn that sea eleven times now. Each version was better. Each version was a lie, because he had never seen it.

Thirty years in the Hollow, and he had mapped seventeen realms he would die without entering.

"You're muttering again," said a voice from the doorway. "The whole street can hear you negotiating with your ink."

Tam Broadfoot stood there with snow in his beard and two loaves tucked under one arm, wearing the expression of a man who had appointed himself responsible for whether Wendell ate. Tam was the valley's baker, its unofficial mayor, and its most committed gossip, in roughly that order of devotion.

"I wasn't muttering," Wendell said. "I was proofreading."

"To the map."

"Maps talk back if you don't keep them civil."

Tam snorted and set the loaves on the corner of the drafting table, directly on top of the coast of Ambrun, which Wendell chose not to take personally. Thirty years will do that. "Council meets tonight. They'll want your report on the border stones. And Ilsa wants you fed before you stand up there looking tragic and hollow-cheeked. You know how she gets."

"I'm eating," Wendell said, pulling a loaf free. It was still warm. He loved Tam for exactly this and would have said so if saying so wouldn't have produced forty more loaves by week's end.

Outside, the Hollow was doing what the Hollow always did in late autumn: settling into itself like an old dog by a fire. Smoke rose straight from chimneys. The orchards along the lane held their last apples like clenched fists. Somewhere beyond the ridge, past the border stones, the world went on—kingdoms rising, wars ending or not ending, magic moving through it all like weather—and none of it could follow anyone down into the valley. That was the whole point of the place. That was why he had come at twenty-two with nothing but his mother's folio and a grief too large to carry through ordinary country.

The Hollow forgot things. That was its gift. Magic died at the borders here, and everything magic touched went with it: curses unraveled, geases faded, the hunted became merely tired. People arrived with names they couldn't explain and left—if they left—with lighter pockets and no memories of why they'd come. The valley kept nothing. Not spells. Not debts. Not, Wendell suspected some mornings, him.

* * *

The burning of the draft was ritual, and ritual, in Wendell's experience, was just armor you put on one piece at a time until you could face the day.

He carried the finished fair copy downstairs to the strongbox where commissioned maps waited for the twice-yearly caravan—the only thing from the outside world the Hollow allowed itself, because maps, the elders said, were not magic. Maps were memory made honest.

But first, the draft.

He'd built the little iron brazier himself, years ago, in the yard behind his cottage, and he'd inscribed around its rim the only words of the old tongue he permitted himself anymore: *what is drawn must be released*. His neighbors thought it decorative. It was not. A draft map, unfinished and unbound, was a door left ajar, and doors left ajar were how trouble walked in. So every completed commission ended the same way: the working sketches, the false starts, the pages where he'd gotten the rivers wrong, all of it fed to the fire while he watched until the last curl of paper went gray.

Tonight the fire was slow about it. One page resisted—a sketch of the Vellwood's northern reaches, done in a hand steadier than his own, because he'd been younger when he drew it and less afraid. The paper browned at the corners and then, instead of catching, seemed to gather the flame inward, holding it the way a cupped hand holds water.

Wendell frowned. Paper did not do that.

He reached for the poker, and the page let go all at once, flaring bright and dying to ash between one heartbeat and the next, leaving behind a smell like rain on hot stone—which was also not the smell paper made—and a single unburned scrap no bigger than his thumbnail. He picked it out of the ashes before he could think better of it. On it, in his own younger ink, was half a word. He recognized it. He wished he didn't. It was the old name for a border: not the line itself but the *promise* of it, the binding-word his mother had forbidden him to write anywhere but in her folio, and even then only in the dark, with the shutters closed.

He dropped the scrap back into the brazier and held it there with the poker until it was certainly, thoroughly gone.

Routine, Wendell told himself. Old paper burns strangely sometimes. Ink does things. There is no crack in the armor; stop checking the straps.

It mostly worked, which was the most one could say for armor.

* * *

The council met in the meetinghouse, which had been a tithe barn in some previous century and still smelled faintly of grain and argument. Nine elders on benches, Ilsa among them at the far end where she always sat, close enough to the door to leave first and far enough from everyone else to be left alone. She was ninety-one and had been Wendell's teacher for thirty of those years, and she looked at him across the room the way she always did: as though he were a problem she had already solved and was simply waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

"Border report," said Elder Maren, who conducted meetings the way surgeons conduct amputations. "Oyelaran. Walk the line?"

"Walked it Tuesday," Wendell said. "All fourteen stones standing. No wear worth noting." He gave them the numbers, the bearings, the small observations that proved diligence. It was a performance he had given twice a year for three decades, and he was good at it, and hating that he was good at it was a luxury he allowed himself precisely never.

"And the outer country?" Maren asked. "Anything come over the ridge we should know of?"

"The usual. Traders' rumors. A kingdom somewhere east crowning a new king." He paused. "There are stories out of the deadlands. Caravans say the ash has started moving. Drifting against the wind."

The benches shifted. Someone coughed.

"Ash moves," said Elder Corwin, who was deaf in one ear and skeptical in both. "Ash is what wind does."

"Not against the wind," Wendell said quietly. "Not uphill."

Silence did its small work around the room. Then Maren said, briskly, "Weather. Nothing more. The covenant holds. It has held since before any name in this room, and it will hold after. The Hollow forgets, gentlemen, and that is our mercy and our wall. Anything else?"

"There's the matter," said Corwin, perking up with the relish of a man saved by an agenda, "of Oyelaran's chimney smoke, which on Sunday was blue, and I'll thank someone to explain to me what burns blue."

"Damp oak," said Wendell, who had burned damp oak on Sunday and something else entirely beneath it, and whose heart performed a small controlled collapse at the near miss.

"See that it doesn't," said Corwin, satisfied, and moved on to complaints about the mill race, and Wendell exhaled one year off his life.

Afterward, in the doorway, Ilsa fell into step beside him without appearing to move. She did that. At ninety-one she walked like a rumor.

"Blue smoke," she said.

"Damp oak."

"Mm." They walked. Snow had begun again, fine and unhurried. "You checked the stones Tuesday," she said.

"I did."

"Then you'll want to check them again tomorrow," Ilsa said, and patted his arm once, and turned up her own lane as if she hadn't just rearranged his entire evening.

* * *

He told himself he wouldn't go. Then he went.

Dawn found him on the western ridge with his breath hanging in front of him and frost in his beard, counting stones he had counted a hundred times. Fourteen. He counted them again, walking the line slow, touching each one the way you touch a fence post you've leaned on all your life.

Stone nine was wrong.

Not fallen. Not cracked. *Moved.* Six feet, perhaps eight, westward—out of the line, out of the bearing he had recorded every season since he was young enough to record things in a hopeful hand. The earth around its base was undisturbed. No drag marks, no frost-heave, no reason at all. The stone had simply decided, overnight, that it belonged somewhere else.

Wendell crouched beside it for a long time, long enough for the cold to climb through his coat and make itself at home. Border stones did not move. Border stones were the one thing in the world his mother had called *fixed*, and she had used the word the way other people used *holy*.

And yet.

He put his palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Not sun-warm—there was no sun yet, and the warmth was underneath, deep, patient, like the flank of something enormous sleeping just below the ground and turning over in its sleep.

From somewhere beyond the ridge—beyond the stones, out in the deadlands where nothing lived and nothing was supposed to *want* anything—came a sound. Small. Far. Almost lost under the wind.

It might have been the ice cracking on the high fells. It might have been the war, clearing its throat after three thousand years.

It might, Wendell thought, getting slowly to his feet with his heart going like a drummer boy's, have been a child.

The snow was coming down harder now, thick and silent, erasing the road behind him and the path ahead. He stood at the edge of the Hollow—the valley that forgot everything, that forgave everything, that had kept him safe for thirty years by keeping him nothing—and listened.

The sound came again. Closer. Definitely small. Definitely crying.

"Oh no," Wendell said aloud, to no one, meaning it completely. "No, no. I am not the man for this."

He was already walking toward it.
