## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came in off the western ridges the way storms always did in the Hollow—reluctantly, as if it too had to check whether it was allowed inside.

Wendell Oyelaran watched it from his workroom window, a cup of barley tea going cold in his hand, and thought, not for the first time, that he had built a life out of watching weather he was never permitted to feel. Thirty years of this. Thirty winters of drawing coastlines he would never smell and mountain ranges whose names existed only because he had invented them, carefully, in ink that faded to gray by morning. The valley did not tolerate magic. That was its whole purpose, its entire personality, you might say—the Hollow was the one place in all the mapped world where nothing extraordinary could survive past the border stones. Which meant Wendell, whose blood was apparently a treaty term rather than a body, lived here the way a debt lives in a locked drawer.

He had gotten quite good at it. He made maps for the council. He fixed fences. He had opinions about drainage ditches that people genuinely valued. Once, at the harvest festival, he had danced, badly, and Ilsa Venn had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down on a hay bale, and for nearly four seconds he had felt like a man rather than a clause.

The knock came just after dusk, when the snow had thickened enough to erase the road.

Three knocks. Small ones. The sort of knocks a fist makes when the fist belongs to someone who has been knocking on doors for a long time and has learned not to hope.

Wendell set down his tea with the particular care of a man hoping the sound will turn out to be a branch. It knocked again. He opened the door onto a wall of white, a smear of lamplight—and a child.

She stood on his step like something the storm had coughed up: small, maybe eight years old, wrapped in a coat that had been fine once and was now mostly memory, its hem stiff with ice. Her hair was black and frosted silver at the tips. She looked up at him with eyes so dark they seemed to have no bottom to them, and she said—

She said something. Words, certainly. They fell out of her chapped lips in a rush, urgent and syllabic, and every single part of Wendell's mind that was trained to catalog languages—the trade tongues, the valley dialect, the high speech of the old courts that he knew only from marginalia—reported back the same finding:

*I do not know what that is.*

And yet he understood her perfectly.

*Please,* she was saying. *It follows me. Please, I wasn't supposed to come here but there was nowhere else, please, the door only opens for—*

"Stop," Wendell said.

She stopped. She looked at him with the expression of a child who has been stopped many times before and expects nothing better.

He understood *that*, too. Not the shape of her fear—he'd have understood that in any language—but the actual words assembling themselves in his head, wearing meanings like borrowed coats. It should not have worked. Language does not work that way. Language is a lock, and you either have the key or you stand outside in the snow, which is precisely where she was standing, and—

*The door only opens for blood.*

His hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"You'd better come inside," he heard himself say, "before the watchmen see you."

Which was, he reflected later, an interesting sentence to choose, given that everything else in him was screaming the opposite instruction.

* * *

He fed her first, because whatever else she was, she was a child shivering in his kitchen, and thirty years of careful anonymity were not worth watching frostbite take a child's fingers. Soup, bread, the good honey he saved for days when being himself felt unbearable. She ate the way children eat when they've learned meals can be taken away: fast, watchful, one eye on him the whole time.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She considered him for a long moment. Then she pointed at herself and said a word—not in the old tongue this time, or if it was, it was short enough to keep its original shape. "*Sef.*"

"Sef." He tried it out. It sat strangely in the mouth, like a stone from a riverbed far away. "I'm Wendell."

She pointed at him again and said a long phrase in the old language, and he understood it, and understanding it made the soup go heavy in his stomach.

*You smell like the covenant.*

"I—" He put down his spoon. "I don't know what you mean."

Her face did something complicated—a child's attempt at adult patience. She pointed at him again, then at her own chest, then drew a circle in the spilled flour on the table, and tapped the center of it twice.

*Same middle. Same center. Same blood.*

"No," Wendell said, more firmly than he meant to. "No, I think you're mistaken, I think the storm has addled you, I think—"

She reached across the table and took his hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed her own small palm against it. And the strangest thing happened, the thing he would spend the rest of his life refusing to describe accurately: for half a heartbeat, he felt the borders of the Hollow. All of them at once. Every stone, every ward, every careful promise laid down by dead men, and threaded through them, faint as a pulse under skin, a pressure that recognized him.

Then it was gone. The lamp burned ordinary. The soup steamed ordinary. Sef let go of his hand and went back to her bread as though she hadn't just rearranged the architecture of his entire existence between bites.

Outside, through the window, a lantern bobbed up the lane toward his house.

Watchmen.

* * *

"In here," Wendell whispered, "and do not make a sound. Not one sound. Can you be quiet?"

Sef gave him a look of profound, almost insulting calm, and climbed into the space behind the wood stove, into the gap between it and the wall where he stored kindling, and pulled the sacking over herself, and vanished. Children, Wendell thought wildly, are professionally good at hiding. It's adults who forget how.

The knock came—full-sized, authoritative, the knock of a man with a badge-shaped sense of his own importance.

"Wendell! Oyelaran! Open up, it's Tam Brask."

He counted to three, arranged his face into mild irritation—it helped that mild irritation was his most practiced expression—and opened the door.

Tam Brask stood hip-deep in snowdrift with two others behind him, lantern held high, snow collecting in his eyebrows like a second pair. "Evening, Wendell. Terrible night."

"It is. What brings the whole watch out to my door?"

"Border stones." Tam blew out a breath. "Third house we've checked. You seen anything odd tonight? Anything come up the road?"

"The road?" Wendell gestured vaguely at the white wall beyond the lantern light. "Tam, I can't see my own fence. What about the border stones?"

"Shifted." Tam lowered his voice, and the other men leaned in, and Wendell understood suddenly that Tam was frightened, actually frightened, under all the officiousness. "Overnight. Moved inward, some of them. A handspan, maybe two. Old Marta swears the east stones are humming. Humming, Wendell. Stones don't hum."

"Stones certainly don't," Wendell agreed. "Cold makes things creak. Sound carries strange in a storm."

"That's what I told her." Tam didn't move. His eyes did a slow circuit of the doorway, the hall, the warm light spilling from the kitchen. "You're sure you've seen nothing? No travelers? Nothing... out of place?"

Behind the stove, Wendell knew, Sef was breathing slowly and deliberately, the way he'd told her, and he found himself thinking—with a clarity that felt like falling—that he was about to lie. To the watch. To the valley that had sheltered him for thirty years without ever asking what he was. He had never once lied to them. It was practically his defining virtue. *Honest Wendell Oyelaran, dull as a fence post and twice as useful.*

"No travelers," he said. "Just me and my maps. Though if the stones are shifting, you ought to tell the council to send for me in the morning—I can survey the line properly. Might be simple frost-heave. Ground moves more than people think."

"Frost-heave." Tam tasted the word, found it nourishing. "Aye. Probably that. Sorry to trouble you, Wendell."

"Any trouble," Wendell said, and closed the door, and stood with his forehead against it until the lantern light faded down the lane.

When he turned around, Sef had emerged from behind the stove and was standing in the middle of his kitchen looking at him—not grateful, exactly. Something older than gratitude. She said something soft in the old tongue, and he understood it, and wished very much that he didn't:

*You lied for me. Blood lies for blood. That's how it starts.*

"Go to bed," Wendell told her, and his voice cracked slightly on *bed*, and he pretended it hadn't. "There's a cot in the workroom. We'll figure this out in the morning."

But sleep did not come for him, and near midnight he found her awake anyway, sitting cross-legged on the cot, and she had taken one of his finished maps from the rack—the draft copy of the Meridian Coast, months of work—and she was holding it out to him with both hands, the way one holds out something fragile and true.

On the map, in charcoal, in a child's unsteady hand, she had drawn a mark.

It was a symbol Wendell had last seen thirty years ago, inked in a folio bound in his mother's handwriting, in a book he kept locked in a box under his floorboards and told himself he had inherited out of sentiment.

Sef looked up at him, dark eyes bottomless in the lamplight, and said one word. Just one. And Wendell understood it, and it terrified him more than the storm, more than the stones, more than anything the morning could bring.

"*Home,*" she said.
