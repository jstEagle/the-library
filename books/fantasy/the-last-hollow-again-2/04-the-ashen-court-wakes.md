## 4. The Ashen Court Wakes

The deadlands did not forgive a careless footfall, and Veyra Solenne had never once been careless.

She moved through the ash-gray scrub at the pace of the patient—three steps, listen, three steps, listen—her cloak the color of old bone and her breathing timed to the wind. Somewhere behind her, four days back now, a child had crossed a line that had not been crossed in thirty years. The covenant had felt it. Everyone in the Court had felt it. The Warden of Seals had stood up from his desk mid-sentence and said, *there*, as though he'd been stung by something under his collar, and then the summons had come down like weather.

*A leak. Find it. Close it.*

Veyra had been finding and closing things for the Ashen Court for nineteen years. She was very good at it, which was a fact she held the way one holds a knife: correctly, and without looking at it too long.

The trouble with this leak was that it bled wrong.

* * *

She found the first sign at the drowned orchard, where the trees stood black and leafless in a sink of brackish water, their branches bent at angles trees do not grow. Thirty years ago—or so the histories said—the last battle of the War of Ashes had ended here, not with a victor but with a silence. The covenant had fallen over the world like a lid on a pot, and everything beneath it had simmered down to gray.

Except that the water in the orchard was moving.

Not flowing. Moving. A slow rotation around the oldest tree, deliberate as a hand stirring tea, and along its surface floated petals. White petals. From black, dead branches.

Veyra crouched at the edge of the sink and did not touch anything, because touching things was how hunters died in stories, and she had no intention of becoming a story. She took out her ledger instead—a slim book bound in slate leather, its pages ruled in the Court's iron-gray ink—and wrote:

*Day 4. Orchard anomaly. Petal-fall from dormant wood. Water turning against current. Covenant strain local but deepening. Cause: unknown. Direction: north-northwest.*

Then, because no one would ever read the next line, because it lived only for her:

*It is beautiful.*

She looked at the sentence for a long moment. Then she closed the ledger and put it away, and told herself, as she always did, that beauty was simply another kind of danger—one you were allowed to notice before you killed it.

* * *

By evening she reached Hollowmere, or what passed for a village out here: eleven houses, a shrine with its god's name chiseled off, and an inn whose sign depicted a fish holding a sword, which struck Veyra as either profound heraldry or someone's idea of a joke two generations stale. She paid for a room she did not need and a stew she did not eat, and listened.

Hunters of the Ashen Court did not interrogate. Interrogation left marks. They sat near people, bought rounds, wore their hoods down so they seemed merely tired travelers, and let the villagers' own tongues do the bleeding.

"Strange season," said the innkeep's wife, wiping the same spot on the counter she'd been wiping since Veyra arrived. "Birds came back through last week."

"Birds," Veyra said.

"Migrating. Wrong direction, mind. Flying north into winter like they'd somewhere to be." The woman laughed, a small dry sound. "Old Tamper swore one landed on his fence and looked at him. Looked right at him, he says. Like it knew him."

"And then?"

"Flew on." She shrugged. "Tamper's been odd ever since. Says the sky feels thinner. Says—" She stopped herself, embarrassed, and wiped harder. "Well. He says a lot of things."

"Does he know which way the birds went?"

"North-northwest." The woman frowned slightly, surprised at herself, at the precision of her own memory. "Why do I remember that? Nobody remembers directions anymore. We just go where the roads are."

*Because the covenant is thinning,* Veyra thought, *and when it thins, things remember themselves.* She smiled at the woman, thanked her, and went upstairs to write in her ledger by candlelight, adding a third column of notes she kept in cipher even from herself some nights.

The war never ended. Every hunter learned this eventually, or suspected it, or drank until they forgot they suspected it. The covenant hadn't ended the War of Ashes—it had *paused* it, pressed the whole ruinous business flat between pages like a flower no one meant to look at again. The Court existed to keep the book closed. That was the catechism. That was the purpose. *We are the weight upon the lid.*

But a pause, unlike an ending, could fail. And lately—

Lately the birds remembered directions. The dead orchard turned its water. And somewhere north-northwest, something small and leaking had crossed thirty years of sealed border like a needle through cloth.

* * *

On the sixth day she caught up to the trail's edge, and there she met Corvane.

He was waiting for her at the crossroads shrine, sitting on the plinth of the nameless god as though it were a garden bench, his long legs crossed and his hood thrown back. He was handsome in the way of well-made weapons, and he was smiling, and Veyra's stomach performed the small cold drop it always performed at the sight of him.

"Hunter Solenne," he said. "Four hundred miles of dead country, and I find you writing in your little book."

"You walked past me?" She kept her voice level. "I didn't hear you."

"No one ever does." He tilted his head. "That used to worry you. It doesn't anymore. Interesting."

"What do you want, Corvane?"

"To walk with you awhile. The Warden thought you might be lonely." He slid off the plinth with liquid ease. "And I thought I might see the leak myself. It's all anyone talks about at court. The child, they're calling it. A child walking out of nowhere speaking Old Tongue like it never went away."

"A rumor."

"A rumor with petals on the water behind it." His smile sharpened. "You've seen them too."

Veyra said nothing, which was answer enough, and Corvane laughed softly, delighted, and fell into step beside her as if invited.

They walked. The scrubland gave way to broken hills, and the broken hills to the pale scar of the old road, running straight toward mountains that were not on any map the Court admitted to having.

"Do you know what I miss?" Corvane said conversationally, after a mile or two of silence. "Fire. Real fire. Everything out here is ash and memory. But before the covenant—" he spread his hands, and for a moment Veyra saw it, the thing he actually was underneath the pleasant face: hunger wearing patience, "—before, a man like me could hold a flame in his fist and speak to it, and it would speak back. Do you remember? No. Of course you don't. You were a child. You think the gray world is the world."

"The gray world is the peaceful world."

"It's the *quiet* world. Peace is different." He glanced sideways at her. "You doubt us. You've doubted us for years. I've read your ledger reports—they're immaculate, every fact correct, and yet somehow each one arrives smelling faintly of reluctance. How do you do that? It's a gift."

"My reports are accurate."

"They are. That's what makes them damning." He stopped walking. So, reluctantly, did she. Ahead, the road climbed toward a pass where the air shimmered very faintly, like heat, except it was nearly winter and the shimmer moved against the wind. Toward the valley. Toward whatever lay inside it.

"Here is what you should know, Solenne, since you'll learn it anyway and I'd rather you heard it from someone with a face." Corvane's voice dropped, warm and terrible. "The Warden isn't sending retrieval parties to close the leak. Retrieval implies return. The Court means to take the child and use what's in it—the tongue, the blood, whatever door it walked through—to renegotiate terms. A paused war is a war nobody won. The Court intends, finally, to win."

"Win," Veyra repeated. "A war of ashes. Against whom? There's nothing left to fight. The other side burned itself out centuries ago."

"Did it?" Corvane smiled again. "Then why does the whole border smell of them waking up? Why did the seals crack in sequence, north to south, like knuckles popping? Something crossed, little hunter. Something old enough to have children who remember the Old Tongue. And our side intends to meet it *armed*." He resumed walking. After a moment, she followed, because the alternative was standing alone at a crossroads with her doubts, and doubts were heavier than company.

At the crest of the pass he paused once more. "When we find them—and we will find them; the leak leads to a valley, and valleys only have so many doors—you'll have a choice to make. Hunters always do, at the end. I hope you make the interesting one." He touched two fingers to his brow in mock salute. "Keep writing in your book. History likes a witness."

Then he was gone between the rocks, without sound, without tracks, and Veyra stood in the wind feeling the way she had felt at seven years old when the Warden's men took her from the burning village she barely remembered: certain that she had been rescued, and unable, even now, to say from what.

* * *

That night she made camp in the lee of the old road's milestone—a stone so worn its distances were illegible, pointing everywhere and nowhere. She built no fire. She opened her ledger to a fresh page, and beneath the day's careful entries she drew a line, and beneath the line she began a second ledger, the private one, the one that lived inside the first:

*Corvane walks ahead of me. The Court wants the child as a weapon. The seals are failing north to south, which means the pause is ending whether anyone chose it or not. The birds remember north-northwest. The water turns. The petals fall upward.*

*Question I am not permitted to ask: if the covenant breaks, and the world wakes, and everything the Court buried comes back—what exactly have I been protecting all these years? And what have I been protecting it from?*

She sat with the pen hovering. Far to the northwest, past the dark hills, a light flickered in a valley that should have shown no lights at all—brief, ordinary, human. A lamp in a window. Someone home.

The covenant was supposed to keep such places safe. Instead, she realized, watching the light gutter and steady, the covenant had made the whole world a locked room, and put the last living thing worth protecting inside it, and then sent men like Corvane to find the key.

Veyra closed the ledger. In the morning she would follow the trail north-northwest, as ordered, as always.

But she wrote one more line first, small, at the bottom of the page, in ink so pale it might have been an accident:

*If I reach them first, I will decide what 'closing the leak' means.*

Then she slept, badly, and dreamed of white petals rising off black water like a slow applause, and woke before dawn with the unshakable feeling that somewhere ahead of her, a cartographer was about to run.
