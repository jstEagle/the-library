## 5. Borders Are Promises

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a friendly knock.

Wendell knew all the valley's knocks the way he knew its weather. There was the baker's cheerful double-rap, the tax-collector's apologetic shuffle, Ilsa's particular three-beat that meant *I have brought soup and opinions, in that order.* This was none of these. This was a fist, and behind the fist were boots, and behind the boots were four watchmen and Alderman Corvus Hale, who wore his chain of office even to bed, as far as anyone could prove.

Wendell opened the door with the expression of a man who had absolutely nothing in his back room except dried mushrooms and regret.

"Master Oyelaran," said Hale. "We're given to understand you have a guest."

"A guest." Wendell let the word sit there like a stone he was pretending not to see. "I live alone, Alderman. Unless you count the moths, and we have an arrangement rather than a friendship."

Hale's eyes went past his shoulder, patient as winter. Behind him, one of the watchmen—Tam, who had been a boy when Wendell arrived and was now old enough to be tired of it—shifted his weight and looked genuinely sorry about the whole business.

"The miller's daughter saw her," Hale said. "In your garden. Two nights past. A child, in the snow, walking out of nowhere at all."

"Children wander."

"Not *out* of nowhere. Nothing comes out of nowhere, Master Oyelaran. That is rather the founding principle of this valley." Hale smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "The council convenes at mid-morning. We would be grateful if you attended. We would be more grateful if she came with you."

They left. Wendell closed the door and stood with his forehead against the wood for a long moment, listening to the house settle around him like an old dog turning over.

In the back room, Sef sat on the floor among his map scrolls, cross-legged, drawing. She had been drawing since before dawn—the same shapes over and over: a circle broken by a line, a tower with no door, a river that forked and then, impossibly, joined itself again. When he came in she looked up, and her face did the thing it always did now, the small hopeful tilt of a creature deciding whether this particular human was still safe.

"I'm not angry at you," Wendell told her, and found to his surprise that it was true. "I'm angry at the situation, which is a coward's anger, because situations can't hear you."

She held up her drawing. The broken circle. She tapped it, then tapped the floor, then tapped her own chest.

"I know," Wendell said quietly. "I know what you are saying. I just don't know how to say anything back yet."

He had spent two days trying not to understand her, and had failed completely. The language came up through him like water through chalk—not learned but *remembered*, syllables surfacing from some cellar of himself he had never been given a key to. He understood her the way he understood his own heartbeat. It terrified him more than the storm that had delivered her, more than the shifted border stones, more than the fact that his oldest folio—a book his mother had pressed into his hands at nine years old and sworn him never to open—matched the child's drawings stroke for stroke.

And now the valley wanted her.

"Wendell." Sef spoke—it was still startling every time, that grave, careful voice—and pointed toward the front of the house, where the council waited. Then she pointed at the door, and then, with terrible clarity, she drew one finger across her throat.

"You've got a flair for the dramatic," Wendell said. "You must get it from my side of things."

But she wasn't being dramatic. She was being eight years old and certain, and the certainty made his hands cold.

* * *

The council hall smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool, as it had for thirty years, as it presumably had for three hundred. Eleven elders sat along the curved table. Ilsa occupied the seat nearest the fire, ostensibly because of her age and actually because she had won the argument about the fire in 1989 and nobody had dared reopen negotiations since.

Sef stood beside Wendell, small and straight-backed, wearing one of his shirts belted with rope, which somehow made her look more dignified than anyone else in the room.

"Master Oyelaran," Hale began, "this council has protected this valley for generations by keeping one law above all others: nothing crosses the border stones. No magic. No strangers. No *leaks*." He pronounced the last word carefully, the way one handles something that might be either scripture or ordnance. "And here we find a child who appeared from beyond the stones, whom you concealed, who speaks—" he consulted a paper, though everyone knew he had memorized it "—a language no scholar in Greywater could identify. The pre-kingdom tongue. Or so the peddler swears, and the peddler is wrong about everything except prices."

"She was lost in the storm," Wendell said. "She's mute—"

"She spoke to the miller's daughter."

"—she was *lost*," Wendell repeated, and heard his own voice do something new. Steadier than he felt. Warmer than he felt. "A child, alone, freezing. I took her in. I fed her. If the council's law requires me to have put her back out into the snow to die, then I'd ask the council to say so plainly, so I can hear what it is we actually keep here."

Silence. Somewhere a log popped. Elder Marsh, who had once fined Wendell for mapping on a rest-day, looked down at her hands.

"The law exists," Hale said, softer now, which was worse, "because the covenant holds while we hold it. Your family knows that better than most, Oyelaran. Your mother—"

"My mother kept maps," Wendell said. "That's all any of us do. Maps and borders and promises. And promises are only worth what we pay for them." He heard the words come out of his mouth and had no idea whose they were. They sounded old. They sounded inherited. "She stays until the roads clear. Then she goes wherever she's going, and the valley forgets her, as it forgets everything. Isn't that our gift?"

It was a lie braided into a truth, and it worked the way such things work: not by deceiving them but by giving them permission to stop looking. Thirty years of telling these people the exact dimensions of every realm beyond their stones, thirty years of scrupulous, exhausting honesty—and the first lie slid through clean as a knife through warm butter. He hated how easy it was. He hated how good he was at it.

Hale studied him for a long moment. "Until the roads clear," he said finally. "Then she goes. And Master Oyelaran—if anything further comes out of the hills following her, the council will want to speak with you again. At length." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Sef. Something moved behind them that was not cruelty exactly. Fear, maybe, dressed in ceremony. "This council adjourned."

* * *

Ilsa caught him in the corridor afterward, catching his sleeve with fingers like bird talons and steering him into the alcove where the brooms lived, which she treated as her private office on the grounds that nobody else wanted it.

"Well," she said. "Thirty years."

"Of what?"

"Honesty. Mostly." She looked up at him—Ilsa was five feet nothing and had been since before Wendell was born—and her eyes were doing something he had never seen them do. They were sad. Not the ordinary sadness of the old; a specific sadness, with a shape, like grief that had been waiting a long time for its appointment. "You lied beautifully, Wendell. I nearly believed you myself. And I taught you your letters."

"Ilsa—"

"I know what your blood is."

The broom closet went very quiet. Outside, someone laughed; inside, the sound died a foot away from the walls, as if the room itself were holding its breath.

"Your mother didn't just keep maps," Ilsa said. "She came here to hide them. To hide *you*. I was younger then, and I asked fewer questions, and I watched her feed those border stones for eleven years, and I watched the stones take and take and take, and I watched her get smaller every winter like a candle burning at both ends." Her grip tightened on his sleeve. "When she died, I promised her I'd keep you. Not your secret. *You.* There's a difference. I've spent thirty years pretending there wasn't."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because today you lied for that child the way your mother lied for you. And because—" Ilsa exhaled, and for a moment she looked every one of her hundred-odd years, and then she squared herself, which was the most familiar thing about her. "Because Hale sent a rider to Greywater last night. Before the council met. Asking after the pre-kingdom tongue."

Wendell's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the foundation, through whatever lay beneath valleys. "Greywater keeps archives. Scholars. If word travels from Greywater—"

"It'll travel further than Greywater. Word about children like her always does." Ilsa patted his arm briskly, the way she did when delivering bad news she considered self-evident. "So. The roads south are clear enough by tomorrow if you leave before light. Take the folio. All of it. And Wendell—" she reached up and, with great firmness, straightened his collar, an act of tenderness disguised entirely as criticism "—you will need provisions, and I have soup, and we will not discuss the fact that I'm crying, because I am not, my eyes are simply old."

"They're leaking, Ilsa."

"Old houses leak. Mind your own architecture."

She bustled off toward her cottage to pack, spine straight, chin up, betraying nothing—which was, Wendell understood suddenly, the whole of how she had loved him: loudly, fiercely, and in perfect secrecy, for thirty years.

Behind him, from the shadow of the stairwell, Sef stepped out. She had heard everything, or enough of it. She held up her drawing—the broken circle—and then pointed, deliberately, out the window, toward the hills. Toward the unmapped lands beyond the valley's edge, the ones he had drawn ten thousand times and never walked.

Then she looked at him, and said the first full sentence he had ever heard from her, in the tongue that rose in him like memory:

*"They are coming. The ash-walkers. We go tonight."*

Far off, past the border stones, past the deadlands, something answered—a sound like a bell rung underwater, low and long, and every hair on Wendell's arms stood up, because he recognized it from his mother's folio, from a page labeled in her handwriting:

*What hunts the key.*

He went to find his boots.
