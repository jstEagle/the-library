## 6. The Unmapped Road

Wendell had spent thirty years drawing roads he never walked, and now, on the first morning of his exile, the road refused to be drawn at all.

They had left before dawn, taking the shepherd's path past the eastern border stone—the one that had shifted, all those weeks ago, by a hand's width toward home. He had told himself it was nothing. He had told himself a great many things over thirty years. Behind him, the valley lay under a lid of grey mist, and Ilsa stood at its edge with her arms folded and her jaw set in the particular way that meant *go, and don't you dare look back until you're over the ridge.* He looked back anyway. She pretended not to notice.

Sef walked beside him with a knapsack nearly as large as she was, containing: one apple (cored), three river stones of no obvious significance, a wooden bird whose wings had been rubbed smooth by someone's long-ago thumb, and—this had been non-negotiable—a rolled sheet of Wendell's oldest map, the forbidden folio page she had matched her drawings to. She carried it the way other children carried blankets.

"Stay close," he said, in her tongue, which came out of his mouth like water finding an old channel. He still did not know why he could speak it. He suspected he would not enjoy the answer.

She stayed close for exactly as long as it took them to reach the first fork, at which point she stopped, tilted her head at the two paths, and said something brisk and decisive that Wendell understood perfectly as: *That way is wrong. It hasn't happened yet.*

"That way," he said, consulting his map, "leads to the Marrow Ford. I mapped it from the surveys. It's a day's walk, good footing, water at the end."

Sef gave him the look that eight-year-olds have apparently perfected in every language since the invention of children: the look reserved for adults who insist on facts. Then she took his sleeve and pulled him onto the *other* path—the one his maps showed terminating in a cliff face somewhere around the old quarries.

The path did not terminate. It climbed, sweetly, through birches that were supposed to be pines, and near midday they crested a rise and looked down on the Marrow Ford glittering below them, reached in half the time by a route that had never existed on any survey Wendell had ever copied.

He sat down on a rock and stared at his map for a long time.

"Well," he said finally. "That's humiliating."

Sef patted his knee. It was possibly the most comforting thing anyone had done for him in a decade.

---

By the second day, Wendell had stopped trusting his own ink.

It wasn't only that the terrain disagreed with the maps. It was that the terrain seemed to be having a conversation about it. A stream they crossed in the morning was on the left side of the trail when they came back that way after lunch—not flooded, not moved, simply *relocated*, with the cheerful indifference of furniture rearranged overnight. A stand of alders he had noted as a landmark was now a stand of rowans, and the rowans, he could have sworn, were slightly smug about it.

And always, always, Sef hummed as she walked—a tuneless little melody, five notes, over and over—and whenever she hummed, Wendell felt the world go soft at the edges, like wet paper.

He began keeping a running tally in the margin of his folio. *Ford: +half day gained. Alders→rowans. North star: present, but shy.* By evening of the third day the tally had become a kind of diary, and the diary had become, without his quite noticing, a confession:

*I have mapped the world from inside a jar. The world is not in the jar.*

He read it back to himself by firelight while Sef slept, curled against the wooden bird, and he thought of his mother, who had burned her drafts in the same ritual he still performed, and who had never once, in all his childhood, let him follow her to the border stones. He had assumed it was protectiveness. Sitting here, outside the jar, watching a hill politely change its mind, he began to suspect it had been embarrassment—or worse, fear. His family had known what maps were for. And it was not recording the world.

* * *

On the fourth day, they met the river that wasn't there.

His folio showed the Vess, broad and slow, meandering east toward the low country. What they found instead was a young river, fast and narrow, cutting a gorge so fresh the banks were still raw earth, as if the land had only just decided on it—and perhaps it had. Sef stopped at the lip of the gorge and made a sound Wendell hadn't heard from her before: a laugh, startled out of her, delighted, both hands pressed to her mouth.

"What," said Wendell, "did you do."

She pointed at the river, then at herself, then shrugged with the magnificent innocence of a child standing next to a broken vase. But she didn't look innocent. She looked *awake*, in a way that frightened him more than the Ashen Court ever could—awake the way he imagined a caged bird feels the first time the door stands open and it isn't sure yet whether the sky is real.

"We can't cross that," he said. "There's no bridge. There's no bridge because this river is four days old."

Sef considered the problem with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she knelt at the gorge's edge, picked up a flat stone, and set it on the ground between them—and set another beside it, and another, arranging them in a curve that Wendell recognized, with a lurch, as the shape of a span. Not a drawing of a bridge. An instruction.

"You're joking," he told her.

She looked up at him, and her eyes were the grey-green of deep water, and she said one word in the old tongue, softly, the way one wakes a sleeper: *Rise.*

The stones rose.

Not dramatically—there was no thunder, no light. They simply lifted, quietly, the way dust motes lift when a window opens, and drifted out over the gorge and settled into the water at stepping-stone intervals, and where they settled, more stone gathered to them, rising from the riverbed like a memory of a bridge that had always wanted to exist. In less time than it took Wendell's heart to beat ten times, there was a crossing under their feet, rough and real and faintly warm.

Wendell did not cross it. He stood on the bank with his folio open to a blank page and his pen shaking in his hand, and he understood, all at once and completely, what he was looking at.

The world was not refusing to hold still. The world was *listening*. It had been listening for thirty years to a valley where magic went to die, and now it was listening to a child who spoke the language it had been built in—and everything Sef touched grew a little more possible. The river was new because she had needed a river to be interesting. The bridge was real because she had asked.

And his maps—the ones he had inherited, the ones he had copied all his life—they weren't records of this. They were *leashes*. Every line a binding, every border a promise the land had been forced to keep. He had spent his life maintaining the cage and calling it cartography.

His mother had burned her drafts because drafts were the only honest maps she was allowed to make.

"Wendell," Sef said—his name, the only word of his language she had kept, worn smooth from use—and tugged his sleeve. The light was going. The gorge wind smelled of snow again. Whatever was coming behind them, it was coming faster than the weather.

He stepped onto the bridge. It held him like a handshake.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a rowan tree—one of Sef's rowans, he noted, though whether she'd grown it or merely claimed it, he couldn't say. He cooked barley and salt pork, and she ate hers and half of his, and afterward she unrolled her treasured map page on the ground between them and smoothed it flat with both small palms.

Then she did the thing she had begun doing each night: she drew.

Charcoal stub, quick strokes. Tonight she drew the bridge—yesterday's bridge, already behind them—and above it she drew a shape that made Wendell's blood go cold and hot at once. A tower. Square-shouldered, crowned with a broken ring, its door drawn open.

He knew the tower. Every line of it. It stood on page one of his mother's folio, drawn in her careful, disciplined hand—the Archive at Corren Vale, the Binding Room, the ruin where the covenant had been written in blood older than kingdoms. He had mapped it a hundred times from secondhand accounts and never understood why his mother had rendered it with such precision, such *intimacy*, as if she'd stood in its doorway herself.

Sef tapped the tower. Then she pointed east, along the young river, toward the mountains.

"No," Wendell said.

She pointed again, patient, insistent, the way she had pointed at the wrong fork four days ago. *That way is right. It hasn't happened yet.*

"Sef, listen to me." He took her hands, gently, and her fingers were cold as river stones. "That place is where the war was *written*. If the Court is hunting you—and they are, they've been a day behind us since the ford, don't think I haven't seen the birds that watch too long—if we walk into the one place their whole rotten covenant comes from—"

She pulled one hand free and pressed it flat against his chest, over his sternum, over the blood in it, and held it there. Her eyes didn't waver.

He understood her. That was the curse of it; he understood her completely. She wasn't saying *I want to go.* She was saying: *You are a key. I am a key. Keys belong in doors, and there is a door that has been waiting for you longer than I've been alive.*

Outside, high on the dark ridge behind them, a bird called—three notes, wrong for any bird of these woods, repeated twice. Then again, closer.

Sef's hand stilled against his chest. She knew the sound too. Her drawings had shown it days ago: a tall figure in ash-grey, walking without bending, with a ledger chained at its hip.

Wendell looked east, at the mountains going blue-black under the stars, at the road his mother had drawn and he had copied and the world had waited thirty years for him to walk. Then he looked west, at the dark, at the patient hunters, at the valley that had forgotten how to hold magic and might, if he turned back, forgive him everything except the truth.

He blew out the lantern.

"We leave before dawn," he whispered, in the old tongue. "And Sef—when we get there, whatever your door wants from me—"

He didn't finish. He couldn't. She squeezed his hand once, which was either comfort or a promise or both, and somewhere behind them on the ridge, the bird stopped calling, because it no longer needed to call. It had heard them.
