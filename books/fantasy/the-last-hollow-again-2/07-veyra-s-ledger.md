## 7. Veyra's Ledger

The village was called Tarnlow, which Veyra knew because she had burned its name into her memory three days ago, when the tracking-sigil on her wrist had flared like a struck match and pointed her off the deadland road toward a cluster of rooftops huddled against the winter hills.

She rode in alone. That was policy—hunters traveled in pairs, but pairs frightened villagers into lies, and a single woman with a tired horse and an honest face got you further than any truth-spell the Court's arcanists had ever brewed. Her partner, Doss, would circle wide and wait at the far treeline, bored and chewing pine resin, and if things went badly he would come in loud, which was the whole point of him.

Tarnlow smelled of woodsmoke and sheep and, faintly, of something else. Something old. The sigil on her wrist itched under her glove, and Veyra had learned over eleven years of service that the itch meant *close*.

She stopped at the well first, because wells were where villages kept their gossip, and found exactly what she expected: three women drawing water, one old man mending a net that hadn't seen a river in years, and four separate conversations dying the moment her shadow crossed the cobbles.

"Traveler," said the oldest of the women, in the tone people used for wolves dressed as grandmothers.

"Pilgrim," Veyra corrected, dismounting. "Bound for the high passes, if they're passable."

"Not in winter." The old man didn't look up from his net. "Not for pilgrims, not for anyone."

"And yet I hear you had visitors recently." She smiled the smile that wasn't one. "A tall man. Gray at the temples. Carries himself like someone who apologizes to furniture. And a little girl."

The net-mending stopped.

There it was. Eleven years of this work had taught Veyra that silence was just a confession wearing a hat. She let it sit between them, patient as frost, until the youngest of the women—the one whose hands trembled on the bucket rope—said, "We don't know anyone like that."

"No," Veyra agreed pleasantly. "You fed them anyway."

* * *

They gave her the barn loft for the night, because refusing shelter to a pilgrim was bad luck in these hills and worse manners, and because the headman—a broad, worried man named Corvel who sweated even in the cold—hoped kindness might buy discretion. Veyra accepted, thanked him warmly, and then spent the evening doing what hunters did best: listening.

She listened while she ate barley stew in Corvel's kitchen. She listened while his wife scrubbed pots and his daughter pretended to sleep behind the curtain. She listened to the pauses, which were more eloquent than the words. The girl had been given bread. The tall man had fixed their loom without being asked—he'd *apologized to the loom*, the daughter whispered through the curtain, delighted—and they'd stayed two nights before slipping out before dawn toward the old archive road.

"They seemed harmless," Corvel said carefully. "The man talked about maps. Rivers and borders. My wife thought he was mad until he drew our valley from memory, every farmstead right, and he's never been here before."

"He's a cartographer," Veyra said. "Of a sort."

"A dangerous sort?"

Veyra turned her spoon in the stew. "He's a man carrying something that belongs to my employers. They want it back. No one needs to be hurt."

It was the standard line, and it had the standard effect, which was none. Corvel nodded the way men nod when they've decided to lie to your face forever, and Veyra thanked him for the stew, and went up to the loft, and lay in the hay listening to the horses shift below, and did not sleep.

Because here was the thing she had not said aloud in eleven years: *no one needs to be hurt* was the sentence the Court taught its hunters, and it was also the sentence that preceded nearly every hurt the Court had ever ordered.

* * *

At midnight she searched the house. Not the house itself—she'd already done that, quickly, professionally—but the outbuilding behind it, the one Corvel had walked past twice without looking at, which was how guilty men walk past things.

Inside: wool, winter apples, a broken sled. And on a nail, tucked behind a coil of rope, a child's cloak. Too small for anyone in Corvel's family. Too thin for this weather. And along its hem, worked into the stitching in thread so fine it looked like nothing unless you held it to lamplight, a pattern.

Veyra knew patterns. It was half her trade. This one was a border-script, the old kind, the pre-kingdom kind—the kind that appeared in Court archives only under seal and triple lock. A warding stitch. A mother's work, or a grandmother's. Someone had loved this child enough to sew protection into her clothes, which told Veyra two things: the child came from people who still remembered the old craft, and those people were very afraid of something.

She sat down on the sled and looked at the little cloak for a long time.

Then she took out her ledger.

Every hunter kept a ledger. It was regulation: date, location, sigil-readings, subjects sighted, actions taken. Hers was a handsome black-bound thing with the Court's ash-tree sigil stamped in silver, and it contained eleven years of entries written in her neat, narrow hand. She opened it now, by lamplight, and read backward.

*Subject located, Hollow border region. Sigil flare, duration 4 hours.* — six months ago.
*Sigil flare, unexplained, duration 11 hours. Requested clarification from Archivist-Marshal. Response: 'Continue monitoring.'*

Eleven hours. She had requested clarification and been told to keep watching. She flipped further back. Three years ago, a village much like this one, much like this night. Two subjects then—an old woman and a boy. She had brought them in. She remembered the boy's hands, small as the cloak's sleeves, and she remembered the Archivist-Marshal saying *the covenant holds, thanks to you,* and she remembered asking, because she was young then and still asked questions, *what does the covenant cost?*

The answer had been a look. She had stopped asking questions after that.

Tonight, sitting on a broken sled in a stranger's barn, holding a child's warded cloak, Veyra finally wrote the question down instead:

*What are we actually collecting?*

And beneath it, because honesty had gotten hold of her hand and wouldn't let go:

*And why does no one above my rank ever say the word 'return'? We never say we'll return them. We say 'recover,' 'secure,' 'contain.' You recover property. You contain threats.*

Her wrist-sigil itched. She pushed the glove cuff back and studied it in the lamplight—the fine gray lines of the tracking-bind, inked into her skin at induction, the mark every hunter wore. She had always assumed it tracked the leaks. Tonight, for the first time, she wondered whether anything else was being tracked through it, or *by* it, and whether the distinction mattered.

She closed the official ledger.

Then she tore three blank pages from the back of a ballad-book she'd bought in a market town years ago and never read, folded them inside her coat, and began, on the first page, in the same neat hand, a second ledger. Private. Unregistered. Treasonous.

*Entry the first,* she wrote. *Tarnlow. Sheltered them two nights. Fed the child. Fixed their loom. The villagers lie badly and kindly. The cloak is warded in pre-kingdom script, which means the child's people never surrendered, whatever the histories say.*

She paused, pen hovering.

*The histories say the war ended. I have served the peace for eleven years and I have never once been asked to protect anything. Only to collect. Tonight I am writing down that I noticed.*

Below that, smaller: *The tall man apologized to a loom. The Archivist-Marshal has never apologized to anyone.*

* * *

In the morning she reported to Doss at the treeline, and Doss reported by courier-sigil to the waystation, and by noon the reply came back down the relay, cold and crisp as the air: *Subjects proceeding toward the Binding Room ruins on the archive road. Vanguard dispatched. Do not engage. Observe and ensure the asset is delivered intact.*

Veyra read it twice.

*Asset.*

Not *the child*. Not even *the subject*. And *delivered intact*—phrasing for cargo, for crates, for things without names. And *vanguard dispatched*: three companies of the Court's regulars, the ones in the ash-gray coats who did not apologize to furniture or looms or anything at all.

She looked up at the white peaks beyond the trees, where the archive road climbed toward the ruined Binding Room, and tried to do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all night. If the goal was to end the old war—to keep it ended—you sent scholars. You sent archivists, negotiators, maybe one hunter to mind the door. You did not send a vanguard.

If the goal was to restart the war on terms you liked—if you needed a key of pre-kingdom blood, young enough to shape, frightened enough to obey—then you sent soldiers. Then you made sure the child arrived intact.

Doss ambled up beside her, resin-stained teeth, cheerful as ever. "Good news? You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to ruin somebody's day."

Veyra folded the courier slip away. "Just orders," she said. "We're to observe."

"Easy duty." He spat, contentedly. "Though I'll tell you what's strange. Third leak this decade. You'd think with a covenant that strong there wouldn't *be* leaks anymore. Almost like somebody up top wants them leaking."

Veyra looked at him. Doss was many things—lazy, talkative, fond of bad wine—but stupid was not among them, and sometimes he said true things sideways, the way drunks and children and wise men do.

"Almost," she agreed.

That evening she added to the private ledger, sitting in the loft again, lamplight low, the warded cloak folded beside her like evidence or like a promise:

*Entry the second. The Court calls her an asset. The vanguard moves at dawn. Whatever is waiting at the Binding Room, it is not peace.*

*I have decided something, though I will not write it plainly yet, because ledgers get found and plainness gets people killed. I will write it the way the child's grandmother stitched that hem: fine, and hidden, and strong enough to hold.*

*Tomorrow I follow the archive road. I am supposed to be watching them.*

*Instead, I find I am watching us.*

Far to the north, past the last farms and the frozen fords, the mountains stood white against a bruised sky, and somewhere along the archive road, a tall man who apologized to furniture was walking a mute girl toward the place where everything had been bound—and Veyra tightened her bootlaces in the dark, and prepared to be the worst hunter the Ashen Court had ever employed.
