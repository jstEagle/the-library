## 10. A Map With No Edges

The first thing to go was the name of the river.

Wendell knew this because he had written it down. That was the trick he'd learned in the early weeks, before he understood what he was losing: write everything down, then write down that you wrote it down. His study had become a nest of papers, each one a small lifeboat. *The river through the valley is called the Merren.* He read it every morning like a prayer, though the word *Merren* now tasted foreign on his tongue, a stranger's coin.

He still knew how to draw it, though. The pen remembered when the man forgot.

He sat at his desk in the grey light of an autumn morning and drew the valley—its borders, its stones, its slow green heart—and found that his hand kept going past the edge of the page. The line ran off the paper and onto the desk, and he let it, because stopping felt like lying.

*You unbound it,* said the note pinned above his inkwell, in his own handwriting, from some morning he no longer retained. *This was your choice. You would make it again. — W.*

He touched the ink. Still wet, somehow. Still true.

Outside, someone was laughing. Children, probably. There were more sounds in the valley these days—the borders had opened like a fist unclenching, and magic had come back the way spring comes: not all at once, not violently, but in small insistences. Moss grew a shade too green near the stones. The well water sang if you listened long enough. Old Marta's bread rose twice as high as it used to, and she complained about it, which everyone agreed was the surest sign that the world had healed.

Wendell did not remember doing the unbinding. He remembered fragments—a ruined archive, a girl's terrified voice speaking a language that made the walls bleed light, a hunter named Veyra turning her blade against her own Court. But the center of it, the moment his blood met the old covenant, was gone. It had been the price. He had known that going in—or so his notes insisted, in increasingly exasperated handwriting, as though past-Wendell had anticipated exactly how much future-Wendell would want to take it back.

*I mean it,* said one note. *Do not go digging in the Binding Room. You'll only frighten yourself.*

He went digging in the Binding Room twice more before he stopped. The second time, Ilsa found him there, sitting among the shattered ledgers, holding a page that described—in his mother's hand, in her careful cartographer's script—what his blood had fed for nine hundred years.

"Come home," Ilsa said.

"I don't know where that is," he said honestly.

She took his arm the way you take the arm of someone who might bolt. "Then follow me. You always were hopeless with directions. All those maps, and not a shred of sense of where you are."

That, at least, was familiar enough to laugh at.

* * *

Sef stayed with Ilsa.

This had been decided in a conversation Wendell only half-remembered, but the arrangement itself was easy to observe, because Sef had annexed Ilsa's cottage the way small children annex everything: completely, and with total confidence. She had drawings pinned to every wall—drawings of the valley, of the border stones, of Veyra standing very straight with her hands folded like a woman being photographed against her will. And everywhere, in her pre-kingdom tongue that was slowly braiding itself into the valley's common speech, new words were growing like seedlings.

"She asked me yesterday what my name was *before*," Ilsa told him over tea. "Before the covenant. What people called me when names meant something older." She stirred her cup. "I told her I didn't know. And do you know what she did? She gave me one. In the old tongue."

"What does it mean?"

"'She who keeps the door.'" Ilsa smiled, and beneath the smile was grief worn smooth as river stone. "I've been keeping doors my whole life, Wendell. Closed ones, mostly. It's strange to have one open."

Wendell looked at his tea. There were things he could almost reach—the shape of a lie he'd told a council, the weight of a child asleep against his shoulder on a cold road—but they slid away like fish. He had learned not to grab. Grabbing only churned the water.

"Does she ask about me?" he asked. "About what I—"

"Every day." Ilsa set down her cup with deliberate gentleness. "And every day I tell her the same thing: that you gave something enormous so she could be free, and that you don't regret it, and that regret isn't required for grief anyway. You're allowed to mourn a man you can't remember being."

"That seems indulgent."

"It seems *necessary*. There's a difference." She leaned forward. "Here is what I know about you, Wendell Oyelaran, whatever's left of the name: thirty years ago you came to this valley to hide from a war you'd already lost something to. You built a life out of routine and ink and distance. And when the moment came, when hiding would have saved you, you walked out the gate. Whatever else the covenant ate, it didn't eat that. That part is structural."

He wanted to argue. Instead he said, "I wrote that down once. *Structural.* I think."

"You wrote it on my pantry door. In ink. I've decided to forgive you."

* * *

Veyra came at midwinter, as promised, though Wendell had needed the promise written down to remember it existed. She arrived with frost in her dark hair and a ledger under her arm, and she stood in his doorway looking at him the way she always did—as if confirming a hypothesis she hoped was wrong.

"The Ashen Court is dissolved," she said. "Formally. The vanguard surrendered at the archive. Your notes—" she tapped her ledger, "—your notes say you'll want the details, so I brought them. All of them. Dates, names, the works."

"You didn't have to."

"Yes, I did. That's rather the point of a witness." She hesitated, then stepped inside, stamping snow from her boots. "The new covenant needs keepers, Wendell. Not binders—keepers. People who remember what the old price was, so no one pays it again. The magic that's coming back—it's freer than anything has been in nine centuries, and free things need tending or they get frightened."

"And you're volunteering?"

"I'm volunteering." Her mouth twitched. "I spent twenty years hunting leaks in a dam. I'd like to spend the next twenty making sure nobody builds another one."

They worked together through the winter afternoons, Veyra dictating and Wendell writing, and here he discovered the strangest mercy of his condition: he could not hold memories, but he could make them legible for others. Every fact about the old war, the binding, the bloodlines—all of it passed through his pen into pages that would outlast his forgetting. He was, he realized one evening with something like wonder, doing the only thing he had ever truly done. Mapping. Just now the territory was the past, and the map was for people who came after.

"You're smiling," Veyra observed suspiciously.

"I'm having a thought. Write it down before it goes."

* * *

Spring came to the valley the way it always had, except more so.

The border stones no longer marked an ending; travelers rubbed their surfaces smooth passing back and forth. Peddlers arrived selling charms that actually charmed. A wandering musician played in the square, and the notes hung in the air afterward like visible things, and nobody minded, because that was simply what music did now.

Wendell walked the valley most mornings with his notebook, drawing what he saw, though the drawings had changed. They used to be precise—every fencepost, every roofline, the cartographer's compulsion. Now they were looser, warmer, full of margins where he noted things like *Marta's dog can bark in two languages now, apparently* and *the willow by the Merren—no. The willow by the river—no.* He would cross it out and just draw the willow.

Some mornings he woke without knowing why he was in this valley, in this house, among these people. The panic would come, cold and complete—I don't belong here, I don't know these faces—and then Ilsa would put tea in front of him, and Sef would show him a drawing, and Veyra would arrive with her ledger, and the panic would find nothing to hold onto. Because belonging, he was learning, was not a memory. It was a practice. It was tea poured daily and names relearned patiently and a door left unlocked because someone always came back to it.

One bright morning in late spring, he sat on his doorstep with Sef, who was teaching him words in her old tongue. She pointed at the river. Said a word. Made him repeat it until he got it right, laughing at his failures with the merciless joy of eight-year-olds everywhere.

"What's it mean?" he asked.

She considered. "It means *the water that keeps going even when it forgets where it started*."

"That's a lot of meaning for one word."

"That's why your language needs three." She patted his hand, patronizingly, the way he supposed he'd once patted hers.

He looked out at the valley—the green going gold at its edges, the smoke rising straight from Ilsa's chimney, the border stones standing open like held breath finally released. Somewhere inside him was a man who had chosen all of this, who had paid for it with himself, who had maybe been afraid and brave in the same breath. Wendell couldn't find that man anymore. But he could feel the shape he'd left, the way a bed holds warmth after its sleeper rises.

"Why did I stay?" he asked the morning, quietly. "I never wrote that down. Why did I stay?"

Sef followed his gaze to the willow, the river, the open hills beyond.

"Because it's home," she said, as if it were the simplest word in any language.

And it was. He checked his notes to be sure. It was.
