## 2. Charms for a Lawless City

Seven years ago, I learned that memory has a weight. Not the weight of a stone or a coin—memory weighs like water. It shifts. It finds the cracks in you and pools there, and if you're not careful, it drowns you from the inside out.

I was careful. I'm always careful.

The girl slept in my spare room—which is to say, the corner behind my loom where I'd dragged a mattress that smelled of mildew and old wool—and she hadn't moved in six hours except to breathe, slow and even, like something that had finally decided it was safe to stop running. Her hands were still filthy. Under the grime there were scars, thin and deliberate, in patterns I almost recognized. Almost. That word again. *Almost* was how all my trouble started.

I should have turned her out at dawn. Instead I sat at my kitchen table with a cup of chicory going cold and watched her sleep through the gap in the door, and told myself it was practical. A child who spoke a language that made my hidden charms hum was either a catastrophe or an opportunity, and both required study.

Liar. The truth was simpler and worse: when she'd looked up at me on my doorstep, rain streaming off her chin, she had the eyes of every stray my mother ever took in. Eyes that said *I know you'll send me away, everyone does.* My mother collected broken things. So did I, apparently. The habit had skipped a generation and landed on me like a debt.

I drank the chicory. I got to work.

* * *

Vell's black market opens when the bells stop ringing, which in the Gullet district means never entirely—the bell-ringers of Saint Verity's are paid by the Wardens to ring off-schedule, so no one can synchronize anything illegal. Clever, in its way. You can't hold a coordinated auction when the hours keep lying to you. So the market breathes instead of marches: stalls appearing and dissolving between one cracked bell and the next, vendors who sell from three different corners before noon so the patrol maps never quite catch them.

My stall was a blanket and a lie. The blanket held buttons, ribbons, dented thimbles, the ordinary junk of a woman mending clothes for coppers. The lie was sewn into the lining: eleven small charms, each one a knot of thread and bone bead and stolen memory, folded small enough to swallow.

Memory-charms. The only magic left worth selling in Vell, because it's the only kind that leaves no trace. A fire-charm scorches. A ward-charm shimmers if a Warden's iron passes near. But a memory-charm? It just *is*—a moment someone gave up willingly or lost unwillingly, pressed into thread by a thief with my particular talents. Buy one and you remember your dead wife's laugh for an hour. Your childhood garden. The taste of a fruit that doesn't grow anymore. Grief, bottled. Nostalgia, pawned.

The Wardens call it theft. Which is rich, coming from them.

"Quentin." Old Maretta hobbled past my stall, ostensibly browsing thimbles. "Patrols came through Fishmonger's Row twice before dawn. Twice. And they were counting."

"Counting what?"

"Stalls." She picked up a thimble, put it down. "They used to count heads. Now they count stalls. Someone's given them a new ledger."

I smiled the smile I keep for neighbors and bad news. "Maybe they're finally organizing their paperwork."

"Maybe." She moved along, and her limp sounded louder than usual, which was Maretta's way of saying *be careful* without saying it where walls might listen.

Counting stalls. Counting *my* stall. I kept my hands busy sorting buttons and let my mind run the arithmetic of dread. Two patrols before dawn meant overtime pay, and overtime pay meant orders from above, and orders from above meant someone in the Wardenhouse had a name they wanted attached to a face.

I didn't have proof yet. But dread doesn't need proof. Dread runs ahead of facts like a dog runs ahead of its master, barking at everything.

* * *

Here's what the histories say, the ones the Wardens allow to be printed: seven years ago, the world broke. Magic—wild, lawless, *corrupting*—tore a wound in the fabric of things, and only the swift action of the newly formed Wardens saved civilization. Magic was outlawed not out of tyranny but mercy. Every charm burned, every mage registered or exiled or worse. Vell became the model city: iron-bound, spell-free, safe.

Here's what actually happened, as far as I could piece together from fragments I'd stolen over the years—fragments sold cheap by people too frightened to keep them:

There was a covenant. Five bearers, bound to something old, something that held the seams of the world shut. And seven years ago, in one night of fire and screaming, it failed. Not wore out. Not faded. *Failed*, all at once, like a rope cut rather than frayed. The Wardens arrived suspiciously fast afterward, with laws already drafted, as though they'd been waiting for the world to break so they'd have an excuse to break it further.

I was in Vell that night. I was fleeing—I'll get to that. And I did something in my panic that I've spent seven years not thinking about. I stole something. Something small, something I told myself was worthless, something that hummed against my palms like a trapped moth. I hid it so deep inside my own hoard of taken memories that even I couldn't find it anymore.

That's the trick of being a memory-thief. You steal so much that eventually you can't tell which losses are yours.

* * *

Midday, the bells lied twice in a row—a long crack followed immediately by another, which meant either Saint Verity's ringer was drunk or the schedule had been scrambled deliberately. Deliberate, most likely. Scrambled bells meant a sweep was coming, sweeping being easier when the market can't predict you.

A customer stopped at my blanket. Young man, clerk's ink stains on his fingers, eyes red-rimmed in the way of someone who'd been crying or about to. He fingered the ribbons without seeing them.

"The gray silk one," he said, barely audible.

Gray silk ribbon, third from the left. Inside it: a woman's voice singing a lullaby in a dialect nobody speaks anymore. I'd taken it from a dying grandmother in the Salt Quarter who'd asked me, with her last coins, to make sure someone remembered how the song went.

"For grief," I said quietly. "One hour. Maybe two, if you're tired. Tired makes it last."

He paid too much. They always do. I watched him walk away clutching the ribbon like a rosary, and I felt the familiar sourness settle under my ribs—the part of this trade I never got clean from. I sold other people's treasures back to strangers. Sometimes that was mercy. Sometimes it was just commerce wearing mercy's coat.

And underneath the sourness, quieter: *how long until they trace the thread?* Because that was the real danger of my craft, the reason I worked alone and moved stalls monthly and slept with a knife and never, ever let anyone close. Every charm I made carried my signature—not a name, nothing so stupid, but a *technique*. A way of folding a memory into thread that was as individual as handwriting. If the Wardens ever got a sample of my work and a competent analyst—

The bells cracked again. Closer.

I began packing. Buttons first, always buttons; they hide the clink of everything else.

* * *

That was when I saw the boy watching me.

Not a boy—a Warden cadet, sixteen maybe, in an ill-fitting black coat, standing at the mouth of Cooper's Lane pretending to examine a poster of outlawed sigils. He'd been at the far end of the row an hour ago. He was looking at my stall the way cadets look at stalls: memorizing it.

But that wasn't what stopped my breath.

It was the mud on his boots. Red mud, the clay-heavy sort that exists in exactly one place in Vell—the riverbank below the old aqueduct, half a mile from my house. Half a mile from the doorstep where a mud-caked child had appeared out of a rainstorm two nights ago.

Coincidence. I'm a professional liar; I know coincidence when I meet one, and this wasn't it.

I finished packing at a widow's pace, unhurried, and melted into the crowd with my basket of lies. Three streets later, doubled back twice, I stopped in the shadow of the tannery and made myself think instead of feel.

They weren't looking for a memory-thief. If they had my signature, they'd have raided already—they don't warn, they take. No. The cadet was mapping. Mapping the market, the rows, the routes. Looking for someone who *appeared*. Someone who came out of nowhere two nights ago, speaking gibberish, leaving red-mud footprints that some sharp-eyed constable had traced backward toward the Gullet and found nothing—because I'd scrubbed the doorstep—but the *asking* would have started, the questions, the quiet knocking on doors—

Maretta. Oh, gods. Maretta, who'd seen the girl's wet clothes drying behind my loom when she brought bread yesterday. Maretta, whose limp got louder when she was afraid.

I walked home fast, not running, running being the one thing you never do when you might be watched. The Gullet swallowed me the way it swallows everything, and my street smelled of tallow smoke and rain that hadn't fallen yet, and my door was exactly as I'd left it.

Inside, the girl was awake. Sitting cross-legged behind the loom, watching me with those grave dark eyes, and as I crossed the threshold she said something in that language that made the charms in my basket-line hum like struck glass—one soft chord of sound, there and gone.

I set down the basket. I knelt so we were level.

"Someone's asking questions about you," I told her, though she couldn't understand the words. "Which means soon they'll stop asking and start taking. I need you to understand me, little one, because I'm about to do something stupid and I'd rather you weren't surprised by it."

She tilted her head. Then she reached out—slowly, asking permission with the pause—and touched the basket-lining where the charms lay hidden. And hummed. One note. The same note the charms answered with.

Every hair on my arms stood up. Because that wasn't mimicry. That was *resonance*, and resonance was covenant-work, and covenant-work had been dead for seven years.

Outside, far off, the bells began to crack again—wrong, urgent, wrong—and beneath their noise, faint but unmistakable, came the rhythmic iron-shod tread of boots. Many boots. Coming up the Gullet, street by street, in the methodical cadence of a search that already knew its answer.
