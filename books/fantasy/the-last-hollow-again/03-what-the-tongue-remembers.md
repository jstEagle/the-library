## 3. What the Tongue Remembers

I dream in the wrong order.

That's the first thing you should know about me. Other people's dreams go forward, like walking. Mine go sideways, and down, and sometimes they go *before*, which isn't a direction at all until it is.

So: I am small — smaller than now, small enough to fit inside a bread basket — and I am standing at the edge of the Hollow, and the Hollow is looking back.

* * *

The Hollow does not have eyes. That would be too easy, too much like a story where the monster can be met and fought and maybe poked with a stick. The Hollow has *absence*. Where its face should be there is a place where a face was decided against. You feel it noticing you the way you feel a tooth about to hurt — not yet, not yet, but soon, and forever after that.

In the dream-before, five people stood in a ring around it. I know this because I was one of them, except I wasn't born yet, except dreams don't care about that kind of bookkeeping. Five people in a ring, holding hands, saying words in the First Tongue, which is my tongue, which is the tongue I spoke before I had a mouth to speak it with.

The words went like this:

*We are the door and we are the lock.*
*We are the wound and we are the stitch.*
*What was taken, we give back.*
*What was broken, we hold together.*

Five voices. And here is the thing that makes my chest go tight even when I'm awake and chewing on somebody's stolen bread: one of the voices was already dead.

Dead voices don't stop. Dead voices just get quieter, the way a bell keeps humming after the clapper's gone still. In the ring of five, the fourth voice came from a woman made mostly of light and mostly gone, and the other four leaned into her silence like leaning into wind, and the seal held anyway.

Held. Past tense. I want you to notice how much work that little word does.

* * *

I woke up on Quentin Blackwood's floor with my cheek pressed to the boards and my hands full of nothing, and for a moment I didn't know where the room ended and the dream began, because the room smelled like rain and old wood and a little bit like magic pretending very hard not to be magic.

Her charms hum under the floorboards. Did you know that? She thinks she hides them well. But I speak the language they're hiding in. When I walked past the loose plank by the stove last night, the charm inside said hello. It said it very quietly, the way people talk in a house where someone might be listening, but it said it.

Nobody has ever said hello to me before. Not in that language. Maybe not in any language.

I said hello back.

The charm shivered. Charms aren't supposed to do that. I think it was surprised. I think maybe I was the first thing in seven years to answer it, and things that go unanswered for seven years forget how conversations work.

Seven years. I know that number the way I know my own teeth. I don't remember learning it.

* * *

Second dream. Or maybe the same dream, folded over. Dreams fold. Paper folds. People fold. Everything important folds, and if you're careful you can carry a whole lot of world in a small space.

This time I'm not at the edge of the Hollow. This time I'm *inside* the remembering of it, which is different from being inside it, the way a story about drowning is different from drowning, though both leave you gasping.

Before the Hollow, there were two worlds lying side by side like two people sharing a bed who haven't admitted they love each other. This one — the one with rain and Vell and bread worth stealing — and another one. The other one had no name that survives, because names are the first thing the Hollow eats. It started as a crack. Cracks always start small. Somebody wanted something and reached across where reaching wasn't allowed, and the place between the worlds tore like wet paper, and what came through the tear wasn't a creature or an army.

It was *unmaking*. It was the idea of taking things back out of existence. It ate the other world first, whole cities, whole skies, and it ate the memory of them as it went, so that by the end nobody could even grieve properly, because grief needs a shape and the shapes were gone.

Five people stopped it. Five. That's the joke of everything — the whole weight of two worlds balanced on five pairs of hands, and four of those pairs are gone now, and I am carrying the fifth pair around inside my body like swallowed stones.

I am the seal. I didn't ask to be. Being a seal is a terrible job. Seals get broken; that's practically their profession.

And one of the five is already dead — I told you that, the voice of light and almost-nothing — and here is the part I only understood this morning, staring at the ceiling of Quentin's kitchen while she pretended to sleep and I pretended not to notice her pretending:

There were supposed to be five.

There are supposed to be five.

The covenant doesn't work with four. It never worked with four. It held with four the way a table holds on three legs — barely, badly, waiting. The fourth bearer died and no one took up her place, because everyone who knew the words was dead or scattered or, in one case, a child who couldn't talk yet.

Me. I'm the case. I'm the talking-now child.

The seal is cracked. I can feel the crack the way you feel weather coming in your bones, except my bones are new and shouldn't know weather yet. Something got *taken* out of the middle of the covenant — a memory, a piece, a keystone — seven years ago. Seven years. There's that number again, following me around like a dog that's decided I'm its person.

Someone stole a piece of the lock on the door between worlds, and they probably didn't even know what they were stealing, and the door has been rattling ever since, and I am the hinge, and the hinge is a child, and children are famously bad at holding doors.

* * *

Third dream. Shortest one. The bad ones are always short.

I stand in the ring of five again, but this time I look at the faces. Dream logic says I shouldn't be able to see faces — the Hollow eats names, and faces come attached — but dreams are generous with me, maybe because I'm generous with them, maybe because I'm the last person alive who speaks the language they're dreaming in.

First bearer: a man with river-water eyes and hands stained blue from ink or dye, laughing even though laughing at the Hollow is either very brave or very stupid.

Second bearer: an old woman whose mouth is sewn shut with silver thread, speaking anyway, her voice coming out of the ground itself, out of bells, out of the spaces between heartbeats.

Third bearer: a young soldier, armor half-melted, crying without any shame about it, holding his sword point-down like he's planting it.

Fourth: the light-woman, mostly gone, singing.

Fifth:

Fifth.

The fifth face turns toward me and it's —

It's —

* * *

I woke up again. Morning this time, gray Vell morning, rain doing its usual job of making everything honest and miserable. Quentin was in the kitchen doing something quiet with her hands, the way she does everything, like noise costs money.

"You talk in your sleep," she said, not looking at me.

I considered lying. Lying is a good skill and I practice it. But her charms were humming under the floorboards, all of them at once, like a choir deciding whether to be afraid, and I thought: this woman hides magic in a city that hangs magicians. She knows about hiding. She might know about the rest.

"I talk in an old language," I said. "Older than sleep."

Something crossed her face — quick, like a fish under ice. She knows some of it. Not the words. But the *shape* of the words, the way you know a song your mother hummed before you could understand it.

"Say something," she said. Very quietly. Like asking might break it.

So I did. I said the opening line of the covenant, the one that means *we are the door and we are the lock*, except there's no way to say it small. The First Tongue doesn't do small. Every word is a load-bearing wall.

Every charm in the house lit up at once.

Not glowed — *lit*, gold and terrified and joyful all at the same time, seven years of hiding answering one sentence like dogs hearing their names after a long war. Quentin spun around, hand pressed flat to the table, and looked at me like I was either a miracle or a fire, and I know which one people usually pick, and it's fire.

"What," she said, "are you?"

And I opened my mouth to tell her the truest thing I know, which is: *I'm the fifth bearer, and the fourth is dead, and the seal is cracking, and something stolen seven years ago has to go back or everything gets unmade.*

But what came out instead, because I am a child and children say the wrong true thing:

"The fifth face in the dream was yours."

* * *

She went very still. Stillness in Quentin isn't calm. I've been watching her long enough — three days, which in abandoned-child time is basically a childhood — to know that her stillness is a locked door with running footsteps behind it.

"That's not possible," she said.

"Neither am I," I said. "Here I am anyway."

Under the floorboards, the charms kept humming. One of them — the oldest one, buried deepest, wrapped in more lies than the others — had changed its tune. It wasn't saying hello anymore.

It was saying *found you.*

And far away, at the edge of my bones where the crack lives, the Hollow turned its absent face toward Vell, and began, patiently, the way all hunger begins, to walk.
