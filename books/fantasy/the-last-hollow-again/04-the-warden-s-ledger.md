## 4. The Warden's Ledger

The raid came on a Tuesday, because ruin in Vell keeps banker's hours.

I'd just made my last sale of the morning—a grief-charm to a cooper whose wife had been dead eleven years and who came back every month anyway, buying the same soft-edged memory of her laughing at his terrible jokes. He paid in copper and didn't haggle, which told me everything about what the memory was worth to him. I was wrapping it in waxed paper when the bells changed their song.

Not the hour bells. The *other* bells. The ones Vell's founders had cast from melted charms, back when they decided magic was a disease and its carriers were a plague. Those bells rang flat and wrong, a sound like a door slamming somewhere behind the world, and every charm-seller in the Gullet market went still as rabbits.

"They're coming," Sable said.

She'd been crouched at my stall's edge doing that thing she did—staring at strangers' hands, cataloguing them, deciding which ones were safe. She said it without fear. That frightened me more than the bells did. Fear you can work with. Calm, in a seven-year-old who spoke dead languages, was just another kind of omen.

"Coming where?"

"Here." Her eyes tracked something above the market, following lines I couldn't see. "Many boots. They walk like they're sorry about it."

*They walk like they're sorry about it.* I'd have laughed if my stomach hadn't dropped through the floorboards. That was Warden doctrine exactly—the apologetic boot, the regretful truncheon, violence wrapped in procedure until you almost thanked them for it.

"Right." I started packing with hands I kept deliberately slow. Panic moves fast; fast draws eyes. "Sable. Listen carefully. If anyone grabs me, you run to the tannery on Corvis Lane. There's a woman called Maretta. Tell her—" I hesitated. What was the passphrase? What had I even arranged? Seven years in Vell and I'd arranged nothing, trusted no one, built myself a life with the structural integrity of wet paper precisely so there'd be nothing for anyone to find.

Sable watched me flounder with the patience of a cat watching a moth exhaust itself against a window.

"You have no plan," she observed.

"I have contingency architecture."

"You have nothing." She stood, mud-colored coat hanging off her like a shed skin. "Come."

And because the universe has a sense of humor calibrated specifically to humiliate me, I followed her. A grown woman, a professional, an artist of deception—and I let a feral child lead me into the dark because she said *come* in that voice, the one that sounded like the ground itself giving instructions.

* * *

She led me down through the Gullet's bones. Every city sits on top of an older version of itself, but Vell had buried its predecessor with unusual enthusiasm—there'd been a whole district down here once, before the Purification, before the Wardens burned the mage-quarters and filled the cellars with rubble rather than leave anything standing that remembered how to be magical. Sable moved through the collapsed streets like she'd memorized them in a dream. Twice we passed gaps in the rubble where light fell in dusty columns, and twice she pulled me back before I heard the boots above us.

We ended up in what must have been a counting-house cellar. There were shelves rotted to lace, a desk with its legs broken, and a smell like old paper having a very long argument with damp. And on the desk, half-buried under a collapsed shelf, a book.

I want to say I felt destiny humming. I didn't. I felt annoyed, because Sable stopped dead and stared at it with her whole body, and I knew—I *knew*—that whatever happened next was going to cost me.

"What is it?"

"The ledger," she said. "It remembers."

"Books don't remember."

"This one does." She looked up at me. In the grey light leaking through a ceiling crack, her eyes were the exact color of deep water over something drowned. "Open it, Quentin."

I should mention: my charms were humming again. They'd hummed the night she arrived at my door, that low resonant whine like struck glass, the sound of hidden magic recognizing hidden magic—or in this case, whatever lived coiled under her skin. It hadn't stopped since. I'd taken to sleeping with earplugs and denial.

So yes. I opened the ledger. Because I'm weak, and curious, and because some part of me had already understood that Sable hadn't found this cellar by accident any more than she'd found my door by accident. Things that speak the First Tongue don't stumble. They're *drawn*.

The book was bound in grey leather gone green at the corners. The first pages were mundane enough—inventories, requisitions, supply tallies in a clerk's crabbed hand. Then I turned past a page dated forty years ago and the handwriting changed, and the entries changed, and my blood changed temperature.

*Confiscation log, Third Purge.* Names. Hundreds of names. And beside each name, not charges or crimes, but descriptions. *Carrier. Faint gift, unmanifested. Strong carrier, manifested, terminated. Carrier child, age approx. 6—held for study.*

Held for study. My hands went cold. I thought of Sable, age approximately six, held by someone, somewhere, learning to walk like sorry boots.

"Keep turning," Sable whispered.

Near the middle of the book, the entries shifted again—from confiscations to something older. The ink faded to brown, the hand grew ornate, formal, a founder's hand. And there, under a header I read three times because my mind kept refusing to accept it:

*Covenant Dispersal—Final Accounting.*

Five names. Five bearers—that word, *bearer*, written with a capital B, the way you write things that frighten you. Beside four of the names: instructions. Relocations. False identities. Sealed orders. The founder of the Wardens—the man whose statue stands in Founders' Square with a sword raised toward heaven, whose face is on the currency, whose name parents invoke to make children behave—hadn't hunted the covenant's keepers.

He'd *scattered* them. Deliberately. Carefully. Like a man hiding the pieces of a weapon he intended to come back for.

And beside the fifth name, the fifth bearer: one word, in different ink, pressed so hard the pen had torn the page.

*TAKEN.*

Below it, a final entry, dated seven years ago. Seven years. My lungs forgot their job for a moment.

*The keystone has surfaced. A thief carries it unknowing. Let her run; the seal will starve without its heart, and a starving seal opens easier than a guarded one.*

A thief carries it unknowing. Let her run.

I sat down on the floor. Not gracefully. My knees simply resigned.

"That's me," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's reading over my shoulder. "Seven years ago. I stole a memory. I steal memories, it's—it's what I do, I took a job, a black-job, high payment, no questions, and the thing I lifted was—" I pressed my palms against my eyes. "I never even looked at it. You don't look at inventory. You wrap it and you sell it and you forget you ever had it except I *couldn't* forget it, it's the only theft I ever made that left a hole—"

"Quentin." Sable crouched beside me. Close enough that I could feel the hum of her, the Hollow-fragment under her skin resonating against my hidden charms like two tuning forks agreeing on a note. "Read the last line."

I took my hands from my eyes. Read the last line.

*When the seal fails, Vell falls first. This is acceptable.*

This is acceptable.

Something went through me then that I can only describe as heat—not anger, anger is too tidy a word for it. This was older and dirtier, the fury of a person who has spent seven years being careful, being small, being *lawful-adjacent*, hiding her selfhood in wax and whispers while a city built statues to the man who had decided, in ink that tore the page, that her kind could burn if it served his design. Who had helped break the covenant that held the world's wound shut—not failed to protect it, not lost it, *broken* it—and then founded an order to hunt the survivors of his own sabotage.

The Wardens weren't protecting Vell from magic.

They were keeping the crime scene clean.

"They know," I said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it made it real and real was the only shape I could fight. "The command knows. Maybe not all of them—but the top. Dorian's office. They've known for decades that the Hollow exists, that the covenant holds it, that they broke it. And instead of fixing it they've been—" I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "They've been *managing* it. Waiting for it to starve. Using the raids, the purges, to make sure no bearer ever gets strong enough, close enough, together enough to reforge it. Every carrier arrested isn't law enforcement. It's maintenance."

Sable nodded, solemn as a funeral bell. "The boots are sorry," she said, "because they are mopping a floor they helped spill the blood on."

Gods. The child talked like a knife wrapped in velvet, and every time I forgot that, she reminded me.

"We go to the magistrates," I said, and even as I said it I heard how hollow it rang, and Sable tilted her head at me the way you listen to a cracked bell. "No. Right. Of course. The magistrates are appointed by the Founder's Council, which was endowed by—" I gestured at the ledger, at forty years of confiscated lives. "The law IS the crime. We're sitting in the evidence vault of a robbery the police committed."

"So what do we do?" Sable asked. And here's the thing that undid me a little: she asked it like she genuinely expected me to have an answer. Like I was someone who solved things. Me, who had spent seven years solving nothing harder than how to stay unnoticed.

But looking at her face—mud-streaked, sharp-eyed, waiting—I understood that the answer to that question had already changed shape inside me. It wasn't *how do I survive* anymore. It wasn't even *how do I save the kid*. It was bigger and stupider and more dangerous than anything I'd ever carried out of anyone's head.

There were five bearers once. One was taken. Which meant others might still exist—scattered, hidden, seeded across the country by a founder who wanted them findable only by himself. And there was a ledger with his hand in it, which meant there might be more records, more names, more sealed orders filed away in Warden archives.

Archives guarded by the man Dorian Ashvane had become.

My brother. My found-family. The boy who'd shared stolen bread with me in a gutter and grown into the boot that walked sorry through the Gullet. If anyone knew what the founder had hidden, and where, it would be him—if he even knew himself what he was heir to.

I closed the ledger. Wrapped it in my coat like contraband, which, in Vell, truth generally is.

"First," I said, standing, my fury settling into something with edges and a handle, "we get this book somewhere safe. Then we find out if any of those four scattered bearers are still breathing." I looked at Sable, at the faint light moving under her skin that I was finally letting myself see. "And then, sweetheart, we're going to have a family reunion with my brother. Whether he likes it or not."

Above us, faint through forty feet of rubble and silence, the bells rang the hour—and underneath them, so quiet I might have imagined it, something answered from below. From further down. From whatever lay beneath even this buried city, patient as a wound.

Sable heard it too. She turned toward the sound, and smiled, and it was the loneliest smile I have ever seen.

"He's awake now," she said.
