## 5. Brother Ash

There are exactly three bridges left in Vell that don't have a Warden checkpoint on them, and I was stupid enough to choose the one Dorian Vane would know.

I should explain that. Not the bridges—the knowing. Because you can't understand why my hands went cold on the railing until you understand that Dorian and I used to count bridges the way other children counted stars.

We grew up in the Undertow, back before the Wardens had a name for themselves, when they were just a gang with better boots. There were six of us then, sleeping in a collapsed wine cellar under the Saltmarket, and we had rules instead of parents: never steal from anyone poorer than you, never say another thief's real name aloud, and never—*never*—leave a mark behind. Dorian invented half those rules. He was fourteen and already built like a door, and he used to sit up at night sketching maps of the city in candle soot, saying, *One day we'll own every way in and out of this place, Quen. Every bridge, every gate. Then nobody can ever be taken from us again.*

He'd meant it as a promise of safety. Somewhere along the way it curdled into a promise of control, and he joined the very law he'd spent his childhood dodging, and I told myself he'd gone to change it from inside, and he told himself whatever commanders tell themselves at night, and here we are.

Here *I* am, rather. On the Ashbridge, at dusk, with a feral child's hand in mine and the smell of rain coming off the river, because Sable had looked at me that afternoon with those hollow-grey eyes and said—not in the First Tongue this time, but in cracked, rusty Common—*"The ash man remembers the water."*

I didn't know what it meant. But I knew who she meant. There is only one man in Vell called the ash man, and only one stretch of water he'd remember, and it runs under Ashbridge, where we scattered our dead foster-mother's ashes when I was eleven and Dorian was fifteen and the world was still a place where promises could be kept.

So either Sable had dreamed her way into my worst-kept secret, or Dorian had sent for me. With Dorian, the two were often the same thing.

He was already there when we came onto the span. Of course he was. He stood at the center in his grey Warden coat with his hands clasped behind him like a man admiring the view, which in Vell means a man pretending not to be nervous. Seven years hadn't softened him. They'd sanded him down to essentials—all bone and duty and that jaw like a verdict.

"Hello, Quen," he said, without turning around.

"Dorian." My voice came out steadier than my pulse. "You're off your patrol route."

"I'm exactly where the ledger says I should be." He turned then, and his eyes went straight past me to Sable, and something moved through his face like weather. Recognition, maybe. Or grief wearing recognition's coat. "Hello, little seal."

Sable's grip tightened on my hand hard enough to grind bone. She stepped half behind me, and she spoke—one word in the First Tongue—and every charm sewn into my lining hummed like struck glass.

"What did she say?" Dorian asked quietly.

"She said you smell like an ending." Which wasn't quite true. She'd said something worse, something that had made the hair on my arms stand up, but there are limits to what I'll translate even now.

Dorian exhaled through his nose. Almost a laugh. "She always did have your gift for flattery."

"*She?*"

"My grandmother. Ellery Vane. Founder of the Wardens." He said it flatly, the way a man confesses a debt. "You found her ledger, didn't you? In the Undermarket vaults. I know you did, because three days ago you started moving like someone who knows how the story ends."

"The ledger says your grandmother helped break the covenant." I kept my voice low. The bridge was empty, but empty in Vell lasts about as long as honest weather. "It says she traded four of the five bearers' names to the Hollow's first breach. It says everything since—the outlawry, the purges, the memory-taxes—is one long cover-up. Your family didn't fight magic, Dorian. Your family *made* the wound and then sold the bandages."

"Yes," he said.

I had prepared, in the hours walking here, for denial. For rage. For the smooth commander's voice explaining necessity. I had prepared for everything except that single, unadorned yes, and it knocked something loose in me that I'm still gathering up off the floor.

"Why?" My voice cracked on it. Seven years of cracks, all of them showing at once. "You were there when the Hollow took the Fen district. You carried me out. You *carried* me, Dorian, you had my blood on your hands for a week—"

"And I signed the order that redrew the maps so nobody would ask where Fen went!" His composure split down the middle, and underneath was the boy from the wine cellar, furious and frightened and fifteen forever. "You think I don't know? You think I sleep? My grandmother broke the covenant to stop the Hollow from eating the whole city, and it worked, Quen—it *held*—and the price was that magic had to die, all of it, everywhere, or the seal would starve and crack. And then seven years ago somebody stole the keystone memory, and the seal started failing, and every Warden ledger since has been me trying to find that thief before the hole in the world finds us first."

The rain started. Soft, at first, like the sky clearing its throat.

"So this is you finding me," I said.

"This is me asking you to give it back." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into something rawer than command. "Whatever you took, Quen. Whatever it cost you. Give it back, let us re-seal it the old way, under Warden custody, and I will lose every record of you. You and the girl both vanish. Clean. I'll burn my own name before I burn yours."

"And if I say no?"

His jaw worked. "Then I do what the coat requires."

There it was. That was always the tragedy of Dorian Vane: he would feel everything and obey anyway. When we were kids he once sat up all night holding our dying friend Tam's hand and still reported Tam's stash to the gang the next morning, because rules were the only parent he trusted and he could no more defy them than fly.

"You know what the keystone is," I said. "Don't you. You've known longer than I have."

"I know what my grandmother wrote in the margin of the last page." His eyes flicked to Sable, then away, like looking directly at her hurt. "'The seal is not a thing. It is a person. Guard her line, or end it.'" A muscle jumped in his cheek. "For seven years I told myself the girl in the prophecies was a metaphor. Then my scouts pulled a mud-caked child out of the flood-channels speaking a language that predates the city, and every warding-stone in the precinct sang when she walked past."

Sable tugged my sleeve. She pointed at Dorian's chest—at the grey coat, at the small silver pin of office shaped like a closed eye—and said something long and liquid and terrible in the First Tongue, and my charms rang so hard two of them snapped their threads.

"She wants to know," I translated, my mouth dry, "if you're going to be the ending or the remembering."

Dorian looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away. And I swear to you, whatever else happened on that bridge, in that moment he was choosing—I watched him choose—with everything in him straining toward the right answer like a man leaning into a gale.

Then the whistle sounded.

Two notes, high and clipped, from the far end of the bridge. Warden signal: target sighted, converge. Dorian's face closed like a shutter, and I understood, with the particular nausea of old knowledge, that he'd never been alone here. That he'd come with backup whether he wanted it or not, because commanders don't get to take walks unaccompanied, and someone in grey had been watching this conversation the entire time.

"Dorian," I breathed.

"I know." His hand went to his side, and for one insane heartbeat I thought—hoped—he was reaching for me. Instead he drew the iron whistle from his collar and blew three sharp notes of his own. *Break off. Pursue.* The oldest signal we had, from the wine-cellar days, repurposed, and God help me I almost admired it: he was ordering them away while ordering them after me, buying seconds with betrayal's currency.

"Run," he said. "Now, Quen. Through the arches, down to the barge stairs—they won't search the water before midnight."

"You're letting us go?"

"I'm *losing* you," he snarled, "there's a difference, and it's the only mercy my rank contains—" and then the squad came over the far rail, six of them in grey, boots ringing on wet stone, and the moment for words was over.

I grabbed Sable and ran.

Behind us I heard Dorian's voice go cold and official, reciting orders, and then—just once, just loud enough—a single word in the old thieves' cant that meant *forgive me*, which isn't a word so much as a wound with grammar. I didn't look back. Looking back is how they catch you; that was rule four, and rule four was also his.

We went over the parapet into the arch shadows, down slick stairs slicker with rain, Sable light as a stolen coin in my arms by the end because her legs were short and the Hollow's pull was getting stronger—I could feel it now, a faint tidal drag on everything, on memory itself, names slipping loose in my head like fish. At the barge stairs I wedged us into the gap beneath a rotted pontoon and put my hand over her mouth and listened to boots thunder past overhead, six sets, then five, then none.

She was shaking. So was I. But children shake outward and I shook inward, and somewhere in the middle of it she took my face in both small hands and looked at me with those hollow-grey eyes and said, clearly, in Common:

"He lied. About one thing."

"Sable—"

"He said the seal is a person." Her thumb traced my cheekbone, gentle as a priest. "But the seal is a *memory*. And you're carrying it." She tilted her head, listening to something inside herself the way you'd listen to a shell. "It's heavy, isn't it? The thing you stole. It's been so heavy you forgot it was there."

I opened my mouth to deny it—to lie to a child, which even I have some standards about—and found I couldn't. Because she was right. There *was* a weight in me, had been for seven years, a shape I'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot. Something I'd taken the night I fled Dorian, in the dark, without knowing what my hands were doing.

The river lapped the pilings. Above us, Vell glittered with its lawful lamps, all of it standing on a lie my legs were made of.

"What do I do?" I asked her. I, a grown woman, a master thief, asking a child for directions. Rule five: never show fear. Rule five died on that pontoon.

Sable smiled, and it was the saddest thing I've seen this side of the Hollow.

"You remember," she said. "*All* of it. Even the parts that break you. That's how seals are made."

And far off across the water, in the direction of the Warden citadel, every bell in Vell began to ring—though no ringer stood in any tower, and no hour had been struck—and the sound was not bronze at all, but something older, calling its bearers home.
