## 6. The Bearer Beneath the Bell

*Quentin*

The ledger rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat. I'd wrapped it in oilcloth and strapped it under my coat, and every time the crowd jostled me in the market lane I flinched as if someone had reached for it—which, in Vell, someone eventually would.

Sable walked beside me with her hood up and her hand fisted in my sleeve. Three days since the raid on the Gullet market. Three days of moving safehouses, of teaching the child which alleys to memorize and which faces to forget, of lying awake listening to Warden boots echo off the cistern walls beneath us. The city was tightening like a fist.

"We need the bell-ringer," I said, for the fourth time that morning.

Sable didn't answer. She never answered in trade-tongue; she only spoke the First Tongue, that old language that made the charms sewn into my collar hum like struck glass. But she tugged my sleeve twice toward the north—toward the Cathedral Quarter—and that was as good as a yes.

The Cathedral Quarter was the one part of Vell the Wardens didn't bother patrolling, because there was nothing left to police. When magic was outlawed seven years ago, the great bells of Saint Verren were the first things to fall silent. Bells, the founding decree said, had been used to carry enchantments across the city—a claim I'd always found absurd until this week, when I read a founder's own handwriting admitting he'd helped break a covenant older than the decree itself. Now the towers stood empty, pigeons roosting in the belfries, and the Quarter's few remaining residents had that particular hollowed-out look of people who stayed in a place after the place had already left them.

Old Tobbe the candle-dipper told me where to look. "The ringer?" He squinted at me through smoke. "You mean the mute one. Third tower. He don't talk, girl, so save your breath." Then he cackled at his own joke, because everyone in Vell is a comedian and none of them are funny.

We climbed.

The third tower's stair was a spiral of rotten oak and pigeon filth, and Sable went up it like water finding its level, quick and silent, while I wheezed behind her and cursed my life choices. At the top, through a broken louver, gray light fell across a small kingdom: a pallet bed, three mismatched cups, a rope bed strung with salvaged cordage, and a man sitting cross-legged among all of it with his hands resting on his knees like a man waiting for something he'd been waiting for a very long time.

He was perhaps fifty, gaunt as a winter branch, with the calloused palms of a lifetime spent hauling bell-ropes. Around his neck hung a leather cord, and on the cord hung a small brass bell no bigger than a thumbnail.

The bell made no sound when he moved. It should have clattered against his chest. It swung and swayed and said nothing at all.

"Master Hollis," I guessed, because Tobbe had given me a name along with the bad joke.

The man looked at me. Then at Sable. And then something happened to his face—not an expression so much as a surrender. He raised one finger to his lips, touched the little brass bell, and shook his head slowly.

*Traded,* the gesture said. *Gone.*

"I know," I said. "I know you can't speak. I'm not here to make you." I lowered myself onto the filthy boards across from him, joints protesting, and set my hands flat where he could see them. Old thief habit: show your palms, show you're unarmed, show you're honest, which is the biggest lie of the three. "I'm here because five people made a promise once. A covenant. And it's breaking."

Hollis went very still.

"The Hollow," I said. "You've heard the word. Don't tell me how—I can see you have." Because his face had gone the color of the light coming through the louvers, gray and cold. "One of the bearers is dead already. I think you're the second. And I think—" I hesitated, hating the next part, hating how much I needed it, "—I think you gave up your voice for a reason. To keep something quiet. Or to keep yourself from saying something."

His hand closed around the silent bell.

For a long moment the only sounds were pigeons and wind and Sable breathing beside me, and then, softly, the child began to sing.

Not loudly. Not in any tongue I knew—the First Tongue, low and wordless-melodic, a sound like the memory of ringing. And the little brass bell at Hollis's throat *answered*. One clear note, impossible, sweet as a coin dropped in a well, hanging in the air between us.

Hollis stared down at it. His shoulders began to shake. I realized, with the particular discomfort of witnessing grief you weren't meant to see, that he was crying without any sound at all—even his weeping had been traded away.

"Seven years," I said quietly. "You've been silent seven years."

He nodded. Then he held up one finger—*wait*—and crawled to the corner of his kingdom, prying up a loose board. From beneath it he drew out a slate and a stub of chalk worn round as a river stone. He wrote, erased, wrote again, and turned it toward me:

**THE OTHERS. HOW MANY LEFT?**

"One dead. You. That leaves two we haven't found." I paused. "And a keystone. Something the covenant needs that's been missing seven years. Maybe stolen. Maybe lost." *Maybe sitting in my own skull without my knowledge,* I did not say, because I hadn't yet worked up the courage to open my own memory-charms and find out what I'd taken from that burning archive all those nights ago, fleeing Dorian with his torchlight behind me and a stranger's stolen memory hot in my hands.

Hollis wiped the slate with his sleeve and wrote:

**WHY ME. WHY NOW.**

"Because the bells remember," Sable said.

I nearly fell off the floor. Both of us whipped around. The child stood by the broken louver, hood fallen back, and she was looking at Hollis with an expression no eight-year-old should own—ancient, patient, sorrowful as a tide.

"You traded your voice so the last true bell-note could sleep inside you instead of dying with the rest," she said, in that same low song-speech, and though the words came out strange and accented, I understood them, the way you understand thunder. "The covenant put its second seal in sound. Sound can be hidden in a person. You hid it well. But the Hollow is waking, and seals don't stay asleep forever."

Hollis's chalk hovered over the slate. Then, slowly, deliberately, he wrote:

**IF I RING IT. WHAT HAPPENS TO ME.**

Sable considered him with those dark eyes. "I don't know," she said. And then, softer, in a way that broke my heart a little: "I think maybe you get it back. I think maybe that's what the covenant does. It gives back what was given."

*It gives back what was given.* I thought of the charms in my collar, the memories I'd sold and stolen and hoarded like a dragon of other people's yesterdays. I thought of the ledger under my coat, and the name written in the founder's hand, and Dorian's face on the bridge that I hadn't stood on yet but would. I thought: *what was given.* What had I been given? What had I taken that should have been given freely?

Hollis looked at the slate a long time. Then he took the silent bell from around his neck, held it out flat on his palm between us, and waited.

"It's not mine to take," I said. "This isn't theft, Master Hollis. This has to be chosen."

Something flickered across his face—surprise, maybe, or amusement, or just the shock of being asked rather than commanded. Seven years in Vell, and I'd forgotten most people had to be asked.

He picked up the chalk one more time. His hand was steady now.

**MY NAME IS HOLLIS VERREN-KAI,** he wrote. **FOURTH OF THAT NAME TO RING THESE BELLS. MY FATHER RANG THE ALARM WHEN THE DECREE CAME DOWN AND THEY TOOK HIS HANDS FOR IT. I SWORE I'D NEVER LET THEM TAKE ANYTHING ELSE FROM ME. SO I GAVE IT AWAY FIRST.**

He turned the slate. Erased it. Wrote again, smaller:

**CHOOSE IS A GOOD WORD. NO ONE HAS USED IT WITH ME IN SEVEN YEARS.**

Then he stood, joints cracking like green wood, and tucked the little bell back inside his shirt, against his heart. He looked at Sable. He looked at me. And he bowed—formal, deep, the bow of a craftsman to his work, or a soldier to a banner.

Sable bowed back, solemn as a small priestess.

I didn't bow. I stuck out my hand instead, because I'm a thief and bows are for people who trust institutions. Hollis took it. His grip was rope-hard and warm.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended, because apparently three strangers choosing each other in a pigeon coop does something to a person. "Welcome to the worst-found family in Vell. We've got a dead bearer, a hunted child, a stolen ledger, and a commander of the Wardens who used to share a blanket with me when we were starving teenagers. Standards are low. Loyalty's high."

Hollis smiled—it transformed his whole gray face into something younger—and tapped his chest twice over the hidden bell. Then he pointed at Sable, then at me, then swept his hand to include all three of us, and mouthed a single silent word.

*Ours.*

Outside, far below, a Warden whistle cut the air—one long blast, two short. The signal for a district lockdown. Too close. Far too close.

Hollis's smile vanished. He grabbed his slate, scrawled fast, and thrust it at me:

**THEY'RE NOT LOOKING FOR YOU THIS TIME.**

"How do you know?"

**BECAUSE THEY PASSED HERE AN HOUR AGO ASKING AFTER A GIRL WHO SPEAKS THE OLD TONGUE. AND THEY SAID THE WORD FOR HER WASN'T PRISONER.**

My stomach dropped through the tower floor, through the Quarter, through the whole rotten city.

"They said *sacrifice*," Sable translated quietly, watching the sky through the broken louver, where the clouds had begun to swirl wrong, slow and deliberate, like water going down a drain. "They're not taking me to a cell, Quentin." Her small hand found mine, cold as the brass bell. "They're taking me to the Hollow."

* * *

*Sable*

Down the stairs, quick quick, the tall-one and the bell-man and me, three hearts going like birds in a sack. The bell-man knows ways. Ways under the Quarter, ways the Wardens forgot, because Wardens only remember things that matter to them and forgotten stairs belong to us now.

The bell-man cannot speak but his hands talk all day. *Here. Careful. Low. Faster.* Hands are an older language than mine even. I like him. He smells of dust and rope and old ringing, like the inside of a bell looks, if a bell could smell.

The tall-one—Quentin, Quentin, the name fits her like a borrowed coat—keeps touching the charms at her collar when she thinks no one sees. I see. I always see. There is a thing sleeping in her pockets and her sleeves and her skin, a thing shaped like a memory, and it hums when I am near, and lately it has begun to hum *louder*, the way the drain-clouds spin, the way the edges of streets go soft at dusk.

I do not want to touch the charms.

I want to touch the charms more than anything.

Both things are true. Both things are true in the First Tongue and they do not fight each other there. Truths can share a room in the old language. It is only people who make them enemies.

In the dark under the Quarter, the bell-man stops us at a door of rusted iron and knocks a pattern: ring-ring, pause, ring. Somewhere beyond, something answers. The tall-one tenses. "Who else knows you're down here?" she whispers.

The bell-man holds up one finger. *Wait.* Always wait. Waiting is his whole religion.

The door opens on a woman with silver in her braids and a lantern in her hand and a scar running ear to chin like a seam where her face was repaired. She looks at the bell-man. She looks at me. Her eyes go wide and wet and she says, in the First Tongue, in words I have not heard since before I can remember:

*"Little seal. We thought the fifth of us was dead."*

Behind her, down a stair that goes farther than stairs should, I feel it: the humming thing inside Quentin's collar, rising to meet me like a dog hearing its name.

And I understand, with terrible clarity, whose memory it is.

It is mine.
