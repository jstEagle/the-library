## 7. Seven Years of Stolen Things

The charms hang from the ceiling like a rain that forgot how to fall.

Sable counts them in the dark because counting is a thing to do when the world has too many edges: forty-one glass beads on copper wire, each one holding a little swallowed light. Quentin calls them inventory. Sable knows better. Inventory doesn't hum. Inventory doesn't lean toward you when you walk past, the way dogs lean toward a hand that once fed them.

These are memories. Stolen ones. Sable can smell it on them the way other children smell bread baking — old mornings, someone's mother's voice, a first snow. Quentin peels them off people who are drowning in them and sells them to people who are dying without them, and she tells herself this makes her a merchant instead of a thief.

Sable is seven years old and has been alive for nine hundred years, so she knows a lie when it keeps someone warm.

* * *

She touches one by accident.

That's what she'll tell Quentin later — *by accident* — though accidents are just truths arriving early. The Hollow taught her that. Everything unmade comes back eventually, wearing a different face.

The bead is warm against her palm, warmer than glass should be, and then it opens like an eye.

Rain. A bridge made of black stone slick with it. A city below, Vell, but Vell younger, angrier, its bells still ringing something holy. And running across that bridge — running *toward* her, through her, inside her own borrowed eyes — a woman with wet hair plastered to her cheek and terror written all over her face like a confession.

Quentin. Younger Quentin. Seven years younger.

Behind her, boots. Many boots. Wardens' boots, iron-shod, and ahead of her a door that shouldn't be there, a door standing alone on the bridge with no walls around it, and the door is open, and through the doorway there is nothing at all.

Not darkness. Darkness is a thing. This is the absence of things. The Hollow, before it learned to hunger politely. Before the covenant frayed down to a child sleeping in mud outside a memory-thief's door.

Younger Quentin stops at the threshold. She's carrying something cupped against her chest like a wounded bird — and Sable feels it now, feels it through seven years and one stolen bead, the weight of it, the *shape* of it. Not a bird. Not a jewel.

A memory. But not a small one, not a mother's-voice memory or a first-snow memory. This one is enormous. It has architecture. It has pillars and a name written in the First Tongue and four other names braided around it like roots around a stone.

The keystone. The remembering-that-holds-the-seal-together. The covenant's heart, kept not in a vault but in a mind — because some promises only hold if someone remembers them perfectly, and the five bearers had chosen one among them to be the vessel.

And Quentin Blackwood, hunted, bleeding, out of options, did the only thing she knew how to do.

She stole it.

From herself? No — Sable turns the bead over inside the memory, looking for the seams. From the vessel. From whoever was supposed to carry it. Except the vessel was empty. The vessel had run. The vessel had been fleeing Dorian Ash and his ledger of names and his iron-shod certainty, and the covenant's heart was sitting in her skull like a coin left in a coat pocket, and she didn't even know —

She didn't even know.

That's the worst part. That's the part that makes the bead go cold in Sable's hand. There's no villain in this memory. There's just a woman at a doorway between worlds, holding everything, understanding nothing, and behind her the boots getting closer, and she does the desperate arithmetic of the hunted: *I can't carry this where I'm going.*

So she pulls it out. The way she pulls griefs out of grieving men in the market. The way she pulls first loves out of widows for a copper apiece. She reaches into her own head with her own forbidden hands and takes the covenant's keystone memory and seals it into a bead of glass and copper, and then she steps back from the doorway — and the door closes, and somewhere very far away a seal begins, slowly, over seven years, to rot.

The last thing in the bead is young Quentin's face, looking down at the charm in her palm, frowning at it like a woman who's found a key and forgotten the lock.

Then the memory ends, and Sable is standing in the workshop with the bead cooling against her skin, and her nose is bleeding, and Quentin is in the doorway holding two bowls of stew and looking at her like the floor has opened.

* * *

"What did you touch."

Not a question. Quentin sets the bowls down without looking at them, which is how Sable knows the stew is about to become irrelevant.

"One," Sable says. Her voice sounds far away to herself, the way it always does after the Tongue has been moving through her. "The third one from the window."

"Those aren't toys." Quentin crosses the room in three strides and takes Sable's chin in her hand, tilting her face toward the lamp, checking the blood, and even now, even furious, her thumb is gentle under Sable's jaw. Sable hates how much she notices that. "They're not sealed for someone like you. They're not sealed for anyone. If one cracked open in your head—"

"It didn't crack." Sable looks up at her. "It opened. Like a door."

Something moves behind Quentin's eyes. Something that recognizes the word and wishes it didn't.

"Sable." Very quiet now. The dangerous kind of quiet, the kind that means fear wearing calm's clothing. "What did you see?"

And here is the moment. Here is the fork in the night. Sable could lie. She's good at lying; the First Tongue is mostly lying, truth bent until it fits inside a mouth. She could say *rain, just rain, a bridge,* and Quentin would narrow her eyes and let it go, because letting things go is the only mercy either of them knows how to practice.

But the Hollow ate a bearer three days ago, and the streets are dissolving, and the bell-ringer with no voice held the line beside them, and Sable has spent nine hundred years learning exactly one lesson:

Secrets are just wounds you refuse to clean.

"You," she says. "Seven years ago. On a bridge. You were running from Dorian."

Quentin goes still. Not the stillness of guilt — the stillness of a woman listening for which direction the blow will come from.

"There was a door," Sable continues, and the Tongue rises in her throat without permission, making her words double, echo, ring like struck bronze. "*There was a door and you were carrying the covenant's heart in your head and you did not know it. You took it out. You put it in glass. You closed the door behind you.*"

"I don't—" Quentin starts, and stops. Her hand drifts up toward the shelf where the charms hang, forty-one small lights, and Sable watches her count them the way Sable counted them, and watches her arrive at the number she's afraid of. "Which one."

"The one you never sell." Sable points. Third from the window. The bead that hums. The bead that leans. "You keep it because you can't remember why you keep it. That's what's inside it. That's what's *missing* inside you."

"That's not possible." Quentin's laugh comes out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I would know. I'd feel a hole that size. Nobody loses something that big and just — walks around selling charms—"

"You walked around for seven years," Sable says gently, "selling pieces of everyone else's past because yours had a door-shaped gap in it, and you told yourself it was because you were careful."

Quentin reaches up. Her fingers stop a breath away from the bead. Sable can see the whole war happening in her wrist — the part of her that wants to know, the part that would rather die ignorant than learn she did this, the part that already knows and has been knowing all along in the cellar of herself where people bury the things that would kill them in daylight.

She touches it.

* * *

Sable sees it happen. She sees the memory come home.

It isn't gentle. Nothing true is. Quentin makes a sound like a rope snapping under load and goes to her knees on the workshop floor, and the bead falls and rolls, and every charm on the ceiling begins to ring at once — soft, mournful, like bells at a funeral for someone whose name nobody wrote down.

Because that's what this is. A funeral. The Quentin who stood on that bridge died seven years ago and never got buried; she just kept walking around, hollowed at the center, calling the hole *carefulness.*

"No." Quentin's fists are on the floorboards. Her knuckles have gone white as bone. "No, no — I wouldn't have. I would have *known*. I'd have felt the covenant, I'd have—"

"You were running," Sable says. She kneels across from her, close enough to see the tears Quentin refuses to acknowledge falling. "Running people don't know anything. Running people only know *away*."

"The seal. The bearers. The—" Quentin lifts her head, and Sable has never seen a person look so thoroughly demolished and so awake at the same time. "There were five of us. Five. I remember — I remember *now*, I remember all of it, the naming, the vow, the—" Her breath hitches. "I was the vessel. I volunteered. I said my mind was the safest vault in Vell because no Warden would ever find a thief's ordinary thoughts worth searching, and they agreed, they *trusted me*, and then Dorian came with his list and I ran, and I—" 

She looks at the bead on the floor. Small. Round. Worthless-looking. The heart of a dead promise in a trinket she'd have sold for three coppers if business were slow enough.

"I broke the world," Quentin says, "to survive a Tuesday."

The wryness is armor going on even now, even mid-collapse, and Sable almost loves her for it. Almost forgives her. Because here is the thing Sable understood in the bead, in the rain, in the doorway: Quentin didn't choose to break the seal. She chose not to die. The world broke itself against her desperation, the way worlds do — carelessly, without asking permission.

But the seal is broken, and the Hollow is open, and the fourth bearer is dead, and the fifth is a bell-ringer with no voice, and the reckoning doesn't care whose fault it was.

"So take it back," Sable says.

Quentin stares at her.

"The memory. It's yours. It came home." Sable picks up the bead and presses it into Quentin's shaking palm and folds her fingers over it. "Now you know what you did. Now you know what it cost. So pick it up, thief. Pick up everything you dropped."

Outside, far off toward the river district, something vast exhales — the sound the Hollow makes when it remembers it's hungry. The lamps gutter. The forty-one charms swing on their wires, ringing their small sad notes.

Quentin closes her hand around the bead. When she stands, she is different. Taller somehow, though nothing has grown. Steadier, though her eyes are red. A woman with her whole self returned to her, carrying all of it, even the shameful parts.

Especially the shameful parts.

"Get your coat," Quentin Blackwood says, and her voice is the First Tongue's twin now — doubled, ringing, sure. "We're going to need Dorian's ledger. And then we're going to need a miracle."

Sable grins, sharp as a splinter.

"Good," she says. "I know where miracles keep their doors."
