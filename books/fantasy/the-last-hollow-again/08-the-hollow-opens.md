## 8. The Hollow Opens

The world ended on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Tuesdays were always the day Vell chose to disappoint me.

I was mending a kettle charm when the light went wrong.

Not dark—wrong. The gray afternoon slanting through my shutters bent sideways, as if the sun had remembered an appointment elsewhere. The kettle in my hands grew cold so fast it frosted over, and every charm in the shop—the hidden ones, the ones I'd spent seven years pretending didn't hum—screamed at once. Not aloud. In the teeth. In the fillings of old scars. Magic doesn't have a voice for panic, so it borrows yours.

Sable was already at the door. Of course she was. Children always know before we do; they haven't yet learned to pretend otherwise.

"It's opening," she said, in Vellish first, then again in the First Tongue, and the second saying made the floorboards bow like a struck chord. Her eyes had that look I hated most—not the feral wariness of the girl who'd appeared on my doorstep, but the ancient, terrible calm of something remembering itself. "It's not angry, Quentin. That's the worst part. It doesn't even know we're here."

"Wonderful," I said. "An apocalypse with no hard feelings."

Oren signed something sharp from the corner—*the bell, now*—and I was already moving. Seven years of running teaches you to pack fast: charms, coin, the ledger, and Sable's hand clamped in mine like I could hold her against the end of things by grip strength alone. We went out through the cellar, into the undercity where Ilex kept her candles and her secrets.

We came up on Tanner's Row, and Tanner's Row was gone.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because later—assuming there is a later—I don't want to remember it prettier than it was.

There was no fire. No flood. No dramatic rending of stone. The street simply *stopped agreeing to exist*. The cobbles nearest the breach weren't destroyed; they were *forgotten*. You could watch it happen. A lamppost stood one moment, and the next it wasn't gone so much as never-had-been, its absence shaped exactly like a lamppost, and then even the shape unraveled. Where it had been there was nothing, and the nothing had texture. It looked like the inside of a word you've lost mid-sentence.

People were still in it. A fishmonger I'd bought eels from—a sour man, generous with his scraps—was halfway through shouting at the sky when the forgetting took him. He didn't scream. Worse: he smiled, the way you smile at a name finally recalled, and then he wasn't anyone, and then he wasn't.

"Don't look at it directly," Sable whispered, dragging me back by two fingers. "If you remember yourself too loudly, it hears."

"That's not comforting."

"I know." She said it with such gentle sincerity that I almost laughed, which would have been hysterical in every sense.

The breach sat where the Old Exchange used to be—a wound in the air maybe thirty feet across, edges rippling like heat-shimmer, and beyond it not blackness but *unmaking*, a color my eyes refused to file. And pouring out of it, slow as smoke from a doused candle, came the memories. Other people's memories, unmoored, drifting through the street like jellyfish: a wedding dance with no dancers, a mother's lullaby humming itself to no child, a soldier's last thought walking on legs no longer attached to anything.

Vell had outlawed magic because magic was dangerous. Vell had never once considered what happens when you outlaw the people who know how to close doors.

* * *

Ilex was waiting for us at the mouth of Candlemaker's Close, and she was already burning.

Not literally—though with Ilex you learned to check. She stood in her doorway like a hearth that had decided to become a fortress, both palms raised toward the drifting edge of the Hollow, and everything she was poured out of her in threads of gold-white light. Seventy years of herb-lore, of grudges, of songs in dead languages, of the exact weight of a sleeping child in your arms—all of it feeding a net she was weaving between the Hollow's rim and the rest of the city.

"You're late," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not.

"We came as fast as—" Oren began signing, but she cut him off with a look.

"I know what you came to say. Don't waste breath on it." She glanced at Sable, and something in her craggy face went soft and furious at once. "There she is. The little seal. Do you know how long I've waited to see you, girl? Three hundred years my line kept the third flame lit, and every one of us died wondering if we'd held it for nothing."

"Ilex." I stepped forward. "Come with us. You don't have to stand here. The rim isn't fixed—we can carry the binding point with us, Sable says, if all three bearers move together—"

"And leave this gap open while you walk?" She shook her head. "Every minute this edge drifts, it eats another street. Another memory. Another *child*, Quentin. Someone has to be the wall."

"Then let me be the wall. I've stolen enough. It's time I gave some back."

That earned me the driest smile ever produced by a dying woman. "Child, you couldn't hold this line ten seconds. Your magic is borrowed goods. Mine is *owed*." She turned back to the Hollow, and her shoulders squared like a woman settling in for a long night's work. "Besides. I've been dead since my daughter passed. This is just the paperwork."

Sable made a sound then that I'll hear until I die—a small, cracked thing, the noise a seal makes when the covenant starts to cost what it was always going to cost. She ran to Ilex and pressed her forehead against the old woman's knuckles and spoke in the First Tongue, rapid and pleading, and I understood none of it except the shape of *please*.

Ilex answered in kind, slow and formal, the way you speak to something sacred. Then, in Vellish, for my benefit: "She asked if it hurts. Tell her no. Tell her it's like putting down a heavy bag after a long road."

"She's lying," Sable said to me, tears cutting clean lines down her grimy face.

"I know," I said. "They always do. It's the last kindness left to us."

The Hollow surged. I don't have a better word—the rim swelled outward like a tide deciding the shore had gotten ideas above itself—and Ilex *roared*, a sound with no human throat behind it, and her golden threads snapped taut and caught the unmaking like a fishing net catching the sea. For one impossible moment the breach stopped. Held. Froze mid-devouring like a wolf photographed mid-leap.

"GO," she bellowed. "Rim of the Hollow, beneath the city, where the first river drowned—the ledger will show you, girl, you've got the founder's own map! And Quentin—" her eyes found mine over her blazing hands, and in them I saw three hundred years of women holding lines nobody thanked them for, "—when you get there, when you pay whatever it costs—don't you dare flinch. My whole life I watched thieves flinch. Be better than the story they wrote about you."

"Ilex—"

"*Go.*"

We went. I did not look back, because I am a coward, and because I knew that if I looked back I'd see the moment the net failed, and some things you should only learn about afterward, in the privacy of grief.

We heard it anyway. A sound like a library exhaling. And then the light behind us changed, and Sable sobbed once, sharply, and kept running, and Oren's hand on my shoulder was the only reason my legs remembered their job.

Two bearers. One seal. One thief with stolen goods and a debt she'd only just learned she owed.

* * *

Beneath Vell, the drowned river still ran—black water in a black channel, older than the city that had built itself on top of its corpse. The ledger led us down stairways that predated stairs, through vaults where the founders' seals had been pried off walls and sold, and all the while the ceiling above us groaned like the hull of a sinking ship.

Because time was bleeding. I don't mean that poetically. Minutes came apart around us. We'd walk a corridor for what felt like an hour and emerge having aged minutes—or the reverse; once Oren's beard was suddenly longer, silver at the chin where brown had been. Sable navigated by touch, palm flat to the stone, murmuring the First Tongue like a prayer, and where she touched, the stone remembered being stone and behaved accordingly.

"The Hollow eats the past first," she told me as we waded the shallows of the dead river. "So we're safe as long as we're going somewhere it hasn't finished eating."

"And when we arrive?"

Her hand found mine in the dark. Small fingers. Mud still in the creases, from the first night, from the threshold. Some things cling.

"When we arrive," she said, "you'll owe it everything you stole. And Quentin—it'll ask for the newest things first. The things you love best. That's how wounds drink."

Ahead, the channel opened into a cavern where the drowned river fell away into a darkness that wasn't dark—it was *absent*—and along the rim of that absence, faint as old scars, glimmered four broken sigils in the rock. Places where bearers had once stood. Two still flickered: the bell-bearer's silence, and, trembling like a guttering wick, Sable herself.

And rising out of the absence, vast and patient and utterly indifferent, came the attention of the Hollow—turning toward us like a head turning toward a heartbeat.

Sable squeezed my hand.

"It knows your name," she whispered. "It's known it for seven years."
