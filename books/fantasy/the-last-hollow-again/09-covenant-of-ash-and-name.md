## 9. Covenant of Ash and Name

*Quentin*

The Hollow didn't look like a wound. I'd expected a scar in the world, something jagged, something that bled. Instead it looked like the place where someone had started erasing a sentence and then given up halfway through.

The street simply stopped. Cobbles faded to gray suggestion, gray suggestion to nothing, and beyond the nothing there was a light that wasn't light—the color of forgetting, if forgetting had a color. My charms hummed against my skin like nervous insects. All of them. Even the ones I'd buried at the bottom of my satchel years ago because they'd done things I didn't like remembering.

Which was, of course, most of them.

"Quentin." Sable tugged my sleeve. Her eyes had gone silver again, the way they did when the First Tongue rose in her throat unbidden. "It knows you're here."

"It and everyone else in Vell," I said. Behind us, down the bleeding hill of the Bellows district, came the sound of boots. Warden boots. Dorian's boots, if I knew anything about rhythm—and I did; I'd taught him half his lockpicking cadence back when we were children stealing bread instead of memories.

"Is it true?" Sable asked. "What the bell-ringer said. That you have to give them all."

I kept my eyes on the edge of the nothing. "Tam says a lot of things. He also traded his voice for silence, so I'd take his opinions with appropriate salt."

"Quentin."

There it was. The First Tongue wrapped around my name like a small hand around a finger, and I couldn't lie to her. Not because of magic—because she was seven years old and already carrying the weight of two realms, and lying to her would have been one cruelty too many in a life I had stocked full of them.

"Yes," I said. "All of them. Every charm. Every stolen memory. Including—" I made myself say it. "Including you."

Her face did something complicated. Children are honest with their faces; it's one of the few advantages they have over me.

"You won't know me," she said.

"No."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"That's generally how sacrifice works, love. Terrible business model. No wonder nobody does it twice."

She laughed. It was a wet, broken little laugh, but it was a laugh, and I decided right then that if the universe wanted my memories as the price for keeping this child alive, the universe was getting a bargain and I hoped it felt guilty about it forever.

The boots crested the hill.

* * *

*Sable*

The man called Dorian walked ahead of the gray-cloaks, and behind his eyes there was a door, and behind the door there was a room, and in the room Quentin was laughing over a stolen watch while he pretended not to laugh too. I saw it because the Hollow lets me see. It has been letting me see since before I could walk, and mostly what it shows me is terrible, but sometimes—like now—it shows me something worse. Something sad.

"You're late," Quentin said to him. She always said things like that when she was frightened. I had learned her tells the way other children learn letters.

Dorian stopped ten paces away. His Wardens spread out along the rim of nothing, and I watched their faces as the Hollow showed them their own worst forgettings—a mother's voice, a name on a grave, a promise made in the dark. Gray-cloaks were supposed to feel nothing. Magic had been cut out of Vell like a rotten tooth. But you cannot cut out longing, and longing is just memory with nowhere to go.

"Sable," Dorian said. He said my name wrong. Everyone says it wrong except Quentin and the dead bell-ringers. "Step away from the witch, child. Whatever she's told you, whatever she's shown you—"

"She hasn't shown me anything." The First Tongue filled my mouth like river water. I let it spill. "You broke it. Your grandfather's grandfather wrote it in the ledger: *the seal must fail for Vell to be safe.* You were never protecting us from magic. You were protecting the breaking."

His hand went to his sword. Then it stopped.

That was the moment. I have thought about it every day since, and it was that small: a hand stopping halfway to a hilt, because a seven-year-old spoke a dead language and meant every word.

"I had orders," Dorian said, and his voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "I had a city to hold together. Seven years ago the wards were failing everywhere and the Council said—"

"The Council said, and you obeyed, and a covenant died, and a girl was left in the mud with a hole in her chest where a seal used to be." Quentin's voice was very quiet. Quiet-Quentin was the dangerous Quentin. "I know, brother. I was there. I helped you burn the archive. I just didn't know what we were burning."

"You didn't ask."

"Neither did you."

And oh, the look that passed between them. I am a seal and a vessel and the last word of an old covenant, and even I flinched from it. Some wounds aren't between realms at all. They're between people, and those are the ones that don't close unless somebody bleeds on purpose.

Dorian turned to his Wardens.

"Ground your pikes," he said.

They stared at him.

"Ground them. That's an order. And if the Council asks why—" his voice went strange, almost young, "—tell them Commander Ash finally read the ledger."

* * *

*Quentin*

So he did it. Dorian Ash, Warden commander, keeper of the law that had hunted me across seven years and four false names—he grounded his pikes at the rim of the end of everything and stood aside.

Don't mistake me. I wasn't grateful. Gratitude requires believing people can change, and I'm a professional cynic. But watching him kneel in the ash with his sword laid flat before him like an offering, I understood something that made the next part bearable:

He hadn't come to stop me. He'd come because some part of him had known for seven years, the way I had, and we'd both been running from the same truth in opposite directions.

"Dorian." He looked up. Gray eyes. I'd forgotten they were gray. No—I hadn't forgotten; I'd sold the memory of them to a merchant in the Salt Market my second year in Vell, for enough coin to eat for a month. Funny, the economy of forgetting. "When this is done, I won't remember you either."

"I know."

"That doesn't anger you?"

He smiled, and for one moment he was twelve years old again, holding a stolen watch, pretending not to laugh. "Everything I've done these seven years was to keep from feeling this. Take it. Take all of it. It's the only mercy anyone's offered me."

Well. When he put it like that.

I knelt at the edge of the Hollow and opened my satchel.

* * *

*Sable*

The charms came out one by one, and each one was a little glass bead with a storm inside it, and each storm was a piece of my Quentin.

I watched her hold the first one up to the Hollow's rim and I heard her say the words Tam had taught her, the old binding words, the words that mean *this was mine and I give it freely*. The bead went dark. Somewhere inside it, a memory of rain on a roof in a city that no longer existed stopped being hers.

Then the next. A woman's face—her mother, maybe, or someone who loved her like a mother. Dark. Gone.

Then the next. A bridge. A boy. A promise.

Then the next, and the next, and the next, and I counted because counting was the only thing keeping me standing, and somewhere past forty I stopped being able to breathe, because I knew which beads were left. The small ones. The warm ones. The ones that hummed when I came near, like they knew me.

"Wait." My voice cracked. "Quentin. Wait."

She looked at me. Her hands were shaking. Hers, shaking—Quentin Blackwood, who never shook, who once picked a Warden's pocket while complimenting his boots.

"Keep one," I said in the First Tongue, and then again in hers, in case the Hollow only listened to one language. "Keep one. Hide it. Cheat. You cheat at everything. Cheat at this."

And she laughed—that real laugh, the one that lived low in her chest—and tears ran down her face, and she said, "Oh, love. If I cheat, it unravels. And if it unravels, I lose you permanently instead of temporarily. Temporary is better. Temporary is the whole trick of being alive."

"That's a horrible philosophy."

"It's the only one that works." She held out the next bead. It glowed amber, and through its skin I could see a kitchen, a candle, a girl covered in mud eating soup like she'd never seen food before. Me. It was me. It was our first night, and she was giving it away, and I—

I spoke.

Not the binding words. Not yet. First I spoke the oldest words I knew, the ones the Hollow taught me in the dark before I had a name: *what is given in love cannot be taken by death; what is named in love cannot be unmade by forgetting.*

The Hollow shivered.

Quentin pressed the bead of our first night to the rim of nothing, and whispered, "This was mine, and she was mine, and I give them freely," and the bead went dark, and I felt it go, felt the shape of her knowing me lift out of the world like heat off a stone.

But the words stayed. My words. The covenant took the memories and left the words, because words are not memory—words are what we make *from* memory, and those belong to whoever speaks them.

She gave them all. Forty-one beads. Forty-one storms going dark. And when the last one sank into the Hollow, the emptiness shuddered and pulled tight like a knot drawn closed, and the light-that-wasn't-light turned gold, and the ground under our feet remembered itself all the way back down the hill, cobbles stitching themselves whole.

Sable, the Hollow said, in a voice like the space between bell-rings. *Seal, speak the name.*

I spoke the name.

Not my name. The covenant's name. Five bearers—one dead, one silent, one kneeling in ash, one broken open at last, and one small girl with silver eyes holding the whole wound shut with two words in the First Tongue.

The gold light folded itself into the ground and vanished.

And Quentin Blackwood looked up at me with clear, empty eyes—the eyes of a stranger—and asked, very politely,

"Do I know you?"

* * *

*Quentin*

I woke on a hillside that smelled like rain and new-baked bread, which was odd, because hillsides don't bake bread.

A girl sat beside me. Small, dark-eyed, mud still crusted at her hem as though she'd worn the same clothes for days and intended to wear them several more. She was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—which irritated me profoundly, because I could always read expressions. It was rather my trade.

"Hello," I said. "Do I know you?"

Something crossed her face—grief, maybe, or its aftermath—but she wiped it away fast, the way you wipe a table before company comes. She stuck out her hand.

"No," she said. "But you're about to. I'm Sable. And you owe me breakfast."

I looked at this stranger, this grubby, imperious, impossible child, and felt something turn over in my chest—an old lock recognizing an old key, though the door it belonged to had been bricked up.

"Well," I heard myself say, as I took her hand. "I suppose I'd better find out what else I owe you."
