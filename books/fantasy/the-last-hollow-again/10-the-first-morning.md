## 10. The First Morning

I wake before the sun, which is a habit the Hollow gave me and I have decided to keep, because a girl should own at least one thing that came out of the worst year of her life without being stolen back.

The room above the bakery is small and smells of yeast and rain. Quentin sleeps in the other bed with her back to me, one hand curled under her cheek like a child's, like mine used to be. She has been sleeping like that for eleven days. Eleven days since the rim of the Hollow, since she poured herself out of her own head and into the wound between worlds and stitched it shut with everything she was.

Everything she *was*. That's the trick of it. She is still here. She just isn't the same here.

Her eyes opened yesterday for the first time, gray as river slate and completely empty of me, and she looked at my face the way you look at a stranger who is standing too close on a crowded street. Polite. Wary. A little annoyed.

"You're the baker's girl," she said.

"I'm not," I said, "but it's a start."

She frowned at that, and something behind her frown flickered — not memory, nothing so generous — more like a draft under a door. Then it closed, and she asked if there was bread, and I said yes, and that was our first conversation of the rest of our lives.

* *

Vell is different this morning. I can hear it through the window: no bells of warning, only bells of hour. No boots in rhythm down the cobbles. Instead there are voices, dozens of them, arguing in the square the way free people argue, which is to say badly and at length and with tremendous joy.

The accord went up on the Warden Hall door three days ago. Written by bearers, not kings — Bell-Master Corren dictated it in his new voice, which is not a voice at all but a way he has of ringing a small handbell against his ribs while Tamir writes down what the tones mean. The fourth bearer, old Yew of the flooded quarter, signed with a pressed leaf. I signed last, in the First Tongue, which looks like frost forming on paper and cannot be forged, which is rather the point.

The accord says: magic is lawful in Vell. Magic is answerable. Every charm must be spoken aloud before its maker, every memory-trade recorded in the open ledger where the old secret ledger used to hide. It says the Wardens will guard doors now instead of hunting through them. And it says, in a line Corren rang so hard he cracked the bell, that *no child shall be made a seal again.*

That line took two days to negotiate. I sat through every hour of it with my hands folded and my chin up, and when they finally agreed, I did not cry until I got home, because a girl who was almost a lock deserves to decide for herself when she becomes a person.

* *

Quentin comes downstairs mid-morning wearing one of my spare shirts, which is enormous on her and makes her look like she is playing at being someone else, which, to be fair, she is.

"Morning," she says, cautious.

"Morning."

She stands in the doorway of the kitchen a moment, working at something. I watch her work at it. She has always been a woman who picks locks; apparently that doesn't stop at doors.

"You knew me," she says finally. "Before. Didn't you. Everyone in this city looks at me like I'm a ghost they liked."

"Yes," I say.

"And we were—" She stops. Starts again. "Were we family?"

And here is the thing nobody tells you about loving someone who forgot you: you get to choose how much truth to hand them, and every amount is wrong, and you give it anyway.

"We were," I say. "You found me at your door in the rain seven years after you'd lost everything, and you didn't want to, and you did it anyway. You fed me things I didn't know the names of. You taught me to pick pockets and to lie and to be kind anyway. You gave up everything you remembered about yourself so the world wouldn't end." I shrug, because my voice is doing something embarrassing. "So. Yes. Family."

She sits down across from me very slowly, the way you sit down when the floor might be lying.

"That sounds like someone worth being," she says.

"She was all right," I say, wry, because if I don't make a joke my whole face will come apart. "Bit of a thief."

Quentin laughs — startled out of her, short and rusty, and then surprised at herself for laughing. "That feels true," she says, wonderingly. "That feels like mine even though I don't remember it."

"It is yours," I say. "It's all still yours. It's just waiting."

* *

We spend the afternoon in the market, because Quentin says she wants to see the city, and I say fine, but I hold her sleeve the whole way, and neither of us mentions it.

Vell has never looked like this. Charms hang openly from stall-posts — little glass beads of borrowed courage, ribbons knotted with safe-passage, a woman selling secondhand lullabies by the bowlful. Children chase sparks down the gutter-streams and no one shouts. Two former Wardens stand at the corner not arresting anyone, looking faintly embarrassed about their entire previous careers, and one of them waves at me, and I wave back, magnanimously, because I am becoming gracious in my old age, which is twelve.

Quentin keeps stopping. At a charm-seller's table she picks up a ribbon of knotted red thread and turns it over and says, without thinking, "Sloppy work, the anchor-knot's backwards," and then freezes, staring at her own hands.

"You know knots," I say.

"I know *knots*," she agrees, shaken and delighted. "How do I know knots?"

"Because some things live deeper than remembering," I tell her. "Corren says the body keeps what the mind gives away. He rings it, so it must be true."

All afternoon it goes like that. She haggles in a dialect she shouldn't know and wins. She steps around a broken cobble without looking, the exact step, the learned step, the step of a woman who ran these alleys for twenty years. Once, passing the mouth of Cutter's Lane, she stops dead and puts her hand flat against the wall and breathes, and I don't ask, and she doesn't explain, but when she takes her hand away there's a small charm glowing there — a warding sigil, drawn perfect, drawn *from nothing*, drawn by hands that remember being kind in dark places.

She looks at it a long time.

"Whoever I was," she says quietly, "she left things everywhere, didn't she."

"Everywhere," I say. "You're going to be finding yourself in Vell for years. Consider it a treasure hunt."

* *

Evening. Corren comes by with his handbell and his ledger and his terrible tea, and asks — ringing gently, asking with tones instead of words — whether the fifth bearer will attend the covenant's first renewal at moonrise.

"Fifth bearer," Quentin repeats, when I've translated. "That's you?"

"That's me."

"What does a bearer do?"

"Holds a piece of the world closed," I say. "Or held. Now mostly we hold meetings. There's a schedule. It's very boring. You'll love it."

She snorts. But later, when Corren has gone and the light is going amber in the window, she says, "Take me. To the moonrise. If I'm allowed."

"You're allowed anywhere," I say. "You bought that for everyone."

* *

The Hollow's rim is a garden now.

That's the only word for it. Where the streets dissolved into unmade memory, where Asher died holding the line, where I spoke the First Tongue until my throat bled and Quentin poured out her whole self like water into cracked earth — there is green. Grass growing in the shapes of streets that no longer exist. A tree coming up through what used to be the Warden Hall steps. Wildflowers in colors that don't have names yet, because the Hollow left a few of its colors behind when it closed, and Vell hasn't decided what to call them.

Asher's name is carved on a stone there, and four of us put flowers on it, and the fifth of us — Quentin — stands a little apart, frowning at the carving like it itches.

"Asher," she reads. "Why does that name feel like—"

"Grief?" I offer.

"Yeah." She presses her knuckles briefly to her sternum. "Like grief with a debt attached."

"He was your friend," I say. "He died so the rest of us could stand here. You'd want to know that. People should get told who they've loved, even if they can't carry it yet."

She nods slowly. Then, unexpectedly, she reaches over and squeezes my shoulder once — an awkward, deliberate gesture, a stranger practicing intimacy — and says, "You're very young to be carrying all this."

"I had help," I say. "I had a lot of help. From a really stubborn thief."

* *

Night. Home. The bakery room with its two beds and its smell of tomorrow's bread already rising from below.

Quentin stands at the window a long time, watching the city glow with lawful charms, and I pretend to be asleep because sometimes people say true things to a sleeping room.

"Seven years ago," she murmurs to the glass, "someone broke the world to survive it. And then a kid showed up at her door and she broke herself to fix it. Whoever she was..." Her reflection watches me not-sleeping, and she knows I'm listening, and she says it anyway. "I hope I get to be her again. But if I don't — I hope I get to be someone she'd have liked."

Then she crosses the room, and I keep my breathing slow, and she tucks the blanket up over my shoulder the way you'd do for a child you chose, and her hand hesitates at my hair, and then — gently, like completing a knot her fingers remember — she smooths it once.

"Goodnight, Sable," she whispers. My name. My name, which no one told her, which she has known for exactly one day, except that she has known it forever, somewhere under everything.

I wait until she's asleep. Then I lie in the dark and let myself cry the good kind, the kind that doesn't cost anything anymore.

Family isn't remembered. I understand that now, down in the place where the Hollow used to sit inside me. Family is remade. Every morning, on purpose, freely. She is remaking it already and doesn't even know — or maybe she knows exactly, maybe that's what choosing means when the choosing is all you have.

Tomorrow she'll find another piece of herself glowing in a wall somewhere. Tomorrow the covenant meets, and there will be arguments, and Corren will crack a bell, and I will sign things in frost. Tomorrow the world holds, because a thief emptied herself into the gap and a child spoke it shut and strangers became bearers and bearers became family.

But tonight — tonight there is one more thing, and I only find it at dawn, getting up early as always.

On the kitchen table, in charcoal, in handwriting I have never seen and recognize instantly: a note propped against the bread.

*To the girl at the threshold,* it says. *I don't remember you. I'm going to learn. Start telling me who we were — and this time, leave nothing out. — Q*

Underneath, smaller, newer, added while I slept:

*P.S. Your name hums. Why does your name hum?*

And far off, past the garden where the Hollow closed, past the edge of everything we mended — something answers her question. Not a voice. Not a wound. A third thing, patient and new, waking in the grass with our colors on it, turning toward Vell like a face toward morning.

I put the note in my pocket, next to my heart, where I keep everything worth stealing.

Then I go upstairs to tell her everything, starting at the beginning — the rain, the door, the humming — and this time, nothing gets left out.
