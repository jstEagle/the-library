## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width, and Xanthe Thackeray decided—after a moment's honest deliberation—that this was none of her business.

Her business was the ledger open before her, bound in grey calfskin with brass corner-plates gone soft and round from a century of hands. Her business was the columns. Columns were honest in a way that almost nothing else in the Hollow Guild was: they added up or they did not, and if they did not, it was because someone had made an error, and errors could be found, named, corrected. Xanthe had spent eleven years finding them. She was very good at it. She suspected that if anyone ever thought to say so out loud, she would not know what to do with the compliment.

She dipped her pen, checked the candle again—still none of her business—and wrote:

*Received of Master Ellery Voss, one (1) jar, sealed wax, contents: childhood, early, approx. ages four through nine, summer-heavy. Condition: good. Smell of cut grass reported by buyer. Sold against future consideration.*

She paused, pen hovering, and considered whether *summer-heavy* belonged in an official record.

It did. That was the thing people outside the guild never understood. The ledgers were not merely accounts; they were the only place where the guild's wares could be described at all. You could not weigh a jar of somebody's childhood on a scale. You could not count borrowed luck in coins without cheating somebody, because luck was not fungible—a lucky week for a farmer was rain at the right time, and a lucky week for a smuggler was an empty customs house, and never the twain. So the guild kept its books the way other institutions kept their souls: carefully, and with adjectives.

Xanthe rather prided herself on her adjectives.

She turned back two pages to check an entry and found the one that had been nagging at her all evening. A fortnight past, the weather-broker Hask had sold a week of good weather to the vineyards above Corren Vale—sun for the ripening, gentle rain at night, no hail, all guaranteed. Fair enough. Weather was among the guild's more respectable commodities; it spoiled slowly and nobody missed it from wherever it came from. But tonight, reconciling the week's takings, she had found a second sale of the same week. Same dates. Same guarantees. Sold twice.

Sold twice was a problem. Sold twice was, specifically, *her* problem, because somewhere down the line a buyer was going to stand in a field under grey clouds holding a receipt, and receipts, in the Hollow Guild, were sacred in the way that nothing else was. The guild could sell your memories back to you at interest. It could not renege on a receipt. Even Magister Corvel, whose smile Xanthe trusted approximately as far as she could throw the guildhouse, observed that rule.

She worked backward through the entries, lips moving slightly, and found the error within the quarter hour. Hask had recorded the second sale on the wrong folio—his hand was a spider's funeral—and so the week appeared twice in the takings but only once in the obligations. She corrected the obligation column, initialed the amendment in the margin with the small neat *x.t.* that was her whole signature on the guild's memory, and allowed herself the tiny pleasure of a job properly done.

Outside, the city of Lowmark went about its night business. From the counting-room window she could hear the river barges knocking against the quay, a drunk singing something patriotic and getting most of the words wrong, and beneath it all the faint hum of the wards along the border hills—the sound you stopped hearing after a while, like your own heartbeat, until some night you lay awake and listened for it and felt obscurely comforted that it was still there.

Xanthe did not listen for the wards. She listened for the kettle in the corridor, which was about to sing, because Wren always made tea at the turn of the watch and always brought her a cup without being asked, and this was the closest thing to affection that either of them had been taught how to exchange.

* * *

"You balance books for a living," Wren said, setting the cup down amid the ledger pages with the confidence of someone who had never once knocked one over. "I want you to know I find that restful. Other people have dreams about falling. I'd have dreams about sums."

"Other people's dreams are also inventory," Xanthe said. "Nightmares go for three crowns the jar, fresh ones. Secondhand nightmares are nearly worthless unless the dreamer died in them."

"That's horrid."

"That's Tuesday." Xanthe wrapped both hands around the cup. Wren was nineteen, archive-trained, with ink permanently stained into the creases of her fingers and the particular ferocious competence of someone who had been sold to the guild young enough not to remember being sold. "How is the deep archive?"

"Wet," Wren said. "Sub-level three flooded again. Odji says the river remembers where it used to go and goes there when it feels like it."

"Odji," Xanthe said, "says a great many things."

"He's usually right, though. That's the annoying part." Wren leaned over the ledger, upside-down reading as easily as right-side-up, another archive habit. "Jar of a childhood. Whose?"

"Doesn't say. Never does. Privacy clause."

"Do you ever wonder—" Wren began, then stopped, and Xanthe watched her decide not to finish the question, the way you decide not to step off a well-lit path.

"I wonder everything," Xanthe said. "It's the answering that's discouraged."

This was true, and they both knew it was true, and the knowing sat between them for a moment like a third person at the table. In the Hollow Guild there were questions you asked and questions you filed. Xanthe had gotten very fast at filing. It was, she sometimes thought late at night, the actual skill she was paid for—not arithmetic, not penmanship, but the swift and painless filing of things that would otherwise keep her awake.

The kettle sang again, further away. Somewhere below, the vault doors turned on their counterweighted hinges, and the night-shift handlers began bringing up the items scheduled for morning release: a debt forgiven in blood and repackaged as a blessing; a door that opened onto nowhere in particular, popular with fugitives; a bottle of the smell of a house that had burned down twenty years ago, which the guild had bought from the fire itself, or so the provenance note claimed, in handwriting Xanthe had never been able to attribute.

"The magister wants the Voss account reviewed before market day," Wren said. "He said you'd want to do it personally."

Corvel wanting things done personally was generally a sign that someone else had already done them badly. "Did he say why?"

"He said—" Wren's brow furrowed, retrieving the exact words, because exactness was her religion too, a lesser god in the same pantheon as Xanthe's columns. "He said, 'Tell her the numbers should sing.'"

Xanthe looked at the ledger. The numbers did not sing. The numbers whispered, which was what honest numbers did, and any number that sang was performing for someone.

"Tell him I'll see to it," she said, and filed it.

* * *

The trouble with filing, she reflected much later, alone again with the candle down to a blue nub, was that files accumulated. Eleven years of them. Most nights they stayed shut. Some nights—one in ten, one in twenty—they stirred.

Tonight was a stirring night, though she would not have called it that. She would have called it *reconciling*, because she was reconciling, and because the work gave her hands something to do while the back of her mind walked corridors it preferred not to walk in daylight.

Item: the guild bought a childhood this morning. Ages four through nine. Someone had walked in—or been carried in—and sold six years of themselves, and had left the guildhouse lighter by six years, and had presumably been paid fairly by the measure of such things, which was to say paid exactly as little as they could be persuaded to accept. Where did the childhood *go*? Into the jar, yes. And after the jar? After the buyer uncorked it and wore those summers for a while? Did it return to its owner? Did it dissolve?

She had never asked. Eleven years, and she had never once asked, because asking meant the answer might be *no*, and *no* was a weight, and she carried quite enough weight already in the form of a calfskin ledger and an oath she did not discuss.

Item: the oath. She did not discuss it, so we will be brief. At nine years old, starving, soldier-orphaned, she had sworn herself to Vessia, the quiet god, the god of thresholds and closed doors, and the god had held her the way a wall holds a leaning body—impersonally, absolutely, without warmth and without collapse. For years she had felt that hold at the edge of every hard decision, a pressure like a hand between the shoulder blades: *not yet, not that way, hold*. Then seven years ago, between one dawn and the next, the pressure had simply stopped. No explanation. No withdrawal of gifts, because Vessia's gift had only ever been the holding. Just silence, vast and level, like a frozen lake where a voice used to be.

The guild's theologians said gods went silent when they slept. The guild's pragmatists said gods went silent when they died. Xanthe, who was both, said nothing, and kept her ledgers, and every year on the anniversary she lit one candle in an empty chapel and let it burn out unattended, which was either devotion or mourning depending on who you asked, and she had never asked herself which.

Item: she was content. This was the strangest item of all, and the hardest to file, because contentment has no column. She liked the work. She liked the quiet hours and the honest columns and Wren's terrible jokes and the way the guildhouse smelled of beeswax and cold stone. She was fed, and sheltered, and necessary in the narrow way that bookkeepers are necessary, and she had arranged her life so carefully around the shape of her own not-asking that the arrangement had become invisible to her, the way a well-fitted yoke is invisible to the ox.

Contentment built on willful blindness is still, day to day, indistinguishable from contentment. That is the trick of it. That is why it sells so well, and to whom.

She finished the Voss account. She blew out the candle, and in the dark the ledger's brass corners caught the last grey light from the window, and for no reason she could name she thought of the ward-hum under the evening sounds, steady as ever, and felt—as she had not felt since childhood—the faint animal urge to check on it. To walk to the window. To make sure.

She did not. She filed it.

She climbed the stairs to her cell with the ledger under her arm, because she always took it with her, because a bookkeeper who leaves her book unguarded is a bookkeeper who deserves what happens to her books, and because—and here was the thought she almost caught, almost examined, and did not—the ledger had begun, lately, to feel less like a tool and more like a leash, and she was not entirely certain which end of it she was holding.

In her cell, she slept the sleep of the conscientious, which is to say lightly, and dreamed, briefly, of a door.

Not one of the guild's doors-to-nowhere. Another kind. Older. Standing open in a hillside, with starlight coming through it from a country that—

She woke before the dream finished, heart going, hand already reaching for the ledger on the nightstand. The entry for tomorrow's releases was written in her own neat hand. Everything balanced. Everything always balanced.

Except, she noticed now, for the first time in eleven years, that the page for the coming week was already full. Appointments, deliveries, acquisitions—in her handwriting, in her numbering, dated forward.

She had not written them.

She sat in the dark with the ledger open on her knees, and far away at the edge of hearing—so faint she told herself she imagined it—the ward-hum stuttered, once, like a held breath.

Three days later, the stars went out.

Xanthe Thackeray, keeper of the Hollow Guild's accounts, did not learn this until she woke.
