## 2. Oath and Silence

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Xanthe finished reconciling the third column of the luck ledger, and she was fairly certain the discrepancy of four farthings was not, in fact, her error but Odji's habit of rounding up in his own favor whenever he ferried guild cargo across the Vess. She made a note of it in the margin — *O. rounds up. Dock him at quarter's end* — and then, because she was who she was, added a smaller note beneath it: *But he fed the ferry-cat all winter out of pocket. Forgive the four farthings.*

That was the trouble with keeping honest books for a dishonest enterprise. Sooner or later you learned everyone's small kindnesses, and then you couldn't punish them without also punishing the cat.

She blew out the candle stub, saved it for tomorrow — waste was a sin in the Hollow Guild, though not one of the seven that got you flayed — and sat for a moment in the dark of her cell above the counting room, listening to the guildhouse settle around her like an old dog circling down to sleep. Somewhere below, the vault doors turned their slow locks. Somewhere above, the roof tiles ticked as they cooled.

Seven years ago tonight, her god had stopped speaking to her.

She knew the date the way farmers know frost dates: not from any almanac, but in her bones, in the particular quality of the silence. On most nights the quiet where Vessia lived felt like a held breath. Tonight it always felt like an empty chair.

Xanthe pulled her blanket over her knees and let herself remember, which was a luxury she permitted exactly once a year, on this night, the way other people permitted themselves a drink.

* * *

She had been nine years old, maybe ten — the war had eaten her birthday along with her village, so no one could say for certain — and she had been starving in a hedgerow outside Corve's Crossing with a rusted pike too heavy for her arms and a sergeant who'd stopped pretending anyone would come back for the rear guard.

The soldiers of the border companies had a saying: *Every man serves somebody, and the clever ones serve something.* Men served generals, and generals served lords, and lords served whoever paid. But there were older things to serve. Things that took oaths.

The chapel at Corve's Crossing had burned, like everything else, but its back wall still stood, and on that wall someone had painted — long ago, in colors that survived fire better than mortar — a figure robed in grey with no face at all, only a smooth oval like an egg or a closed door. Beneath it, in letters half flaked away: **VESSIA KEEPS WHAT IS KEPT.**

Xanthe hadn't understood it. She'd been nine. What she understood was that the figure looked like it had nothing left to take from her, and that when she knelt in the ash before it and said, out loud, in the cracked voice of a starving child, *"I'll keep what's kept if you'll keep me,"* something had answered.

Not words. Words came later, when she was older and the bond had deepened into the channel the sworn called *the ledger-line* — a thread of awareness running between her chest and somewhere vast and patient and grey. What answered first was warmth. A single pulse of it, like a hand laid briefly on the crown of her head, and then gone.

She had cried, because no one had touched her kindly in two years, and because even once and gone, it was more than she'd expected from the universe.

Vessia, she learned in the years after, was not a war god, though sworn soldiers fought well under the silence. Vessia was a god of holdings. Of thresholds and storehouses, of contracts and custody, of the sacred principle that some things must be kept safe even when keeping them costs more than they're worth. Her temples had been banks before banks had names. Her priests had been wardens of hostages, guardians of heirlooms, minders of borders. And her sworn — her small, scattered company of the oathbound — kept accounts.

*"An honest ledger is a form of prayer,"* her first mistress had told her, a one-eyed ex-soldier named Dame Ferren who taught Xanthe her numbers with a switch and her theology with stories. *"Every entry is a promise that the world happened the way you wrote it. Lie in your books and you lie to the god. The god doesn't punish liars, girl. She just stops being able to see them."*

Xanthe had decided, at thirteen, that being seen by Vessia was the entire point of existence. It was a simple religion and she practiced it devoutly: keep true books, speak true words, hold what was entrusted to you, ask nothing back except the thread of presence in your chest that said, wordlessly, every morning, *I am here. You are seen.*

And then, seven years ago tonight, on the eve of her thirtieth year, the thread had gone quiet.

Not faded. Not thinned. *Severed*, as cleanly as if snipped with shears. She had woken at midnight with her hand pressed flat to her sternum and the absolute certainty that something on the other end of the line had either died or hung up.

She'd gone to Dame Ferren, who by then was old and half-blind, and found the old woman already sitting up in the dark, waiting for her.

"You feel it too," Xanthe had said.

"Every sworn soul in the country feels it, I'd wager." Ferren's voice was steady, which frightened Xanthe more than weeping would have. "Gods go quiet, girl. It happens. Wars, plagues, bad centuries. The old texts call it *the withdrawn hand*. Doesn't mean she's gone."

"Doesn't mean she's here, either."

"No." Ferren had reached out and gripped Xanthe's wrist with surprising strength. "So here's what you do until she comes back, or doesn't. You keep the books anyway. You be seen whether or not you're seeing. That's the whole test of a faith, Xanthe. Praying when the god answers is commerce. Praying into silence—" the old woman had smiled with three teeth, "*that's love.*"

Xanthe had carried that sentence for seven years like a stone in her boot. Some days it grounded her. Most days it just hurt.

* * *

Present-day Xanthe was still holding the stone when the knock came at her door — three soft raps, evenly spaced, in the unmistakable rhythm of a man who had never once knocked without meaning something by it.

"Ledger-keeper." Magister Corvel's voice through the wood was warm as banked coals. "A moment, if the accounts can spare you."

They couldn't. They never could. "Come in, Magister."

Corvel entered the way he entered everywhere: as if the room had been waiting for him and ought to be grateful. He was a spare, silvering man with the kind of handsome that comes from good bone structure rather than good living, and he wore his grey magister's robes the way other men wore armor. In his hands, wrapped in linen, was a small object the size of a plum.

"I've brought you something for the anniversary," he said.

"There's no anniversary."

"Of course there is. Seven years since the Withdrawal." He set the object on her desk and unwrapped it with the delicacy of a man unveiling a relic. Inside lay a jar — glass, stoppered with wax, sealed with the guild's hollow-circle sigil. Something moved inside it. Something that glowed faintly, the color of late afternoon light through kitchen windows.

Xanthe went very still. "That's a memory-jar."

"It is." Corvel smiled. "Guild stock. Bottled childhood, grade three — common memories, gently extracted, fully consensual, all taxes paid. We've forty of them in the deep vault. This one came in last month from a farmwife downriver who needed the coin more than she needed her ninth summer."

"And you're giving it to me because—"

"Because I'm told that opening one and holding the memory for a minute is the closest a sworn soul can come to hearing the god again." His voice softened into something almost tender. "Borrowed grace, ledger-keeper. Just for tonight. Just for you. You've balanced our books flawlessly for eleven years. Consider it interest."

It was a kindness. It was, on its face, an unambiguous kindness, and that was precisely why every instinct Xanthe owned stood up and began to pace.

Corvel did not give gifts. Corvel gave instruments. In the eleven years she'd kept his ledgers she had catalogued the pattern the way she catalogued everything: the bonus that arrived the week before he wanted a falsified entry; the promotion offered the day after a rival conveniently died; the rare, devastating warmth he deployed like a lamp to draw moths. Nothing about Magister Corvel was free. Everything was *priced*, and the price was always revealed after purchase.

But oh, the jar glowed.

"You said fully consensual," Xanthe said. "You said taxes paid. Why bring it to me instead of selling it? Grade-three childhood goes for eight crowns to collectors. I know because I logged the sale of one in March."

"Because I value loyalty over crowns." He folded his hands. "And because I confess a curiosity. Seven years of perfect service into the silence. I want to know if the faith holds. If my ledger-keeper prays into the dark, or merely waits by the phone."

"That's not a gift. That's an audit."

"Everything is an audit, Xanthe. Life audits us constantly. The wise simply prepare their entries." He nudged the jar an inch toward her. "Open it or don't. Keep it or don't. I ask only one thing: tell me honestly what it does to you. An honest report. From the guild's most honest keeper."

He left. Of course he left. Corvel never stayed to watch his instruments work; watching was for lesser men. The door clicked shut behind him and Xanthe sat alone with a bottled summer and the memory of Dame Ferren's three-toothed smile.

*Praying into silence — that's love.*

She should refuse it. She knew she should. The jar was a test dressed as a gift, and passing a corrupt test was its own kind of failure, and there were perhaps four people in the Hollow Guild whose approval she actually cared about, and none of them was Corvel.

She picked up the jar anyway.

In her defense — and she would make this exact defense to herself, at length, in the years to come, with increasingly thin conviction — she told herself it was reconnaissance. A ledger-keeper should know the stock. How could she audit the trade in bottled memories if she didn't understand the goods? This wasn't weakness. This was due diligence. Due diligence that glowed the color of kitchen windows and fit in her palm like a bird's egg, warm, although glass had no business being warm.

She broke the wax seal.

The memory rose out of the jar like steam and poured into her — not through her eyes or ears but directly into the place where the ledger-thread used to live, and for one suspended second, Xanthe Thackeray was nine years old and it was late August and she was sitting on a fence post shelling peas with her grandmother, whose hands smelled of dirt and dill, and the peas made that soft *tick-tick-tick* sound in the tin bowl, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and her whole body knew — the way only a child's body knows — that this evening would never end, that evenings like this were the permanent condition of the world—

Then it faded, as borrowed things do, and she was thirty-seven in a cold cell with wax crumbs on her desk and wet eyes and the specific, bottomless ache of a thing remembered that had never been hers.

Her grandmother had died in the war. Her village had burned. Someone else's ninth summer now lived in her chest beside the ghost of her own, and she could not tell anymore which grief belonged to whom.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone she used for balancing columns that refused to balance. "That was foolish."

The silence where her god lived did not answer. It never answered. But tonight — and she would spend the next several weeks refusing to examine this — tonight the silence felt fractionally less empty. As if something vast and patient had leaned, very slightly, closer to the glass.

* * *

She reported to Corvel the next morning, as instructed, and gave him honesty, because lying to an auditor of souls seemed like poor craft. "It works. It hurts. It's crueler than a weapon and I suspect that's why you wanted data on it."

Something flickered behind Corvel's pleasant expression — not guilt; she'd stopped expecting guilt from him around year six — but interest. Sharp and sudden, like a fish rising.

"Crueler how?"

"It gives you something you lost and then takes away the fact that you ever lost anything. It un-grieves you and re-grieves you in the same minute." She kept her voice level, clerical. "If the guild means to sell these, Magister, you should know they don't sell joy. They sell the reminder that joy was possible, which is a different product entirely, and I'd price it higher."

Corvel regarded her for a long moment, and then he laughed — genuinely, warmly, the laugh of a man delighted by an unexpected dividend. "Oh, you're wasted on the books. Do you know that? Truly wasted." He turned toward the door, then paused, silhouetted against the corridor light. "One more question, ledger-keeper, purely professional. If the guild asked you to choose between your ledgers and your oath — between what you've recorded and what you swore — which would you keep?"

"The same thing," Xanthe said. "That's the point of an honest ledger. It *is* the oath, written down."

"Mm," said Corvel, and the sound was so soft, so satisfied, that she thought about it for the rest of the day, the way you think about a pebble in your shoe after you've shaken it out and can't find it.

It was only much later — months later, on the night the stars went out — standing on the guildhouse roof with the sky bleeding black above her and the wards screaming down like glass, that she finally understood what he'd been measuring.

He hadn't been testing her faith.

He'd been confirming that she believed the ledgers were sacred.

Because soon, very soon, the guild would need someone to write down — in a trusted hand, in an honest book — exactly what they intended to buy with a country.
