## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Xanthe Thackeray had always liked the guildhouse roof at night. It was the one place in Hollowreach where nobody asked her for anything—no ledgers to balance, no handlers to nod at, no debtors weeping politely into their sleeves while she explained, for the third time, that yes, the interest on a borrowed memory compounded, and no, she did not make the rules.

She climbed out through the trapdoor above the counting room with a blanket, a bottle of plum wine she had officially recorded as "spoiled," and the particular contentment of a woman who had finished her work and was pretending she hadn't noticed the parts of it that bothered her.

Below, the city of Hollowreach glowed the way it always did: lamplight in amber strings along the riverfront, the green-gold shimmer of ward-lamps on every corner, and overhead—the reason anyone came up here—the stars.

There were a great many things Xanthe could account for. She could tell you the going rate for a week of good weather (four marks, or one honest apology from someone who'd wronged you). She knew what a jar of childhood went for at auction, and why the ones with dogs in them fetched triple. She knew the exact weight of luck, because Brannoc's scales measured it: lighter than grief, heavier than hope, roughly the weight of a sparrow deciding to land.

But the stars she had never been able to price, and this pleased her in a way she didn't examine too closely. Every year the Guild tried. Some magister would commission a survey—*celestial inventory, all rights reserved*—and every year the survey came back with figures so absurd they were quietly shelved. The stars, it turned out, refused to be owned by anyone small enough to count them.

"Good," Xanthe said to the sky now, tipping the bottle. "Stay impossible."

She was halfway through the wine when the first star flickered.

She noticed it the way a bookkeeper notices a misfiled entry—not the thing itself, but the wrongness of it. A star in the northern arc, one she'd have sworn she'd seen every night of her life, blinked like a candle in a draft.

Then another.

Then, between one breath and the next, the whole northern quarter of the sky went out. Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is gone—as if something had simply finished with it.

Xanthe stood up very slowly, the blanket sliding off her shoulders, and watched the darkness spread southward like ink dropped in water. The stars did not fall. They were not covered. They were *withdrawn*, each one plucked from the ledger of heaven with a neat line struck through it, and somewhere in the back of her mind—a place she kept locked even from herself—a voice whispered: *someone is balancing an account.*

"No," she said aloud, and was surprised at how much her voice shook.

The last stars went, and then the moon, which did not go out but seemed to *fold*, folding smaller and smaller until it was a coin, a fleck, nothing, and the dark that remained was total in a way night had never managed. It wasn't the dark of a cloudy evening. It was the dark behind your eyelids. It was the dark before anything.

And then the wards broke.

She felt it through the roof tiles first—a shudder under her knees—and then she heard it: a sound like a cathedral window the size of the horizon giving way all at once. The green-gold shimmer that ringed the country, that every child learned to draw in school with crayons held sideways, shattered. Shards of light rained down over Hollowreach, slow as ash, beautiful and terrible, and where they touched the ground they left scorch-marks shaped like letters in no alphabet Xanthe knew.

The city began to scream. Not in unison—that would come later—but in scattered pockets, dogs first, then roosters, then people, the animal certainty that the world had just been robbed.

Xanthe stayed on the roof. Later she would not be able to explain why. Perhaps because running toward stairs felt like agreeing that the ground still existed. Perhaps because some deep-buried part of her, the part that balanced ledgers, understood instinctively that this was not a disaster.

It was a *transaction*. And transactions had buyers. Transactions had sellers.

The trapdoor banged open behind her.

"Ledger-keeper!" Magister Corvel's voice, smooth as oiled walnut even now. He stood half out of the hatch, his silver hair unruffled, his coat buttoned, looking for all the world like a man who had stepped out to check whether he'd left the stove lit. "You'll want to come inside."

"What happened?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"Astronomical irregularity." He smiled. It reached exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. "The Guild will manage it. We always do. Come inside, Xanthe. The doors are being sealed."

"Sealed against what?"

Corvel looked up—at the black, black sky—with an expression she would spend many sleepless nights trying to classify afterward. If she hadn't known better, she would have called it satisfaction.

"Against disappointment," he said, and held the door.

Xanthe took one last look at the empty heavens, picked up the plum wine, and went inside. The lock on the trapdoor clicked shut behind her like a full stop at the end of a sentence she hadn't finished reading.

* * *

Four miles downriver, Odji was arguing with a catfish.

This was normal. The catfish—who Odji insisted was named Grandmother and who had, by any reasonable accounting, seniority over everyone in Hollowreach—had opinions about bait, and Odji had long since stopped fishing to eat and started fishing to converse. His ferry was moored at the reed-bank below Lamplight Steps, lines slack, lantern swinging, the river murmuring its old gossip against the hull.

"Grandmother, I am telling you," Odji said, hauling in a tangle of weed, "if the man wanted to cross at midnight, he should not have offered me a button. A *button*. What am I, a tailor?"

The catfish declined to surface.

"You are wise," Odji agreed. "Wise and ugly. Like my mother."

He was reaching for the thermos of chicory coffee when the light changed.

Not the lamplight—the *river*. Odji knew the Sable like he knew his own pulse, and the Sable at night wore a particular color, a faint silver wash the old ferrymen called *star-sweat*, the reflection of the sky off the water. Every riverman navigated by it when fog ate the banks.

The star-sweat vanished.

Odji set down the thermos with exaggerated care, the way you do when you don't trust your hands. He looked up. The sky was gone. All of it. The whole ceiling of the world, lifted away like the lid off a pot, and underneath was nothing, and the nothing was looking back down at him with no eyes at all.

"Eh," said Odji, after a long moment. "That's new."

Then the border wards shattered—he heard it as much as felt it, a crack rolling down the river valley from the north like the world's largest pane of glass—and the shards came drifting over the water, falling slow as snow, and where they touched the Sable they hissed out little rings of steam, and the fish went silent. All of them. Even Grandmother. The river itself seemed to hold its breath.

Odji had lived sixty-one years on that water. He had ferried brides, smugglers, tax collectors, one runaway duke (in a barrel; excellent tipper), and twice a funeral party who paid him to sing badly so the mourners wouldn't have to. He had seen the flood of '09 take the east steps, and the winter the river froze so hard they drove carts across it, and the night the ghost-light walked the shallows at Marrow Bend and every sensible boatman tied up and drank until sunrise.

He had never once seen the river afraid.

But it was afraid now. He could feel it through the soles of his boots, a trembling in the current, the water pulling oddly—not downstream, but *sideways*, as though it were trying to look over its shoulder.

"All right," Odji told the darkness conversationally, because his father had taught him that fear shared aloud shrinks to a manageable size. "All right, all right. Somebody up there has done something extremely stupid, and I do not intend to find out what by drowning in it."

He hauled in his lines. Grandmother surfaced at last, gave him one baleful golden look, and slid under, which from her was either a blessing or a warning, and historically had been both.

He was lighting the second lantern—no reason, except that two lamps felt braver than one—when the screaming started from the direction of the city, thin and high across the water, and beneath it, stranger and worse, a sound he couldn't name at first. Then he could.

Bells. Every temple bell in Hollowreach ringing at once, unscheduled, unringed by human hands—or perhaps rung by every hand at once—in a pattern with no pattern, and Odji stood in his ferry with two lamps and a thermos and listened to a country cry out in its sleep.

He looked north, upriver, toward the old capital, where the river came from and where, his grandmother had always said, the first door ever made still stood in a mountain, if mountains could be believed.

"Well," he said to the dark. "I expect somebody will need a boat."

It was not bravery. It was arithmetic. In sixty-one years, Odji had learned one law above all others: when the impossible happens, somebody always needs a ferry, and if you're already holding the pole, you may as well be kind about it.

He pushed off into the starless current, and the Sable carried him toward the screams, muttering its old gossip in a voice he almost didn't recognize.

* * *

In the archives, three floors underground, Wren did not notice the sky go out at all.

This was, in fairness, standard procedure. Wren was seventeen, apprenticed to the Guild's Records Division on the strength of a handwriting so immaculate it had made a senior clerk weep, and Wren had spent the past six hours in the sub-cellar cataloguing a donation of bottled weather, during which time the sun could have exploded, the moon could have married the sun, and Wren would have noted only that the lamp was flickering again.

The donation was forty-one jars. Storms, mostly. One jar of fog with a note attached—*slightly haunted, disclose to buyer*—and one small round jar labeled, in faded copperplate, *a summer afternoon, 3 p.m., shade of an apple tree, unused.*

Wren held that one a long time. Inside, the light was gold and lazy, and if you turned it just right you could see the ghost of leaves moving, and hear—faintly, faintly—someone laughing, and a dog barking, and the specific creak of a swing being pushed by a hand that loved you.

Unused. Someone had owned this afternoon and never opened it, and now here it sat, in a cellar, waiting to be sold.

"That's sad," Wren informed it solemnly, and entered it in the catalogue in their best hand: *Lot 41. Contents: one (1) afternoon, quality excellent, provenance unknown. Recommend premium shelf.*

That was when the lamps died.

All of them, at once, and the bottled afternoons and storms and fogs lit the cellar in shifting colors—gold, grey, one furious purple—and in that strange glow Wren saw the dust hanging in the air begin to *fall upward*.

"Wren?" called Mother Hesk, the head archivist, from the stairwell, and her voice had a wobble in it Wren had never heard before. "Wren, are you—stay where you are. Don't touch the shelves."

"Why? What's happening?"

"The wards," Mother Hesk said, and then, sounding genuinely baffled, as if reporting a fact about the weather that contradicted everything she knew about weather: "The wards are *broken*. All of them. The whole border, like—like glass."

Wren climbed the stairs two at a time anyway, because Wren had never once in their life stayed where they were told, and burst out into the archive proper, where the tall windows looked onto the inner courtyard, and the courtyard looked up at the sky.

There was no sky.

There was a hole where the sky had been, smooth and absolute, and standing in the courtyard were a dozen clerks and archivists, all staring upward, all silent, and one of them—the old map-maker, Fenwick—was quietly, methodically tearing a map into pieces and letting the pieces drift around his feet.

"Fenwick?" Wren whispered.

"It won't hold," the old man said, not looking at them. His voice was terribly calm. "I've drawn this country four hundred times, child. Four hundred times, and tonight I can feel the edges going soft. Whatever's out there—" he gestured at the nothing overhead, "—it isn't coming *in*. It's erasing us *out*."

Above them, through the open courtyard, the last shards of the border wards came drifting down like luminous snow, and one landed on Wren's palm and dissolved, leaving a mark like a burn and a word in their head they had no translation for—a word that felt, unmistakably, like *receipt*.

Somewhere above, the guildhouse doors boomed shut—one, two, three, the great iron bolts of lockdown, a sound apprentices were drilled to recognize as safety.

It did not sound like safety. It sounded like a coffin lid.

"Everyone inside," Magister Corvel's voice rang through the speaking-tubes, calm as a lullaby. "Seal the vaults. The Guild has matters well in hand."

Wren stood in the courtyard a moment longer, the ghost-word burning on their palm, and did the only sensible thing an archivist could do in the face of the end of the world.

They ran back downstairs, snatched a blank ledger off the shelf, and wrote at the top of the first page, in their most immaculate hand:

*Night of the Black Sky. Witnessed by me. Nobody else is writing it down, so somebody has to.*

Then they added, after a moment's thought, in smaller letters:

*Note: Lot 41 (afternoon, unused) moved to my personal satchel. Not stolen. Borrowed. Will return it when the world makes sense again.*

* * *

By midnight, Hollowreach had arranged itself into the shapes panic always takes. Fires burned in the Docks Quarter where looters mistook fear for opportunity. Temples overflowed, then emptied, then filled again with people who prayed louder the second time. The Wardens' horns sounded the assembly and got no answer worth having.

And in the guildhouse on Vantage Hill, the masters met behind doors that had never, in living memory, been locked during a crisis, and the staff were sent to their quarters with instructions that amounted to *see nothing, record nothing, ask nothing*—which was, of course, the entire job description, and so should have been comforting, and was not.

Xanthe stood at her window in the counting room, watching the starless dark, and found herself doing the worst thing a bookkeeper can do.

She was auditing her own memory.

Seven years of silence from Vessia. Seven years of prayers that fell like coins down a bottomless well. And tonight, watching the sky get *withdrawn*—not destroyed, withdrawn, the way assets were withdrawn before a company folded—she remembered, with terrible clarity, Corvel's face in the lamplight of the oath-hall seven years ago, the night the god went quiet. He hadn't grieved. She had told herself he was too disciplined to grieve.

She had lied to herself beautifully. It was, she realized now, the finest entry she'd ever falsified, and she'd balanced it perfectly for seven years.

A knock at the door. Wren's face appeared, pale as paper, clutching a satchel.

"Ledger-keeper—I mean—Xanthe. The masters are meeting. Right now. And I was shelving near the council stair, and I heard—" Wren swallowed. "I heard Magister Corvel say, 'The first payment cleared.' "

Xanthe looked at the child, at the satchel with its suspicious bulge, at the black window behind them both.

"Payment," she repeated, "for what?"

"I don't know," Wren whispered. "But Xanthe—it sounded like he was *happy*."

Outside, over a country with no stars and no border and no sky worth the name, the first false dawn came up grey as ash, and somewhere in the counting house below, the ledgers waited—every page of them, including the pages Xanthe had never written.
