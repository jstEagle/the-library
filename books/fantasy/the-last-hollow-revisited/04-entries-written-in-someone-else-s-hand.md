## 4. Entries Written in Someone Else's Hand

The guildhouse had locked its doors at moonrise, and by the second hour past midnight it had begun to make noises.

Not the usual noises. A building as old as the Hollow Guild's counting house earned a repertoire of creaks the way an old soldier earns scars—settling timbers, a shutter that had never once sat flush in Xanthe's twelve years of tenancy. Those she could sleep through. What she heard now was different: a low groaning from the cellar stairs, like something very heavy being dragged across stone, and beneath it a whispering that came and went in gusts, as if the house itself were trying to remember a song.

Xanthe lit her third candle of the evening and decided, with the particular stubbornness of an accountant facing irregular figures, to work through it.

If the world was ending—and the sky outside her window was a flat, starless black, smooth as poured ink, which rather suggested it might be—then someone ought to know where the money stood. It was the one comfort she could offer herself. Ledgers did not care about omens. Ink did not tremble at a darkened sky. There was a column for every horror, and once you found the right column, even catastrophe became arithmetic.

She carried the master ledgers up from the vault three at a time, stacking them on her desk until they formed a small paper rampart between her and the window. The candlelight caught their spines: *Receipts, Winter Quarter*. *Disbursements, Unusual.* *Anomalous Holdings, Volumes I–IX.* She rolled her sleeves, uncapped her pen, and began the audit she'd been meaning to do properly for years and had never done at all because doing it properly meant asking questions of people who preferred not to be asked.

"Right," she said to the empty room. "Let's see what we've been buying."

* * *

For the first hour, everything was almost reassuringly ordinary—which is to say, strange in the familiar ways. The Hollow Guild traded in impossible things, but it traded in them *cleanly*, and Xanthe had spent half her life making sure of it.

She ran her finger down entries she knew by heart. One jar of childhood, aged six summers, sold twice—the second buyer given a carefully worded note that what he was purchasing was a *copy*, and copies of childhoods always came out slightly sweeter than the original, like fruit painted from memory. A week of good weather, sold to two wedding parties in the same month; both couples had been delighted, and neither had ever learned why their guests kept remarking on the luck of it. Three doors to nowhere, leased, not sold—the distinction mattered enormously, and Xanthe had drafted the lease language herself after the unfortunate business with the door that had developed opinions.

Her own hand was everywhere. Small, precise letters, numbers that lined up to the copper. She felt the old quiet pride stir in her chest, the feeling that had gotten her through hungry years: *I am good at this. This is mine.*

That was when she turned to page 114 of *Disbursements, Unusual* and found an entry dated eleven days before she was born.

She stared at it for a long time.

The handwriting was hers. Not similar—hers. The little hook she made on the descender of a *y*, the way she crossed her sevens twice because a magister had told her once that single-crossed sevens invited forgery. The entry read:

*Paid: one border ward, complete. To: [entry struck through]. By order of the Inner Circle. Received in full and entered without amendment.*
— *X. Thackeray, Keeper of Accounts*

Xanthe set down her pen very carefully, the way one sets down a snake one hopes is merely asleep.

"That's not possible," she informed the ledger conversationally. "I wasn't born. I want that noted."

The ledger declined to note anything.

She checked the date again, thinking perhaps she'd misread the year. She had not misread the year. Eleven days before her birth, someone had written a receipt in her exact hand for the purchase of the entire border ward system of the country—the same wards that had shattered four nights ago like a dropped plate, the same wards whose falling had taken the stars with them.

She turned pages with hands that had gone strangely cold. More entries followed, marching backward through decades. *Ward-fall, preliminary survey. Paid.* *Cosmological severance, feasibility study. Received, findings favorable.* *Silencing of intermediary divine presence, retainer. Paid quarterly.* That last one stopped her breath for a moment—an *intermediary divine presence*—but there was no name attached, only a reference number she didn't recognize, filed under a sigil belonging to no guild department she'd ever audited.

Every page signed *X. Thackeray*. Every page in her hand. Entries spanning sixty years, written by a woman who was, at various points in the record, either not yet born or four years old or off fighting in somebody's border war with a sword too big for her and an oath too big for both.

"Well," she said finally, to the candle. "Either I'm much older than I think, or I'm much better at delegation than I remember."

The joke landed nowhere, as jokes do at such moments, but saying it aloud steadied her enough to keep going.

* * *

By the fourth hour she had filled nine pages of her own notebook with transcriptions, cross-references, and increasingly frantic marginalia. The pattern was undeniable and worse than undeniable—it was *organized*. The ward-fall hadn't been a disaster. It was a delivery. Somewhere back through the decades, the Inner Circle had placed an order, arranged payment in installments, and waited, patient as farmers, for the crop to come in. And every installment, every survey, every cold administrative step toward tearing the sky down had been recorded, initialed, and certified accurate—in her name.

There was a knock at her door. Three soft raps, then two more, then one—Wren's knock, which Wren had invented himself and defended fiercely against all suggestions that it was unnecessarily complicated.

"Come in."

Wren slipped inside carrying a lamp and an expression like a cat that has seen something under the furniture it wishes it hadn't. He was sixteen, archive-trained, and possessed of the unshakable belief that problems could be solved by filing them correctly. He looked at the ledgers strewn across her desk, at her face, and his shoulders dropped.

"You found them," he said. "The wrong pages."

"You knew?"

"I file things." He set his lamp down and perched on the edge of her chair-back, too agitated to sit properly. "I've been re-sorting the archives since the sky went out, because—if the world ends, someone should at least know where everything *is*, that seemed important—and there are whole drawers that don't match. Documents in your hand. Dozens. Hundreds. I thought maybe... I don't know. Copyists? Apprentice versions? You must have trained people, maybe some of them copied your style so exactly that—"

"Wren."

"—because honestly some of these are *better* than yours, the early ones are a bit wobbly actually, you can see where you hadn't settled into the double-crossed sevens yet—"

"*Wren.*"

He stopped. In the lamplight his freckles stood out pale against skin gone grey. "I checked," he said quietly. "Against samples. Sealed samples, from the vault, with wax over the dates. It isn't imitation. It's the same hand. Every stroke."

Xanthe looked down at the open ledger, at her own handwriting describing, in meticulous detail, the purchase of the darkness overhead. Sixty years of it. A lifetime of entries she had never made, in a life she had barely begun.

"When I took my oath," she said slowly, "Magister Corvel told me the ledgers were sacred. He said a keeper's accounts would outlive her, that her hand would go on speaking after she was dust. I thought it was a lovely thing to say. I wrote it in my journal."

"It's less lovely now," Wren offered.

"It's considerably less lovely now."

They sat together in the candle-hiss for a moment. Down in the cellar, the dragging noise came again, longer this time, and the whispering rose with it. Wren flinched. Xanthe found she didn't. Some part of her had already classified the sounds under *not tonight's problem*, and that part of her was running the show, because the alternative to bookkeeping was screaming, and she had a professional reputation to maintain.

"There's one more thing," Wren said. He hesitated, then slid a slim folio from inside his coat. "It was wedged behind the Anomalous Holdings. Behind volume nine, which nobody has opened since before I was hired, which means whoever put it there wanted it *found eventually*, just not easily." He held it out. "It's addressed to you."

The cover bore her name. Her hand again—but shakier, hurried, the writing of someone working fast and frightened.

"Don't read it here," Wren blurted. "Sorry—I mean, obviously it's yours, but the archivist's rule: never read a sealed letter in the room where it was hidden. Bad practice. Things hear."

"Things hear," Xanthe repeated.

"It's a real rule! Volume three of—" He caught her look and subsided. "I'll go. I'll be in the east stacks if you need me. Or the west stacks. I'm going to be in whichever stacks feel least haunted."

He gathered his lamp and let himself out. Xanthe listened to his footsteps fade, then picked up the folio. It was light. It smelled faintly of river water, though the vault was nowhere near the river.

She did not open it. Instead she laid it beside the ledgers, squared its edges until they aligned perfectly with the stack—a habit, a tic, the last clean thing in the room—and looked out the window at the starless dark.

Sixty years of entries. Which meant the plan predated her entirely. Which meant her name, her hand, her *identity* had been in use before she existed, the way a coat hangs in a shop waiting for someone to grow into it. She had always believed her carefulness was hers—her virtue, her craft, the thing that separated her from the smugglers and frauds the guild employed elsewhere. Now every careful stroke she'd ever made felt like tracing lines someone else had drawn first.

And underneath that, colder: if her hand had been signing these documents for sixty years, then someone had *taught* it to. Someone had shaped her, trained her, placed her at this desk with the patience of the whole operation behind it. Corvel. Corvel, who tested her loyalty twice a year with small cruelties dressed as examinations. Corvel, who had said *your hand will speak after you're dust* with that thin smile of his, and she had thought it poetry.

It had been a job description.

* * *

Near dawn, unable to sit still, she carried the folio down to the vault, because if things heard, she preferred they hear her below ground with a locked door between them and the rest of the house.

The vault was cold and smelled of tallow and old paper. She lit the standing lamp, closed the door, and broke the folio's seal.

Inside was a single sheet, written in her hand but pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places, as though the writer had been gripping the pen like a weapon.

*Keeper—*

*If you are reading this, then the night has come, and I am sorry for how you are learning it. I have written in your hand for longer than you have been alive. Understand: I did not steal it. I borrowed it, the way the guild borrows everything, and borrowing is the guild's oldest lie.*

*You will find the contracts in the drowned archive beneath the old capital. Everything true is filed there. Everything else—including this—is only a copy of a copy.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop taking your time, which I know you count carefully: ask yourself what happened to Vessia seven years ago. Ask why a god who promised to keep you would go quiet. We were told she left. We were told she abandoned us. I have begun to suspect we were told a great many things, and that the ledgers—the real ones—were kept somewhere we were never meant to balance them.*

*You are the best of us. That is not sentiment; it is an assessment, and I was a keeper too. They chose you because you cannot abide an unbalanced line. Good. Neither could I. It is the only thing that saved me, and I did not manage to save enough.*

*Follow the numbers. They go down, and then they go under, and then they go home.*

*—K.*

Xanthe read it three times. Then she sat down on the cold flagstones with her back against the shelf of *Anomalous Holdings*, held the letter against her chest, and laughed—once, sharply, the way a rope snaps.

A previous keeper. A keeper who had seen this coming and hidden a letter behind the one drawer nobody opened. Who had apologized. Who had called her *the best of us* and meant it as an indictment of everyone else.

The floor had not literally dropped out of her identity, she reflected. Something worse: the floor had turned out to be a trapdoor, installed decades ago, maintained in excellent condition, and signed off in her own hand.

Above her, through the vault ceiling, came the sound of the guildhouse waking—boots in corridors, voices raised, someone hammering on doors. And cutting through it all, clear and unhurried, the bell that summoned all keepers to the counting hall. Corvel's bell. He would be standing at the head of the long table with his thin smile, ready to explain the darkened sky, and she would have to walk in there and stand among colleagues whose names appeared in ledgers she now understood she'd been keeping all along.

Xanthe folded the letter into her coat, straightened her sleeves, and blew out the lamp.

Then, because she was who she was—even now, even this—she paused at the door and corrected one thing first: she took the false pages, all sixty years of them, out of the master ledgers and hid them in the empty *Anomalous Holdings, Volume IX*, where no one had looked in living memory.

Let him explain an unbalanced line. She wanted to see his face.
