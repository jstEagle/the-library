## 5. Debtors at the Door

Wren had worked in the Hollow Guild's archives for three years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one universal truth: nobody reads the fine print until the sky falls on it.

The sky had fallen the night before. Literally. She had watched it happen from the archive window, her ink-stained fingers pressed against the glass, as every star in heaven went out like candles snuffed by a passing hand. And now, at dawn, the city was discovering what that meant, and it had come to the guildhouse to complain.

The first knock came before the lamps were even lit. Then another. Then a fist. Then many fists, in a rhythm that could only be described as percussive civic distress.

"They're queueing," said Odji, who had arrived through the kitchen door the way he arrived everywhere—unannounced, damp, and smelling faintly of river. He set down a crate of smoked eels on the counting table as if this were an ordinary morning. "Round the block. Twice. There's a woman selling chestnuts to the queue."

"Someone's profiteering off our crisis," Wren said.

"Somebody always does." Odji shrugged his great shoulders. "At least they're good chestnuts."

Wren climbed onto a stool so she could see out of the high window over the entrance hall. She was small enough that most furniture treated her as a suggestion rather than an occupant. Below, in the grey light of a starless morning, the street seethed. Merchants whose luck-contracts had been sold twice over. Widows holding promissory notes for memories of husbands who might no longer have existed in quite the same way. A man clutching a bottle labeled *one perfect summer afternoon* and weeping quietly into his collar. And behind them all, pressing at the doors like weather, everyone else—the people who owed the guild and the people the guild owed, which after last night amounted to nearly everybody in Vellsgate.

"What do we do?" Wren asked.

Thackeray came down the stairs.

Wren had known Xanthe Thackeray for three years and had never once seen her look uncertain. She was the ledger-keeper, the one whose handwriting appeared on every page like scripture, the one Magister Corvel trusted with the guild's whole impossible economy. This morning, descending into the noise with her coat half-buttoned and her hair escaping its braid, she looked like a woman reading a bill she couldn't pay.

She stopped at the foot of the stairs. Listened to the knocking. Looked at Wren, then Odji, then the ledger-room door, where the ledgers sat—the ledgers Wren had spent a sleepless night not mentioning, because Thackeray had found pages in them written in her own hand that she did not remember writing, and some things you don't say out loud until you've decided what they mean.

"Open the doors," Thackeray said.

Odji raised an eyebrow the size of a mussel shell. "All of them? It's a riot out there with manners."

"All of them."

* * *

It went worse than expected for approximately four minutes, and better than anyone could have hoped thereafter.

The crowd poured into the entrance hall like the river coming home. They shouted. They thrust contracts. A cheese merchant stood on a chair and declared that since the stars were gone, his luck-debt was void by act of heaven, and when no one contradicted him he began to cry with relief. An old soldier tried to return a bottled memory because, he said, "it isn't mine anymore, I can't feel the edges of it," and no one knew what to do with that either.

Thackeray stood on the central stair and let them shout themselves hoarse. Then, in the quiet, she did something that made Wren's stomach drop through three floors of guildhouse.

She took the nearest contract—a pawn-ticket for a week of good weather, held by a fisherman with salt-cracked hands—and she tore it in half. Then quarters. Then she dropped the pieces and stepped on them, gently, like crushing a spider she felt bad about.

"The debt is cancelled," she said. Her voice carried the way quiet voices do in loud rooms. "Next."

For a heartbeat, nobody moved. The fisherman stared at the paper snow around her boots. Then somebody in the back yelled "NEXT!" with the joy of a man at a hanging turned festival, and the hall became something between a bank run and a wedding.

Wren fetched the ledgers. Thackeray tore. Page after page, contract after contract, names read aloud and struck through with two strokes of a pen before the paper followed. Bottled childhoods returned to their owners unopened. Borrowed courage forgiven. A door to nowhere, Wren noted with professional interest, was simply given away to a tinker who said he'd always wanted one.

"This is treason," Wren whispered, hauling the next ledger up the steps. Her heart was doing something complicated—half terror, half a feeling she didn't have a name for yet, warm and dangerous as brandy.

"It's bookkeeping," said Thackeray, tearing again. "I'm very good at it."

"You'll hang."

"I'll hang anyway, if what's in those ledgers is true." She paused, quill hovering. "Might as well be hanged for charity."

Somewhere in the third hour, Wren realized she was smiling, and that she couldn't stop, and that this was possibly the worst possible moment—but there it was. Three years of filing other people's sorrows in alphabetical order, and here was Thackeray setting fire to the alphabet.

* * *

Brannoc arrived at noon, and he arrived the way disasters do: loudly, and slightly on fire.

Not actually on fire. But his left glove was scorched, his beard contained at least three kinds of debris, and he came through the still-open front doors shouldering past petitioners like a barge through reeds. He had been the guild's finest ward-smith once, before whatever disgrace Wren had never been told the shape of. Now he looked like a man who had been arguing with masonry all night and losing on points.

"It wasn't a failure," he said, without preamble, without hello, planting both fists on the counting table where Wren was attempting to restore order to the wreckage of the files. "The border wards. Everyone keeps saying they failed. They didn't fail. You don't get a failure pattern like that. I've built wards since before you could write, girl, and that—" he jabbed a charred finger toward the black window, toward the missing stars— "that was a *sale*."

The hall had mostly emptied by then, the torn contracts drifting in the corners like the world's saddest confetti. Thackeray came down the last steps slowly.

"You're sure."

"I'm the one who maintained the anchor-points for eleven years. Someone paid me to keep them singing, and someone higher up paid to cut their throats." Brannoc's voice cracked on the last word, and Wren saw that under the fury he was grieving—he had loved those wards the way Odji loved the river, the way Wren loved the smell of old vellum. "And the coin that bought it didn't come from outside. Ward-work leaves fingerprints in the metal. Guild mint-marks. Ours."

Silence. Even Odji stopped eating his eel.

"So it wasn't the sky falling on us," Wren said, very quietly. "We fell on the sky."

"That's the long and short of it, yes." Brannoc sagged onto a stool that groaned but held. "And I'd bet my remaining reputation—and mind you, that's a small pot—that Corvel signed the order."

Thackeray closed her eyes for exactly one breath. When she opened them, something had finished settling in her face, the way silt settles in a jar. Whatever she'd been hoping the strange pages in the ledgers meant, this was the answer, and she was done hoping otherwise.

"Then we can't stay here," she said. "They'll come for the ledgers. They'll come for me. And when they're done tidying up, there won't be a guild left to owe anything to—just whatever they're building in the dark."

"Building?" Odji asked. "Building what?"

Nobody answered, because none of them knew yet. But the word hung there, and Wren would think about it later, in the weeks of looping roads and villages with too many names: *building.* As though the dark were a foundation. As though something were being raised on the ruins of the stars.

"We need to leave tonight," said Thackeray. "And we need to be nobody when we do." She looked around at the three of them—at the ferryman, the apprentice, the disgraced smith—and something almost like a smile touched her mouth, tired and crooked. "Congratulations. You're nobodies now. All of you."

"I've been a nobody for years," Odji said comfortably. "It's restful."

* * *

They packed in the hour before dusk, which is to say Wren packed and the others carried.

She took almost nothing from the archives. Just one slim volume—the earliest ledger, the one whose first page predated the guild itself, copied in a hand older than any living member—and, after a moment's guilty hesitation, her own notebook of marginalia, because a person's notes were the closest thing she had to a diary and she refused to be unwritten without them.

Outside, Vellsgate was fraying at the seams in ways the eye kept catching sideways. Lamps burned colors they shouldn't. A street she had walked ten thousand times seemed, just for a blink, to be paved in cobblestones she didn't recognize—and then it wasn't. Children played a skipping game whose rhyme had changed since morning; Wren knew, because she'd heard the old version yesterday, and the new version had one fewer verse, and nobody but her noticed.

"Does it hurt them?" she asked Brannoc, watching a baker's boy argue cheerfully with his mother about whether their shop had always been on this corner.

"Not yet. Not fast." Brannoc heaved a crate of tools onto the cart Odji had borrowed from a cousin who owed him—or perhaps whom he owed; Wren had given up tracking. "Unwriting's patient work. Like rot. You don't feel the beam go soft till the house leans."

"And the capital?"

"The capital's where the axe swings from." He spat, with feeling. "So naturally that's where you lot want to walk."

"Naturally," said Odji, hitching up the cart.

Thackeray came down last. She had changed into plain clothes—a ferryman's oilskin coat that made her look, for the first time since Wren had known her, like an ordinary woman instead of an instrument of an extraordinary institution. She carried the ledgers wrapped in sacking, strapped across her back like a soldier's pack. At the door she stopped, took one key from her ring, and locked the guildhouse behind them. Then she looked at the key a moment, and put it under the mat.

"In case anyone needs shelter," she said, catching Wren's expression.

"It's the guildhouse. Everyone who needs shelter hates us."

"Everyone who needed shelter came yesterday," Thackeray said. "And left lighter. Let the lock be for whoever comes tomorrow."

* * *

They went down to the river, because Odji insisted all sensible journeys begin with water, and because roads were already becoming unreliable in the outer districts. The *Kindly Eel*—a flat-bottomed ferry barge named, Odji claimed, after himself in his younger days, a claim Wren chose not to examine—waited at his private mooring, laden with eels, lanterns, and one extremely judgmental heron that had simply decided to come along.

They cast off as full dark fell. No stars above. Only the lamps of Vellsgate shrinking astern, and the black water sliding by, and the four of them in a circle around a small stove, passing a kettle like it was communion.

Wren found herself cataloguing them, the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she loved things.

Odji, at the tiller, humming something old in a language the river probably spoke. Brannoc, asleep sitting up within minutes, his scorched gloves still on, muttering ward-rhythms into his beard. Thackeray, awake, always awake, turning her quill over and over in her fingers as though it might confess.

"Wren," Thackeray said suddenly.

"Yes?"

"Why did you stay? Yesterday, when the sky went out. You could have run home. Everyone else did."

Wren considered lying, decided it would be wasted effort, and told the truth. "Because you were on the roof. And I thought—if the ledger-keeper stays, then it can't be the end of the world yet. The end of the world wouldn't wait for someone to finish the accounts."

A silence. On the water, the heron shifted its feet with a sound like disapproval.

"That's terrible logic," Thackeray said.

"I know."

"But thank you." She looked down at the quill, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a small, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "Three years I thought you filed things because you were frightened of everything else. Turns out you file things because you're brave about them."

"I'm going to have that engraved," Wren said. "*Brave About Them. A Memorial.*"

"Put it on my headstone. 'She Balanced.' "

They were both laughing now, quietly, absurdly, on a black river under a starless sky, heading for a capital that was eating the country edge-first. It shouldn't have been funny. It was the funniest thing that had ever happened to Wren. She understood, dimly, that this was what fear did when it had nowhere else to go—it turned into this, into warmth, into the fierce ridiculous tenderness of people in the same boat, literally, in her case.

Ahead, the river bent toward the old capital road, and the dark there was deeper than dark ought to be. Brannoc stirred in his sleep and said, clearly, as if reporting to a foreman: *"Anchor-point seven is cold. Somebody's paying."*

Wren looked at Thackeray. Thackeray was no longer laughing. In the lamplight her face had gone very still, the stillness of a woman reading the last line of a page she'd written herself and didn't remember writing.

"Magister Corvel," she said softly, to no one, to the river, to whoever had bought the sky, "what did you purchase?"

The heron, who knew more than any of them, said nothing, and the *Kindly Eel* carried them on into the country that was forgetting itself.
