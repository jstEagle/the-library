## 6. What Corvel Bought

Brannoc had not slept in three days, and the wards he was carving were the worst work of his life.

He sat in the back room of Odji's ferry-house with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and a burin in his hand, and the little warding sigil came out crooked, its lines wobbling like a drunkard's fence. He scraped it away and started again. The third attempt was no better. On the fourth he put down the tool entirely and pressed the heels of both hands against his eyes until he saw constellations that had no business existing anymore.

"You're going to ruin your eyes," said Wren from the doorway.

"They're already ruined. I ruined them for a living." He lowered his hands and looked at her. She was holding two bowls of something steaming and grey, and she set one on the table beside him without asking whether he wanted it. Nobody asked him anything anymore; they just assumed, and fed him, and left lamps burning near him at night because they'd noticed he flinched at the dark. It was unbearable. He would have died for any of them by the second week.

"Is it going well?" she asked, nodding at the half-carved door-latch. "The ward?"

"No."

"Oh." She considered this with the serious, unflinching honesty of someone who had spent her apprenticeship among archives. "Should I tell the others it's going well anyway? For morale?"

"Tell them whatever you like. It won't hold longer than a month. Maybe less." He picked up the burin again and turned the latch over in the lamplight. "A ward-smith can work with bad materials. Bad tools, bad weather, a bad hand — all of that, we manage. What I can't work with is—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "What I can't work with is not knowing why they fell."

"The stars went out," Wren said. "That's why they—"

"That's what people *think*. People think the wards failed under the strain of losing the sky, like a roof giving way in a storm." He shook his head. "Wards don't fail like that. Wards are built to fail like that. There's a difference, girl, and I've spent thirty years learning it, and I'm telling you the border lattice didn't break. It was *released*."

She sat down across from him, bowl forgotten. "Released by whom?"

"That," said Brannoc, "is the question I've been carving myself sick trying to answer. And tonight I think I finally did."

* * *

He had gone back, two nights ago, to the place where he had sworn he never would: the ward-house at Grayharbor Gate, where he'd worked before the guild had taken everything from him — his license, his name-stamp, his right to touch a boundary-stone in this country ever again. They'd cast him out seven years ago for asking questions about an order of sigil-plate he'd been told to install and not examine. He had spent those seven years drinking with a bitterness so refined it could have been sold through the guild as a vintage.

But the city was fraying now, and fraying cities have loose doors. The gate-wardens had abandoned their posts when the sky went black; there was no one to stop a disgraced old man letting himself into the maintenance vaults with a stolen key and a lamp.

The lattice-heart of Grayharbor Gate was a chamber of bronze and glass, a great slow engine of light that should have been guttering, damaged, dying — the heart of a thing that had fallen. Instead Brannoc had stood in front of it with his lamp raised and felt his stomach turn over, because the lattice wasn't broken at all.

It was *unbound*. Every anchor-sigil along the border circuit had been released in sequence, cleanly, deliberately, by a hand that knew exactly which order to use. Not sabotage — release. The difference between smashing a lock and turning a key. Someone had walked the whole length of the country's edge, or sent someone, and unlocked the sky the way you'd unlock a stable before leading the horse out.

And there was more. In the release-channels, where the discharge of a falling ward should have scorched everything raw, Brannoc had found the payment-marks. Little stamped impressions in the cooling bronze, the kind guild assayers press into bullion to certify its weight and purity. He'd known the stamp on sight. Every smith in the country knew it, the way every child knows the shape of the moon: a hollow circle with a keyhole at its center.

The Hollow Guild had paid for the wards' release. Had *purchased* it, certified the coin, signed the receipt in cold bronze.

"I checked it eleven times," he told Wren now, in the ferry-house back room, his voice low as though the walls might be taking dictation. "I checked it until my lamp burned out and I had to feel the marks with my fingers in the dark. Guild assay-stamps, fresh-struck, in the discharge channels of every anchor-point I could reach. The wards weren't murdered. They were bought out of their contract."

Wren had gone very pale. "Xanthe keeps the ledgers. If coin moved, she'd have recorded it. She records *everything* — she recorded that Odji owes a debt for half a jar of pickled eels, she'd have recorded the sky."

"Aye. Which means either the ledger lies, or the ledger has pages our Xanthe's never seen." He turned the crooked latch over once more and set it down. "Either way, girl, your mistress is balancing books that somebody else is writing."

* * *

They gathered after midnight, the four of them, around the ferry-house stove because it was the only room in the district that still kept a decent fire. Odji had dragged his boat up onto the bank days ago — no point ferrying on a river whose far banks sometimes weren't there — and now he crouched beside the stove turning a river-charm over in his broad dark fingers, the way he did when he was listening harder than his face admitted. Xanthe sat with the ledgers stacked at her elbow like a wall she'd built against the world. Wren perched on a crate with her knees drawn up. And Brannoc stood, because he couldn't sit and say what he had to say.

"You all want to know what happened to the sky," he began. "So I'll tell you what I know, and then you'll wish I didn't."

"Cheerful fellow," murmured Odji.

"The border wards did not fail. They were released — cleanly, in sequence, by master-hand. And the release was paid for. Guild coin, guild assay-stamp, struck into the mechanism itself." He watched Xanthe's face as he said it. She didn't flinch, exactly. Something behind her eyes went very still and very far away, the look of a bookkeeper finding a column that won't balance. "Someone inside your guild bought the fall of the wards, Xanthe Thackeray. Paid out of your own coffers, under your own seal, and you never saw a line of it."

"That's not possible," Xanthe said. But her voice had the flatness of someone reciting a rule she no longer believed in. "Every transaction passes through me. Every one. That's the whole point of me."

"Then ask yourself who taught you the point of you."

The silence stretched. Odji stopped turning the charm.

"It gets worse," Brannoc said, and almost smiled, because it always got worse; that was the one law of his life that had never once failed. "I went looking through the old order-files while I was in the vaults. Thirty years of installation orders, routed through the guild to my old house. Most of it's routine — boundary renewal, seasonal rebinding. But there's a thread running through the last twenty years, small orders, easy to miss if you're not the poor fool installing them. Anchor-points replaced with new sigil-plate. Reinforcement channels rerouted. Release-mechanisms installed at points that had never needed them." He held up his scarred hands. "I know these orders. I installed some of them. I asked questions about one batch, seven years ago, and I was stripped of my stamp for it. Now I understand what I was really building. We weren't maintaining the borders. We were converting them. Turning a wall into a *door*, fitted with a lock that only opens from one side."

"Opens outward," Wren whispered.

"Aye. Opens outward, locks inward. Somebody spent two decades quietly rebuilding the skin of this country into a lid." Brannoc let that sit in the stove-warmth. "You don't build a lid over your own head unless you intend to close it."

"And the stars?" Odji asked. His voice was gentle, but his eyes weren't. "The sky didn't just go dark, smith. It went *gone*. My grandmother's river-marks don't answer anymore. The water doesn't remember its own name downstream of the weir. You can't buy that with coin."

"You can't buy it with ordinary coin," Brannoc agreed. "But the Hollow Guild doesn't trade in ordinary things, does it?" He looked at Xanthe. "Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Doors to nowhere. I've heard what you keep in that guildhouse. So ask yourself what the price would be, to purchase the sky entire. Ask yourself what kind of currency buys a cosmos — and who in this country holds enough of it."

Xanthe had gone white to the lips. "The ledgers," she said. "The impossible transactions. The ones with no counterparty listed. I always thought they were eccentricities. Old debts. Vessia's own accounts." Her hand drifted toward the stack at her elbow and stopped short of touching it, as though the leather had grown hot. "There are entries going back decades. Purchases with nothing purchased. Payments into accounts with no holder. I balanced them. Gods forgive me, I *balanced* them, I made them tidy—"

"You were the broom," Brannoc said, not unkindly. "Sweeping the trail clean without ever knowing what walked there."

* * *

It was past the smallest hour when he told them the rest, and by then none of them had moved much, and the stove had burned down to a red eye that watched them all.

"Here's the shape of it, as best I can read the marks," Brannoc said. "Corvel. Corvel and the inner circle — the magisters, the sealed council, whoever else sits above your pay grade. Twenty years of quiet conversion-work on the border lattice. A paid release of the wards, timed to one night. And the sky itself — the stars, the old lights, whatever watches us from up there — drawn off, taken, *bought*, using the only treasury rich enough to cover it." He spread his hands. "Why? Because a man doesn't spend a nation's sky on a whim. You take away heaven for one reason only. So that there's room to put yourself there instead."

"Gods," Wren said faintly.

"Crowning themselves in the dark," Brannoc agreed. "That's my reading of it. Seal the country off from everything beyond — every star, every god, every power older than the guild. Cut the people off from anything higher to pray to. And then step into the vacancy. New divinity, minted in-house. Worship by monopoly." He laughed, short and ugly. "Thirty years a ward-smith and I never once thought about what the walls were *for*. Only how to make them stand. Turns out I was bricklaying for a coronation."

Odji spoke slowly, watching the coals. "My grandmother used to say the river remembers every name it's ever carried. That's why we ferry-folk mark our boats with old words — so the water knows us." He looked up. "If they seal the country, do the rivers forget too? Do the people?"

"That's the part I don't know," Brannoc admitted. "But I know this: a land cut off from its sky isn't a country anymore. It's a strongbox. And everything inside it — every memory, every road, every child born under a black heaven — belongs to whoever holds the key." He looked at Xanthe. "Your masters aren't fleeing the catastrophe, woman. They *are* the catastrophe. And the worst of it is the patience. Twenty years of small orders and quiet payments, and nobody — not the crown, not the temples, not the finest ward-smith in the realm — nobody thought to check the seams."

"Except you," Wren said. "They caught you checking the seams."

"Aye. And they let me live. Which frightened me for seven years without my knowing why." He rubbed his jaw. "Now I know. A disgraced smith is a warning to other smiths, but he's also harmless — or he seems so. Or perhaps they simply never imagined anyone would piece it together. Arrogance does that to clever men. It makes them sloppy about the small witnesses."

Xanthe finally touched the ledgers. She drew the top volume toward her and laid her palm flat on its cover, and for a long moment she looked less like a bookkeeper and more like a woman standing at a grave.

"If it's true," she said quietly, "then every entry I ever recorded was a lie wearing true arithmetic. Every debt I collected, every impossible thing I catalogued — it was all feeding this. And Corvel —" Her voice caught, steadied. "Corvel taught me my numbers. He found me half-starved and gave me a pen instead of a sword, and I thought that was mercy."

"Men like that are generous the way farmers are generous to oxen," Brannoc said. "Forgive me. But you've seen it now, same as I have. The question isn't what he bought. The question is what he still needs."

"He needs the ledgers," Xanthe said.

All of them looked at her.

"The original contracts. The true accounts — the ones I've never seen, the ones with the real entries in them. Whatever was done to the sky, it was done by contract. And a contract can be *enforced*." She lifted her eyes from the leather, and Brannoc saw something kindle in her that he liked enormously: the look of a woman who has just discovered that her most despised skill is also a weapon. "If the sealing isn't finished — if the lid still needs closing properly — then somewhere in this country there are documents that say so. Signatures. Terms. A paper trail from here to the theft of heaven."

"Ledgers as evidence," Wren breathed. "Ledgers as *leverage*."

"Ledgers as a target," Brannoc corrected grimly. "Because if I were Corvel, and I'd spent twenty years buying a throne of night, the very first thing I'd want locked away safe is the receipts." He looked around the stove at each of them in turn — the ferryman, the apprentice, the apostate bookkeeper — this absurd little congregation of nobodies. "And I'd send someone to collect them. Someone patient. Someone who knows exactly how the keeper of those ledgers thinks, and fights, and grieves."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, down in the fraying streets, a bell began to ring — not an alarm, just some parish clock striking the hour, stubborn and ordinary, as though the night were still a night like other nights.

"So what do we do?" Wren asked.

"We run," Brannoc said. "Tonight. Before whoever he sends arrives." He was already reaching for his coat, his ruined hands moving faster than they had in years. "Because I've met the inner circle's enforcers, girl, and I'll tell you what frightens me most. It's not their blades."

Xanthe stood, gathering the ledgers to her chest like a child, like a shield, like both at once.

"Then what?"

Brannoc pulled the door open onto the black, starless street, and the wind that came in smelled of river and cold iron.

"It's that the person Corvel trusts to bring you in," he said, "will be someone you love."
