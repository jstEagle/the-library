## 7. The Country That No Longer Exists

Odji had ferried the Sable for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one thing the river taught better than any priest: everything moves, and if you fight the moving you drown. So when the road to the old capital began behaving like a river that had changed its mind, he was, he reflected, perhaps the only one of them not actively panicking.

"Third time past this mill," Brannoc said flatly.

They stood at a crossroads where a signpost pointed four directions, three of which were east. The mill sat where it had sat an hour ago, its wheel turning in water Odji could have sworn ran the other way before. The miller's wife waved at them from the doorway. She had waved an hour ago too, with the same arm, mid-same-sentence by the look of her mouth.

"Not third time," Odji said. "Same time. We are not going in circles. We are being folded."

"That's worse," Wren said. She had the map spread across a fence rail and was weighting the corners with stones, which was pointless, because the map had begun disagreeing with itself. The ink crawled when you weren't looking. The town of Harrow's Bend had migrated half an inch north overnight, dragging its little printed church with it like a snail shell.

"It's worse," Brannoc agreed, "but I want us saying what's true."

Odji liked Brannoc for this. The ward-smith was a slab of a man with a beard you could lose a cat in, and since the night the sky went black he had taken it upon himself to be the group's official announcer of doom. Someone had to do it, and Xanthe did it quietly, Wren did it tearfully, so Brannoc did it loudly, like a man reading out prices at market.

Xanthe came back down the lane from the mill, carrying bread and a jug of cider she'd traded for two coins that were, as far as anyone knew, still legal currency in a country that might not exist by winter.

"Miller's wife says her sister's wedding happened last spring," Xanthe reported. "She also says it hasn't happened yet. Both, cheerfully. Same sentence. She didn't notice."

"The country's coming apart from the edges," Brannoc said. "Told you."

"You did tell us," Wren said. "You tell us every morning. You're very consistent, Brannoc. It's comforting in a horrible way."

Odji took the cider jug, because someone had to be practical. "In my experience," he said, "when a thing is coming apart, you do not stand at the edge discussing it. You go toward the middle, where the hands holding it together still have grip." He nodded east — the real east, he hoped, the one his bones insisted on. "The capital is the middle."

Nobody argued. They never argued much with Odji about direction. He suspected it was because he was old and Brown-skinned and looked like a man who had opinions worth waiting for, or possibly because he was the only one among them who had ever actually been anywhere.

They walked.

* * *

By the second day, the strangeness stopped being a novelty.

It was small things first. A village called Tetchley on Monday that answered to Tethley on Tuesday, whose inhabitants got faintly offended if you used the wrong spelling, though none of them could say why. A shepherd who remembered his flock having forty sheep and counted thirty-nine and then, counting again, forty-one, and who had decided, sensibly, to stop counting. A bridge over a stream no wider than a stride that took eleven steps to cross, each step landing with the particular wet slap of a longer crossing than the eye allowed.

Then bigger things. A courier overtook them on the road going east at a trot, nodded politely, and passed them again an hour later going west, looking tired but not surprised. A funeral procession crossed their path twice — same mourners, same coffin, second time with one fewer mourner, and Odji did not ask which end of the procession the missing man had been standing at.

Wren kept a journal of it, because Wren kept a journal of everything, and by Wednesday evening her entries had developed a tone Odji could only describe as scholarly screaming.

*"The road doesn't loop,"* she read aloud by firelight, while Brannoc pretended not to listen and Xanthe actually listened, sharpening her knife in slow strokes. *"That's the horror of it. Loops repeat. This is more like — like a sentence being edited while you read it. We walk the same mile and it's a different mile each time, and the country is smoothing over the joins behind us. The edges aren't crumbling, they're being — rewritten. Tidied. Like someone's cleaning up a draft."*

"Corvel," Xanthe said, not looking up from her knife.

"Or whatever Corvel serves," Brannoc said. "Gods don't tidy. Gods don't need to."

"My god went quiet seven years ago," Xanthe said, "and I told myself she was listening anyway." She put the knife away. "I'm done telling myself things."

Odji poked the fire. It was a good fire. He had built fires in rain, in fog, once underwater, and this one behaved, which was more than he could say for anything else in the country.

"You know what I miss?" he said, because someone should say something warm before everyone went to bed frightened. "Fish. Real fish, from the river, stupid fat bream that fight you the whole way up. Out here there's streams full of fish that look right and taste like nothing. Like eating the idea of a fish."

"That's grim," Wren said.

"It's true. And truth is Brannoc's job, but he was busy."

Brannoc snorted. Xanthe almost smiled. Small victories. On a night when the stars overhead were absent and the darkness above the trees had a texture to it, like felt, like something you could reach up and touch and leave a handprint in — small victories mattered.

* * *

On the fourth day they met the woman who arrived before she left.

She came walking toward them on the road, a travel-stained merchant with a pack and a donkey, and she stopped dead when she saw them, staring at Xanthe especially, and then she laughed — a wild, unhinged little laugh that Odji recognized, because he'd heard it from men who'd been swept overboard and lived.

"Oh thank all forgotten gods, other people. Are you real? Pinch me. No — don't, actually, I've had enough of being pinched by reality this week."

Her name was Sefa. She was a spice trader out of Vell's Crossing, heading south, and she had, she explained over their shared lunch, left Vell's Crossing tomorrow.

"Tomorrow," Wren repeated carefully.

"Three days from now. I remember packing. I remember kissing my husband goodbye and him saying 'write often' and me saying 'I always do,' and I remember all of it clearly, and it hasn't happened. But here I am with the blisters from the walking I haven't done." She held up her feet, blistered, as evidence. "And I've met myself twice on this road. Once going the other way. She looked better rested than me, which felt rude."

"And where's Vell's Crossing now?" Xanthe asked, in her ledger-voice, the voice she used when she wanted facts instead of feelings.

Sefa blinked. "What do you mean, *now*?"

"I mean, can you find it again? If you turned around and walked back."

The spice trader was quiet for a moment. Her donkey ate grass with the untroubled patience of an animal too dumb to notice the apocalypse.

"No," she admitted. "I walked back yesterday. Or I will. There was a crossroads, and then a hill, and then the road just — ended. Not blocked. Ended. Like the last page of a book. Grass growing right up to it, birds singing beyond it, but the road's done, and when I stepped past the edge—" she shivered. "There wasn't anything wrong. That's the worst part. I stepped past and I was somewhere else entirely, a farm track, and a boy there told me I was standing in the middle of Marlow Fen, which has been a lake for two hundred years. And the lake was gone. Drained. Never existed, according to him, except he knew the word *Marlow*, he just thought it meant the farm."

"Unwritten," Wren whispered, writing it down. "From the edges inward. Oh, it's exactly like Brannoc said—"

"Told you," Brannoc said.

"—and the rate of it, if Vell's Crossing is already gone, we have weeks, maybe—"

"Weeks to reach the capital," Xanthe finished. "Which is why we don't turn around. Which is why we walk faster."

Sefa looked between them. "Where are you all going, exactly?"

"Away from the ending of things," Odji said, "and toward the man doing the ending, to ask him impolite questions."

"Oh," said Sefa. "Well. Good luck." She paused. "Actually — take the southern fork at the shrine ahead. Everyone takes the northern fork because it looks newer, but the northern fork goes through Ashcombe, and Ashcombe isn't there anymore. I watched it not be there. Very upsetting."

They thanked her. She gave Wren a bag of pepper as a farewell gift — "money's only money while the country agrees on it, but pepper is always pepper" — and went on her impossible way, arriving steadily at destinations she hadn't departed.

"She'll be all right," Odji said, watching her go. "People are harder to erase than roads."

"You sound sure," Xanthe said.

"I ferry people for a living. Roads I would not trust with a rowboat."

* * *

The shrine Sefa mentioned was a small stone thing at the fork, older than any god currently in fashion, worn smooth by centuries of travelers touching it for luck. Odji touched it out of habit and found it warm, faintly, like a stone that had recently been sat upon, and he chose to take that as a good omen rather than think too hard about it.

The southern fork wound through country that still mostly agreed with itself, and they made good time, and that evening they camped in the ruins of a coaching inn that had burned down — or would burn down; there was disagreement among the locals — and it was there, over a supper of trail rations improved by Sefa's pepper, that Wren asked the question Odji had been waiting for someone to ask.

"How do you know where east is?"

Odji looked up from his bowl. "What?"

"The stars are gone. The sun comes up late and in the wrong place some mornings. Compass needles spin. Every map we own is lying to us. So how do *you* know we're going the right way?" Her voice wasn't challenging. It was genuinely frightened, the fear of an apprentice archivist whose entire trade was knowing where information lived. "Everyone keeps following you. I keep following you. Why do we trust your sense of direction more than our own eyes?"

Odji set down his bowl. He considered giving the easy answer — thirty-one years on the river, a body that knows — but Wren deserved better than easy, and besides, the fire was warm and the night was long.

"When I was young," he said, "younger than you, my grandmother took me out on the Sable at night and blindfolded me, and put the steering pole in my hands, and said, *find the deep channel.* And I couldn't. Nobody could. So she said: stop trying to know where the water is. Know where *you* are. Your feet feel the boat lean. Your hands feel the pole drag. The river tells you about itself through you, not to your eyes." He tapped his own chest. "East is in here. Has been since before the stars went out. Whatever Corvel stole from the sky, he did not steal it from me, because I never kept it up there to begin with."

Silence around the fire. Then Brannoc, gruffly: "My grandmother made me mend fences."

"Also valuable," Odji conceded.

"She said it builds character. She was lying. It builds the ability to mend fences."

Laughter, even from Xanthe — a short, surprised bark of it, quickly smothered, but real. Odji counted it a triumph greater than any he'd won poling a ferry through floodwater.

Later, when the others slept, Xanthe stayed up, as she always did, sitting watch with the ledgers — the true ones, the ones with pages in her own handwriting that she hadn't written. Odji joined her. He didn't sleep well these nights; the dark above the trees had a pressure to it, like the moment before a storm breaks, and he'd rather sit with company than lie under it alone.

"They're good people," he said quietly. "Your debtors. Your defectors. You know that, yes?"

"They're my responsibility."

"They're your people. Responsibility is what you call it when you're afraid to say the other word."

Xanthe gave him a sideways look, the kind she probably thought was stern and was mostly tired. "You're a bossy old man."

"I'm a ferryman. Bossiness is how nobody drowns." He nodded at the ledger in her lap. "Anything new in there?"

She was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone careful and thin, the way it did whenever the book was involved.

"There's an entry dated three weeks from now."

Odji felt the cold come up off the ground, off the whole unwritten dark country, and settle into his old bones. "What does it say?"

"*'The Hollow opens for the one who pays in names.'*" She closed the book. "And beneath it, in the margin — my handwriting again, but shakier, like it was written running — one more line. *'Bring them all. Don't let her come alone.'*"

"Her?"

"I don't know." Xanthe stared into the fire. "Vessia. Me. Someone we haven't met yet."

Odji looked east, toward the capital he could not see, in the country that was forgetting itself mile by mile, and found that his hands, resting on his knees, had gone cold despite the fire.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd best arrive early. I hate keeping appointments I haven't made."

Far off to the northeast, where the old capital slept under its starless sky, the horizon flickered — once, twice — like a lamp guttering in a house where someone was deciding, room by room, whether to keep the lights on at all.
