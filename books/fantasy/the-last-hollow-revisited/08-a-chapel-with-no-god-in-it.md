## 8. A Chapel with No God In It

The storm caught them an hour out of Millbrook, and the chapel caught them an hour after that.

It stood alone on a bald hill like a tooth left in an old jaw—Vessia's chapel, unmistakably, even roofless and half-swallowed by ivy, because only Vessia's chapels were built without a single door on their western face. Where other gods got great bronze portals to impress the faithful, Vessia got a wall. A blank one. *You do not come to me,* the architecture said. *I decide when we speak.*

Xanthe had sworn her oath in a chapel just like it, twenty-two years and one lifetime ago.

"Charming," Brannoc said, ducking under a fallen lintel with the wariness of a man who had spent his career making things hold together and distrusted anything that hadn't. "Structurally, this is a dare."

"It's shelter," Odji said, shaking rain off his coat in the doorway like a river dog. "Argue with it tomorrow."

Wren slipped inside last, dripping, clutching the satchel of ledgers against their chest as though the storm might audit them. "There's no god in it," they observed, peering into the dark. "Shouldn't there be a—a presence? A hum? I read that old chapels hum."

"Old chapels creak," Odji said. "Everything else is poetry."

Xanthe didn't answer. She was standing in the nave with rain running off her chin, looking up at where the altar should have been, and feeling something she couldn't put a ledger-entry to. Not grief exactly. Grief she knew; grief had columns and subtotals. This was more like opening a strongbox you'd been carrying for years and finding it empty—and being unable to remember whether you'd ever actually looked inside before.

Seven years of silence. Seven years since Vessia had last spoken to her—not in words, the god didn't waste words, but in that pressure behind the sternum, that sense of being *held in account*, which sounds cold until you've been a starving child with nothing and someone, something, has decided you count.

She'd told herself the silence was discipline. Gods were busy. Faith was measured in how well you kept the books when the auditor wasn't watching. She'd built a whole life on that reasoning, floor by floor, and now she knew what the floors were made of, and the chapel was very quiet, and she was so tired.

"I'll see to a fire," Odji said quietly, and steered Wren toward the vestry with a hand between their shoulder blades. Brannoc followed, giving Xanthe a look she pretended not to see—the look of a man deciding not to ask questions he'd regret the answers to.

Which left her alone with the dark, the drip of rain through the broken roof, and the silence of Vessia.

* * *

She didn't mean to shout. She'd planned, if anything, a dignified conversation. But dignity had been dying by inches since the night the stars went out, and somewhere between the drowned archive and this hilltop it had finished bleeding out, and what came out of her instead was raw.

"Say something."

The ivy dripped. The fire crackled faintly from the vestry. Somewhere overhead, wind worried at loose slate.

"You owe me that much." Her voice bounced off stone. "You took my name first—did you know that? Before Corvel ever touched the ledgers. I was nine years old and I was *nobody*, and you gave me a place in the world. You gave me work. You gave me—" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You gave me rules. Do you know what I did with those rules? I built my whole self out of them. I have been *faithful*. I balanced accounts while my friends starved around me because you taught me that everything evens out, that nothing is owed that isn't recorded, that if I just kept the ledgers true then—then what? Then you'd speak?"

Silence.

"I buried Marisol." The name tore going out. It always did. "My sword-sister, back when Ivetta and I were three and the world was simple. Fever, in a border camp, and there was guild-luck enough in the strongbox to buy her a week of good weather and I *asked you*, Vessia. I asked and asked, and you said nothing, and I obeyed the rules anyway because you taught me obedience was holy. And now I find out your silence wasn't discipline. Now I find out maybe it wasn't even *you*. So say something. Say anything. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me you're gone. Tell me you never existed and I've been talking to a wall my whole life—at least a wall would be honest."

Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the echo of it rolling down the nave, hated the smallness of a woman screaming at weather in a ruined church.

Nothing. Of course nothing.

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the dark went red. And that was when she noticed the color on her fingertips.

Fresco paint. Flaking from the wall beside her where she'd braced herself—except the flake had fallen away and beneath it, in the lightning-flash gloom, was pigment. Old pigment, deep blue, the exact shade the guild used for entries in the master ledger. The shade reserved, in fact, for one kind of entry above all others.

Doors.

"Odji," she called, not taking her eyes off it. "Bring the light."

* * *

The fresco covered the entire eastern apse, hidden under centuries of smoke-grime and a skin of plaster some well-meaning renovator had slapped over it, and once Odji's lamp found its edge, the four of them cleared it in shifts with knife-blades and wet cloth and increasingly irreverent commentary.

"There's a horse here," Wren announced. "Or possibly a very ambitious dog."

"A door," Brannoc corrected. He'd gone quiet in the way he did around ward-work, his smith's hands hovering near the images like he could feel heat off them. "Look—horse, cart, and behind them, a doorway drawn in the blue. That's not decoration. That's classification."

Because that was what the fresco was, as it emerged inch by inch: a taxonomy. Vessia figured large at the center—taller than any mortal proportion, robed in the same deep blue, face worn to a featureless smoothness by time or by design—but Vessia was not, as every iconography Xanthe had ever learned insisted, bestowing gifts. There were no coins raining on the faithful. No scales tipping toward mercy. No open hand.

Instead, Vessia held chains. And the chains ran outward across the whole curve of the apse, and at the end of every chain was a door, and every door was shut, and every door was bound—wrapped in chain, sealed with sigils Brannoc identified under his breath with mounting alarm, locked with keys depicted hanging from the god's belt in rows, dozens of them, a jailer's ring.

"That's wrong," Xanthe said. Her voice sounded far away. "That's *heretical*. Vessia opens doors. Vessia gives passage. The whole rite of accounting—the whole point—is that debts paid are doors opened. That's what Corvel taught us. That's what the liturgy says."

"Corvel," Odji said mildly, "also taught us the stars were eternal right up until they weren't. Maybe check the source."

Wren had climbed onto the broken altar rail for a better view and was tracing the sequence of panels with one finger. "It's a story. Left to right. Look—in the beginning panel the doors are already chained, see? They start shut. And then—" they squinted, "—there's people. Little people, petitioners, holding out ledgers. And Vessia is... choosing. Unlocking some. One here, one there. Like—"

"Like lending," Brannoc said grimly. "Not giving. Lending. Every door she opens gets re-locked in the next panel. Look at the bottom register—there's a tally running along the base of the whole thing, all the way around the apse. Marks. Thousands of them."

Xanthe stepped closer, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the rain still drying in her clothes.

She recognized the tally-marks.

Four vertical strokes and a diagonal. Grouped in fives. It was the oldest notation in the guild's books, the one apprentices learned before they learned letters, the one used in the margin of every page for entries too old or too dangerous to write out plain. She'd made ten thousand of them herself over the years. She had assumed they were a bookkeeping convenience.

They were older than the guild. They were *hers*—the god's. Vessia had been keeping count of something, here on this wall, long before the Hollow Guild existed to keep count of anything. And the thing being counted, panel after panel after panel, was doors opened and doors re-sealed. Debts issued. Debts collected.

"She wasn't a giver of power," Xanthe heard herself say. Each word landed like a coin on stone. "She was a jailer. All of it—the memories, the luck, the doors to nowhere—we thought we were trading treasures. We were trading *keys*. Off a prisoner's ring. From a prison she built."

"Prison for what?" Wren asked, and then answered themselves, turning slowly back to the central figure, to the vast smooth face, to the expression that time had worn off but whose posture remained: weight forward, shoulders set, arms straining slightly against nothing—or rather against everything, against the sum total of every chain she held. "For whatever's behind the doors."

"No," Brannoc said softly. He had reached the final panel, the one nearest the floor, half-hidden under rubble, and he scraped the grime from it with two fingers. "Worse than that. Look."

The last panel showed the country itself—the hills and rivers of home, rendered small, sitting *inside* a doorframe. Not beside a door. Within one. The whole nation, borders and all, framed in blue, and Vessia's chains wrapped around the frame, and the god's smooth face turned not outward toward the viewer but *inward*, watching, guarding from outside.

A jailer doesn't stand inside the cell. A jailer stands at the door of it.

"The country's the prison," Odji said. He said it the way he read river-currents, flat and certain. "We've been living inside the lock."

The lamp guttered. In the vestry, the fire popped. Nobody spoke for a long moment, and the silence had a different texture than the god's silence—it was the silence of four people simultaneously recalculating everything they'd ever been told.

Then Xanthe started to laugh. It startled all of them, herself included—laughing, in a ruined church, at the end of the world—but it was that or weep and she'd done her weeping in private, in installments, for years, and she was fresh out.

"All those sermons," she managed. "*Vessia holds the key to your freedom.* We sang it. I sang it at Marisol's pyre. And it was true, it was literally true, we just never asked what the key was *for*." She wiped her eyes. "And Corvel. Corvel and his circle, devouring divinity, crowning themselves in the dark—they're not breaking in somewhere. They're not stealing treasure. They're picking the lock of the cell we live in and calling themselves gods for having the nerve."

"Does it change anything?" Wren asked quietly. "What we're doing?"

Xanthe considered the question honestly, because Wren deserved honesty and because lying in a dead god's chapel seemed unwise. "Yes," she said finally. "Before tonight, I was going to the Last Hollow to undo a theft. Steal back the stars. Punish the thieves. Simple ledger-work—debits and credits, restore the balance." She laid her palm flat against the final panel, against the painted frame of the painted world. "But if the whole country is a cell, and the Last Hollow is a door out of it—then the question isn't whether we open it. It's what we let through when we do. Because a door out of a prison, friends, swings both ways."

"And whatever Vessia spent eternity locking *in* with us—" Brannoc began.

"—is on our side of the door," Xanthe finished. "Yes."

Outside, the storm had moved on, leaving the hilltop washed and moonlit—and Xanthe, stepping to the empty western doorway to breathe air that tasted of wet grass and ozone, saw the thing that ended the night's theology.

Lanterns. Down in the valley road, strung out in a line, moving with the disciplined spacing she knew better than her own heartbeat. Enforcer formation. Six, eight, ten of them, closing on the chapel from the direction of Millbrook.

And at the head of the line, tall and unhurried despite the mud, walked a woman whose stride Xanthe could have picked out of a thousand—had picked out of a thousand, across a hundred battlefields, back when they'd shared a bedroll and a god and a name.

Ivetta had found them.

Behind Xanthe, in the ruined apse, the lamp threw Vessia's chained doors into flickering relief, and the jailer's smooth face seemed—for one trick of the light—to be watching the lanterns come, and waiting to see what her former clerk would do about it.
