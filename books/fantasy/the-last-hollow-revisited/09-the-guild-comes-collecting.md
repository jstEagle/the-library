## 9. The Guild Comes Collecting

Wren had learned three things about travel since leaving the guildhouse: that her feet were traitors, that Brannoc snored in a key she could only describe as "collapsing barn," and that the world was much larger than the archives had led her to believe.

The archives had not lied, exactly. They had simply been written by people who thought of distance as an entry in a ledger—*three days to Marrowford, road tolls two silver*—and never as three days of actual walking, with blisters involved.

"You're limping on purpose," Brannoc said, falling into step beside her. "I've seen you walk. You're doing it wrong."

"I'm doing it *injured*."

"Same thing, done badly."

Wren glared up at him. Brannoc was very tall and very broad and had the face of a man who'd been carved by someone who got bored halfway through, but he carried his ward-smith's kit like it weighed nothing and complained like it weighed everything. She had decided, somewhere around the second day, that she liked him enormously.

Ahead of them, Xanthe walked the way she always walked: evenly, quietly, like a sentence someone had edited until nothing unnecessary remained. Odji drifted at the rear, humming to himself, which he did constantly and which Wren had come to understand was how the ferryman listened to the world. He hummed differently near water. Near old water, he went quiet altogether.

They were making for the crossing at Tarnlow Bridge—or what the maps insisted was Tarnlow Bridge, though since the stars had gone out, Wren trusted maps the way she trusted merchants' smiles.

"The ledger says the bridge is safe," she offered.

Xanthe didn't turn around. "The ledger says a great many things."

"It's *your* ledger."

"That's what worries me."

It was meant lightly—Wren could hear the almost-humor under it—but it landed wrong anyway, because they all knew what she'd found in those pages. Entries in Xanthe's own handwriting describing things she had never recorded. A purchase order for the sky. Wren had watched her read them by candlelight, watched her face do something careful and terrible, watched her close the book gently, as if it might bruise.

Since then Xanthe had been carrying the ledgers strapped across her back like a wounded animal she refused to put down.

They crested the last hill an hour before dusk, and there was the river—wide, brown-gold, moving slow as syrup—and there was the bridge, and there was also the bridge.

Wren stopped walking. "That's," she said. "Hm."

There were two of them.

Not side by side. *Interleaved.* One bridge stood where the maps put it, old stone, five arches, mossy and dignified. The other occupied the same space—a ghost-bridge of pale timber and iron, newer-looking, arched higher, its railings painted a cheerful guild-blue. Where the two structures overlapped, the stone showed through the timber like bones through skin, and where a lantern hung from one railing, its light fell twice, once warm and once cold, in slightly different places.

"They've doubled," Brannoc said, with professional interest. "The unmaking's reached the crossings. A thing that connects two banks can't decide which memory of itself is true anymore, so it's trying to be both."

"Can we cross it?" Odji asked.

"We can cross *a* bridge," Brannoc said. "Which one we cross when we get off is anyone's guess."

Xanthe studied it for a long moment, and Wren saw her do the thing she did—weigh it, cost it, enter it invisibly into some internal account. "We cross," she said. "There's no other ford for thirty miles, and I don't like our shadow."

Wren looked back down the road. She saw nothing. But Xanthe had been saying that for two days now, *I don't like our shadow*, and Xanthe had survived longer in the Hollow Guild than Wren had survived in its archive, so nobody argued.

They crossed.

It was, Wren would reflect later, going tolerably well right up until the moment it wasn't.

* * *

The trouble with a doubled bridge, Brannoc explained as they walked, was that it existed in two places at once, which meant two sets of acoustics, two sets of echoes, and—if you were unlucky—two sets of footsteps.

Wren heard the second set before she understood it.

Boots. Behind them. Not their own—the rhythm was wrong, too many, too disciplined. Marching.

"Ivetta," Xanthe said quietly. She hadn't turned around. Her voice did something Wren had never heard it do—not fear exactly, but the memory of an old wound being asked politely whether it would like to reopen. "Six of them. Maybe seven."

"How can you possibly—" Wren began.

"She walks on her heels when she wants to be heard and her toes when she doesn't." Xanthe finally glanced back. "She wants to be heard."

The enforcers came out of the far mist like figures stepping out of a bad memory. Six of them, grey-cloaked, unhurried, and at their head a woman built like a drawn bow, sword already loose in its sheath, dark hair cropped short, a scar splitting one eyebrow. She stopped maybe forty paces off, on the timber half of the bridge, while they stood on the stone half, and somehow both halves of the bridge held both parties at once, so that Wren kept seeing double—six enemies here, six enemies *there*, flickering between the railings.

"Sister," Ivetta called.

"Ivetta." Xanthe's voice was level. "You brought friends."

"You brought thieves and debtors and a ward-breaker who should be in irons." Ivetta's eyes moved across each of them the way a clerk checks a list. When they reached Wren, they paused. "The archivist. Corvel wants the ledgers. He doesn't especially want the rest of you, but I told him you wouldn't hand them over alone."

"That was generous of you."

"I know you, Thackeray." Something moved under the flatness of Ivetta's voice. Wren couldn't name it. Loyalty, maybe, wearing armor. "You'll die holding paper before you give it up clean. So I'm asking first. Hand them over. Come home. Whatever you think you found in those pages, the guild can explain it better than your imagination can."

"Can it?" Xanthe asked. "Can it explain why my handwriting signed contracts twenty years before I could write?"

For the first time, one of the other enforcers shifted uncomfortably. Wren noted it with the small fierce satisfaction of someone who had spent years noticing things nobody wanted noticed.

"I have my orders," Ivetta said.

"You always did."

"And you always followed yours. That's what I keep coming back to, Thackeray. Seven years you kept the books. Seven years you never asked where the coin came from. Now suddenly—" she gestured, at the doubled bridge, at the starless sky, at all of it, "—suddenly you're a rebel because you read something that upset you?"

"Yes," Xanthe said simply. "Reading something that upsetting ought to change a person. That's rather the point of reading."

And Wren—foolish, blistered, exhausted Wren—fell a little bit in love with her right there on the bridge, in the way one falls in love with a teacher, or a lighthouse.

Ivetta sighed, and drew her sword.

* * *

The skirmish lasted, by Wren's count, somewhere between four minutes and a lifetime.

It began with the enforcers advancing, and it immediately became strange, because they were advancing on the timber bridge while Xanthe's group stood on the stone one, and the two bridges kept trading places. Wren watched a grey-cloak plant a boot firmly on a plank and step down onto mossy cobble. Watched Odji sidestep a sword-thrust that arrived from *around* a railing that was currently elsewhere. The fight happened in overlapping versions, echoes arriving late, shouts coming from mouths that hadn't opened yet.

Brannoc roared something and slammed his palm against the bridge-stone, and Wren felt the wards wake—a shudder, a hum in her teeth. The nearest stretch of timber bridge turned briefly solid and *stubborn*, and two enforcers stumbled as the planks refused to flex underfoot.

"Don't kill them!" Xanthe shouted—to Brannoc, to everyone. "None of them! They're following orders, same as I ever did!"

"That is a *terrible* tactical doctrine!" Brannoc bellowed back, blocking a blade with his forearm-guard.

"It's mine!"

Ivetta came for Xanthe directly, of course. Of course she did. Wren scrambled backward along the stone parapet, hugging the ledgers' spare satchel—he'd made her carry it, *you run faster than me and you know which pages matter*—and watched the two sword-sisters meet in the middle of a bridge that existed in two places at once.

They fought the way old friends argue. Every strike was a sentence; every parry, a rebuttal. Ivetta drove Xanthe back along the timber railings, fast and hard, and Xanthe gave ground like water, turning every blow just enough, spending nothing, and Wren realized with a lurch that Xanthe wasn't fighting to win. She was fighting to *keep talking*.

"Ivetta—" clash — "the wards—"

"—were failing—"

"—were *sold*—"

"Liar's arithmetic!" Ivetta hammered her back a step. "Corvel showed us the ledgers. Clean ones. Real ones. The border tax records, the ward maintenance schedules, everything paid and proper—"

"He wrote you clean books because he knew you'd check!"

Something flickered across Ivetta's face. Doubt has a particular shape, Wren knew—she'd seen it in novices caught in archival errors, that awful instant of *oh no, was I wrong?*—and for half a heartbeat Ivetta wore it.

Then an enforcer screamed.

One of the grey-cloaks had pressed Odji toward the outer railing, and the railing, being presently more timber than stone, gave a creak and slid sideways into its other self, taking the man's balance with it. He windmilled, caught the rail of the *other* bridge, which chose that moment to be somewhere else entirely—

And the river took him.

Except the river, too, was doubled. Wren saw him hit water that was brown-gold and slow, and water that was grey-green and fast, sometimes both, and the current below the bridge began doing something rivers should not do—flowing in two directions at once, braiding around itself like a rope twisting tight.

"Odji!" Wren shrieked, because the ferryman had gone still at the railing, utterly still, head cocked, listening.

He was humming. Low. Two notes at once, one for each river.

"*Old river,*" Odji called out, clear and calm over the chaos, in the tone of a man greeting a colleague. "*Grandmother Water.* You had a name before the maps came. You had a name before the bridges. Sess-amarra. Sess-amarra, grandmother, your children are fighting on your back—"

The river *shivered*.

Wren felt it through the soles of her blistered feet. The whole doubled span groaned, stone and timber settling together like an old woman pulling her skirts straight, and the brawling currents untwisted into one broad, slow, single-minded flow—one river, one name, one opinion.

The drowning enforcer surfaced in ordinary water, coughing, and the current—almost courteously—swept him against the near bank instead of under the arches.

Every fighter on the bridge paused. Even Ivetta.

Because the bridge had chosen too. The timber version faded like breath off glass, and there was only the old stone bridge, mossy and dignified, and standing on it six enforcers and four fugitives, all suddenly very aware of the drop beneath them and the name the river remembered having.

"What did you *do*?" Ivetta demanded.

"Told the truth to something older than the guild," Odji said placidly. "Works more often than you'd think."

Xanthe lowered her sword—not sheathed it, lowered it, point-down, the way one does with a tool no longer needed. "Go home, Ivetta."

"My orders—"

"Your orders say bring me the ledgers. Look at where you're standing." Xanthe spread her free hand at the healed bridge, the calmed river, the mist beginning to gather again downstream. "This country is being unwritten, and your masters are holding the pen. I've read the entries, Iv. In my own hand. Pages I never wrote. If you take these ledgers to Corvel, you're not collecting a debt—you're delivering the last proof he needs."

"You could be lying."

"I taught you to spot a liar. Did I ever lie to you?"

Ivetta's jaw worked. Around her, the enforcers had regrouped into a loose line, but nobody advanced, and Wren understood why: the river was watching them. Everyone could feel it. Grandmother Water had opinions about guests who brawled on her back, and she was keeping track.

"Seven days," Ivetta said at last, hoarse. "I'll report resistance. It buys you seven days before Corvel sends someone who won't argue on bridges."

"Ivetta—"

"Seven days, Thackeray." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the old name—the guild-name, the sister-name—and Wren watched two kinds of loyalty stand on one bridge and fail, entirely, to resolve. "Run well."

She turned. The enforcers collected their soaked, coughing colleague and withdrew into the mist, boots striking heels, wanting this time, Wren thought, *not* to be heard.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

Then Brannoc sat down abruptly on the mossy cobblestones and said, "I'm going to be sick from adrenaline," and was.

* * *

They camped that night on the far bank, in the lee of a fallen milestone whose inscriptions had begun arguing with themselves—Marrowford, 12 miles / Marrowford, 40 miles / MARROWFORD, DO NOT—under circumstances best described in an appendix.

Wren took first watch because she couldn't have slept if the gods themselves had tucked her in. Her hands were still shaking. She wrapped them around a cup of Odji's terrible river-tea and watched the water move, all one direction now, obedient to its old name.

Odji settled beside her without being asked. He did that.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

"No. Yes. My hands won't stop."

"That's the body finishing the fight after the mind calls it off. Perfectly normal. Happens to me every time I win an argument with a customer." He sipped his tea. "You did well today. You kept the satchel and you kept your head, in reverse order of difficulty."

"I mostly hid behind a railing."

"A railing that was frequently elsewhere. That's advanced work."

Despite everything, Wren laughed—a wobbly, post-battle laugh, the kind that comes out sideways. Down the bank, Xanthe sat apart, as she always did, cleaning her sword by starlight, which was to say by no light at all, which was to say from memory.

"She knew her footsteps," Wren said quietly. "From behind. Through mist. On a doubled bridge."

"Yes."

"Seven years in the guild together and she can hear Ivetta's *heels*."

"Mm." Odji gazed out at the water. "You know what I heard today? When the two of them fought. Underneath the steel. Do you know what I heard?"

Wren shook her head.

"Two people who each think the other is the one worth saving." He drained his tea and rose, joints crackling like green firewood. "That kind of loyalty cuts worse than any blade, little archivist. And it doesn't stop cutting just because you've walked away from it."

He wandered off toward his bedroll, humming something in two notes at once, one for each version of the night.

Wren stayed on watch. Around midnight, soft footsteps came to her—Xanthe, sleepless, wrapping a blanket around Wren's shoulders without a word, the way you'd settle a ledger entry that had been left open too long.

"Ivetta will report," Wren said. "Seven days. Then someone worse comes."

"I know."

"Where do we go that's seven days away and safer than here?"

Xanthe was quiet for a long time. Out on the black water, the river murmured its old name to itself, over and over, like a prayer or a boast.

"South," she said at last. "To the old capital. There's an archive under it—the real one, drowned, the guild pretends it doesn't exist. If there are answers anywhere, they're underwater." She paused, and something bleak and dry touched her voice. "I've spent my whole life keeping accounts. It's past time I audited the auditors."

She walked back toward the fire, and Wren watched her go, and above them the sky stayed empty all the way to the hills, but the river, at least, remembered its name.

Seven days. Wren counted them the only way she knew how: carefully, in ink, in the margin of the truest ledger she had.

Behind them, far back down the south road, something began to follow that made no sound at all—not even footsteps.

Not even heels.
