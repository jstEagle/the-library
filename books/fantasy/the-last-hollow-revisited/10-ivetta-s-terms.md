## 10. Ivetta's Terms

Ivetta Vane had been walking for three days when she finally caught up with them, and in those three days she had rehearsed this conversation forty-one times.

She knew the number because counting was what she did instead of sleeping. Forty-one rehearsals, and every single one had gone differently from the one before it. In seven of them she drew her sword immediately. In eleven of them Xanthe did. In the best one—the one she kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth—they sat down across from each other like reasonable people, drank something hot, and parted as friends who happened to be on opposite sides of the end of the world.

Ivetta had never once in her life managed to have the conversation she rehearsed.

The trail was easy enough to follow, even for someone who'd spent her career following people who didn't want to be followed. Brannoc left boot-prints deep as graves—heavier than he looked, that one, or else he carried more than he looked, which amounted to the same thing in Ivetta's experience. The ferryman's stride was long and unhurried, a man who had never once hurried anywhere in his life and resented being asked to start. The apprentice—Wren—left small prints with an odd hitch in the left heel, favoring an old injury, probably from the archives, probably from hauling ledgers up ladders built for taller people.

And Xanthe.

Xanthe's prints were the ones Ivetta studied longest, crouched over them at dawn on the second day while frost formed on her eyelashes. Even, economical, weight balanced exactly where you'd want it if you expected to have to move suddenly. She hadn't changed since the academy. She still walked like a woman keeping two sets of books—one for the world to see, one for herself.

That was the trouble with knowing someone twenty years. You couldn't stop reading them.

* * *

She caught them at midday, in a fold of hills where the road bent around a shrine so old its god's name had worn off the stone. The country was getting strange out here—Ivetta had seen it herself, a village that greeted her by name though she'd never been there, a bridge that took longer to cross than its length should allow—but the strangeness didn't care about her errand, so she put it out of her mind the way she put most things out of her mind, which is to say firmly and with both hands.

They saw her coming from a long way off. Of course they did. There were four of them and one of her, and one of them had spent years teaching the other three how not to be surprised by anything.

So Ivetta did the only sensible thing. She stopped thirty paces out, set her sword-belt down on the grass in plain view, and raised both empty hands.

"Before anyone gets heroic," she called, "I'd like it noted that I am unarmed, tired, and extremely hungry, and that any violence visited upon me in this condition will look terrible in whatever histories get written."

There was a pause. Then Odji's voice drifted down the hill, mild as milk: "We could feed her and then decide about the histories."

"That," said Wren, "is exactly the kind of reasoning that got us all into this."

"It is not. It is exactly the kind of reasoning that has kept us alive. Getting us into this was Xanthe."

"Thank you, Odji," said Xanthe, without turning around.

Ivetta stood very still and let the voices wash over her, because hearing Xanthe's voice for the first time in a season was doing something to her chest that she preferred not to examine while anyone was watching. Twenty years ago they had sworn their oaths side by side, kneeling in the cold of the guildhouse undercroft, and Magister Corvel had laid his hand on each of their heads and told them they were now instruments of a necessary silence. Ivetta had believed him completely. Xanthe had believed him completely too—that was the thing people got wrong about her, then and now. Xanthe wasn't a doubter. Xanthe was a believer who checked the arithmetic.

And somewhere between here and the guildhouse, the arithmetic had come out wrong.

* * *

They fed her, because of course they did. That was the other trouble with knowing someone twenty years: you knew exactly which levers worked, and Xanthe had always been helpless before a hungry person. It didn't matter if the hungry person had come to arrest her. Food first. Questions after. Ivetta had watched her do it with beggars, with deserters, once with a man who'd tried to rob her and done such a bad job of it that Xanthe ended up giving him a meal and a lecture and, inexplicably, a job.

So they sat around a small fire in the lee of the nameless shrine, and Wren ladled out barley soup with the wary dignity of someone serving soup to a wolf, and Brannoc sat where he could see the road behind her, because Brannoc trusted nothing and everyone and had the good manners to be obvious about it.

"You came alone," Brannoc said. Not a question.

"I came alone."

"How many are behind you?"

"None. Yet." Ivetta wrapped both hands around the bowl. Her fingers ached from three days of cold. "Corvel doesn't send people behind me. Corvel sends me, and then waits to see whether I'm worth sending anyone after."

"And if you're not?"

"Then nobody follows me anywhere, and I have a great deal of time to think about my choices." She looked up, found Xanthe watching her across the fire with that level, ledger-balancing gaze, and made herself smile. It felt like lifting something heavy. "You've gotten thin, Thackeray."

"You've gotten sentimental, Vane. You set your sword down before I even saw you."

"I rehearsed it. Forty-one times."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face—not quite a laugh, but the place a laugh would live if it had somewhere to stay. "Forty-two now."

"Forty-two," Ivetta agreed, "and this one wasn't in any of them."

The fire crackled. Somewhere downslope, a bird sang three notes and then thought better of it. Ivetta ate her soup and let the quiet sit, because rushing this would ruin it, and because—and here was the honest truth she'd admit to no one at the guildhouse, least of all to Corvel's smooth, patient face—she wanted a few more minutes of sitting at a fire with Xanthe Thackeray before she ruined everything.

Because that was the job. She'd accepted it on the riverbank outside Greyharbor with the ward-glass still falling out of the sky like burning snow: go after Thackeray, bring back the ledgers, and if she won't come willingly—

Well. Ivetta was hoping, with a desperation she'd have been ashamed to voice, that it wouldn't come to that. But hope was a luxury for people whose oldest friend hadn't turned traitor, and Ivetta had never been able to afford luxuries.

* * *

"The amnesty is real," she said, when the bowls were empty and Wren had taken hers to scrub at the stream with the focused energy of someone avoiding conversation. "I want you to understand that first. I wouldn't have come if it weren't real."

Xanthe turned her bowl slowly in her hands. "Whose amnesty?"

"The guild's. Which means—" Ivetta paused, and hated the pause, because Xanthe heard pauses the way musicians heard wrong notes. "Which means Corvel's. You know how it works. The magisters don't sign things; they let the guild sign for them, and then the guild isn't responsible, and then somehow neither is anybody."

"I know how it works," Xanthe said quietly. "I used to write it."

"Yes," said Ivetta. "You did."

Brannoc shifted against his pack. Odji had produced a pipe from somewhere and was smoking it with the serenity of a man watching weather he had no intention of going out in. Only Wren had stopped moving, halfway back from the stream, bowl dripping, listening with the naked hunger of the young for information they haven't yet learned to pretend they don't want.

"Here's the offer," Ivetta said, and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the only gift she had to give. "Full amnesty. Your debts voided—which, knowing the guild's bookkeeping, means something, because you're carrying more debt than anyone alive. Your oath-status restored. Your guild-name returned. Protection, papers, passage. And—" here was the part she'd rehearsed worst, the part that stuck in her throat like a fishbone, "—a seat. Not a runner's seat. A chair. Corvel wants you at the table for what comes next. He says—" and she made herself repeat it verbatim, because verbatim was evidence, "—he says there was never anyone else he trusted with the true accounts."

Silence. Long enough that the fire had time to pop twice.

"He said that," Xanthe repeated.

"He said that."

"Word for word?"

"Ivetta," Odji said gently, from behind his smoke, "you are negotiating like a woman selling a horse with a limp. Everyone can see the limp."

"The limp," Ivetta said, "is that I believe him, which should tell you everything, because I have known Corvel for twenty-five years and I have never once believed anything he said until the stars went out, and now I believe everything, and I cannot make myself stop, and it is making me insane." She heard her own voice climbing and dragged it back down. "There. Now you know the state of me. Ask your questions."

Wren had crept back to the fire. "What happens to the ledgers? If she gives them over?"

"They complete the sealing," Ivetta said. Every account owed, every transaction recorded, every name and price and signature gathered into one place—the wards needed a full ledger to bind to, Brannoc had said, and Brannoc had built half the border wards himself, so either he was right or he was lying, and Ivetta no longer trusted her ability to tell which with anyone. "Everything that was bought has to be accounted for. Everything that was sold. The country comes apart at the edges because the edges aren't paid for anymore. The ledgers settle it. After that—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than the speech had. "After that, the country holds together. Sealed, whole, safe. No more unwriting. No more villages forgetting their own names. Just—" she gestured upward, at the blank black sky where stars should have been, where stars had been for every year of her life until a month ago, "—no sky. But we'd lived under a roof our whole lives anyway. We just didn't know it."

"You think that's safety," Wren said. Not hostile. Genuinely asking.

"I think it's the only offer on the table that ends with the country still existing," Ivetta said. "Yes. I think that's safety."

* * *

Later—much later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls and the fire had been fed down to a red, thoughtful glow—Ivetta walked to the edge of the camp and found Xanthere already there, standing in the dark, looking at the starless sky the way you'd look at a wound you'd bandaged yourself and suspected you'd done it wrong.

"No guards on me tonight?" Ivetta asked.

"You didn't bring a sword."

"I brought three days of walking and twenty years of history. That's worse than a sword and you know it."

Xanthe almost smiled. Almost. "What happens to you, if I refuse?"

Ah. There it was. Ivetta had known the question was coming since the second rehearsal, and had prepared four answers, and now discarded all four in favor of the truth, because the truth was the only coin she had left that Xanthe might actually accept.

"Nothing immediate. Corvel doesn't waste assets. He'll send someone else—Kessik, maybe, or the twins. They'll be less polite than me." She turned to face her. "But eventually, if you keep refusing, and keep costing the guild, then one day I'll be given an order I can't soften and can't slow down, and I'll either follow it or I won't, and either way I stop being useful, and either way—" she spread her hands, "—I don't get to choose what I am anymore. Do you understand? That's the price of refusing. Not for you. For me."

"Ivetta—"

"Don't." Ivetta held up a hand. "Don't apologize, and don't explain, and above all don't try to recruit me. Wren's been working on me all evening with the eyes. The apprentice eyes. I'm immune to the apprentice eyes."

"Wren's eyes are genuinely very effective."

"I know. I trained them myself, indirectly, through you. Doesn't matter." She lowered her hand. Under the dead sky her friend's face was pale and sharp and familiar as her own reflection, and Ivetta found, to her horror, that her eyes were stinging, and blinked it away furiously, because she'd be damned if she cried in front of the last person in the world she was trying to convince of anything. "Twenty years ago we knelt down together in the dark and swore ourselves to a silent god and a necessary silence, and I have kept that oath every day since, including the days it made me sick, including the day it ordered me to burn the Kellam records with the families' names still in them. I kept it. I am still keeping it. And you—" her voice did something traitorous, wobbled, steadied, "—you were better at the oath than I ever was, and you broke it, and the worst part, Xanthe, the truly unbearable part, is that I read those pages you stole. The forged entries. The ward purchase. I read them, and I understood them, and some cold little corner of me went *oh*. Oh, of course. Of course that's what we were."

"Then come with me," Xanthe said softly.

"There it is."

"There it is. Come with me. Bring the ledgers—bring them to the archive, not to Corvel. Help me end it properly instead of sealing it shut. You said you can't choose what you are anymore. This is how you choose."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, with the wind moving through the grass and the shrine's nameless god presumably listening, if nameless gods listen—Ivetta let herself consider it. Let herself feel the shape of it. Walking away from the guildhouse with her sword unbuckled and her conscience unbuckled too, standing next to Xanthe at the Last Hollow, whatever that was, doing the one right thing at the end of everything.

Then she thought of Corvel's face when he'd briefed her. Not cruel. Never cruel. Patient, the way a man is patient with a fever he knows will break. *Bring her home, Vane.* Home. As if the guildhouse had ever been that. As if either of them had ever had one.

"I can't," she said. And because Xanthe deserved better than a bare refusal: "Not because I believe in the sealing. Maybe I do. Maybe I don't. But because if I walk away from it now, then every family I burned, every record I destroyed, every order I softened instead of refused—it was all for nothing. All of it. And I can survive having done those things if they were in service of something. I cannot survive them being nothing." She laughed, short and bitter. "You see? You were the better one of us after all. You found out early. I have to find out the hard way, at the end, in front of everyone."

Xanthe reached out and took her hand. Just that. Held it, the way they'd held hands crossing the frozen yard at the academy when they were nine and ten and the instructors said mercy was a ledger error.

"Amnesty refused, then," Xanthe said quietly.

"Amnesty refused." Ivetta squeezed once and let go. "Tomorrow I report that I found you. I'll tell them you were headed for the Sablefen, because you will be, because it's the only way to the capital that Corvel hasn't sealed, and because I know you, Thackeray, I know your routes like I know my own scars. I'll tell them to give me two days' lead before they send anyone. Two days is what I can buy you. It isn't much."

"It's everything."

"No," said Ivetta. "It isn't. But it's mine to give, so take it."

She turned and walked back toward the dying fire, and behind her she heard Xanthe say, very softly, to the nameless god or the starless sky or possibly just to herself:

"Two days."

* * *

Ivetta did not sleep. She sat up through the small hours watching the camp breathe—Odji's slow roll of a snore, Brannoc sleeping upright against his pack like a man expecting to be robbed, Wren curled tight as a comma with one hand still touching the strap of the satchel that held the ledgers—and she counted.

By dawn she had counted to four hundred and six reasons to turn back, and not one of them had outweighed the four hundred and seventh: that somewhere ahead of these four impossible people was a door, and that when it opened, Ivetta Vane wanted—needed, with the whole starving weight of her—simply to know what came through it.

She rose, buckled on her sword, and started walking back the way she'd come.

Behind her, faint on the morning wind, she heard Odji say cheerfully:

"Well. Now we run."
