## 11. Borrowed Luck Runs Out

Brannoc had warded doors for thirty years before the guild took his name off the register, and in all that time he had learned one iron truth: nothing good ever came of a marsh you couldn't see the far edge of.

The Sablefen spread before them like a spilled inkpot gone wrong — black water stitched with hummocks of peat and stunted alder, fog lying on it so thick it seemed less like weather than like the fen itself breathing. Somewhere out there, three days' walk if the maps were honest (they weren't; nothing was honest anymore), lay the old causeway road to the capital.

"Cheerful place," Odji said. He stood at the fen's edge with his ferryman's pole planted like a lance, squinting into the grey. "Reminds me of my mother-in-law."

"You've never been married," Wren said.

"That's what she told me."

Xanthe crouched at the margin of the water, testing the ground with two fingers the way she tested everything — carefully, skeptically, as though the world might try to cheat her. Brannoc watched her and felt the old familiar ache of looking at something well-made that had been put to the wrong use. She'd have made a fine ward-smith, once. Steady hands. A mind that counted everything twice.

"Four charms left," she said without looking up.

"Five." Brannoc patted the oilcloth bundle inside his coat. "You always forget the one sewn into your boot heel."

"I forget it because I pretend it isn't there."

"Well, it is. Five charms for four travelers and a fen that eats roads." He hefted the bundle. Guild-luck was a smith's term, really — the warders called it *residual favor*, luck distilled from a thousand small bargains the guild had made over the centuries, pressed into wax-sealed lozenges the size of a thumbnail. Swallow one, or crack it against your palm, and for an hour or so the world leaned your way. Doors unlatched for you. Bridges held. Arrows missed by the width of a hair.

The guild hoarded it the way misers hoard gold, which told Brannoc everything he needed to know about what they thought luck was worth. Everything. They'd sold the stars and kept the luck.

He cracked the first charm against his palm now, and the fog drew back from their path like a curtain being politely held aside.

"There," he said. "See? The fen likes us today."

"The fen doesn't like anyone," Odji said. "It just hasn't noticed us yet."

* * *

For the first day, the luck worked exactly as promised.

They walked the drowned causeway in single file — Xanthe leading, Wren behind her with the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a swaddled infant, then Odji, then Brannoc taking the rear because he was heaviest and if anything gave way he wanted it to give way under him first. Black water slid past on either side, deep enough to swallow a man whole and patient enough not to care. Once, something long and low surfaced near Odji's feet, regarded him with a wet golden eye, and sank again.

"Fen-pike," Odji said. "Big fellow. I knew a man who wrestled one."

"What happened to him?"

"He won." Odji paused. "Mostly."

By evening they reached a pilgrim shelter — a stone hut raised on stilts above the water, older than any of them, its door still bearing the faint scar of a ward Brannoc's own master might have cut. He touched the old groove with something like reverence. Whoever had carved it had been good. Sloppy on the third turn, but good.

They ate hard bread and smoked fish from Odji's endless supply, and Wren read aloud from one of the rescued archive fragments by candlelight, stumbling over the old script, and for an hour the Sablefen was almost bearable. Almost homey. Brannoc found himself laughing — actually laughing — at Wren's impression of Magister Corvel's lecturing voice, and thought: *this*. This was the thing the guild had never understood. You couldn't buy this. It didn't come in lozenges.

He slept better than he had in weeks, and hated himself a little for how much the charm in his blood had to do with it.

* * *

The trouble started on the second morning, when Brannoc cracked the second charm and felt nothing happen.

He shook it. Held it to the light. The lozenge sat dull and grey in his hand, its inner shimmer gone, like an ember after the fire's moved on. He'd seen it before — luck burned out, spent, dead wax — but never this fast. A fresh charm should have lasted until nightfall.

"How long did we sleep?" he asked.

"Six hours," Xanthe said. "Why?"

"Nothing." He pocketed the dead charm and cracked the third.

This one took — barely. A thin warmth spread through his chest, and the fog peeled back maybe ten yards instead of fifty, and the causeway under their boots seemed to firm up from *probably solid* to merely *mostly solid*. But it felt thin. Stretched. Like a coat worn too many winters.

"Brannoc?" Xanthe had stopped. Of course she had. She counted everything twice.

"Luck's running shallow," he admitted. "The fen's drinking faster than we're pouring."

"That's not how luck works."

"It's how luck works in a country that's being unwritten, love." He said it gently. There was no point saying it otherwise. "Everything here is thinner than it should be. Roads. Names. Time, maybe. Why not luck?"

Wren had gone pale in the way she went pale when her archivist mind caught hold of something awful. "The wards are gone. The wards were — oh. Oh no. Were the wards *holding the luck in place*? Like a dam?"

Nobody answered, which was answer enough. Brannoc looked down at the black water sliding beside the causeway and tried very hard not to think about what else might have been held back by all those beautiful, terrible wards.

"Three charms," he said. "Two live ones, one half-dead. We're most of a day across, maybe more if the causeway breaks. We ration from here. No more charms unless someone's drowning."

"Drowning," Odji repeated thoughtfully. "In a fen. What are the odds."

"Don't," said everyone else, together.

* * *

The causeway broke in the middle of the second afternoon, precisely where the map showed it breaking, which was the first time the map had been right about anything since they'd entered the fen, and Brannoc chose to take that as an insult rather than an omen.

A stretch of the old road — twenty feet of it, maybe thirty — had simply slumped into the black water, leaving a gap full of submerged rubble and whatever lived in submerged rubble. On the far side, the causeway climbed again toward a ridge of dry ground where they could see, impossibly, a stand of birches growing in a straight line like soldiers at attention.

"We go around," Odji said.

"Through the open water?" Xanthe shook her head. "Look at it. That's not shallows. That's a channel."

"We swim it."

"You can't swim through a channel in the Sablefen carrying ledgers and a pole and—" Wren began.

"I've poled heavier things than myself across worse water."

"With luck, sure," Brannoc said quietly. "Without it, the fen decides where you float. And the fen's decided it doesn't like us."

Silence. Fog curled between them. Somewhere out in the black, something made a sound like a door opening underwater, which was a sound Brannoc had heard exactly once before and had spent fifteen years trying to forget.

Then Wren stepped forward and unslung the ledgers from her chest.

"No," Xanthe said immediately.

"They're why we're here. If we lose them, everything we learned in the archive is worth nothing, and Corvel wins by default, and—" Wren's voice cracked and steadied. "I'm the lightest. I'll carry them across. I can jump the gap if I start from the rubble. Brannoc, can you ward me a footing?"

Every eye turned to him. He looked at the gap, at the water, at Wren's face — nineteen years old, ink-stained, terrified, absolutely certain — and felt thirty years of craft rise up in his chest like a tide.

"I can give you one step," he said. "One. Out there on the rubble line, there's an old foundation stone — feel for it, flat, knee-high. I'll wake whatever ward-scar is left on it. You land on it, you push off, you make the far side. One step, girl. Don't ask it for two."

"One step," Wren breathed.

"And take this." He dug the fourth charm from the bundle — the last live one — and pressed it into her hand. "Crack it the moment you land. Not before. Promise me."

She promised. They all watched her pick her way along the broken causeway edge to the jumping-off point, a small figure with a large burden, and Brannoc knelt and laid both palms flat against the nearest standing stone and did the oldest work he knew. He didn't create anything. Warding was never creation; it was *reminding* — reminding stone that it had once agreed to hold, reminding water that it had once agreed to stay back, reminding the world of its own promises. He poured everything he had into that reminder, every scrap of craft and stubbornness, and felt an answering shiver run out through the rubble field like a bell struck once beneath a lake.

"Now!" he shouted.

Wren ran. Jumped. For one glorious, suspended moment she was airborne against the grey sky, ledgers clutched to her chest, and Brannoc thought — *she'll make it, she'll—*

She landed short.

Not on the foundation stone. On a lip of rubble at the channel's edge, slick with weed, and her foot shot sideways and the ledgers flew free of her arms and she went down into the black water with hardly a splash, the way the Sablefen takes things — politely, completely.

"WREN!"

Brannoc was moving before the shout finished leaving his throat. Later he would piece it together: Xanthe diving from the other side, fully armored in her sense of duty; Odji plunging his pole into the channel and bellowing something in river-tongue, some old name for the water that sounded like a curse and a prayer braided together. But at the time there was only the cold, only the black, only the desperate logic of a big man who had spent his life keeping doors shut now throwing himself through the widest-open one he'd ever seen.

The Sablefen closed over him like a mouth.

Underwater was worse than he'd imagined. No light, no up, no down — just pressure and cold and the taste of a thousand drowned years. His coat dragged at him. His lungs lit up like forge-coals. And somewhere in the dark ahead, faint as a candle through fog, he saw a flicker of white: paper. Pages. The ledgers, blooming open in the water like a dying flower, ink lifting off them in ghostly threads.

And below the ledgers, sinking, a small flailing shape.

Brannoc kicked. His boots filled with fen. His chest screamed. He caught a fistful of Wren's collar and hauled, and she turned in the dark with wide eyes and grabbed him with both hands — grabbed him the way drowning people grab, total and trusting, and together they rose, and rose, and broke the surface gasping into a world of grey rain and Xanthe's arm locking around his neck.

The fifth charm — the one sewn into her boot heel, the one she pretended wasn't there — was dissolving. He could feel it, a fading warmth against his ankle where her leg tangled with his, luck bleeding out of its last hiding place into four bodies clinging to each other in black water.

"Stone," Odji was shouting. "Left! LEFT, you great ox!"

There was a stone. There was, absurdly, the foundation stone, right where Brannoc's waking hand had put its promise, and Odji's pole hooked Brannoc under the arms and dragged them all toward it, and somehow — he never remembered how — there were four people and two bundles of ledgers and one ferryman's pole crowded onto a rock the size of a dinner table, shaking and streaming and alive.

* * *

They dried out that night on the ridge beneath the soldier-straight birches, around a smokeless fire because smoke attracted things with too many opinions. Every stitch of clothing steamed gently. The ledgers lay open on a flat rock, pages splayed like wounded birds, and Wren blotted them page by page with the hem of her shirt, her jaw tight, refusing help, refusing sympathy, refusing everything except the work.

Brannoc sat wrapped in Odji's spare cloak and took inventory out loud, because someone had to.

"One charm left. Half-burned. Dead wax besides." He turned the little lozenge over in his fingers. "That's the whole treasury. That's everything the Hollow Guild ever stole from the world, and we've got a fingernail of it left."

"Good," Xanthe said.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting with her back against a birch, armor off, hair drying in wild copper waves, watching the fog retreat from the ridge.

"Good?" Wren said, without looking up from her blotting.

"Good. We've been carrying stolen goods this whole way and telling ourselves it was different because we stole them second." Xanthe took the dead lozenge from Brannoc's hand, weighed it once, and flicked it out into the dark. Somewhere in the fen, a fish rose to inspect it and was disappointed. "From here on, whatever gets us across gets us across honestly. Our own legs. Our own luck — the kind you can't press into wax." She glanced around the fire, and Brannoc saw something in her face he'd never seen in the guildhouse: not the ledger-keeper's careful blankness, but an actual, unguarded expression. It looked, he realized, like relief.

Odji stretched until his spine crackled. "Speak for yourself. I've got plenty of luck left."

"You do?"

"Of course. I'm sitting next to it." He nodded around the circle. "All of it."

Wren finally lifted her head from the ledgers. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and shining. "You jumped in," she said to Brannoc. "You can't even swim properly. I saw you. You swim like a wardrobe."

"A wardrobe that saved you."

"A wardrobe that *sank twice* and then saved me." Her voice wobbled dangerously. "You didn't even hesitate. None of you hesitated. In the guild, nobody — when I dropped the Corvel folio in year three, they made me copy the penalty ledger for a month. Nobody dove in after anything. Nothing was worth diving for."

Brannoc reached over and ruffled her damp hair the way he'd once ruffled his apprentices', back when he'd had apprentices, back when the word meant something warmer than *indentured*.

"That's the difference, girl," he said. "The guild kept accounts. We keep each other. Different arithmetic entirely."

Wren laughed — wet, hiccupping, real — and bent back to her pages, and Odji began humming one of his endless river songs, and Xanthe sat watching the dark with her shoulders finally, visibly, down away from her ears.

Brannoc should have slept. Instead he found himself studying the last live charm in the firelight, turning it, feeling its thin fading warmth — and frowning. Because now that he looked, truly looked, he could see it plainly: the shimmer inside wasn't just burning out. It was being *drawn*. Pulled steadily northward, toward the capital, the way a candle flame leans in a draft.

All the luck in the country, flowing one direction. Into one place. Feeding one hunger.

"Xanthe," he said quietly, holding it up so the lean of its inner light was plain. "It points."

She looked at the charm. Then north, past the birches, toward a horizon where — faint as memory, getting fainter by the hour — there should have been stars.

"Then we stop following the road," she said, "and follow the draft."
