## 12. The Archive Beneath the Capital

The capital had a smell now. That was the first thing I noticed as we came down off the ridge at dusk — not rot exactly, and not smoke, but something like the air after lightning stays too long indoors. A storm that never quite finishes deciding.

"Charming place," Brannoc said, pulling his hood lower. "Very festive."

"It's the wards," Wren said, in the tone she used when she was about to be right about something and wanted us to know it in advance. "Or rather, it's what's *missing*. The old capital sat inside nine concentric ward-rings. Each one hummed. You'd feel it in your teeth if you stood still long enough. Now—" she gestured at the whole grey sprawl of rooftops below us, "—nothing. The city's like a bell someone took the clapper out of. All shape, no sound."

"And the sound went where?" Odji asked.

"That," said Wren, "is what we're going to find out."

We were going to find it out in a drowned archive beneath a flooded cistern district, because apparently that was simply my life now.

* * *

The Sablefen had nearly killed us; the capital intended to finish the job politely. The streets near the river had gone soft — not collapsed, but *uncertain*, cobblestones that couldn't remember whether they'd been laid in rows or spirals. We crossed a square where the fountain was dry except for one thin stream that fell upward, hesitated, thought better of it, and came back down. A cat watched this happen with the profound indifference only cats can manage, and I found that oddly comforting. If the world could still produce a cat that unbothered, perhaps it wasn't entirely finished.

"The archive's under the Cistern Quarter," Wren whispered, consulting a map she'd copied from a book we'd fished out of a chapel wall. Literally out of the wall — Brannoc had pried up a loose stone and there it was, which is the sort of thing that happens to Wren and never to me. "It was built under the water table on purpose. Water remembers, they say. Keeps ink honest. Keeps thieves out."

"Keeps *us* out," Odji noted.

"We're not thieves," I said.

"We are absolutely thieves," Odji said.

"We're auditors," I corrected. "There's a difference. Thieves take things. Auditors establish provenance." I said it with more confidence than I felt, because the truth was that for eleven years I had kept the Hollow Guild's ledgers and had never once been permitted to see an original contract. Copies only. Certified copies, sealed with wax I wasn't allowed to question. I had told myself that was protocol. Protocol, hierarchy, the natural order of things.

I had recently learned the word for what that actually was: *compartmentalization*. It's a lovely word. It means they knew you'd want to look.

* * *

Getting into the Cistern Quarter required wading, which required leaving most of our gear behind, which required trusting each other, which we did, mostly. Odji carried Wren on his back through the deeper stretches, narrating the whole way like a river-guide with a death wish: "Mind the left, mind the left, that archway used to be a door to somewhere in Vellosa, don't ask me how I know—"

"You *do* know," Wren said, from his shoulders. "You always do this. You drop a fact and then pretend you didn't."

"I'm a ferryman. Facts come and go with the tide."

"That's not how facts work."

"On a river it is."

Brannoc brought up the rear with his ward-smith's kit strapped high and dry across his chest, muttering measurements. Every few minutes he'd stop, press two fingers to a wall, and frown. "These walls have been cut," he said the third time. "Not broken. Cut. Somebody siphoned the warding out of the stone itself, clean as tapping a keg."

"Corvel?" I asked.

"Corvel-shaped," he allowed. He was careful with that name lately. Careful with all our names, actually — he'd started saying them quietly, like he was afraid they might be overheard by the dark. Given everything, I couldn't blame him.

The archive's entrance was where Wren's map promised: a stair descending from the flooded undercroft of a customs house, its top steps green with weed, its bottom steps vanishing into black water that smelled of cold iron and old paper. Someone had left lanterns hanging on hooks along the descent — guild lanterns, hollow-eyed, their oil long since burned or stolen. But the water level was wrong. Lower than the map said it should be. Lower than physics said it should be.

"The city's drinking its own history," Wren murmured. "The wards Corvel built need matter to anchor on. He's pulling the groundwater into the spellwork."

"So we walk down into a drained archive," Brannoc said, "that is being actively eaten by a man becoming a god."

"Yes," I said.

"Right. Just checking we all had the same meeting."

* * *

Inside, the drowned archive turned out to be drowned only in memory. The shelves rose out of shallow standing water, ten feet tall, made of blackwood that hadn't rotted because blackwood refuses to. Everything smelled of ink and mineral. Our footsteps echoed wrong — too many echoes, as though the room were keeping spares.

Wren lit her little lamp and immediately began vibrating with scholarly joy, which I will admit lifted my heart despite everything. "Xanthe. *Look.* These aren't copies. These are originals. Do you understand what—"

"I understand," I said quietly. "I've spent eleven years certifying copies of these."

She stopped vibrating. She looked at me the way you look at someone when a joke turns out not to be one. "You never saw them?"

"I saw the reading room. I saw the certified facsimiles. I assumed—" I touched the spine of the nearest ledger, and my hand shook slightly, and I let it. "I assumed trust was the point of service."

Odji put a hand briefly on my shoulder, said nothing, and drifted toward the far stacks to keep watch. He's good at that — knowing when silence is a gift rather than a wound. It's why I'd follow him anywhere, including here.

We split the search three ways. Brannoc took the contract vaults by date; Wren took anything referencing the border wards; I took the oldest section, because if you're looking for the beginning of a lie, you go to where the ink is thickest.

I found it an hour later, in a case that should have been locked and wasn't, because locks are a courtesy the powerful extend to each other.

The original oath-contracts. Mine among them — I recognized my own signature, seventeen years old and desperate, the letters cramped around a thumbprint pressed in blood. Beside it, contracts older than the guildhouse, older than the country's current borders. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a folio labeled in Magister Corvel's precise, patient hand:

**VESSEIA — ACQUISITION AND DIVISION OF ASSETS.**

* * *

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times I kept flinching away from the middle paragraphs like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

It was an inventory. That was the horror of it — the banality. It read like any ledger I'd ever balanced. *Item: the god Vessia, bound in the Hollow at the founding of the Guild, per Contract Zero. Item: her voice, divided nine ways among the undersigned. Item: her sight. Item: her mercy. Item: her silence.*

Her silence. Listed. As an asset. With a delivery date seven years past.

I sat down in the shallow water without meaning to. My knees just quit.

"Xanthe?" Wren was beside me instantly. "What is it? What did you—"

"She wasn't silent," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like someone else doing an impression of me, badly. "Seven years ago, when she stopped answering — I thought I'd failed. I thought I'd done something wrong, some breach of the oath, and she'd turned her face away. I prayed harder. I gave more. I *bled* for her, Wren, I cut the offering-marks myself every season, thinking if I was faithful enough she'd speak again." I held the folio out with a hand that would not stop shaking. "She didn't go silent. They *took her voice out of her* and split it between nine people, and Corvel has been sitting on his ninth ever since, letting the rest of us starve on the memory of her."

The lamp flame bent in air that had no draft. Somewhere deep in the stacks, water dripped in a rhythm almost like breathing.

Wren read. I watched her face do what mine must have done — the narrowing, the widening, the moment where comprehension arrives and brings its friend nausea. "Contract Zero," she whispered. "The guild wasn't built to trade in impossible things. The guild was built to *hold one*. Vessia herself. They caught a god at the founding and they've been selling pieces of her for two hundred years." She looked up. "Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Doors to nowhere. Xanthe, those weren't products. Those were *her*. They've been vending a dismembered goddess by the jarful and calling it commerce."

And there it was — terrible clarity, arriving exactly as advertised. Every entry I'd ever recorded rearranged itself in my head. The jar of someone's childhood I'd logged on my first night: that was Vessia's gift for memory, decanted. The week of good weather sold twice: her providence, scraped thin and thinned further. The luck Odji had hoarded, the luck that burned out in the Sablefen: her blessing, metered out until the well ran dry. Eleven years of careful bookkeeping, and every line item had been a piece of a prisoner.

"I certified copies," I said. "Of her ransom notes."

Brannoc appeared at the end of the aisle, moving fast and low, and I knew before he spoke that we were out of time for grief. "Company," he breathed. "Above us. Lantern-light through the grates. At least six, moving in formation. Guild drill." His eyes went to the folio in my hands. "Did you find it?"

"I found her," I said, and stood up, and the water streamed off my skirts, and something in my chest that had been grieving for seven years quietly changed its mind about what it was grieving for. Not abandonment. Theft. You can survive being abandoned. Theft wants answering.

"Wren — can you carry it? Copy what matters if we have to run?"

"Give me a minute and a knife and I'll give you the whole thing from memory."

"No time for a minute," Odji called softly from the stacks' edge. "They're coming down the main stair. And Xanthe—" he paused, and even in the lamplight I could see him smiling, that crooked river-ferryman smile that meant either salvation or disaster, usually both. "There's another way out. There's always another way out. But it goes through the Hollow proper."

"The Hollow," Brannoc said flatly. "The actual Hollow. The place the guild is named for. The door out of the world."

"The same."

"How do you know there's a way through?"

Odji tapped the side of his nose. "Because the river remembers, and I listen. Come on. Quickly and quietly, and nobody touches anything that whispers."

We moved. Behind us the lanterns of the enforcers bloomed along the upper gallery like a row of cold yellow eyes, and I tucked the folio against my heart — Vessia's voice, her sight, her mercy, itemized and awaiting return — and followed my strange, borrowed family down into the dark, toward the last door anyone had ever bothered to lock from the outside.

Somewhere above us, faint through the stone, a voice that was almost Corvel's said, almost kindly: *"She'll come to the Hollow. She always balances her accounts."*

He wasn't wrong.

That was the problem. For once in my life, I intended to be.
