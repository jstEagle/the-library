## 13. Corvel Ascendant

Magister Corvel had always believed that divinity would feel like falling. Instead it felt like standing very still while the world rearranged itself around him, the way furniture does in a dream — familiar shapes sliding to new places without any hand touching them.

He sat in the Guildmaster's chair at the top of the Hollow Guildhouse, which had been his chair for eleven years and was now, technically, a throne. He had not moved it. Thrones that move themselves are showing off, and Corvel had built a career on never showing off until the exact moment showing off became useful.

The capital lay below him in half-darkness. Not night — night had been abolished, along with stars — but a deep amber gloom that pooled in the streets like spilled honey. His gloom. He had designed the light himself: warm enough to comfort, dim enough that no one could see the edges of things. People were kinder at the edges of their sight. He had learned that from ledgers.

Ah, the ledgers. His fingers itched for them still, after everything.

"Magister." Pellin stood at the foot of the dais, holding a tray with two cups of tea, because even apotheosis required tea, and Pellin had been bringing him tea for twenty years and saw no reason the end of the world should interrupt a good habit. "The third district has settled."

"Settled," Corvel repeated, tasting the euphemism.

"Their memories have been... adjusted. The weavers remember the guild as their patron family going back nine generations. The fishwives remember you personally blessing their boats." Pellin set down the tray. "One old woman remembers teaching you your letters."

"I never learned letters from anyone. I taught myself out of a ledger I stole from a dead man."

"Yes, Magister. She remembers it anyway. Very fondly. She cries when she tells her neighbors."

Corvel took his tea. It was good tea — real leaf, not the acorn-and-chicory the city drank now — and he let himself enjoy it for precisely four seconds, which was all he ever allowed himself of anything enjoyable before turning it into fuel.

"And how does it sit?" he asked. "In them. The new memory."

Pellin hesitated. This was why Corvel kept him. Everyone else in the inner circle had begun lying to him the moment his eyes started glowing faintly in candlelight, on the theory that gods prefer flattery. Pellin alone understood that a god who is lied to is just a man with better lighting.

"It sits like borrowed shoes," Pellin said. "They walk in them. They don't stumble. But they keep... flexing their toes."

Corvel nodded slowly. That was the flaw, then. Not fatal — nothing about this was fatal, the mathematics had been checked three times by three people who hated each other enough to check honestly — but real. Worship written into memory held only as firmly as the memory did, and human memory was a leaky jar. They would need topping-up. Rituals. Festivals. Little ceremonies where the faithful retold the story of their own devotion until the seams vanished.

He had planned for that too. He planned for everything. It was, he sometimes thought, the great tragedy of his life: he had planned so thoroughly for becoming a god that he might have forgotten to become one.

No. Not forgotten. Postponed. There was a difference, and the difference was Xanthe Thackeray.

* * *

She was coming. Of course she was coming.

Corvel had watched her since the night the stars went out — not with scrying, which was vulgar and left residue, but with arithmetic. Every ledger she'd ever kept told him exactly what kind of creature she was. A woman who balanced accounts down to the last copper. Who noticed when a column was short by a single hour of borrowed luck. Who had spent years inside the guild's machinery believing herself a cog, when in truth she was the only person who had ever read the whole machine.

She had found the false entries by now. He'd made sure they were findable — signed in her hand, dated in her neat, merciless script. Some of the others had called that cruelty. Corvel had called it curriculum. You cannot teach a woman she's been lied to unless you show her the lie in language she trusts. Her own handwriting had been the only honest voice she'd ever heard.

And now she would come up through the drowned archive, past the original contracts, past the truth about Vessia, and she would arrive at his door furious and grieving and more dangerous than she had ever been in her life.

Good. He preferred people at their most dangerous. Comfortable people lied to themselves; desperate ones told the truth whether they meant to or not.

"Pellin. When she arrives — and she will arrive within six days, mark me — I want no enforcers between the archive stair and my study. Clear the corridor entirely."

Pellin's cup paused halfway to his mouth. "You want her to walk in unopposed?"

"I want her to walk in *unafraid*. Fear makes people stupid, and I intend to have an intelligent conversation with her." He turned his cup so the handle faced right, then faced left, then faced right again. A habit. Gods were allowed habits. "Also, send down to the kitchens. If she comes with those debtors of hers — the ferryman, the apprentice, the ward-smith — there will be food waiting. Good bread. The apprentice looks underfed."

"You're feeding the people hunting you?"

"They're not hunting me. They're hunting an idea of me, and the idea is wrong, and bread is the fastest way I know to correct a first impression." Corvel smiled, thin and genuine, which unsettled Pellin more than the glowing eyes ever had. "Besides. I once fed a starving girl in a border garrison. It cost me half a ration and bought me twenty years of the best bookkeeping this guild has ever seen. I know the return on bread."

* * *

That had been the first offer. He thought about it now, in the amber dark, the way he thought about all his investments.

She had been perhaps fourteen. A camp follower of a mercenary company that had dissolved around her like sugar in rain, leaving her alone in a garrison town eating candle wax to fool her stomach. Corvel had been there auditing a garrison's debts — the guild collected everywhere; war was simply another market — and he had found her in the quartermaster's storeroom, reading. Not stealing. Reading. An inventory list, upside down, sounding the words out under her breath.

Most men would have chased her off or worse. Corvel had sat down across from her, produced half a loaf and a pot of honey from his satchel, and said: *"Numbers first. Then honey."*

She had done the numbers. All of them. Faster than the garrison's own clerk, with corrections in the margins that were, infuriatingly, correct.

*"Who taught you?"*

*"Nobody. Numbers don't lie, so nobody needs to teach you how to hear them."*

And Corvel, who had built his life on the principle that numbers don't lie and therefore neither should he, had felt something turn over in his chest — the closest thing to love he would ever permit himself.

*"Come with me,"* he'd said. *"I can give you work no one else can. Ledgers of impossible things. Bottled childhoods. Luck by the week. Doors that open nowhere. And in exchange—"*

*"In exchange what?"* She hadn't been frightened. That was the remarkable thing. Hungry, wary, fourteen, and not frightened.

*"In exchange, you'll never be hungry again, never be owned again, and never be lied to again."*

It was the last promise that had been true for the longest time, and Corvel had known even then, watching her lick honey off her thumb with the gravity of a scholar, that someday he would break it — because every path he could see ahead required it, and Corvel did not deviate from paths he could see.

He had offered her mentorship that day. He was about to offer her something larger. And the terrible thing — the thing he admitted to himself at four seconds' allowance per day, no more — was that part of him hoped she would refuse, the way part of a father hopes the child won't take the family trade.

The rest of him knew better. The rest of him had run the numbers.

* * *

On the fourth day, the city began to sing.

Not literally — though some nights, when his power pooled thickest, he could hear the rewritten prayers humming up through the flagstones like heat through a stove. No: on the fourth day the city began singing in the ordinary human way, at festivals he had invented eight days ago and which everyone now remembered celebrating since childhood. The Festival of the Veiled Sky. Bonfires in the squares, garlands on the doors, children dressed in amber cloth to honor the Kind Dark that kept them safe from the cold indifferent stars.

Corvel stood at a window above the great square and watched ten thousand people celebrate their own shackles with total joy, and he felt — he searched himself carefully — he felt satisfaction. Clean, earned, properly audited satisfaction.

The old woman who remembered teaching him his letters danced near the biggest bonfire, arm in arm with the fishwives whose boats he had blessed. She laughed at something. The laugh carried.

"There," said Pellin beside him quietly. "Is that so bad? Look at them. Fed, safe, together. The wards used to eat a village every generation when they failed. Now nothing eats anyone. Is a lie that keeps children alive worse than a truth that starves them?"

"That," said Corvel, "is the finest argument for tyranny ever composed, and I wrote it, and I am still going to answer it honestly: yes. Because it doesn't hold. Borrowed shoes, Pellin. They flex their toes. In fifty years the memories fray, the rituals hollow out, and some clever child finds a seam and pulls. Truth is the only structure that survives its architect." He paused. "Which is why the truth must be that there is no truth worth surviving. I've accounted for it."

"You account for everything."

"Someone has to. It's the whole reason for gods, historically. The universe keeps terrible books." He turned from the window. "Has there been word from the bridge companies? Ivetta's pursuit?"

"She reports the Thackeray woman crossed the Sablefen. Lost three enforcers and all their hoarded luck doing it. Ivetta writes—" Pellin consulted a slip of paper, and his mouth twitched, "—that the marsh 'ate her luck like a fat man eats pies,' and that Brannoc nearly drowned pulling the apprentice out, and that the group now travels with, quote, 'no advantages whatsoever except each other.'"

Corvel was quiet a moment. Something in his chest performed an operation he declined to examine.

"No advantages except each other," he repeated. "Yes. That's her ledger-hand all over. She always did balance emotional columns the same way she balanced copper ones — she'd rather carry less if it meant carrying it honestly." He shook his head. "Ivetta will fail, you know. Not for lack of skill. For lack of doubt. Doubt is the only weapon that works on Xanthe, and Ivetta has none. Which means the next time they meet, it will be at the door itself."

"What door?"

"The only door left, Pellin." He smiled. "Fetch me the third-ring maps. The ones of the mountain passes. And bring the good honey — I find I'm nostalgic."

* * *

She came on the sixth day, one day ahead of his projection, which annoyed him professionally and pleased him personally, and he noted both feelings in the mental ledger where he stored such things.

She came alone.

That was the surprise. He had budgeted for four — the ferryman, the apprentice, the ward-smith, and possibly Ivetta trailing like a bad debt — and instead the corridor guards reported a single figure walking up through the amber dark with a ledger bag over her shoulder and her sword left behind, deliberately, where the guards could see her leave it.

A message. *I come to talk, not to fight.* Or a trap of her own. With Xanthe it was usually both; she'd learned accounting from him, after all, and accounting was just the art of making one number serve two purposes.

"Show her in," Corvel said. "Tea for two. The good leaf. And Pellin — wait outside. Whatever you hear, whatever happens, you do not come through that door unless I call your name twice."

Pellin looked at him for a long moment with twenty years of loyalty and one year of growing dread. "Magister. If she's come to kill you—"

"Then she'll have earned the conversation first, and I'll have deserved it second, and either way your tea service won't change the outcome." He softened, slightly, the way he did maybe twice a decade. "Go on. Wait outside. That's an order and a kindness, and I've rarely issued both at once."

The door opened.

Xanthe Thackeray looked — he catalogued it instantly, the auditor in him unable to stop — thinner. Marsh-tired. A new scar along her jaw she hadn't earned in his service. Her eyes went around the study once: the throne-that-was-a-chair, the ledgers stacked in their familiar order, the window over the singing square. Taking inventory. Of course she was. She was checking whether he had changed the room, because if he had changed the room, he had changed, and if he hadn't, some part of the man she'd sworn to might still be recoverable.

He had changed nothing. He let her see that he had changed nothing.

"You left your sword downstairs," he said.

"You left your god upstairs," she said. "We're both traveling light."

There she was. Twenty years compressed into one exchange. He gestured to the chair across the desk — not the dais; he had come down to the desk for her, another message — and poured the tea himself, because servants at moments like this were theater, and theater was for audiences, and there was no audience here. Only the two of them and the oldest account between them.

"Before anything else," Corvel said, "I owe you a confession, because you were promised you'd never be lied to, and I broke that promise, and broken contracts accrue interest. Ask me anything. Tonight, in this room, I will not lie to you."

Her hands were steady around the cup. Her voice was not quite. "Vessia. Did you devour her?"

"Yes." He said it plainly, the way she'd taught him numbers should be said. "Eighteen years ago. We split her — myself and the inner circle, seven shares. Mine was the largest, because I did the work. She didn't scream, Xanthe. That's what no one tells you about gods. They go quietly, like old clerks closing a book at the end of a long day. She was *tired*. Tired of keeping doors shut that everyone wanted open. We took the burden. That's how I choose to enter it in the record, and I know how it sounds, and I'm entering it anyway."

"And the stars. The wards. The country unwriting itself at the edges — Odji showed me a village that had forgotten its own name."

"All necessary entries." He leaned forward. "Now. Here is what I brought you here to say, and I'll say it fast, because you're at your best when you're arguing and I want you at your best."

He laid it out the way he'd laid out the honey-bread offer twenty years ago: terms, plainly stated, no ornament. A place at his right hand. Not servant — partner. Co-architect. When the sealing completed, when the country closed like a fist around its own small light, there would be two minds needed to keep it running forever: one to be worshipped, and one to keep the books honest. Worship rots the worshipper — he'd seen it already in the others, Sarelle weeping over her own statue, Doss giggling at nothing — but bookkeeping stayed clean. Bookkeeping was the priesthood that actually worked.

"I need someone," he said, "who will tell me when my columns don't add up. Forever is a long time to be wrong in. Everyone I might ask has already started lying to me — did you know that? Sarelle called my ascension 'flawless' last week. Flawless. There is no such thing as a flawless entry. She's worshipping me ahead of schedule and it's making her stupid, and soon it will make *me* stupid, and I will rule a country of liars as a liar among liars, and the whole edifice will be rotten in a century." He spread his hands. "Unless. Unless there is one person in the world who remembers what my handwriting looked like before, and isn't afraid to tell me it's slipped."

The amber light moved. Somewhere below, the festival sang.

"And what," Xanthe said quietly, "did you offer her? My mentor. Before me."

Corvel went still.

"Because I found her notes," Xanthe continued. "In the archive, under the contracts. She refused you too, didn't she. And there's an entry in your hand — dated the week after — a purchase. One soul, guild coin, entered as *acquisition of a liability*." Her voice cracked, finally, on the last word, and he watched her hate it cracking. "You killed her for saying no. So tell me honestly, since tonight you're honest: what happens to me when I say it?"

And here was the four-second allowance, spent all at once, the whole day's portion in a single draught: Corvel looked at the only person he had ever loved in the way one loves a well-kept book, and told the truth.

"Nothing," he said. "I swear it on the last honest column I have. Refuse, and you walk out that door untouched, and Ivetta stands down, and you and your ferryman and your apprentice and your ward-smith are free to reach the Last Hollow and try to open it. I won't stop you. I can't afford to lie about it, Xanthe — you're the only auditor left whose verdict I'd trust. If I cheated you, you'd smell it, and then the one clean number in my life would be dirty too."

"Then you've already lost," she said. "Because if you let us reach the door, we'll open it. The stars come back. Your kingdom ends. You must know that."

"I do know it." He refilled her tea, unhurried. "I've run it every way. Sixty percent likelihood you succeed, given the toll the door demands — and you don't yet know what the toll is, and I confess I envy you the next five minutes of finding out. But understand what I'm offering, girl. I am offering you sixty-forty odds against eternity, versus a seat beside me at certainty. Most people in history would call that not a choice at all."

"Most people in history," Xanthe said, rising, putting her cup down with a small precise click that he would hear for the rest of his immortal life, "never kept honest books."

She walked to the door. Stopped. Did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the honey was good. And the margins were right. Both of those were true, and I'm keeping both, and neither one buys you my name on your contract."

The door closed behind her. Corvel sat alone in the amber dark for a long time, then rang for Pellin, and when Pellin came he said, in a voice almost steady:

"Double the watch on the mountain road. And Pellin — she'll pay the toll. Whatever it costs her, she'll pay it, because she never in her life left a debt unpaid, even one she owed to herself. We have perhaps three days. Wake Sarelle. Wake them all." He rose and went to the window, where the bonfires of the Veiled Sky burned against a sky with nothing in it, and felt, for the first time in twenty years, a number he could not balance.

"Godhood," he said softly, to the glass, to the empty dark, to the absent stars, "is going to require overtime."

Far to the north, beneath a mountain that remembered being a door, something ancient heard the renunciation begin — and started, at long last, to open.
