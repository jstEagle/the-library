## 14. The Price of the Door

Wren had always believed that the worst thing an archive could do to you was bore you. She'd spent three years in the Hollow Guild's records room under a magister who could make the history of currency feel like watching paint dry in slow motion, and she had come out of it with a firm conviction: paper was safe. Paper was patient. Paper never asked anything of you.

The drowned archive beneath the old capital had thoroughly disabused her of this notion.

It sat below the waterline of a city that no longer quite agreed it existed, in a vault that breathed—she was sure of it, the walls swelled and sighed like something sleeping—and every shelf held contracts that hummed faintly when you touched them, as if they resented being read. She had found the original guild charter. She had found the deed to Vessia's silence, signed by nine hands she now knew belonged to the inner circle. And now, on the lowest tier, in a niche behind a false wall of ledger-spines, she had found a door.

Not a metaphorical door. An actual one. Small, iron-banded, older than any script she knew, standing alone in its alcove with nothing built around it—as if the archive had grown up ashamed around it and pretended it wasn't there.

"It's warm," she said, because her voice seemed necessary to prove she still had a body.

Brannoc crouched beside her, ward-smith's eyes narrowing at the iron. "That's not heat. That's attention." He held his palm near the bands without touching. "This thing is looking at us."

"Everything down here looks at us," Odji said from the top of the stairs, where he had stationed himself with the practiced ease of a man who had decided exactly how much spelunking he was willing to do, and it was none. "In my experience, when everything is looking at you, you leave."

"You live on a river," Brannoc said.

"The river doesn't look at me. The river has better manners."

Wren barely heard them. She had lifted the ledger resting on a stone plinth before the door—a slim volume, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin—and opened it to the first page.

*Terms of the Last Hollow,* it read. *Conditions of passage. Recorded for whoever is fool enough or desperate enough to need this.*

She read it once in silence. Then again, aloud, because some things needed air to become real.

"'The Last Hollow opens for one soul only, and only once. It opens not with keys nor blood nor payment in gold, which it does not want.'" Her voice wobbled on the next line. "'It opens for surrender. Whoever would pass must give up every name the Guild ever gave them. Every name. Written name, sworn name, taken name, true name if the Guild ever learned it. The door will know what is withheld.'"

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the unwritten capital groaned through another small erasure—a street forgetting itself, a bell tower deciding it had never been built.

Then Xanthe's voice came from behind them, level as a ledger line. "Read me the rest."

Wren hadn't heard her come down the stairs. She turned, and there was Xanthe in the doorway, lantern light catching the ink stains that never fully left her fingers, her face doing the careful blank thing Wren had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"There's more," Wren said. "It lists what surrender means. Not death. The door doesn't kill. But—" She looked down at the page rather than at Xanthe. "Whoever passes gives up the names. All of them. Including the name everyone knows them by. Including 'Xanthe Thackeray.' They walk out of the Hollow as someone who hasn't been named yet. Someone with no debts, no oaths, no guild-record, no history the Guild can claim." A breath. "Someone nobody's ledgers have ever touched."

"Sounds lovely," Odji said, without any conviction at all.

"And the door only opens once," Brannoc added quietly. "For one person. It says so twice. Doors that lie about their terms don't survive their own contracts—I taught wards long enough to know the honest ones are redundant."

Xanthe crossed the chamber and took the ledger from Wren's hands. Wren watched her read, watched her thumb move along the margin the way it did when she was auditing, hunting for the trick in the terms. It was a habit so familiar it hurt. Three weeks ago Wren had been a debtor with a forged apprenticeship and a talent for getting lost in archives; then the stars went out, and Xanthe Thackeray had torn up her debt with her own hands, folded the page into a little boat, and set it burning on the guildhouse fountain "so the paperwork knows it's dead."

Now the same woman stood reading the price of the only way out of an unmaking country, and her face said she was already paying it.

"No," Wren said.

Xanthe didn't look up. "I haven't said anything."

"You've said it with your whole spine. I've known you six weeks; I can read your spine." Wren snatched the ledger back, which was probably insubordination, possibly theft, definitely the bravest thing she'd done since forging her own discharge papers. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," Xanthe said, gently, the way you tell someone a patient has died. "You heard the charter readings. Corvel finishes the sealing at the dark of the moon. That's four nights. After that, there's no door worth opening, because there'll be no outside left to open onto. One of us goes through and opens it from the other side and lets the stars back in. We established this on the road. Everyone voted. Even Odji voted, and he voted by saying 'fine,' which from him is basically a speech."

"I voted 'fine' for *someone else* to do it," Odji called down the stairs.

"Well, someone else is volunteering," Xanthe said. "Problem solved."

"That is not how voting works!" Wren heard her own voice crack and hated it. She was twenty-three years old and she had survived the guildhouse, the bridge that existed in two places, the Sablefen, and the specific horror of watching Brannoc go under black water, and apparently the thing that would break her was bookkeeping. But that was the point, wasn't it? That was always the point. Xanthe had spent years making herself into an entry in someone else's ledger, and here was a door offering to erase the entry entirely, and Xanthe—the woman who kept accounts balanced down to the last copper, who felt physical pain at an unrecorded transaction—was going to sign herself away and call it fair trade.

"Give me the book," Xanthe said.

"No."

"Wren."

"You're not even listening to yourself. Do you hear what you're asking? You'd walk out of that door and there wouldn't be a Xanthe Thackeray anymore. There'd be—" Wren groped for it and found something worse than any word: "There'd be a stranger. And we'd be standing on the other side knowing a stranger. Everything you are—the oath-keeping, the terrible handwriting, the way you tear up debts and pretend you're not crying—" Her throat closed. "The door doesn't get to have that. The Guild took enough of you already. I won't hand it the rest."

For a moment Xanthe just looked at her. Then she did the worst thing: she smiled, small and crooked, and reached out and straightened the collar of Wren's coat, the gesture of an older sister, a keeper, a person arranging the world so it hurt less.

"When I tore up your debt," she said, "did you ask whether I could afford it?"

"This is different."

"It's exactly the same. It's the only kind of arithmetic I've ever been good at." She turned to the others. "Brannoc?"

Brannoc had picked up a loose iron band that had fallen off the door sometime in the last century and was turning it over in scarred hands. He took his time answering. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flat care of a man handling something volatile.

"I sold wards to the inner circle for eleven years. I told myself I was smithing protection, not building a cage. Every day of those eleven years, I paid a little of myself into their project and called it craft." He looked up. "So believe me when I say: I know what it costs to be useful to people who are unwriting the world. And I know that the ones who pay easiest are always the ones who've convinced themselves they're the cheapest thing in the room." He set the band down. "You think you're an entry in their books, Xanthe. You're not. You stopped being theirs the night you tore up those debts. The door can't take what they don't own."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face—fast, quickly mastered—but Wren saw it, and she grabbed hold of it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman. "He's right. Listen to him. You keep saying the door takes your names, but whose names are they? 'Thackeray'—that's the surname they assigned you when they pulled you out of the child barracks. Your oath-name, whatever it is—that belongs to Vessia's chain of command, and Vessia got eaten. Every single thing the door wants from you is stolen goods. You'd be surrendering loot back to the thieves."

"And keeping what?" Xanthe asked softly. "What do I have that they didn't give me?"

And there it was. Wren felt Odji come down two steps without appearing to decide to, drawn despite himself. Because they all knew the answer, and the answer was standing in this cold breathing room holding a lantern: three debtors and a defector who had, in six chaotic weeks, become the only inventory Xanthe Thackeray counted as hers.

"Us," Wren said. "You have us. And here's the part you haven't audited, because you're constitutionally incapable of counting yourself as an asset: if you walk through that door as a stranger, then the person we know—the person who fished me out of the Sablefen, who carried Brannoc half a mile with a dislocated shoulder, who sits up on watch even when it's not her turn because she doesn't trust anyone else's vigilance—*that person* walks through too, wearing a stranger's face, and doesn't remember why we matter to her. The door doesn't take your names. It takes *ours*. It takes the fact that we love you. You'd be paying with our memories as much as yours."

"That's not in the terms," Xanthe said, but her voice had changed.

"It doesn't have to be in the terms. It's in the consequences. You taught me the difference yourself, first week, when I tried to write off a bad loan as a loss. 'Consequences aren't in the contract, Wren, but they're always in the bill.'"

Odji laughed—one short, surprised bark—and then covered it, because laughing felt illegal in this room. "The apprentice has learned to quote the master back at her. This is how mutinies start."

"Good," Wren said. "Mutiny. I'm calling a formal one. Motion on the floor: Xanthe Thackeray is forbidden from sacrificing herself until the group has exhausted all alternatives, defined as: every alternative, including stupid ones, including ones involving Odji's ferry, including—" she flipped desperately forward in the ledger— "wait."

"What?" Brannoc said.

"Wait, wait." Her finger stopped on a passage in the final pages, cramped marginalia in a hand different from the rest—older, hurried, pressed hard enough to tear the paper. "There's an addendum. Someone wrote against the terms. 'Let the record show the undersigned objects. The Hollow was never meant to take. It was meant to *give*. The surrender-clause was added later, by men who feared an exit they couldn't tax. The door honors what it is given freely—but it cannot distinguish between a name surrendered and a name *returned*. If the passer comes to it having already given up her names—if she arrives with empty hands and a full heart—the toll may find nothing to collect.'"

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

"May," Brannoc repeated. "*May* find nothing to collect."

"It's marginalia," Xanthe said. "Unsigned. Unverified. You'd gamble on an annotation?"

"I'd gamble on anything except losing you to a technicality." Wren thrust the book at her. "Look at the hand. Look at it properly. You're the auditor—you know hands the way I know scripts."

Xanthe took the ledger. Held it close to the lantern. And Wren watched the color leave her face one degree at a time, because whatever she saw in that looping, hurried script, she recognized it.

"That's—" Odji started.

"My hand," Xanthe said. Barely a sound. "Older. From before. From *before* the silence." She sat down abruptly on the plinth step, ledger open in her lap, staring at her own future handwriting. "Vessia wrote this. Or someone Vessia wrote through. It's the same hand I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Every entry I've ever made, every ledger line, I learned a hand that came from somewhere. I thought the guild taught me. I thought—" A laugh escaped her, cracked and wondering. "They didn't teach me to write. Something wrote *through* me for years and let me believe it was training."

"So the god's been leaving you notes," Odji said, "in your own homework."

"That is either deeply comforting or profoundly horrifying," Brannoc offered.

"Yes," Xanthe said.

Wren knelt in front of her. Close up, she could see Xanthe's hands were shaking, just slightly, the fine tremor of a woman whose foundations had shifted again—and yet she wasn't blank anymore. She was *lit*, somewhere underneath, like coals under ash.

"Here's what I think," Wren said carefully. "I think the door asks for names the Guild gave you. Fine. So we spend four days taking them back first. Every one. We return them ourselves—return them where they came from, to the people who actually own them. You give 'Thackeray' back to nobody, because nobody owns it. You give the oath-name back to a dead god's memory, witnessed by all of us. You stand at that door already empty-handed—not erased, but *freed*. And then the toll reaches for its payment and finds you already paid yourself out, honestly, with witnesses, and there's nothing left to steal." She swallowed. "You walk through as no one's property. And maybe—maybe—you walk through as *you*."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. "That's not how contracts work."

"It's exactly how *honest* contracts work," Brannoc said. "A toll collected twice is fraud. Even gods answer to that."

"Even eaten gods," Odji murmured, and this time nobody laughed, but nobody looked afraid either.

Xanthe closed the ledger. Pressed both palms flat against its cover, the way she did with account books when she'd finished a column and wanted the numbers to settle. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and Wren realized with a lurch that steady, on Xanthe, was the most frightening register of all.

"All right. Here are my terms, and you'll witness them, because apparently that's how we do business now." She counted on ink-stained fingers. "One: we don't decide tonight. Two: if I go through that door and come out a stranger, you three introduce yourselves slowly and do *not* let me wander off, because a person with no names and no friends in an unwriting country is a tragedy waiting for a footnote. Three—" and here her composure slipped, just a hairline fracture, just enough for Wren to see the frightened girl from the child barracks underneath the mercenary, the one who had sworn herself to a silent god because hunger leaves few options— "Three: if it works, if I'm still me on the other side... somebody remind me who I am. In case I forget to check."

Wren took her hand. Odji put his river-charm on the ledger cover without a word, a carved fish worn smooth by a decade of a thumb worrying it. Brannoc, after a moment, added a bent iron nail from his pocket—the first ward he'd ever forged, he'd once said, back when he still believed in doors.

"We keep excellent records," Wren said. "All of us together. Between the four of us, we could rebuild your whole ledger from memory. Every debt you tore up. Every kindness you pretended was procedure." She squeezed. "You are extremely well documented, Xanthe Thackeray. Whatever the door thinks it's collecting, we hold copies."

Above them, far away, the capital shuddered through another erasure, and dust sifted down through the breathing dark like snow falling upward. Four nights until the dark of the moon. Somewhere overhead, Corvel was learning to wear divinity like an ill-fitting coat.

And in the lamplight, Xanthe looked at the little pile of offerings on the closed ledger—the fish, the nail, Wren's hand around hers—and said, very quietly:

"Then let's go take my name back."

But as they climbed toward the surface, toward a country busy forgetting itself, Wren glanced back once at the small iron-banded door, and saw—just for a heartbeat, just long enough to doubt every clever theory she'd built—warm light leaking through the seam beneath it.

As if something inside had already begun counting.
