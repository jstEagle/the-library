## 15. What We Keep

Odji had ferried a lot of strange cargo down the Vell in his time—grieving widows, smuggled peacocks, one very apologetic ghost—but he had never carried anything as fragile as what they carried now: a plan that only worked if nobody talked about it too loudly.

So when they found the old lockkeeper's cottage tucked into the bend where the river forgot which way it was going, he called it early. "We stop here," he said. "One night. Nobody argues with a man who owns a boat."

"You don't own this boat," Wren said.

"I own it spiritually," said Odji. "It tells me things."

The cottage was stone and moss and a roof that had opinions about weather but mostly kept them to itself. The lock itself had been dead for thirty years; the gates stood open like a jaw that had given up on biting anything. Inside, there was a hearth, two stools, a table scarred by generations of knives and elbows, and a smell of old river reeds that Odji found so deeply comforting it nearly made him sit down and weep, and he wasn't even sure he was joking about that.

Brannoc went in first, because Brannoc always went first, shoulders squared against a world that had already taken everything from him except his stubbornness and his hands. He checked every corner for ambushers with the thoroughness of a man who expected the walls themselves to be lying to him.

"No wards," he reported. "No traps. No guild-marks." He paused. "There's a mouse."

"Is it hostile?" Wren asked gravely.

"It looked at me funny."

"Then we post watch."

Xanthe laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if she'd forgotten her mouth could do that—and Odji felt something in his chest unknot a little. That was the whole point of tonight, though he hadn't said so out loud. Tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever the road stopped lying to them long enough to let them arrive, they'd be standing in front of the Last Hollow, and Xanthe would have to say goodbye to her own name. Tonight they could afford one evening of pretending that people got to keep things.

He built the fire while Wren raided their packs for supper. Their supplies had been reduced, somewhere in the Sablefen, to hard bread, dried fish, a jar of pickled onions of dubious provenance, and half a wheel of cheese that Brannoc guarded with the intensity other men reserved for firstborn children.

"Cheese is mine," Brannoc announced, sitting on the pack.

"That cheese belongs to all of us," Wren said.

"This cheese has been through the Sablefen with me. We have a bond."

"Brannoc."

"Odji, tell her."

"I'm staying out of it," Odji said. "I've seen what happens to mediators on this crew. They get assigned extra watches."

In the end the cheese was divided into four pieces so unequal that only diplomacy prevented bloodshed, and they ate while the fire cracked and the river muttered its old, old song outside—the one it sang before names, before maps, back when water just went wherever it pleased. Odji knew that song better than he knew most prayers. It was the reason he'd survived the night the stars went out, and the reason he'd known which way to run when the bridge existed in two places at once. Rivers remembered. It was rivers' whole business.

* * *

After supper, Brannoc did what he always did after supper: took out the ruined ward-plate from the border tower and turned it over in his big careful hands, reading damage the way other men read scripture.

"Still thinking about it?" Odji asked, settling onto the second stool across from him.

"The fracture lines." Brannoc held the plate up to the firelight. Thin dark seams spidered across it, delicate as frost on a window. "I told them it couldn't fail. I signed my name under that guarantee. And then—" He stopped, jaw working.

"And then it didn't fail," Odji finished quietly. "It was sold."

"Aye."

Odji nodded slowly. He was not a man of great learning—he'd learned his letters late, off the side of shipping manifests, and still read numbers faster than words—but he understood craftsmanship, and he understood what it did to a maker to learn the flaw was never in the work. It was in the world. In the people who'd bought the world and broken it on purpose.

"You'll build again," he said. "Better ones. Doors that open because you want them open, not because somebody paid you to close everything else."

Brannoc grunted, which from Brannoc was practically a sonnet.

Across the room, Wren had gone quiet in the way that meant gears were turning. She was nineteen and had spent four years in the guild archives learning that knowledge was currency, and she hoarded questions the way Brannoc hoarded cheese. Now she crawled over to sit beside the smith, hugged her knees, and looked up at him with an expression Odji recognized from every child who'd ever begged a ride on his ferry: the look of someone about to ask for something enormous.

"Brannoc," she said, "can I teach you your letters?"

The big man went still. "I get by," he said. "I know enough. Signs. Numbers. My mark." He tapped the crooked sigil he used instead of a signature, burned into his memory by years of shame and one very patient guild clerk who'd never once laughed at him.

"I know," Wren said quickly. "I'm not saying—I just thought. If we're going toward the capital, there might be documents. Contracts. Things worth reading yourself instead of trusting someone else to read for you." She hesitated. "Trusting me is fine. But trusting yourself would be better."

Odji watched the idea move through Brannoc like weather. Fear first—that was always first with him, fear wearing the mask of pride. Then something hungrier underneath. Odji had seen men look at gold like that. He'd seen men look at freedom like that, once, from the wrong side of a debt-contract.

"All right," Brannoc said at last, gruff as gravel. "But if you laugh—"

"When have I ever?"

"The onion incident."

"That was a cough."

"It was not a cough, it was a laugh, I heard the laugh in the cough—"

"First lesson," Wren said solemnly, producing a stick and smoothing a patch of ash near the hearth. "A. It looks like a little mountain with a door in it. Everything important is a mountain with a door in it, if you think about it."

And Odji leaned back against the wall and watched the biggest man he'd ever traveled with hunch over a stick of char like a boy with his first fishing pole, tongue between his teeth, drawing a wobbly triangle while a nineteen-year-old archivist told him about doors in mountains, and he thought: *this. This is what Corvel doesn't understand.* You can buy luck and memories and doors to nowhere. But you cannot buy this. A person teaching another person letters by firelight is the single least profitable transaction in the world, and therefore the only one the Hollow Guild never figured out how to put in a ledger.

* * *

Later, when Brannoc had mastered A, B, and a C that looked like it had been through a flood (which, Odji pointed out, was thematically appropriate), the stories started. They always did, around fires, in the hour when tiredness makes everyone honest.

Wren went first, because Wren always volunteered first and then immediately regretted it. She told them about the night she'd been indentured to the guild—eight years old, sold by an aunt who cried about it, which Wren said somehow made it worse. "She cried the whole way to the guildhouse. Big dramatic tears. Got a discount on her own sister's debt for it, too. Grief at guild rates." She smiled her sharp little smile. "That's the thing nobody tells you. The Guild didn't deal in evil. Evil's expensive and unreliable. They dealt in *discounts*. Every terrible thing in that building was somebody's reasonable decision."

Nobody said anything to that. There was nothing to say. Odji reached over and squeezed her shoulder once, briefly, the way you'd steady a lantern in wind, and she leaned into it for exactly as long as dignity allowed.

Then Brannoc told a story nobody expected: about his daughter, before. Before the wards failed and his name became a byword for the worst catastrophe in a century, there had been a girl named Maren who'd thought her father could fix anything, because he'd once fixed her wooden bird so it flew again, sort of, if you threw it right. "She'd throw it off the porch and shout 'fly, stupid!' at it," he said, and his voice did something complicated in the middle. "I keep thinking—I'm going to find her. When the country's real again. And she's going to ask me why the stars went out, and I'm going to have to tell her the truth, and she's nine now, and nine-year-olds deserve better answers than the truth."

"They do," Odji agreed. "But they also see through lies faster than anyone. So tell her true, and then fix her something. That's the whole trick of fathers. Words are optional. Repairs are mandatory."

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. "You have children?"

"Had. Have. Depends how you count, when the country keeps forgetting itself." Odji turned his cup in his hands. "Two sons, back when the Vell had banks I recognized. One ran messages. One argued with me constantly about whether the river had a soul." He smiled at the fire. "I hope he won that argument. He was usually right about things I didn't want to hear."

Xanthe, who had been silent through all of it—she was often silent, but this silence had weight, the silence of a woman doing sums she didn't like—finally spoke.

"When this is done," she said, "if it works. What will you do? All of you. Not the mission. After."

"Fish," Odji said instantly.

"That's it? Fish?"

"Fishing is not *it*. Fishing is a philosophy with worms involved." He gestured with his cup. "You're asking what we keep. After we give everything else away. I keep the river. It's kept me. Seems fair to return the favor."

Wren said, "Books. Real ones, not ledgers. I want to write down everything the Guild destroyed so it can't be unwritten twice."

Brannoc said nothing for a moment, then: "Maren. Whatever's left of a workshop. And maybe—" he glanced at Wren, embarrassed, "—maybe finishing my letters. Seems stupid to quit at C."

"It's not stupid," Wren said fiercely. "C is a difficult letter. It has three jobs. No other letter has three jobs."

They were all quiet then, and the fire settled, and outside the dead lock the river went on singing its pre-name song, and Odji felt the tenderness settle over the four of them like a blanket—thin, patched, insufficient against what was coming, and held over them anyway. Tenderness was armor. Not the kind that stops the blade. The kind that makes you decide, afterward, to stand back up.

* * *

It was near midnight when he found Xanthe on the lock-gate, sitting with her boots dangling over black water, keeping watch in the way of people who are actually avoiding sleep. Odji lowered himself down beside her with the groans of a man whose knees had strong opinions, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the river slid past, carrying moonlight it had stolen fair and square.

"My grandmother used to sit like that," Odji said eventually. "On the stern of her boat. Said a ferryman should spend an hour a day doing nothing but looking at water, or the water forgets to look back."

"Did it work?"

"She lived to ninety-four and drowned at ninety-five, and only because she fell in trying to save a cat. So yes. Mostly."

Xanthe huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "Tomorrow I start giving the names back. Or the day after. I can feel it coming, like weather in a bad knee." She stared at the water. "I've been Xanthe Thackeray since I was eleven years old. Before that I was a guild-name too, did you know? They give you one when they take the old one. It's supposed to feel like being born again. It feels like being *filed*."

"And after?" Odji asked gently. "When the ledger's blank. Who's left?"

"I don't know. That's the terrifying part. That's the—" Her breath caught. "Everyone else gets to keep something. You keep the river. Wren keeps books. Brannoc keeps Maren. I keep—" She spread her empty hands. "An oath to a god who might be dead. A trade I learned from monsters. A handwriting that turns out to belong to a lie."

Odji reached into his coat.

He'd been carrying the thing for three days, waiting for the right hour, and this was it, or close enough; hours like this didn't improve with storage. From an inner pocket he brought out a small charm woven of river-reed and red thread, three knots in it, the middle knot tied in the old style that ferry-people used and nobody else remembered anymore.

"What's that?"

"Luck-charm. Made it myself, which means it's either powerful or worthless, depending on your faith in ferry-men." He pressed it into her palm and folded her fingers over it. "Reed from the Vell where it runs past my mother's grave. Thread from Wren's repair kit—don't tell her, she counts her thread. Three knots: one for what you were, one for what you are, one for whoever you turn out to be."

Xanthe looked down at it. In the dark her face did several things in quick succession, none of them composed, and Odji had the grace to pretend he hadn't seen.

"Odji, I can't take—"

"You can. You must. It's not a gift, it's a *loan*, and I charge ruinous interest, so you'd better survive." He nudged her shoulder with his own, gently, the way you'd bump a boat free of a dock. "Listen. You think you're the one with nothing to keep. But here's what I know, and I know it the way I know the current—by falling in a lot." He held up one finger. "You tore up those debts before you knew any of us would follow you. Second finger: you refused to kill Ivetta when every sensible part of winning said otherwise. Third finger: tomorrow you're going to walk up to a door that eats names, and go through anyway, because the stars need letting back in more than you need a self." He shrugged. "That's not nothing to keep, woman. That's the only thing worth keeping. Everything else is luggage."

The river murmured below them. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, a star—or the place where a star would remember being—trembled behind the sealed sky.

"For whoever you turn out to be," Odji said again, softly. "Whoever that is, I already like her. She owes me money."

Xanthe closed her hand around the charm until her knuckles went white, and when she spoke her voice was rough and steady at once, which was, Odji thought, the truest voice she owned.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the cat. Everything good in my family starts with the cat."

Behind them, from inside the cottage, came a sudden crash, followed by Wren's voice, high with outrage: "BRANNOC. Did you eat the *rest of the cheese*?"

And Brannoc's voice, deep and entirely unrepentant: "IT WAS A DIFFICULT LETTER."

Xanthe laughed—really laughed, helpless and bright—and Odji sat back on the old lock-gate with his boots over the black water and thought: *let the unmaking come, then. Let Corvel come with all his stolen heaven.* These four had a fire, a charm, half a wheel of cheese between them, and each other, and no ledger in any drowned archive had ever recorded a treasure with a higher exchange rate.

Far to the north, past the bend where the river forgot its way, the mountains waited with their door.

Come morning, they would go and answer it.
