## 16. Storm of Unmaking

The world began forgetting the color of things.

Odji noticed it first, because rivers are honest and he had spent sixty years listening to one. The Sablefen's brown water, which had carried him from every grief of his life to every small mercy after, went pale at its edges—not gray exactly, but *uncommitted*, as if the water could no longer remember whether it wanted to be river or sky or nothing at all.

He poled the ferry through it with Wren and Brannoc huddled in the bow and Xanthe standing at the stern like a figurehead carved by someone in a hurry, and he said, quietly, so as not to frighten anyone: "The water's losing its nerve."

"Rivers don't have nerve," Brannoc said. His hands were still shaking from the fen. They had not stopped shaking since the luck burned out and he had gone under holding Wren's collar, and Odji suspected they would shake for a long time yet, and did not blame him.

"Everything has nerve," Odji said. "It's just that most things keep theirs where you can't see it." He nodded upstream, toward the smudge on the horizon that was supposed to be the capital and was now more of an apology for one. "That city is about to lose its whole nervous system. Look."

They looked. Where the capital should have been there was a shape like a thought someone was trying very hard not to finish—towers present in outline but absent in conviction, a skyline drawn in pencil and then half-erased by a careless thumb. Above it, where stars should have been, there was only the flat black ceiling the country had lived under for weeks. But now the ceiling had cracks in it. Hairline fractures, faint as spider silk, glowing at their edges with something that was not light so much as the *memory* of light, pressing down like a held breath.

"The door," Xanthe said. "It's straining against the seal. Corvel can't hold it closed forever, so he's going to stop trying to hold it and start trying to *own* it." She turned, and Odji saw that her face in the strange half-light looked younger and older at once, the way faces do when they have finally stopped pretending. "We have until his ascension finishes. After that, no door opens anywhere, ever, and everything inside this country becomes whatever he decides it always was."

"Cheerful fellow," said Odji, and poled faster.

* * *

Wren had never been afraid of cities. Cities were archives that happened to be standing up—every street a ledger line, every doorway a footnote—and she had spent her whole apprenticeship learning to read them. But the capital was becoming a book with pages tearing out mid-sentence, and reading it made her want to be sick.

The southern quarter was simply gone. Not ruined. *Gone.* There was a hole in the world shaped like six thousand homes, and the hole had edges, and the edges were soft, like the rim of a candle flame, and when Wren stared too long at the place where the Tanner's Row she'd visited as a child should have been, her own memories of it started sliding sideways—the smell of tallow becoming the smell of rain becoming the smell of nothing at all.

"Don't look directly at it," Brannoc said, pulling her back by the shoulder. He had ward-smith's eyes; he saw the unmaking the way she saw text. "It's a draft. An erasure in progress. If you hold it in your head too firmly you'll get pulled into the revision."

"What do we do instead?"

"We walk looking at our feet," he said, "and we say each other's names a lot."

So they did that. It felt foolish and it worked, which Wren would later decide was the definition of most good magic. *Xanthe.* *Brannoc.* *Odji.* *Wren.* A litany, passed back and forth as they picked along the capital's western rim, where the streets still remembered themselves well enough to hold weight. Once, crossing a square that flickered between a market and an empty field and a market again, Wren watched a fruit-seller's stall exist for three full seconds—apples gleaming, a copper kettle singing over a brazier—and then subside like a wave going out, leaving bare cobbles and a single apple that rolled to Xanthe's boot and stopped.

Xanthe picked it up. She looked at it for a long moment.

"Eat it," Odji advised. "No sense letting a good apple be unmade hungry."

She ate it, walking, and Wren thought: this is what she is. She takes what the world offers before the world changes its mind. It was not a bad way to survive. It might even, Wren thought with a sudden fierce hope, be a way to save.

The archive had given them the mountain's name—*Vessel's Crown*, which even Wren, who trusted few coincidences, found almost too on-the-nose—and a road, and the road lasted for two miles before it forgot itself and became a sheep track and then a suggestion. Beyond the last standing suburb the land came apart in earnest. Hills traded places while they watched. A forest stood in four locations at once, and birdsong came from all four simultaneously, which was beautiful in a way that made Wren's teeth ache.

"How far?" Brannoc asked, for the tenth time.

Xanthe consulted the ledger she carried against her chest like a child. "By the old maps, a day and a half. By the new—" She gestured at a hill that was currently relocating. "Unknown. We go toward the crack in the sky. When in doubt, uphill."

"When in doubt, uphill," Odji repeated solemnly. "I'll carve it on my ferry."

"You left your ferry."

"I know," Odji said, wounded. "That's why it hurts."

* * *

Corvel stood at the top of the Spire of Accounts, which had once been the tallest building in the country and was now the tallest thing in it, because everything shorter than the Spire kept getting smaller.

He did not experience the unmaking as loss. This surprised him, a little. He had expected—what? Nostalgia? Some tremor of regret as the streets of his childhood dissolved into potential? Instead he felt *clean*. Every memory in the capital was raw material now, and he was learning to work it, the way a smith works iron: heat it, turn it, hammer it into the shape that serves.

Below him, ten thousand citizens moved through the surviving streets with the smooth unhurried gait of the revised. He had begun with the pliable ones—debtors, mostly, whose ledgers his guild had kept for generations, who were already accustomed to owing. Into their debts he wrote himself. *You owe me your wonder. You owe me your awe. Payable daily, in worship.* It was astonishingly easy. Gratitude and debt had always been the same coin in this country; he had merely minted a new face on it.

The ascension was not finished. That was the irritation. Divinity, it turned out, was not a thing you seized but a thing you *digested*, and Vessia's devoured godhead sat in him and his eight colleagues like nine swallowed suns, heavy, slow, resistant. He needed time. He needed silence. And above all he needed the ledgers—not for power, power he had, but for *legitimacy*. An account book written in Xanthe Thackeray's immaculate hand, recording the purchase of the wards, the sale of the sky, the transfer of divinity: that was a contract. Contracts, completed and countersigned, became law. Law became nature. Nature became inevitable.

And she was climbing his mountain with the wretched thing hugged to her breast like a favorite cat.

"She'll bring it to me," he told the empty air at the top of the Spire, and the air, being mostly rewritten already, agreed with him. "She's an accountant. Accountants deliver."

But he set watchers on the road anyway. Nine of them. He had been a careful man for sixty years; apotheosis would not cure him of it, though he intended to let it try.

* * *

They lost the supplies on the second night.

It happened without drama, which was the worst part. They camped in the lee of a barn that had been in three counties that morning and settled on none of them, and when Wren woke, the packs were simply *less*—not emptied, not scattered, just reduced, as if the concept of "provisions" had been trimmed for length. The rope was shorter. The bread was fewer. Brannoc's toolkit contained excellent tools, all of them slightly wrong, a hammer whose head was a little too large for any nail in the world.

"It's eating the margins first," Wren said, turning a heel of bread over in her hands. "Unmaking works like editing. You cut what seems least essential."

"So we're nonessential," Brannoc said.

"Our *supplies* are nonessential. There's a difference."

"There bloody well isn't, from the supplies' point of view."

Odji laughed—a real laugh, rolling up from his boots—and clapped the ward-smith on the back hard enough to stagger him. "Good. Stay funny. Funny people are hard to erase. I've known men the river took and I've known men the war took, but I never knew a man laughter took."

"Give it time," Brannoc said, but he smiled, and his hands, Wren noticed, had almost stopped shaking.

They climbed through the dark hours because sleeping felt like surrender. The crack in the sky widened overhead, and through it—if you tilted your head and squinted and believed, which Wren was increasingly willing to do—you could see *through*: pinpricks of gold, patient and enormous, waiting on the other side of the seal like travelers at a locked gate.

"Stars," Xanthe said softly. She had stopped walking to stare up, and the others stopped too, because you followed Xanthe's gaze the way water follows a slope; it was simply the direction things went. "Real ones. They're right there. All this time they've been right *there*."

"Do you think they know?" Wren asked. "What's happening to us?"

"I think," Xanthe said slowly, "that they're light, and light doesn't wait politely forever. I think if we open that door even a crack, they won't trickle through. They'll *flood*."

"Flood's a strong word," said Odji, who had strong opinions about floods. Then he considered the crack in the sky, and the nine swallowed suns sitting heavy in some magister's gut below, and amended: "Correct word. Terrible, correct word."

* * *

The watchers found them at dawn, on a ridge that was briefly, blessedly, only in one place.

There were nine of them, guild enforcers in gray, and they did not charge. They walked. That was the horror of it—they walked at an easy pace across the unstable ground as though the ground had agreed to hold still for them, and Wren understood with a lurch that these nine had already been partly rewritten. Corvel's new law ran in them. The unmaking parted around its own employees.

"They're not here to fight," Xanthe breathed. "They're here to *escort*. Oh, that arrogant—"

"Nine against four," Brannoc said, already pulling chalk from his ruined kit, already kneeling at the ridge's edge. "I can't ward a road that won't stay put. But I can ward *us*. Everyone stand where I'm standing and think about being a door."

"A door?" Odji said.

"A door that opens onto somewhere else. Anywhere else. Doesn't matter where—it matters that it's *not here*." Chalk shrieked across stone. Brannoc's hands were steady now, absolutely steady, the steadiest Wren had ever seen them, and she realized he had found the thing he was for. Not the guild's doors. Not the sealed borders. *This*: four frightened people and a circle of chalk and a hillside changing its mind beneath them.

The enforcers were fifty yards off. Forty.

"Brannoc," Xanthe said.

"Working."

Thirty.

"Brannoc—"

"*Working.*"

The ground between them and the enforcers chose, in that moment, to become a lake. Nobody had decided it; the geography was fraying so badly that possibilities were leaking through, and for one glorious second there was a lake on the ridge, deep and cold and utterly improbable. The enforcers, being partly rewritten, partly real, and entirely committed to walking forward, walked straight into it.

What followed was undignified beyond description. Gray cloaks thrashed. A helmet bobbed. One enforcer surfaced shouting something about *escorting* and went under again, and Odji—who had fished drowning men out of the Sablefen for forty years and had opinions about doing it for free—was laughing so hard he had to sit down.

"Go!" Brannoc gasped, chalk-white, dragging the last stroke of his circle closed. "While the ground's confused! Confusion favors doors!"

They ran. Uphill. Always uphill. Behind them the lake resolved back into ridge, and the enforcers crawled out of a puddle that had briefly been a body of water, and Wren did not look back again because looking back was how you lost things.

* * *

By the time the sun should have risen—no sun rose anymore; there was only the cracked ceiling brightening grudgingly, like a sky doing its job from memory—the mountain was in sight.

Vessel's Crown. It thrust up through the fraying land like a keel through fog, and unlike everything around it, it did not flicker. It was *fixed*. Older than the country, older perhaps than the guild, wearing snow and silence and a single road that switchbacked up its flank toward a notch near the summit where, even at this distance, even in the broken light, there was a darkness that was not shadow. Shadow moves. This did not. It was the shape of a door that had been shut for a very long time, and Wren felt it in her sternum the way you feel a held note.

"That's it," Xanthe said. She said it quietly, but the quiet carried. "That's the Last Hollow."

They stood together in the wind and looked at it. Nobody spoke for a moment, and the wind said nothing either, because the wind up here had forgotten how.

Then the ground behind them shuddered, and they all turned, and saw the capital—or what remained of it—flare with a light that had no color and no mercy. From the top of the distant Spire, something vast was unfolding. They were too far away to see Corvel. They could see what he was becoming, which was visible even at twenty miles: a column of stolen radiance, nine swallowed suns rising up one throat, and the cracks in the sky above the city widening in answer, straining, *hurting*.

"He feels the door," Xanthe said. Her hand had gone to the ledger at her chest. "He knows what we're doing. He's going to hurry."

"Then so do we," said Odji, and started walking.

They climbed. The road held—barely, grudgingly, one switchback at a time—and the unmaking rose up the mountain behind them like a tide, erasing the road's lower reaches as they gained its upper ones, so that they were, Wren understood dimly, running up a staircase that was being dismantled beneath them. Supplies gone. Certainty gone. Time going.

Only each other left. Which, Wren thought, gripping Brannoc's sleeve as the path narrowed, hauling Odji over a gap where the mountain briefly forgot to be continuous, watching Xanthe break trail ahead with the ledger hugged close and her jaw set like the mountain itself—

Only each other left. Which might be exactly enough. It had better be.

Near the summit, where the air thinned and the door's darkness filled half the sky, Xanthe stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into her back.

On the threshold, framed against the Hollow's patient black, stood a woman in gray with a sword drawn and her stance wide, blocking the way.

Xanthe's sword-sister. Ivetta.

"I hoped I wouldn't have to do this," Ivetta called, and her voice cracked on *hoped*, and did not move aside.
