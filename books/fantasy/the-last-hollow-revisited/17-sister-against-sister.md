## 17. Sister Against Sister

The mountain had a door in it the way a drum has a skin: something stretched taut across a hollow, waiting to be struck.

Xanthe felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her sternum, like the moment before a sneeze or a confession. The path up from the valley had been strange even by the standards of the last month; they'd crossed a stream twice without turning around, and at one point Wren's shadow arrived a full minute ahead of her and waited, politely, for its owner to catch up.

But now they stood before it, and there was no mistaking it for anything but what it was.

The Last Hollow was set into the mountainside like an eye half-closed. Not carved—*grown*, Brannoc said later, though he wasn't there yet. It was a doorway of black stone older than any masonry Xanthe knew, framed in script that predated the alphabet Vessia's chapels used, and through its center ran a seam of light so faint you could only see it when you weren't looking directly at it.

"Is it supposed to look like that," Wren whispered, "or is it broken?"

"It looks like that," Odji said. He had his river-charm out, the little carved fish on its leather cord, and it was trembling against his palm like something alive. "Doors to nowhere always look like that. That's how you know they go somewhere."

Brannoc put out one scarred hand as if to touch the frame and stopped an inch short, fingers curling back. "Wards," he muttered. "Old ones. Good ones." A pause. "Better than mine ever were."

"You'll get no argument from me," Odji said.

Xanthe barely heard them. She was looking at the seam of light, and the ledger was open in her mind—the true ledger, the one she'd read in the drowned archive, the one written in her own hand by someone who wasn't her. *The door opens for surrender only. Every name. Every oath.* She had walked three hundred miles to stand in front of this sentence made of stone.

She took a step forward.

A blade came down between her foot and the ground—not striking, just arriving, point-first, with the soft finality of a period at the end of a long paragraph.

"I wouldn't," said Ivetta.

* * *

They had been sword-sisters for eleven years. They had shared watch-fires, hangovers, a tent through the worst winter either of them could remember, and exactly one secret each—Ivetta's being that she was afraid of geese, which Xanthe had promised never to tell anyone and was now, under mortal duress, keeping with great difficulty even in her own head.

Ivetta looked terrible. That was the first thing Xanthe noticed, before the drawn steel, before the six enforcers fanning out along the ridge behind her. Her old friend had shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose things in, and her left arm was bound where a bridge in two places had tried to keep her.

"Geese," Xanthe said, because eleven years buys you certain privileges.

"Don't." Ivetta's voice cracked on the word, and she hated that Xanthe heard it. "Don't do that. Don't make this small."

"The last time we talked, you hit me with a boat hook."

"That was a warning shot."

"It was a *boat hook*, Iv."

Behind Xanthe, she heard Odji murmur to Wren, "Should we be doing something?" and Wren murmur back, "I think we're watching history," and Brannoc mutter, "I think we're watching two idiots who love each other fight," and all three of them settle into the particular silence of people who have correctly identified their role in a scene.

Ivetta lifted her chin. Six enforcers spread along the ridge, and Xanthe counted the odds—bad, then worse—and then noticed that none of the six had actually advanced past their captain. Waiting. Watching. Even the guild's dogs, it seemed, wanted to see how this went.

"Stand aside," Ivetta said. "Please." The *please* cost her something visible. "You don't know what's coming. Corvel's ascension starts tonight. By morning the sealing completes, and everything inside the border is safe. Fed. Whole. No more unwritten villages, no more roads that lie. Yes, we lose the stars. But Xanthe—" her voice dropped, urgent, pleading— "nobody up here has seen a star in a month anyway. The clouds came with the dark. Ask any farmer. Ask any child. What are we mourning? A memory?"

"A truth," Xanthe said.

"A *sky*." Ivetta spat. "You want to trade every living soul in this country for a sky? Because that's the toll. You've read it. The door doesn't open gently. When it opens, everything Corvel built comes undone—the wards, the walls, the whole careful architecture of safety—and whatever's been pressing on the outside gets *in*. You've seen what's been pressing."

Xanthe had. In the marsh, in the loops of road, in the village that remembered a different name than its own gravestones did—there had been a pressure at the edges of things, patient and vast, like the sea testing a seawall.

"Yes," she said. "And I still say open it."

"Why?" And here Ivetta's composure finally broke, all the way down, and underneath it was not anger but grief, enormous and unarmored. "Because your god told you to? Your god who's been silent for seven years? Xanthe, I've watched you carry that silence like a wound. I've bandaged you after contracts and never once heard you pray. And now, when the silence finally makes sense—when it turns out there was nothing there to answer you—you'd die for the absence? Open the world for it?"

It was a good argument. It was, Xanthe thought, the best argument anyone had made against her since the archive, and it deserved better than deflection.

So she didn't deflect.

"I don't know," she said. "Honestly. I don't know what Vessia wanted. I don't know if she wanted anything. Maybe she was just devoured, full stop, and everything I've believed since I was nine years old is a story I told a starving child so she could sleep at night." She shrugged, palms open, empty. "But here's what I know, Iv. Corvel bought the wards with guild coin. He ate a god—or helped eat her—and split her among his friends. He sealed the borders so nobody could leave and nothing could arrive, and he called it safety. And when I asked him what happens to the people who don't want to live inside a locked box with a man-shaped lock, do you know what he said?"

"What?"

"He said, *they will learn to prefer it.*" Xanthe let that sit. "That's not safety, Ivetta. That's a jar. We sell jars at the guild. Bottled memories. I've catalogued hundreds. Do you know what every single one of them has in common?"

"They're valuable," Ivetta said flatly.

"They're *still*. Nobody in a bottle grows. Nothing new gets in. It's perfect and it's finished and it's dead, and Corvel wants to put the whole country in one because he thinks a closed system is a controlled one." She shook her head slowly. "You believe he'll share the divinity. I've read the inner circle's contracts. There's no sharing clause, Iv. There never is. In twenty years of ledgers, I never once recorded a partnership that didn't end with someone holding the whole account."

Ivetta's jaw tightened. She knew it was true. Xanthe could see her knowing it.

"Even so," Ivetta said, and her sword came up into guard, and the conversation was over. "Orders are orders. And you're my sister. So I'm going to give you the only mercy my orders allow: I'm going to beat you instead of killing you. Walk away after. Take your ragamuffins and your river-boatman and disappear, and I will report that you fell off the mountain."

"And the ledgers?"

"The ledgers come with me." A flicker of apology. "Those orders aren't flexible."

Xanthe sighed and drew her own blade.

Eleven years of muscle memory settled into her grip like an old coat. Behind her, Wren said, "Wait, wait—we're just letting this happen?" and Odji said, "Shh. This is how they talk."

* * *

They fought the way they'd always fought, which is to say: badly, by the standards of anyone watching, and perfectly, by the standards of each other.

Ivetta opened with the low feint to the knee that she'd invented during the Marsh War and had never once landed, and Xanthe turned it aside with the parry she'd invented thirty seconds after Ivetta invented the feint. Ivetta grinned. It was a terrible thing to see—a grin with grief behind it—but it was real.

"You kept that," she said, pressing forward.

"You kept the feint. Fair's fair."

Steel rang on the black stone. The enforcers on the ridge shifted, uncertain; their captain had said *beat her, don't kill her*, and it was becoming clear that neither outcome was imminent. This was not a duel so much as a long argument conducted in a language only two people alive still spoke fluently.

*I loved the guild,* Ivetta's blades said. *The guild fed me when the army discarded me.*

*The guild lied to us,* Xanthe's answered. *It fed us on purpose so we'd never ask who cooked.*

*It gave us each other.*

*It gave us each other and then pointed us at children during the Ferrow collection. Remember Ferrow? Tell me you remember Ferrow.*

Ivetta stumbled—half a step, no more, but Xanthe saw the memory land behind her eyes, the shuttered house, the crying they'd both pretended afterward hadn't happened. Her next strike came hard and graceless, all fury, and Xanthe gave ground up the slope toward the door rather than answer it in kind.

"Stop retreating!" Ivetta snarled.

"I'm not retreating. I'm *listening*."

"To what?"

"To how tired you are."

That was a mistake. Or it would have been, against anyone else. Against Ivetta it simply made her honest: she came on in a flurry that drove Xanthe back three steps, four, five, until Xanthe's heel found the threshold of the Last Hollow and the seam of light pulsed once beneath her boot like a heartbeat noticing her.

And Ivetta stopped.

Not because she'd won. Xanthe saw it happen—the exact moment her old friend understood that she *hadn't* won, that she'd driven her sister backward onto the very thing she'd climbed a mountain to protect her from, and that the door was right there, and that the only reason it wasn't opening was that Xanthe was choosing, still choosing, moment by moment, not to step through without settling this first.

"You could just go," Ivetta whispered. "Right now. Why haven't you gone?"

"Because you'd follow me through," Xanthe said. "Or die trying. And I'm not leaving you on the wrong side of a miracle, you absolute goose-fearing lunatic."

Something in Ivetta's face crumpled and reassembled itself, worse.

"Fight me," she said. "*Properly.* Stop talking. Stop listening. Just—win or lose, cleanly, the way we were trained, and let it be finished—" Her voice climbed, cracked, climbed again. "Because if you win some other way, some *soft* way, then I have to sit with what you're saying, and Xanthe, I can't, I *can't*, because if you're right then the last eleven years were—"

"Were spent loving someone who lied to you," Xanthe said quietly. "I know. Mine too."

She dropped her sword.

It clattered on the stone, loud as a verdict, and rolled to rest against the doorframe. Xanthe stood empty-handed on the threshold of the Last Hollow, arms open, in front of six armed enforcers and her oldest friend, and did the single most dangerous thing she had done in a career of dangerous things.

She trusted Ivetta.

"Kill me or believe me," she said. "Those are the options now. I'm done fighting you. I've been fighting you since the night the stars went out, in every way except the one you'd respect, and I'm sorry—it was cowardice, dressed up as strategy. So. Here. Unarmored. The door behind me, your orders in front of me. Choose."

The mountain held its breath. Somewhere below, Odji was whispering, urgently, "Is she mad, she's mad, she's completely—" and Wren shushed him with tears already running down her face.

Ivetta stood with her point leveled at Xanthe's chest, and her hand shook, and Xanthe watched the war move through her—the war that was never really about swords at all.

"You'll get us all killed," Ivetta said hoarsely.

"Maybe."

"The country might not survive it."

"It isn't surviving now. It's being *archived*."

"The stars might not come back."

"Then we'll have opened a door and been honest about the dark," Xanthe said. "Which is more than Corvel offers. He promises light and delivers a lid. At least a door admits what it is."

The sword tip wavered.

And then—slowly, like a drawbridge lowering—Ivetta turned the blade aside and drove it point-down into the mountain stone, where it stood quivering, upright, abandoned.

"Six years ago," she said, in a voice Xanthe had never heard from her, small and stripped, "you asked me why I stayed in the guild. Do you remember? After Ferrow. You asked, and I said *because it's home*. And you nodded, and we drank, and neither of us said the true thing, which is that home shouldn't need guards on its doors." She laughed, wet and awful. "Your shop does. Your stupid future shop, with the unlocked back door. Home that trusts people to walk in."

"Ivetta—"

"If the stars come back," Ivetta said, stepping away from her sword, away from her orders, away from eleven years, "if they *actually come back*—you find me. You find me and you say 'told you so' and I will buy the drinks and I will never argue with you about a door again."

"Deal."

"But if this kills everyone—"

"Then I'll owe you the drinks," Xanthe said. "In whatever comes after."

Ivetta looked at her for a long moment. Then she turned to her enforcers, who had watched the entire exchange with the expressions of people realizing their pensions had just dissolved.

"Well?" she barked. "You heard the captain's orders change. Stand down. Go home to your families—if any of us still have families by morning, which is apparently the wager." And then, softer, almost to herself, as the ridge began to empty: "Goose, indeed."

* * *

They came up the slope together—Wren running first, because Wren always ran first at things like this, then Brannoc moving with the careful dignity of a man pretending he hadn't been hiding behind a rock, then Odji, who pressed his river-charm into Ivetta's hand without a word and closed her fingers around it.

"For luck," he said. "Borrowed. From a river that remembers your name kindly, I think. Rivers gossip."

Ivetta stared at the little wooden fish. "I fought these people for a month."

"We grow on you," Odji said. "Like moss. Like family."

Xanthe turned back to the door. The seam of light was brighter now—or perhaps it was simply that dusk had fallen while no one was watching, and the first true darkness of the evening made the door's faint glow legible at last. Beyond it, far beyond it, something stirred. Something vast and patient and *pressing*.

The stars. Coming home.

Her hands shook. She looked at them—her ledger-keeper's hands, her killer's hands, her sister's hands—and behind her, her ragged, impossible family arranged itself in a half-circle, close enough to touch, saying nothing, asking nothing, staying.

Beyond the ridge, far to the south, the sky above the capital had begun to glow with a light that was not starlight and never would be. Corvel's apotheosis, rising like an infected dawn.

Whatever Xanthe was about to do, she would have to do it before that false sunrise finished climbing.

She stepped up to the Last Hollow, laid both palms against the black stone, and began to speak her names aloud—one by one, like coins counted out of a purse she could never refill.
