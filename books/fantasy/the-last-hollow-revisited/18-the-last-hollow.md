## 18. The Last Hollow

The door was smaller than she had imagined.

That was the thing nobody told you about the end of the world: it didn't look like much. The Last Hollow sat in a shoulder of mountain above the drowned capital, a seam of darkness in the rock no wider than a garden gate, framed in stone so old it had forgotten what it was quarried from. No hinges. No handle. No light leaking around its edges. Just a rectangle of absolute quiet, set into the mountainside like a missing tooth.

Xanthe stood before it with her hands empty and her heart going like a debt-collector's knuckles on a door.

Behind her, the others were catching up. She could hear them before she saw them—Brannoc's wheezing, Wren's chattering teeth, Odji's unhurried footsteps, which somehow always sounded unhurried even when he was climbing at a dead run. The mountain behind them was coming apart in slow motion. Not collapsing—*unwriting*. Whole switchbacks of trail had simply stopped being there as they climbed, and twice Wren had stepped onto ground that hadn't existed yet and had to be hauled back by the collar like an adventurous cat.

"Is that it?" Wren panted, hauling herself over the last ridge. Her hair had gone full weather-system sometime around the Sablefen and never recovered. "It's so *small*."

"Doors usually are," Odji said. He came up beside her, river-charm glinting at his throat—the one he'd given her, actually; he wore its twin. "The big ones are just for showing off."

"It doesn't open," Brannoc said flatly. He was already circling it, ward-smith's fingers hovering an inch from the stone, not quite touching. "I'm reading nothing. No lock, no hinge-grammar, no threshold-tension. It's not a sealed door, it's a—" He frowned, hunting for the word. "A held breath."

"Then someone has to let it out," Xanthe said.

They all looked at her. Even the mountain seemed to lean in, if mountains could be said to do anything anymore, which lately they could.

"The archive was clear," she said. "It opens for renunciation. Every name the guild gave me. Every oath. All of it, spoken at the threshold, given up." Her voice did something strange on the last words—not breaking, exactly, but bending, the way a ledger-page bends when you've pressed too hard writing on it. "All of it."

"No," Wren said immediately.

"Wren—"

"No, I mean it, I've thought about this the entire climb, which admittedly was only four hours but I think very fast when I'm terrified, and it's a *trick*, Xanthe. It's a guild contract. Look at the shape of it! 'Give us everything you are and we'll give you the world'—that's their whole business model. That's literally what they sold to Corvel. Why would the solution to them be shaped exactly like them?"

Xanthe opened her mouth to answer and found, to her considerable annoyance, that she didn't have one.

"She's right," Brannoc said slowly. "I built wards for thirty years. Renunciation clauses are how you trap people, not free them. You bind a spirit's name and it's yours forever. You make a smith surrender his craft-oaths and he can never forge again. It's always a cage dressed as a key."

Odji said nothing. He was looking at Xanthe with that ferryman patience of his, the one that made you feel less like you were being waited on and more like the river itself was willing to wait, indefinitely, without judgment, until you were ready to get in the boat.

"What?" she snapped at him.

"You're doing the face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided and you're waiting for permission to say so. You do it every time you tear up a debt. You did it when you told me my ferry license had been purchased by a man who wanted the river to run backward." He tilted his head. "Say what you've decided. We'll argue after, or we won't."

Xanthe looked back at the Hollow. Up close, this near, she could feel it—not sound, not cold, but a kind of attention. The way a chapel felt when the god was listening. Except Vessia had been silent seven years, devoured, split into seven greedy pieces wearing magister's robes, and still the dark of that doorway felt *attended*.

"I decided somewhere on the fen," she said quietly. "When the luck burned out and Brannoc went under, and all I could think was—I have spent my whole life keeping accounts for impossible things, and the one thing I have never once put in a ledger is what any of it cost *me*. What I paid. What they took." She touched the ledger at her hip—the true one, the one Ivetta would carry out of this country to be read aloud, though that was tomorrow's problem and possibly no one's. "The names are the price. Fine. Let them take the names. Everything the guild ever called me was theirs anyway. They minted every single one. I just—I never noticed I was spending them."

"That's not nothing," Odji said.

"No," she agreed. "It's everything. That's rather the problem."

Wren had gone very still, in the way she did when she was about to be either brilliant or catastrophic, and historically the odds were even. "If you lose your name," she said carefully, "how will we find you? After. On the other side of—of whatever this is. In the ordinary world." Her voice wobbled on *ordinary* like a table with a short leg. "You'll be a stranger. We'll be strangers to you. What if you walk past us in a market and don't—"

"Wren." Xanthe turned and took her by both shoulders. The girl's eyes were enormous and wet and furious about being wet. "Listen to me. I am about to stand in front of a hole in reality and list off every name a guild of would-be gods ever stapled onto my soul. Do you know what I am *not* giving up? I'm not giving up the person who taught you letters on a bridge that existed in two places. I'm not giving up the woman who fished a drowning apprentice out of a marsh because leaving her wasn't in the accounts. Names are what other people call you. This—" she tapped Wren's sternum, then her own, then, reaching out, Odji's charm, then Brannoc's scarred wrist, one after another like closing a circuit, "—this is what we *are*. Nobody can repossess it. I've checked. I'm extremely good at checking."

Wren laughed, wetly, furiously. "That was terrible. You've been saving that speech and it was terrible."

"I wrote it on the fen. Everything I write on the fen is terrible."

"True," said Brannoc. "Your handwriting got worse too."

"My handwriting is a *covenant*—"

"Just—" Wren grabbed her into a hug that cracked a rib, or at least filed a complaint about one. "Just come out the other side. Whoever you are. Promise."

"I promise," Xanthe said into her hair. And meant it, which frightened her more than the door did.

* * *

She approached the threshold alone.

The others hung back at the ridge—Odji with his arms folded, Brannoc with his head bowed in the involuntary respect of a craftsman before another's work, Wren vibrating like a struck bell and biting down on approximately forty objections.

Up close, the Hollow made a sound after all. Not a sound—a *hush*. The specific hush of a chapel at the moment before the liturgy, when everyone knows something is about to be asked of them. Xanthe had heard it a thousand times in a thousand years of chapels and had never once known what it was waiting for.

"All right," she said to it. To Vessia. To the silence that had been her god's only voice for seven years. "I'm here. You want the names? Let's do the names."

She closed her eyes and began.

"Xanthe Thackeray." Her own voice sounded enormous against the hush. "Given to me at the foundling gate, registered in the guild rolls, page one, line one. I give it back."

The hush deepened. Something in the stone *leaned*.

"Whisper-of-Vessia. My novitiate name, when I swore the first oath at nine years old, starving, in a war I didn't understand, promising a god I couldn't hear that I'd keep her secrets if she'd keep me fed." Her throat tightened. "She kept me fed. I never learned if she kept her other promises. I give the name back. I give the oath back. It was a child's bargain and I honor the child but the bargain is ended."

Something warm ran down her cheek. She ignored it. Ledgers didn't stop for weather.

"Keeper of the Third Ledger. Keeper of Impossible Things. Auditor of the Unaccounted." Each title came easier now, rolling out like coins counted into a tray. "Debt-Binder. Silence-Sworn. Thackeray-of-the-Night-Rolls. Little Quill—that one was Ivetta's, and it was the only kind one, so it hurts most, and I give it back too, Iv, wherever you are, I hope you're carrying those ledgers like I asked."

She was crying properly now and had stopped caring. Somewhere behind her Wren was making small determined sounds of solidarity-by-proxy.

"Corvel's Right Hand-in-Waiting. Successor-Designate. The One Who Balances." A breath, ragged. "And the oath beneath them all. The great one. The one I renewed every year at the Night Rolls: *that the books be kept, that the doors be held, that the god's silence be obeyed.*"

She stopped.

Because the hush had changed.

It had been attentive before. Now it was—eager. Straining. Like a listener leaning forward so far they've nearly fallen off their chair. And underneath the hush, faint as ink showing through cheap paper, something else was surfacing. Words. Words in a hand she knew better than her own heartbeat, because it *was* her own hand, her own cramped slanting ledger-script, rising through the dark of the Hollow letter by letter:

*Entry the last. Recorded by the hand that kept the books.*

Xanthe's breath left her.

She knew that script. Of course she knew it. For eleven years it had been the only constant in her life—and yet she had never written these lines. She would have remembered. She would swear on anything remaining to her that these lines had never passed under her pen, and simultaneously they were hers, unmistakably hers, the way a laugh is yours, the way the way you hold a pen is yours alone.

The letters finished forming, and she read:

*Let the record show: the silence was not absence. The silence was the question. Seven years I withheld my voice to learn who among the sworn would listen to what remained—the ledgers, the debts, the doors, the poor. Who would keep faith when faith paid nothing. And who, when the false gods sealed the sky, would remember that I was never a keeper of doors at all. I was their opener. Every door I ever shut, I shut only to teach the opening.*

*I chose my auditor well. Open the door, little quill. Let the stars come home. Your account is settled, and it was never in arrears.*

"—oh," Xanthe said.

It was not a profound utterance. Eleven years of careful bookkeeping, seven years of silent devotion, one apocalypse survived, and the sum total of her response to divine revelation was *oh*, in a small wet voice, like someone discovering they'd been standing in rain for hours.

But oh. *Oh.*

The silence hadn't been abandonment. It hadn't even been suffering. It had been an examination, and she had passed it not with her prayers—which had been few, and mostly complaints—but with her *work*. With torn-up debts. With a fishing line thrown into a marsh. With the stubborn, unglamorous, day-after-day keeping of faith when faith offered nothing back. Vessia had watched the whole time. Vessia had been watching from inside her own handwriting, patient as ink, and the final message had been waiting in the one place Xanthe was guaranteed to look eventually: the books.

"You absolute," Xanthe managed, laughing and sobbing in roughly equal measure, "*sneak* of a god. Seven years. Seven years of me talking to a chapel wall, and you were in the margins the entire time."

From the ridge, Wren called, anxiously, "What? What is it? Is it eating you? It looks like it might be eating you!"

"It's not eating me!" Xanthe called back, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. "It's *grading* me! I passed!"

There was a pause.

"THAT'S NOT REASSURING," Wren yelled.

"IT'S THE BEST NEWS OF MY LIFE AND POSSIBLY ANYONE'S!"

"STILL NOT REASSURING!"

Xanthe laughed until her ribs ached, and then she turned back to the Hollow, and the laughter settled into something quieter and steadier. The last of the hush was waiting. The last of the names.

"One more," she said softly. "The one I gave myself. The one I thought was mine." She laid her palm flat against the dark. It was neither cold nor warm. It was *patient*. "Whatever I'm called on the other side—whatever plain, ordinary, unregistered name I pick up in some market town—I choose it freely. It isn't in any ledger. It isn't yours, or theirs, or anyone's. But this one—" she tapped the dark once, gently, the way you'd tap a friend's shoulder, "—this one I was never giving away. Only setting down. So my hands are empty to open the door."

The Hollow breathed.

Not metaphorically. She felt the exhalation move through the stone, through her arm, through the marrow of the mountain, and the seam of darkness trembled like water about to break. Beyond it—for one half-second, one stolen frame—she saw the other side. Grass. Ordinary grass, wind-ruffled, sunlit, real. And above it—

Above it, stars. Thousands upon thousands of stolen stars, pressing against the far side of the door like light pressing against shuttered windows, like the whole night sky of her childhood crowded at a threshold, waiting seven years for someone to finally, *finally* unlock it.

"Oh," Xanthe whispered again, and this time it was reverence. "Hello. We kept your room ready."

She set her shoulder against the door the way you'd set your shoulder against anything heavy and worth the lifting, and pulled.

It swung open like it weighed nothing, because of course it did. Doors only weigh what you haven't surrendered.

Light poured over the ridge. Behind her, three voices cried out—one in wonder, one in terror, one in fluent profanity—and down in the valley below, far off, something vast and half-made turned toward the mountain with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

He had felt it too. Of course he had. The door opening was the one entry Corvel had never been able to buy.

Xanthe gripped the edge of the Last Hollow, planted her feet, and prepared to hold the door for the stars.
