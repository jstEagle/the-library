## 19. Let There Be Stars

The door at the top of the mountain was smaller than Xanthe had expected.

She had imagined, all these weeks of blood and bog and borrowed luck, something grand — a cathedral gate of black iron, a mouth wide enough to swallow a country. Instead it was a plain door in a plain stone face, weathered grey, with an iron ring gone green and a lintel worn smooth by hands older than any guild ledger could count. Someone had once carved a word above it, but the country's unmaking had reached even here, and the letters kept rearranging themselves: *HOLLOW*, then *WHOLE*, then *LOW*, then nothing at all.

"It keeps trying," Wren said softly. She stood at Xanthe's shoulder, ink-stained fingers pressed to her own mouth as if to keep from disturbing it. "Trying to be what it is."

"Then let's give it something true to hold onto," Brannoc rumbled. He planted himself before the door like a ward-smith taking a commission, though there was no ward left in him worth setting — he'd spent them all on the fen, and his hands still shook from it. "Whatever comes through that pass behind us, we hold this threshold until she says the words."

Behind them, down the switchback trail, the world was ending in earnest.

Xanthe could see it from here — the capital's half-dark glow guttering like a lamp low on oil, and above it, where the stars should have been, a ceiling of absolute black that seemed lately to *breathe*. Corvel's ascension. Seven years of silence, decades of theft, one night of vanished sky, all of it gathering toward a crown no honest head should bear.

And coming up the mountain toward them, unhurried as a man walking to his own investiture, came the light of him.

"Odji?" Xanthe said.

The ferryman leaned against the doorframe with the ease of a man who had spent forty years letting rivers decide things for him. He turned the little wooden charm in his fingers — the one he'd given her three nights ago, carved with a knot pattern his grandmother used to tie into mooring lines. "For whoever you turn out to be." Now he pressed it back into her hand.

"You'll want both hands free for the door," he said. "I'll want mine for the river."

"There's no river up a mountain, old man."

"There's always a river," Odji said. "You just have to know its name."

* * *

Corvel arrived the way weather arrives.

One moment the pass below was empty; the next it was full of him — not his body, not entirely, though the body was there, Magister Corvel in his grey travelling coat with his grey patient face, but around him and through him moved a light that did not belong to anything alive. It was starlight, Xanthe realized, and her stomach turned. It was *their* starlight, the stolen sky of a whole nation, divided seven ways among the inner circle and now gathered into him alone, because the others had been devoured first. He had eaten his fellow gods at breakfast.

He stopped twenty paces off. The mountain grass beneath his feet had begun, very quietly, to forget how to be grass.

"Xanthe Thackeray." His voice was the same voice that had tested her loyalty with small cruelties for fifteen years, the same voice that had signed her name to the ward-fall while she slept. That sameness was the most horrifying thing about him. Apotheosis had changed everything except him. "You've brought me my ledgers after all. And my debtors. And my door." He smiled the way a banker smiles at interest accruing. "You always did balance accounts beautifully."

"The ledgers are ash," Brannoc said flatly. "Burned on the bridge, before Ivetta took us. You're welcome."

"Ivetta took you? Then Ivetta has them." Corvel's smile widened a fraction. "It doesn't matter, child. I don't need records anymore. Records were for men who could die. I am about to become the thing the records were keeping accounts *with*." He looked past them all, at the small grey door, and something almost tender crossed his ruined-holy face. "Do you know what this is, Xanthe? Do you finally know?"

"A door out," Wren whispered.

"A door *in*," Corvel corrected gently. "The first door. The original contract between a country and its sky. When we closed the border wards, we didn't cut your country off from history — history got in anyway, it always does, it leaks like river water. But this—" he gestured, and the black ceiling above seemed to lean closer, "—this is where the sky itself was admitted. Every star that ever shone on this soil entered through this frame. And tonight I will stand in it and take delivery of the whole consignment."

"You'll burn," Brannoc said. "Ward-law, god-law, whatever law made that door — nothing mortal stands in a threshold like that and stays himself."

"I stopped being mortal the night the stars went out," Corvel said. "Step aside, Xanthe. You were never a rebel. You were an accountant having a bad quarter. Come home. There will be a place for you at the right hand of the new sky — there was for Aldous Vey, when he asked, and you are worth ten of Aldous."

And there it was. The offer that had hollowed out her mentor, her teacher, the woman who had taught her double-entry bookkeeping and which knife to use on which occasion and how to love a silent god without hating Him for it. Xanthe looked at the place where Corvel's right hand would be, and thought of Aldous Vey's last recorded entry, written in a hand gone strange: *paid in full, received nothing.*

"No," Xanthe said.

It came out quiet. She said it again, louder, to hear it in her own ears against the weight of his light.

"No. Not because I'm brave, Magister. Because I've read your books. All seven years of them, every forged page in my hand, every transaction you hid inside my honesty. And here is what your ledgers actually say, if anyone ever audits them properly: you bought divinity on margin. You borrowed against a sky you hadn't captured yet, and the collateral was a country, and now the note has come due, and you are standing at the only counter in the world where payment can be made." She held up the river-charm, and Odji's knot-pattern caught what little light remained. "I'm not stepping aside. I'm opening the till."

Corvel sighed, and the sigh bent the mountain wind sideways.

"So be it," he said, and began to walk forward, and where he walked the trail forgot itself behind him, stone becoming idea becoming nothing, and Wren grabbed Brannoc's arm and Brannoc set his feet like a man bracing a floodgate, and Odji stepped out into the path alone with his palms open, the way he stood when the Sablefen fog came down and he had to talk the water into behaving.

"Old river-name," Odji called back over his shoulder, cheerful as sin. "Everybody hold onto somebody."

* * *

What Odji did then, Xanthe never fully saw, and afterward he refused to explain it beyond saying that all rivers remember being rain, and rain remembers falling, and falling is just a kind of going-downhill, and every mountain is a river that hasn't admitted it yet.

He spoke a word.

It was not a long word. It sounded like water deciding.

And the mountainside under Corvel's feet — the switchback trail, the scree, the bedrock, the bones of the hill itself — *remembered*. For one impossible moment the whole massif recalled that it had once, in the deep time before names, been seabed, and it shifted its weight like an old man turning over in sleep. The trail tilted. The scree ran. Corvel, mid-stride in his mantle of stolen light, suddenly had to attend to the undignified business of not falling down a mountain, and the four of them turned as one and put their backs to the door.

"Now," Wren breathed. "Xanthe — the words —"

But Xanthe already knew the toll. The archive beneath the drowned capital had told her; Wren's translation had confirmed it; and in the nights since, walking toward this door through a country being unwritten around them, she had done what she always did with a difficult account. She had reconciled it.

*The Hollow opens only for one who surrenders every name the guild ever gave her.*

Not just Xanthe Thackeray. Not just the guild-name stitched into her collar. Every alias, every cover, every designation, every name a handler had pinned to her like a tag on a jar of someone's childhood. The door wanted the whole inventory. It wanted her, itemized, surrendered, receipted.

"They argued about whether I should pay it," she said, mostly to herself, laying her palm flat against wood gone smooth as riverstone. "Three nights they argued. Odji said a person isn't their names. Wren cried. Brannoc offered to pay it for me, bless his enormous stupid heart, as if a door like this takes substitute payment." She laughed, and it cracked halfway through and came out closer to a sob. "As if I'd let anyone else do my job."

The door was warm under her hand. Through the iron ring she felt something pulse, slow and vast and patient, like a heart the size of a horizon.

"Vessia," she said. "If You can hear me — and I think now maybe You couldn't, not because You were silent, but because You were *swallowed* — then hear this instead. I kept Your books badly, because I kept them blind. But I kept them. And somewhere in all those columns I think I learned the one thing You were actually teaching."

She thought of the fresco in the ruined chapel: Vessia not as a giver of power but a jailer of doors. A god whose whole divinity was the space between *in* and *out*. A god who had let herself be devoured rather than let her jailers' prisoners forget the door existed.

"You weren't testing our loyalty," Xanthe said. "You were waiting to see who would open it."

She spoke the renunciation.

She gave back *Xanthe Thackeray*, and felt the shape of her mother's kitchen go dim and far away. She gave back *Thackeray of the Third Desk*, and the smell of ledger-ink and candlewax. She gave back *Sparrow*, her field name; *Ledger-Keeper*, her office; *the Quiet Hand*, the name enforcers used when they thought she couldn't hear. She gave back the cruel ones too — *Corvel's creature*, which Ivetta had spat at her on the bridge, and *traitor*, which she had earned honestly enough. Name after name, spoken aloud into the iron ring, each one lifting off her like a coat handed over at a door, and with every one she grew lighter and more terrified and more exactly, precisely *herself*, whoever that was about to turn out to be.

The last name was the hardest. It wasn't a guild-name at all.

"*Child of Vessia,*" she whispered. "That one I gave myself. Take it too. Take all of it."

The door opened inward.

* * *

Later — much later, in the reckoning and the years after — people would ask her what it had looked like, the moment the stars came back. She never found an answer that satisfied anyone, least of all herself, because the truth was that it did not look like anything so much as *an account settling*.

The Hollow opened, and behind the door was not a room.

Behind the door was the sky's own memory of this country — every dawn it had ever had, filed and folded, every constellation that had wheeled over these hills since before the first wall, every scrap of night the guild had bottled and sold and hoarded and stolen. And it had been waiting, all this time, on the far side of a locked door, the way spring waits behind winter, the way a letter waits in a drawer, the way Odji's ferry waited every morning for the first passenger without ever once doubting it would come.

The stars poured back.

There was no other verb for it. They came through the doorway like a river through a broken weir, like a congregation through doors flung open on a feast day, thousands upon thousands upon thousands, and they went *up*, streaming over the four small figures on the threshold and rising into the black breathing ceiling, and everywhere they touched, the ceiling remembered what it was supposed to be. Holes opened in the dark. The holes filled with light. Constellations snapped back into their ancient shapes like joints finding their sockets, and the country below — the looping roads, the villages with two names, the travelers arriving before they departed — felt the correction run through it like a spine straightening. Somewhere far away a road stopped looping. Somewhere a village chose, at last, its oldest name.

And Corvel, standing in the flood-path of everything he had tried to own —

He screamed. Of course he screamed. But what burned away was not flesh. It was the light — the stolen starlight peeling off him in ribbons, called home by the oldest law there is, the law every accountant knows and no thief escapes: *what was borrowed must return to its owner.* His borrowed divinity streamed upward in seven-colored threads, the shares of six dead colleagues and one devoured god, and the threads flew through the open door and did not come back. What was left standing in the pass was a thin old man in a grey coat, kneeling on a mountainside, holding nothing.

He looked up at her. His face was only a face again, and it was the most terrible thing she had ever seen, because underneath everything it was *relieved*.

"Xanthe," he said — her name, or what was left of it, since she had given hers away. "I had it. I almost—"

"You had a ledger with the numbers scratched out," she said. "That was all you ever had."

The stars finished arriving. The last of them — a small red one, low near where the southern horizon ought to be — slipped through the door and hung there a moment, as if checking the address, and then rose to join its neighbors, and the sky was *full*, obscenely, gloriously full, a sky like the ones in the oldest stories, a sky nobody alive had ever seen.

Odji sat down heavily on the doorstep and started, quietly, to laugh.

"Well," he said, wiping his eyes. "There's my navigation fixed for life."

* * *

They came down the mountain at dawn, under a sky so crowded with stars fading politely into morning that Wren kept stopping dead in the trail to argue with it. "That's not right, that's not the old charts, there are *more* — there were never this many —" and Brannoc said, "Maybe the charts were made during a robbery, girl, and nobody thought to recount," and Wren laughed until she had to sit down on a rock.

The country had come back overnight. Not gently — there would be grief in it, villages that had forgotten themselves too long, people who had lived two lives in the gap and would have to choose between them. But the roads held. Maps held. A courier passed them on the trail going the direction he'd come from, looking deeply embarrassed about the whole affair.

Near the bottom of the switchbacks they met Ivetta.

She stood aside on the trail with the true ledgers strapped to her back — the ones Xanthe had burned had been decoys, and everyone had known it, and somehow that was going to matter enormously in the days to come. She looked at the four of them, and at the sky, and at the small woman walking at their center who had no name that any ledger could find.

"The guild's done," Ivetta said. It wasn't a question. "Enforcers are scattering. Some are running. Some are—" she paused, "—some are asking what happens to people who did terrible things for a lie, when the lie dies."

"That depends," said the woman who had been Xanthe Thackeray, "on whether anyone reads the ledgers out loud."

Ivetta's jaw tightened. She shifted the strap on her shoulder. "I was going to bring them to the magistrates."

"Were you," said the woman, and smiled, tired and crooked and new. "Or were you going to bring them to a town square, and start reading at the beginning, and keep reading until someone stopped you — and then keep reading anyway?"

Ivetta looked at her for a long moment. The morning star — the small red one, stubbornly still visible — hung above them both.

"I'll need help," Ivetta said at last. "It's a lot of pages."

"Find me in a year," said the woman with no guild-name, and touched Odji's arm, and turned down the trail toward the ordinary world, where a ferry was waiting on a real river under real stars, and somewhere ahead of her a small shop of possible things stood with its back door unlocked, waiting for her to come and choose a name worth putting over the door.

Behind them, high on the mountain, the Last Hollow stood open.

No one moved to close it.
