## 20. Aftermath of Light

Wren had always imagined that the end of the world would be loud.

It was not. The morning after the stars came back was so quiet she could hear the river three streets away, and someone in the ruined quarter sweeping ash off a step with a broom that needed replacing—*swish, swish, swish*—as though the world had merely gotten very dusty overnight and somebody ought to tidy it up before breakfast.

She lay on her back in what remained of the guildhouse's counting room and watched dust motes drift through a hole in the ceiling where, until yesterday, there had been a ceiling. Through the hole: sky. Blue at the edges, going gold at the top, and scattered across it like spilled salt, *stars*, pale and stubborn in the daylight, as if they'd come home early from a long trip and hadn't yet adjusted to local hours.

"You're staring again," Odji said from somewhere near the door. He had a way of appearing in doorways without seeming to cross rooms, which Wren suspected was less magic than habit; ferryman were like that.

"There are stars out," Wren said. "In the daytime."

"There are." He came and stood beside her, looking up through the hole too. His face did the thing it always did when he looked at the sky now—a small tightening, like a man checking that his purse was still on his belt. "Been doing it all morning. Everyone has. Half the city is standing in the streets pointing up like a flock of confused pigeons."

"Is that bad?"

"It's honest," said Odji. "Which is rarer."

* * *

The guildhouse had not burned, exactly. It had *unmade* itself in pieces, the way a lie comes apart when you pull one thread and find all the others attached. Corvel's half-made divinity had gone out of it like heat out of an opened oven, and without that heat holding the walls in their agreed-upon shapes, whole wings had simply remembered they were something else—a granary, once, before the guild bought it; a chapel; a stretch of ordinary street that had been folded inside for ninety years and was now unfolding, creaking, into the light.

Wren walked its halls with the ledger-clutch under her arm—the true ledgers, the ones Xanthe had pulled from the drowned archive—and took inventory of the impossible.

Debts were moot. That was the first thing everyone understood by midmorning, though nobody had announced it. A debt recorded against a country that had briefly stopped existing was a strange kind of nothing, and the guild's whole terrible economy ran on certainty. Certainty was gone. In the entrance hall, a woman who'd sold six years of her mother's voice stood holding the receipt, weeping—not because the debt was void but because she didn't know whether the voice would come back on its own, or if she'd have to go find it, wherever voices went when they were bottled. Brannoc, who had appointed himself the guildhouse's reluctant caretaker on the grounds that "someone has to keep the doors honest," told her gently to wait a season. "Things are coming home," he said. "Stars first. The rest will follow. Things remember their way back better than people think."

Enforcers scattered like startled birds. Wren saw them go—one group at dawn, moving west along the wall road with their gray cloaks already looking like just cloaks; another at noon, arguing loudly about whether they were still employed, which was, Wren thought, the most guild question imaginable. Nobody fought them. There was no one left to give orders worth fighting over.

And Ivetta walked out through the front door carrying the ledgers.

Wren watched her go from the window of what had been the apprentices' scriptorium, back when Wren had been an apprentice and the word *apprentice* had meant something narrower than it did now. Ivetta carried the ledger-clutch strapped across her back like a sword, and she walked like a soldier too, straight-backed, unhesitating—but she paused at the gate. Just for a moment. She looked back at the broken house, and then up—at the stars, Wren realized, following her gaze. Even Ivetta was checking.

Then she went, down the hill toward the city, to do the thing she had said she would do in the flat, tired voice of someone who has lost every argument that mattered and found, to her surprise, that losing it felt cleaner than winning would have: read them aloud. Every contract. Every purchase. The ward-fall, the star-theft, the names of everyone who signed. Publicly. In the square. Where no one could unwrite it, because the country was done being unwritten.

"She'll be lynched," Brannoc said behind Wren.

"No," Wren said. She surprised herself with how sure she was. "Half this city owes the guild money they don't owe anymore. They'll want to hear exactly how much they were robbed. And the other half—" she hesitated, "—the other half will want to know if anything they sold can be bought back."

Brannoc grunted. It was his thinking sound, and his agreeing sound, and often both at once.

* * *

They gathered at dusk in the counting room, because it was the only big room left with a floor and most of a roof, and because—though nobody said this either—it was Xanthe's room. Her desk still stood in the middle of it, scarred and ink-stained, absurdly intact among the ruin, like a shipwreck survivor clinging to a piece of furniture.

Xanthe herself sat on the edge of the desk, and Wren studied her sideways while pretending to study the ledger-clutch, because Wren had spent two years learning to read things and Xanthe Thackeray was the hardest text she'd ever attempted.

There was no name above her head anymore. Wren could see the space where it should be—that faint shimmer the guild put on its own, the little tag of ownership, *property of the Hollow Guild, handle with care*. Everyone in the room had one once. Now there was just air, and underneath the air, a person. Wren kept expecting the absence to look like a wound. Mostly it looked like a woman sitting very still, listening to a silence that had finally stopped asking anything of her.

"Read me the last page," Xanthe said.

Wren blinked. "The last page of what?"

"Whatever's in your hands. You've been clutching that book like a talisman since noon. Either read it or marry it."

So Wren opened the ledger to the final written entry, and her throat went tight, because she knew whose handwriting it was. Not the forged hand that had signed decades of crimes in Xanthe's name—the real one, the careful, slightly left-slanting script Wren had spent a year learning to imitate for forgery drills and had never once managed to make look alive.

*Entry, final. For the record of whoever keeps records after me.*

*The accounts do not balance. I checked four times. What the guild stole cannot be returned at cost; what we spent cannot be recovered; what Vessia gave cannot be repaid because she is not here to receive payment. I am closing these books with a deficit.*

*I find I am not sorry about it.*

"Some accountant," Odji said softly.

"The best kind," said Xanthe. "An honest one, at the end."

Wren read it twice more. Then she asked the question that had been sitting in her chest all day, heavy as a swallowed stone. "What happens to us? The debts—I mean ours. To each other." She heard how it sounded and rushed on. "Not money-debts. The other kind. We're—we were bound together by running. If there's nothing chasing us—"

"Then we get to find out what holds," Odji said.

Brannoc cleared his throat. It was a large sound, like a door deciding to open. "I've been thinking," he said, in the tone of a man announcing a natural disaster, "about ward-work."

"Oh no," said Odji.

"*Good* ward-work," Brannoc insisted. "Doors that lock when you want them locked and open when you want them open. Wards that belong to the person behind the door instead of some magister in a tower. Small work. Honest work." He glared around the room, daring anyone to laugh. Nobody did, quite. "Someone's got to re-ward every threshold in this country after what the guild did to them. Might as well be someone who knows how the rot was done."

"That's years of work," Wren said.

"Yes," Brannoc said, with tremendous satisfaction, as if *years of work* were the finest phrase in any language.

"And I," said Odji, "have a ferry. Which is still a ferry. The river kept its name in the end—all its names, actually, it's got rather a lot now and it insists on all of them—but it's still the same water, and it still wants carrying across it." He turned the river-charm over in his fingers. He'd been doing that all day, Wren noticed. Turning it, turning it. Waiting, perhaps, to give it to whoever Xanthe turned out to be, and finding that she wasn't finished turning into anyone yet.

They all looked at Xanthe.

"I'm going to sweep this room," she said.

A pause. "The counting room," Odji said carefully. "The guild's counting room."

"It's a room," Xanthe said. "It has good light. Someone swept it every night for eleven years and I intend to find out who, and thank her." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but land adjacent to one. "After that, I don't know. I've never not known before. It's louder than I expected, having no plan. Like a room with all the furniture taken out. You hear your own footsteps."

Wren understood that completely, which was why she said, before she could stop herself: "I know what I'm going to do."

Everyone looked at her. Wren, who had spent her whole guild career perfecting the art of being overlooked, felt her ears go hot anyway.

"The archive under the capital—it's drowning, but it's not dead. And there are thousands of people like me. Apprentices. Scribes. Kids they took in because we were cheap and quiet and good at copying. Nobody's told them anything except that the world ended. Somebody should tell them it started." She gripped the ledger tighter. "Somebody should teach them to read what's true and check it twice. Somebody should—" she faltered, "—I could start a school. A small one. In the ruins part. There's light."

"A school of accounting," said Odji, delighted, "for the children of a post-miracle city."

"A school of *checking*," Wren said. "It's not the same thing at all."

"No," Xanthe said quietly, and Wren looked up to find her watching with an expression Wren couldn't parse yet but intended to spend years parsing. "It really isn't."

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted off to sleep in whatever corners claimed them, Wren climbed to the roof—or what served as a roof, a slope of collapsed slating that had arranged itself into something almost comfortable—and found Xanthere already there. Not-Xanthe. Wren was going to have to solve the name problem eventually. Tonight was not eventually.

The stars were out in force. All of them, Wren supposed, though she only knew a handful of names, and those from guild star-charts used for navigation tariffs. The sky was so crowded it looked embarrassed, like a library after a flood.

"Ivetta made the square by sundown," Wren said, sitting down at what she judged to be a respectful distance. "Odji heard. She got through the first three contracts before anyone threw anything. By the fourth, people were just... listening. She reads well. Of course she does. She probably rehearsed hating every line."

"Mm," said Xanthe.

"You could go listen tomorrow."

"I could."

"You won't, though."

"Not yet." Xanthe tipped her head back. "It's a strange thing, Wren, hearing your crimes read aloud by someone else when you only ever wrote half of them. I want to be brave enough to stand in that crowd. I'm not sure I am yet. I'm allowing myself to become sure slowly. At the speed of sweeping."

Wren nodded as though this made complete sense, which, after everything, it did.

They sat a while. Down in the city, lights were coming on—real lamplight, oil-bought, ordinary, in windows that had spent a week being uncertain whether they were windows. Somewhere a dog barked at the stars, which seemed fair enough.

"Wren," Xanthe said at last. "The toll. The door asked me for every name the guild gave me, and I paid it, and I've been trying to remember since whether 'Wren' was one of mine to lose." She turned her head. "Was it? Did I pay for you too?"

Wren's heart did something complicated. "No," she said. "You didn't buy me. You tore up my debt the night the wards fell, before any of it. Before you knew why. I checked the entry." She smiled at her knees. "You wrote it in the margin. *Paid in full, cause: she asked nicely.*"

"That doesn't sound like my professional hand."

"It absolutely does not."

Xanthe laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter, and Wren thought: *that's it, that's the sound of someone walking out of a story into a life.* She filed it away the way she filed everything, in the archive behind her ribs, in the wing marked *things worth keeping that were never ours to keep, kept anyway.*

Below them, Brannoc's snores began, seismic and reassuring. Odji's lamp glowed by the river. Somewhere across the dark city, Ivetta's voice was reading the truth to strangers by torchlight, and the truth, for once, was winning.

"Wren," Xanthe said again, softer. "Tomorrow, when you start your school—would you take a student? Older one. Slow. Bad habits. Eleven years of writing fiction in a ledger."

Wren grinned at the stars until they blurred.

"Class starts at dawn," she said. "Bring your own broom."
