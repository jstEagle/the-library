## 21. A Woman With No Guild-Name

The clerk at the census office in Millbrook had a voice like wet paper and the patience of a saint who had long since stopped expecting miracles.

"Name?" he asked, for the third time.

"I told you," she said. "I don't have one."

He looked up over his spectacles. He was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular weariness of someone whose entire career consisted of arguing with people about what they were called. "Everyone has a name."

"Not everyone." She set her hands flat on the counter. "Mine was taken as payment. It's gone. I checked." She had checked. She had stood at the foot of the mountain in the first light after the stars came back, saying every name aloud until there were no names left to say, and what remained afterward was not emptiness exactly. It was more like a room that had been swept very thoroughly. Clean floors. Open windows.

The clerk sighed and dipped his pen. "Then we'll have to enter you as something. The ledger doesn't have a column for *nobody*."

*Ledger.* The word still landed in her chest like a dropped coin, but it landed more softly these days. She was learning to let it settle instead of snatching at it.

"What did people call you before you had a name?" he asked. "Your mother must have called you something. Everyone's mother calls them something."

She thought about it. She had been so young when the guild took her that her mother's voice was less a memory than a warmth behind a closed door — you knew the fire was there from the glow under it. But there had been a word. A small word, said in exasperation and in tenderness both, usually while pulling her out of a river or away from a market stall she'd been robbing.

"*Juniper,*" she said, surprising herself. "They called me Juniper. After the bushes by the house. They're ugly and they grow anywhere and you can't kill them."

The clerk wrote it down without comment. "Surname?"

"I don't have one of those either."

"You can't be *Juniper* on its own. People will think you're a herb shop."

A woman waiting behind her snorted. Juniper turned and found herself smiling — actually smiling, the muscles rusty but willing — and heard herself say, "Fine. Juniper Crane."

It was the first thing she'd chosen for herself since she was seven years old, and it took her breath away how little it cost. One bird, one tree. Both ordinary things. Both things that flew or grew wherever they pleased and answered to no guild.

The clerk stamped the page. "Welcome to Millbrook, Juniper Crane. Next!"

* * *

Millbrook was a river town that had never been important enough for the guild to bother with, which meant it was full of things the guild would have found laughably inefficient: a baker who gave day-old bread to anyone who looked hungry and kept no record of it; a ferryman — not Odji, but cut from similar cloth — who sometimes carried people across for a story instead of a fare; an inn where the beds were lumpy and the gossip was free-flowing and nobody had ever sold a week of good weather twice.

She took a room above the chandler's shop. The chandler was a broad, cheerful widow named Marta who made candles that smelled of beeswax and honesty, and who asked exactly zero questions about why her new lodger flinched when a customer mentioned accounts.

The work found her the way work does in small towns: gradually, then all at once.

First Marta asked if she'd mind tallying the wax order, since numbers made Marta's eyes swim. Then the baker needed someone to reconcile a season of flour deliveries against what he'd actually paid. Then the miller, then the cooper, then half the street. Within two months, Juniper Crane had a reputation as the person who could make a muddle of figures sit down and behave, and she charged fair rates, and she slept well, which was new.

There was a morning early on — she remembered it clearly — when she sat in the baker's back room surrounded by his chaotic ledgers and felt the old craft rise in her hands like a tide. The columns wanted balancing. Her fingers itched for it. And underneath the itch, faint but unmistakable, was the old sweetness: the deep contentment of a ledger that balances, everything accounted for, nothing hidden.

That was how Corvel had caught her, once. That sweetness. He had known that a person who loved a balanced book could be taught to love anything, so long as the book stayed balanced.

She put down the pen. She went to the window. She watched the baker argue cheerfully with a goose in his yard, badly, and lose, and she laughed until her ribs hurt, and then she went back inside and finished the ledgers honestly, including the three sacks of flour he'd quietly given to the school and pretended not to.

Some entries deserved to stay unbalanced. She was keeping those now too. It was a different kind of accounting, and it suited her better.

* * *

Word of Corvel reached her the way all news reached Millbrook: late, garbled, and over pie.

"He's in the capital still," Odji wrote, in a letter that smelled of river and tobacco. "They built him a cell out of the old counting-house. Iron bars, but honest ones — no wards, no tricks. Brannoc checked. He says it's the first building in that city that tells the truth about itself."

Odji's letters arrived every few weeks, each one longer than the last, because Odji believed postage should carry its weight. Wren sent shorter notes, dense with exclamation marks and updates on the archive: *we catalogued another wing!! some of the bottled memories are going home to their owners. one man cried for six hours. good crying. mostly.*

But it was Ivetta who wrote about Corvel, because Ivetta was the one carrying the true ledgers through their public reading, town by town, and she passed through the capital between stages.

*I sat with him,* she wrote, in handwriting like a fence made of knives. *An hour. I don't know why. Habit, maybe.*

*He's small, Juniper. Did you know he'd gotten small? He sits in that cell with his hands folded like he's still taking meetings. He asked me if the readings were well attended. I told him yes. He nodded and said, "Good. Truth keeps poorly in private hands." As if that were his idea all along. As if he hadn't spent forty years proving otherwise.*

*Here is the strange part. I hated him for an hour, and then I pitied him for a minute, and then I went and ate an enormous dinner, because that's what you do with feelings that big. But I keep thinking: he's waiting for judgment. Not escaping it. Not arguing it. There are magistrates coming from the coast — real ones, ordinary ones, men and women who've never held a god's worth of power and wouldn't want it. He could have run. Brannoc says the cell couldn't hold him even mortal; he knows doors. He stays.*

*I don't understand it yet. Maybe you will. You always understood him better than any of us. That was the trap, I think, but also maybe the gift.*

Juniper read the letter four times by candlelight, and then she did something she had not done since the mountain: she prayed.

Not the old prayer — the kneeling, blood-and-oath kind, addressed to a jailer of doors. Just words, sent up to the returned stars through the open window. *Vessia. If you're anywhere. If being devoured left any crumb of you. What do I do with a man who waits for judgment?*

The stars said nothing, which was right and proper. Stars are not gods. They are only faraway fires, burning whether or not anyone watches, and she had learned to love them precisely for that.

But the next morning, sweeping the chandler's step, she found the answer lying where answers usually lie: in the question itself. *What do I do with a man who waits for judgment?*

Nothing. That was the answer. Nothing. Judgment was not hers anymore. It had never been hers; she had only borrowed it, the way the guild borrowed everything, luck and memories and divinity, and called the borrowing ownership.

Corvel would stand before ordinary magistrates, in an ordinary courtroom, and be weighed in an ordinary scale. It was the most humiliating fate imaginable for a man who had tried to become a god, and it was exactly what he deserved, and — this was the part that surprised her — she felt no triumph about it at all. Only a tired, clean sort of peace, like a ledger finally closed.

She swept the step. She went in and helped Marta pour candles. The wax smell filled the room, sweet and plain, and she thought: *this is what I fought for.* Not glory. This. An ordinary Tuesday, in an ordinary town, with honest smoke rising from honest wicks.

* * *

In autumn, a girl started hanging around the shop.

She was perhaps fourteen, sharp-elbowed and sharp-eyed, with flour permanently on one sleeve — the baker's daughter, or possibly niece; in Millbrook the distinction was decorative. Her name was Tansy, and she appeared first to buy a single candle, then to watch Marta work, then, eventually, to hover near the back room where Juniper did the neighborhood's accounts.

"You're the number-woman," Tansy announced one afternoon, planting herself in the doorway like a nail that intended to be driven.

"I'm a person who does numbers."

"That's what I said." Tansy came in uninvited and studied the ledgers upside down. "You write funny."

"It's an old hand. Guild-trained."

"The Hollow Guild? My gran says they stole the stars."

"They did," Juniper said. "And my friends and I stole them back."

Tansy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Gran also says you can't trust anyone who keeps perfect books."

"Your gran," Juniper said, "is a wise woman, and you may quote me."

But the girl kept coming back, and somewhere around the first frost Juniper noticed that Tansy had begun, unprompted, to straighten the baker's delivery tallies, and that she had a natural feel for it — the same itch Juniper had once felt, the love of the balance. And Juniper sat with that for a long evening, turning it over, afraid of it, because everything she had ever loved well had been turned into a weapon by someone cleverer.

Then she thought about Vessia in the fresco, and the door, and the test. The silence had never meant *do not use your gifts.* It had meant *open the door anyway.* Gifts were not the trap. Hoarding them was.

So on a grey morning with woodsmoke on the wind, she pulled a second chair up to the table in the back room and said, "Sit down, Tansy. Rule one: a number is a promise somebody made. Your whole job is finding out whether they kept it."

Tansy sat. Her eyes lit like struck flint.

Juniper began, as her own teacher had never done, with rule two: *"and when the books tell you something terrible, you believe them."*

* * *

Winter came, and with it a letter that made her sit down slowly on the stairs.

It was from the coastal magistrates — forwarded through Wren, through Odji, through channels that had apparently decided a woman with no guild-name still had standing in this one matter. *The trial of Magister Corvel and the surviving members of the Hollow Guild's inner circle will commence at midwinter,* it read, in dry legal prose. *Witnesses of record are requested. Your testimony regarding the ledger-forgery of 40 years' duration has been deemed material.*

Material. Forty years of forged entries, transactions signed in her hand that she had never written — and now the courts wanted her to stand up and say, formally, on the record: *that is not mine.*

She read the letter three times. Her hands were steady. That was the strangest part. Once, the mere suggestion of standing in front of guild power and contradicting it would have emptied her knees.

She thought about what testimony would mean. Standing in the capital, in a courtroom within sight of the counting-house cell, speaking the truth about the ledgers in front of the man who had taught her to love them crooked. It would be the last entry in the last book. After that, truly, she would be only Juniper Crane, chandler's lodger, teacher of one apprentice, owner of a door she kept unlocked.

Marta found her on the stairs with the letter and read it upside down, as was Marta's way.

"Well?" Marta said. "Are you going?"

"Yes."

"Good." Marta handed her a candle. "Take this. For the road. Beeswax burns clean — none of your tallow smudge. If you're going to testify, testify bright."

Juniper laughed, and packed, and at the bottom of her bag, wrapped in oilcloth, she placed the one object she'd carried out of her old life: a small river-charm woven of reed and red thread, given to her by a ferryman *for whoever you turn out to be.*

Whoever she turned out to be, it seemed, was a woman about to go say one true thing in a loud room. The charm fit fine in that pocket.

At the door of the coach, Tansy was waiting with her flour-dusted sleeve and her chin up. "Bring me back a law book," she demanded.

"Rule three," Juniper said, climbing aboard. "Never bring an apprentice a law book before she's earned it."

"Then I'll earn it. Hurry back."

The coach rolled east, toward the capital, toward the cell with its honest iron, toward the last column of the longest ledger she would ever close. Juniper Crane watched Millbrook shrink behind her — the baker losing to his goose, the candles burning clean in every window — and found that she was not afraid.

Whatever waited in that courtroom, it could take many things from her. Her name was already spent. Her god was already free. Her family was scattered across a country that existed again, real roads to real towns, and every one of them would take her back.

There was nothing left to steal except the truth, and she had already written it down.

She just hoped, watching the miles unwind beneath her, that Corvel was ready to hear it read aloud — because she had it on excellent authority that the reading would be thorough, the witnesses numerous, and the margins annotated in a hand he would recognize instantly as better than his own.
