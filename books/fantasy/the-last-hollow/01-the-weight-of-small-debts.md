## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

There are three rules to keeping ledgers for the Hollow Guild, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

First: write everything down. Memory is a currency, and currencies inflate.

Second: never round against the client. A debt of half a sigh is still a debt, and the Hollow collects on half-sighs with more enthusiasm than most bailiffs show for whole estates.

Third — and this is the one that took me eleven years and one very bad winter to understand — the books do not care what you feel about them. They are not moral instruments. They are mirrors with arithmetic in them. You look into a ledger long enough, you see yourself, and you had better hope you can live with the reflection.

My name is Quentin Ashgrove, and I balance impossible things for a living.

* * *

The guildhouse of Vellum & Ash sits on Cindermarrow Street like an old man slouched in a chair he refuses to be buried in. Three stories of blackened timber and soot-stained stone, windows fogged from the inside by the breath of trades that should never have been breathed on at all. On the ground floor, the brokers take appointments: a widow trading her late husband's last spoken word for a winter's firewood; a soldier selling three years of his youth to a merchant who wanted to remember being brave; a child — we don't allow children, officially — but there are always exceptions, and the exceptions always have money.

I work on the second floor, in the Counting Room, where the light comes through horn panes the color of weak tea and everything smells of tallow, ink, and old bone. My desk is oak, scarred by forty years of other men's nibs before mine ever touched it. To my left, the Day Books — the trades as they happen, recorded in the broker's hand, witnessed twice. To my right, the Master Ledgers — my hand alone, reconciled nightly against the Day Books, because trust is a lovely thing but arithmetic is better.

And above the desk, on a shelf I built myself, sit the bones.

Not decoration. Not trophies. Working tools, though no one at Vellum & Ash has ever quite admitted that out loud. Femurs mostly, and knucklebones, and one skull I've never been able to identify beyond *old, and patient, and fond of me in a distant sort of way*. I carve them — small marks, tally-scores, names where names were given freely — and when I carve them, I hear the dead.

Not the dead of the guildhouse, particularly. The dead generally. All of them, faintly, the way you hear rain on a roof without counting the drops. Most of what they say is nothing. Grief repeated until it wears smooth as riverstone. Names. Regrets shaped exactly like regrets everywhere. Occasionally something useful — a debt unpaid, a promise unkept, the location of a ring some dead man swallowed rather than surrender to his brother. The guild pays me well for those last ones. The guild does not ask how I know, and I have never volunteered that the answer is *a dead woman told me*, because the guild's official position is that the veil is a resource, and resources do not talk back.

The dead, for their part, seem to like being carved. It gives them somewhere to put their voices, I think. A place to stand while they say their piece. Everyone wants that, eventually. The living just pretend otherwise.

That evening — it was the third of Emberfall, cold enough that the ink wanted warming before it would flow — I was reconciling October's accounts, and everything was fine, and then everything was not.

* * *

Let me describe a normal reconciliation, so you understand what abnormal looks like.

The Day Books come up from the floor each morning, stacked and ribboned. Each entry carries a trade-code, a valuation, two witness signatures, and a settlement mark showing what was given and what was taken. My job is to make the Master Ledger agree. When it agrees, I initial the margin in green. When it disagrees, I flag it in red and send it down with a note, and the broker responsible either corrects the entry or comes upstairs to argue, and I win, because I am right more often than they are and because Master Corvane has made it very clear that the ledgers answer to me.

It is a good system. That is not sarcasm; I want to be clear about that, because people who meet me later tend to assume I was born cynical, and I wasn't. I believed in the system the way some men believe in the sea. It was vast, it was older than me, it had rules, and if you respected the rules it would carry you. The Hollow Guild traded in impossible things — memories, years, shadows, the last words of the dying, luck, grief measured by the ounce — and someone had to keep the count honest or the whole edifice rotted. I was proud of that. I am still, in the way you can be proud of a leg you've since lost.

So: third of Emberfall. Candle burning down its second inch. Bones murmuring overhead in their usual way — old Halric going on about his daughter, as he has every night for six years; the nameless skull humming something almost like a hymn; a new femur I'd carved only last week, belonging to a ferryman drowned off Tarn Quay, still learning how to be quiet.

Day Book 44, folio ninety-one through one-oh-six. Green, green, red — a broker named Pellith had valued a shadow-trade four drams high, sloppy, I sent it down — green, green.

Then folio one-twelve.

The entry was short. Trades often are; the impossible compresses beautifully. What caught my eye first was not what was written but what was *not* written. There was a gap in the folio sequence — no, worse than a gap. There was an entry whose ink sat wrong on the page. Every other line on that leaf had soaked in true, brown-black, settled into the paper's grain like it had grown there. This one floated. And beneath it, when I tilted the page toward the candle, I saw them: ghost-lines. The pressed shadows of strokes that had been scraped away with a knife and rewritten over.

Someone had erased a trade and entered another in its place. Badly. In a hurry. With a knife meant for apples, judging by the gouge in the margin.

Now, understand: erasures happen. Brokers make errors, entries get struck and re-entered properly, and there is a procedure — strike-through in the original hand, initials of the correcting party, date, reason. What does not happen is silent scraping. Silent scraping means someone did not want the correction seen. And there is exactly one class of person at Vellum & Ash who can touch the Day Books after they leave the floor, and that class does not include brokers like Pellith, and it does not include me.

It includes the Masters.

I sat with that for a while. The candle ticked. Above me, old Halric stopped mid-complaint, the way the dead do when they notice the living noticing something.

I got my loupe. I got the lamp close. And I read the ghost-lines under the replacement entry.

The replacement said: *One lot, miscellaneous salvage, veiled origin. Valued: 40 crowns. Settled in kind.*

The scraped-away lines beneath — fragmentary, maddening, but I have spent twenty years reading the handwriting of liars, and scraped ink holds its shape like a bruise holds the shape of the blow — said something else entirely.

I could make out: *...toll...* And: *...boundary...* And in the valuation column, where the surviving entry said forty crowns, the ghost-figure was not a figure at all. It was a unit I had never once seen used in eleven years of balancing every strange trade the guild had ever struck.

The unit was *lives*. Plural.

*Boundary-toll,* the ghost-line said, or enough of it that any reasonable man would fill in the rest, and I filled it in, sitting there in the tea-colored light with my heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. Boundary-toll. As though the veil between the worlds were a road, and someone were charging passage on it, and the passage-price were being paid in —

In what? I didn't know. That was the horror of it, at first — not the knowledge but the shape of the not-knowing, the way a ledger entry with a missing figure itches in the mind. Forty crowns is a price. Lives is a cost. I knew the difference the way I knew my own hands, and someone above me had looked at that difference and decided the books should lie about it.

The dead went quiet. All of them at once, even Halric, even the hymn-humming skull. The silence lasted perhaps ten seconds, and in those ten seconds the Counting Room felt like the inside of a held breath.

Then they all spoke at once.

I won't pretend I understood it. A hundred voices overlapping, none louder than a whisper, none distinct, a sound like wind through a field of dry grass if the grass remembered being people. It rose and fell and stopped, and afterward old Halric resumed his complaint about his daughter exactly mid-word, as though nothing had happened, and maybe for him nothing had. But I have kept bones long enough to know their weather, and I would swear on every ledger I've ever balanced that for ten seconds the dead had looked at the same page I was looking at.

Or at what used to be on it.

* * *

I did what a careful man does. I copied the folio — both versions, the lie and the bruise beneath it — into my pocket-book, in my own shorthand, which no one at Vellum & Ash reads because no one at Vellum & Ash thinks bookkeeping requires cryptography. Then I closed the Day Book, tied its ribbon, and set it aside to go down to the floor in the morning like any other night.

And then I sat there and had an argument with myself, which I will now summarize honestly.

*It could be innocent.* Clerical laziness. A Master tidying a messy entry without following procedure because procedure is for clerks. Power does that; it stops bothering with the small decencies first, because nobody dares object to the small ones.

*It could be nothing to do with you.* I reconcile. I do not investigate. Eleven years, and my signature has never been questioned, because I mind my column and let others mind theirs.

*But.* And here is where the argument turned. *But* the ledger answers to me. Those were Corvane's words, spoken over wine the day he raised my wage, and I had taken them as an honor. If the books lie and I sign them green, then the lie is mine. There is no version of careful that includes certifying a falsehood. There is only the version where I didn't look.

I had always looked. That was the entire point of me.

Above the desk, the ferryman's femur — new-carved, still learning quiet — made a sound I had not heard a bone make before. Not a word. More like the noise a man makes when he sees something fall and cannot reach it.

I told myself it was settling wood. Bones don't settle. I told myself anyway.

* * *

Master Corvane keeps rooms on the top floor, behind doors of grey oak with iron hinges, and it is understood that one does not knock on them without cause. I stood in the stairwell for a long moment that night, pocket-book flat against my ribs, weighing causes.

Here is what you should know about Guildmaster Corvane, since he will matter later: he is not a villain of the sort that appears in the cheap romances sold outside the Nightfair. He is courteous. He remembers names — yours, your mother's, your mother's debts, which is somehow worse. He has run Vellum & Ash for thirty years without a scandal reaching the street, and the street believes this is because there have been no scandals, and I believed it too, because I checked the numbers, and the numbers had always agreed.

The numbers had always agreed because I made them agree. Because the entries came to me clean.

Clean. I turned the word over that night like a coin I suspected of being clipped.

I did not knock. I want to be clear that my cowardice or prudence — history may adjudicate — happened early and completely. I went home instead, to my rooms above the chandler's on Bellows Lane, and I ate bread and cheese standing up, and I did not taste either, and around midnight I took out the pocket-book and read my own shorthand again as though it might have softened overnight.

*Toll. Boundary. Lives.*

There was a knock at my door.

At midnight. At my door, where no one knocked, because no one visited Quentin Ashgrove except the chandler's boy collecting for tallow, and the chandler's boy collected at dusk.

I put the pocket-book in my coat, which tells you something about how far my self-deception stretched even then, and opened the door on the chain.

The hallway was empty. No — not empty. Someone had been there. The wall-lamp was still swinging, very slightly, and on the mat lay a single sheet of cheap paper folded once, and when I picked it up — I should not have picked it up, a careful man sends for the watch, but the watch in this city works for men like Corvane, and some part of me already knew that — when I picked it up, the dead in my coat pocket, in the small carved token I carry everywhere, a knucklebone worn smooth as a pebble, whispered one word.

*Run.*

I didn't run. Not yet. I'm telling you this so you understand the order of my failures: first curiosity, then loyalty, then arithmetic, and only last, much too late, the running.

The note was unsigned. Seven words in a hand I did not know, hurried, the ink blotted where the writer's sleeve had dragged:

*The courier knows. Do not certify tomorrow.*

I stood in my doorway a long time, listening to the building settle and the lamplight swing itself still, and I thought about folio one-twelve, and lives, plural, and a toll charged on the boundary of worlds.

Tomorrow I would be asked to sign the October accounts green. Tomorrow, if I signed, the lie became mine, entered fair in my hand, initialed in green, permanent as anything is.

I am a careful man. Careful men weigh risks. So I weighed: my position, my wage, my eleven years of clean columns, against seven words from a stranger who knew things they shouldn't.

And underneath the weighing, quiet as mice in a wall, the bones' word kept repeating itself.

*Run. Run. Run.*

I did not sleep. And in the morning, I went to work — because I was, God help me, still a man who trusted systems — carrying the note, and the copy, and the small stubborn belief that if I simply asked the right question of the right person, the ledger-world would tilt back level, the way it always had.

The courier arrived at closing time that day, bleeding, and I learned how wrong a careful man can be.
