## 3. What the Ledgers Owe

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who has ever hidden a dead man's map inside a hollowed femur and then tried to lie down next to it like it was a pillow. The bone sat in my workbench drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, radiating a silence so loud I could hear it over my own heartbeat. The courier's ghost had gone quiet since the guildhouse emptied — not gone, I was almost sure, but folded up small, the way a moth folds itself against a wall when it wants to be mistaken for a stain.

So instead of sleeping, I did what I always do when the world frightens me: I balanced columns.

The Vellum & Ash ledger-room smells of tallow, ink, and old mistakes. Three long tables under a skylight furred with grime, forty years of entries in my own hand, and the particular hush of a room where every object has been counted twice. I lit two lamps because one felt like cowardice, opened the master ledger to the current quarter, and began reconciling trades the way I have every morning for eleven years: item, counter-item, toll, seal.

It took me until the third page to find the wrongness, which is either a testament to my thoroughness or an indictment of my eyesight.

*Boundary-toll, 40 measures, received. Counter-entry: none.*

There it was, sitting in the middle of a page like a tooth knocked into soup. A receipt with no trade attached. Forty measures of boundary-toll coming *in*, and nothing going out. In eleven years of keeping the Hollow Guild's books I had never once seen an unbalanced entry survive longer than a day. The ledgers were the one honest thing in this city. Men lied to their wives, their gods, and their own reflections, but the books at Vellum & Ash balanced or someone made them balance, and I had always believed those were the same thing.

Someone above me had erased the other half of the entry. Not smudged it, not blotted it — erased it, with a scraper and probably a steady hand, leaving only that faint ghost-gloss where the vellum's sizing had been disturbed. I know that gloss. I have made it myself, correcting my own errors, back when I still believed errors were the worst thing a ledger could hold.

Forty measures of boundary-toll. Received from whom? Paid for what? The column notation said *source withheld per instruction*, and beneath that, in a hand I did not recognize — fine, vertical, unhurried — a single word: *surplus.*

I sat with that word for a long while. Surplus implies excess. Excess implies something producing more than it needs. And boundary-toll is not grain or wool; it is the tax the veil between realms levies on everything that crosses it — years off a life here, a memory there, a name worn thin as river glass. The guild brokers it. That much I'd known, in the abstract way you know your employer deals in things you'd rather not examine too closely. Impossible goods cross the veil; the veil takes its cut; we record the cut.

But a *surplus* meant the veil was producing more toll than the crossings required. It meant the boundary itself was bleeding. And somebody was collecting the blood.

The dead murmured in the walls while I read. They always do — the guildhouse is built on older bones than anyone admits, and my particular affliction is that bones talk to me whether I've carved them or not. Usually it's inventory chatter: *owed, owed, paid, paid,* the way water drips. Tonight they were saying something else. Tonight, for the first time, they sounded *careful.* Like men whispering in a house where someone might be listening on the stairs.

I copied the orphaned entry into my private book — every carver keeps one, though we're not supposed to — and then I did the thing I am least proud of before breakfast: I scraped the ghost-gloss smooth with my thumbnail, so that even the evidence of erasure looked like nothing at all.

Then Corvane arrived.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane does not knock. He arrives the way weather arrives, announced by pressure in the ears and a dimming of the lamps, and everyone pretends this is normal. He came down into the ledger-room at the gray end of morning wearing his traveling coat, which he never wears indoors, and carrying two cups of chicory, which he has never once brought me in eleven years.

"Quentin." He set a cup by my elbow. "You look terrible."

"I didn't sleep well."

"No," he said, "I imagine you didn't," and sat down across from me without being asked, which was the second strangest thing to happen to me that week, and it was only Tuesday.

Corvane is not a large man, but rooms arrange themselves around him. He has the stillness of a heron over water, and the same relationship to patience — infinite right up until it isn't. In eleven years he has questioned me exactly twice, both times about arithmetic errors, both times ending with him saying *the books are the guild's soul, Ashgrove, keep them clean,* and me nodding like a man agreeing to anything just to be released back into the light.

He did not ask about arithmetic now.

"A courier came to the door last night," he said. "After closing. You were the last one out — you signed the night-log at the eleventh bell."

"I did."

"There's blood on the threshold stone. Quite a lot of it." He turned his cup but didn't drink. "And yet the night-log shows no visitor entered. No delivery recorded. Nothing carried out. A man bleeds to death on our steps and our meticulous keeper of records logged — nothing."

I heard myself say, "He died before I could open the door."

Which was a lie.

Not a complicated lie. Not even a good one. But it was the first lie I had ever told Corvane, possibly the first consequential lie I had ever told anyone, and I want to be precise about what that felt like, because it felt less like guilt than like *physics.* Somewhere behind me, a column tipped. I could feel it go — a faint rotational sickness, as if the whole ledger-world were a table and someone had just sawed one leg shorter. For eleven years my virtue had been structural. I told the truth the way mortar holds a wall: not because I was noble, but because everything I'd built stood on it. And now I had pulled one stone out to see if the arch would notice.

It noticed.

"You opened the door?" Corvane pressed, gently. Too gently. His gentleness has a blade in it; I've watched him use soft questions on debtors the way surgeons use probes. "You went out to him?"

"He collapsed as I was locking up. I—"

"Yes?"

I looked at my hands. My hands, which do not shake, which can carve a name into a knucklebone small enough to swallow. They were steady. My voice was the problem.

"I checked his pulse. He was dead. I called the watch-house bell, waited at a distance, and came home." Another lie, smaller, load-bearing. "I don't know who he was."

"Mm." Corvane drank his chicory at last, slowly. "Because the strange thing, Quentin — one of several strange things — is that the watch found no body."

"The watch—" 

"Found no body. Blood, yes. A scatter of waybill chits, blank ones, cheap stock. No corpse. As though a man bled out on our doorstep and then got up and walked away, or as though someone removed him between the fourth bell and dawn." He let that sit in the air between us, patient as frost. "Our door wardens saw nothing. Our roof-wardens saw nothing. Which leaves, functionally, one person who was present."

"And that's me."

"That's you." He smiled, and the smile was warm, and I have since learned to distrust warmth in that building more than I distrust cold. "I'm not accusing you, Ashgrove. I'm asking you, colleague to colleague, keeper to keeper: is there anything in the books from last night that shouldn't be there? Anything at all? An anomaly, however small?"

And here is the moment I would like to claim as courage, though it was mostly terror wearing courage's coat. Because I knew what he was really asking. He wasn't asking about anomalies. He was asking whether I had already found the erased entry — whether I was ahead of him or behind him — and every answer I could give was dangerous. Say *no* and I'm either innocent or lying. Say *yes* and I've told the guildmaster, whose vertical handwriting sits directly beneath the word *surplus*, that I know his books are bleeding.

"The books are clean," I said. Third lie of the morning, and this one I delivered smoothly, which frightened me more than the content. Smoothness is a skill, and skills are doors, and I had just unlocked one I couldn't see the far side of.

Corvane held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allows, nodded once — acceptance, or filing-for-later, impossible to tell — and rose. At the door he paused.

"Whatever came to us last night," he said, "wanted to come to *you.* Remember that. The guild remembers things, Quentin. It's rather our whole business."

Then the weather left the room, and I exhaled for the first time in an hour, and discovered that my private book was open on the table and my thumb was pressing flat against the page where I'd copied the entry, as if I could pin the truth down by weight alone.

* * *

That afternoon I closed the ledger-room door, wedged a chair under the knob out of a caution I'd have mocked in anyone else, and unwrapped the courier's map.

Bone. Of course it was bone — a scapula, broad as a dinner plate, thin as good vellum, and worked over its whole face with marks finer than anything I could produce. Whoever cut this had been a master, and masters of bone-cutting are rarer than masters of anything else, because bone resists. Bone remembers being alive. Every line you score into it, the material argues with you.

The courier's ghost woke the moment the oilcloth came off. Not loud — a voice like breath on a window, like someone reading aloud in the next room through a wall. He had a name, finally. He gave it to me the way the dead give things: sideways, in pieces, trusting me to assemble. *Ossen,* he said, or *Osven,* or possibly *Osvin* — the consonants kept shifting, the way names do when they've been dead a while. I'll call him Ossian, because he seemed to prefer it by evening, and because arguing nomenclature with a ghost is a poor use of the hours I have left.

"Read the rim first," Ossian said, when I asked where to begin. "Always the rim. The center is a trap for people who read maps like pictures."

The rim bore a sequence of small glyphs — covenant-marks, I realized, the old sigils from before the Guild, from before the Masters, from when the pact between the realms was a public thing with terms any warden could recite. I recognized three of them from guild seals, degraded, decorative, their meanings filed off generations ago like the serial numbers on repossessed goods. On the map they weren't decoration. They were instructions.

The first mark resolved itself under lamplight around midday: a circle crossed by a falling line, nested in waves, with a notch cut from its lower edge. Water. Depth. A structure missing a piece. Beneath it, in glyph-arithmetic I could actually read — thank whatever patron watches over accountants — a coordinate pair keyed to the old survey stones along the Merrow road.

Lowmere. The drowned chapel.

Everyone in the trade knows Lowmere, the way sailors know a reef. A chapel town swallowed forty years back when the boundary sagged and the river took advantage — one of those quiet disasters the guild's own reports called a "boundary adjustment," a phrase I had transcribed a dozen times without once wondering who adjusted what, or why the adjustment always seemed to benefit the same signatories. The chapel still stands under six feet of black water, its spire breaking the surface on dry summers like a drowned man's last word.

"The first keystone is there," Ossian said. "Under the floor. Under the altar. The old wardens sank them when the covenant began to fail — hid the hinges of the world so the locksmiths couldn't sell them."

"Your guild," I said. "My guild."

"Our guild," he corrected, gently, the way you correct a child who hasn't yet understood he's been robbed. "Until they started eating it."

Eating. He used that word twice more that afternoon, and each time the dead in the walls went very still, listening, the way animals go still before weather. The guild was feeding on the boundary. The surplus wasn't leakage. It was harvest. Forty measures here, forty there, skimmed off the slow death of the pact between worlds, sold at Nightfair to buyers whose names I had inked into ledgers with my own careful hand — *one year of vigor, sealed; one memory, third decade, sealed; one true name, sealed* — and I had priced them all, tallied them all, balanced them all, and called the balancing honesty.

I sat with that until the lamp burned low. Then I did something I have never done in a ledger-room: I wrote a new entry in my private book, dated it, and titled it not with a number but with a sentence.

*Entry the first: what the guild owes the boundary, unknown, collectible.*

Ossian watched me write it. Ghosts can't read, exactly — they feel intention the way you feel heat off a stove — but he made a sound like approval, a small dry click, bone acknowledging bone.

"You understand they'll come for the map," he said.

"They'll come for me."

"For both. Preferably in one package. Corvane asked you questions this morning not because he suspects you — suspicion is for subordinates. He asked because he needs to know how much you *know*, and knowledge, in our guild, is assessed the way assessors value land." He drifted toward the shuttered window, thinner in the daylight, translucent as smoke in a draft. "By what can be built on it."

I looked at the map, at the drowned chapel mark, at the coordinate pair that would take me two days' barge down the Merrow to a town that exists mostly as a warning. I thought about my desk upstairs, my forty years of clean columns, my reputation as the man whose books never lie — a reputation that had, as of this morning, become a liability, because the man whose books never lie has just discovered the books lie constantly, and liars hate witnesses with clean hands.

Fear is a strange accountant. It ran its figures while I sat there: stay, and be quietly disappeared the way Ossian was disappeared — no body, no entry, a scrape-mark on the vellum where my name used to be. Or go, and be hunted, but hunted *knowing*, which is a different animal entirely from hunted blind.

Somewhere in the walls, the dead had stopped murmuring and started waiting. I could feel them leaning, forty voices, fifty, all of them owed something, all of them patient in the specific terrible way of creditors.

"Go to Lowmere," Ossian said. It wasn't a suggestion. Ghosts don't suggest. "Raise the first keystone. After that, you won't need me to tell you what the guild owes. You'll be able to hear it."

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the map glowed faintly — covenant-bone does that near its own kind, and there was keystone-sleeping under Lowmere, and keystone-sleeping under Karrek ash, and keystone-sleeping in a vault four floors below the room where I had just lied to the most dangerous man I know.

Three marks on the rim. Three keys. And a final glyph at the map's heart that wouldn't resolve at all, that swam under my eyes like a fish under ice, that Ossian refused — *refused*, with a flatness that scared me more than Corvane's gentleness — to explain.

"Not yet," was all he said. "First Lowmere. First, the water gives something back."

I packed my tools that night. Carving knives, wax, chalk, the private book, and the femur with its hollow — empty now, the map moved to a place sewn into my coat where no searcher's fingers would think to press. I signed the night-log at the eleventh bell, as always, in my own hand, as always, and walked out of Vellum & Ash for what my careful life insisted was an ordinary errand and some older, truer ledger already knew was a defection.

Behind me, in the dark of the ledger-room, I swear the orphaned entry was waiting on its page. *Surplus,* it said. *Source withheld.*

Not for long, I promised it. Not for long.
