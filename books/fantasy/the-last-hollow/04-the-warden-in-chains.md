## 4. The Warden in Chains

They let me keep one spoon in my cell. I have thought about this a great deal over the past eleven months, because it is the kind of thing a person thinks about when the alternative is thinking about anything else.

One spoon. Not a knife — knives are contraband in the Warden's Rest, even for women who spent nine years carrying a covenant-blade and never once drew it in anger. Not a mirror, though there is nothing in this cell worth reflecting anyway. One spoon, dull-bowled, tin-handled, issued at breakfast and surrendered at supper like a small loan from an institution that trusts me exactly as far as the length of my arm.

I used to be Sera Ilvane, Senior Covenant-Warden, Third Mark, keeper of the boundary reports for the western reaches of the Hollow's reach. Now I am the woman who said no to the wrong signature on the wrong page, and the institution has decided that what I am owed is porridge and a spoon and a very long time to consider the economics of obedience.

The porridge, for the record, is terrible. The spoon is the best part of my day.

So when the door unlocked at an hour when doors do not unlock — the dead hour, the third bell after midnight, when even the rats conduct their business quietly — I did not reach for the spoon. I had already decided, somewhere in month four or five, that if they ever came for me it would not be to kill me. Killing me was free. They could have done it any morning and saved the cost of my meals. No: if they came, it was because they needed something only I could do, and that meant I had leverage, and leverage meant I should eat first.

You learn things in prison. Mostly you learn yourself, which is tedious, but occasionally you learn something useful.

Two men stood in the corridor. Guild livery under travel cloaks, which is a contradiction the guild has never bothered to resolve — they dress like servants of the covenant while serving its auctioneers. Between them, filling the doorway the way cold fills a cellar, stood Master Corvane himself.

I had seen him twice before, both times across the width of a hearing chamber, both times while he explained to me — patiently, kindly, the way you explain arithmetic to a child who keeps insisting two and two make something other than four — that the boundary reports required *adjustment*, and that adjustment was not falsification, and that falsification was such an ugly word for such tidy work.

"Sera Ilvane." He said my name the way a man reads a ledger line he has finally found use for. "You look well."

"I look like porridge," I said. "Eleven months of it."

"And yet your eyes are sharp. Good. I need them sharp." He stepped into the cell as though it were his — everything is his, that is rather the point of Corvane — and examined my walls with mild interest. Eleven months of tally marks, disguised as scratches, disguised as boredom. He saw through all three layers, of course. "Do you know why you are here, Warden?"

"Because the door was locked and now it isn't?"

A smile touched him without arriving anywhere. "Because you are the finest tracker the wardens ever produced, and because you refused, once, memorably, to lie about what you found. Both qualities are now urgently relevant." He produced a folded paper. Not a warrant. An assignment. "Three nights ago a courier died in the guildhouse of Vellum & Ash. Before dying, he passed something to a ledger-keeper there. Something stolen. Something dangerous. I want it found, and I want the thief found with it, and I want both delivered to me intact."

"And if I refuse?"

"You will serve out your term. Twenty-two more years, I believe. The porridge will remain consistent." He folded his hands. "And the truth, Warden — whatever the courier carried, whatever he died protecting — will stay buried behind Corvane's seal, where truths go to be eaten."

That was the hook, and he knew it was the hook, and he hung it in front of me anyway because he believed — reasonably, from everything he knew of me — that I would bite truth over freedom every time. It is a known weakness. It got me imprisoned in the first place.

I looked at the assignment paper. I looked at the door. I calculated, with the cold bookkeeping that eleven months of porridge had honed to a blade, exactly how much of this I could turn to my own purposes before he noticed the ledger tilting.

"I'll need my blade back," I said.

Corvane's smile arrived properly this time, and it was worse than the smile that hadn't.

*

They gave me my old office instead of my old rank. That distinction matters, and Corvane wanted me to understand it mattered, so he made a small ceremony of handing me the key. My office in the warden-house at Vellum & Ash — dust-sheeted, mouse-haunted, my case files stacked where I'd left them, my name still inked on the door plate in flaking gilt. Everything preserved. Nothing returned.

"A hunter needs her den," Corvane said. "Consider it provisionally yours. As is your freedom. Provisionally." He paused at the threshold. "The courier's name was Merrick Vale. You may begin there."

He left. I sat down at my desk, blew a year of dust off the lamp, and lit it, and felt something I had not felt in eleven months, which was competence, and hated how good it tasted.

Merrick Vale. I knew the name before I reached the second drawer. I knew it the way you know a scar's weather — it ached before the rain.

Third drawer down, case files marked **B-7 through B-9: Boundary Irregularities, Western Reaches**. I pulled B-8 because I remembered the number the way you remember a debt. Inside: a report filed six years ago under my own hand, countersigned by a superior who no longer held his post, concerning a courier licensed to run the veil-road between Karrek and the coast. Merrick Vale, licensed courier, Second Mark. Flagged once, in my files, for a curious pattern: he crossed the boundary nineteen times in a single season along routes where nothing needed carrying.

Nineteen crossings. No cargo logged worth the toll. I had written, at the bottom of that file, in my younger and more trusting hand: *Recommend interview re: purpose of repeated transits. Possible smuggling. Possible worse.*

The interview had never happened. Someone above me had closed the file with a notation I now understood better than I wished to: *Resolved administratively.*

Resolved administratively. Four words that in the guild's grammar meant *someone paid, someone looked away, and the question died quietly in a drawer.* I had accepted that notation at thirty-one years old because accepting it was easier than climbing the ladder of who signed it. And now the man at the bottom of the drawer was dead on the floor of Vellum & Ash with something dangerous pressed into a stranger's hands, and the man who had resolved him administratively wanted me to fetch the question back.

I sat with the file open for a long time. The lamp burned low. Somewhere below, the city went about its midnight business, unaware that the boundary between it and everything else was being sold off in increments by men with tidy handwriting.

Here is the thing about grief that no one tells you when they hand you the pragmatic version: it does not replace the grief. It just gives it a job. I had spent eleven months grieving my honor, my rank, my mornings without walls — clean grief, abstract grief, the kind you can fold up and put in a drawer next to the spoon. But sitting there with Merrick Vale's file open, reading my own young handwriting recommending an interview that might have kept him alive, I grieved something specific. A man I had never met, whom I had failed by filing him correctly and then letting the filing be the end of it.

*I recommend interview re: purpose of repeated transits.*

I should have climbed the ladder. I should have asked who resolved it and why, at thirty-one, when asking still cost less than silence eventually would. Instead I had initialled the closure and moved to the next file, and six years later a courier bled out on a guildhouse floor whispering words to a bone-carver, and the difference between those two facts was me.

Well. The guild had hired a bloodhound to retrieve their stolen goods. Bloodhounds are permitted to sniff along the way.

*

Corvane's briefing, delivered the following noon in a room with too many locks, was a masterwork of selective honesty. The courier had been a thief — that much he offered freely. What he stole was "guild property, sensitive, covenant-related." The ledger-keeper who received it was named Quentin Ashgrove, a bone-carver of unblemished record, twelve years' service, no debts flagged, no irregularities. "Which itself is irregular," Corvane noted dryly. "Men with unblemished records are either saints or very careful liars, and I have met few saints in accounting."

"Why tell me his whole history?" I asked. "If you want him taken, I need his habits, yes, but you're giving me his *character*. That's not hunting information. That's sympathy information."

Corvane regarded me with something adjacent to respect. "Because I want him alive, Warden, and men are brought in alive by hunters who can predict them, and men are predicted by understanding them. Ashgrove believes in systems. Ledgers, rules, entries balanced. A man like that does not steal for gain — he steals because he found an entry that offended his belief. Find the entry, and you find him standing in front of it, outraged." He smiled faintly. "It is how I would steal, were I foolish enough to steal."

"And what was the entry?"

"That," said Corvane pleasantly, "is above your provisional clearance."

Of course it was. I signed the hunting writ anyway — my name, my hand, on paper that made me the guild's instrument again, and if that tasted familiar it is because everything in my life lately tastes of signing things I will later wish I hadn't. But I signed it with my eyes open this time, which is either growth or a more sophisticated form of the same sin, and I suppose the covenant will rule on that eventually.

One condition I wrote into the writ myself, in clause language so dry no clerk would bother striking it: *Hunter retains authority to pursue ancillary lines of inquiry arising from the primary investigation.*

Ancillary lines of inquiry. In plain speech: I follow the truth wherever it goes, and the writ covers me while I do. Corvane read the clause. Corvane understood the clause — he is many things, but never careless with language. He signed it anyway, because he believed he was handing a leash to a hound, and leashes run both directions if you pull hard enough.

We would see whose neck the collar ended up on.

*

I started where all good hunts start: not with the quarry, but with the ground.

The courier had died in the ledger-room of Vellum & Ash at closing bell. I walked the room that evening with the guild's grudging permission and a clerk hovering nervously, and I read the space the way I used to read boundaries — for the places where the official account and the physical evidence disagreed. The official account said Vale collapsed of a wound sustained elsewhere, pressed nothing into anyone's hands, died of blood loss. The floor told a different story: the drag-marks of a body moved twice, once badly; a dark stain scrubbed but not scoured; and, near the ledger shelves, a faint residue of bone-dust on the flagstones, the fine white powder of carved marrow.

Bone-dust. In a room full of paper. Bone-carvers work at benches, not shelves. Which meant Quentin Ashgrove had been standing at the shelves — or kneeling — with carved bone in his hands, in the minutes after a man died in front of him.

And then there was the thing I did not write down, did not say aloud, did not put in any report: the room was *quiet* wrong. I am a warden. I have stood on thin places where the veil wears to gauze, and I know the pressure of a boundary against the skin, the way the air gets dense with attention, the sense of being observed by something patient on the other side. The ledger-room had a ghost of that pressure. Faint. Fading. Like a crowd that had been shouting and had recently, abruptly, stopped.

Or like a crowd holding its breath.

I finished my examination. I thanked the nervous clerk. I walked out into the ash-grey evening and stood on the guildhouse steps and let myself admit, at last, plainly, the sentence I had been circling since the cell door opened:

Corvane did not want the map back. Corvane wanted the map back *first* — wanted it before anyone learned what was written on it, before anyone followed it anywhere, before it became a story instead of a possession. And a man who wants a truth buried does not send a bloodhound known for digging unless he believes the leash will hold.

Merrick Vale had crossed the veil nineteen times in one season, carrying nothing, logging nothing, and someone had resolved the question administratively, and now Vale was dead and his cargo was loose, and every instinct I owned — the ones that refused to falsify reports, the ones that cost me eleven months of porridge — stood up inside me and pointed west, away from the obvious roads, toward the places a careful man with a bone map might run.

Because here is what Corvane had missed, in his elegant arithmetic of hounds and leashes: I had read my old case files before I read his briefing, and I knew something he did not know I knew. In file B-8, beneath the nineteen crossings, I had once noted the coordinates of Vale's most frequent transit point. A chapel district. Lowmere. Drowned these twenty years, half-sunk, half-forgotten — a place where the boundary ran strange and shallow, where the water remembered being land.

Whatever Vale had stolen, it began at Lowmere. And whatever Quentin Ashgrove was running from, he would run toward the first mark on that map the way water runs downhill.

I went back to my provisional office, took down my old blade from the evidence stores — they gave it back oiled and sharp, another leash made to feel like a gift — and packed a saddlebag with the economy of a woman who has lost everything once and knows precisely what she needs to lose nothing twice.

Tomorrow I would ride for Lowmere. Officially, to run the thief to ground.

Unofficially — in the clause-language of my own heart, dry and deniable and true — to reach the truth before the men who eat truths could get their teeth in it.

The last light went off the guildhouse windows as I worked. Behind the glass of the tower above, I imagined Corvane at his desk, balancing his beautiful books, certain that the hound slept at the foot of the bed.

Let him balance. Every entry comes due. I had eleven months of porridge and one dull spoon and a dead courier's unfinished business, and I intended to collect.
