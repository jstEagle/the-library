## 5. Lowmere Rising

The barge out of Vellum & Ash smelled of eels and old money, which is to say it smelled like the city itself, just with more water involved. I paid the bargeman three times his usual rate not to ask questions, which he accepted with the professional grace of a man who had spent forty years on a river that ran through a place called the Hollow Guild's territory. He asked no questions. He did, however, look at me the way men look at other men who are carrying something they shouldn't be, and I decided this was fine, because looking was free and questions were expensive.

I sat in the stern with my back against a crate of dried pike — a cargo so unremarkable it circled back around to suspicious — and held my hand in my pocket around the courier's bone.

The dead in the city had been screaming since the night the courier died. All of them at once, every voice, overlapping into a single white roar that I could not parse and could not silence, because silencing the dead is not among a bone-carver's skills. We listen. That's the whole job. The listening is supposed to have *texture* to it — one voice, then another, each with its own grain and weight, the way a ledger has entries rather than a single smeared line of ink. What I'd been hearing for two days had no entries. It was a ledger on fire.

Out here, past the last warehouses, the roar thinned. Not gone. Thinned. Like walking out of a crowd into a field where someone is still shouting, but only one someone.

The bone in my pocket warmed against my palm.

"You're doing it wrong," said the ghost of Wren Calloway, courier of the Hollow Guild, lately deceased, currently resident in a finger-bone I had carved into a whistle-shape at three in the morning because sleep had stopped being an option.

"Doing what wrong?" I said quietly, because the bargeman was ten feet away and I had a reputation to maintain as a man who talked to himself, which was bad enough, without also being a man who answered himself.

"Sitting. Breathing. Existing in a state of general panic." The voice came from the bone and from somewhere behind my left ear simultaneously, which is a sensation I recommend to no one. "You're gripping me like a talisman. I'm not a talisman. I'm a colleague."

"You're dead."

"I'm *between assignments*. There's a difference, and the difference is you." A pause, and when the voice came again it had lost some of its flippancy. "I gave you the map because you were the only person in that building who reads the ledgers honestly. Don't make me a bad judge of character, Ashgrove. I hate being wrong. It's undignified for the deceased."

I looked out at the water. The Lowmere road ran along the river's east bank, drowned thirty years ago when the chapel valley flooded — officially a dam project, unofficially, if you believed the older ledgers, something else entirely. I'd read those entries. I'd filed them under *anomalies, historical, unresolved*, which is guild-speak for *true but inconvenient*.

"How do you know about Lowmere?" I asked.

"Because I carried things there," Wren said. "Before I carried maps. You think couriers just move paper? We move *weight*, Ashgrove. Everything that crosses the veil has weight, and somebody has to haul it." The bone pulsed once, warm as a held hand. "And I know what's under that chapel, because I helped put it back under. Twice."

* * *

Lowmere announced itself by absence.

Every village we passed had its sounds — dogs, children, the particular argumentative clatter of geese — and then, as the barge slid into the flooded valley, all of it dropped away beneath the waterline. The lake lay flat as poured pewter. Where rooftops should have broken the surface, nothing did; the flood had been deep enough and long enough that even the chimneys had surrendered. Only the chapel tower still stood above the water, a grey finger at the valley's center, leaning slightly, as towers do when they've been standing in a place they weren't built for.

The bargeman brought us alongside a half-drowned boathouse whose roof made an island. He didn't offer to wait. I didn't ask him to. There is a species of man who understands that certain fares end at certain shores, and he was that species, and I tipped him anyway, because whatever else was true, he had rowed me toward a thing that frightened him, and courage deserves its entry in the book.

Then I was alone on a roof in the middle of a drowned valley with a bone whistle and a map drawn on a slab of courier's scapula, and the sun was going down, and the dead were quiet in a way that dead should never be quiet.

"It's under the chapel," I said. "That much the map makes clear. But the chapel is under the lake, and I am a carver of bones, not a diver."

"You're not diving," Wren said. "You're *calling*. That's the whole point of you, Ashgrove. Do you think it's an accident that the map came to a bone-hearer? The keystones don't come up out of the ground for shovels. They come up for voices." A rustle in my pocket, though the pocket didn't move — the ghost shifting its weight in a body it no longer had. "Take out the map. Read me the first mark. Word for word. It matters how it's phrased."

I drew the scapula out. In the dying light the carved lines stood up in shadow, and I read: *"First stone, first debt. Beneath the bell, beneath the water, beneath the name. Let the keeper speak what was owed and call it due."*

"That's not instructions," I said. "That's a collections notice."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, like the laugh had surprised them too. "Of course it is. What did you think a covenant was? It's a contract. Every contract has terms, and every term has a collector." The laughter drained away. "The old wardens wrote the keystones as clauses. Each stone holds one promise the first brokers made, and each promise can be *invoked* by anyone who carries the ledger-talent. You can't dig them up. You can only call them due and let them answer."

"And if they answer badly?"

"Then you'll wish you'd drowned quietly," Wren said, "but you won't, because I'll be coaching you the whole time. Now get in the water."

* * *

I want it noted, for the record, for whatever ledger ultimately contains my life, that I went into that lake in October, clothed, carrying a bone map wrapped in oilskin, at the instruction of a dead woman, and that I did it with only moderate complaining.

The water was cold in the way that water over old grief is cold — not the honest cold of weather but a cold with intent behind it. I waded off the boathouse roof and down a slope that had once been the chapel's front path, feeling for flagstones with my boots, and the lake closed over my chest, and then my chin, and then everything.

Underwater, the world went silver-grey and enormous. And the silence changed quality. Above, the dead had been absent. Below, they were present in such density that their presence became a kind of pressure — not words, not yet, but the way a room feels before someone speaks. The whole drowned valley was a congregation waiting for its cue.

My lungs began their negotiation. I surfaced, gasping, treading water beside the chapel tower's base, where ancient stonework rose out of the lake like the shoulder of a submerged animal.

"I can't stay under," I said. "I'm not a fish. I'm barely a swimmer."

"You don't need to stay under. You need to put your hand on the stone below the bell and speak. The speaking happens above water or below, the covenant doesn't care, it's not precious about acoustics." Wren's voice had gone focused now, stripped of performance. "Climb the tower. The belfry's above water. The bell rope's rotted through, but there are brackets in the wall. You're a carver. Your hands know stone."

My hands knew stone. It is possibly the only thing they knew better than ledgers. I climbed the drowned tower in the last light, fingers finding chisel-marks three hundred years old, and pulled myself into the belfry as the sky went the color of a bruise healing.

The bell hung above me, green with age, its rope a frayed stub. Below the bell, set into the floor of the belfry, was a stone that did not match the others — darker, denser, carved with lines that hurt slightly to look at, the way small print hurts when you already suspect what it says.

"Beneath the bell," I breathed. "Beneath the water, beneath the name. Whose name?"

"Mine," Wren said softly. "Or — the name I carried. Couriers take covenant-names when they swear in. Mine was Lowmere. I was born here, Ashgrove. Before the flood. I was six years old when the water came, and the guild told everyone it was an engineering decision." A pause, long enough that I thought the connection had failed. "Put your hand on the keystone. Say the mark. Then say what I'm about to tell you, exactly, because the covenant takes precision seriously and so do I."

I knelt. My palm met the dark stone, and it was warm — warmer than the air, warmer than any stone had a right to be at the bottom of an October dusk, warm as a sleeping animal.

"The first stone, the first debt," I recited. "Let the keeper speak what was owed."

"Now say: *the toll of Lowmere remains unpaid. Thirty years of boundary-rent, collected from the drowned and never delivered. Let the account be opened.*"

I said it. My voice cracked on *drowned* and I let it crack, because some words should break in the mouth.

The stone woke.

There is no other word for it. Heat bloomed under my palm, and light came up through the carved lines like ink reversing — the grooves filling with a pale gold glow — and the whole belfry rang, though nothing had struck the bell. The note went down through the tower, down through the water, down into the drowned streets below, and the lake *answered*.

The water moved. Not waves — movement. The entire surface of the lake, all at once, slid backward from the tower like a tide withdrawing in fast-forward, sliding down the drowned valley walls, uncovering rooftops, gable-ends, a well-head, a street sign fused with weed. In the space of a hundred heartbeats the waterline fell farther than any tide could take it, farther than any dam could drain, until the lake was a ring of shallow pools far out across the valley floor and the dead village of Lowmere stood naked in the moonlight, dripping, intact, *waiting*.

I had seen wonders. I worked for a guild that traded in impossible things; wonder was technically part of my remit. But I had never seen a world remember itself, and I stood in the belfry with my hand on a singing stone and felt tears freeze on my face and did not care.

"It's beautiful," I whispered.

"Don't say that," Wren said sharply.

"What?"

"*Don't say that.* Wonder is a door, Ashgrove. You of all people should know — attention is an invitation. You just shouted hello into a room that's been locked for thirty years." The bone in my pocket had gone cold for the first time since the courier died. Cold, and trembling, if a piece of bone can be said to tremble. "Something down there kept the accounts while the village slept. Something had to. Debts don't maintain themselves."

Below us, in the newly exposed streets of Lowmere, the water finished draining with a long, sighing gurgle.

And then, in the silence after, I heard them.

Not the congregation-pressure from underwater. Not the murmur of ordinary dead. This was one voice, or one voice made of many, rising from the deepest part of the valley — the old millrace, maybe, or wherever the chapel's crypt had settled — and it spoke with the unhurried diction of a professional who has been interrupted mid-task and intends to make that someone's problem.

"*Who opened the account?*"

My hand was still on the keystone. I felt the question travel up my arm and lodge behind my sternum.

"Wren," I said. My voice sounded very small inside the ringing belfry. "What's down there?"

The ghost took its time answering, and I understood, in that interval, why couriers swore oath-names and carried weights and died in guildhouse doorways pressing maps into strangers' hands. Because the answer, when it came, was the kind you spend your whole life outrunning.

"A warden," Wren said. "The original warden of Lowmere. The one who was supposed to keep the covenant's terms when the flood happened, and instead stayed behind with the drowning village and held the ledger open herself. She's been keeping it for thirty years. Alone. Underwater." Another pause. "She's not dead, Ashgrove. That's the worst part. The flood put her halfway across, and she's been holding the line between ever since, and she has been *waiting for someone to come collect*—"

Down in the valley, at the edge of the moonlight, something stood up out of the drained millrace.

It was shaped like a person the way a ledger is shaped like a book — correctly, formally, with all the parts in order — but water streamed off it in ribbons that fell upward, and when it turned what might have been a face toward the chapel tower, every dead voice in the valley spoke together, gently, terribly, like a clerk reading terms aloud:

"*You called the debt. Come down and settle.*"

The keystone under my hand pulsed once, gold fading to a warning ember, and Wren said, very quietly, the way people speak at funerals:

"Well. First step done. Two keystones to go — assuming we survive the collection."

Far out on the dark water, the figure began to walk toward the chapel. It did not hurry. Debtors never do; they know exactly how much time you owe *them*.
