## 6. Two Thieves on One Road

**SERA**

I had been a warden for eleven years before they took the badge, and in eleven years I had learned exactly three things about fugitives: they sleep too long, they spend too freely, and they cannot resist a warm room and a hot meal after three days of fear. Quentin Ashgrove, it turned out, was all three things at once, which should have made him easy to catch and instead made me feel like I was kicking a dog that had already been run over twice.

The inn at Threeway Cross was called the Halfway House, which was either an honest name or a very old joke, and its wine was the color of regret and tasted roughly the same. I sat in the corner with my back to the wall — habit, not paranoia, though by then the two had stopped being distinguishable — and watched the bone-carver eat stew like a man who had forgotten food existed and was making up for lost time.

He looked smaller than his file photograph. Guild records always make people look important; it's a side effect of the ink they use, which is mixed with oath-salts so that signatures bind. In person he was narrow-shouldered, ink-stained to the elbow, with the particular posture of someone who has spent years hunched over ledgers trying to make crooked numbers sit still. His hands were beautiful, though. Warden's observation: you can tell everything about a craftsman from his hands, and Ashgrove's hands moved even while he ate, worrying at the air, carving nothing.

I let him finish the stew. Then I let him start on the bread. There is no kindness in letting a mark finish his meal; it's simply that arrests go easier when the arrested party isn't hungry, and I was operating alone, without backup, against a man who could allegedly hear the dead. I wanted every advantage the world would give me, and the world was giving me very little.

When I finally crossed the room and set my hand flat on his table, he didn't startle. That was the first surprise. He looked up at me with the weary expression of a clerk who has been expecting an auditor, and said, "You're Ilvane."

"You know me?"

"Your handwriting is on half the boundary reports I've processed." He wiped his mouth. "Very disciplined loops. You dot your i's like you're pinning them down so they can't testify against you later."

That was the second surprise. Wardens are trained to be forgettable — grey cloaks, grey voices, faces you couldn't pick out of a lineup of one. But of course. He'd read my reports. Thousands of them, probably. I had spent eleven years writing documents for the guild's filing system, and somewhere in Vellum & Ash there was a man who knew me better than anyone alive, because he'd read what I wrote when I thought no one was reading.

"I'm placing you under guild arrest," I said. "For theft of guild property, flight from questioning, and the murder of a courier named Wick Thorne."

"I didn't kill him." No protest, no trembling. Just a correction, flat as a ledger entry. "And I'd rather not be arrested, if it's all the same. Sit down. The chair's free, and if you're going to ruin my evening you can at least do it at eye level."

I sat down. Later, when I tried to reconstruct why, I told myself it was tactics — better to control the environment, better to keep him talking. The truth was simpler and more embarrassing: he had said *sit down* the way a warden says it, with total unearned authority, and eleven years of service had built a groove in me that such commands rolled along like water in a channel.

"The map," I said. "Show me the map."

"Why?"

"Because I've seen it before."

**QUENTIN**

Here is what nobody tells you about being hunted: the first day, you flinch at every footstep. By the third day, you've sorted footsteps into categories — boots that don't matter, boots that might, and boots that walk with the deliberate heel-first tread of someone who has done this professionally. Hers were the third kind. I heard her come into the Halfway House twenty minutes before she sat down across from me, and I spent those twenty minutes doing arithmetic.

Option one: run. Out the back, over the sheep field, into the dark. But Sera Ilvane had tracked me across forty miles of bad road through three false names, and running only worked on people who got tired.

Option two: talk. Dangerous, but I had spent my whole life talking to things that weren't there, and a disgraced warden was at least corporeal.

Option three: show her the map.

I want to say I chose option three because I was clever. Really, I chose it because I was tired, because the dead had been murmuring in my pocket all day — Wick's ghost, patient and fading, saying *show her, show her, she signed the reports that mattered* — and because when she put her hand on my table I recognized her hands. A carver knows hands. Hers had the callus pattern of someone who had gripped a covenant-warden's staff for a decade and missed it.

She asked to see the map. She said she'd seen it before. And something in her voice made the dead go quiet, the way a courtroom goes quiet when the judge leans forward.

I took out the bone map — Wick's femur, carved fine as lace, the covenant's road etched in lines that only showed themselves under lamplight held at an angle — and laid it on the table between us like a card I wasn't sure how to play.

Sera Ilvane went pale.

Not shocked-pale. Not frightened-pale. I've seen both on debtors' faces. This was recognition-pale, the color of a woman seeing a signature she'd hoped never to see again. Her fingers hovered over the bone without touching it, the way you hover over a document you already know will ruin you.

"Where did you get this?" she whispered.

"A dying courier pressed it into my hands and died on the floor of my guildhouse. Why? What is it?"

"It's a warrant," she said. "Or it used to be. Fifty years ago, the covenant-wardens carried maps like this — keystone routes, anchor terms, the whole architecture of the pact. They were called *covenant-keys*, and there were five of them, one per warden-command, each carved from the bone of the last anchor." Her finger finally touched the edge of the femur, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "They were supposed to have been destroyed when the wardens were disbanded. I've read the destruction certificates. I filed two of them myself."

"And?"

"And I have seen this exact map before, Quentin Ashgrove. In the guild vaults. Under Corvane's seal. In a case marked *unresolved assets*." She looked up at me, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone recalculating everything. "It was listed as a duplicate. A copy. I stood in that vault six years ago and looked at its twin and signed the inventory sheet, because that was my job, because signing was my job, because I had learned by then that asking questions about unresolved assets was how wardens stop being wardens."

The dead in my pocket stirred. Wick's voice, thin as smoke: *told you. Told you she'd know.*

"The twin," I said slowly. "You're telling me the guild has another one of these."

"I'm telling you worse than that." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw — I hadn't noticed it before; warden training includes not being noticed. "A map isn't a document, carver. It's a claim. These keystones aren't stones. They're *stakes*. Pounded into the boundary when the covenant was young, and the maps are the deeds. Whoever holds the maps holds the right of reforging." Her voice dropped further. "Corvane doesn't just have a copy. Corvane has been sitting on a legal claim to remake the covenant for fifty years, and everyone who could contest it has been dying conveniently ever since."

I thought about the erased entry. *Boundary-toll, 40 measures, source withheld.* I thought about a courier bleeding out on good floorboards. I thought about the word *withheld*, which in my trade is not a neutral term; it is a wound in a page.

"That's why he killed Wick," I said.

"That's why he'll kill you," she corrected. "Wick was a courier. Couriers die. But you—" and here she did something I didn't expect. She stopped. She looked at her own hands, turned them over, looked at them like unfamiliar tools. "You balanced the ledgers. You're the only person alive who knows what the guild actually owes. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Tired," I said.

"It makes you the audit," she said. "You're not a thief, Ashgrove. You're an outstanding balance with legs."

* * *

**SERA**

We argued for two hours. I want that on the record, even though there is no record anymore except whatever this account turns out to be. We argued, properly, like professionals, and neither of us raised our voice, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was enjoying myself, which was the most alarming development of the entire night.

His position: turn ourselves in, force a public accounting, trust the guild's own charter courts. It was a naive position and he defended it beautifully, with citations, because of course he had memorized the charter. The man had internalized the rulebook so completely that he believed the rulebook loved him back.

My position: the charter courts answer to Corvane, the wardens answer to the charter courts, and I had personally watched the machinery grind up a village called Fenharrow and file the paperwork under *weather event*. I knew how the machine worked from the inside. That was the whole problem with me.

"You lied to Corvane," I pointed out. "About the courier. About the map. Your first lie in — what, thirty years? And it bought you three days of life. What do you think your second lie buys?"

"I don't want to build anything out of lies."

"Good news," I said. "Neither do I. But there's a difference between lying and refusing to hand your throat to someone who's shown you the knife. Corvane asked you gentle questions. Did you notice how gentle? That's not kindness. That's a man who already knows the answers and is checking whether you'll insult him by denying them."

He was quiet for a long time. Around us the common room emptied — the Halfway House keeps farmer's hours — and the innkeep lit fewer lamps, and the shadows came in off the moor and pooled under our table like they wanted to listen. Maybe they did. On the boundary roads, shadows often do.

"The drowned chapel at Lowmere," he said finally. "The first mark on the map. I'm going there. Not to escape. To raise the keystone."

I stared at him. "Raise it. You mean recover it."

"I mean raise it. The map says *raise*. Wick was very specific." He tapped the femur. "And before you ask how I know what a dead courier was specific about — I carve bones. I hear what's in them. It's not a skill I asked for. It's mostly a nuisance. But right now it's the only testimony in this whole rotten estate that isn't owned by the guild."

There it was. The thing I would later recognize as the moment the alliance actually formed, though we both pretended for another hour that we were still negotiating. He wasn't offering me evidence. He was offering me a witness. Eleven years I had worked cases where the truth existed only in documents, and documents could be edited — I knew, I'd been ordered to edit them. But a dead man speaking through his own carved bone couldn't be edited by anyone except the carver, and the carver was sitting across from me looking like he wished he were anywhere else.

"If I help you," I said, "I'm finished. Whatever's left of my record, my pension, my name — gone. If we fail, we die. If we succeed—" I paused, because I genuinely didn't know what waited on the other side of that clause. "If we succeed, I don't know what happens. The covenant reforges, the guild falls, and two people who spent their lives inside systems end up outside all of them, together, with nothing."

"Together?" he said. "Bold of you to assume I'm the *together* type."

"You hid a map instead of surrendering it while a guildmaster smiled at you. That's the together type. That's exactly the together type." I pulled the wine bottle toward us and poured the last of it into both cups. It was terrible. It was honestly, impressively terrible — I suspect vinegar with ambitions. "Terms," I said. "One: I don't follow orders anymore, mine or yours. We decide things out loud, like adults with poor prospects. Two: when this is done, the whole ledger goes public. Every entry. Including mine."

"Including yours?"

"*Especially* mine. You'll understand eventually. Three—" and here I hesitated, because the third term was the one that cost me. "Three: you teach me to hear them. The dead. If I'm going to burn my life down for their sake, I want to know what they actually sound like. I've spent eleven years taking other people's word for it."

Quentin Ashgrove looked at me across two cups of criminal wine, and for the first time since I'd walked in, he smiled. It was a small smile, clerk-shaped, the kind that gets filed rather than felt. But it reached his eyes, and his eyes were kinder than his face had promised.

"That's not a term," he said. "That's just Tuesday. Fair warning: most of them complain about the weather and unfinished business. It's less mystical than you're hoping."

"Everything is less mystical than you're hoping," I said. "That's what being a professional means."

We shook on it. His grip was dry and precise; mine was stronger than he expected, and I enjoyed the flicker of surprise. Outside, the moor wind worried the shutters, and somewhere far off — south, toward Lowmere, toward water — something made a sound that was not quite a bell and not quite a tide.

The dead in his pocket, I would learn later, had gone silent at that sound. All of them. Listening.

"Lowmere," Quentin said quietly. "The map says the chapel drowned in the flood year. It doesn't say what drowned *with* it."

"No," I agreed, draining my cup. "Maps never do. That's what makes them maps instead of promises."

We left before dawn, separately, and met again on the north road where the fog was thick enough to hide even a warden's conscience. But that part comes later. What matters is this: at some hour past midnight, in a bad inn at a crossroads, a bone-carver and a disgraced warden struck an alliance over wine neither of them finished, and neither of them betrayed the other, and in my long experience of the guild, of wardens, of people —

that was the rarest trade either of us had ever witnessed.
