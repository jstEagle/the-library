## 7. The Market of Impossible Things

The Nightfair opens when the moon is at its meanest — that's what the old brokers used to say, back when I still believed the guild's proverbs were wisdom rather than inventory. It appears in a different place each time: a hay meadow, a drained canal bed, once, notoriously, the courtyard of a magistrate who had been asking too many questions about trade licenses. The fair goes where it is least convenient to follow. That is not an accident. That is the business model.

We found it that night in a limestone quarry outside Karrek, half a day ahead of schedule and half a mile below the water table, which should have been impossible and was therefore exactly right. Lanterns strung on gut-string between the quarry walls. Canvas stalls in colors that didn't quite agree with each other. And everywhere, everywhere, the murmur of transactions.

I had been to the Nightfair twice before, in my ledger-keeping days, always as an auditor, always with a guild escort, always with the understanding that what happened there stayed in the columns. This time I came with a forged broker's badge, a stolen ledger-satchel, and Sera Ilvane walking beside me wearing a warden's coat she had sworn never to wear again, her jaw set like someone chewing on a very old grudge.

"Rules," she said quietly, before we joined the crowd descending the quarry road. "We don't split up. We don't buy anything. We don't touch anything we can't afford to owe."

"That last one describes most of my life lately."

"Then you'll fit right in." She adjusted the collar of the coat. "Keep your hands in your pockets. Brokers don't browse. Browsing is for people with souls left to spend."

I put my hands in my pockets. It seemed prudent.

* * *

The first stall sold years.

Not time in the abstract — I'd have understood that, or at least been able to price it. Actual years. Little stoppered gourds, each one labeled in a clerk's hand: *Nineteen, uneventful. Twenty-three, mostly grief, discounted. Thirty-one, contains a wedding.* A woman behind the counter was explaining to a young man that the wedding year cost extra because joy was dense; it took up more room in the vessel, and vessels were expensive.

I knew the accounting. God help me, I knew the accounting. I had written entries like that myself, in my neat hand, in ledgers I had believed were recording trades rather than making them possible. There is a difference, I had always told myself, between the man who keeps the books and the man who fills them. Standing in front of a shelf of bottled decades, I felt that difference collapse like wet paper.

Sera watched my face. "Breathe," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You've gone the color of the discount bin."

"I'm recalibrating," I said. "It's a professional habit."

We moved on. The next stall sold memories by weight — you brought your own, they weighed them on brass scales, and paid out according to density. A farmer was selling his father's funeral. He kept trying to explain to the buyer that he wanted to keep the part where his father laughed at the eulogy, and the buyer kept explaining, patiently, the way one explains arithmetic to a child, that laughter was part of the lot. You couldn't split a memory without spoiling both halves. Everyone learned this eventually. That was why the fair did such steady business: everyone came back to buy back something they'd sold, and the resale markup was criminal.

The next stall sold names. Just names. Your own, if you wanted a fresh start, or someone else's, if you wanted a fresh start as a different person entirely. There were bins of them, sorted alphabetically, and I confess I lingered at the H's longer than strictly necessary, because *Ashgrove* had begun to feel like a debt collection notice. But names were dearer than I could afford, even hypothetically, and besides — Sera was already moving, scanning the rows of stalls with a warden's economy, looking for the thing we'd actually come for.

"Boundary-toll," she murmured. "Corvane's ledgers showed shipments routed through the Nightfair. If the guild is trading in it, there'll be a licensed booth. Guild colors. Look for vellum."

Vellum & Ash. My own guildhouse's mark, hanging over a stall in a lawless market at the bottom of a hole in the earth. I found it near the quarry's far wall, where the lanterns burned a shade too white and the crowd thinned as though the air itself were discouraging company.

The stall was smaller than I expected. Neat. Tidy. Rows of glass vials racked in velvet like surgical instruments, each one glowing faintly with a light I recognized and had spent my whole career pretending not to recognize. It was the light of the veil. The thin place between realms has a color — a pale, restless silver, like moonlight that hasn't decided anything yet — and every vial on those racks held a measure of it.

The sign above read: **BOUNDARY-TOLL, GENUINE. BULK RATES AVAILABLE.**

A broker sat behind the counter, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers and the pleasant blankness of someone who has done a terrible job so long it has become merely a job. He looked up at our badges and smiled the smile of one colleague greeting another.

"Evening. Buying or selling?"

"Assessing," said Sera smoothly. "Regional audit."

His smile flickered — audits were nobody's favorite word — but the warden's coat did its work. "Nothing irregular here," he said. "All toll properly harvested. All provenance documented."

"Provenance," I said. My voice came out strange. "Documented how?"

He shrugged, expansive. "Standard harvest manifests. Toll comes off the boundary where it runs thin — crossings, thresholds, dying hours, that sort of thing. The veil bleeds easiest around death. Always has. We just... collect what leaks." He tapped a rack of vials. "Pure stock. None of your diluted cut with river-water and wishful thinking. This is the good stuff."

"The good stuff," Sera repeated flatly.

"Best in three counties." He leaned forward, confiding, salesman to customer, and made the mistake of assuming I was a customer. "Between us? Business has never been better. The covenant's got old and thin, see, and thin means leaky, and leaky means *harvest*. Every death near a crossing, every soul that passes over — it sheds a little light on the way through. We catch it in the vials before it dissipates. Perfectly legal under the old trade charters." He winked. "Well. Perfectly *gray*."

I looked at the vials. I looked at them for a long time.

Because here is what I understood, standing there, and what I will spend the rest of my life understanding: the courier had died at the guildhouse door. On a threshold. At a crossing. In his dying hour, his soul had passed over the boundary — and shed its light on the way through. And somewhere between his last breath and the burial, someone from my guild had come along with a rack of vials and caught it.

Every entry I had ever recorded under *boundary-toll* resolved suddenly into its true denominator. Not an abstract substance. Not a natural resource. The light off dying souls, skimmed like cream, priced by the dram. My ledgers hadn't been recording commerce. They had been itemizing the dead.

"Quentin." Sera's hand was on my arm. "Quentin. You've stopped breathing again."

"I'm fine," I said, which was a lie, and getting easier.

* * *

I told Sera I needed a moment. She gave me the look of a woman who knows precisely what a moment costs and decided to let me have this one. I drifted down the row of stalls with my hands in my pockets and my heart going like a trapped bird, and that was when I saw the second rack.

It stood apart from the main display, on a side table, half-shadowed, as though even the broker found it slightly indecent. Smaller vials. Finer work. Each one labeled not with a volume or a grade but with a *moment*.

That was the luxury trade. Anyone could buy raw toll by the measure. But connoisseurs — and the Nightfair had connoisseurs, men and women with pale eyes and soft voices who collected endings the way other people collected porcelain — connoisseurs bought specific moments. The last sight. The last regret. The final thought, decanted whole, still warm.

I read the labels because I couldn't not.

*Last snowfall witnessed, aged 61.*
*Final words to a daughter, unfinished.*
*The moment of realizing.*

And then, third from the top, in a clerk's hand I knew — I *knew*, because I had trained half the guild's junior scriveners myself, and that looping ascender on the K was mine, taught by me, signed off by me —

*Death-moments, courier Wren Calloway, lot of six. Harvested Vellum & Ash, city house. Premium grade. Died at threshold; excellent yield.*

Six vials. Six moments of a man I had held while he bled out on my floor, whose map was hidden in my coat lining not four feet from the stall where his death was being sold by the piece.

I want to tell you I did something dramatic. I want to report that I swept the rack to the floor, or invoked some clause, or at minimum knocked over a lantern. What I actually did was stand very still, in a market built on the principle that everything has a price, and discover the one commodity I could not bear to see ticketed. The broker across the way was laughing at something a customer said. The lanterns burned their too-white light. Somewhere a woman was haggling over a bottled wedding.

My ledgers. My beautiful, balanced, honest ledgers. Every column I had ever ruled, every sum I had ever checked twice — I had thought I was keeping accounts for a trading house. I had been keeping accounts for a slaughterhouse that had the courtesy to use elegant stationery. The guild didn't deal in impossible things. The guild dealt in the most possible thing there is, the one transaction every living creature must eventually complete, and it had hired me — precise, careful, trustworthy Quentin Ashgrove — to make the arithmetic look respectable.

Wren Calloway had pressed a map into my hands and died trusting the wrong building. And my guild had weighed him, graded him, and put him on a rack marked *premium*.

"Sir?" The soft-bodied broker had drifted over, drawn perhaps by the smell of a sale or the sight of a man staring at merchandise with an expression usually reserved for funerals. "Something catch your eye? The Calloway lot is exceptional. Threshold deaths carry the cleanest light. Very little struggle in the yield."

"Very little struggle," I repeated.

"Oh, aye, he went quick." The broker smiled, helpful, proud of his stock. "You can always tell. Struggling souls cloud the vial. His are clear as springwater."

Clear as springwater. I thought of Wren's hand closing over mine. I thought of *the Hollow remembers*, gasped more than spoken. I thought of the silence that had fallen over all the dead in the guildhouse at the moment of his passing, and then the roar of them, every voice at once, as though a door had blown open somewhere.

They had known. The dead had known what was coming for him, or what had become of him, and they had tried to tell me in the only language they had.

"Sir? Shall I wrap the lot?"

"No," I said. My voice was steady. I was almost proud of it. "No. I'm not buying tonight."

"Pity. Rare stock." He turned to rearrange his vials, and I turned to find Sera, and it was then that I noticed the sixth vial.

Five of the six were identical, small, neat, uniform. The sixth was different — larger, older glass, its label faded, its contents not silver but shot through with threads of gold, like light caught mid-decision. The label had been rewritten. Underneath the clerk's notation, in a different hand, hurried, scratched deep enough to score the glass:

*If anyone ever reads this — I hid it well. Tell the bone-carver. The Hollow remembers. — W.C.*

Wren had known. Dying on my threshold, feeling himself begin to pour out into whatever waited beyond, he had done the one thing a courier could do: he had marked his own cargo. One moment of his death — the truest one, the golden one, the one that mattered — he had hidden inside the harvest itself, addressed to whoever came looking.

To me. He had addressed it to me, months before I knew I would be the one standing here.

"Sera," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable, which was undignified, and I found I no longer cared. "Sera. Come look at this."

She came. She read the label. I watched her face do what mine had done — the flinch, the hardening, the recalculation of everything. Then she looked up at the broker, and her voice was the voice of a warden reading charges.

"This lot. The Calloway lot. It's evidence in an open covenant case. I'm confiscating it."

The broker's smile performed a small, desperate dance. "There are procedures—"

"There certainly are," said Sera Ilvane, who had been stripped of her rank for following procedures, and picked up the rack.

* * *

We did not run. Running draws eyes, and the Nightfair's eyes are the kind that remember faces and sell them later. We walked. Fast, purposeful, broker-and-warden walking, two professionals with somewhere official to be, until the quarry road climbed us out of the lantern-light and the fair's murmur faded behind us into ordinary night noise — wind, stone, the distant idiot confidence of crickets.

Only when we reached the moor path did Sera stop, set the rack down on a flat rock, and exhale like a woman surfacing.

"Well," she said. "Now we know."

"Now I know," I said. "You suspected. You said the reports didn't add up. I *kept the books*, Sera. I balanced those entries. I prided myself on it. Do you understand what that makes me?"

"A man who trusted his employers."

"A man who *priced*—" I stopped. My hands were shaking, and I looked at them with a kind of clinical disgust, these careful hands, these hands that had ruled ten thousand tidy lines. "Six vials. He's for sale in six pieces, and there's a *markup* on the good ones, and I audited the supply chain. I probably signed the transport manifest. I probably wrote 'premium grade' in my own hand."

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its warden's edge and found something underneath it, rougher and more human. "When I signed my first boundary adjustment, I told myself it was administrative. A rounding error. A village slipped halfway out of the world, Quentin, and I filed it under *acceptable variance* because the alternative was admitting what I'd helped do." She looked at the vials gleaming on the rock between us, five silver and one gold. "Guilt deferred is guilt with interest. I know the rate."

I crouched beside the rack. The fifth vial — the golden one, Wren's hidden moment — pulsed faintly when my fingers neared it, the way bone does when a ghost is close. And through the glass, faint as breath on a winter pane, I heard him.

Not words. Not yet. Something more like attention — the sense of a presence leaning toward me from the other side of a very thin wall, patient, hopeful, waiting to be let in.

"He hid one for me," I said. "Before he died. He hid one moment where the harvest couldn't fully take it, and he addressed it to whoever came looking. To the bone-carver." I wrapped the vial carefully and put it in my breast pocket, against the map, against the bone token I carried. "I'm going to open it. Tonight. By firelight, where the veil is quiet, with the hearing I've got. Whatever's in there — his last thought, his last message — he meant it for this exact moment."

Sera nodded slowly. Then she paused, and her expression shifted into something I hadn't seen on her before — not the warden, not the cynic, but the woman under both, looking at me with something dangerously close to gentleness.

"Quentin. Before you open it. Whatever he says — whatever the last moment holds — you need to hear it knowing it isn't your fault. Can you hold that and listen at the same time?"

I thought about lying to her. I had gotten better at it lately, and not in ways I liked.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'll listen anyway. Some entries you can't balance. You just have to read them all the way to the bottom."

We walked down off the moor toward a ruined shepherd's hut I'd marked on the way in, where there was dry stone for a fire and enough distance from the fair that its lanterns were only a smudge of dishonest light on the horizon. Sera gathered wood while I cleared a space, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the fire wouldn't say better.

I set the golden vial upright on the flat stone. Inside, the light moved — not swirling, not settling, but *waiting*, the way the dead wait, the way Wren had waited on my floor with his blood spreading and his message undelivered.

I reached for the stopper.

Behind me, Sera fed the fire, and the flames threw her shadow long across the ruin — and in that shadow, for half a heartbeat, I saw something else. A figure. Tall, robed, watching us from the doorway of the hut, gone when I turned.

The dead around the Nightfair had gone silent when we took the vials. Now, faintly, from every direction of the dark moor, they began — all together, softly, like a congregation settling into pews — to sing.
