## 8. Sera's Confession

The dead were singing.

I lay awake listening to them for the better part of an hour before I admitted it to myself, because there is a particular kind of humiliation in being a former covenant-warden who cannot tell ghost-song from wind. Wind doesn't harmonize. Wind doesn't hold its breath between verses. And wind certainly doesn't sing in the voice of a woman I once interviewed in a border office, hands folded on her lap, asking me — politely, so politely — whether anyone was coming to help her village.

I had told her a report would be filed.

A report was filed.

* * *

The fire had burned down to that stage where it looks like it's thinking about quitting. Quentin sat across from it with his knife and a knuckle of sheep bone he'd bought at the Nightfair for what he swore was sentimental value, though everything Quentin bought was ostensibly for some other reason than the real one. He carved without looking at his hands. That's how he did most things — ledgers, lies, kindnesses — as if his hands knew the work better than he wanted to admit aloud.

"You're not sleeping," he said. Not a question. The man could hear the dead through solid bone; my insomnia probably rang like a bell to him.

"Neither are you."

"The dead get chatty after midnight." He turned the bone over. "They're singing."

"I heard." I fed the fire a stick and watched it catch. "Do you know the song?"

"No. But they all know it together, which means someone taught it to all of them at once. That takes either a church or a catastrophe." He said it mildly, the way you'd note the weather. Then he looked up at me across the flames, and his eyes had that ledger-keeper's stillness in them — the patience of a man waiting for an entry to be made. "You know it."

It wasn't a question either. Quentin didn't ask questions he already had answers to; it was wasteful, and waste offended him somewhere below the level of conscious thought.

"I know it," I said.

And then, because I was tired, and the fire was low, and the dead were singing a song I'd helped put them under the ground of — I opened my mouth and let the worst thing I owned fall out of it.

* * *

"Her name was Maren Ostrey," I said. "She kept the mill at Fenharrow. You won't find Fenharrow on any current map. That's rather the point."

Quentin set down his knife. I took that as encouragement, or permission, or possibly just a man clearing space for something heavy.

"Fenharrow sat on a boundary seam. A thin place — not Last Hollow thin, nothing like that, but thin enough that the guild wanted it. There was a toll to collect there, a slow steady seepage of boundary-stuff, and the masters had decided the seepage could be widened. Widened means moved. Moved means someone signs off on an adjustment." I heard myself use the word the way the paperwork used it, flat and administrative, and hated the taste. "An adjustment, Sera Ilvane, warden second-class, reports the seam is stable enough for reallocation. One signature. One line in a ledger."

"And you signed," Quentin said quietly.

"I signed." The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark the singing swelled and settled again, patient as tide. "I want to be clear about what I'm not going to do, Ashgrove. I'm not going to tell you I was deceived. I wasn't. I read the survey. I saw the projections — the seam would drift, the village would sit half in one realm and half in the other, and everyone in it would spend the rest of their lives unable to fully touch either one. Bread you can't taste. Doors your hand passes through on bad days. Children who fade when they cry. I read all of it. My superior told me the numbers were within tolerance, and tolerance meant acceptable, and acceptable meant sign, so I signed, because I was twenty-six years old and I had passed every examination the Guild ever set and I was very good at doing what the form said."

"What happened to the village?"

"It slipped. Halfway out. Exactly like the projection said." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "That's the joke, if you want it. It worked. The adjustment was a success. The toll doubled. I got a commendation and then, six months later, a demotion — not for the adjustment, mind you. For a clerical error they invented afterward, because demoting me for the adjustment would have set a precedent, and precedents are dangerous things in a ledger-house. So they punished me for obeying and called it discipline, and I spent eleven years believing that if I just followed the rules harder, more precisely, more perfectly than anyone alive, I could earn back the thing I'd lost."

"Which was?"

I looked at him. "My signature. I wanted a version of my name that hadn't done it. That's not a thing you can be promoted back into, Ashgrove. But I didn't know that yet, so I worked."

The fire had gone down to embers and I fed it again, needing something to do with my hands that wasn't wringing them.

"Maren Ostrey came to my office twice after the adjustment. First time she asked when it would be fixed. I showed her the remediation schedule, which was fiction. Second time she didn't ask anything. She just stood there, and she was starting to go translucent at the edges — you could see the lamp behind her hand — and she thanked me. She thanked me for filing the report. And then she went home to a village that was halfway out of the world, and she sang. They all sang. It was the only thing that still worked properly, singing. Sound crosses easier than flesh. So every evening the whole village would stand in their half-real houses and sing loud enough to hear themselves in both worlds at once." My voice cracked on the last of it, and I let it, because I was tired of carrying it whole. "That's the song out there right now. The dead around this moor learned it from the living who slipped, or maybe the dead taught the living first, I've never been able to work out which. Either way, I know every verse. I filed the paperwork that put them there."

Silence, except for the fire and the far-off congregation.

Quentin didn't say *it wasn't your fault*. I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice a door held open — not the gesture itself but the absence of the slam. Most people, given a confession like mine, reach immediately for absolution, because absolution is cheap and makes the listener feel generous. Quentin Ashgrove kept ledgers. He did not give away what wasn't owed.

Instead he said: "What was the entry called? In the ledger."

I blinked. "What?"

"When you signed. Every transaction has a line-item name. What was yours?"

"Boundary reallocation, Fenharrow parish, tier-two adjustment." I recited it the way you recite anything you've said to yourself ten thousand times at three in the morning. "Why does that matter?"

"Because of what it's called." He turned the half-carved bone in his fingers. "Reallocation. Adjustment. Tolerance. Nobody in those documents ever wrote the true entry, which would read: *took the world away from four hundred people, sold the difference.* Your signature didn't cause it, Sera. Your signature recorded it. The decision was made above you, priced above you, and profited above you. You were the pen."

"The pen that could have refused."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, and I loved him a little for that, in the way you love someone who tells you the truth about a wound instead of pretending it isn't bleeding. "You could have refused. Some part of you knew it even then, or you wouldn't have spent eleven years trying to outrun it. But here's the thing I know, and I know it better than anyone breathing, because keeping accounts is all I have ever been good at." He leaned forward, and the ember-light caught the bones at his throat, the small carved ones he wore like a clerk's rosary. "There are two kinds of debts. Debts you can pay, and debts you can't pay — only rewrite. If I owe a man coin, I pay coin. But if I owe a man his mother, no amount of coin closes the entry. It sits on the book forever, accruing interest in guilt, unless someone rewrites it. Changes what the entry *is*. Turns 'debt unpaid' into 'debt carried forward, honestly declared.' It never goes away. But it stops eating you from inside, because it's finally written truthfully."

"And how does one rewrite a village?" I asked, and it came out crueler than I intended, and he took it anyway, unoffended.

"You don't rewrite it by paying," he said. "You rewrite it by telling the truth about it in the place that matters. You walked into Corvane's archive and stole the proof of what the guild does. You're walking toward the Hollow with a map that will reforge the covenant. Somewhere in the new terms, there is a line for Fenharrow. There has to be. A covenant that costs one person everything is broken — well. That's a lesson for later." Something crossed his face there, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "But when the new terms are written, you write Fenharrow into them. By name. All four hundred names, if we can get them. And then the entry reads differently. Not paid. Carried forward. Honestly declared. Yours to carry, openly, for the rest of your life — but no longer a secret debt, and secrets are where debts do their feeding."

I stared at him across the fire for a long moment.

"That's the most accountant-like act of grace I've ever heard," I said finally.

"I'm told I'm a romantic," said Quentin Ashgrove, entirely deadpan, and picked his knife back up.

* * *

We sat with it a while. The embers ticked. Out in the dark the singing thinned toward dawn, verse by verse, like a congregation filing out.

"Why now?" he asked eventually. "You could have carried it to the Hollow and buried it there. Why tell me?"

Because you keep accounts, I thought. Because you're the only person I've met in eleven years who understands that a thing undeclared isn't a thing forgiven.

"Because tomorrow we go to Karrek," I said. "Ash-fields. Shrine. Bone-hounds — guild-bred, guild-handled, and their handlers answer to wardens, and wardens answer to the same chain of command I used to sit in. When we hit that shrine, I'll be fighting the institution I spent my life serving, and I need to walk in knowing exactly what I am. Not an exonerated woman. Not a victim. A deserter with debts. It changes how you fight. You stop protecting yourself."

He considered that. "And if you die there?"

"Then write me down honestly." I managed something that was almost a smile. "Sera Ilvane. Warden, demoted. Cause: carried forward. Let whoever reads it understand exactly what they're getting."

"I'll carve it in bone," he said. "Bone keeps."

Something in the way he said it — light, throwaway, a clerk making conversation — snagged on the edge of my exhaustion, and I almost missed what was underneath it. Almost.

Because Quentin had gone still. Knife stopped mid-carving. Head tilted, the way he tilted it when the dead spoke, listening to something past the firelight, past the hills, past the ordinary seams of the night.

"Ashgrove?"

"Hush." His eyes had gone distant and wide. "One of them's talking. Not singing. Talking." A pause. His face changed — drained, then lit, then drained again, like weather moving fast over water. "The courier. He's saying—"

"What? Saying what?"

Quentin looked up at me, and in the ember-light I saw the color leave him.

"He says Karrek isn't guarded by hounds anymore. Corvane moved the second keystone three days ago." His hand closed around the bone map in his pocket, knuckles white. "He says it's being taken to the Hollow ahead of us. And he says the man carrying it—" 

The fire chose that exact moment to collapse into ash, all its heat gone at once, as if something on the moor had breathed it out.

"—is wearing my face," Quentin finished.
