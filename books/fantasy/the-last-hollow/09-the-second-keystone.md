## 9. The Second Keystone

The ash-fields of Karrek looked like someone had tried to bury the sky and given up halfway.

Grey to every horizon, grey underfoot, grey in the air until it settled on your lips like a confession you hadn't made yet. The old burn had happened generations ago — some covenant dispute, Sera said, or an accident of trade, depending on which ledger you believed — and nothing had grown since except the things that fed on what was left. Lichens the color of old teeth. Wind with a taste like struck flint.

"It gets in everything," I said, spitting for the fourth time that hour.

"Everything," Sera agreed. "Including your attitude."

"My attitude was already ruined. This is just weather."

She almost smiled. We'd been on the road eleven days since Lowmere, and I'd learned to count her smiles the way I counted entries — rare, significant, and worth more than their face value.

The courier's ghost rode in my pocket, as he always did now, folded into the sliver of his own shinbone I'd carved into a whistle-shape without quite meaning to. He didn't speak much during the day anymore. The map was eating him by inches — each keystone we woke took something, though he insisted he couldn't feel it, which was either true or the bravest lie I'd ever been told by a dead man. At night, when the wind dropped, he hummed. Old road songs. Songs for walking.

Tonight there would be no humming. Tonight we were going to steal from a shrine guarded by the guild's own hounds, and if the stories were even half-true, the hounds did not sleep.

* * *

You should understand something about bone-hounds, because I carved bones for a living and even I found them distasteful.

They are not dogs. Dogs die and stay dead, which is one of their virtues. Bone-hounds are built — ribcage lattices and jaw-hinges, knuckle-joints where hips should be, all of it stitched together with tendon-sinew and bound with a handler's voice. A living handler speaks the binding words once, and the hound holds them forever. The voice doesn't leave the construct until the handler dies or releases it. Which means every hound out there is carrying somebody's breath around inside it like a letter never delivered.

I'd heard one once, years ago, through a shipment of raw stock bound for the guild kennels. It had said *sit* in a woman's voice, over and over, at nothing, forever. I'd refused the commission. I lost the fee. I slept fine.

There were six hounds at the Karrek shrine. And when we crested the last ridge at dusk and looked down at the bowl of grey where the old temple crouched — half-swallowed by its own ashes, its bell-tower leaning like a drunk against nothing — I heard not six voices but dozens, layered and fraying, because handlers died and their bindings passed down, voice stacked on voice, generation after generation of guild wardens who had stood this post and spoken the words and gone home, or not gone home, until the hounds were less guard-dogs than graveyards with teeth.

"They'll hear us before they see us," Sera murmured beside me. She'd gone still in the way she went still before violence, which I was learning to read like weather. "Smell us after that. Ash kills scent, so we have until they taste our breath on the wind. Maybe forty paces."

"And then?"

"Then I fight magnificently and you run for the shrine."

"That's your plan? That's barely a sentence."

"It's a whole strategy. Sentences are how plans die." She checked the long knife at her hip, then the shorter one at her back, then the one in her boot, because Sera Ilvance did not believe in traveling light any more than she believed in optimism. "Quentin. Look at me."

I looked. Ash had gotten into the lines around her eyes and made her look older, or maybe just honest.

"If it goes wrong, you take the stone and go. Not 'you try to help.' Not 'we regroup.' You go. The keystones matter more than either of us, and you're the only one who can wake them." She paused. "And you're the only one who can hear what the stones say when they do."

"I'm not leaving you in a pit full of bone-hounds."

"You absolutely are. That's the job description."

"The job description I signed was ledgers."

"Yes, and look how well that's going."

* * *

We came down through the ash-drifts with the wind in our faces and the last light dying behind the ridge, and I will tell you honestly that my hands shook the entire way. Not from cold. From listening.

Because the closer we got, the louder the voices in the shrine became, and they weren't saying *sit*, and they weren't saying *hold*. They were saying sorry.

*I'm sorry, girl, I'm sorry, I never meant thirty years, I meant to come back and release you—*

*—the new binding words are wrong, I knew they were wrong, Corvane changed them and I spoke them anyway—*

*—good dog. Good dog. There's no dog in you at all and I'm sorry I ever called you that.*

I stopped at the edge of the outer wall, one hand on burnt stone, and pressed my palm flat, and let it all come through. That's the thing about my gift nobody envies: I don't get to choose what I hear. The dead speak through whatever bone is nearest, and Karrek was made of bone now — the handlers buried under the shrine floor, the hounds patrolling above them, centuries of grief packed into one grey bowl like sediment.

"Quentin." Sera's hand on my shoulder. "What do you hear?"

"They're apologizing," I said. "All of them. The handlers. They've been apologizing for years and the hounds can't answer."

Something moved behind her eyes — that flicker she got whenever the guild turned out worse than her worst memory of it, which was already considerable. "Can you use that?"

"Use it?" I stared at her. "Sera, these aren't levers. These are people."

"People who built the thing trying to kill us. I'm not saying enjoy it. I'm saying if there's a door in that wall of grief, walk through it."

She was right. That was the awful arithmetic of her, the pragmatism I kept mistaking for coldness and kept being wrong about. So I closed my eyes and put my whole attention into the nearest hound — a big one, ribs like a broken harp, circling the shrine's western door — and I listened past the apology-stack to find the oldest voice underneath, the first handler, the one whose words the others had inherited.

An old man. A patient voice. And underneath the sorrow, a single repeated instruction, worn smooth as a riverstone:

*Guard the stone until the debt comes due.*

I opened my eyes. "Sera. The original binding. It says the hounds hold the stone until the debt comes due. Until someone collects."

"Collects what?"

"A debt owed to the shrine. To the covenant." My heart was doing something unprofessional. "Karrek burned because of a boundary-trade. I've seen the entry. The guild took toll from this place and never paid the reclamation. It's in the ledgers, Sera. I've balanced that line myself. It's been sitting unpaid for two hundred years."

"So collect it."

"I'm a clerk, not a collector."

"You're a clerk the dead talk to. Close enough."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I set it down plainly because I still don't fully believe it.

While Sera drew the eastern pair of hounds away — moving through the ashdrifts low and fast and silent as a held breath, magnificent before the fighting even started — I walked to the western door of the shrine with my hands empty and my ledgers in my head, and I spoke the collection clause aloud.

It's not dramatic language. Covenant clauses never are; they're written by accountants, and accountants fear drama the way priests fear doubt. I stood in the doorway with six bone-hounds turning toward me, jaws unfolding like instruments, and I recited the entry number, the date of the Karrek boundary-trade, the amount of unpaid reclamation, and the standing penalty for arrears across two centuries of compound interest. My voice cracked twice. My knees wanted to leave without me.

But the oldest handler's voice rose through the floor, through the hounds' hollow chests, through every bone in that burnt bowl, and it said:

*Paid.*

And the hounds stopped.

Not all at once. Like a wave going out — the nearest one freezing mid-stride, jaws open, eyeless skull tilted as though hearing something very far away. Then the next. Then the two chasing Sera in the eastern drifts, their clatter stopping so abruptly she nearly fell over her own momentum. Six constructs, standing in the ash, holding two hundred years of borrowed voices.

And then, one by one, they lay down.

That sound — the collapse of six bone-lattices onto ash, gentle, deliberate, like tired men taking off their boots — I will hear it until I die. Underneath it, faint as breath, dozens of voices saying *thank you* in overlapping generations, and one voice, the oldest, saying clearly:

*Take what you came for, keeper. The post is ended.*

* * *

The shrine's interior was small and smelled of cold iron and older smoke. The keystone sat in a recess beneath the ruined altar: a wedge of pale stone shot through with veins that glowed faintly, like lightning deciding whether to happen. Smaller than I expected. All impossible things are smaller than you expect; it's how they fit into the world's cracks.

I reached for it, and the moment my fingers touched the surface, both realms leaned close. I felt the veil the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — a thinning, an attentiveness, something vast turning its face toward a very small man in a very burnt room. The stone wasn't asleep like the first one had been. The stone was waiting.

"Got it," I called, tucking it into the lead-lined case. "Sera? We can go."

Which is when I heard the crossbow.

Later, Sera would tell me she'd heard the guild party an hour earlier and chosen not to mention it, on the grounds that I'd have insisted on leaving, and she judged the odds better with surprise on our side. I have forgiven her. Mostly. What actually happened was this: three guild enforcers came over the southern wall with the efficient silence of professionals, and Sera met them in the open ash between the shrine and the drifts, and I watched her fight the way you watch weather — awed, terrified, and aware of your total irrelevance to the outcome.

She fought magnificently. That was the plan, and she honored it. Two enforcers went down inside ten seconds, one disarmed and unconscious, one bleeding and reconsidering his career. But the third was good — guild-trained, patient, willing to trade wounds for position — and he got his blade into her below the ribs while she was finishing his colleague.

I screamed her name. It did nothing useful. Screams rarely balance anything.

What did something useful was stranger: the fallen enforcer's sword-belt buckle, bone-handled, guild-issue, chose that moment to become a mouthpiece for every handler ever buried at Karrek, and the dead roared through it — not words, just fury, centuries of it, the accumulated outrage of people who had served faithfully and been spent like currency — and the third enforcer faltered. One heartbeat. Half of one.

Sera put her short blade through the gap in his armor and he sat down in the ash and did not get up.

Then she sat down too, and I ran to her across that terrible grey field faster than I have ever moved for any reason in my careful life.

* * *

The wound was deep but wrong-sided — it had slid along the rib instead of finding the soft places beneath, which the universe apparently still occasionally granted as mercy. I bound it with strips of her own cloak and the clean linen from my pack, pressing hard while she swore at me in at least four dialects, and the blood came through the first bandage and most of the second before it slowed.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still. This is me holding still."

"This is you narrating. Holding still is quieter."

She laughed, and winced, and laughed again, and I realized I'd never heard her laugh before, and hated that it took blood to buy it.

We made camp in the shrine itself, because the hounds lay around it like a dismantled fence and I judged — correctly — that no other guild patrol would approach a site where six bone-constructs had lain down in plain view. Something had ended here. Even the living could smell it.

I built a small fire in the altar's shadow. Sera lay propped against the wall, grey-faced under the ash-grey, watching me feed sticks to the flames. The second keystone sat in its case between us, pulsing its slow uncertain light, and the courier's ghost hummed one bar of a road song and stopped himself, as if embarrassed by hope.

"You collected a debt tonight," Sera said at last. "Two hundred years of it. With a speech."

"With an entry. There's a difference."

"There really isn't, Quentin. That's what Corvane has never understood, and what you keep not believing about yourself." She shifted, hissed, settled. "The ledgers aren't the guild's power. They're the covenant's memory. And tonight the memory chose you over the masters."

I didn't answer. Through the shrine floor, faintly, the dead handlers were singing — a working song, something old, wordless in the way dreams are wordless — and I let it wash over me and thought about what she'd said and was too tired and too frightened and too grateful to examine it properly.

Her eyes were closing. Blood loss makes honest people of us all. Just before sleep took her, she said, quietly, without opening them:

"Don't leave before I wake. Whatever happens. That's not the job description. That's just — the request."

"I'll be here," I said. "I keep entries."

She smiled, asleep or nearly, and I sat by the fire keeping watch over the second keystone and the first person I had ever chosen over a system.

Near dawn, the courier's ghost spoke, urgent and thin, and I heard in his fading voice the thing that ended our rest:

*Quentin — the wind changed. There's a summons riding it. Guild seal, warden's name.* A pause, and then, gently, the way you deliver bad news to someone you love: *It's for Sera. Corvane wants her home.*
