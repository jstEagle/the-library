## 10. Corvane's Offer

They came for me at dawn, which was flattering. In my experience, guild summons arrive either at dawn or at midnight, and the hour tells you everything about how they intend to use you. Midnight means interrogation. Dawn means opportunity — the kind that arrives wrapped in silk with a knife sewn into the lining.

The messenger was a boy of maybe nineteen, wearing Vellum & Ash grey and a nervousness he hadn't yet learned to hide. He wouldn't meet my eyes as I dressed. He'd been told what I'd done, or what they said I'd done, and he was afraid the shame might be contagious.

"Guildmaster's compliments," he said to a point roughly six inches left of my face.

"His compliments," I repeated. "That must have cost him something."

The boy blinked. I let it go. Cruelty to messengers is the habit of people who can't reach the people who sent them, and I was trying very hard not to be that person anymore.

They walked me through the guildhouse at Vellum & Ash like a prisoner being shown her own gallows — past the counting floors where clerks bent over ledgers of impossible trades, past the vault door I had once held credentials for, past the long gallery where the portraits of dead masters watched me with the particular smugness of oil paint. My boots echoed. Everyone else's didn't. That's a trick of the building, or of my nerves; I've never decided which.

Corvane received me in his study at the top of the house.

I had met Guildmaster Corvane perhaps a dozen times before my disgrace, always across a table, always in daylight, always with witnesses. He was not what people expected when they heard *guildmaster of Vellum & Ash*. People expected menace — a big man with a scar and a voice like gravel. Corvane was slight, silver-templed, with reading spectacles he wore pushed up on his forehead like an affectation, and hands that were soft in the way of a man who has never once carried anything heavier than a pen. His voice was mild. His eyes were the color of ledger ink, and about as warm.

"Warden Ilvane." He gestured to a chair. "Or may I say Sera? We're among colleagues."

"We were never colleagues, Guildmaster. You signed things. I enforced them."

"An elegant division of labor." He smiled as though I'd paid him a compliment. On the desk between us lay a folder — thin, official, sealed with grey wax. I knew that seal. It was the one they used for reinstatements.

I did not sit. He sat anyway, unhurried, and poured two cups of tea from a pot that had been waiting, steaming, which meant he'd known exactly how long I'd take to walk from my cell to his study. That kind of precision should have frightened me more than it did. What frightened me, actually, was that part of me wanted the tea.

"You're wondering why you're here," he said.

"I'm wondering why you released me three days ago instead of leaving me in the cells where your own tribunal put me. Everything since has just been decoration."

"Direct. Good. I remember that about you." He folded his soft hands on the folder. "You refused to falsify the Merrowfield boundary reports. You told the tribunal the numbers were wrong. You were demoted rather than discharged because the tribunal understood — as I understand — that you weren't wronging the guild. You were protecting it. From its own sloppiness."

"The numbers were wrong because the boundary was dying, and your reports said otherwise for six consecutive quarters."

"And now you believe a courier died carrying a map that proves it." He opened the folder with one finger. Inside: papers. Copies of my old case files. The courier's name — Tam Vessett — flagged in red ink, and beside it, in Corvane's neat hand, a single word: *recovered?*

My stomach dropped with a professional's precision. Not fear, exactly. Inventory. He was telling me what he knew, and every item was a hook.

"A bone-carver named Quentin Ashgrove took something off that man's body before we arrived," Corvane continued, conversationally, as though discussing grain tariffs. "Ashgrove has fled south. Toward Lowmere, we believe. Toward the drowned chapel." He looked up at me over the spectacles he still hadn't put on. "I want him found. I want the map recovered intact. And I would prefer — genuinely prefer — that Ashgrove survive the encounter. Dead men make poor witnesses and worse accountants."

"And you're asking me because—"

"Because you are the best tracker this guild has produced in thirty years, and because you spent four years mapping the covenant wards along the southern reach, and because—" here he paused, and the mildness in his voice acquired an edge so fine I almost missed it, "—because you are currently a disgraced warden living on guild charity in a cell the size of a coffin, and I am offering to un-ring that bell."

He slid the folder across the desk. Full reinstatement. Senior rank. Her case files expunged. A salary figure that made my eyebrows move despite myself.

I didn't touch it. "And if I decline?"

"Then nothing happens to you today." He said it gently, and that was the most frightening thing he'd said all morning. "You'll be escorted back to comfortable quarters. You'll think about it. I'm told thinking is something you do well."

There it was. No threat, no deadline, no knife in the silk. Just time, and the knowledge that time in a guild cell does its own work, slowly, the way water works on stone. He wasn't going to threaten me into hunting Quentin Ashgrove. He was going to make me *want* to.

Which meant, I thought, watching him pour my untouched tea out and set the cup aside without comment, that whatever was on that map mattered enough to be patient about.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't hurt the way you expect. You imagine it will feel like falling. It doesn't. It feels like being filed away — like becoming an entry in someone else's ledger, marked *closed*, and discovering that the world agrees. For eight months in that cell I had been no one's problem, no one's colleague, no one's warden. I had been a closed account.

So yes. When Corvane slid that folder across the desk, some traitorous corner of me reached for it. I could feel the shape of the life it offered: the gallery again, the credentials again, my name spoken in rooms where names meant something. I had built that life once, plank by plank, report by honest report, and I had loved it — loved the order of it, the sense that the veil between realms was a thing a person could stand between and hold, the way you hold a door for strangers.

That was the trap, of course. Not the salary. The belonging.

Because I remembered Tam Vessett.

He'd been a junior courier in my last year of active service, all elbows and bad jokes, forever complaining that the southern route gave him nosebleeds. I'd signed his route clearances myself. And eleven days ago somebody had pulled his body out of an alley behind the guildhouse with his ribs caved in and a look on his face I recognized from too many boundary incidents — the look of a man whose last sight had been something the world wasn't shaped to contain.

His name sat in Corvane's folder with a question mark after *recovered*. As if Tam himself were a tradeable good. As if his death were an asset awaiting audit.

* * *

"Tell me something, Warden," Corvane said, and now he finally put the spectacles on, which somehow made his eyes worse — smaller, sharper, framed. "Do you know what the covenant actually *is*? Not the catechism. The arithmetic."

"The covenant holds the veil," I said. "It was forged at the Last Hollow to keep the realms from grinding against each other. Wardens enforce it. That's the whole of my education."

"That's the whole of the education we give wardens, yes." He rose and went to the window. Below us, the city went about its morning, smoke rising straight in the still air. "Let me tell you the rest, since you've earned it. You of all people — you read the boundary gauges honestly. You saw the readings decline. Did you ever ask yourself why the strong realm bleeds into the weak one at all? Why the veil doesn't simply seal?"

"It's not a wall. It's a wound that healed crooked."

"A fair image. Now consider: a wound that heals crooked pulls. Every day, vitality crosses from our side to the other — life, luck, memory, years. The far realm drinks it. Our fields are a little barer, our children a little fewer, our winters a little longer, and everyone accepts this as the natural tax of existence." He turned. "But it isn't natural. It's a *term*. The covenant starves the strong realm to feed the weak one. Deliberately. Perpetually. Written into the original pact by people who believed balance required sacrifice — someone's sacrifice, always, forever."

I kept my face still. "And the guild?"

"The guild harvests the surplus." He said it the way a priest says *amen*. "What leaks through anyway. What pools at the thin places. Memory. Years. Names. The toll that would otherwise rot in the ditches between worlds. We gather it, we price it, we sell it — and every coin of it goes to keep the machinery running. The Nightfair. The brokerage. The whole edifice. We are not parasites on the covenant, Warden. We are its *gleaners*. Without us, the surplus would feed nothing. It would simply bleed away."

"Into whose pockets?" I asked.

He smiled, unoffended. "You've been in a cell too long. Cynicism is just grief that's learned to count."

"Answer the question."

"The answer depends entirely on whether you take the folder." He came back to the desk and stood over it, and for a moment — just a moment — the mildness fell away, and I saw the thing underneath, the way you see bedrock through thin soil. "A man named Ashgrove is carrying a map of the broken covenant toward keystones that, if gathered, will reforge the pact under its original terms. Do you understand what that means? Original terms, Wera. Full tithe. The strong realm gives until it kneels. Every harvest halved. Every winter doubled. Millions of lives diminished so that a document written by dead idealists can feel righteous about itself."

"Or the alternative," I said quietly, "is that the guild keeps skimming the boundary until there's no boundary left to skim. I've seen what happens to villages that slip halfway out of the world, Guildmaster. I signed the report on one."

Something crossed his face — fast, gone before I could name it. Recognition, maybe. Or satisfaction.

"Yes," he said softly. "Karrek Fen. Your signature is in the file. Which brings me to my final point, and I offer it without cruelty." He leaned forward. "You obeyed, Warden. When it mattered, you signed. Whatever you've told yourself in that cell about conscience and refusal — when the choice was between your career and the guild's convenience, you chose the guild. I don't hold it against you. It makes you honest about what people are. People protect what's theirs. The covenant asks them not to. The guild merely asks them to be paid fairly for what they'd surrender anyway."

He let that sit. Then, gently: "Bring me Ashgrove and the map. Reforge nothing. And the file closes, the name clears, and you spend the rest of your career holding doors again — real ones, ones that matter."

I looked at the folder. Grey wax, senior rank, my name spelled correctly.

And I thought about Tam Vessett's face. About Karrek Fen, and the half-gone village I had helped push another inch out of the world with a signature and a steady hand. About a bone-carver with careful fingers who had looked at me across a crossroads inn and seen — not a warden, not a disgrace, just a tired professional doing her job, and had decided that was worth trusting anyway.

Corvane had built his offer like he built everything: precisely, patiently, out of true materials. That was the horror of it. Nothing he'd said was a lie. The covenant did demand sacrifice. The guild did glean real surplus. The temptation wasn't the salary — it was the *reasonableness*, the sense that a sensible person would sign.

I had signed reasonable documents before. I knew where they ended up.

"Warden?" he prompted.

I said nothing.

I stood there in the morning light of his study and I said absolutely nothing, and I watched him watch me, and I understood — with the clarity that only arrives too late or exactly on time — that silence was itself an answer. That every second I didn't refuse him aloud, he would log as hesitation, and hesitation, in his arithmetic, was negotiable. That my silence was already an entry in his ledger, and entries in Corvane's ledger were never neutral. They accrued.

Say yes, and I damned Quentin Ashgrove. Say no, and I walked back into a cell with my defiance noted and my usefulness ended. Say nothing, and I stayed — in his accounting — a piece on the board, movable, for sale, merely awaiting the right price.

So I said nothing. And in saying nothing, I damned myself — gladly, though I didn't yet know the word for it.

"Escort Warden Ilvane back to her quarters," Corvane said to the doorway, mild as milk. Then, as they came for me, he added, almost kindly: "Take your time thinking, Sera. But do finish thinking soon. Mr. Ashgrove is making progress faster than I'd like, and progress, in my experience, is a debt that comes due."

They walked me down through the gallery, past the painted dead masters, past the vault door. And somewhere below us — three floors down, or a hundred miles south, distance being a flexible thing near the thin places — I imagined I could hear a drowned chapel exhaling, and water drawing back from stones that had waited a long time to be lifted.

By nightfall I had stolen a horse, a warding-kit, and a copy of my own old case files from the records room, and I was riding south with a reinstatement folder I intended to return unread.

Corvane thought he'd offered me a price.

He'd actually shown me the exits.
