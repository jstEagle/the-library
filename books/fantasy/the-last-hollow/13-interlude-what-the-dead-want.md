## 13. Interlude: What the Dead Want

The ruins had no name that Sera knew, and the dead who lived there had forgotten theirs, which struck me as the fairest arrangement I'd heard of in months.

We made camp in what had once been a tithe-barn — you could tell by the tithe-slots in the wall, narrow stone mouths where farmers had posted grain they owed and never saw again. There was a symmetry there I chose not to examine too closely, given my profession. A roof held over maybe half its beams. A hearth of old fieldstone that Sera rebuilt with the efficiency of someone who'd slept badly in a hundred worse places. She said almost nothing while she did it, which I'd learned was her way of saying she trusted me not to die if left unsupervised.

Three keystones sat wrapped in wool at the bottom of my pack, and even through the wool and the leather and the canvas they hummed like bees deciding whether to be angry. I'd taken to sleeping with them an arm's length away. Not far enough to be safe. Far enough to pretend.

I built the fire small. Small fires are a habit of careful men, and whatever else the last weeks had made me, they hadn't made me careless yet.

"I'm going to do some work," I told Sera.

She looked up from cleaning her blade — a thing she did every night whether it needed it or not, which I understood was less maintenance than prayer. "What kind of work?"

"The kind you'll want to sit down for."

* * *

Here is what people get wrong about bone-carving, when they think about it at all, which is rarely: they imagine I carve bones the way a carpenter carves wood, imposing shape on material. It's closer to tuning. Every bone remembers the body it belonged to, the way a bell remembers being rung. Most of the time the memory sits quiet, a hum below hearing. But if you cut the right line in the right place — a groove along the grain of what the bone witnessed — you open it. You give the memory somewhere to stand.

My kit came out onto a flat stone by the fire: knives, rasps, a dish of oil, and the small inventory of the dead I carry everywhere. A knucklebone from a Vellum & Ash clerk named Odell, who'd spent forty years misfiling entries out of kindness and died apologizing. A vertebra from a drowned girl whose name I never learned but whose humming I knew better than my own mother's voice. A rib sliver from a guild courier — no, not the courier; another one, older, who'd taught me the river routes I'd used to flee the city, dead three years and still giving directions.

And in my breast pocket, wrapped in silk because it deserved better than leather: the courier's finger bone. The one he'd pressed into my hand along with the map, in the last ten seconds of a life spent running.

"You're going to give him a body," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"A borrowed one. For a night." I set the silk on my knee. "He's been fading since Ghost Country. The map's eating him — he made it out of himself, and every mile we walk toward the Hollow, it reads him a little thinner. By the time we arrive there may not be enough of him left to tell us what we need to know."

"So this is extraction."

"This is courtesy," I said. "Extraction is what happens after. He has things to tell us either way. I'd rather he told us them as a guest."

Sera put her blade down. That was her version of sitting down for something. "How does it work?"

"Badly, then well, then badly again. Like most honest transactions." I took up the smallest knife. "I carve a token for each of them. A little standing figure, jointed at the shoulders. The bone becomes a door instead of just a memory. They can wear it for a night — speak with a throat, hold a cup, feel the fire. In exchange they owe the token back at dawn. Everyone signs. Well. Everyone agrees. The dead don't sign; signatures imply doubt."

"And the cost? There's always a cost. You've taught me that much."

I turned the courier's finger bone over in the firelight. "The cost is that they remember having hands. And then they lose them again at dawn. Some of them refuse for exactly that reason." I looked up. "Odell always says yes anyway. Kindness is a habit that survives death, apparently. It certainly survived his filing system."

* * *

I worked until the moon cleared the broken rafters.

Carving tokens is slow, meditative labor, and it gives the dead room to talk, so I'll admit the barn wasn't silent. The drowned girl hummed while I carved her token and critiqued my proportions. Odell narrated his own carving the way he'd once narrated other people's paperwork — *left shoulder slightly proud, Master Ashgrove, if I may* — and I let him, because correcting him would have been crueler than the flaw.

But it was the courier I saved for last, and when I finally picked up his bone, everything else went quiet. Even the keystone-hum in my pack settled, as if the stones were listening too. Maybe they were. I've stopped assuming the stones aren't listening.

His name was Merrick Stave. I'd known it since Lowmere — he'd told me in the barge dark, coaching my fingers through the first keystone's raising, patient as a schoolmaster with a student who kept nearly drowning them both. But knowing a name and knowing a man are different trades entirely.

"Are you sure?" I asked the bone. Standard practice. Consent matters more from the dead, not less; they've had longer to think about what they want.

The answer came the way it always comes — not sound, but pressure, meaning arriving behind my eyes like weather. *Yes. Before I'm too thin to be anyone.*

So I carved.

It took two hours, and I won't describe every pass of the knife, because some work should stay between the craftsman and the material. But I'll say this: his bone was worn smooth on one side from years of being carried against another object — the map-case, I realized, the map he'd rendered out of his own marrow — and I carved around that wear rather than through it. Some histories deserve to remain legible.

When the token stood finished on the flat stone — six inches tall, jointed, hollow-eyed, wearing the suggestion of a courier's travel cloak in three economical cuts — the fire guttered sideways though there was no wind. The hum in my pack rose and held a single note, like a choir finding pitch.

Then the token breathed.

I don't mean it moved. Movement is easy; any ghost with grievances can shove a chair. I mean it drew air, expanded, settled — performed the whole ordinary miracle of respiration for the first time in however many days it had been dead. The hollow eyes filled with a grey light like dawn behind fog. And Merrick Stave stood up on my flat stone, looked down at his own small carved hands, flexed them, and laughed.

It was a terrible laugh, thin and rusted, the laugh of a throat remembering its job. But it was a laugh.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's — Quentin Ashgrove, you magnificent ledger-keeper, that's *hands.*"

"Evening, Merrick."

"Is that — " he turned, and the grey light of his eyes found Sera, who had gone very still by the fire. "You're the warden. Ilvane. I read your case files. Well — I stole your case files. Twice. Excellent theft targets, yours. Very well organized."

"That's the second nicest thing anyone's said about my filing," Sera said.

"It's the only nice thing," Merrick said. "Your filing is the only reason I got as far as I did." He looked around the ruin, at the fire, at the sky through the missing roof, with the unhurried hunger of a man memorizing. I'd seen the dying do it. I'd done it myself at funerals. "One night. You said one night?"

"One night," I confirmed. "Dawn calls the debt. Token returns, you return, everyone's square."

"Square." He tried the word out. "You know what I traded in, before? Nothing square. Nothing priced fairly. That was the whole trick of the Nightfair — nothing on those shelves costs what it's worth, because nobody alive knows what anything's worth until it's gone." He crouched by the fire and held his carved hands to the heat. I watched him discover warmth the way a man discovers a language. "Can I have some of that wine? I know it's bad wine. Sera Ilvane travels with nothing else."

"It's an acquired taste," Sera said, and poured him a thimbleful into a bottle cap, because we were traveling light and grief makes improvisers of us all.

Merrick drank it, shuddered, and grinned. "Perfect. I missed bad wine *so much.*"

* * *

We gave him the rest of the evening the way you give the dying their preferences: without argument. He wanted to hear about the road, so we told him about the road — the ash-fields, the hounds, the vault fire that Sera described with such relish you'd have thought she'd burned it for pleasure rather than survival. He wanted to hold the map, and when I unwrapped it he ran his carved fingers over its surface with something like tenderness, reading his own skeleton written in lines and marks.

"It hurts less now," he said. "Being read. While I was alive-adjacent, every time you consulted it, it felt like being skimmed. Like someone flipping through my diary for the useful bits." He glanced up. "No offense. You flip through diaries professionally, I understand."

"None taken. Mostly."

"Now it's just information. That's what death gets you eventually, if you're lucky — everything becomes information. The pain goes into the record and stops being pain." He set the map down carefully. "Which is why I'd like to spend the good hours I have left telling you about the Hollow. Properly. Not in riddles, not in map-marks. You've earned plain speech, and plain speech is the one luxury the guild never stocked."

Sera moved closer to the fire. I took out my notebook — habit, accusation, comfort; all three.

"The covenant," Merrick began, "was not built to keep the realms apart. Everyone assumes that. Wardens' manuals say it, guild doctrine says it, every frightened peasant prayer says it: the veil exists to separate. Wrong. The veil exists because the realms were *wounded* separating, and the covenant is a scar that learned to manage itself. At the Last Hollow, the worlds never fully came apart. They touch there. Still touch. A place where the boundary is skin-thin and breathing."

"The tear," Sera said. "We saw it from the ridge. It howls."

"It howls because it's untreated," Merrick said. "A wound that size, left open, doesn't heal — it metabolizes. Everything near it starts leaking. Years leak. Names leak. Death leaks across in both directions, which is why the dead near the Hollow cross over so easily, and why the living nearby start forgetting their mothers' faces." He turned his thimble-cap slowly on the stone. "The original anchor-covenant bound five keystones into a lattice around that wound, and it required an anchor. A living person, standing at the center, willing to belong to both realms at once. To hold them together by holding both. Forever."

"Forever," Sera repeated, flatly.

"That was the original term. One person. Chosen, consented, sustained — the first anchor lasted four hundred years, and the covenant provided for her. Community, purpose, continuity. She was honored, visited, remembered. It was a hard office but not a lonely one." His grey eyes flicked up. "Then the guild happened."

"Corvane," I said.

"Corvane's predecessors, then Corvane. Over generations they did what institutions do: they economized. Anchors were expensive to sustain. Honoring them was inefficient. So the terms eroded — quietly, entry by entry, amendment by amendment. Support withdrawn. Visitation ended. The anchor became a prisoner, then a secret, then a rumor. And here's the part that will keep you awake, Quentin, so I'll say it plainly: an unsupported anchor doesn't fail cleanly. It fails *leaking*. Its belonging spills out, and the guild learned to catch the spill and bottle it. Boundary-toll. Memories. Years. Names. Every vial in the Nightfair is drainage from a wound the guild keeps deliberately untended, because the drainage is profitable and the cure is not."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark beyond the walls, the drowned girl's humming had gone quiet, and I realized every dead voice within a mile was listening.

"They didn't break the covenant," Sera said slowly. "They starved it."

"On schedule," Merrick agreed. "Amendment by amendment. Corvane holds the current ledger of amendments. He thinks it's his shield. It's actually his confession — every erosion he's maintained is signed in his predecessors' hands and his own." He smiled, and it was strange to see a smile on a face of carved bone, stranger still that it suited him. "Your collection clause, Quentin. The one that opened the prison. It works because the covenant itself enforces debts. And the guild owes the covenant *centuries* of withheld support. The math is not subtle. The math is a landslide waiting for someone to say the word 'due.'"

I wrote that down, and my hand was steadier than I expected.

"There's more," Merrick said, and the lightness went out of his voice. "The fifth keystone. You need to hear this tonight, while I still have the clarity to explain it, because tomorrow I'll be a hum in your pocket again and you'll be walking into the Hollow on what I tell you now."

"The map says the fifth isn't an object," Sera said.

"No. It's a person. The anchor-line never died out — it was hidden, generation to generation, by people who understood what the guild would do with such a bloodline. The last living candidate is named in the map's final mark, in the old script, the one I encoded because I didn't trust paper." He looked at me across the fire, and his carved hands lay very still on his knees, and I felt the barn go cold in the specific way rooms go cold when a truth arrives and takes off its coat.

"Merrick," I said. "Who?"

"Read the final line, Quentin. Read it aloud. It needs to be spoken to take."

I unwrapped the map with hands that had stopped pretending to be steady. Found the last mark, the tight old script I'd been avoiding since Lowmere, telling myself I'd translate it when there was time, when we were closer, when —

I read it aloud.

"*The anchor shall be born where the realms first touched. The anchor shall be named for ash. The anchor shall hear the dead and keep their accounts.*"

The fire popped. Sera looked at me. I looked at the fire, because it was easier than looking at her, and because I was doing the arithmetic every careful man does when his whole life rearranges itself around a new entry: born on the boundary. Named Quentin. *Ashgrove.* Hears the dead. Keeps accounts.

"Well," I said, when I could. "That explains why the courier handed the map to *me.*"

"He didn't choose you," Merrick said gently. "The map did. I just carried it. And for what it's worth, keeper — I've watched you raise keystones, talk down hounds, cheat prison wards, and lie to a guildmaster's face without flinching. The covenant could do worse than a man who hears the dead and actually writes down what they say." He lifted his bottle-cap in a small toast. "But you should sleep on it. Dawn will call my debt soon, and I'd rather spend the last of it watching the stars through that hole in the roof than explaining destiny. Destiny explains poorly."

* * *

He kept his word about the stars. We sat up most of the night, the three of us — the bone-carver, the disgraced warden, and the dead courier in his six inches of borrowed body — passing the bad wine and naming constellations wrong on purpose, which was Merrick's idea and Sera's unexpected delight. The drowned girl started humming again. Odell offered Merrick advice on estate organization, posthumously, and was politely declined.

Near the end, as the sky went grey at the edges, Merrick took my wrist with both carved hands. They were already going translucent at the fingertips; the debt was gathering.

"When you stand in the Hollow," he said quietly, "and it offers you the center — and it will offer, it's polite that way — remember what the original terms were. Not the eroded ones. The real ones. No anchor was ever meant to be alone. The loneliness isn't the price, Quentin. The loneliness is the *amendment.*" His grey eyes held mine. "Check the original entry. Check it against Corvane's ledger. And then decide what you're actually agreeing to."

"Dawn," I said, because my throat had closed around anything longer.

"Dawn," he agreed, and smiled, and this time the smile stayed all the way through. "Thank you for the hands, keeper. And the wine. Terrible wine. Best night I've had in years — which, granted."

The sun touched the broken rafters. The token exhaled, folded gently at the joints, and was a token again, warm as a held hand. I wrapped it in silk and put it in my breast pocket, over the heartbeat I suddenly understood was collateral.

Sera stood, shouldered her pack, and looked east along the moor road, where the land sloped down toward a horizon that hummed faintly, like the pack, like the bones, like everything now.

"Last Hollow's three days out," she said. "And apparently we already have an anchor." Her eyes came to mine, and there was something in them I couldn't file under any heading I owned. "So let's go find out what you're worth."

I checked the original entry that night in my notebook, the one Merrick's words had sent me hunting for in the courier's marginalia, copied in a dead man's careful hand:

*The anchor shall not bear the covenant alone.*

Someone had struck through it long ago. But ink remembers, and so do I — and I intended to make the guild pay for every word they'd erased.
