## 14. The Toll Collectors

*Quentin*

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a guild ambush, and I had heard it twice before in my life without understanding what it was. It sounds like the dead holding their breath.

We were three days out of Ghost Country, following the old drover's road down toward the river at Marleford, and for most of that time the courier's ghost — Tam, he'd finally given me a name to go with the fading voice — had been quiet in the bone token I carried. Fading was the wrong word, maybe. Thinning. Like ink washed one time too many. He still spoke, but each word cost him something now, and I'd stopped asking questions I didn't absolutely need answered. The map needed him more than I did, and I resented the map a little for it.

Sera walked ahead of me with the easy, rolling stride of a woman who had spent years patrolling boundaries and had never once learned to relax on open ground. Three keystones rode against my chest in a leather wrap I'd sewn myself, and they were heavier than stones had any right to be — not weight of mass but weight of *attention*, as though each one knew it was being carried somewhere important and wanted to be consulted about the route.

"The ford at Marleford," Sera said, not turning around. "You're sure?"

"Tam's sure."

"Tam also told us the second keystone would be unguarded."

"He said it would be *lightly* guarded," I said. "The hounds were an act of creative interpretation on the guild's part."

She snorted. Behind us, the moor stretched brown and empty under a sky the color of dishwater. Ahead, the river ran low and bright through its valley, and the ford itself was visible from the ridge — a wide, stony shallows where the water braided around pale shelves of rock. A drover's inn sat on the far bank, smoke rising from its chimney. Everything about the scene said *ordinary*. Crossing places are supposed to say ordinary. That's their whole trick.

That's when the silence started.

It came up through my boots first, through the bones of my feet, which have always been the best listeners I own. The murmur of the dead — the constant low tide of them I've lived with since I was nine years old — simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped, like a door shut on a room full of talkers.

"Sera," I said.

"I know." Her hand had already gone to her sword. "I felt it."

"You don't have bones that listen."

"No. But I spent eleven years walking boundary lines, and thin places go quiet exactly like this when someone's standing on them." She turned, and her face had gone into the flat, professional calm I'd learned to read as its own kind of alarm. "How many do you think?"

I closed my eyes and listened with everything I had. The dead weren't silent because they were absent. They were silent because they were *hiding*, pressed down into the earth like children under blankets, and underneath their held breath I could feel the reason: living men, many men, moving with the deliberate patience of people who had been paid to wait.

"Twelve," I said. "Maybe fifteen. Spread along both banks."

"Enforcers?"

"Toll collectors." I said the word and tasted its irony like copper. In the guild's old ledgers, toll collectors were the respectable end of enforcement — the ones who came for debts rather than throats. But Corvane hadn't sent respectable anything after us. These would be his own instruments, the ones whose names never appeared in any ledger because their work was the erasure.

Sera looked at the ford, then back along the road behind us, calculating. I could see the arithmetic running behind her eyes — she was good at it, better than me; I balanced numbers all day and still froze when they turned into bodies.

"There's a crossing six miles south," she said. "Fishermen's stairs at Kellet. Narrower, slower, but no cover for an ambush."

"And if they've covered Kellet too?"

"Then we fight somewhere worse than here." She drew her sword a hand's width and let it slide home again — a habit, a prayer made of steel. "Here at least we can see them coming. And the river's low. If it goes wrong, you run north along the bank while I hold the ford. You take the keystones and—"

"No."

"You haven't let me finish the sentence."

"I know how the sentence ends, Sera. I've been listening to versions of it since Karrek." I adjusted the strap across my chest, feeling the three stones shift against my sternum like sleeping animals. "You hold the ford, I run, we meet at the shrine past Hollowmere except one of us doesn't come, and the one who does spends the rest of the book being grim about it. I've read the ledger on that arrangement. The entries don't balance."

Something moved behind her expression — not quite a smile, but the place where smiles went to wait out bad weather. "One of us has to carry them if it goes wrong, Quentin. That's not grimness. That's arithmetic."

"I'm the bookkeeper," I said. "I get to audit the arithmetic."

And then the ambush stopped waiting politely, because ambushes are like debt collectors in that respect — they come when *they* judge the moment right, not when you've finished your argument.

They rose out of the ground. That's the only honest way to describe it. Toll collectors wear covenant-cloaks woven with boundary-silt, cloth that remembers being invisible, and eight of them unfolded from the riverbank grass like the landscape deciding to stand up. Four more dropped from the willows on the far side. The silence of the dead broke all at once into a shriek of warnings I couldn't separate — *behind, left, the water, THE WATER* — and Sera was already moving, already shouting the only order that mattered:

"*Run, Quentin!*"

* * *

*Sera*

I have fought men who wanted to kill me and men who wanted to capture me, and I will tell you which is harder, because nobody believes it until they learn it bleeding.

A man who wants you dead commits. He steps into your blade because stopping matters more than his skin. A man under orders to take you alive hesitates at every exchange — checks his grip, pulls his cut short, watches his fellows for the signal to net instead of skewer. Hesitation is a gift to anyone holding a sword, and so the correct tactical assessment of eight toll collectors under strict orders was: manageable, exhausting, survivable.

The correct assessment assumed I intended to survive.

Quentin ran. Thank every saint of every half-forgotten covenant, the man actually ran — north along the bank with the awkward, flailing stride of a scholar who has never once sprinted for his life, one hand clamped over the keystone-wrap at his chest. The stones would slow him. The stones would keep him honest, too; he couldn't drop them even if panic suggested it, because Quentin Ashgrove does not abandon debts, and he had somehow filed those three keystones under *debts* in whatever ledger he kept behind his ribs.

Two enforcers peeled off after him. Good. Two I didn't have to fight.

The rest came at me across the shallows, and the water was knee-deep and treacherous and entirely on my side of the argument, because I have fought in rivers before and they haven't. The first man reached me with a weighted net and I took his wrist off at the joint — not killing, disabling, there's a difference and I could hear the difference screaming — and the second came high with a hooked bill meant to drag, and I gave him the point instead, through the meat of his shoulder, spinning him into the current.

"Six of you," I said, backing up the shingle. "I've had worse odds at dinner parties."

They circled. Professionals. They weren't going to feed themselves to me one at a time out of pride, and I wasn't going to break formation to punish them for their caution, and so for four or five heartbeats we performed the oldest dance in soldiering: everyone measuring, nobody bleeding, the river talking over all of us.

In those heartbeats, I looked north.

He'd made maybe two hundred yards. The two chasers were gaining — of course they were; he ran like a man fleeing a burning library, protecting the books. One of them raised a throwing-net, coiled and ready, and I did the calculation I had sworn an hour ago I wouldn't do.

If I stayed here, I held five. Maybe I beat five; maybe they got tired of taking me alive and Corvane's orders softened. Either way, Quentin escaped with the keystones, and the covenant could still be reforged, and one disgraced warden became an entry marked *cost, acceptable* in somebody's report.

Every professional instinct I owned signed off on it. Every one. I had spent my whole career making exactly this trade — the one body against the mission, the small grief now against the large catastrophe later — and I had been *good* at it, and being good at it was precisely why I'd ended up in a cell, because a woman who trades bodies fluently will eventually sign off on a village sliding halfway out of the world and call it an adjustment.

"Sorry," I said, to the six professionals, to the river, to myself. "Change of plan."

I went north.

Not along the bank — through the willows, cutting the chord of the bend, taking the slope at a slide that nearly took my ankles with it. The two chasing Quentin heard me coming and did the arithmetic of their own, and to their credit they split to meet me rather than continuing the pursuit, which meant one of them was now between me and Quentin, and I put my shoulder into him like a battering ram wearing armor.

He went down hard. The other one — the net-thrower — loosed anyway, wild, the weighted mesh catching Quentin across the back and shoulders and hauling him off his feet into the shingle. I heard him cry out, heard the stones clatter inside their wrap, heard him *still trying to gather them*, the idiot, the brave, stubborn, ledger-brained idiot, dragging himself forward on his elbows with a net dragging him backward—

An enforcer hit me from the side. Then another. Then the river was in my mouth and my sword arm was pinned and someone's knee was on my spine and the last thing I saw clearly was Quentin turning around.

He had gotten free of the net. He had the keystones. He was twenty feet from the tree line and the moor beyond it, and no one was within thirty feet of him, and he looked at me facedown in the shallows with a boot on my neck —

— and Quentin Ashgrove, the most careful man I have ever known, the man who audits arithmetic, turned around and came back.

"You absolute *fool*," I said, and then a weighted gauntlet introduced itself to my temple and the river and the sky traded places.

* * *

*Quentin*

Let me tell you what the dead said while I lay in the shallows of the Marleford ford with a knee on my back and Sera's blood diluting pink in the current around us.

They said nothing. That was the terrible part. After days of murmuring — directions, warnings, fragments of names, Tam's thinning voice counting our miles — the moment the enforcers cinched the irons on us both, the dead went quiet again. Not hiding this time. *Attentive.* The way a courtroom goes quiet when the verdict is about to be read.

I had run. I want that entered into the record accurately, because I intend to be honest about this even if no one else ever is. When Sera shouted, I ran. I ran the way she told me to, north along the bank, clutching three keystones that pulsed against my chest like panicked hearts, and for two hundred yards I did exactly what the plan required and exactly what duty demanded, and I want it noted that I am capable of doing the right thing.

And then I looked back.

There's a version of the ledger where I keep running. I've written it in my head perhaps forty times since, sitting in this cell with straw that smells of other men's despair. In that version, Sera is captured and I escape and I reach the Last Hollow alone with three of five keystones and no idea how to find the fourth, and the story ends with the covenant starved and the veil torn and every ghost I've ever heard howling into a gap between worlds that I could have closed, if only I'd been willing to spend one warden.

The arithmetic works in that version. That's what frightens me. The arithmetic works beautifully, and I stood in the shallows with it laid out clean and balanced before me, and I understood — really understood, for the first time in my careful life — why Corvane sleeps soundly at night. All you need is a ledger big enough that people become line items. The math takes care of the mercy.

I turned around.

The enforcer I hit didn't expect it — bookkeepers aren't supposed to lead with their elbows — and I'll admit my victory lasted roughly a second and a half before the world resolved itself into hands and rope and the taste of river mud. But in that second and a half I saw Sera's face as they dragged her up out of the water, and she wasn't angry. She was *appalled*. She looked at me coming back the way a mathematician looks at a proof that violates its own axioms, and somewhere under the appalled there was something else, something that made the beating afterward almost worth it.

Almost.

Now we sit in the bed of a guild wagon, chained back to back, bumping along roads I'm too bruised to identify, under guard too numerous to count. The keystones are gone — taken, wrapped, catalogued by a collector with the gentle competence of a customs officer, and that frightened me more than the irons. Corvane isn't destroying them. He's filing them.

"They'll take us to the cells under Vellum & Ash," Sera said. Her voice was hoarse; she'd swallowed half the Marleford. "Separate blocks. They always separate us. Interrogation protocol."

"You sound very familiar with interrogation protocol."

"I wrote part of it." A pause, chains shifting. "The part about separating prisoners, specifically. It works, Quentin. Isolation breaks people faster than pain. Whatever they ask you, remember that the loneliness is the instrument, not the questions."

"Noted," I said. "Any advice for the loneliness itself?"

"Yes." She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had dropped into the register she used for true things, the one with no professional edge on it at all. "Talk to the wall. Talk to anything. The dead, if they'll have you. Keep a voice on the other side of the stone, even an imagined one. People go mad in cells not from darkness but from silence — from believing no one is there." Another shift of chain. "So. For the record, Ashgrove, so you don't forget it in the dark: I'm here. Whatever wall they put between us, I'm on the other side of it. Knock twice if you can hear me."

I knocked twice on the wagon bed, and felt her answer through the boards, faint but unmistakable, like a pulse through bone.

Above us, through the canvas, the dead began to murmur again — softly, cautiously, the way voices return to a house after a funeral. And threaded through all of them, thinner than ever, nearly worn through like the last thread of a well-loved coat, came Tam's voice:

*"Vellum & Ash has old walls, bookkeeper. Old mortar. Old records buried in it. When they lock you in — listen to the walls."*

The wagon turned onto the paved approach to Grayharbor, and somewhere ahead, the guildhouse waited with its lamps lit and its ledgers open, patient as a spider, ready to enter us into its books.

Corvane thought he was collecting a debt.

He had no idea what I'd found out about *his* account.

But that discovery — like the walls, like the mortar, like everything worth knowing — would have to wait until we were behind the locks that were supposed to keep us from finding it.
