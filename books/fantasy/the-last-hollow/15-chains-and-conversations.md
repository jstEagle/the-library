## 15. Chains and Conversations

They took my boots first.

I want you to appreciate the pettiness of that. Not my knives — I'd surrendered those at the ford without a fight, and Corvane's people had catalogued each one with the reverence of archivists handling scripture. Not my warden's sigil, which they pried off with tongs like it might bite. My boots. Because in the cells beneath Vellum & Ash, a prisoner with laces is apparently a prisoner with options, and Guildmaster Corvane has survived forty years of impossible trade by assuming everyone is exactly as dangerous as they can possibly be.

So I sat barefoot on a stone shelf that had been someone's bed since before the guild had a name for itself, in a cell the size of a generous coffin, and I listened to the wall.

Not because walls talk. Because Quentin Ashgrove was on the other side of this one, and bone-carvers hear through anything, and if he was going to be a captive then by every god whose names the covenant forgot, so was I, and we were going to have a conversation about it.

I rapped twice on the stone. Pause. Two more.

A long silence. Then, muffled, faintly incredulous: "Sera?"

"The same."

"You're supposed to be running. You had the ford, the current was—"

"I know how rivers work, Quentin." I leaned my forehead against the cold. "You turned back for me. Did you think I'd let you be the only idiot on this road?"

Through the stone, something that might have been a laugh or might have been the sound a man makes when relief hits him harder than fear does. "They captured you buying me time. That's not idiocy, that's tactics."

"It's idiocy with good posture. There's a difference." I drew my knees up. The floor was damp in the way old cells are damp, as if the building itself were slowly crying. "Are you whole? They didn't—"

"They've been almost courteous. Fed me. Gave me ink and paper, of all things. Corvane seems to want me comfortable." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register I'd learned to distrust, the careful one, the ledger-voice. "He wants something."

"Corvane always wants something. It's the only honest thing about him." I picked at a loose thread on my sleeve. "What did he ask?"

"He hasn't yet. That's what worries me. He just... talked. About the guild. About the covenant. About how everything I believed about the boundary was a simplification written by frightened people." Another pause. "He's good, Sera. He didn't argue with anything we discovered. He agreed with all of it and made it sound like arithmetic."

I closed my eyes. Of course he did. I'd sat across from Corvane once, years ago, early in my warden days, over a boundary dispute in the eastern marches, and I remembered thinking: here is a man who has never lost an argument because he has never had one. He simply reclassifies your position until it's his.

"Don't argue with him," I said. "Don't agree either. If he offers you anything, tell him you need time. Time is the one commodity he prices honestly, because he knows exactly what it costs."

"And you learned this where? Warden school?"

"Warden school teaches you to file reports. Everything else I learned from being filed away." I heard myself and winced. Grief makes me witty; it's a defense mechanism and a bad one. "Quentin. Whatever he offers — and he will offer — remember that the guild doesn't make partners. It makes instruments with better quarters."

Silence from the other side. Long enough that I wondered if he'd fallen asleep, or if the guards had come, or if the wall between us had finally decided to be just a wall after all.

Then, quietly: "How long have we been down here?"

"A day. Maybe two. They kept me drugged at first — valerian smoke through the vents, cheap trick. So I'm estimating generously."

"They took three keystones off you."

"Yes." My jaw still ached remembering it. "And you?"

"All four. They had the fourth already." His voice did something complicated. "Which means Corvane has been sitting on a quarter of the covenant this entire time, letting us do the dangerous work of finding the rest."

"Or he lost track of it decades ago and only found it again when we started making noise. Guild vaults are graveyards with filing systems." I lay back on the shelf and stared at the ceiling, which was close enough to touch and offered nothing. "Either way, he has four-fifths of a reforged covenant and no anchor. That's why you're alive, Quentin. That's why we're both alive. We're not prisoners. We're components."

"That's somehow worse."

"Everything true is worse. That's how you know."

* * *

They came for me on the second day, or maybe the third — the cells ate light and excreted nothing, so time went soft at the edges. Two enforcers, the professional kind who don't sneer because sneering implies an opinion, and they walked me up three flights and down a corridor lined with ledgers behind glass, hundreds of them, the guild's whole history bound in vellum and ash-gray leather. Corvane's office sat at the end like a spider at the center of a very well-organized web.

He looked older than I remembered. Not weaker — older the way a riverbed is old, worn into its shape by decades of the same water. He had wine set out. Two cups. He poured both, which was either courtesy or theater, and pushed one across the desk.

"Sera Ilvane." He said my name like he was checking an entry against a ledger. "The last honest warden, or the most dishonest, depending on which report you read."

"I've read them all. They're inconsistent."

"They're mine." He smiled, thin and real. "Sit. Please. This isn't an interrogation. Interrogations are for people whose answers I don't already own."

I sat. I didn't drink. He drank for both of us, unhurried, and set the cup down with a click that sounded like a stamp coming down on paper.

"You refused to falsify the Merrowfield adjustment," he said. "Eight years ago. You wrote a report saying the boundary there was failing, that the village would slip, that intervention was required. And your superior rewrote it, and you appealed, and you were demoted instead of punished because — and I want you to appreciate the precision of this — because you obeyed. You filed the rewritten version. Your signature is on it."

"I know what my signature is on."

"Of course you do. You're a professional." He folded his hands. "Here is what I find interesting, Warden. You have spent eight years marinating in that shame, and in all that time, did it ever occur to you to ask why the adjustment was approved? Why the guild let a village slide halfway out of the world and called it maintenance?"

"Because the toll was cheaper than rescue. I did the sums myself. That's why I appealed."

"No." He said it gently, and gentle from Corvane was worse than cruel from anyone else. "Because the village wasn't a cost, Sera. It was a harvest. A place half-out of the world bleeds boundary-stuff continuously, like a wound that never scabs. The guild has twelve such places. Thirteen, now, thanks to you and your bone-carver reopening old roads." He spread his hands, a priest revealing the altar. "You thought you signed off on neglect. You signed off on farming."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way it does when the floor turns out to be a ledger page. I thought of the half-gone living in Ghost Country, the stranded dead, the woman who'd asked me whether her house was still standing anywhere at all. I'd called it failure. Neglect. An entry I could someday rewrite.

Corvane watched me do the math and let me.

"Why are you telling me this?" My voice came out level, which I counted as a professional triumph.

"Because I'm about to offer you reinstatement. Full rank, full clearance, your case files restored, your demotion expunged. All of it contingent on one small deliverable." He slid a sheet of paper across the desk. On it, in my own handwriting from months ago, was the route Quentin and I had taken out of Lowmere. They'd copied it from my confiscated field notes. "The bone-carver. Delivered intact, willing or otherwise. You know him better than anyone in this building now. You know what he responds to."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you go back to your cell, and eventually the cells stop mattering, and eventually you stop mattering, and the covenant goes on starving exactly as it always has, and Merrowfield keeps bleeding, and the next warden signs the next adjustment." He picked up his wine. "I'm not threatening you, Sera. Threats are for changing minds. I'm simply showing you the price list."

I looked at the paper with my own handwriting on it. Eight years of shame, itemized and priced. And underneath it, unspoken but loud as a struck bell: he was right that I'd obeyed. He was right that my signature was on the rewritten report. He was even right, in his terrible way, that the guild had never hidden any of this — I just hadn't known what I was reading.

That was the trap. Not the offer. The truth of it, arranged to look like a mirror.

I stood up. Barefoot, no weapons, no rank, standing in front of the most powerful man in the Hollow Guild, and I said nothing. I turned and walked to the door, and the enforcers flanked me, and Corvane's voice followed me down the corridor, pleasant as closing-time bells:

"Take your time, Warden. It's the one thing I'll sell you at cost."

* * *

Back in the cell, I put my palms flat against the wall and knocked our pattern — two, pause, two — and waited longer than I wanted to before he answered.

"You met him," Quentin said. It wasn't a question. Bone-hearing, probably; he could likely feel my heartbeat through the mortar. "What did he offer?"

"Reinstatement. My old life back. For you." I said it flat, because dressing it up would be a lie and I'd done enough lying for one lifetime. "He also told me what Merrowfield really was. What the adjustments really were. The villages weren't neglected, Quentin. They were cultivated."

Silence. Then, softly, with the particular horror of a man watching his own profession confess: "Boundary-toll isn't a fee. It's a crop."

"It's a crop, and we've been reading the fields as wasteland." I slid down until I was sitting against the wall, knees drawn up, my forehead nearly touching the stone. Through it, faint as a pulse under bandages, I could feel warmth. His side of the wall was warmer than mine. Small stupid comfort. I took it anyway. "Tell me what he offered you."

A pause with weight in it. "Partnership."

"Quentin."

"Not partnership with me personally. With the idea of me." His voice took on the cadence of recitation — he'd memorized Corvane's speech, or Corvane had given it in a form meant to be repeated. "The covenant needs an anchor. Anchors break because one person holds too much belonging alone. Corvane's solution: don't use a person. Use a brokerage. Rotate the burden. Sell shares of the anchoring — measured, priced, transferable. The guild becomes the covenant's steward. No more villages bleeding. No more couriers dying with maps. Just... administration."

"And he said this like it was reasonable."

"He said it like it was arithmetic." A breath. "Sera, the worst part — the part I keep turning over like a stone looking for what's underneath — is that it might work. A distributed anchor. Shared load. It solves the problem that breaks every anchor. It's elegant."

"But?"

"But it means the covenant belongs to the guild. Every crossing, every veil-thin place, every prayer that gets answered — priced and permitted by Corvane's office, forever. And the moment the guild owns the covenant, the temptation to skim starts again. It always starts again. I know, because I used to be the man who balanced the books while it happened." Something scraped faintly against stone — his fingers, maybe, tracing the mortar the way he traced bone. "What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I got up and left."

"That's not nothing. That's the loudest answer available."

"Well. I've always had a gift for emphasis." I smiled at the dark, and he must have heard it in my voice, because he made the laugh-sound again, brief and tired.

We sat with the silence for a while, the two of us and the wall, four feet of ancient stone doing the work of a confessional screen. It occurred to me that I had spent my entire adult life behind walls — warden's walls, procedural walls, the wall of a demotion I wore like a coat — and that this was the first wall I'd ever been glad of, because it held the only person in the world who knew all my worst entries and hadn't closed my account.

"Quentin. When he comes back — and he will, he'll give you his 'time' speech too — what are you going to do?"

Another pause. Longer. When he answered, his voice had gone smooth and unreadable, the ledger-voice again, and I felt my stomach drop because I recognized it from the wrong side of too many negotiations.

"I'm going to listen," he said. "Really listen. Ask questions. Make him show me the numbers."

"Quentin—"

"Because a man like Corvane shows you everything when he thinks you're already his." And there — a hairline crack in the smoothness, something underneath it moving fast, like water under ice. "Because the fourth keystone is somewhere in this building, and buildings have records, and records have debts. Because I've spent fifteen years learning what every kind of stone sounds like when it's hiding something, and prison walls are the oldest gossips alive."

I pressed my palm flat against the wall, hard, as if I could push meaning through the stone by pressure alone.

"You're going to play along."

"I'm going to consider," he corrected, gently. "Out loud. At length. While he feels certain."

"That's a knife-edge walk, Quentin. Corvane eats men who try that. He doesn't punish them — he *promotes* them, into shapes they can't come back from."

"I know." Quiet now. Honest. "But you taught me something at the Nightfair, Sera. You said the guild's whole power rests on nobody ever reading the fine print aloud. And I am — professionally, catastrophically — a man who reads fine print aloud."

For a moment I couldn't speak. Through the wall came his warmth, his breathing, the small sounds of a man settling in to wait, patient as sediment. I thought about telling him no. I thought about shouting until the guards came and they moved one of us, broke the only channel we had. I thought about the smart play, the warden's play, which was to protect the asset and wait for a better opportunity.

Instead I said: "If you do this, you do it with a signal. Knock three times when you're performing, two when you mean it. And if Corvane moves you — anywhere — you leave a mark on whatever wall they take you past. A carver's mark. I'll find it."

"Sera—"

"I burned my old life in a vault for you. Let me have the wall." My voice cracked on it, just slightly, and I hated that and let it happen, because some entries shouldn't be smoothed. "Three knocks for performance. Two for true. Say it back."

A long breath from the other side. Then, soft: "Three for performance. Two for true." And then, barely audible, almost swallowed by the stone: "Be careful, Sera."

"Careful is for wardens. I'm unemployed."

I heard the laugh-sound again, and this time it stayed a laugh, and I sat against the wall with my hand flat on the stone until sleep took me, listening to him breathe on the far side of four feet of rock, closer than anyone had been to me in years.

Some time later — hours, or the memory of hours — footsteps came down the corridor outside his cell. Unhurried. Well-shod. A key turned with the confidence of a lock that had never met a stranger.

Through the wall, warm and welcoming and pitched perfectly to carry, Guildmaster Corvane said:

"Master Ashgrove. I trust you've had time to consider my proposal. Because I've brought something to show you — the fourth keystone, as promised. And I've brought something better than that." A pause, silk over steel. "I've brought the terms. In writing. Signed, witnessed, and binding — the full contract, ready for your mark. All I need is your consent."

On my side of the wall, in the dark, I waited for the knock.

Three times. Slow. Even. Performance.

I closed my eyes, and began to pray to every forgotten god that Quentin Ashgrove was a better liar than the honest man he'd spent his whole life being.
