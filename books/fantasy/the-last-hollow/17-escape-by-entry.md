## 17. Escape by Entry

**QUENTIN**

There is a particular humiliation in discovering that the wall you've been talking through for two days has been listening back.

I had spent the hours since Corvane's visit with my palm flat against the cell's mortar, the way I'd once pressed my ear to ledgers to hear what the numbers were ashamed of. The prison of the Vellum & Ash guildhouse — the Undercroft, they called it, as if a prettier name would make the damp more dignified — was old stone over older stone, and every layer had been mortared by men who carved their marks into the wet lime the way I carve into bone. Names. Dates. Small prayers. And underneath all of it, sunk deep in the foundation like a coin in a Christmas pudding: records.

The Undercroft had been built on the bones of an older guildhouse, and that house on the bones of one older still, and none of them had ever thrown anything away. Guildmen don't. We are magpies of obligation. Every contract, every boundary-report, every receipt for every impossible thing ever traded — pressed into the mortar, pressed into the stone, humming there at frequencies only the dead and the deeply indebted can hear.

I heard them. All night, both nights, a choir of paperwork.

And threaded through the choir, one voice louder than the rest, because debt makes the dead shout: *Corvane. Corvane. Corvane owes.*

"Tell me something I don't know," I muttered.

But the stones were more specific than I was. They always are. That's the trouble with systems — people assume the ledger forgets. The ledger never forgets. It simply waits for someone qualified to read it aloud.

I am qualified. Forty years of balancing entries no honest accountant should have been asked to touch, and now the whole rotten archive of the place was reciting itself through my fingertips, and the headline entry, the one written in the oldest hand, the one the covenant itself had co-signed:

*Guildmaster Aldous Corvane, keeper of the tolls, collector of the boundary's surplus, hath withheld from the covenant its due across nine-and-thirty years. Nine-and-thirty years of skimmed passage, diverted tithe, sold death-moments entered as 'miscellaneous.' The covenant defers collection until invoked. Clause the Seventh: any bound servant of the ledger may call the debt due, and the debt shall be collected from the debtor's holdings, wards, works, and walls.*

I sat back on my cot in the dark and laughed, quietly, because laughing loudly in a prison gets you a visit from men with opinions about your teeth.

Thirty-nine years of skimming. The man had built his empire out of the gap between what the boundary owed and what he'd reported it owed. He had fed on the dying veil the way I'd suspected since chapter one of this whole disaster — except worse, because he hadn't even done it cleanly. He'd left entries. He'd left *receipts.* Somewhere along the way Aldous Corvane, who trusted systems the way I used to trust systems, had become so certain nothing could read the books but him that he'd stopped balancing his own.

The system Quentin served becomes the weapon against its master. I want to claim I thought of that phrase myself, but honestly it was Sera's voice through the wall earlier that evening — "your ledgers got us into this, Ashgrove; see if they can get us out" — said as a joke, in the flat tone she uses when it isn't one.

So I did what I do. I opened a book. The book was a prison. And I began, in the careful clerk's cadence I use for entries too large to be spoken casually, to read.

*By the terms of the covenant of the Last Hollow, clause the Seventh, as recorded in the founding mortar of this house and witnessed by every dead hand that laid it—*

The candle guttered though there was no draft.

*I, Quentin Ashgrove, bone-carver and bound keeper of the ledgers of Vellum & Ash, do hereby invoke collection of the debt owed by Aldous Corvane, Guildmaster, unto the covenant, principal and accrued interest, thirty-nine years' standing—*

Somewhere above me, a bell that hadn't rung in a century rang once and cracked.

*—and let collection be made against his holdings, wards, works, and walls, beginning with those walls that hold me.*

The mortar under my palm went warm. Then it went soft. Then, with a sound like a very large sigh from a very tired building, the door of my cell swung open, unlocked by a lock that had just remembered it worked for the covenant before it worked for Corvane.

I stood there a moment, heart hammering, feeling profoundly foolish. Forty years of service and my great act of rebellion was reading a document aloud. It shouldn't work. But then, most of the guild's magic is just bureaucracy with better posture, and I had just filed the most devastating complaint in organizational history.

Through the wall, muffled but unmistakable, came Sera's voice: "Ashgrove. Tell me that was you."

"It was the architecture," I called back. "But yes. Also me."

A pause. Then her door, three cells down, groaned open too, and I heard her laugh — short, rusty, genuinely startled out of her. "Remind me never to owe you money."

"Sera," I said, "everyone owes me money. That's rather the point."

**SERA**

Here is what nobody tells you about escaping a prison: the hard part isn't the doors. The hard part is deciding how many doors behind you to leave open.

Quentin didn't hesitate. I watched him stand in the corridor of the Undercroft while the whole building shuddered around us — wards failing in sequence, each one going down with a sound like a struck glass — and I watched him look at the other cells, the ones holding people Corvane had locked up for debts of their own, or for knowing too much, or for being inconvenient, which in the guild's accounting is the same category as the first two.

"We're on a clock," I told him. "Every warden in this house is going to come down those stairs."

"I know." He was already working the third lock. His hands shook, and he kept working anyway, and I thought — not for the first time, and with considerably less irritation than the first time — that courage comes in stranger shapes than swords.

"The fourth keystone," he said, working by feel, because the lights had died with the wards. "Corvane keeps it in his strongroom, top of the east stair. But the vault registry — the real one, not the one they show wardens — is down here. Sub-level. If we're taking the stone, I want to take the *entry* too. Proof of what he paid for it. Proof of everything."

"You want receipts during a prison break."

"I want receipts during a war," Quentin said mildly. "Prison break is just the filing method."

And that — gods help me — is when I understood I wasn't following this man out of the Undercroft. I was following him into whatever came after, and I'd have followed him into considerably worse, and the smart tactical move was to stop cataloguing that feeling and start counting stairs.

We took the sub-level first. The vault registry was a room of hanging bone-tags, thousands of them, each inscribed with a trade. Quentin moved through them like a priest among relics, pulling tags, reading names. I kept watch at the stair and listened to our escape plan clatter toward us from above: boots, many boots, and the particular barked rhythm of guild enforcers organizing a search pattern. They were good. They'd been trained well. By people like me, once.

"Ashgrove," I said. "Stairs. Now or never."

"One moment—" A tag came free. He held it up to the grey light of my witch-lamp, and even upside-down I could read it: *KEystone IV — acquired from the estate of Warden Maren Ilvane. Price: waived. Debtor acknowledged: none.*

"My mother's estate," I said.

"They took it when she died. Waived the price. Entered no debtor." His voice had gone quiet and terrible, the way it does when the ledger shows him something cruel dressed up as kindness. "They made your inheritance a gift so it couldn't be called a theft. Sera — clause the Seventh doesn't just collect Corvane's debts. It collects everything entered under *debtor: none.* Everything the guild took without paying. The covenant has been keeping a list."

"How long a list?"

He looked up at the ceiling, at the whole weight of the guildhouse above us, and I saw the answer land on him like weather.

"Boots," I repeated. "Now."

We ran.

* * *

**QUENTIN**

Let the record show that the strongroom of Aldous Corvane, warded against fire, flood, theft, scrying, and the direct intervention of minor deities, was defeated by clause the Seventh in four seconds flat, because Corvane had built his wards on guild credit and the covenant had just foreclosed.

The keystone lay on black velvet like a sleeping thing — a wedge of pale stone shot through with veins of gold, warm to the touch, warmer than stone should be. When I lifted it, every dead voice in the building spoke at once, and for once they weren't murmuring. They were cheering.

Which is when the enforcers found us.

Six of them in the doorway, pikes leveled, and behind them a seventh figure I recognized with a lurch: Warden Doss, the man who had signed Sera's demotion order, the one whose name she says like a curse she's trying to quit. He had his hand raised in the guild's binding-sign, and I felt the air thicken with the beginnings of a holding-ward —

— and the holding-ward fizzled, because the covenant had already claimed every ward in this building, and Doss's power had gone the way of Corvane's locks.

Doss stared at his own empty hand. In eleven years of warden service, I imagine he'd never once seen his magic simply decline to arrive.

"Stand down," I said, and I didn't say it loudly, but I said it in ledger-cadence, and the room's stones carried it the way they carry everything. "This building is currently executing clause the Seventh against your Guildmaster. Every ward you're standing inside belongs to the covenant tonight. You can be present for the collection, or you can be part of it. Those are the options. There is no third column."

Enforcers are simple creatures, bless them: give them a clear entry and they'll follow it. Two lowered their pikes immediately. One actually saluted, which I chose not to examine too closely. But Doss stood his ground, face working, and Sera stepped past me into the lamplight, and the temperature of the room changed.

"Doss."

"Ilvane." His jaw tightened. "You're running with a thief and a traitor's ledger. I always said you'd end—"

"You signed the falsification order," she said. "Lowmere annex, the boundary adjustment. You signed it, and then you filed the report that blamed *me*, and you've drawn your pension off that signature for six years. So before you finish whatever sentence you're choking on — check your own account, warden. Because the covenant is collecting tonight, and I promise you it knows exactly what you owe."

I heard it, of course. Through the floor, through the mortar, through the man himself: *Doss owes. Doss owes.* Not a fortune. Not a corruption. Just a small, ordinary, human-sized debt of cowardice, compounded annually.

Doss looked at Sera. Sera looked at Doss. Something passed between them that had nothing to do with keystones, and then he stepped aside, and waved his enforcers aside with him, and said, in a voice like gravel apologizing: "East postern's unguarded. The courtyard ward failed ten minutes ago. Go."

Sera paused beside him. She didn't forgive him. I don't think forgiveness was available yet, maybe not ever. But she said, "Maren Ilvane's keystone goes home tonight," and Doss closed his eyes as if she'd struck him gently, and we went.

* * *

**SERA**

The courtyard was chaos in the beautiful, bureaucratic sense: alarms ringing from broken bells, enforcers receiving orders from officers whose authority had just evaporated mid-sentence, half the guildhouse's magical infrastructure executing a foreclosure it hadn't consented to. Prisoners were pouring out of the Undercroft behind us — a dozen, then more, blinking in the torchlight, and among them I spotted faces from my old case files. People Corvane had shelved. Witnesses, mostly. People who'd seen the toll-collecting up close and been stored like documents.

One of them fell into step beside me on the way to the postern: a thin woman with grey-streaked hair and the ink-stained fingers of a guild archivist. Pell. Odile Pell. She'd been the one who *found* the discrepancies in my Lowmere reports — found them, and brought them to Corvane instead of to me, and watched me take the fall without saying a word.

"Pell," I said.

"Ilvane." She didn't flinch. I gave her credit for that. "Your mother's keystone?"

"Reclaimed."

"Then I'm coming with you. I kept copies, Ilvane. Thirty years of copies. Toll-diversions, false reports, the Nightfair supply chains — everything, duplicated and hidden where Corvane's people never thought to look, because I was too much of a coward to use them and too stubborn to burn them." She glanced sideways at me. "Consider it restitution. Entered voluntarily. I'm told that matters, lately."

Behind us, the guildhouse made a sound I'll remember until I die — a long structural groan, stone settling into new ownership, the whole edifice exhaling thirty-nine years of held breath. Somewhere in its depths, Aldous Corvane was learning what his own ledger sounded like when it spoke back.

Quentin caught up to me at the postern, keystone wrapped in sacking against his chest, soot on his face and the wildest, brightest look in his eyes — the look of a man who has spent his whole life trusting systems finally catching one doing something good.

"Four keystones," he breathed. "We have four."

"And Corvane has nothing left to bargain with," I said, "except the road ahead of us."

Pell snorted. "He'll still come. Men like Corvane don't surrender accounts. They escalate them."

She was right, of course. Even as we slipped out the postern into the moor-dark, even as the guildhouse's last failing ward flickered overhead like a dying star, I could feel it in my wardens' instincts — the pressure of pursuit gathering somewhere behind us, patient and furious and very well funded.

Four keystones. One road to the Last Hollow. And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, the courier's ghost whispering through the bone in Quentin's pocket:

*"Hurry. The Hollow remembers — and so does he."*
