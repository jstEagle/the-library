## 18. The Road to the Last Hollow

I had been a warden for eleven years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things about pursuit: the pursued always overestimate their lead, the pursuers always underestimate the terrain, and both of them lie about how tired they are.

We were lying about how tired we were. Both of us.

The moors above Karrek stretched out like a ledger nobody had balanced — grey grass, grey stone, grey sky pressing down until it seemed less like weather and more like a ceiling with opinions. The wind came off the western fells in long, flat shoves that made walking feel like an argument you were losing. Behind us, maybe four miles back and gaining nothing but pride, rode what remained of Corvane's toll-collectors: nine riders when we'd broken cover at dawn, seven now, because the moor had collected its own tolls from the unwary. One horse had gone down into a peat sinkhole with a sound I will be hearing in my dreams for some time. The rider had walked away. The horse had not, and I did not let myself think about it, because thinking about it meant stopping, and stopping meant dying, and I was behind on both of those.

"Tell me again," Quentin said, half a stride ahead of me because he had somehow decided that the man carrying three covenant keystones in a bone-carved satchel was the one who should break trail, "why we're running toward the place they want us to go."

"Because they want us to go there slowly," I said. "And badly. And on their schedule. There's a difference between fleeing and retreating, Ashgrove. Fleeing is what prey does. Retreating is a tactical decision made by someone who intends to arrive somewhere first."

"And which are we doing?"

"Ask me again at the ridge."

He didn't ask again at the ridge. What happened at the ridge was that we crested it, saw the whole grey bowl of the moor spread out below us with the Hollow Road cutting through it like a scar someone had tried to hide under bad stitching, and Quentin stopped so suddenly I nearly went into his back.

"Sera." His voice had gone strange. Not frightened — Quentin did frightened very well and I knew all its registers by now — but something underneath frightened. Reverent, maybe. "Look at the grass."

I looked. At first it was just grass, wind-flattened, the colour of old pewter. Then I saw what he was seeing: the flattening moved wrong. Wind comes in gusts; this came in waves, long rolling lines bending the moor-grass outward from a point far to the west, as though something enormous were breathing under the land itself. Out. In. Out. Each wave took a full minute to cross the distance between us and the horizon, and each one passed through us with a faint pressure against the ears, like descending into deep water.

"The Hollow's waking up," Quentin said. "The keystones — they're calling to it."

"Then it can call louder," I said, and started down the slope. "It'll have to get in line."

* * *

We made the waystation at dusk. It wasn't much — a drystone shelter built by drovers two hundred years dead, a roof of turf slabs, a hearth ring blackened by generations of fires that had never once been lit for pleasure. But it had walls, and walls meant I could build a fire small enough to hide and still keep my hands working, and my hands needed to be working, because they wanted to shake and I don't permit them to.

Quentin laid out the courier's map on a flat stone while I cooked the last of the travel bread over the coals. The map was bone — thin plates of it, laced together with sinew, and it had a habit I was learning to hate: it changed. Not dramatically. It was worse than dramatic. It revised. Lines that had shown a river would show a road by morning. A landmark marked with a skull would quietly lose the skull overnight, as if deciding the warning had been premature. The courier who'd died pressing this thing into Quentin's hands had left us a document that fact-checked itself in real time, and I trusted it more than any living colleague I'd ever had, which said things about my career I preferred not to examine.

Tonight the map had new writing.

"Here." Quentin's finger hovered over the final plate without touching it. Bone maps don't like being touched by anyone but their reader; I'd learned that lesson in chapter and verse during our third week, when I'd picked one up and it had shown me a detailed diagram of every boundary violation I'd ever signed off on, arranged in order of shame. "The last section. It's legible now. Last night it was just — texture."

"What does it say?"

He read it silently first. I watched his lips move, watched the colour leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Quentin."

"'Four stones make the frame,'" he recited, slowly. "'The fifth makes the door. The fifth keystone is not a stone. It was never a stone.'" He stopped.

"Keep reading."

"It's a person." He said it flatly, the way people deliver diagnoses. "The original anchor. When the covenant was forged at the Last Hollow, the realms couldn't be joined by craft alone — they needed something alive standing at the seam. Someone born on the boundary, half of each world, able to hold both at once. The first anchor stood there for sixty years. When she weakened, they hid her successor. And her successor's successor. Generations of them, raised in secret, kept apart, taught nothing of what they were—" He stopped again.

"Quentin."

"There's a name." He turned the plate toward the firelight. Etched into the bone in characters that hadn't been there yesterday, fine as hair: not a proper name, but a designation, the kind guild clerks used for assets rather than people. Anchor-Candidate, Line of Vell. Born at the crossing of the Lowmere road and the old king's highway, on the night the boundary thinned. "Sera, that's—"

"I know where that is." My voice came out level, which I counted as a professional achievement. "That's the crossroads outside Ashford Mill. That's—" I did the arithmetic. I hated the answer. "That's where you were born, Quentin. You told me. Your mother was travelling. She never made it to the birthing house."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the dark moor, the breathing pressure rolled over us again, out and in, patient as debt.

"That's not proof," Quentin said.

"No. It's a coincidence. I've read enough falsified reports to know a coincidence when I see one, and coincidences look exactly like this." I crouched next to him at the stone. Up close, in the firelight, I could see what the last months had done to him — the weight gone off him, the careful clerk's composure cracked open into something rawer and more stubborn. He looked, I thought, like a man who had spent his whole life balancing other people's books and had only just discovered his own name written in one of them. "Read me the rest."

His jaw worked. "There isn't any rest. That's the final line. Everything after is blank bone."

"Blank bone on a map that revises itself means one of two things. Either the future hasn't been written yet—" I heard my own voice do the thing it did when I was afraid, go quiet and precise, "—or it has, and the map thinks we already know."

* * *

I took first watch, because I always take first watch, because sleep comes easier to me knowing Quentin is breathing thirty feet away and harder knowing he might not be at dawn. He argued, briefly, on principle, then lost consciousness mid-sentence with the unglamorous totality of a genuinely exhausted man, one hand curled around the satchel strap even in sleep. Three keystones. Four, if you counted the person. Five, if you counted me, and I had begun to suspect the covenant did.

I sat with the fire and did what wardens do, which is inventory threats.

Corvane had seven riders left, possibly eight if the sunk-horse man had found remounts at the Karrek garrison, which he would have, because Corvane planned contingencies the way other men planned breakfast. Against that we had: two fugitives, one of them wounded in the shoulder and healing slower than she admitted, the other armed primarily with accounting metaphors. We had a head start of perhaps five hours. We had a road that ran straight and exposed for twelve miles before it reached the fells where the Hollow Road began its climb. And we had whatever was making the moor breathe, which was either our salvation or the reason Corvane wanted us to arrive exhausted, and I genuinely could not determine which.

There was a fourth thing we had, but I didn't put it on the list, because lists are for threats you're willing to name.

The courier's ghost lived in the bone in Quentin's pocket — a carved token, a little figure no bigger than my thumb, holding a shape the ghost could borrow when it had the strength. Most nights it was too thin to speak. Tonight, near midnight, the token warmed against Quentin's side and the ghost surfaced, and I heard the conversation from across the fire in fragments, the ghost's voice like wind through a keyhole.

"—riding ahead of you," it was saying. "They don't wait for the main column. Three of them, light horses, they'll reach the fells by midmorning."

"Can you tell which route?" Quentin, whispering, hunched around the token like a boy with a stolen sweet.

"They'll take the ford at Marrow Bridge. It's the fast way and they think speed is the same as strength." A pause, and when the voice came again it had that dry quality ghosts develop, humour with nowhere to spend it. "You should sleep, carver. You balance everyone's accounts but your own."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're doing what Sera does. You've started copying her."

"I have not."

"You hold your watch the way she holds hers. You've started saying 'ask me again at the ridge.' You threatened a toll-collector with a late fee last week. That was her sentence. You stole her sentence."

Across the fire, I looked away from them and studied the darkness, and was grateful, in a distant and irritated way, that neither of them could see whether I was smiling.

* * *

We broke camp an hour before dawn and made the fells by ten, and I will spare you the details of those hours except to record that the moor continued breathing, that Quentin talked to the keystones in his satchel the way other men talk to nervous horses, and that at Marrow Bridge we found evidence that the advance riders had come through ahead of us after all — fresh hoof-churned mud, a dropped gauntlet, and the ford's water running faintly wrong, with a shimmer along its surface like oil on a drum skin.

"They crossed," Quentin said. "How far ahead?"

"Two hours. Maybe three." I studied the bridge — an old packhorse span, three arches, moss-slick. Something about the middle arch bothered me, and it took me a moment to recognize why: the stones were wet above the waterline, all of them, evenly, as though the river had recently been much higher. Or as though the river had recently been somewhere else entirely. "Quentin. When you crossed here as a child, coming to the city — do you remember this bridge?"

He frowned. "I remember the mill. I remember my mother lifting me to see the water." A pause. His hand went to his pocket, to the token. "Why?"

"Because I think this is where your line was anchored. The crossroads, the bridge, the boundary-thinning — the map named the place, and here it is. And I think Corvane knows it too. Why else send riders ahead to a bridge in the middle of empty moorland?" I looked at the water, at its wrong shine. "Unless they weren't riding ahead to cut us off. Unless they were riding ahead to check whether the anchor-candidate's birthplace still sits on the seam."

The wind moved the grass. The land breathed. And the middle arch of the bridge — I swear this, and I have never sworn lightly — exhaled. A single thread of mist curled out from between the stones, hung in the cold air, and dissolved.

Quentin stared at it. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet. "The map says the fifth keystone is a person. Born on the boundary." He turned and looked at me, and I watched him arrive at the thought the way you watch someone walk into weather they can't yet feel. "Born at a crossing. Half in one world, half in another. Able to stand at the seam and hold both." He laughed — one short, unhappy note. "My mother was a hedge-witch from the Lowmere country, Sera. Nobody's idea of a local girl. And my father — I never knew my father. Guild records list him as 'unrecorded, presumed transient.' Presumed transient. That's how they write about things that cross boundaries."

"Quentin—"

"The courier's ghost said 'the Hollow remembers.' The first night. Before it died, it said that, and I thought it meant the place." He pulled the bone map from his coat and held it up, and even from where I stood I could see that the blank space beyond the final line was no longer quite blank. Characters were surfacing in it the way bodies surface in drowned chapels — slowly, reluctantly, from below.

I took the map from him, because my hands were steadier than his, and I read what the bone had decided to admit.

*Anchor-Candidate, Line of Vell. Confirmed: present tense, living, approaching.* And beneath it, in letters finer than hair, almost an apology:

*Candidate bears the mark. Candidate has heard the dead since childhood. Candidate was born upon the seam and has never once stood wholly in either world.*

*Candidate: Quentin Ashgrove.*

The moor breathed out. The mist rose from the river in a long white sheet, and for a moment, through it, I saw the bridge as it must have been forty years ago on a thinned-boundary night — lanterns, a labouring woman, a midwife with boundary-warden training who knew exactly what she was delivering and swore herself to secrecy about it forever.

I handed the map back. Quentin read it. I watched a man discover, on a bridge in the middle of nowhere, pursued by killers, that he had been the destination all along — that the courier hadn't died bringing him a map so much as bringing the map *to him*, home to roost, and that every step of our flight had been, in the ledger's cold phrasing, *confirmed: approaching.*

"So that's why the keystones wake for me," he said finally. "That's why the chapel water parted. That's why the hounds hesitated." He looked up at me, and there was terror in his face, yes, but underneath it — worse, or better, I honestly could not tell — there was relief. The terrible relief of a question answered. "Sera. I'm not carrying the fifth keystone."

"No," I said. My voice did not shake. Warden discipline; it holds even when everything else is forfeit. "You are the fifth keystone. And Corvane doesn't need to capture you anymore, Quentin. He just needs you to arrive."

Behind us, far back along the moor road, I heard hooves. Seven riders, closing at a canter, no longer bothering to hide.

Quentin closed his hand around the satchel strap. The mist cleared off the bridge, and the road climbed away westward toward the Hollow, and the land beneath us breathed in, and held.

"Well," he said. "Ask me again at the ridge."
