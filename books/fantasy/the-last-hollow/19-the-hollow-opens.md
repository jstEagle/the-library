## 19. The Hollow Opens

The moor ended the way all good lies end: abruptly, and with the ground giving out beneath you.

One moment Sera and I were climbing through heather the color of old bruises, the courier's ghost flickering ahead of us like a candle in a draft, and the next the land simply stopped pretending to be land. Below us, set into the earth like a wound that had never learned to close, was the Hollow.

I have balanced ledgers for impossible things for most of my adult life. I have recorded trades of memories, years, names, deaths. I thought I understood what "impossible" meant as a category of goods.

I did not understand anything.

The Hollow was not a place so much as an absence with ambitions. A vast bowl sunk into the moor, perhaps half a mile across, its walls spiraling down in terraces of black stone that might have been carved or might have been grown — I genuinely could not tell you which, and I have since made my peace with not knowing. At its center, where the bottom should have been, there was instead a tear. Not a hole in the ground. A hole in *everything*. The veil between realms hung there like silk that someone had slashed with a careless knife, and through the slash I could see — no. That's not right either. Through it I could see two things at once, occupying the same space, refusing to resolve: the other realm's sky, which was the color of deep water, and our own sky, which was the color of evening, and both of them were correct, and both of them were screaming.

Because yes. It howled. The torn edge of the covenant sang a note I felt in my teeth, in the bones in my pocket, in the fillings Sera kept threatening to have replaced. Every dead voice I had ever heard was a whisper compared to this. The boundary itself, wounded for generations, bleeding toll into guild coffers, had finally worked up the volume to complain.

"It's worse than the reports," Sera said beside me. Her voice was flat, which is what her voice does instead of trembling. "The wardens' reports described a seam. This is a *gash*."

"The reports were written by people paid to describe a seam," I said. "I know. I filed some of them."

She glanced at me. Under the grime and the healing hound-scratches and the general aura of a woman who had burned down her own past three days ago, there was something almost like sympathy. "You did."

"I priced them," I corrected. "There's a difference. There used to be a difference. I've spent four months learning there wasn't."

Below us, scattered along the spiral terraces, the keystones in my satchel began to hum. All four of them at once, harmonizing with the wound, and I felt them pull at the strap like dogs smelling home.

*The Hollow remembers,* the courier had whispered, dying on my guildhouse floor with his blood soaking into ledger-vellum. He remembered it now, all right. His ghost stood at my shoulder, thinner than he'd been even a week ago, translucent enough that I could count the terraces through him. The map had eaten most of him by now. What remained looked at the tear below us with something I chose to read as longing rather than terror.

"That's where I was taking you," he said. His voice had gone quiet too, worn smooth like a stone carried too long in a pocket. "I never got to see it whole. My grandmother did. She said before the guild started harvesting, the veil here didn't howl. It *sang*. Two songs, one verse apart." He smiled, and the smile cost him visible effort. "You're going to fix the harmony, bone-carver."

"No pressure," I muttered.

"None," Sera agreed. "Just the structural integrity of reality."

We started down.

* * *

The descent took hours, and I will spare you the worst of it, because some of what lives on those terraces does not deserve description and none of it deserved interruption. But I will tell you about the anchors.

Every few turns of the spiral, we passed them: niches cut into the black stone, and in each niche, remains. Some were bones, old and fused to the rock. Some were stranger — outlines, impressions, shapes pressed into the stone like fossils of people who had sat down and become geology. Anchor after anchor after anchor, going back further than the guild, further than the covenant's written terms, back to whoever had first stood in this wound and said *enough*.

The courier drifted from niche to niche, reading them the way I read ledgers. "Here's one from the third age. Here's a pair — look, two together, they must have tried sharing the burden once. Didn't hold. Here's—" He stopped. Went still, which for a ghost is saying something.

"What?" Sera asked.

"He's young," the courier said softly. "Sixteen, maybe. The carving says he volunteered when his mother's term ended. Says he lasted eleven years." The ghost looked up at me, and his translucence made his expression hard to parse, but I am a professional in expressions, dead and living alike. "Quentin. They all lasted a while, at first. Then less. The closer ones to the tear — shorter terms. The covenant's gotten hungrier as the guild has starved it. Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything," I said. Which was true. Planning implies alternatives, and I had burned mine on the road behind us, along with Sera's credentials and Corvane's patience. "I'm going to stand in the wound and hold it shut. That's not a plan, it's a job posting."

"With no term limit," Sera said. She had gone ahead of me on the path and now turned, blocking it, arms crossed, wearing the exact posture she used on smugglers and perjurers. "That's the part you keep glossing over, Ashgrove. Forever. You heard the map. Born on the boundary, blood of both sides, the last living candidate. You stand in that tear, the covenant reforges around you, and you stay. Until the stone wears out. Until *you* wear out."

"I did the arithmetic on the barge at Lowmere," I said. "I've done it every night since. I'm very good at arithmetic. It's the one thing Corvane never managed to take from me."

"And?"

"And every sum comes out the same. One man, held in place forever, against two realms falling apart." I shrugged, and hated how casual I made it sound, because it wasn't casual, it was the least casual thing I would ever do. "It balances. That's all a covenant is, Sera. An entry that balances."

Something moved across her face then — fast, gone before I could name it. Later, much later, she told me what it was, but at the time I misfiled it as anger, and let her walk on ahead of me, and did not think about it again until it was far too late to matter.

* * *

The bottom of the Hollow was not stone.

It was the boundary itself — a floor of veilskin, stretched taut across the tear, translucent, shivering underfoot like the membrane on new milk. Standing on it was standing on the skin of everything. Beneath my boots I could see the other realm moving: rivers running backward, forests that grew downward, cities lit by a soft blue nothing. And around the edges of the vision, our own world superimposed — the moor, the heather, the small stubborn lights of farms — both realities layered like double-exposed vellum.

Four sockets waited at the center, arranged in a ring, and a fifth space at their middle shaped exactly like a standing person.

Of course it was. It had always been a person. The stones were just keys; the lock wanted flesh.

I knelt and began setting the keystones. Lowmere's drowned granite, humming with chapel-water. Karrek's ash-black slate, still warm from Sera's blood. The vault-stone, scorched from our escape, that had cost us the alarm and nearly cost us more. And the fourth, pried from Corvane's own strongroom during our escape from his cells — I confess I enjoyed that part more than was spiritually healthy.

Each stone sank into its socket like a word into a sentence. The howling changed pitch. Rose. Became — briefly, horribly — articulate.

*PAYMENT*, said the boundary, in a voice made of every voice I had ever heard through bone, all of them speaking in unison, the courier's among them, my mother's among them, voices I had never known and somehow recognized. *THE COVENANT STANDS ON PAYMENT.*

"I know," I said. "I've read your terms. I wrote summaries of your terms for people who didn't want you to know they'd read them." I stepped toward the fifth space. "I'm the payment. Quentin Ashgrove, born on the boundary line at Vellum & Ash, half of here and half of nowhere. Living anchor, volunteering, term indefinite. Enter it in whatever ledger you keep."

*ENTERED*, said everything.

And the covenant began to reforge around me.

* * *

I don't have words for what happened next, and I am a man whose profession is words, so understand what it costs me to admit that. But I'll try, because someone should write it down honestly, and honesty has become the only currency I trust.

The moment my feet found the fifth space, the tear *recognized* me. Both realms poured in — not painfully, though there was pain, but the pain was incidental, the way hunger is incidental to a feast. I saw the other realm whole. Not the sliver through the veil, not the guild's harvested glimpses sold by the vial, but *whole*: every river, every grief, every city, the entire vast breathing weight of a second world that had been leaning against ours for ten thousand years, kept apart and kept close by a pact and a person.

And I saw ours equally whole, simultaneously, without turning my head. I could feel the moor's heather rooting. I could feel the tide at Lowmere, finally allowed to rise. I could feel Sera standing six feet away, watching me with her hand pressed over her mouth, and I could feel the heartbeat under her palm, and I could feel the heartbeat of the drowned chapel, and of the ash-fields, and of everything, everywhere, both worlds, all at once.

Do you understand? I *belonged* to everything. Every place, every moment, every living and dead thing in two realities knew my name and had room for me in it.

No wonder they break.

No wonder every anchor in those niches lasted a while and then less. It isn't the pain. Pain you can budget for; I've kept books on worse. It's the belonging. No single soul is shaped to hold that much of it. It pours in and keeps pouring, and you love everything, and everything loves you back, and there is no *edge* to you anymore, no border where Quentin stops and the rest begins — and a self needs borders the way a ledger needs margins. Without them, the entries smear. You dissolve into the accounts. Somewhere around year five, or year nine, or year eleven, if you were sixteen and brave, you stop being an anchor and become a place.

I felt it starting already. Felt my edges going generous. And some small, stubborn, bookkeeping corner of me — the corner that had noticed one erased entry and refused to look away, the corner that had lied to Corvane and hidden a dying man's map — that corner planted its feet and said: *not yet. Hold the line. Balance the entry. But not yet.*

I raised my hands, and the keystones rose with me, and the tear's edges began to draw together, silk pulling toward silk, the howl bending slowly toward song. It was working. The covenant was accepting me. In a few minutes it would be done, and I would be done, and the worlds would be safe for everyone except the one person standing in the middle of them.

*Good trade*, I told myself, wry even now, because the habit dies last. *Best margin you ever cleared.*

Then I opened my eyes — I still had eyes, apparently, still had a choice about using them — and looked at Sera Ilvane one last time.

She was crying. Disgraced covenant-warden Sera Ilvane, who fought bone-hounds barehanded and signed confessions like closing arguments, was crying, and her mouth was moving, and through the roar of two realms I read her lips, because I am very good at reading things:

*Don't you dare balance this alone.*

And I laughed. Or the boundary laughed through me, or maybe there was no difference anymore, and I began to say — I don't know what. Yes. Goodbye. Something with her name in it.

But before the word could form, before the covenant could finish closing around me and make me permanent —

the tear *shuddered*.

Not from my reforging. From outside it. A cold point of pressure against the covenant's oldest clause, sharp as a stylus, precise as an entry called due — and a voice, hers, cutting through both realms at once, invoking words I had taught her three nights ago by firelight, words from a buried ledger, words with Corvane's name attached to a debt decades unpaid:

*"By the collection clause of the original covenant, I call due what is owed."*

The keystones flared white.

And the covenant, which had just accepted one anchor forever, paused — mid-entry, mid-binding, mid-*me* — and turned its attention, vast and terrible and scrupulously fair, toward the woman standing at the edge of the fifth space, holding a debt in one hand and, God help me, reaching for my hand with the other.
