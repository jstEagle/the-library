## 22. New Entries

The trouble with running an honest brokerage is that honesty turns out to be a full-time occupation. I had imagined, in the months after the Hollow closed over our heads like a healing wound, that transparency would be the easy part. You write down what crossed the veil. You write down what it cost. You nail the ledger to the door where anyone can read it. Simple.

Nobody told me that people would *read* it.

"Quentin," Sera called from the front room of the brokerage — which was also the kitchen, which was also the parlor, because the Hollow's edge did not believe in wasted space — "there's a woman here asking why her grandmother's crossing was priced at 'one memory of rain.' She wants to know whose memory."

I set down my carving knife. The bone token I'd been working — a small thing, a sparrow, for old Marta Tull who had waited eleven years by our door for her son to come back from the far side — sat half-finished on the bench. The dead are patient, but I've learned not to test them. It's rude.

"Tell her it's the grandmother's," I said, "and that we can show her the entry."

"And if she doesn't like it?"

"Then she doesn't have to like it. She just has to see it."

There was a pause, and then Sera's voice again, drier than chalk: "You know that answer would have gotten us both executed under Corvane."

"Under Corvane," I said, "the answer would have been a lie, and then a bill, and then a burial. I prefer this."

I heard her snort — the small, unguarded sound she only makes when she thinks no one important is listening — and then the murmur of the front room taking in another customer. I stayed at my bench a moment longer. Through the window, the moor rolled away toward the Last Hollow, which these days looked less like a wound and more like a valley that happened to be holding its breath. The veil there shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone, and twice a day — dawn and dusk — you could stand at our fence and watch things cross. Not stolen things. Not smuggled things. Just... crossings. A heron once. A dead man's whistled tune. Once, memorably, an entire fog bank that turned out to be the accumulated sighs of a drowned chapel's congregation, finally permitted to move along.

I carved sparrows now instead of ledgers' worth of secrets into bone. Well. I still kept ledgers. Some habits are vertebrae; they hold you up.

But the bones spoke differently lately. That was the change nobody warned me about. For years the dead had murmured through my carvings — grievances, confessions, the occasional unsolicited opinion about my posture. Now, when I picked up a finished token, what came through was mostly gratitude. *Thank you for writing it down,* they said. *Thank you for the price that was true.* As though the simple fact of an honest entry were a kind of funeral rite all its own. Maybe it is. I'm not a theologian. I'm a bookkeeper who hears ghosts, which is either less or more than a priest depending on the day.

The courier's voice was among them, some nights. Fainter each season, as he'd promised he'd be. He'd gone through at the reforging with the rest of the Hollow's stranded dead — I watched him go, or felt him go, a warmth leaving my pocket where his bone map had lived for so many months. But gratitude, it turns out, has excellent carrying power across a veil. *The Hollow remembers,* he'd said when he was dying. These days what he said was simpler. *It holds. Keep the entries.*

I intended to. I intend to still, which is fortunate, because intention turns out to be the entire job description.

* * *

The woman in the front room was named Odile Ferrance, and she had her grandmother's chin and her grandmother's temper, which made arguing with her feel briefly like seance. She stood at our public ledger — a great oak-bound book chained to its lectern, pages open to any passerby, ink guaranteed legible by yours truly — and she ran her finger down the column of crossings with the expression of someone auditing an enemy.

"One memory of rain," she repeated. "That's obscene."

"It's precise," Sera said, arms folded. She stood the way she always stood when facing down a customer: like a warden at a gate, which she had been and in some ways remained, the disgraced rank having been quietly restored by the guild's honest remnant last spring. She'd taken the reinstatement with a handshake and immediately filed a request to keep working out here instead of the guildhouse. "Your grandmother traded it herself. Voluntary. Witnessed. Here's her mark." Sera turned the ledger around without being asked. We don't make people ask anymore. That was the whole point.

Odile looked. Her finger found the entry, and the little pressed-flower mark beside it that her grandmother had insisted on making rather than a signature, because she'd never learned letters.

"She gave up a *memory*?"

"A memory of rain," I said, coming to stand beside Sera. "One. Specific. The afternoon of the storm when she was nine and sheltered under the mill bridge. She chose it herself from three options we showed her. The toll collector on the far side priced it fairly — you'll see the counter-entry two lines down. Your grandmother received, in exchange, one hour at the veil's edge to speak with her late husband. The hour cost exactly what the memory was worth. No more." I tapped the columns. "Balance carried forward: nothing. Nobody owes anybody."

Odile stared at the page for a long time. Outside, dusk was coming on, and the Hollow began its evening shimmer, and somewhere in the back of my skull a chorus of the departed cleared their throats the way crowds do before something good.

"That's it?" Odile said finally. "No interest? No fee for the brokerage?"

"The covenant pays us," Sera said. "Badly. In firewood rights and a roof that doesn't leak much."

"It leaks in the northeast corner," I said.

"The covenant pays us badly," Sera amended, "and Quentin complains."

Odile looked between us, and something in her face loosened — the particular loosening of a person who has come prepared for a fight and finds, disorientingly, a receipt. "My mother told me places like this were parasites," she said. "Feeding on grief."

"They were," Sera said simply. "Some still are. Check their ledgers. If they won't show you the ledger, walk away."

"If they *have* a hidden ledger—"

"Walk faster," I said.

Odile copied her grandmother's entries into her own notebook, asked three more questions — good ones, the kind I used to dread and now rather enjoy — and left at full dark with her grandmother's flower-mark pressed into her memory and, I suspect, a story to tell at every table in Lowmere for a month. Word travels. That was the other thing nobody warned me about: honesty is contagious, but slowly, the way trust is. You don't convert anyone. You just leave the book open long enough that checking it becomes a habit.

After she'd gone, Sera bolted the door and leaned against it, and for a moment we stood in the lamplight in the tired silence of people who have argued with strangers all day and won every round and lost several hours.

"Northeast corner," she said.

"I'll patch it tomorrow."

"You said that Tuesday."

"I said it Wednesday. Tuesday I was finishing Marta's sparrow." I hesitated. "Her boy came through last week. At dawn. He stopped at the fence and looked at the house for a while, and then he went on. I think he was saying goodbye."

Sera was quiet. Then: "Did you give him the token?"

"He didn't need it. But I'll bury it by the fence anyway. Some debts you pay even after they're settled. It keeps the hand honest."

She crossed the room and looked at the half-finished sparrow on my bench, turning it in the lamplight with those warden's hands that had broken a hound's jaw in Karrek and later, gentler, held mine through the worst night of the reforging. "You carve differently now," she said. "I never told you. Before — your work looked like it was bracing for something. Now it looks like it's listening."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't make it weird, Ashgrove."

Too late. It was already weird. Weirdness, I find, is load-bearing in any marriage of professionals.

* * *

We married in autumn, if you want the truth of it, in a ceremony of our own devising that consisted mostly of entering it in the ledger. *Two names, jointly bound, consideration mutual, term: ongoing.* The guild's honest remnant sent a delegation; the dead sent, via my bones, a great deal of unsolicited advice about venue. The covenant itself, which has opinions the way weather has opinions, marked the occasion with a double shimmer across the whole valley that made every hair on both our arms stand up, which Sera declared "approving" and I declared "the boundary equivalent of clearing its throat," and we agreed to disagree, which is most of marriage anyway.

People ask sometimes — travelers, pilgrims, the occasional suspicious guild auditor — how we bear it. Living at the edge. Hearing the dead. Watching everything cross and knowing the price of every passage. They ask it the way you'd ask a lighthouse keeper how he bears the loneliness, expecting a tragic answer.

Here is the tragic answer I don't give them: I spent thirty years keeping secret ledgers for men who fed on the space between worlds, and I heard the dead complain about it every single day, and I thought that was what hearing meant — being a drainpipe for grievance. It took a dying courier pressing a bone map into my hands and a warden breaking a binding that should have cost me everything to teach me otherwise. The dead weren't complaining about dying. They were complaining about being *unrecorded*. About passing through a world that priced them in secret and remembered them not at all.

Now they're recorded. Now the book is open. Now, when old Marta's boy crossed at dawn, there was an entry waiting for him with his name spelled right, and I swear — I will swear this to my own grave and expect it entered accurately — the whole valley felt lighter for an hour afterward.

Home, I've come to believe, is not a place you're given. It's a debt you choose to keep paying. Every patched shingle, every honest column, every sparrow buried by a fence. Sera understood it before I did, in her way. She broke the ledger's binding at the Hollow not because the price was unfair but because a covenant that costs one person everything isn't a covenant; it's a sacrifice wearing accounting clothes. Splitting the cost diminished us both. It also, impossibly, completed us both. Two halves of an anchor, holding the same rope.

Some nights I take the ledger out to the fence and read the day's entries aloud to the dark, because the dead like being read to, and because the covenant — whatever it is, whatever it's becoming, imperfect and alive and ours — seems to settle a little deeper into the ground with every word. Sera comes out with bad wine and corrects my pronunciation of the Lowmere names. The veil shimmers. The heron crosses, occasionally, going the wrong direction, and we've agreed not to ask.

Tonight, reading, I reached the bottom of the page and found an entry I hadn't written.

It was in my own hand — that's the part that stopped my breath, my own cramped bookkeeper's script, unmistakable — but I had written no such line. It sat below the day's final crossing, neat as scripture:

*Entry pending: one debt, called due, collection date approaching. The Hollow remembers. Bring the fifth quill.*

Sera read it over my shoulder. The wine bottle stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Quentin," she said, very calmly, in the voice she used for reporting breaches, "we reforged the covenant with five keystones. There were only ever five."

"I know."

"So what," she said, "is a fifth *quill*?"

Out past the fence, the Hollow shimmered — twice, three times, a rhythm almost like impatience — and in the bones on my bench, every finished sparrow began to whisper at once.
