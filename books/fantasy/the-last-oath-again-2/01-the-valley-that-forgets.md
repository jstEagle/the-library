## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The geese told Mirabel about the stranger three hours before he arrived.

*He comes up the river road,* said the eldest of them, a gray-necked gander with a voice like a door hinge. *Limping. Bleeding. Carrying something heavy against his chest like it might hatch.*

Mirabel crouched at the edge of the millpond, fingers in the cold water, and did not say anything back, because saying anything back was the one thing they could never do. The geese didn't seem to mind. Animals never minded silence; they read it fine.

"You'll want to move the flock off the low meadow," they said aloud, to no one, in the flat tone people used for talking to themselves. Old Marta was stacking firewood by her door across the lane, and she glanced over, and Mirabel gave her a small wave and a smile, the kind that meant *nothing here, nothing to see.* Marta smiled back and went inside. In Ashenmere, everyone had learned to look away from things. It was practically a civic duty.

The valley itself did most of the work. Ashenmere sat in a fold of the hills like a coin dropped in a pocket—sheltered, unnoticed, easy to forget even from the ridge above it. The mists came down every morning off the shoulders of the pass and pooled in the fields until noon, and travelers who stumbled in by accident always left with the vague, apologetic feeling of having misremembered something. *Was there a village there? I thought there was a village there.* The roads bent around the valley the way water bends around a stone. Maps, if any mapmaker ever bothered, showed a blank.

That was the covenant's doing, though nobody under forty called it anything so grand. The elders spoke of "the arrangement," or "the quiet," or sometimes just "how we live." Magic could not follow you into Ashenmere. Prophecy could not find you. Kings' wars passed overhead like weather. In exchange for what? Mirabel had asked once, at nine years old, and Mother Sefi had gone very still and said, *In exchange for nothing, little one. Nothing is the price, and nothing is the payment, and you will thank me when you're older.* They hadn't thanked her. They still hadn't. But they had learned to keep their mouth shut about the other thing—the gift, the listening, the voices in fur and feather that had followed them since before they could remember.

Because that was the joke at the heart of the valley, wasn't it. A place where magic couldn't follow—and yet here was Mirabel, born in the miller's loft during a storm, adopted by two mothers who'd found them abandoned on the covenant-stone itself, carrying a magic that hadn't gotten the message. Or maybe it hadn't needed to follow them. Maybe it had been born in. Nobody knew. Mirabel had never dared ask the animals, because the animals were honest, and honesty was dangerous here.

*You're doing the face again,* said the gander.

"I don't have a face."

*You have a face where your eyebrows go sad and your mouth goes stubborn. You're doing it now.*

Mirabel stood, wiped their hands on their apron, and went to open the market stall.

* * *

Market day in Ashenmere looked like market day anywhere: canvas awnings snapping in the wind off the mere, a smell of woodsmoke and wet wool and frying onions, children weaving between the carts while their mothers pretended not to watch them. Mirabel sold cheese and eggs and the odd jar of honey from the hives their mother Tala kept on the south slope, and they were good at it—not because they loved trade but because they loved listening, and half of selling is knowing what a person actually wants.

The goat wanted to complain about the dog. The dog wanted the goat to stop complaining. Widow Anselm wanted someone to ask after her hip so she could refuse help. The cooper's boy wanted Mirabel to notice he'd grown two inches since spring, which Mirabel obligingly did, and watched him glow like a struck flint. It was a good morning. It was almost enough to make them forget the ache underneath everything, the one that lived in the valley like water in stone—that soft, persistent grief of belonging somewhere built on forgetting. Because that was the thing about Ashenmere nobody said out loud: safety bought with invisibility means you are safe the way an unmarked grave is safe. Everyone here had been handed peace, and everyone here had paid for it with mattering.

Mirabel felt it worst at the edges of the market, where the mist ended and the world began. Sometimes they caught themselves staring up the river road as if waiting for something. They had never known what. Now, perhaps, they were about to find out.

It came just past midday. First the dogs went silent—all of them at once, a silence with edges to it—and then the crows lifted off the tithe-barn roof in a ragged black sheet, and then came the sound of a man trying very hard to walk quietly and failing.

He came out of the mist like something the river had rejected. Tall, hollow-cheeked, dressed for a warmer country—a scholar's long coat scorched along one sleeve, its hem stiff with old mud and new blood. He carried a book against his chest the way the gander had said: both arms wrapped around it, chin tucked over it, like a soldier carrying a wounded friend. The book was bound in dark leather gone green with age, closed with a lock of dull iron, and there was no title on it, only a mark stamped deep into the cover that Mirabel's eyes slid off of, twice, before they forced themselves to see it: a circle broken by a single vertical line.

A door. Someone had stamped a door on the cover.

"Cheese," said the stranger. His voice cracked on the second consonant. He swayed. "Please. I have—" He fumbled at his belt with one hand, keeping the other locked around the book. "I have coin. Or I can work. I only need—"

Then his knees went.

Mirabel got to him before anyone else did—they were closest, and besides, some instinct older than sense made them move—and took his weight across their shoulders, staggering under it. Up close he smelled of smoke and fever and road dust, and beneath all that of ink, the particular sharp smell of a man who lived among books. His blood soaked warm through their sleeve. His lips moved against their ear, and what came out was barely a whisper:

"Not the elders. Don't let them take it to the elders. Please. It has to be—it names—" His eyes rolled, feverish, searching Mirabel's face with sudden terrible attention. "Your face," he breathed. "I've seen your face. How do I know your—"

Then he fainted, and the book slipped, and Mirabel caught it one-handed against their hip, and the whole market square went quiet as held breath.

They should have given it up. Every rule they'd ever been taught said so: strangers went to the elders, strange things went to the elders, and above all the valley kept nothing, remembered nothing, *was* nothing, and that was how it survived. Mirabel stood there in the trampled grass with a bleeding man's arm over their neck and a locked century-old book pressed against their ribs, and felt the eyes of Ashenmere sliding toward them like water finding a drain.

"The fever house," they heard themselves say, loud and steady. "This man needs the fever house. Mother Tala, bring the cart."

Tala came. Tala always came. She looked at Mirabel—at the blood, at the book, at the stranger's scorched coat, at whatever expression Mirabel was failing to keep off their face—and something flickered behind her eyes, the old fear, the one every parent in Ashenmere carried like a stone in the shoe. Not fear of the stranger. Fear of what the stranger might mean.

"Of course, love," she said, and went for the cart.

* * *

They laid Idris—for that was his name, pried out of him in lucid moments between fevers—in the fever house's back room, and Mirabel sat with him through the afternoon while the light went amber and then gray. Their mothers came and went. The elders sent word that they would come at dusk, and that the book should be ready for them, and Mirabel said yes, of course, and put the book under the blanket instead.

It was stupid. It was the single most dangerous thing they had ever done. They did it anyway, because the man had said *it names*, and because he had looked at their face like a man looking at a letter he'd been carrying for a hundred miles, and because—for the first time in twenty-two years—something from outside the mist had come looking for *them*, specifically, by name if the fever-talk could be trusted, and Mirabel found they would rather burn than give that up unexamined.

The animals kept them company. A hedgehog had taken up residence under the fever house floor and offered commentary throughout; a moth kept landing on Mirabel's knuckle, which the hedgehog found deeply unprofessional.

*Ask it what it names,* the hedgehog said, for the fourth time.

"It's locked."

*You're locked. Everything worth anything is locked.*

Dusk came. Torches bloomed in the lane. Through the window Mirabel watched the elders cross the square in their gray shawls—seven of them, moving slowly, the way they did when they wanted their age to do the arguing for them—and Mother Sefi walking beside them with her hands folded tight, not looking at the fever house, which was worse than looking.

And under the blanket, against Mirabel's thigh, the locked book grew suddenly, unmistakably warm. Warm as a living thing. And through the wall, faint but clear, a crow landed on the sill and said, in its dry scrap of a voice, the words it had flown all day from the northern pass to say:

*Riders. Armed riders in the pass, coming down. Coming fast.*

The knock at the door sounded, and the book burned warm as a coal against Mirabel's skin, and somewhere above the valley, the mist began to thin.
