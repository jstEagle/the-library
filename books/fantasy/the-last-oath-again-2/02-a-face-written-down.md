## 2. A Face Written Down

The fever broke on the second night, and Idris woke to the smell of beeswax and old smoke and the particular silence of a house where people were deciding what to do with him.

He knew that silence. He had heard it in three cities as the usurper's writ spread west—heard it in the pauses of innkeepers' answers, in the way neighbors stopped greeting each other when he passed. It was the sound of ordinary people weighing his life against their own.

He was lying in a narrow bed under a quilt patched with river-stone colors. Through the window's small pane he could see the valley: Ashenmere at dusk, terraced fields going blue, a millwheel turning with the patient rhythm of something that had turned the same way for four hundred years. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, untroubled columns. No watchtowers. No wards glinting on the ridgeline. Just a valley folded into its hills like a coin into a palm.

*Invisible,* he thought, and his chest hurt with the truth of it. This place did not hide because it was ashamed. It hid because hiding was the only thing that had kept it alive.

On the table beside the bed sat the book.

His captors—or hosts; the distinction had grown thin—had not chained it or locked it away. They had simply set it there, wrapped in oilcloth, as if proximity were containment enough. Perhaps in Ashenmere it was. Perhaps a valley built on forgetting believed that anything placed quietly enough would eventually be forgotten too.

Idris sat up. His ribs screamed where the pass scree had opened him, and his left hand still would not fully close, but he reached for the book anyway, because he had been reaching for it since Ossuary burned, since the archive at Vell fell, since Master Enkidu pressed it into his arms in a doorway full of ash and said *run, boy, run until running is done.*

The clasps were bronze, worked into the shapes of closed eyes. He had never found a key. He had never needed one. The book opened for grief the way some locks open for blood—he had learned that in the first week of his flight, weeping over Enkidu's body, and felt the clasps give like knuckles loosening.

Now, in this quiet valley house, he laid both hands flat on the cover and thought about the archive. About the shelves of Ossuary's recorded centuries going up like wheat. About the names that existed nowhere else now except in this one book, carried out through the pass by a scholar too cowardly to die with his colleagues.

The clasps sighed open.

The pages inside were not paper. They were vellum so old it had gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had that quality Idris had seen only twice before—the quality of writing that expected to be read. Letters stood up off the page slightly, the way a sleeper sits up when someone says their name.

He turned past the genealogies of dead kings. Past the ledger of the roads, every mile of Ossuary catalogued in a hand so fine it could only have been written by lamplight and love. Past the hymns, the contracts, the recipes, the children's rhymes—a whole country compressed into a single binding, because compression was all that remained.

Then he reached the page dated a hundred years past, and stopped breathing.

It was a sketch. Charcoal, lightly fixed, the work of someone who drew faces often and well. A young person—not quite man, not quite woman, or perhaps deliberately both—looking back over one shoulder at the artist, as if interrupted mid-turn. Wide cheekbones. Eyes that held the particular wariness of someone who has spent a lifetime being careful about being seen. One hand half-raised, fingers curled as if around something invisible. As if listening to it.

Below the portrait, in the same hand as the rest of the book:

*Mirabel Oyelaran. Born of Ashenmere. Speaker without training, keeper without office. The door out of a country that no longer exists opens by this one's oath, and closes by this one's silence.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear:

*Do not let them burn the witness.*

Idris read it four times. Then he read the date again, because the mind does that—it checks the impossible against itself, hoping arithmetic will intervene.

A hundred years. The sketch was older than anyone in this valley. Older than the valley's oldest grandmother. Whoever had drawn Mirabel Oyelaran had drawn them from prophecy or vision or some instrument of knowing that the covenant in these hills was specifically designed to suppress—and they had drawn the face of a living stranger sleeping one room away.

Because it *was* the stranger. Of course it was. Idris had collapsed at the market with the book clutched to his chest, half-dead of wounds and winter, and the first face he had seen clearly—that young person shouldering through the crowd toward him, shouting for water, for space, for help—was this face. He had thought, even then, in the gray sludge of near-death: *I know you.* He had assumed fever.

The hand half-raised in the sketch. Yesterday, kneeling beside him in the mud, that same hand had hovered over his wound, and Idris had watched the market pigeons land in a ring around them both—watched them stand there, unnaturally still, like an honor guard—while the stranger whispered something under their breath that might have been prayer or might have been conversation.

*Speaker without training.* No wonder they had hidden it. No wonder the whole valley seemed built of careful silences.

A floorboard creaked outside the door. Idris closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin, and lay back down, and pretended to sleep, and listened to the muffled council convening beyond the wall.

* * *

They gathered in the main room below, and the house's timbers carried their voices to him with the honesty of old wood.

There were five of them. He had counted heads through the fever-haze: Mother Yew, the eldest, whose hands were stained permanently with indigo; the twins Ansa and Bode, who farmed the high terraces; a stern woman named Ferren who spoke little and looked at Idris the way farmers look at weather; and Mother Sade, the younger of the two women who had taken in the wounded stranger years ago—adopted mothers, he'd gathered, though the word *orphan* had never once been said aloud in his hearing. In Ashenmere, apparently, even grief was kept quiet.

"The elders vote tomorrow," Mother Yew said. "But we decide tonight what to tell them."

"Burn it," said Bode. No hesitation. "Burn the book, send him on his way, and nothing ever happened here."

"It's a book," Ansa said. "It's a hundred years old and it came out of a country nobody remembers. There are people who would pay—"

"People," Ferren said, and the single word did more work than any speech.

"Ansa means sell it," Bode said. "Which is worse. Selling it puts it in a road. Burning it puts it in ash. Which would you rather the usurper find?"

"The usurper finds nothing," Mother Sade said. "That is the entire point of us. That has always been the entire point." Her voice had a crack running through it, like good pottery. "Four hundred years ago our grandmothers made a bargain. We don't speak magic here. We don't name ourselves in any prophecy. We live, and we are let alone, and in exchange no army ever comes up the pass. And now this man walks into our market bleeding a century-old book that names our child—"

"Our *child*," Mother Yew said softly.

"Yes," Sade said. "Our child. Our Mirabel, who we raised, who we fed, who we taught to keep quiet about whatever it is they do with the animals—which, yes, Yew, I know, we agreed never to say it aloud, but it's saying itself now, isn't it? The book says it. So either we burn the book, or we admit that everything we've protected here was already broken the moment Mirabel was born."

Silence, below. Idris lay in the dark with his heart going like a bird in a cupped hand.

"And if we burn it?" Ferren asked, at last, in her gravel voice. "Does burning stop what's written? Or just stop us reading it?"

"You've seen it too, then," Sade said.

"I saw the face. Anyone who looks after them sees the face." A pause. "Burning a warning doesn't stop the thing warned of. It stops the preparation."

"So we prepare for what? Soldiers? Prophecy?"

"We prepare for the question," Ferren said. "The book calls Mirabel a door. Doors don't choose which side they're opened from. If we burn the book and pretend otherwise, we haven't protected Mirabel. We've just made sure Mirabel learns the truth from strangers with worse intentions than ours."

"That's if the book tells the truth."

"It told the truth about the face."

Another silence. When Mother Yew spoke, her voice had aged ten years in an hour. "The elders will vote to burn it. You know they will. Fear votes faster than hope."

"Then we decide tonight," Sade said, "whether we obey fear."

Idris heard a chair scrape. Heard footsteps cross the room below—two sets, unhurried, deliberate—and then the stairs began to creak, and he had perhaps six seconds to decide whether to be asleep.

He chose not to be. Lying was how scholars died in occupied countries; he had watched it happen. He sat up as the door opened, and the lamplight came in with Mother Sade and Mother Yew, and all three of them regarded each other across the small room like people meeting on a bridge neither side controls.

"You heard," Sade said. Not a question.

"I heard," Idris said. "And before your elders vote—I want to show you what they'll be voting to burn."

He unwrapped the oilcloth. The clasps resisted him this time—they had tasted the room, maybe, smelled the fear in it—but grief was always available to him now, a well he never had to dig, and they opened. He turned to the page and laid the book in Mother Yew's lap, and watched her face as she looked at the charcoal sketch of the child she had helped raise.

Her breath went out of her slowly, like air leaving bread.

"A hundred years," she whispered. She touched the corner of the page, not the face. Even now, even with proof, she would not touch the face. "This is older than the covenant. Older than the forgetting. Someone drew our Mirabel before—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Before Mirabel was anything at all."

"Not before," Idris said. "Outside. That's what I've come to understand, carrying this thing across half the world. Time doesn't work on prophecy the way it works on us. The book didn't predict Mirabel. It *recorded* them. From somewhere time hasn't reached yet." He leaned forward. "Mothers. Listen to me. Tomorrow your elders will vote to burn this book because burning feels like safety. But the last line—read the last line."

Sade read it aloud, her voice steady until the final words. "*Do not let them burn the witness.*"

"Witness," Yew repeated.

"Not savior. Not king. Witness." Idris took the book back with hands that shook and hated themselves for shaking. "I fled a country that was erased—every map, every record, every memory outside these hills. Do you understand what that means? It means there are millions of the dead who exist nowhere now except in this binding. And the book says the only door out of that erasure opens by Mirabel's oath and closes by their silence. Your valley bought invisibility with a bargain. But bargains have parties, and one party to yours was Ossuary, and Ossuary is gone, and the bill has come due a hundred years late, addressed to your child."

"And if we refuse?" Sade asked. "If we burn it, and hide them deeper, and wait it out?"

"Then the usurper's inquisitors find it eventually. They are already looking—I didn't lead them here, but they follow me the way wolves follow a wounded thing, and my being here has started clocks whether you like it or not. And when they come, they will come to a valley that chose ignorance. Ignorance is not protection. Ignorance is just dying surprised."

Mother Yew looked at him for a long moment. Then at the window, where the valley lay dark and trusting under its hills.

"You talk like a man who has already lost everything," she said.

"I am," Idris said. "That's why I can't lose this too."

Downstairs, faintly, a door opened and closed. Footsteps in the yard—light, quick, trying very hard to be quiet, failing the way young people fail at quiet when their hearts are loud. Both mothers heard it. All three of them went still.

"They've been on the stairs," Mother Sade said quietly, "for the last ten minutes."

Above the hills, past the ridge, past the pass, the night wind came down into the valley carrying, very faintly, the smell of horses and iron. Idris did not need a prophet's gift to know what that meant. Somewhere north, men were riding with fire in their saddlebags, following the scent of a book.

And somewhere below, in the dark yard, Mirabel Oyelaran was standing among the chickens and the goats, listening to voices none of the adults could hear—and deciding.
