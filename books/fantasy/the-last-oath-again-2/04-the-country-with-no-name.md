## 4. The Country With No Name

**Mirabel**

The pass spat them out into a country that did not exist.

Mirabel felt it before they understood it—the way a tooth knows it has cracked long before the tongue finds the fracture. Behind them, Ashenmere's mountains closed like a book. Ahead, the land simply went on, and yet every instinct Mirabel had, every story told in every kitchen of the valley, insisted that *on* was impossible. Beyond the pass there was nothing. Beyond the pass the maps stopped, the songs stopped, the names stopped. Generations of Ashenmere children had grown up believing the world ended at those peaks, and here was the proof of the lie, spread out green and gray under a sky the color of old pewter.

"Say something," Idris said hoarsely. He had not slept since the elders' vote; he looked like a man carrying his own funeral pyre on his back, which, in a sense, he was—the locked book rode against his ribs in its oilcloth wrapping.

"It's real," Mirabel said. "That's what you want me to say."

"I want you to say it so *I* can believe it." He laughed, a dry broken sound. "I have read about Ossuary for eleven years. Footnotes. Marginalia. A river named in one source, crossed out in another. And now—" he gestured, and his hand shook— "*now.*"

The crows came down off the crags as they descended, six of them, riding the wind in loose formation, and their voices arrived in Mirabel's head like gravel poured into a bowl.

*Old roads below,* said the eldest, a ragged-throated female they called Sorrel because her beak bore a scar the exact color of sorrel leaves. *Old roads and old hunger. The two-legged dead do not walk here, little speaker, but their names still do.*

"What does that mean?" Mirabel asked aloud, and then had to explain to Idris why they were talking to birds.

Below them the valley floor unrolled: terraced fields gone to seed a century ago, stone walls standing waist-high like the ribs of something enormous, orchards where the trees had gone wild and bitter but still, impossibly, fruited. No dust on the roads. No weeds through the flagstones. As if the country itself refused to accept that it had been forgotten, and kept itself swept for guests who would never come.

"The purge wasn't just armies," Idris said quietly. He had stopped walking. "My order's oldest records said the usurper didn't conquer Ossuary. He *unwrote* it. Paid magicians—before magic turned on him—to cut it out of memory. Out of prophecy. Out of maps." He touched the book through the oilcloth. "But beasts don't keep human records. Beasts remember."

"That's why the covenant," Mirabel said slowly. The thought had been forming all day, cold and heavy. "Ashenmere hides from prophecy too. We're invisible. I always thought it was protection. But maybe we're just—" they searched for the word and hated it— "*spare*. Kept blank in case someone needed to carry a message through the erased part."

Idris looked at them with an expression Mirabel couldn't name, somewhere between pity and apology.

"Don't," Mirabel said. "Don't look at me like my whole life is a footnote in your book."

They walked on. The crows wheeled above, patient as punctuation.

Near dusk they smelled woodsmoke where no smoke should be.

*Travelers,* Sorrel reported. *Two. One smells of soldier. One smells of nothing, which is worse.*

Mirabel and Idris froze behind a fallen wall. Through the gap they watched a fire burn small and disciplined in the lee of a ruined granary. Beside it sat a broad-shouldered woman mending a boot with the efficient violence of someone who had been ordered to mend boots by people she despised, and beside her, small and quick-eyed, a child who was eating what appeared to be half a stolen chicken with the focus of a professional.

The woman saw them first. Of course she did; she'd heard them a mile off, probably. She didn't reach for the sword at her hip. She just raised her voice.

"If you're inquisitors, come slow, because I'd rather die tired than run scared. If you're not, the chicken's gone, but there's barley."

"We're not inquisitors," Mirabel called back, because lying seemed pointless to someone with eyes like hers.

"Everyone says that." She shrugged. "Barley's still yours."

*

Her name was Sefa, and she had deserted from the usurper's Third Column four months ago, after the order came down to burn a village whose only crime was appearing in an old ledger. The child was Wren, who had no surname, no origin story, and no interest in providing either, and who had picked Sefa's pocket within an hour of meeting her and then, insultingly, returned everything except the buttons.

"You're going into Ossuary proper," Sefa said, when Mirabel explained—carefully, incompletely—where they were headed. She said it flatly, the way you'd confirm a diagnosis. "Past the Bone Hills."

"Yes."

"Then you need me. Because your birds know the roads, but birds don't sleep, and things in this country hunt what sleeps." She tied off the boot repair. "I marched through the edge of it once, before I knew what it was. My whole column lost a night. Just—one night, gone, and we woke up three days older and nobody could agree on what we'd eaten."

"That's not a reason to come," Idris said. "That's a reason to run."

Sefa looked at him with something close to contempt. "I ran from one burning village. You think I can watch another? You think any of us can?" She jerked her chin at the book. "Whatever that thing is, the usurper wants it burned or buried, and that's enough of a recommendation for me."

Wren swallowed the last of the chicken. "I'm coming too."

"You are absolutely not," Sefa said.

"I know the Bone Hills. I've lived in the hills since I was seven, and the Bone Hills are the best hills, because everyone's afraid of them and nobody checks the traps." Wren wiped greasy fingers on a shirt that had clearly lost that battle long ago. "Also you'll lose the road without me and die stupidly, and I'll feel bad forever."

"You'll feel bad forever anyway," Sefa muttered. "You stole a corpse's ring two weeks ago."

"*Found* a corpse's ring. There's a difference, morally." Wren stood, small and certain as a drawn bolt. "Besides. Everyone here is running from something. I'm just running faster."

Mirabel looked at these two strangers—a deserter with a soldier's hands and a thief with a child's arithmetic—and felt the strange vertigo of a door opening. In Ashenmere they had known every face since birth, loved and smothered and hidden among the same hundred families until love and hiding became the same act. This was the opposite of that. This was four people and a fire and no reason at all to trust each other, choosing it anyway.

"All right," Mirabel said. "But we move at dawn, we share what we have, and nobody dies stupidly."

"No promises on the last one," said Wren.

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa had marched with liars, cowards, zealots, and worse, and she knew within a week that this company was none of those things, which somehow made it harder.

The scholar, Idris, drove himself past exhaustion, reading the book by firelight when he thought no one watched—reading, Sefa suspected, the way a starving man chews leather. Whatever was written in it frightened him more than the inquisitors did. Once she caught him tracing a sketch on the page with one finger, lips moving, saying over and over: *a hundred years, how could it be a hundred years.*

The child, Wren, was a marvel and a menace: could find water in bare stone, could pick any lock made by human hands, could not walk past anything shiny without acquiring it. Sefa woke twice to find her boots rearranged and once to find a sprig of rowan tucked under her bedroll, which Wren claimed was for luck and Sefa privately suspected was an apology for something not yet discovered missing.

And the valley-born one—Mirabel—who spoke to crows as easily as breathing, who flinched when anyone said the word *home*, who carried themselves like someone performing the role of a person while the actual person stood slightly behind, watching.

Sefa had deserted an army. She knew what leaving cost. She recognized the wound when she saw it.

They fought constantly. That was how Sefa knew it was working.

"You can't ask the badgers to scout the shrine," Wren argued on the fifth night, around a fire of bone-dry gorse. "Badgers hold grudges longer than people. Ask a fox."

"Foxes lie," Mirabel said.

"Foxes *negotiate.*"

"There's a difference?"

"Obviously! Foxes tell you the truth if you pay them. Badgers tell you the truth if they feel like it, and then bill you for it later."

"And crows?" Sefa asked, enjoying herself more than she'd admit.

"Crows tell everyone," Mirabel and Wren said together, and then glared at each other for agreeing.

Idris laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a moment the firelight made them all look less like fugitives and more like something else. Something Sefa hadn't had since she was younger than Wren. She looked away, suspicious of the feeling, and found Mirabel watching her with that too-perceptive gaze.

"You miss it," Mirabel said quietly. "The column. Even hating it."

"I miss being sure," Sefa said. "It's not the same thing. I'm learning that late."

Ahead, dark against the stars, the Bone Hills rose like a spine of the world. And on the ridge line, brief as a struck match, something glinted—not starlight, not moonlight. Lantern-glass. Moving. Keeping pace with them along the high ground.

Sefa stood up slowly, hand on her sword.

"Company," she said. "And they've been following us since noon."
