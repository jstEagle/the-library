## 5. The Other Speaker

Sefa had learned to read a country by its birds.

In the king's army—*the usurper's army now*, he corrected himself, and the correction still tasted like blood—they had taught him to read terrain: the slope that would break a charge, the treeline that hid a picket, the streambed where an ambush waited with its throat already open. But the birds were older teachers than any sergeant. When the finches went quiet along a hedgerow, men were coming. When the crows lifted off a field in silence rather than in their usual argument, something dead lay below them.

For three days since Ossuary's edge had swallowed them, the birds had said nothing at all.

That was the wrongness he could not name to the others. A country erased from maps should have been empty of voices, and instead it was *full*—thrushes in the ruined orchards, herons standing patient as sentries in the drowned rice terraces—but every one of them was silent about the road ahead. They looked at Sefa when he passed. He could feel it, that attention, like sunlight through a crack in a shutter. They looked at him and they looked at Mirabel, and they did not speak to either of them, and it was the not-speaking that frightened him, because silence in an army meant discipline, and discipline meant orders.

"They're holding their tongues," he told Mirabel on the third evening, while Wren picked through the bones of a farmhouse for anything worth stealing and Idris's book sat wrapped in oilcloth between them like a sleeping animal. "Birds chatter. That's what they do. These ones are *choosing* not to."

Mirabel glanced up from the fire they were building small and smokeless, the way valley folk built fires, and Sefa watched that look again—the one that made his chest hurt in a way he refused to examine. Mirabel listened to birds the way other people listened to weather. Casually. As if the whole world were simply talking and they'd never thought to be amazed.

"Maybe they're waiting for us to say something first," Mirabel said.

"Maybe," Sefa said, "they're waiting for someone else entirely."

He was right, though he wouldn't know it until the moon was up.

* * *

The ambush came at the hour when even deserters sleep worst—the deep trough of night when the body forgets it is supposed to be afraid and the mind remembers everything at once. Sefa woke not to sound but to absence: the crickets had stopped, all of them, in a single instant, like a door closing.

He had his knife out before his eyes opened.

There was a woman sitting at the edge of their campfire's ash, on the far side, where no one had approached. She had not come through the dark so much as she had simply decided to be there, the way frost decides to be on a window. She was dressed in inquisitor grey but without the insignia, the way a soldier strips the badges when he wants to move fast or lie well. Her hair was cropped short. Her hands were folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral she approved of.

And around her, in the black branches above the camp, sat every bird within a mile, silent, watching.

"Wren," Sefa breathed. The child was already awake—he was always already awake; Sefa had stopped wondering what dreams a thief slept through—and Wren's hand found his sleeve in the dark.

"I see her," Wren whispered. "I don't like her."

Mirabel sat up slowly. And Sefa saw something pass across their face that he had never seen there before—not fear, exactly, though fear lived underneath it. It was recognition. It was the look of someone seeing a portrait of themselves painted by a stranger who knew too much.

"You're Kess," Mirabel said.

The woman smiled without warmth. "You say my name like you've been expecting me. Good. I'd hate to think Idris wasted the whole journey teaching you nothing." She tilted her head toward the wrapped book. "How is the old man? Still pretending the burning will buy mercy?"

Sefa moved then, putting himself between her and the others, knife low, the old geometry of protection asserting itself in his body before his mind consented to it. "That's far enough."

Kess looked at him the way one looks at a fence. "Deserter. I know your file. Third Lancers, the year the river forts fell. You ran during the retreat from Callow Ford." She said it gently, almost kindly, which made it worse. "Don't mistake me for the ones who'll shoot you for it. I'm here because I have a message, and messages deserve a quiet room. This is the quietest room in Ossuary tonight—I made sure of it."

"The birds," Sefa said. His mouth had gone dry. "They're yours."

"They were always mine." Something flickered behind her eyes then, some old bruise pressed. "That's the part none of them understand. Not the inquisitors, not the elders in your little valley. They think the gift is a thing you find, like a coin in the road. It isn't. It's a *choosing*. Every day. Silence or service. I chose service eleven years ago and I have eaten well and slept safe ever since." She unfolded her hands. "So here is my message, valley-child, and hear all of it before you do anything foolish."

She stood. Up close she was younger than her voice—younger than Sefa, maybe, though she carried herself like the mountains carried snow, as if age were a weight she'd learned to distribute evenly.

"Lord Inquisitor Vane offers you passage home. Full pardon for the scholar's crime, though the scholar himself—" a small shrug, elegant and terrible, "—is past pardoning, whatever happens. The valley keeps its covenant. Ashenmere stays unseen, unprophesied, unmapped. Your mothers grow old in peace. The crows go back to gossiping about beetles. All of it, restored, forever—*if* you give me the book and walk back through the pass."

"And if not?" Mirabel asked quietly.

"If not, you are unmade." Kess said it without theater, which was somehow more frightening than theater. "Not killed. Killed is quick and honest. Unmade. The covenant works both ways, valley-child—it doesn't just hide you from prophecy, it hides you from *record*. One word from Vane and every ledger, every register, every memory written down of Mirabel Oyelaran goes blank. Your mothers will remember a child who died young, and grieve you twice. The world will have a hole shaped like you and no reason to wonder why. I've seen it done. There's a man in the capital who walks through markets and no one steps aside for him, no one sells to him, no one remembers lending him bread. He screams some nights. The screaming doesn't help."

Wren made a small sound beside Sefa, and Sefa's hand found the boy's shoulder without thinking.

"Why you?" Mirabel asked. And then, softer, with a gentleness that seemed to strike Kess harder than any blade could have: "Why did *you* take the message? Why walk out here yourself?"

For a moment—one breath, two—the mask slipped, and Sefa saw what was underneath, and it was hunger. Not for power. For *company*. The hunger of someone who had stood alone in a crowd of the obedient for eleven years, talking to birds because birds were the only things that answered honestly.

"Because I wanted to see you," Kess said. "The book has your face in it, drawn a hundred years before you were born. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere in that valley, a hundred years ago, someone wrote down a promise about a child who could talk to crows. And I read that page in Vane's library and I wept like a fool, because I thought—I thought I was the only one. My whole life, the only one." Her jaw tightened; the mask came back down, mortar and iron. "I wanted to see what the prophecy chose instead of me. What the world does with the one who stays silent."

"I didn't choose anything," Mirabel said. "I just never had anyone to betray."

The words landed like a dropped stone in a well. Sefa heard the long fall of them.

Kess laughed once, sharp, wounded. "No. You haven't yet. That's the difference between us—that and nothing else. Same gift. Same silence. Same loneliness wearing different clothes." She reached into her coat and drew out a strip of paper, setting it on a stone between them: a token, grey wax, Vane's seal—a closed eye. "Three days. The host crosses the pass in three days regardless. Bring the book to the shrine at the fork of the drowned road, or bring nothing and be found. Those are the whole of your choices, valley-child. I'd advise you to be boring."

She turned to go, then paused, half-turned, and looked at Mirabel one last time. Whatever she saw in their face made her expression flicker—envy, recognition, grief, all braided into something Sefa had no clean name for.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I hope you run. Not for your sake." Her mouth twisted. "If you surrender, then it was all for nothing—all of it, every choice I made, every year I told myself there was no other door. If you run, then maybe the other version of me was possible too." She stepped backward into the dark between two dead fruit trees. "Three days."

Then the birds rose off the branches all at once, a black exhalation against the moon, and she was gone as completely as she had arrived.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The fire had died to a red memory. Wren had pressed himself against Sefa's side, and Sefa kept his hand on the boy's shoulder, feeling the tremor there, feeling ashamed of the tremor in his own chest answering it like a struck bell.

"She let us live," Wren finally whispered. "Why would she let us live?"

"Because she needs us to make her choice mean something," Mirabel said.

They were looking at the token on the stone, the closed grey eye, and Sefa watched their face and understood suddenly that the ambush hadn't ended. It had only changed shape. Kess had walked into their camp carrying a message, yes—but she had also walked out carrying theirs. Somewhere in that cropped-haired, well-fed, well-armored body was a person who had wept over a page in a library, and people who wept over pages could be reached. Or broken. Sefa had seen both happen to better soldiers than he'd ever been.

"So," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the most useful trick the army had ever taught him. "Three days. An inquisitor host behind us. A gate ahead of us that needs a dead man's name." He looked around the circle of faces—the child, the scholar's empty bedroll, Mirabel with the moonlight on them. "Anyone want to argue for going home?"

Silence. But not the birds' silence, disciplined and ordered. This was the other kind—the kind a family makes when everyone has already decided the same dangerous thing and no one wants to be the first to say it aloud.

Mirabel picked up the token, turned it over once, and set it down again on the stone.

"No," they said. "But I want to know why the badger has been sitting at the edge of camp since midnight, waiting for us to follow."

And there, at the treeline, grey and patient as a gravestone, it was.
