## 6. Doors in the Bone Hills

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her life—bread, knives, a silver hairpin off a sleeping magistrate's wife once—but she had never before tried to steal her way into another country. That was what it felt like. The badger walked ahead of them through the fog of the Bone Hills like a key walking on four short legs, and every step he took was one more lock turning.

"His name is Ossik," Mirabel said for the fourth time that morning.

"I heard you."

"You keep calling him 'it.'"

"He hasn't told *me* his name," Wren said. "I don't presume."

The badger paused and looked back over his shoulder at her with an expression she could only describe as judicial. His fur was gone white as chalk along his muzzle and shoulders, and his left ear was a torn rag of old scars, but his eyes were black and wet and ancient as wells. When he looked at you, you felt audited.

*She does not presume,* he rumbled, in the way that all beast-speech came—not words exactly, but meanings that pressed themselves into your skull like thumbs into wax. Wren was not a speaker; she'd never claimed to be. But somewhere between Ashenmere and here the party had stopped needing Mirabel to translate everything, and now the animals' meanings leaked through to all of them, like water through a bad roof. *Presumption is all she has.*

"That's slander," Wren said.

"It's accurate," Sefa said from behind her, not even trying to hide the grin.

Wren decided not to dignify that. She scrambled up onto the next ridge instead, boots finding purchase in bone-white rock, and got her first real look at what lay ahead—and stopped so suddenly that Sefa nearly walked into her back.

The hills were named honestly. They rose out of the fog in long pale curves, ribbed like the carcass of something enormous, and set into their flanks were shapes that Wren's eyes refused, at first, to make sense of. Towers. Arches. Stairways running up bare stone to nowhere. A whole city's skeleton, grown into the hills themselves, its walls fused with the rock as if the mountains had swallowed a kingdom and kept its bones on display.

"Saints' teeth," Sefa breathed.

"No saints," Ossik corrected grimly. "Kings. This was the high road of Ossuary. Once there were lanterns." He said *once* the way other people said *never again*.

Mirabel came up beside her, hugging Idris's book against their chest the way they always did, arms crossed over it like a mother shielding a child from rain. Wren had watched them do it for weeks now. She'd also watched Mirabel talk to it when they thought no one was listening—apologizing to it, coaxing it, asking it questions it never answered. It was, Wren thought privately, the worst-behaved object she had ever seen, and she had once known a fence in Corvane who swore his dice lied to him.

"The door's close," Mirabel said. Their voice had that stretched-thin quality it got when hope and fear were fighting for the same throat. "Ossik says two days. Maybe less, if the weather holds."

The weather did not hold.

* * *

It came down the valleys like something chasing them: sleet with a core of true cold, the kind that got under wool and into joints and made small children swear like soldiers. Wren had been cold before. She'd slept three winters in a drain culvert behind the tannery row in Corvane, and she considered herself an expert on cold the way Ossik was an expert on roads. But this cold had a personality. It wanted them to stop. It pressed down on them like a hand.

They sheltered that night beneath an archway that had once been somebody's gatehouse, and Wren sat closest to the small fire Sefa coaxed from wet gorse, because nobody argued about it anymore. In the first week they'd fought over fire proximity like cats. Now it was just understood: smallest person, nearest warmth. A family arranged itself around her without ever using the word.

Wren hated how much she liked it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about belonging. You started keeping track of things. You knew Sefa took the first watch because she still didn't trust her own dreams after deserting. You knew Mirabel counted everyone aloud under their breath each night—*four, four, four*—like a prayer against subtraction, ever since Idris. You knew your own chest did something stupid and painful when the badger grunted at you approvingly, which was more affection than anyone in Corvane had ever paid her.

So when she caught Mirabel awake past midnight, firelight guttering on their face, tracing the book's pages with one finger over and over like a gambler touching a losing coin, Wren recognized the look. She'd worn it herself, the winter she'd decided she was going to steal enough to buy passage south or die trying. Hope sharpening into obsession. It looked almost identical to hunger.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

"So should you."

"I'm on thieving hours." She shuffled over and peered at the page upside down. Inked lines. A drawing of hills—*these* hills, unmistakably, the same ribbed skyline—with a gate at their heart, and beneath it a line of script in a language that predated the printing presses of three nations. "What's it say?"

"The same thing it always says." Mirabel's voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone holding something very heavy by a very thin rope. "*The door knows one name, and the name went into the ground with the last king. No tongue carries it. No book keeps it.*" They closed the book gently, like shutting a coffin. "A hundred years of scholars have read this page, Wren. Do you know what that means? Everyone who might have remembered the name is dead. Everyone who might have written it down wrote *this* instead—a warning that it can't be written down."

"Locks are just puzzles," Wren said. "Puzzles have answers. Somebody made this lock. Makers leave flaws. My whole life is proof that locks lie."

Mirabel looked at her then, really looked, and something in their face cracked open—not despair exactly, but its cousin. "And if there's no flaw? If we've come all this way—" Their hand flattened on the book's cover. "Idris died for this. I'm not going to stand at the door and knock politely."

"There it is," Wren said softly.

"What?"

"Nothing." She pulled her blanket up. "Go to sleep, Mirabel. Four. We're all four. Nobody else gets subtracted."

But she lay awake a long time after, listening to the sleet tick on thousand-year-old stone, doing what she did best: casing a lock in her head. Somewhere ahead of them a gate waited that no living name could open, and Wren found, to her own surprise, that she wasn't afraid of it. She was offended by it. Doors existed to be opened. It was practically a law of nature, older than any prophecy.

* * *

The sleet broke on the second morning, and the world came out scrubbed and glittering, and Ossik led them down a defile so narrow they had to walk single file, their shoulders brushing walls carved with processions of figures whose faces had weathered to smooth blanks. Wren trailed her fingers along them as she passed. Kings, maybe. Priests. Children. All of them faceless now, erased not by any usurper's decree but by simple wind and time—the slow erasure that no covenant or purge could match.

"They're beautiful," Mirabel whispered.

"They're warnings," Ossik said. "This was the road kings took to their oaths. Every figure you touch swore the old oath in their day, and every oath ended the same way: with the speaker bound to whatever they swore on, forever." He stopped. Ahead, the defile opened like a mouth. "We're here."

Wren had imagined the door a hundred ways in two days of walking—as a slab of black iron, as a wall with no seam, as a cave mouth breathing cold. None of it prepared her for the truth.

It stood alone in a bowl of valley where nothing grew, a freestanding arch taller than ten men, carved from a stone that wasn't stone—grey shot through with veins of gold that moved, slowly, like light through honey. Through the arch she could see only mist. Not fog. Mist that didn't move with the wind. The air around the arch hummed at the bottom edge of hearing, a note she felt in her fillings.

And covering every inch of it, inside and out, were names.

Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. Carved in scripts she didn't recognize and some she half-did, layered over one another until the surface looked like a storm of letters frozen mid-fall. Her eyes kept snagging on them, sliding off, snagging again. Reading it felt like eavesdropping on a hundred years of whispers.

"It's a ledger," Sefa said hoarsely. She'd gone grey around the mouth. As a soldier she'd seen mass graves; Wren watched her count the names the way she counted bodies. "Every name that ever opened it. Or—" her jaw worked, "—every name it ever took."

"Not quite," Ossik said. The old badger limped forward to the base of the arch and sat, formal as a magistrate. "These are the witnesses. Each who passed spoke the oath and signed themselves into the stone. The last signature is the king's." He nodded upward, toward the crown of the arch, where one carving sat alone above all others, larger, deeper cut, its letters filled with gold that had not dulled. "And beneath his name is the seal. The door has been shut since the day he died, because the door opens for the oath of whoever stands before it—and the oath requires a name the stone will accept."

"A name no living person knows," Mirabel repeated. They stepped forward, and the humming changed pitch, deepened, like a held breath. The veins of gold brightened faintly where they stood.

Wren watched their face and saw the obsession complete itself, hope annealed into something harder and more dangerous. Mirabel pressed both palms flat against the arch, and the mist inside it shivered.

"It knows me," Mirabel breathed. "Wren, do you feel it? It's *waiting*. It's been waiting a hundred years for someone to stand here and—"

"Careful," Sefa snapped, hand going to her sword out of pure habit. "Doors that wait are doors that want something."

"Everything wants something," Mirabel said. They weren't listening anymore; Wren could see that. They'd gone somewhere past listening, into that country where Idris's death and a hundred dead years and a valley full of mothers who forgot on purpose all weighed together in one hand. "The name died with the king. Fine. Then we stop looking for the name." They turned, and their eyes were terrible and bright. "We find the *oath*. Idris said—his last words—the door answers not a king's name but a witness's oath. The name is just the price. The oath is the mechanism."

"And if you swear wrong?" Wren asked quietly. "If it takes you like it took them?" She pointed up at the storm of carved signatures. "Look at how many people thought they had it right."

For a long moment nobody answered. The wind moved the mist in the arch, and the mist ignored the wind, and the gold veins pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

Then Ossik's ears went stiff as blades, and his growl rolled out low and flat across the empty valley.

*Riders,* he said. *On the southern ridge. Many. They fly the grey and red.*

Sefa cursed. Wren spun, squinting through the cold clear air—and saw them: a long thin line descending out of the high pass, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who had never once needed to hurry.

Inquisitors. Here. Days too early—or maybe not early at all. Maybe they'd always known exactly where the door was, and had simply been waiting to see who would lead them to it.

"Mirabel," Wren said. "Whatever you're going to do—do it fast."
