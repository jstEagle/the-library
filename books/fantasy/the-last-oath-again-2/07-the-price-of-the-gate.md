## 7. The Price of the Gate

Idris had always known the book would cost him something. He had simply assumed it would be taken from him at the end, pried from his dead hands by some clerk in some archive, catalogued and shelved and forgotten—the scholar's fate he'd chosen at nineteen when he'd traded a comfortable life in the free cities for the smell of old vellum and the company of dead languages.

He had not imagined dying for it on a hillside that smelled like wet stone and sheep dung, with his boots full of meltwater and a child's hand gripping his sleeve too hard.

"They're close," Sefa said. The deserter lay flat against the ridge crest, her body gone very still in the way of people who had learned stillness as a survival trade. "Three riders on the road below. Four more in the treeline, dismounted. They've split to pinch us against the scree."

"How can you possibly—" Wren began.

"Because I ran from those colors once, and they taught me how they hunt." Sefa didn't lift her head. "Idris. How long to burn it?"

The question landed in Idris's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way before it struck anything.

"Not the book," he said.

Mirabel looked up from where they crouched beside him, ash-grey eyes catching the last of the light. Behind them, the badger—old, grey-muzzled, patient as geology—waited at the mouth of the game trail with the terrible calm of a creature who understood exactly what men did to one another.

"The book is the only copy of—"

"The book," Idris said, "is half decoy." He had never said it aloud. Not to Mirabel, not to Sefa, not in all the weeks of cold camps and shared hunger. It felt like confessing to a fraud, which in a way it was. "When the purge came, I couldn't carry everything. There were four hundred pages of commentary, marginalia, translations of translations—I burned them myself rather than let the inquisitors read how much we knew. What's left is the core text and the final leaves. And forty blank pages I cut to match, sewn in behind the true ones, so that anyone who seized it and started flipping would find—" he made himself smile, though his face had gone strange and cold, "—a great deal of nothing."

Wren stared at him. "You put *rubbish* in a holy book?"

"I put *time* in a holy book." Idris drew the book out of its oilcloth wrapping and held it against his chest. Through the leather he could feel the shape of it, the weight of a century of waiting. "An inquisitor who believes they've captured everything stops hunting so hard. An inquisitor who finds blank vellum starts asking whether there was ever anything here at all. Time, child. I bought us time with paper, and now I'm going to buy more of it the same way."

"You're not making sense," Mirabel said quietly.

"I'm making a trade," Idris said. "And you're going to hate me for it, and then you're going to live, and one day you'll understand it was fair."

* * *

They argued for eleven minutes. He counted them against the bells of a city he'd never see again, an old habit from the years before the purge, when time had been a thing you spent instead of a thing that hunted you. Sefa argued because she was a soldier and soldiers do not abandon their wounded; Wren argued because Wren argued with everything, on principle, as if the world could be shamed into kindness by sheer volume; Mirabel argued without raising their voice at all, which was somehow worse, standing close enough that Idris could see the pulse jumping in their throat.

"If you stay," Mirabel said, "they'll kill you."

"They'll try," Idris said. "I'm a slippery old man. Ask anyone. Ask my dissertation committee."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Idris agreed. "It really isn't. But listen to me, both of you—listen, because we don't have time for this twice." He caught Mirabel's wrist, and the book between them felt suddenly warm, or perhaps that was only his own blood remembering itself. "The gate in the Bone Hills needs a name. You've been turning yourselves inside out trying to find it—a king's name, a founder's name, some word buried under Ossuary's ruin. I've been reading the wrong question into the text for twenty years, and I think I finally read it right three nights ago, in the dark, listening to you talk to that badger."

Mirabel went very still.

"The last king of Ossuary died nameless," Idris said. "That's in the histories—that part survived, because it was shameful, and shame always survives better than glory. His own court unmade his name before the end; erased it from every record; spoke it never again. That's why the door has been shut a hundred years. It isn't sealed by magic. It's sealed by *silence*." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "A king's name won't open it, because no king ever gave it anything. But an oath—an oath spoken aloud, witnessed, given freely by someone with no crown and no army and nothing to gain—" He stopped. Breathed. "The door doesn't want to be commanded. It wants to be *testified to*. Do you understand? Someone has to stand in front of it and swear that Ossuary existed. That's the key. Not a name. A witness."

"And you know this how?" Sefa demanded. "Because if you're guessing—"

"I'm a scholar," Idris said. "Guessing carefully is the whole profession." He pressed the book into Mirabel's arms. "Take it. Take the badger's road and don't look back no matter what you hear. And Mirabel—" he gripped their shoulder, feeling the bird-bone fragility of them, this person a hundred-year-old prophecy had drawn in ink before they were born, "—when you speak that oath, speak it for everyone who couldn't. Not just the valley. Not just yourself. The dead keep faith better than the living do. It's the least we owe them."

Below them, faintly, came the sound of a horn. Two notes. The inquisitors' signal for *found*.

* * *

He walked down toward the road with the decoy pages bundled in his coat and a torch stub in his hand, and he was surprised to find his legs steady. Fear was there—it was a vast cold ocean filling every hollow of him—but beneath it was something else, something he hadn't felt since he was young: the clean clarity of a sentence finally finished. Twenty years of scholarship had been, he realized, mostly procrastination dressed as devotion. This walk down the hill was the actual work. Everything else had been preparation.

They let him get within thirty paces. Of course they did. Inquisitors liked to watch their quarry approach; it confirmed the rightness of the hunt. The leader was a tall woman with a grey cloak and a face like a folded letter, and beside her—Idris's stomach turned, though he'd known, he'd known since Kess had spared them at the ford—walked the other beast-speaker, Kess, hands loose at their sides, eyes fixed somewhere over Idris's left shoulder as if the sky had become suddenly interesting.

"Scholar Idris Vane," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone reading a list aloud. "Holder of a stolen text. Confidant of sedition. You are instructed to surrender the book and submit to questioning."

"I have surrendered the book," Idris said, holding up the bundle. "Here. Come and take it."

She didn't move. Neither did he. Somewhere above them, small and silent, four figures were moving along the badger's trail toward the Bone Hills, and every second of this pleasant conversation was a brick in the wall between them and the riders.

"You're stalling," Kess said. Their voice was flat, but Idris heard the hairline fracture in it, the sound of someone who had already decided what they were about to allow and hated themselves for it.

"Astute," Idris said. "You should have been a scholar."

"We tracked you across two provinces," the inquisitor said. "Did you imagine a fire would stop us?"

"Oh, I don't need a fire to stop you." Idris struck the torch against his flint, and the bundle in his other hand caught light with a soft, almost gentle sound, like a sigh. "I need it to stop *you*. Those pages are the only surviving account of what your master did in Ossuary. Every village. Every name. Who signed the orders." The flames climbed his sleeves and he barely noticed; the pain seemed to belong to someone slightly behind him. "It's burning, madam. In ninety seconds there will be nothing left but ash and whatever you can scrape off my body, and you will have to explain to your usurper why you returned with smoke and a corpse instead of proof. Unless—"

"Unless?" The word cracked out of her like a whip.

"Unless the beast-speaker you brought," Idris said, looking directly at Kess, whose face had gone white as bone, "tells you what they already know. That the book was never the weapon. That the weapon is a sentence, and sentences can't be burned, and one is walking up that mountain right now wearing a borrowed coat."

"Kess," the inquisitor said slowly, "what is he talking about?"

Kess said nothing. Kess looked at the burning bundle, and then up the slope, and Idris watched—through smoke and tears and the first real roar of flame—watched them choose, in that moment, to be slow. To hesitate. To spend three seconds they would never get back staring at a horizon where a small figure with a book was disappearing into the rocks.

It was enough. It would have to be enough.

The fire reached his lungs. The hill tilted. The last thing Idris Vane saw clearly was not the inquisitors closing in, nor the grey cloak, nor the flames eating the lie he'd built to save the truth. It was the badger, motionless on the ridgeline above, watching him with ancient, unhurried eyes—as if taking down a deposition, as if entering his death into a record older than any nation.

*Witness*, the old man thought, with the last of him, *it counts even if no one claps.*

Then the dark took the page, and somewhere far above, in the wind-scoured silence under the Bone Hills, a door waited for a voice.
