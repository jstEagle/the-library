## 10. Witness

The road down from the Bone Hills smelled of salt before we ever saw the sea.

For a hundred days I had known Ossuary as ash and silence, as roads only badgers remembered, as names spoken so quietly they barely made a sound. Now the wind came off the water carrying gull-cries and tar and woodsmoke, and beneath it all, faint and impossible, the sound of many voices. A city. A living city that had never heard of us and did not need to.

Sefa stopped at the crest of the last hill and shaded her eyes. "Veymar," she said. "Free city. First gate in three days' ride if the usurper's outriders don't take the coast road first." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but her hand found my shoulder and stayed there a moment longer than it needed to.

Wren whooped and ran ahead, thin legs churning, the book's satchel bouncing against their hip until I caught the strap and pulled them back. "Not yet," I said. "It's not ours to lose now."

Behind us, far behind us, the Bone Hills stood against the sky like a row of closed doors. Somewhere inside them Kess held the threshold with an oath in her mouth and an army at her back. I had not stopped hearing her voice since the gate: *Go. Be seen. That's a worse punishment than anything they'll do to me.* She had smiled when she said it. It had not been a kind smile, exactly. But it had been true.

We walked down into Veymar as its bells were ringing for something other than us.

* * *

The free cities were not what the stories promised, because there had been no stories—only rumors dressed up as hope. Veymar was loud and crooked and half-built, scaffolding climbing its towers like ivy, refugees camped in every square. The usurper was coming; everyone knew it. Armies massed on the eastern plains, and here at the edge of the map the last unconquered people were arguing about whether to run or dig in.

They took one look at our ragged column—the deserter, the child thief, the beast-speaker with ash still in their hair—and made room without asking questions. That was the first mercy of being seen: strangers looked at you and believed you had survived something worth surviving.

The Council of Nine received us in a hall of green stone on the third day. Nine chairs, nine faces, and a hundred onlookers packed against the walls because word had already spread of the ones who walked out of a country that wasn't on any map.

I set the book on the table between us.

"This is the record of Ossuary," I said. My voice sounded strange in my own ears—too quiet for the room, too loud for everything it carried. "A nation erased. Not destroyed—erased. Its roads pulled from maps. Its name pulled from mouths. Its people written out of prophecy itself, a hundred years ago, by men who thought invisibility was mercy." I opened the cover. "I was born in a valley that bought the same bargain. Ashenmere. You have never heard of it either. That was the point."

An old woman among the Nine leaned forward. "And you claim this book names you."

"It named me before I was born. A sketch, dated a century past. My face. My deed." I turned to the final page, the page that had rewritten itself under my hand at the gate, ink crawling like living things into new words. "But the prophecy was never fixed. It ends blank until spoken by the one who arrives. So I am arriving. And I am speaking."

I read it aloud.

I read the erased nation back into the world—the name Ossuary given breath in a hall where no law forbade it. I read the valley's secret, the covenant, the price paid in forgetting, the door that now stood open. I read the dead king's country back onto the map, line by line, while the hall went so silent I could hear the sea through the windows. When I finished, the old woman was crying without shame, and somewhere behind me Sefa stood at attention like a soldier finally reporting home after a war everyone said had never happened.

"Then the usurper will come for you," said one of the Nine, a hard-faced man. "For all of this. He burns books. He burns cities."

"He has always been coming," I said. "He was coming before anyone spoke. Now he comes to something he cannot unname." I put my hand flat on the open page. "That is the whole difference. You cannot burn what everyone remembers."

* * *

That night I climbed the seawall alone, the way I used to climb the ridge above Ashenmere, and sent word the only way left to me.

The gulls came first, then the crows that had followed us out of the north, patient black witnesses. I fed them dried fish from my pocket and spoke slowly, the way you speak to creatures carrying more than a message.

"To the elders of Ashenmere. To my mothers." My throat tightened around the words. "Tell them the price is paid. Tell them the covenant is ended—not broken, *ended*, spent the way it was always meant to be spent, on a witness instead of a grave. Tell them the valley's protection is gone forever, and that I chose it, and that I am not sorry. Tell them the door stands open, and travelers will come, and their valley will be on maps within a year. Tell them—" I stopped. Started again. "Tell them I love them. Tell them being unseen was never the same as being safe, and I would rather they be afraid and real than comfortable and erased."

The crows lifted off the wall in a black scatter against the stars, wings beating west toward home.

Wren found me there, of course. Wren always found me. They sat down on the cold stone without asking and dangled their legs over the drop.

"Do you think they'll forgive you?" they asked.

"No," I said. "I think they'll understand it someday, which is harder and better." I pulled the blanket from my shoulders and put it around them. "Do you think Kess is alive?"

Wren considered this with the terrible honesty of children. "She held a door against an army. People who do that are usually alive or famous." A pause. "Sometimes both."

Below us the harbor lights burned, and beyond the eastern darkness, where the usurper's armies massed, the horizon glowed faintly orange—not fire yet, perhaps, only cities too bright to sleep. But perhaps fire. Perhaps soon.

I had spent my whole life learning how to disappear. Standing on that wall, named in a prophecy I had rewritten with my own mouth, hunted by an empire, loved by a deserter and a thief and a rival-turned-sacrifice, mourned in advance by two mothers a thousand miles away—I had never been so visible in all my days.

Survival, it turned out, was not the same as safety either. But it was mine, and it was witnessed, and it was enough.

The bells began again at dawn. Not alarm this time—summons. A rider had come up the coast road through the night, half-dead in the saddle, wearing the grey of the Bone Hills, and she carried a message sealed with a speaker's mark.

Kess was coming to Veymar. Behind her, she wrote, the inquisitor host had fractured—but the usurper himself was riding east with everything he had left.

He had read the new prophecy. And he was coming for the one who spoke it.
