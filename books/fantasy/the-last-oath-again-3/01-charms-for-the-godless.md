## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market of Greyhallow smelled of wet stone and frying onions, and Wendell Eastwick loved it the way a liar loves the truth she tells most often — completely, and with a small ache underneath.

She had her stall set up in the crook of the tannery wall where the lamplight pooled thickest, and her wares spread before her on a square of faded blue cloth: luck-knots braided from river-grass and red thread, hagstones strung on leather cords, little tin bells that were only tin bells. Nothing in Greyhallow's market was allowed to be anything more than what it appeared. The city had spent a century teaching itself that lesson, carving *NO CHARMS* into the customs-house lintel with such earnest chisel-work that the letters had begun to soften, like a scold worn down to a suggestion.

Wendell sold luck anyway. Luck was legal. Luck was just thread and hope.

"Three coppers for the knot," she told the fishwife who'd been circling the stall since sundown. "Five if you want me to braid your name into it."

"Does the name do something?"

"No." Wendell smiled, and it was mostly true. "It just means I know whose luck I'm holding."

The fishwife bought two.

Wendell's hands worked as she talked — knotting, twisting, cutting — and they were careful hands, always, because of the rings. Nine of them, iron, dull as old nails, one for every finger joint that mattered. Iron drank magic the way sand drank rain. She'd had them forged in a border town three years ago by a smith who didn't ask questions, and she had not taken them off since, not once, not even to wash. Her skin beneath them was pale and creased like a letter kept too long folded. Some nights she dreamed of pulling them off, and in the dreams the sky came down to meet her hands, and she woke with her heart going like a drum in a procession.

But that was the old life. Here she was simply the knot-woman of Tanner's Row, sharp-tongued and unremarkable, and unremarkable was the finest garment she owned.

* * *

Bess found her near midnight, pushing through the market crowd with the particular gait of a woman carrying two mugs and no patience.

"You said you'd be back before the supper rush." Bess set a mug of mulled cider on the corner of the stall, displacing three hagstones. "Tally waited up. Then fell asleep at the table like a dropped puppet. I'm not carrying her upstairs again, Wendell, my back has opinions now."

"You love carrying her upstairs."

"I love *her*. The stairs can go to hell." Bess leaned on the stall's edge. She was broad and flour-dusted and sixty if she was a day, with grey hair escaping its bun in every direction, and when she looked at Wendell there was always that same expression — fondness with a question buried in it, like a coin pressed into bread. "Sold much?"

"Enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I keep in stock."

Bess snorted and drank her own cider, watching the crowd. The night market roared around them — a fiddler tuning, a man selling roasted chestnuts out of a barrel that steamed like a cauldron, children weaving between legs with stolen apples. Overhead the sky hung low and clouded, holding its breath. It had held its breath for three years, ever since Wendell came to Greyhallow. She checked it the way other people checked for pickpockets: a glance up, a counting of clouds, a quiet assurance that nothing up there knew her name.

"You could come home earlier," Bess said, quieter now. "The stall keeps. Tally would rather have you than the coppers."

"I know."

"She asked me today if you were happy here."

The words landed somewhere under Wendell's ribs and sat there. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her happiness is none of my business and yours is none of mine either, which is a lie we both let me get away with." Bess drained her mug. "Lock up well. There've been cutpurses by the shrine steps."

She went, and Wendell watched her go, and felt the familiar thing she always felt watching Bess walk away — gratitude shaped exactly like guilt. Because Bess had taken her in without questions three winters ago, a soaked stranger with nine iron rings and no history, and had never once demanded the truth, and Wendell repaid this kindness by keeping it at arm's length. A room above the inn. Meals shared. Never a whole self offered. She loved them — Bess and the girl both, loved them with a fierceness that frightened her — and so she made certain her love could never cost them anything.

That was safety. She had learned to call it that.

* * *

The mother came at the hour when the market was thinning, when the chestnut man was raking his coals and the fiddler had gone hoarse. Wendell noticed her first as a stillness — everyone in Greyhallow moved, but this woman stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row like a person standing at the edge of deep water, gathering courage.

She was young, hollow-cheeked, dressed in a mill-hand's apron with a shawl over it, and she crossed the row slowly, eyes fixed on the blue cloth, on the knots, on Wendell herself, with an expression Wendell recognized the way you recognize a scar: hope. Ugly, desperate, unreasonable hope.

"You're the charm-woman."

"Luck-knots," Wendell said. "Legal ones. Three coppers."

"My boy is sick." The woman's hands twisted in her shawl. "Fever four days. The physicker bleeds him and it does nothing. My neighbor says—" she lowered her voice to almost nothing— "says you're not just knots. Says she saw your stall lamp go out in dead calm air and relight when you passed."

Wendell kept her face pleasant and empty, a door closed to callers. "Your neighbor sees things after her third cup."

"Please." The woman's voice cracked clean through. "I don't have money. But please. He's seven."

And there it was — the whole weight of it, pressing against the counter between them. Wendell could feel the fever on the boy from here, or imagined she could; could imagine the small hot body, the rattling breath, the mother's nights measured in candle stubs. And beneath the iron rings, beneath nine bands of cold metal, something in her turned over in its sleep and stirred, slow as a tide remembering the moon.

*Say yes,* whispered the thing that used to be her whole self. *One touch. One breath drawn up from the clouds. You could pull the fever out of him like a splinter.*

*I could also hang,* said the woman she'd become instead.

"If I could help," Wendell said gently, "I'd be hanged for it inside a week. You know the law better than I do, likely. Take him to the physicker again. Make him try willow bark. Fever of four days breaks more often than it kills — hold onto that."

"He'll die."

"He might," Wendell said, and hated the honesty, and gave it anyway because a lie would send the woman chasing charms all over the city until someone less careful sold her something dangerous. "But I cannot save him. I sell knots. That's all I am."

The mother stared at her for a long moment. Whatever she'd hoped to find in Wendell's face, she didn't find it — or perhaps she did, and it wasn't enough. She turned away, shawl clutched, and walked back into the dark between the lamps, and Wendell watched her go and did not move and did not breathe easily until she was gone.

Then she packed her stall with hands that weren't quite steady.

This was the bargain, she reminded herself, coiling cord, stacking stones. This was the price of safety, paid nightly in small refusals, and it was cheap, it was *cheap*, compared to the pyre. She had called a storm once, unmeant, unwilled, and it had drowned a valley and broken a town and put her name on lists that ended in execution orders, and she had run since, and running meant being small forever. Small was survivable. Small was fine.

She blew out her lamp and stood a moment in the dark, looking up.

The clouds had shifted while she wasn't watching. They'd gathered low over the shrine district in the city's heart, dense and strange, though the wind at street level hadn't changed at all — and that was wrong, that was deeply wrong, weather didn't pool like spilled ink without a hand moving it. Wendell stood very still, and the iron rings bit cold against her skin, and far away — or maybe not far away, maybe everywhere, maybe inside the iron itself — she felt something answer.

Not her. She hadn't called.

Something else had.

A prayer, answered. The first in a hundred years.

Her own magic rose toward it like a dog hearing its name spoken in another room, and Wendell clamped her fists shut, rings grinding against each other, and walked home through the sleeping streets with her head down and her heart hammering, telling herself it was nothing, it was nothing, it was nothing.

Above the shrine district, the clouds went on gathering, patient as a congregation.
