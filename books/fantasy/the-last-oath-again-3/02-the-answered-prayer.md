## 2. The Answered Prayer

Wendell woke before dawn to the sound of the city holding its breath.

She had slept badly, as she always did after a refusal. The mother's face had followed her down into sleep — that grey, hollowed face with its cracked lips and its terrible, hopeful eyes — *please, storm-bringer, please* — and Wendell had turned her away with iron rings biting into her fingers and a lie sitting in her mouth like a stone: *I'm sorry. I can't help you.*

Not *I won't.* Not *there's nothing to be done.* Just *I can't*, which was true in every way that mattered and a lie in all the ways that counted.

Now she lay on her narrow bed above Bess's taproom, listening to Greyhallow breathe, and something was wrong with the breathing.

She couldn't have said what, at first. The city made its usual pre-dawn sounds — the creak of the fish-carts rolling down to the harbor, the first cough of Bess's kitchen fire below her floorboards, gulls quarreling over the midden heaps. But beneath it all ran a hum, faint and low, like a struck bell heard through three walls. Like the moment after lightning when the air itself remembers.

Wendell sat up slowly. Her hands were trembling, and she didn't know why, and she hated not knowing why.

*Iron,* she thought. She reached for the rings stacked on the nightstand — seven of them, plain grey metal, cold as confession — and worked them onto her fingers one by one. The hum did not stop. But it grew quieter, or she grew deafer to it, and either way she could stand.

She dressed in the dark. She went downstairs.

* * *

Bess was already at the hearth, sleeves rolled, wrestling dough with the focused violence of a woman who solved most problems with her forearms. She glanced up when Wendell came in, and her flour-white hands stilled.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," Bess said.

"I heard one." Wendell lowered herself onto a bench near the fire. The warmth should have comforted her. It only reminded her how far away heat was from lightning, how carefully ordinary this room was built to be. "Something's wrong with the air this morning."

"The air's fine. Tally's the one you want to worry about." Bess nodded toward the corner where Tally slept curled in a chair by the banked oven, a blanket pulled up to his chin. He'd insisted on sleeping there, close to the kitchen warmth, ever since he'd come down with the fever three days ago. "He's worse. I was half-minded to fetch old Marta, but—" She shrugged, and the shrug said everything the words didn't. Doctors cost coin, and coin was thin this season, and prayer cost nothing because prayer did nothing. That was the bargain Greyhallow had lived under for a hundred years, and everyone knew its terms by heart.

Wendell crossed to the boy. Tally was nine years old, all elbows and stubbornness, a child who had survived the workhouse and the streets and the winter two years back when the harbor froze and the fish died in their thousands. He had come to Bess's door with lice and a stolen spoon and an unshakeable conviction that Wendell Eastwick was the most interesting person alive. He slept now with his brow furrowed even in fever, as if illness were an opponent who might cheat him.

She pressed the back of her hand to his forehead. Heat bloomed against her skin, dry and wrong, and beneath it — beneath it she felt something else. A tremor in him. Not the shiver of fever. Something deeper, like a plucked string still singing.

Her stomach turned over.

"Tally," she said softly. "Tally, wake up."

His eyes opened. They were clear. Bright and clear and utterly free of fever, and he looked up at her with mild annoyance, the way he looked at anyone who interrupted a good dream.

"What? I'm fine. Is breakfast on?"

Wendell's hand was still on his forehead. Cool. Cool as riverstone. Three days of burning, and overnight — between one hour and the next, sometime in the dark — the fever had simply *left* him.

"Bess," Wendell said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, careful. "When did you last check on him?"

"Middle of the night, why?" Bess wiped her hands. "He was still hot as a kettle. I put a cool cloth—"

"He's not hot now."

Bess crossed the room in four strides and pressed her palm flat against the boy's brow, then his cheeks, then held her hand above his chest as if feeling for rain. Her expression moved through disbelief, suspicion, and finally arrived at a kind of fierce, frightened joy that Wendell recognized because she had once worn it herself, years ago, in another life.

"That's not right," Bess said. "That's not — fevers don't just *leave*. Marta said if it broke natural we'd know, there'd be sweat, there'd be—"

"Did anything happen in the night?" Wendell kept her voice level. "Anyone come? Anything strange?"

Bess frowned. "Well. His mother came."

The words landed in the quiet kitchen like a dropped glass.

"She what?"

"Came round past midnight, weeping, wouldn't be turned away. You'd refused her, so she went where people go when nothing else works." Bess's voice dropped. "The old shrine on Tanner's Lane. The one to Veyra. Nobody's tended it since my grandmother's day, but she knelt down in the dirt and prayed anyway. What else does a mother have left?" Bess shook her head. "She came here after, sat with him till nearly morning, praying over him. Then she went home. And now he's—" She gestured at Tally, who was inspecting the bread situation with open interest. "It's coincidence," Bess said firmly, as if saying it loudly enough would make it true. "Fevers break. It happens."

"It doesn't happen like this," Wendell said quietly.

Because she could feel it — standing this close to the boy, she could feel it humming in him like a struck bell, the same note she'd heard through the walls of her room. A residue. A signature. Power had passed through this child in the night, ancient and vast and utterly indifferent to him personally, the way a flood is indifferent to the ant it carries. Something had answered that woman's prayer. Something had *heard*.

For the first time in a hundred years.

* * *

She told Bess she needed air. She walked, and walking did not help.

Greyhallow in the early light was a city built on top of its own griefs. Once, Wendell knew from the books she wasn't supposed to own, this had been a god-haunted country — shrines on every corner, offerings in every doorway, gods walking openly among mortals until the first king shattered the oath-stone and bound the divine out of the world. The magic laws had come later, aimed at mortal practitioners, but the shrines had been abandoned long before, left to stand like teeth in the city's jaw: the little shrine on Tanner's Lane with its weather-worn statue of a woman holding scales; the sea-god's alcove by the fish-gate where sailors used to leave copper coins; the ruined temple up on Callow Hill whose dome had fallen in a century ago and never been rebuilt.

Wendell walked past them all, and they were all *humming*.

Tanner's Lane first. She told herself she was being foolish, but her feet carried her there anyway, down the crooked street where the tanneries' reek had faded to a memory soaked into the stones. The shrine stood in its niche, small and neglected, pigeon-droppings whitening the goddess's shoulders. But the dirt before it was churned and knee-printed — a woman had knelt here for hours — and the air around the statue shimmered, very slightly, the way air shimmers over a forge.

And the statue's stone face — Wendell stepped closer, hating herself — the statue's carved lips were *wet*, as though the stone itself had wept in the night.

She backed away. Around her, the street was waking, and she saw it happening everywhere at once: a fishwife pausing before the sea-alcove, frowning, touching the old coins there as if expecting them to be warm. An old man on Callow Hill's lower steps, staring up at the broken temple with his mouth open. Two children pressing their ears to the shrine wall and giggling — *it tickles, it tickles* — because children would feel it first, children always felt it first.

By midmorning it was everywhere. In the market she heard it spreading mouth to mouth faster than any rumor of bread prices: Old Marta's patient, the one dying of the lung-rot, had woken whole. The chapel bell in Lowtown had rung itself at midnight. A woman swore the fountain on Copper Street ran sweet instead of brackish. The city that had spent a century learning not to hope was hoping, recklessly, hungrily, and Wendell moved through it all with her seven iron rings and her head down, feeling like the only sober soul in a tavern full of drunks.

Because she alone knew what hope cost here. She alone remembered.

* * *

She ended up on the harbor wall at dusk, because it was the farthest point from shrines and the closest thing to home the wind had.

The sea was restless. Not storm-restless — the water lay flat and grey — but restless underneath, currents shifting against each other, the tide coming in heavier than the almanac promised. Wendell felt it in her teeth. She felt everything in her teeth today.

Her magic was stirring.

Three years of silence, three years of iron and discipline and small careful luck-knots, and now it rose in her like a tide answering a moon she could not see. She could feel it below her sternum, that deep familiar pressure she had named and renamed and finally just called *the weather in me* — except now it wasn't hers alone. It was being *pulled*. Something vast was drawing breath somewhere beyond the world's edge, and every loose scrap of power in the kingdom was dragging toward it, and Wendell Eastwick, storm-caller, exiled daughter of the Ashvale towers, was a loose scrap of power whether she liked it or not.

She gripped the harbor wall. Granite bit her palms. Below her, the water heaved against the pilings with a rhythm almost like speech.

*I know you,* the pull seemed to say. *I remember what you are. Come home.*

"No," Wendell whispered, and the wind took the word and played with it, worried it like a dog with a bone.

This was the trap of it, the thing no one who hadn't lived exile could understand: it wasn't only dread twisting her guts. It was longing too, shameless and enormous, her magic rising toward whatever called it like a homesick child toward a lit window. For three years she had starved the gift down to embers, and embers remember fire. If she opened her hands right now, right here, she could raise a squall off the harbor that would send every fishing boat scrambling for mooring, and it would feel like *breathing*, like being *whole*, and everyone would see, and the wardens would come, and—

A bell rang across the city. Not the chapel bell — the palace bell, tolling slow and heavy, once, twice, a third time. Wendell had never heard it toll outside a royal death.

But nobody had died. She knew that somehow, in her bones, in the weather in her chest. Something had *woken* instead.

Down the quay, lamplighters were moving through the blue dusk, and above the rooftops the first stars were coming out over Greyhallow, and Wendell stood with her iron rings and her shaking hands and understood, with absolute clarity, that the life she had built — the knots and the taproom and Tally's laughter and Bess's dough-thumping certainties — was already ending. The past hadn't knocked at her door.

It had answered someone else's prayer. And it was coming for her next.
