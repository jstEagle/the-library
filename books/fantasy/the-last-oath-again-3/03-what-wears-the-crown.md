## 3. What Wears the Crown

The candles in the council chamber had been burning green for three nights.

Ilsa Vane noticed it the way she noticed everything: quietly, and with the particular unease of a soldier who has spent twenty years learning which details matter. Candles did not burn green. Tallow burned yellow, beeswax gold, temple-wax white. These flames sat low and steady in their sconces, casting a light like pond-water over the long table where the king's ministers bent over their ledgers, and no one else remarked upon it at all.

That was the first wrongness. There were others, accumulating like silt.

She stood her post behind the king's shoulder, as she had every morning for six years, hands folded at the small of her back, oath-ring cold against her sternum beneath her breastplate. Captain of the Oath-Guard. Sworn on iron and salt and the old words that half the court believed were superstition and the other half believed were the only true thing left in Vaelmark. She had spoken those words to a young king with tired eyes and his father's crown sitting too heavy on his brow, and she had meant them down to the marrow: *Your word is my wall. Your wall is my grave.*

She had not imagined she might one day need to swear an oath against the man himself.

"Read the next decree," said King Aldric.

His voice was wrong. That was the second thing. It was still his voice — the same low timbre, the same northern burr he'd never trained out of his speech — but underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, ran something older and vaster and terribly patient. When he spoke plainly it hid well. But when he read the decrees —

Chancellor Orrey cleared his throat and unrolled the scroll. "By royal decree: the tithe of vigor is extended to the districts of Marrowgate and the Weavers' Quarter. All persons of age shall present themselves at the collection shrines—"

"Collection shrines." Master Ferren, the treasurer, looked up from his ledger with the frown of a man who has found a sum that will not reconcile. "Highness, forgive me. Last month these were called *devotion shrines*. The month before, harvest-blessing stations. The language keeps changing."

"The language keeps becoming more accurate." The king did not look up. His hands lay flat on the table, fingers spread, and Ilsa saw — because she was the only one who ever stood close enough to see — that his knuckles had gone the color of candle-tallow. Of the candles. Green-gold light moved beneath his skin like something swimming under ice. "Read on, Chancellor."

Ferren subsided. They all subsided. That was the third wrongness: a room full of the sharpest minds in the kingdom, and not one of them had asked the obvious question since the prayers started being answered. *Answered by whom? At what cost?* They signed what they were given. They went home pale and slept twelve hours and came back pale again, and thanked the gods — the gods! — for their king's renewed piety.

The chancellor read on. The decrees flowed. Grain levies rerouted through the palace granaries, though the palace kitchens fed barely three hundred souls. A new observatory ordered built atop the old shrine of Veyra-the-Tidemother, its foundations sunk deep, deeper than any observatory required. Compulsory attendance at evening prayer for all palace staff — and Ilsa had seen the guards after evening prayer now, standing their watches with a dreamy slackness, their eyes like coins dropped in deep water.

And through it all, the green light in the candles burned a little lower each day, and the city outside the windows grew a little grayer, as though someone had drawn a veil between Greyhallow and the sun.

When the session ended and the ministers shuffled out with their scrolls, the king remained seated, motionless, watching the door long after the last of them had gone.

"Captain." Still that doubled voice. "A moment."

Ilsa stepped forward. Six years of standing behind him; she knew the geography of his moods the way she knew the walls of the citadel. This was not his mood. This was weather moving through a country she used to know.

"Highness."

"You have been watching me." He turned his head. His eyes were his own brown, warm, human — and behind them, far back, something looked out at her the way a woman looks out a window at rain she has no intention of going into. "You think I do not notice. You watch me the way a physician watches a wound. Tell me, Captain Vane — do you believe your king is ill?"

Every instinct she owned screamed at once, and she mastered them as she always did, folding them down flat. "I believe the realm faces strange days, Highness. My duty is unchanged by them."

"Ah." He smiled. It was Aldric's smile — crooked, self-deprecating, the smile that had once made a raw young lieutenant forget herself entirely for half a heartbeat — but it arrived a half-second too late, like thunder after lightning. "Duty. Yes. You are very dutiful, Ilsa. It is one of the things I—" He stopped. Blinked. And for one naked instant the thing behind his eyes receded, and it was only Aldric there, exhausted and frightened and drowning, mouthing something at her — two syllables, *don't*, or perhaps *run* — before the tide rolled back in and smoothed his face to calm. "One of the things I value most in my guard. Dismissed, Captain."

She saluted. She walked out with her boots striking the flagstones at parade rhythm, because parade rhythm was the only thing holding her spine straight.

In the corridor, alone, she pressed her hand flat against her sternum until the oath-ring beneath the mail bit skin, and made herself breathe.

*Don't.* Or *run.* She would turn it over all night, as she'd turned over a hundred such fragments. The king surfacing inside his own body like a man breaking water for air. She could not decide which possibility horrified her more: that she had imagined it, or that she hadn't.

* * *

That night she broke into the archive.

Not the public chancery with its clerks and its catalogues — the deep archive, the one beneath the east wing, sealed since the reign of Aldric's grandfather, accessible to the crown and to no one else. She went at the hour when even the night guard dozed against their pikes, wearing no armor and carrying no torch, because Ilsa Vane had learned her trade in border forts where getting caught meant dying slowly, and some skills do not rust.

The lock yielded to a thin blade and thirty years of patience. Inside, dust hung in the air like a held breath. Her little hooded lantern showed shelves of vellum going back past the founding of the city, and she realized, with a strange lurch, that she had no idea what she was looking for. Only that the answers to *what wears the crown* would not be found in the ledgers upstairs.

She searched by instinct. Old oaths interested her; she pulled volumes of them. Coronation rites. The rite of the Vigil, which every monarch kept on the eve of crowning — alone, in the shrine of Veyra, as tradition demanded. Tradition. She'd always assumed it was empty ceremony, like the mock jousts at midsummer.

Then she found the marginalia.

A chronicler's hand, cramped and hurried, in a history of the first king, Torvald the Unifier. The passage concerned Torvald's famous victory over "the meddling gods" — standard founding myth, taught to children. But scrawled beside it, in ink gone brown as old blood:

*He did not defeat them. He bound them. The oath-stone was the seal — the pact that drew the line between divine and mortal, that the gods should take nothing from us without giving, that we should owe them worship but never our selves. Torvald shattered it so that no power above his own should remain. The shards were scattered. The priests wept and were silenced.*

*We have lived a hundred years godless and free. May it last.*

May it last. Ilsa set the lantern down on the reading desk with a hand that was not quite steady. Outside, faintly, through stone and earth, she could feel it: a hum, low and rhythmic, like a hymn sung by a throat too large to be contained in any body. The shrines, singing. The whole city humming along in its sleep.

If the stone had been broken — if the binding had been broken —

Her mind refused to finish the sentence. Her body finished it instead: gooseflesh rising up both arms, the animal knowledge of a thing standing behind you.

She thought of the king's knuckles, tallow-pale. Of the guards after evening prayer. Of the districts added, week by week, to the tithe of vigor. *Vigor.* Such a clean administrative word for whatever was being carried out of ten thousand sleeping houses and poured into the palace like wine into a bottomless cup.

She took the chronicle. Theft from the royal archive was treason; she noted this detachedly, the way one notes the weather, and wrapped the book in her cloak, and climbed the stairs into the gray hour before dawn.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the king held open court, and Ilsa watched him grant the petitions.

This was Aldric's favorite duty once — she remembered it, remembered him leaning forward on the throne, genuinely delighted by farmers and fishwives, remembering names, asking after daughters and drowned sons. Now he sat straight and still while the petitioners filed past, and granted everything, and the green candlelight pooled around the dais like spilled broth.

An old woman came to thank the gods for her grandson's healing — the fevered boy from the tannery lanes, restored overnight, a miracle, the first miracle in living memory. The king smiled his late-arriving smile and said the gods were generous, and the hall murmured its wonder, and Ilsa stood behind his shoulder doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

Miracles returning after a hundred years. Decrees draining the city's strength. A binding broken somewhere, by someone, and a god — *which god?* — drinking deep of a kingdom that had forgotten how to be careful.

After court, she requested a private audience. It was denied. So was the second request, and the third, and on the fifth day the captain of the inner watch — a good man, a dull man, a man who attended evening prayer — informed her pleasantly that the king's schedule no longer permitted audiences with the Oath-Guard, who would of course continue their most valued ceremonial duties.

*Ceremonial.* The word landed like a slap. She thanked him and walked away before her face could betray her.

That evening she stood her final post outside the throne room door and listened to the sounds within: the king's voice, rising and falling in a cadence that was not speech, not quite — a liturgy in a language that predated the kingdom, that predated perhaps the stones of the palace itself — and beneath it that vast hum, swelling now, close now, like a tide coming in over a drowning coast.

Ilsa Vane closed her eyes and reviewed her oath. *Your word is my wall.* But what if the word was stolen? What if the wall sheltered a devourer? Every law she knew, every chain of command, every loyal bone in her body pointed one direction: upward, to the crown, to obedience. And everything she had seen for five days pointed the other way, down into the dark archive, out into the gray city, toward whatever stranger might still remember the old lore of bindings and unbindings.

Because there would be someone. There was always someone. In a city where charms were contraband and prayers had gone unanswered for a century, someone had survived who knew what answered them now.

She needed a storm-caller. Exiled, hidden, struck from the rolls — but storms leave traces, and so do the people who call them. She had heard the rumors, three winters past, of the night the sky over Greyhallow split open without a cloud in sight. She had heard where the woman fled afterward.

Ilsa turned her collar up against the green-lit dark and went, quietly, to find Wendell Eastwick.
