## 4. Iron Rings Broken

The rings had always been the worst part.

Wendell counted them each morning, as she had for three years — seven bands of cold iron, one for every finger but her thumbs, forged by a smith in Lowmarsh who never asked why a woman wanted her hands caged. They were plain things, pitted with rust at the seams, ugly as coffin nails. She wore them the way other women wore wedding bands: faithfully, constantly, and with a grief she did not speak of.

Iron drank magic. That was its nature, the way sand drank rain. With the rings on, Wendell Eastwick was only a charm-seller with clever fingers and a talent for knots. Without them, she was the storm that had drowned the village of Harrow's Bend, that had torn a chapel roof off like a lid from a pot, that had put three years and an ocean of distance between herself and everyone who once knew her name.

She checked them now, standing in the doorway of Bess's inn, watching the sky over Greyhallow do something it should not have been able to do.

It was midday, and the sun was up, and still the clouds were coming. Not drifting — *coming*, the way armies come, rank upon rank out of the west, green-black and low-bellied, boiling over the hills like something poured. The city's bells had begun to ring in no pattern anyone recognized. Down in the streets people were stopping mid-stride to look up, market baskets forgotten in their hands, faces turned upward with expressions Wendell knew too well. Hope. The naked, stupid, beautiful hope of people who had prayed into silence for a hundred years and suddenly heard breathing on the other side of the door.

Her own skin prickled beneath the iron.

*Not yet,* she told the feeling, as she had told it a thousand times. *You are nothing. You are a knot-seller. You are safe.*

But the tide in her blood had stopped listening weeks ago, ever since the boy Tam was healed, ever since the shrines began to hum, and today it rose against the iron like floodwater against a door.

"Wendell." Bess appeared beside her, wiping flour from her forearms, her broad face pinched. "Tally went to the Lantern Quarter. I told her to stay in — you know how she listens — but there was a procession going past, all those pilgrims from the river villages, and she wanted to see."

"When?"

"An hour ago." Bess's voice dropped. "Wendell, the sky."

The sky. Yes. Wendell was already pulling her coat from its hook, already moving, the seven rings clicking against the doorframe as she passed through it — seven small, useless sounds.

* *

Greyhallow in the grip of god-surge was a city gone strange. Incense smoke rolled through the streets in rivers, thick enough to taste — myrrh and old tallow and something sweeter underneath, something that made the back of Wendell's teeth ache. Pilgrims knelt in the gutters with their arms spread wide. A woman stood on a fountain's edge preaching in tongues, her voice cracking with joy. And everywhere, rising out of every shrine and niche and household altar, that hum — a sound below hearing, felt in the sternum, felt in the fillings of teeth, the sound of a held note that had been held so long the world had grown around it.

Wendell ran through it with her collar up and her fists clenched inside the iron, and with every block the pressure behind the metal grew. It was like walking deeper underwater. The surge pressed on her gift the way deep water presses on a diver's ears, and somewhere under the city's noise she could feel the true source of it, far away, enormous, patient — a great slow heartbeat of devotion being drunk somewhere north of here. Being *used*.

She didn't let herself think about what could drink a kingdom. She had Tally to find.

The Lantern Quarter was worse. The old shrine of Veyra stood at its heart — a squat granite thing, centuries old, its goddess-statue worn faceless by weather, ignored for generations the way all the shrines had been ignored. But now it blazed. Candles by the thousand choked its steps. Worshippers packed the square shoulder to shoulder, swaying, singing, their voices climbing toward the bruise-colored sky, and above them the clouds had begun to *turn*, slowly, in a circle centered on the shrine's crooked spire.

Wendell shoved through the crowd, calling Tally's name, and got no answer but song.

Then the spire sang back.

It was not a loud sound at first — a groan of stone finding a new pitch, a tuning-fork note that climbed and climbed. The crowd faltered. Faces tilted up. Wendell looked at the shrine and saw, with a storm-caller's eye that she would have given anything to close, exactly what was happening: the surge pouring into the old granite faster than the dead god's house could hold it, filling it like a wineskin, the mortar between the stones beginning to weep light—

The spire cracked from crown to root.

What came after happened quickly and would live in Wendell's memory forever in fragments: the crowd's scream becoming one animal voice; the statue of Veyra splitting down the middle like a loaf; a section of the roofline peeling away with a slowness that seemed almost gentle; and beneath that falling mass of granite — small, red-cloaked, frozen — a girl of eleven years with a braid coming loose and a face Wendell had raised from infancy, looking up at death with the terrible calm of someone who has not yet learned what it is.

There was no decision. There was no thought at all. There was only the oldest reflex in her body, older than fear, older than exile.

Wendell tore the rings from her fingers.

Seven small sounds — seven small *screams* — and then the world opened. Three years of dammed storm broke through her all at once, and she cried out, not in triumph but in agony, because it was like being unmade and remade in the same instant, like a drowned woman breaching surface, every nerve igniting. The air went white around her hands. Above the square, the clouds answered their mistress before she even called — the whole churning wheel of sky convulsed, lightning crawling along its underside like veins of fire in smoked glass.

She threw both arms at the falling roof and *pulled the storm down between*.

Thunder detonated over the Lantern Quarter with a crack that burst windows down three streets. The bolt struck the collapsing masonry dead-center and did not destroy it — it *held* it, a pillar of white fire and compressed thunder propping the tons of broken shrine aloft while the crowd stampeded clear, while Bess's girl stood blinking in the sudden shadowless glare, while Wendell stood rooted in the emptying square with her arms upraised and her hair lifting around her head in a dark corona, every secret she had ever kept written in fire across the sky.

The last of the worshippers fled. The last of the rubble settled onto the lightning's shoulders and stayed there, groaning. And when Wendell finally let the storm go — let it reel back up into the clouds with a sound like a sigh — the silence afterward was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

Tally was running toward her, mouth working. Wendell couldn't hear her yet. Her ears were full of thunder's after-ring. She knelt, and the girl crashed into her, and Wendell held her and felt — through the ringing, through the terror — the strangest thing.

Relief. Vast and shameful and unmistakable. Three years of hiding, and now it was over, and some starved creature in her chest was *thanking* the sky.

Then boots, many boots, and steel.

* *

They came out of the smoke in formation — palace guard, six of them, and ahead of them a woman Wendell recognized even before the crowd's whispers named her: Captain Ilsa Vane, oath-guard to King Aldric himself, tall and gray-eyed and grim as a drawn blade, her cloak still smoking faintly at the hem as though she'd run through fire to get here. Perhaps she had.

"Stand away from the child," the captain said.

"They're staying exactly where they are," Wendell said. Her voice came out scraped raw. She rose slowly, putting herself between the soldiers and Tally, and saw their eyes flick to her bare hands — to the faint blue shimmer still crawling in the creases of her palms, refusing to die. Seven iron rings lay scattered in the ash around her feet like a broken rosary. Everyone in the square understood what they meant. Everyone in Greyhallow knew what bare-handed storm-light meant.

Storm-caller. The word moved through the onlookers in a whisper, gathering weight, and Wendell watched three years of careful nothing collapse in real time. She waited for the panic to come, the old panic, the animal certainty that her life was ending.

It didn't come. What came instead was that awful, quiet relief again, spreading through her like warmth into frozen hands. *They see me.* Whatever happened next, no one would ever again look through her as if she were furniture. It was a humiliating thing to feel with swords pointed at her chest, and she felt it anyway, fully.

"On your knees," said one of the guards, leveling a crossbow.

"She saved Tally," Bess's voice rang out from the crowd, and others took it up — *she held the roof, she held it, we'd be dead* — but the captain lifted one hand and the square went silent, because captains' hands carry more authority than crowds do.

Ilsa Vane walked forward alone. Up close her gray eyes were doing something complicated: cataloguing Wendell the way a soldier catalogues a weapon found on a battlefield. "Wendell Eastwick," she said quietly, and Wendell's blood went cold, because that was her true name, her *convicted* name, the name from the trial at Harrow's Bend. "Exiled storm-caller. Sentenced under the old law, which says I put you in irons and the headsman finishes the paperwork by morning."

"Captain—" Bess started.

"I know what the law says." Vane didn't raise her voice. She was looking at Wendell's hands again, and then, unexpectedly, at her face, with an expression that was not hatred and was not pity and took Wendell a long moment to name: recognition. One keeper of terrible secrets looking at another. "I also know what I've been watching for three weeks. I know the king's decrees are draining this city like leeches. I know the shrines aren't answering prayers — they're *harvesting* them. And I know," she said, very softly, "that whatever wears Aldric's crown right now, it isn't Aldric."

The square held its breath. Somewhere overhead, the turning clouds groaned.

"I can't unbind a god with swords," Vane said. "But a storm-caller pulled lightning out of a clear sky to save one child. That's either the most reckless criminal in the kingdom or the only person in it I can trust." From inside her cloak she drew a folded parchment bearing the royal seal — or what had been the royal seal; the wax had been scorched and re-stamped, and something about the new impression made Wendell's skin crawl. "So here is your bargain, Wendell Eastwick. Come to the palace. Help me free my king. Do that, and I will spend the last drop of my oath getting your sentence repealed — pardon, clean name, the whole rotten law struck from the record if I have to burn the archive to do it."

"And if I refuse?"

Vane's jaw tightened. "Then I arrest you now and pray the headsman's blade works on whatever the surge has planned next. Because I don't think it will." Her eyes went up, briefly, to the wheeling green-black sky. "Neither does it."

Wendell looked down at Tally, whose small hand had found hers and gripped it hard. She looked at the seven rings in the ash. She thought of Bess's kitchen, of luck-knots sold to strangers, of three years of safety that had always, always tasted like a locked room.

She picked up the rings, one by one, and left them lying where they fell.

"All right, Captain," she said. "Take me to your king."

Vane nodded once and turned — and Wendell saw her hesitate, just a half-step, her hand going briefly to her own breastbone as if pressing down on something buried there, something that hurt.

"Captain?" Wendell said.

"Nothing." But Vane's gray eyes had gone distant and afraid. "Only that the king spoke to me in my sleep last night. In his old voice. And he said—" she swallowed— "*don't come for me.*"

Above Greyhallow, the clouds finished their turn, and began to tighten.
