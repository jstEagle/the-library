## 5. The Oath-Stone

The archives beneath Veyrholm Palace smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath — a sweetness like incense burned down to nothing, like a prayer that had been sitting in a room for three hundred years waiting for someone to say it aloud.

Ilsa Vane had walked these corridors since she was nineteen years old, when she'd sworn her oath with her hand on Aldric's father's sword and thought she understood what duty meant. She had never once come down here. The archives were for scholars and priests, and the king's oath-guard went where the king went, no further.

But the king did not go anywhere now. The king sat in his tower chamber eating honeyed figs and issuing decrees in a voice like water running backward, and Captain Ilsa Vane had smuggled a condemned storm-caller into the heart of the palace at midnight because there was no one else left to ask.

"Mind the third step," Wendell murmured behind her. "It's loose."

"You've been down here before?"

"I've been everywhere in this city before." A soft huff of laughter, quickly swallowed. "Three years of hiding teaches you every back stair in Greyhallow. This is the first time I've ever used one to walk *toward* trouble instead of away from it."

Ilsa lifted her lantern. The light caught shelves rising into darkness, row after row of ledgers bound in calfskin, scrolls sealed with wax gone the color of old blood, and — here and there — things that were neither book nor scroll: a birdcage with no bird, its door fused shut; a stone hand gripping a rusted scepter; jars of preserved something she decided firmly not to examine. Her grandmother had told her once, when Ilsa was small enough to still believe grandmothers knew things, that the palace was built on top of an older palace, which was built on top of a temple, which was built on top of a grave. She had thought it a story to make children behave.

Now she counted steps as they descended, and lost count somewhere past forty.

"The decree lists," she said. "You said the answers would be in the decree lists."

"I said the *shape* of the answer would be." Wendell moved past her, and Ilsa watched how she did it — fingers trailing along the shelf edges, reading by touch, head tilted as though listening to something below hearing. Even with the iron rings still on her fingers, dull and dark, the woman seemed to carry weather with her. "A god doesn't take power all at once, Captain. Power taken all at once shatters what holds it. Veyra has been drinking this kingdom through a straw for months. Someone taught her patience."

"And that someone would be recorded where?"

"In the oldest lies we have." Wendell stopped before a shelf near the wall, where the ledgers grew smaller and the script on their spines grew stranger. "Histories written by kings are confessions wearing crowns. We just have to learn to hear them right."

* * *

They found it two hours past midnight, in a ledger so old its pages had gone translucent, the ink showing through from both sides like veins under skin.

Ilsa read it three times before the words would sit still long enough to mean anything.

*"In the first year of his reign, Aldric the First did cause the Oath-Stone to be broken, that the gods might be sundered from the governance of men, and did gather its shards unto the deep vaults of the crown; and the priests did wail at this impiety; and the king did answer them: let them wail. What god may overrule a king who holds the keys of heaven?"*

"That's not possible," Ilsa said, and heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on recruits. "Every schoolchild learns the Separation was a holy act. The First King freed us from divine tyranny. There are *festivals*—"

"There are festivals for everything kings do well," Wendell said quietly. She was not looking at the page. She was looking at Ilsa. "Read the next entry."

Ilsa turned the fragile leaf. The next entry was shorter, and written in a different hand, cramped and hurried, as if the scribe had been afraid of being interrupted.

*"The shards resist the vault. They hum. His Majesty dreams of rain and wakes with frost upon his lips. The priests say the Stone remembers its purpose. His Majesty says the Stone will learn obedience, as all things must."*

The lantern flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"Where is the rest?" Ilsa demanded, turning pages faster, tearing one — she heard it go and could not bring herself to care. "There should be more. There should be *centuries* more. The Separation happened nine hundred years ago and this ledger stops in the First King's lifetime—"

"Because the rest of the record was destroyed." Wendell took the book gently from her hands, the way you'd take a blade from someone about to cut themselves. "Or rewritten. Or both. Look at the marginalia — see these marks? Little crosses, scratched out and re-inked? Someone went through this archive, generation after generation, correcting the record. Erasing the Stone. Turning a theft into a sacrament."

"Who?"

"Whoever benefited." Wendell set the ledger down on the reading table between them, open to the last legible page. "And there's only been one continuous beneficiary for nine hundred years."

* * *

They found the second truth in a priest's private journal, misfiled among tax records from the reign of Aldric the Third — hidden, perhaps, by someone who had hoped it would never be found, or found too late to matter.

The priest — one Father Emmerich, whose dates placed him six hundred years dead — had written in a small, precise hand that deteriorated across the pages like a man walking downhill:

*"I have examined the crown vaults at His Majesty's command and found the shards of the Oath-Stone arranged in a pattern no mason made. They have moved. They lie in the shape of a wheel, and at the center, where the hub should be, the air itself shines faintly. Brother Anselm says I am imagining it. But I have measured the shine each month for a year, and it grows. Something is drawing the fragments together. Something patient. I have begun to suspect the Stone was never truly broken — only scattered — and that a thing scattered can be gathered, and a thing gathered can be whole, and a god who was locked out of the world by a door of nine hundred pieces need only wait for the pieces to remember each other.*

*I have petitioned the King to scatter the shards anew. He has refused me twice with a smile I did not recognize. Tonight he said a strange thing. He said: 'Father, the door was never meant to keep her out.'*

*I write this and hide it among the taxes because no one reads the taxes, and I pray to a god I no longer trust to forgive what I must do next, which is nothing, because I am one old man and the wheel turns whether I scream at it or not."*

Ilsa closed the journal with hands that were not entirely steady.

The silence of the archives pressed against her ears. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone, the city slept — or didn't sleep, but lay awake in the grip of a devotion it hadn't chosen, praying to a god that answered, giving without knowing what it gave. She thought of the decrees. The tithe of vitality dressed up in pious language. The granary requisitions. The conscription of healers to the palace. Each one reasonable. Each one signed in a hand almost exactly like the king's.

Almost.

"He didn't know," she said. "Aldric. Our Aldric. He didn't know what his ancestor did."

"No." Wendell's voice was very quiet. "And his ancestor didn't know what he was starting. That's the poison of it, Captain. No one has to be monstrous. They only have to want something badly enough to break a rule, and then their children inherit the breaking instead of the wanting." She touched the journal's cover, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar. "The First King wanted to be free of gods. So he broke heaven's lock and put the key in his crown. And every king after wore it, and every king after dreamed strange dreams, and none of them asked why the crown was heavier than it looked."

"And Veyra."

"Veyra has spent nine centuries being patient inside the thing that imprisoned her. Mending herself into it. Waiting for a king weak enough, or devout enough, or lonely enough—" Wendell stopped. When she spoke again her voice had roughened. "Lonely enough to let her in. She didn't conquer the crown. She was *invited*. That's how she works. That's always how she works. She offers you the thing you lost, and by the time you understand the price, your name is already on the contract."

Ilsa thought of Aldric's face the night before the change — the gray dawn look of a man carrying a kingdom alone after his queen died, after his heir was stillborn, after the prayers he'd been raised to believe in came back to him unanswered year after year. She thought of how he had stood at the window and said, only once, only to her: *What is a king for, Ilsa, if even God won't speak to him?*

She had given him the guard-oath answer. *The king is for the kingdom.* It had been true. It was still true. It felt now like a knife she'd handed someone and forgotten about until they fell on it.

"The stone," she said. "The real one, the whole one, before it broke. Where was it kept?"

Wendell was silent a moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a folded scrap of paper — Tally's handwriting on it, Ilsa saw, a child's careful letters — and used the back of it to sketch as she talked. "The histories agree on one thing the censors missed: the Stone stood at the meeting of waters. River and sea. Greyhallow exists *because* of the Stone — the first city grew around the shrine that guarded it. Which means—"

"The Saltmarsh," Ilsa breathed. "The drowned shrine off the river mouth. Fishermen won't go near it. They say it sings in storms."

"They're right. It does." Wendell folded the paper away, and her face, in the lantern light, held something Ilsa hadn't seen in her yet — not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a woman hearing her own name called from a great distance. "That's where the reforging happens. That's where she'll have brought the shards, all nine hundred years' worth, gathering them home to the place they were first sworn. And it's where we have to go."

"We?"

Wendell met her eyes. "You didn't arrest me to watch, Captain."

No. She hadn't. Ilsa looked down at the open journal, at Father Emmerich's last legible line — *the wheel turns whether I scream at it or not* — and made a promise to a dead man she had never met: *I'm going to scream at it anyway.*

Above them, through forty feet of stone and nine hundred years of buried truth, the palace hummed. Faintly. Patiently.

Like a wheel, beginning to turn.
