## 6. A Kingdom That Prays

The road east of Greyhallow smelled wrong.

Wendell noticed it first as a sweetness, faint and cloying, like honey left too long in the sun — and then understood that it was not a smell at all but a taste, sitting on her tongue the way a storm sat on the horizon. Her magic had been awake for six days now, ever since Ilsa Vane had broken her iron rings with the key from her own belt and said, *I need a storm-caller, and you are the only one left in the kingdom.* The gift moved in her chest like water finding its old channel, restless, greedy, glad. She had spent three years teaching it to sleep. It had no intention of sleeping now.

"You feel it too," Ilsa said. She rode ahead, straight-backed in her guard's leathers, though she had shed the captain's insignia before they passed the city gate. A woman hunting treason could not wear it openly.

"It's like walking with my ear against a door," Wendell said. "Something on the other side is singing."

They crested the hill above Millbrook just past midday, and the singing stopped being a metaphor.

The village knelt.

Every soul in it — the miller's family, the blacksmith, three generations of farmers in their good coats, children with scrubbed faces — knelt in the square around the old shrine of Veyra, which had stood cracked and moss-eaten since before Wendell was born. It did not stand cracked now. The stone had healed itself smooth as river glass, and light came off it the way heat comes off a forge, wavering, golden, thick enough to drink. And the villagers drank it. She could see them drinking. Their mouths were open, their eyes closed, their hands raised palm-up like beggars at a feast, and on every face there was an expression so serene it looked, at a distance, like death.

"They're praying," Ilsa said quietly. "All of them. At once."

Wendell's skin prickled. This close to the shrine, her storm-gift pulled toward the light like iron to a lodestone, and she clamped down on it hard, fists white on the reins. "Look at their hands."

Ilsa looked. Then Wendell saw her go still in the way soldiers go still — everything stopped but the eyes, and the eyes took everything in.

The kneeling villagers were aging. Not dying — nothing so kind. Aging. She watched a young woman's brown hair silver from the roots outward, strand by strand, like frost climbing a windowpane. A boy of perhaps ten had lines carved around his mouth that no child should own. They gave and gave, and the light took and took, and they smiled through all of it, blissful as brides.

"Veyra isn't answering prayers," Wendell said. Her voice came out low and rough. "She's eating them."

* * *

They did not stop in Millbrook. There was nowhere to stop; every door stood open and empty, every hearth cold except the one before the shrine. But at the village's edge an old man sat alone by a well, too frail to kneel with the others, and when he saw them he lifted a hand that trembled like a leaf.

"She came to me first," he said, unasked. His voice was papery, delighted. "Forty years I prayed and nothing. Nothing! Then last month, warm as a mother's hand on my brow. She said—" He pressed both palms to his hollow cheeks, laughing, weeping, laughing again. "She said I'd given enough for one life. She let me rest. Everyone else gets to give more."

"Give what?" Ilsa asked, though her face said she knew.

"Their years." He said it the way other men spoke of tithes or taxes, matter-of-fact, even proud. "What are years to us? We have so many, and she has been hungry so long. You can feel how long, can't you?" He leaned toward Wendell suddenly, and his milky eyes fixed on her with terrible clarity. "You've got weather in you, girl. She'd love you best of all."

Wendell's blood went to ice water. Beside her, Ilsa's hand drifted to her sword hilt, but the old man only sighed and settled back against the well, gazing up at the gold-lit sky.

"She says the kingdom will be born again," he murmured. "Clean. Devout. No more storms."

They rode out fast after that, and neither spoke until the shrine's glow had sunk behind the hills.

* * *

The second village was worse because it was emptier.

Harrowmere had given itself entirely. The fields lay untended, wheat going gold and then gray in the wind. Doors hung open on half-set tables, beds with the blankets thrown back, toys in yards where no one had come back for them. The whole population — Wendell counted the shrine's congregation at something over two hundred — knelt in a perfect spiral around the restored altar, arranged like petals, and the light rising off them had a texture now, almost a sound, a vast slow inhale that never reached its exhale.

Among the kneeling, Wendell saw faces she almost recognized. A woman who might have been the herb-seller from the night market, years older than any herb-seller ought to be. A man wearing the faded coat of a Greyhallow ferryman.

"They walked here," Ilsa said. "From Greyhallow. On foot. To do this."

"People don't walk two hundred miles to starve themselves into old age," Wendell said. "Not unless someone's holding the door of heaven open and telling them it's warm inside."

"That's exactly what she's doing."

"Yes." Wendell dismounted before she fully decided to. "And I want to see if it feels like what I think it feels like."

"Wendell—"

"I have to know."

She approached the spiral slowly. The light thickened around her ankles, her knees, her throat, warm as bathwater, sweet as that honey-rot smell on the road. Up close, the worshippers' serenity was worse than agony would have been. A farmer near the outer ring turned his face toward her without opening his eyes, and his smile was the smile of a man hearing music no one else could hear, and his hands, raised in offering, were the spotted, veined, ancient hands of a man of eighty.

He was maybe thirty-five.

*Give it,* the light seemed to murmur, in a voice that was not a voice, that bypassed her ears entirely and spoke directly into the marrow of her. *You have carried it so long, little storm. Carried it alone, in hiding, in iron rings, in exile. Give it here, where it will be loved. Where it will be used. You will never have to hide anything ever again.*

And the terrible thing — the thing Wendell would not say aloud, would barely admit to herself in the privacy of her own skull — was that part of her wanted to.

Not the power. She had made her peace with burying the power; three years of knots and charms had proven she could live small. What called to her was the surrender underneath it. The permission. These people had set down every weight they owned — guilt, doubt, loneliness, the endless labor of choosing — and someone else was carrying all of it now, and they had never looked lighter in their lives. Wendell knew about carrying. She knew about the specific exhaustion of being the only one in the room who could act and must not. For one shining, horrifying moment she understood these kneeling strangers completely, envied them completely, and felt her own raised hands begin to lift—

A sound broke through. Paper. Small, crinkling paper, pulled from an inner pocket by fingers she hadn't consciously commanded.

Tally's letters.

Ilsa had brought them, of course she had — the woman was thorough — but it was Wendell who had asked, red-faced and gruff, *bring the letters, I'll want — I'll want something from home,* and Ilsa had said nothing and packed them without comment, which was its own kindness.

Wendell opened the newest one right there in the light, standing among the hollow-eyed faithful, and read it by the glow of the god who wanted to eat her.

*Dear Wendell,* it began, in Tally's ferocious slanted hand. *Bess says I have to tell you things instead of just asking when you're coming home, so: the roof leaked over the kitchen and Bess stood under it yelling at it like it could be shamed, and it worked, actually, which proves yelling is magic too. I sold eleven luck-knots while you were gone. I charged double because you weren't there to make me be fair. Mrs. Corren bought three and said your knots always worked better anyway and I told her it was because you cheat at tying them. Come home soon. If you die doing whatever you're doing I will be extremely angry and also I will keep your room exactly the same forever, which Bess says is morbid, but I think it's practical.*

*P.S. I tied a knot for you. Don't lose it. It's a stupid knot but I made it so it counts.*

Around Wendell's wrist, a clumsy scrap of grey wool, badly braided, tied in a pattern that meant nothing to anyone but a girl in Greyhallow who had learned knots from a woman who cheated at tying them.

She laughed. It came out wet and startled, and the light recoiled from the sound of it, rippling away from her like water from a hot stone. The farmer's serene face flickered — confusion crossing it, then fear, then a grief so profound it looked like drowning — and Wendell stepped backward out of the spiral with her heart slamming and Tally's letter crushed in her fist.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole rotten trick. Veyra offered rest, and rest was real, and the offering was sincere — but what the god took in exchange was not merely years. It was everyone you would have grown old beside. Every leaky roof worth yelling at. Every badly tied knot. The kneeling villagers were not giving their time to heaven. They were abandoning their lives mid-sentence, and heaven was thanking them for it.

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the light, to the god inside it, to the wanting thing in her own chest. "No. I know what I am. I'm hers." She touched the grey wool at her wrist. "Find another storm."

* * *

They made camp that night in a shepherd's hut on the high moor, beyond the reach of any shrine, and the darkness there was honest darkness, full of wind and nothing else. Wendell sat by the fire reading Tally's letters for the fourth time while Ilsa cleaned her sword with the methodical care of a woman sharpening her courage along with the blade.

"How many villages between here and the stone?" Wendell asked finally.

"Nine." Ilsa didn't look up from the whetstone. "By tomorrow night we'll be close enough that the devotion-thick air reaches us. Past that—" She paused. "Past that, the reports say the kneeling doesn't stop. Whole valleys on their knees. Travelers who went in and came out three days later with white hair and smiles on their faces, unable to say why they turned back."

"And the stone itself?"

"At the heart of it. In the ruins of the First Temple, where Aldric's ancestor shattered it." Ilsa set the sword down and looked at Wendell across the fire, and in the firelight her soldier's composure slipped just far enough to show the woman beneath it — exhausted, afraid, and stubborn as bedrock. "There's something else. Tonight, riding the last ridge, I felt it through the guard-oath. The bond every sworn shield carries with the king." Her jaw worked. "He's still in there. Faint. Like a heartbeat under water. But he's fighting, Wendell. He's been fighting alone in there for weeks, and I don't know how much longer a man can fight alone."

Wendell folded Tally's letters closed, all of them, and tucked them against her heart where the storm stirred and the god whispered and the memory of a girl yelling at a leaky roof held the line.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

Far to the east, past nine villages and the dark spine of the hills, a light was rising off the land like a second dawn — and somewhere beneath it, a king was drowning.
