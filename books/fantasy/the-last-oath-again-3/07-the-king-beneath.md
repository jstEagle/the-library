## 7. The King Beneath

The oath woke her before dawn.

It had slept for eleven years — a warm coal behind Ilsa's breastbone, lit the day she knelt in the coronation hall and swore herself to Aldric Vane-Erris with her sword flat across her palms. Every guard carried it. It was supposed to be a formality, a thread of ceremony, something that hummed faintly when the king was near so that his protectors could find him blindfolded in a burning palace. She had felt it perhaps a dozen times since: a tug, a warmth, nothing more.

Tonight it burned.

She rose from her cot in the guard barracks without lighting a lamp, dressed in the dark by muscle and habit, and followed the pull of it down through the sleeping palace like a fisher following a line into deep water. Past the state apartments with their shuttered windows. Past the chapel where the new shrines had been raised, their votive lamps guttering even though no draft moved. Down stairs she had not known existed until this week, when Captain's keys had begun opening doors she'd never had reason to open.

The coal pulled her lower. The air changed — older, mineral, tasting of the mountain the palace was built into. Her candle, when she finally lit it at the bottom of the stair, showed a corridor of bare stone, unadorned, predating every gilded thing above. The old kingdom's bones.

At its end stood a door of black oak banded in silver, and the silver was singing.

Not loudly. A thin, continuous note, like a wet finger circling a glass rim, and Ilsa understood suddenly why the servants who cleaned these depths never spoke of what they heard. The oath behind her sternum answered it, vibrating in sympathy, and when she pressed her palm to the door the note climbed into audibility and the lock — no keyhole, only a depression shaped like a spread hand — accepted her.

*Of course,* she thought, stepping through. *He built us into him. All of us. We were always the key.*

The room beyond was round and low-ceilinged, a hollow in the living rock, and King Aldric sat in its center on a plain wooden chair.

That was the first horror: the chair. No throne. No bed. Just a chair, set facing the wall as if he had been made to stand in a corner like a punished child, and the king of Callowmere sat in it in his nightshirt, barefoot, his hands loose in his lap, his head bowed. His breathing was slow and even and wrong — too even, metronomic, the breath of a man being kept rather than living.

The second horror was the light. It came from nowhere and everywhere, a pale gold suffusion like sun through honey, and it pooled around Aldric's shoulders in a shape that was almost wings. Almost. The suggestion of them made Ilsa's eyes water if she looked directly, the way staring at the sun's reflection off a blade did.

"Veyra," she said aloud, and the name fell dead in the stone, swallowed.

The figure did not turn. But Aldric's head lifted.

*Ilsa.*

His voice came not through his mouth but through the oath — direct, intimate, unbearably close, the way a mother's heartbeat reaches a child still unborn. She hadn't heard his true voice in weeks, not since the decrees began, not since the man on the throne had started pronouncing judgments in that resonant borrowed cadence while his eyes stayed fixed on some middle distance where no one else could follow. This was different. This was *him*, ragged and human and drowning.

"Your Majesty." She dropped to one knee out of reflex, then caught herself, then knelt anyway because some things deserved kneeling. "I'm here."

*You shouldn't be.* A flicker of something like laughter, terrible and thin. *Or you should have come years ago. I've lost track. Does it matter? Is the city still standing?*

"The city stands." She forced steadiness into her voice. "Greyhallow stands. Your Majesty—"

*Aldric.* The word arrived with the weight of a hand on her shoulder. *Eleven years you've called me Majesty. Give me my name back. They took everything else. Let me keep the name.*

"Aldric." It cost her. Saying it plainly, without rank or armor around it, felt like opening a visor in battle — necessary, and exposing her throat. "What did it do to you?"

*Sat down inside me and started redecorating.* His presence in the oath shuddered. *It doesn't hurt, Ilsa. That's the worst of it. It's like being held. Like being the most beloved thing in the world. Every prayer in the kingdom pours through me and I can feel all of it — an old woman thanking the gods for rain, a boy praying over a sick brother, whole villages singing — and Veyra lets me feel it all because feeling it is the hook. Do you understand? I am drunk every moment on being needed. And meanwhile it drinks me. My memories go first. I've forgotten my mother's face. I forgot the taste of plums last month. I'm forgetting the sea.*

His mind brushed hers through the oath, and Ilsa saw — could not help seeing — the interior landscape of the king: a vast golden cathedral of devotion, pillars of light going up forever, and beneath it, far below, a small dark cellar where a man crouched among the few things he had hidden from the god. A memory of a grey coast. A boy's wooden horse. The smell of bread.

And water rising.

"You're drowning," she said.

*Slowly. Gracefully. With music playing.* The bitterness was almost elegant. *Here is what matters, and you will listen because you are the only person alive who can hear me. Veyra is nearly strong enough to finish mending the oath-stone. When that happens, the division between gods and mortals ends — permanently, in her favor, forever. Everything your ancestors broke their backs to build, undone. And there is a storm-caller now, they tell me — Veyra is furious about her, which means Veyra is afraid of her. Wendell Eastwick. If anyone can reforge the stone properly—*

"She's coming. She's coming with me."

*Then listen.* And here the drowning man's voice went flat and clear, the voice of the king who had once, at nineteen, ordered his own coronation guards paid double during famine years because he would not ask men to defend a crown while their children starved. *When Wendell reforges the stone, the binding must be severed from inside. Someone has to hold the anchor point. That someone dies, Ilsa. That someone is me. Tell her not to try to save me. Promise me. Swear it on the oath.*

"I don't swear on the oath anymore." The words came out of her before she had decided to say them, rough and low. "I've been thinking about oaths. About what we do with them. We take a beautiful thing — a promise — and we hammer it into a weapon and a chain and a cage, and we call the cage duty, and we die inside it smiling."

*Ilsa—*

"I watched you give decrees I knew were killing the city," she said, "and I obeyed, because duty. I found the ledgers showing the vitality-tithe, whole streets grown grey and dim, and I told myself the king must know best, because duty. I let duty eat me the way Veyra eats you, and it turns out I was just worshipping a smaller god with a colder altar." Her hands had closed into fists at her sides. "No. I won't promise to let you die. I refuse to choose between my king and my kingdom, because I have spent eleven years doing exactly that and it has made me a hollow thing that follows orders into hell."

*You must choose—*

"I loved you." It came out quiet, which somehow made it louder than anything she had said in the round stone room. The golden light seemed to flinch. "For six years. Since the winter you gave your own fur cloak to the gatekeeper's fevered daughter and told no one and I saw you do it. Before that, maybe. Since the day I swore the oath and realized the coal it put in my chest wasn't duty at all — it was just you, always burning somewhere I couldn't reach. I built my whole life around a love I never spoke because you were crowned and I was sworn and duty said *be a wall, not a woman*." She was crying now, she realized distantly, and did not wipe her face; soldiers' tears in a place like this were offerings, not weaknesses. "So no. I have finished choosing between duty and love. Duty brought me here. Love is going to get you out."

The light around Aldric convulsed. For a moment the almost-wings flared wide, and Veyra's attention swept through the chamber like a searchlight — Ilsa felt it pass over her, a pressure like deep water, a curiosity vast and ancient and utterly indifferent to her smallness—

Then it withdrew, satisfied that the intruder was only a mortal grieving, and grief was permitted. Grief fed it too.

*Ilsa.* Aldric again, urgent, fraying. *Listen to me. If you attempt extraction, it will feel it leaving and it will raze the city in its death-throes unless the anchor holds. The anchor is me. There is no version of this where I walk out.*

"There's a version where you walk out broken," she said. "Broken walks. Broken talks. Broken eats bread and remembers the sea. I've seen men live on less."

*And if the storm-caller fails?*

"Then I'll hold the anchor myself."

Silence. Through the oath, she felt him take this in — felt the exact moment the drowning man understood that someone had dived in after him, and the terrible arithmetic he began doing, weighing her life against his against the kingdom's, the arithmetic she refused on principle to perform.

*You always were insubordinate,* he said at last, and underneath the dry words, something in him shifted — hope, dangerous and small, kindling in the flooded cellar of him. *It's why I promoted you. Why I—* He stopped. Started again. *The guard-oath runs both ways. Did you know? The founders built it as a bridge, not a leash. If you open your end fully — if you stop holding anything back — I can pour myself along it. Slowly. While Veyra sleeps sated after the tithe-processions. It will take days. It may kill you instead.*

"Then we'll find out which of us drowns better," Ilsa said, and settled herself cross-legged on the cold stone before the chair, facing him, and opened the oath.

The bridge lit between them — silver thread through black air — and she began, carefully, patiently, to pull a king up out of a golden sea.

Above them, far above, dawn was breaking over Greyhallow, and in the east, beyond the mountains, a storm-caller was already on the road, carrying letters from an orphan and walking toward the shattered heart of the world.

* * *

They had three days before the next tithe-procession. Ilsa spent them learning the shape of the work.

By day she commanded the guard as though nothing had changed — drills, reports, the theater of normalcy — and by night she descended to the stone room and opened herself, and Aldric poured. Memories first, because memories were lightest: his mother's face returned to him in fragments across two nights, and he wept like a child when it reassembled. Then skills, then names, then the long inventory of a self, each piece ferried across the silver thread while Ilsa's body grew colder and her sleep thinned to a grey sliver.

On the third evening, carrying a load of the king's childhood, she paused at the top of the under-stair and looked back toward the sealed door. The singing of the silver bands had changed pitch.

Lower. Hungrier. Awake.

Veyra had noticed the missing weight.

Ilsa blew out her candle and ran for the stables, because there was no time left for careful ferrying — only for the storm-caller, and the stone, and whatever came after. Behind her, far below, the god turned over in its stolen kingdom and began, softly, to sing back.

And on the eastern road, past the emptied villages, Wendell Eastwick felt the change in the weather and quickened her step, Tally's letter crumpled in her fist like a talisman, walking straight toward the lightning.
