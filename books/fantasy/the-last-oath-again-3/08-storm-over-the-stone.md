## 8. Storm Over the Stone

The mountain had been a god's jaw once, Wendell thought, climbing the last switchback through the ash-fall. Something enormous had bitten down on the world here and left only this: a crater ringed with black stone, and at its heart, the broken halves of the oath-stone jutting from the earth like two teeth of some buried giant.

She could feel it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air, a silence with weight. Three years of iron rings had taught her to read magic like weather, and what hung over this place was no weather she knew. It was pressure without rain. Hunger without a mouth.

Ilsa climbed beside her, sword slung across her back, saying nothing. They had not spoken since the village of Hollowmere, where they had walked between kneeling worshippers with their eyes gone soft as candlewax, giving their years away in cupfuls to a shrine that hummed like a hive. Ilsa had wept silently the whole way out. Wendell had envied them, and hated herself for it, and held Tally's last letter in her pocket until the paper went soft as cloth.

Now the letter was dry again against her hip. She touched it once, the way you touch a charm.

The stone waited.

It was larger than the archives' drawings had suggested — two great shards, each taller than a man, sundered cleanly, their faces carved with script so old it predated the kingdom, predating perhaps the idea of kingdoms. Between the shards the air itself had been stitched shut with golden light, thread by patient thread, centuries of mending done from inside a crown. Veyra's work. The seam glowed like a wound packed with honey, and around it, drifting motes of devotion rose from the land below — prayers made visible, drawn up the mountainside in a slow silver spiral, feeding the seam, feeding her.

Wendell stopped ten paces out. The iron rings on her fingers were cold enough to burn.

"You came."

The voice did not come from the stone. It came from everywhere — from the ash underfoot, from the wind that shouldn't have been there, from somewhere behind Wendell's own ribs. Then the light gathered, folded, became a woman standing barefoot between the shards, robed in grey that moved like stormcloud deciding whether to be merciful. She had Wendell's face, almost. A face like every storm-caller's face, because storms had only ever needed one.

"Veyra," Wendell said.

"You're smaller than I hoped." The goddess tilted her head, and her eyes were the color of lightning seen through closed eyelids. "And more tired. Good. Tired things listen."

* * *

"I know what you lost," Veyra said, and began to walk the circle of the shattered stone, unhurried, bare feet printing no mark in the ash. "Shall I say it aloud? You've never said it yourself. Not once in three years. That's a long time to hold your breath."

"I didn't come to talk."

"No one ever does." The goddess smiled, gentle as overcast. "They come to fight, or beg, or die, and all the while what they want is to be told the truth about themselves. So. Wendell Eastwick. Exiled from Merrow's Reach at nineteen for a storm that killed four people — none of whom you meant, all of whom you loved, one of whom called you sister as the water took her."

"Stop."

"The word left your mouth sideways," Veyra went on, soft, relentless, circling closer. "You reached for the sky the way children reach for their mothers, and the sky answered too well, because you were never taught restraint — because *they* feared you even as they raised you — and when the flood receded they put you on a cart and drove you into the rain. No trial. Your own father wouldn't look at you."

"*Stop.*"

"So you bound yourself in iron." Veyra lifted Wendell's hand — the goddess's fingers cool and dry as old paper — and turned it in the light, rings glinting. "Three years. Charms sold in a night market to people who'd burn you if they knew. Love kept at arm's length like something borrowed. An innkeeper who calls you family and a child who calls you *Wen*, and you flinch every time, because everything you've ever been given has been taken back with interest."

The ash was rising now around them both, stirred by nothing. Or by Wendell. She couldn't tell anymore where her pulse ended and the mountain began.

"I can give it back," Veyra said. "All of it. Pardon, written and sealed. Merrow's Reach opened to you — your father alive and sorry, if you want him; gone quietly, if you don't. And the storm—" her grip tightened, and the iron rings groaned, "—the storm unchained. No rings. No hiding. Every cloud yours to call, every thunderhead a door you may open whenever you wish, forever. This is not a lie, storm-caller. Gods cannot lie. It is the one advantage mortals have over us, and I am surrendering it for you."

The rings cracked. One slid off her finger and hit the ash, and the sound went through Wendell like a bell through water.

And oh, it would be easy. That was the horror of it, how easy. She could feel her magic already leaning toward the offer like a plant toward light — three years of drought and here was the whole sea, offered, poured out, hers. She thought of the sky above Greyhallow, flat and gray and *closed*, and how she had learned to look at it the way you look at a locked room in a house you used to live in. She thought of her hands full of lightning. She thought of home.

Home. The word had teeth either way.

"You fed on Hollowmere," Wendell said. Her voice came out low and steady, which surprised her. "On that mother in the market whose son you healed just to bait me. On the king. On everyone who ever knelt because kneeling was the only prayer anyone answered."

"I gave them what they begged for."

"They begged for rain. For their children's fevers to break. For someone to *hear* them." Wendell pulled her hand free. "You gave them what served you. That's not hearing. That's farming."

"And what will you give them?" Veyra's voice changed — still gentle, but vast now, the gentleness of an avalanche. "Reforge the stone and you cut the gods off from them forever. No more answered prayers. Ever. You'll hand them back the silence they spent a century learning to live in, and they will thank you, and they will go on praying anyway, into the dark, unheard. Is that mercy? Is that *belonging*? You, of all creatures, selling them the same exile you've been drowning in?"

The seam of gold light pulsed. The silver spiral of devotion quickened, motes streaming past Wendell's shoulders like snow falling upward.

"Take my offer," Veyra said, quieter now, almost kind. "Keep your power. Keep your pardon. And when the storms come — and they will always come — you'll be the one who answers. You'll be their god, Wendell Eastwick. Isn't that what you've wanted since the day they exiled you? To be *heard*?"

* * *

And Wendell stood at the edge of it, toes curled over the rim of everything she had ever lost, and she nearly stepped in.

That was the truth she would carry afterward, the truth she would tell Tally years later when the girl asked what it was like to face a god: *I nearly said yes.* Not because she was weak. Because the offer was real. Because homesickness is not a weakness but a gravity, and three years is long enough to forget which way is up.

Her right hand was bare now. The second ring was cracking. She could hear her own storm gathering in her blood like a crowd before a gate, and the gate was opening, opening—

"Wen?"

Not the mountain. Memory.

Tally, age nine, sitting cross-legged on Bess's kitchen floor with a luck-knot in her fists, scowling at it. *"You tied it wrong on purpose, didn't you. So it'd only bring medium luck. Because big luck scares you."*

Tally, feverish, small under wool blankets while Wendell sat up all night doing nothing, doing *nothing*, because doing anything might mean exposure, might mean the pyre, and hating herself with every heartbeat. *"You stayed,"* the girl had whispered at dawn. *"Everyone else prays and leaves. You stay."*

Tally's letter, soft as cloth in her pocket: *Bess says come home soon. I told her you will. I told her you always do.*

Always do.

Not a god. Not a miracle. Just a person who comes home, imperfectly, in ordinary weather, because someone is waiting — and that, Wendell understood suddenly, standing in the mouth of perfect power, was the entire difference between everything.

"No," she said.

Veyra's smile did not fade so much as stop pretending. "Then you choose the silence."

"No," Wendell said again, louder, and tore the remaining rings from her fingers and let them fall. "I choose the noise. The messy, mortal, badly-prayed-for noise. I choose an innkeeper who burns the stew and an orphan who ties knots wrong and a captain who cries behind her own shoulder and a king I've never met who deserves to drown alone far less than I deserved to drown alone at nineteen. I choose *them*. Not because they're perfect. Because they're mine."

The sky answered.

There had been no clouds over the crater — Veyra's hunger allowed no weather but her own — but Wendell called anyway, called the way she had at nineteen, with nothing held back and nothing sideways, and the horizon *tore*. Clouds came boiling over the mountain rim from every direction at once, black-bellied and shrieking with static, and the first thunderclap arrived not as sound but as a fist against the chest of the world.

Veyra screamed — not in fear, Wendell realized, but in fury, in *recognition*, because this was the oldest thing a storm-caller could do, older than oaths, older than crowns: claim the sky and mean it.

Wendell stepped between the shards and laid both palms on the golden seam.

It burned. Of course it burned. She poured herself into it anyway — three years of rationed lightning, three years of swallowed grief, the flood at Merrow's Reach and the faces in it, the market stalls and the kitchen floor and the arm's-length love finally, *finally* let all the way in — everything, into the stone, reforging what her ancestors' king had shattered, welding god to god and mortal to mortal with the only metal that had ever held:

A storm freely given.

The oath-stone blazed white. Above her, Veyra rose out of her own shape, vast and ragged and coming down like the end of the world —

— and the last thing Wendell saw before the light took everything was Ilsa Vane, sword drawn, planting herself between Wendell's body and the falling god, screaming something into the wind that might have been a name.

Then the mountain spoke with a thousand years of withheld thunder,

and the sky broke open, and did not stop breaking.
