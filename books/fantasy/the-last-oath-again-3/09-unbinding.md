## 9. Unbinding

The storm came to Wendell like a horse coming home.

It did not ask permission. It did not wait for her fear to settle or her hands to stop shaking. It rose out of her bones the way water rises in a well when you finally, finally lower the bucket — three years of iron rings and held breath and luck-knots sold to strangers, all of it drawn up at once into the bruise-dark sky above the shattered oath-stone.

She stood on the broken rim of the thing and let it happen.

The stone itself was older than language. Once it had been a single pillar tall as a cathedral spire, and now it was a field of fragments, some as small as coins, some large as cottages, scattered across a hollow in the mountains like the bones of something enormous that had died badly. Veyra's power pooled between the pieces in slow golden tides, mending, always mending, thread by thread of stolen devotion knitting the god back toward wholeness.

Wendell meant to unmake all of it.

Lightning walked her spine. She could feel every charge in the sky lining up along her arms like soldiers awaiting an order, and for one vertiginous moment she understood why she had been exiled — not because she had called the storm that drowned Harrow Fen, but because some small, secret part of her had *wanted* to, had felt the sky open its throat and thought: yes, this is what I am for.

*Not anymore,* she told the storm. *Not tonight. Tonight you work.*

She drove her palms against the largest fragment. It was warm, blood-warm, and beneath the warmth she felt the old carving — the oath-line, the words the first king had broken with hammer and ambition: *What is divine shall keep to heaven; what is mortal shall keep to earth; and neither shall feed upon the other.* The grooves were worn nearly smooth, but they were still there, still waiting, patient as graves.

"Then hold," Wendell whispered, and called the lightning down through her own body.

* * *

Ilsa Vane knelt in the mud forty paces from the stone, holding King Aldric's convulsing body against her chest.

He weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it — the man who had once trained beside her in the yard until their shoulders bled, the man whose laughter used to shake dust from the armory rafters, now weighed less than his own armor. Whatever Veyra had not yet drained had gone to fighting her, and what remained of Aldric thrashed and arched and spoke in two voices braided together, hers and his, gold and grey, arguing in a language Ilsa didn't know but understood anyway.

*Let go,* said the gold voice through his mouth.
*Never,* said the grey, fainter, from somewhere far underneath.

"Hold on," Ilsa said into his hair. It was absurd. She was the Captain of the Oath-Guard, and the best counsel she had was the kind you gave frightened children in thunderstorms. But the guard-oath was still live between them — sworn on the same stone Wendell was now trying to reforge, back when the stone still had enough of itself left to bind things — and through it she could feel him drowning. Not dying. Drowning. There was a difference, and the difference was hope, and she gripped both.

The first death-throe hit when Wendell's lightning touched the stone.

Ilsa felt it come up through the king's body like a wave through a ship. His spine bowed; his heels dug furrows in the ash-mud; the gold voice screamed from his open mouth, a sound like a temple bell being murdered, and every fragment of the oath-stone within a hundred yards began to sing in answer — high and thin and grieving, the sound of a lock remembering it was once a door.

"Stay," Ilsa told him. "Aldric. Stay *here.*"

His hand found her gauntlet and closed around it with the last of his human strength. Through the oath-bond his mind brushed hers, and for one instant she was inside — inside the drowning place, where a young king stood waist-deep in golden light going grey at the edges, and he looked at her with eyes that were only his own and said:

*Ilsa. If this kills me—*

"It won't."

*If it does. Don't let them make me a saint. Don't let them build shrines. I have seen what devotion does—*

"You are not going to die," Ilsa said aloud, in the mud, in the screaming wind, because some things had to be said with a body even when the soul was listening elsewhere. "You're going to be tedious and difficult and you're going to drink too much at midwinter, and I am going to stand next to you while you do it, because that is my oath, and I never swore anything to a god. Only to you."

Something in the golden light flinched.

Another throe came, worse than the first, and then another, each one arriving faster, the god's death-rhythm quickening like a heart before the end. Ilsa wrapped both arms around the king and took it. It went through her like cold fire; she tasted copper; somewhere very far away she heard her own voice making sounds she would be ashamed of later. She did not let go. Her armor cracked along the shoulder seam. She did not let go. The world went white at the edges, gold-white, the color of Veyra's fury—

—and through it, threaded thin but unmistakable, came the smell of rain about to fall true. Clean rain. Greyhallow rain. Rain with nothing holy in it at all.

*She's doing it,* Ilsa thought. *God help us both, the exiled girl is doing it.*

* * *

Wendell had imagined the reforging as lifting something heavy.

It was not lifting. It was *pouring*, and she was the cup.

Every bolt she called down went through her first — through the iron rings she'd torn off and thrown away, through the scars the rings had left, through the place behind her sternum where three years of careful silence had built a wall she was now dismantling brick by screaming brick. The storm passed through her and into the oath-line, and the oath-line drank it, and where it drank the fragments moved.

They moved slowly. They ground against each other like millstones. A piece the size of a door slid six inches across the hollow with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and Wendell laughed — actually laughed, wild and gasping, because it was working, it was *working*, and no one had ever told her that magic could feel like this, like purpose instead of punishment.

Veyra noticed.

The tide of golden light between the stones stopped pooling and started *rising*. It gathered above the hollow in a shape that was almost a woman — tall, radiant, wearing a crown made of every prayer ever answered wrong — and it looked down at Wendell with eyes like collection plates.

*You could stop,* the god said, in a voice like hymns sung by empty mouths. *You could have everything back. Harrow Fen forgiven. Your name cleared. Look how they love me, storm-caller. Look how willingly they give. You could be loved like that.*

"I've seen how they love you," Wendell said, teeth gritted, hands smoking against the stone. "I walked through your villages. I saw the hollow ones smiling."

*They are happy.*

"They are *gone.*"

She pulled the next bolt down herself, reached into the sky with both hands the way she hadn't since Harrow Fen, and hauled the lightning out of the clouds like hauling a net full of fish. It went through her and it *hurt*, God, it hurt, it burned away the last of her reserves the way a furnace burns the last of the wood, and in the burning she saw Tally's face — not a vision, just memory, just the orphan girl sitting cross-legged on Bess's kitchen floor asking, *Wendell, what happens to storms after they end?*

And she had said: *They become weather, love. They become ordinary. That's the best thing there is.*

"That's the best thing there is," she said aloud, to the god, to the sky, to herself, and poured everything.

* * *

The last fragment rose.

Wendell felt it rise through her palms, felt the oath-line complete itself under her fingers letter by letter, and Veyra screamed — a scream that came out of the king's body forty paces away and out of every shrine in the kingdom and out of the clouds themselves, one long note of rage and terror and, beneath it, unmistakably, relief. Gods, too, could grow tired of hunger.

*This will cost you,* the god said, and for one moment the voice was almost gentle. Almost pitying. *You know what it costs. Say the word and I will spare you. I can spare anyone. It is the only mercy I have left.*

Wendell thought of her magic. Three years of hiding it, yes — but also three years of knowing it was there, curled under her ribs like a sleeping animal. The luck-knots. The small warmths. The way the sky had always, always known her name.

She thought of Tally. Of Bess's kitchen. Of belonging kept at arm's length because keeping it close meant one day losing it, and losing it while carrying magic meant becoming the storm again, alone, exiled twice over.

"Take it," Wendell said.

And the last piece of the oath-stone settled home, and the line closed, and the division that had stood between gods and mortals for a thousand years stood again — and everything Veyra was poured backward out of the world through the crack Wendell had opened, taking with it, as the toll for passage, the storm in Wendell Eastwick's chest.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and rain.

Real rain. Ordinary rain, fat and cool and smelling of wet stone, falling on the hollow where the oath-stone stood whole again — not tall as a spire, but standing, a pillar the height of two men, plain grey granite with old words cut deep and shining faintly, like a scar that has finally stopped aching.

Ilsa lay in the mud with the king half beneath her. He was breathing. Shallowly, unevenly, but breathing, and when she got a hand behind his head he opened his eyes, and they were grey, only grey, tired as an old soldier's, and entirely, completely his own.

"The shrine bells," he croaked. "I can't hear them anymore."

"No," Ilsa said, and her voice broke on it, and she decided she would allow herself that, once, here in the rain where only a dead god could witness it. "You won't ever hear them again."

He wept then, briefly, horribly, the weeping of a man surfacing from deep water — and she held him through that too, because holding people through things had apparently become the whole of her duty, and she found she did not mind.

Fifty feet away, Wendell Eastwick sat down hard on a fallen fragment of the old stone and waited to feel the storm die.

It went quietly. That was the cruelest part. No tearing, no final convulsion — just a receding, like a tide going out past the point where it would ever come back. She felt it pass through her fingers, through her scars, out through the soles of her boots into the ground, and she reached after it the way you reach after a dream, grasping at shapes already gone. When it was finished she was simply a woman sitting in the rain. Cold. Ordinary. Lighter than she had been since childhood, and grieving the weight.

She put her face in her hands and cried for the storm, because it had been, for all the ruin it brought, the truest thing she had ever loved.

* * *

They came down out of the mountains three days later, slow, because Aldric could ride only an hour at a stretch and Ilsa would not leave him, and Wendell walked beside the horses saying almost nothing, watching her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger.

At the foot of the pass, where the road bent toward Greyhallow, someone was waiting.

Two someones. One large and red-faced and furious, in an innkeeper's apron she had clearly not paused to remove; one small and gap-toothed and running before anyone could shout a warning.

Tally hit Wendell at the knees hard enough to knock her sideways.

"You *lied,*" the girl was shouting into her coat, fists bunching in the wool, voice cracking. "You said you were coming back and then the whole city said you were taken to the palace to be *executed* and Bess wouldn't let me come and I *hate*—"

"Tally." Wendell crouched, wincing, gathering her in. Over the girl's head she saw Bess bearing down like weather, saw Captain Vane dismounting with her hand already raised — *it's all right, stand down* — saw King Aldric, grey and swaying in the saddle, watching the scene with an expression of dazed, wondering humanity, as though he had forgotten such a thing could exist in his kingdom.

"Wendell," Bess said, stopping an arm's length away, breathing hard, glaring through tears she would deny to her grave. "Your soup's gone cold twice. Get up off your knees, girl, and walk home."

Wendell Eastwick — magicless, pardoned, soaked through by ordinary rain that had followed them all the way down from the pass — stood, took Tally's hand, and turned toward Greyhallow, where the lamps were being lit against the evening and the first honest thunder in a century was rolling harmlessly out over the sea.

Behind her, the king cleared his throat, and said, in a voice still rough as gravel: "There will be proclamations. Trials. The law-books on magic are going into the fire, all of them — but first—" he hesitated, looking older and younger than any king she'd ever seen, "—first, I'm told, there is a woman in this company who owes me an explanation of what exactly a *luck-knot* is, and whether I can buy one without being arrested."

Tally giggled. Bess snorted. And Wendell laughed — really laughed, for the first time since Harrow Fen — as the road carried all of them home.
