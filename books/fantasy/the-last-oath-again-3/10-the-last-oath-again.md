## 10. The Last Oath Again

The rain came on the third morning after the decree, and Wendell stood in the courtyard of the palace infirmary with her face turned up to it like a woman greeting an old friend she had wronged.

It was ordinary rain. That was the miracle of it. No taste of ozone, no pressure behind her teeth, no whisper in the water calling her name. Just cold grey weather falling on a city that had forgotten what weather was for, drumming on the slate roofs of Greyhallow, running in bright threads down the gargoyles' worn faces, filling the gutters with a sound like applause.

She could not feel it.

That was the strangest part — not the emptiness itself but its texture, the way a missing tooth is always discovered by the tongue. For three years she had kept her storm pressed down beneath iron rings and careful silence, and even caged, it had been *there*, a second heartbeat under her skin, a tide that rose when clouds gathered. Now the rings sat loose on fingers that were only fingers. The sky opened above her and gave her nothing back, and she stood in the courtyard and wept without quite knowing which grief she wept for.

"Wendell."

Aldric's voice. She had learned, these past weeks, to hear how carefully he spoke now — as if words might crack like ice over deep water. He crossed the courtyard slowly, leaning on Ilsa's arm more than he would ever admit to needing. The king looked older than his years. Whatever Veyra had left behind in him, it showed in the hollows of his cheeks and the way his eyes went distant sometimes, listening for a voice that no longer answered.

"You should be resting," Wendell said.

"I've done nothing but rest for weeks." He stopped beside her and tilted his face into the rain, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Water beaded in his beard. "Ilsa tells me you haven't eaten."

"Ilsa worries too much."

"Ilsa is right." He said it mildly, but there was iron under it — the old Aldric surfacing through the ruin, the man who had held a kingdom together while a god wore him like a coat. "The decree goes out at noon. All of it. The magic laws repealed, every one, back to the first. Charms lawful. Shrines open to any god or none. And—" he paused, and Wendell watched him gather himself, "—a full pardon, named and sealed, for Wendell Eastwick, storm-caller, who broke herself to save a king she'd never met."

"Your archivists will hate the phrasing."

"My archivists can write their own version if they think they'll do better." He turned to look at her then, and his eyes were wet, though whether from rain or something else she couldn't say. "I owe you a debt no seal can cover. So I'll say the plain thing instead: thank you. And I'm sorry. My ancestor built the cage you were born into. It took your whole life to break it open."

"Not my whole life," Wendell said. "Just the part I wasn't using anyway."

He laughed — a rough, surprised sound, as though laughter were another muscle he was relearning — and squeezed her shoulder once, briefly, before Ilsa steered him back toward the warm rooms and the endless business of a kingdom learning to breathe again.

Wendell stayed in the rain until her clothes were soaked through, because it was the last time, she thought, that the sky would ever touch her this way. Ordinary weather. She wanted to memorize it.

* * *

They came for her on the sixth day.

She had expected exile — had packed for it, in fact, a single bag by the infirmary door, holding everything she owned now that her magic was gone: two shirts, a comb, Tally's letters tied in string, the broken luck-knot from the night market that she hadn't been able to throw away. When the knock came at dawn she was already dressed for the road, and she opened the door braced for the escort, the formal words, the road east to the border and whatever waited beyond it.

Bess hit her like a landslide.

The innkeeper was not a small woman, and three years of hauling kegs and hauling orphans out of trouble had not softened her. She wrapped Wendell in arms that smelled of woodsmoke and bread and the particular soap of the Drowned Lantern's kitchen, and Wendell felt something in her chest come apart that she had been holding together since the oath-stone, since the shrine, since the night market three years ago when she first decided that loving people quietly was safer than loving them out loud.

"You absolute *fool*," Bess said into her hair, which was as close as Bess ever came to *I thought you were going to die* or *I missed you* or any of the truer things underneath. "You absolute, self-sacrificing, mule-headed fool. You wrote us *one letter*. One! 'Dear Bess, I'm alive, don't worry.' Don't worry! As if that's a thing anyone's ever done!"

"There was rather a lot happening—"

"There's always a lot happening! That's never once stopped you from writing!" Bess pulled back, gripped her by both shoulders, and looked her over with the fierce inventory of a woman counting what remained. Her eyes caught on Wendell's hands — bare now, the iron rings sold or given away — and softened in a way that made Wendell want to look away. "Oh, love."

"It's all right," Wendell said, and found, testing the words, that they were mostly true. "It was the price. I knew the price."

"Knowing doesn't make it cheap." Bess said it gently, and then stepped aside, because there was someone else in the corridor, hovering by the wall with her hands jammed in her pockets the way she did when she didn't trust herself not to cry.

Tally had grown. That was the first thing — absurdly, the first thing Wendell noticed, that the girl was half a head taller than when she'd left, all elbows and new angles, her hair cut short and uneven in the way of someone who'd done it herself with a kitchen knife. Thirteen going on fourteen. A lifetime, in the life of a child.

"You're shorter than I remembered," Tally said.

"You're taller."

"Somebody had to be." But her voice cracked on the last word, and then she crossed the room fast and hit Wendell nearly as hard as Bess had, arms around her middle, face buried against her shoulder, shaking. "You *promised*," she said, muffled and furious. "You promised you'd come back, and then the whole city's saying you called a storm off the palace and got struck by a *god*, and nobody would tell me anything, and I had to hear it from a *caravan driver*—"

"I know. I'm sorry. I'm here."

"You're *here*?" Tally pulled back, red-eyed, glaring. "Here where? You've got a bag packed. I can see it. You were going to *leave*."

And there it was — the thing Wendell had been carrying since the day her magic guttered out, laid bare by a thirteen-year-old in a palace corridor. She looked at the bag by the door. She looked at the two people who had crossed half a kingdom to stand in front of it.

"I thought," Wendell said slowly, "that without the magic, I'd have nothing to offer Greyhallow. That I'd been useful there because I could keep the charms working, keep the luck-knots lucky, and now—" She spread her empty hands. Rain-wrinkled fingers. Nothing in them. "Now I'm just a woman who used to be able to call storms."

"Right," Tally said. "So you were going to sneak off in the night instead of letting anyone decide for themselves whether we wanted you. Again."

"Tally—"

"No, because *again*, Wendell. Three years you lived above our inn and you never once told us what you were. Not because we'd have turned you in — because you decided for us. You decided we were safer not knowing, and lonelier not knowing, and you never asked." The girl's chin was up, her jaw set, and Wendell saw in her suddenly the woman she would become, and loved her so hard it hurt. "So. I'm deciding now. We walked six days in the rain to come get you. Bess closed the inn. Bess *never* closes the inn. There's a sign on the door that says GONE TO FETCH OUR GIRL, BACK WHEN WE'VE GOT HER. So you're coming home, and you're staying home, and if you argue I will put frogs in your bed like I did when I was nine and I am *not too old*."

Behind her, Bess nodded solemnly, as though endorsing a legal document.

Wendell laughed — and then the laugh broke, and she sat down on the edge of the infirmary cot because her legs had stopped being reliable, and cried in front of them, openly, the way she had never once let herself cry in three years of careful hiding. Bess sat beside her and put one heavy arm around her shoulders. Tally sat on her other side and leaned her head against Wendell's arm, and none of them said anything for a long time, because there are things that words only get in the way of.

Finally Wendell wiped her face and looked at the packed bag, and stood, and carried it to the window, and dumped every shirt and comb and letter out onto the floor.

"I'll need a bigger bag," she said, "if I'm staying."

* * *

They left the capital three days later, on a morning so clear the mountains stood out sharp as knife-edges, and Wendell rode in the cart between Bess and Tally and watched the country unroll — villages rebuilding, shrines repainted in colors no god demanded, children running alongside the road shouting questions about the storm-caller until Tally shouted back that yes, that was her, and no, she wouldn't make it rain on command, and *yes*, she really had punched a god, which was not strictly true but which Wendell did not correct.

At the crest of the last hill, Greyhallow spread below them in the afternoon light: the crooked streets, the harbor smoke, the Drowned Lantern's sign swinging over its door. Clouds were gathering over the sea — real ones, honest ones, the kind that meant nothing except rain coming.

Wendell climbed down from the cart and stood a moment in the road. She raised her right hand — the one that had worn the iron rings, the one that had held the storm, the one that was only a hand now — and pressed it flat over her heart.

"I swear it," she said quietly. Not to any god; there were none listening, and that was its own kind of grace. To the city, maybe. To the two people waiting by the cart. To herself, most of all. "No more hiding. Whatever I am, whoever I turn out to be without the storm — you get all of it, out loud, in the open, every day. Even the bad days. *Especially* the bad days."

"That's a good oath," Tally said gravely. "Better than the old one."

"The old one was longer."

"The old one cost you everything. This one just costs honesty." Tally grinned, gap-toothed still, one incisor newly loose. "Cheap at the price."

The first drops fell as they walked down into the city — fat, unhurried raindrops, smelling of salt and stone and home. Wendell tipped her face up and let them land, and felt nothing stir in her blood, and found that this, too, was a kind of peace. The sky did what skies did. She did what she did. Neither owed the other anything anymore.

By the time they reached the Drowned Lantern the gutters were running and the sign creaked overhead, and Bess was already talking about stew, and Tally was already arguing about whose turn it was to haul water, and Wendell Eastwick stood in the doorway of the only place that had ever been hers and listened to the rain come down on Greyhallow —

ordinary weather,

falling on ordinary people,

and every drop of it, at last, free.
