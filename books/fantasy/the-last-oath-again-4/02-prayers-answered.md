## 2. Prayers Answered

The trouble with mapping the impossible, I had always believed, was that the impossible stayed put. It had geography. It kept to its borders like a well-mannered kingdom. The Guild of Impossible Things had built its entire reputation on the premise that wonder could be filed, cross-referenced, and shelved — that a thing which should not exist could at least be given a drawer number and left in peace.

By morning bell on the third day after the ledger, the impossible had stopped staying put.

I noticed it first in the smell of the city. Vellum-and-ink mornings in Saltreach had a particular character: coal smoke from the binderies, brine off the tidal flats, the sour-sweet rot of kelp the carts hadn't yet cleared. But that morning there was something under all of it, something older, like the air inside a cave that has been sealed for centuries. Ozone and stone and something almost sweet, the way my grandmother's cedar chest had smelled when I opened it as a child and found it full of nothing but cold.

The streets were full of people standing still.

Not crowds — that was the uncanny part. Just individuals, scattered along the Middenway and the fish-terraces and the steps up to the Lantern Quarter, each one frozen mid-stride with their face tilted toward nothing, as if listening to a voice only they could hear. A fishwife with her basket still swinging on her arm. A lamplighter with his pole half-raised. They weren't praying, exactly. Praying is an act; this was a posture. The posture of people who have suddenly remembered someone is listening.

I stopped beside a boy no older than ten who stood at the mouth of Cooper's Alley with his eyes shut and his lips moving soundlessly. His knuckles were white around a wooden toy boat.

"Are you all right?" I asked.

He opened his eyes. They were wet, and they were terrified, and he looked at me the way you look at someone when you have just been told your own secret.

"She answered," he whispered. "The Lady of the Deep Water. My da's been lost since the spring tides. I asked her every night since I was six and she never—" His voice cracked. "She said yes."

"Then that's good," I said, because what else do you say to a child.

He shook his head slowly. "She said *yes*, mistress. She didn't say *how*."

He ran. I watched him go, the little boat clutched against his chest like a shield, and felt the first cold thread of dread work its way under my skin. Because I knew what the boy did not: that the old gods had not answered prayers in four hundred years. That their silence was not neglect but law — a treaty older than the guild, older than the mountain, sealed in divine fire and recorded in ledgers like the one I had found beneath my grandmother's name. The gods slept because they had agreed to sleep. Everyone alive had grown up in the long quiet and called it the natural order.

And natural orders, I thought, watching the fishwife finally move on down the Middenway with her face still tilted upward, are only contracts that haven't been broken yet.

* * *

The shrine on Tallow Row was small enough that most people walked past it without seeing it. It had been a fountain once, then a trough, then a niche where someone had set a chipped statue of a woman holding scales that no longer balanced — one pan missing entirely, so that she weighed nothing against everything. The guild's census of sacred sites listed it as Category Four: Inert, Non-Responsive, Of Historical Interest Only. I had walked past it perhaps a thousand times on my way to the archive annex.

That afternoon it was humming.

I heard it before I saw it — a low tone, felt more than heard, the way you feel a struck bell through the soles of your feet. The chipped goddess's empty scale pan had begun to fill. Not with water. With light, thin as skimmed milk, pouring upward out of the stone into the pan that wasn't there, and the light trembled the way a scale trembles when something precious is being measured against something terrible.

A man knelt before it. He was a dockhand by his hands — rope-burned, tar-black in the creases — and he was weeping without any of the dignity weeping usually permits. His prayer came out in fragments between sobs, and I caught pieces of it as I slowed, then stopped, unable to make myself walk past.

"—she can't read, that's the thing, she can't read and they'll turn her out, please, please, let her learn, let her daughter read—"

The light in the absent pan brightened. The hum climbed a note. And I want to be honest about what happened next, because I have turned it over a thousand times since and I still cannot decide whether the god was cruel or merely precise, and I no longer believe those are different things.

Across the street, in the window of the stationer's shop, a lamp fell.

It was an oil lamp, cheap tin, the kind every shop keeps burning over its displays. It hit the sill, rolled, dropped into the bin of waste paper below — and the paper went up like a breath. Fire took the stationer's front room in seconds. The dockhand didn't see it; his eyes were shut, his prayer cresting toward its end. But the stationer's daughter came running out of the shop, shrieking her father's name, dragging him clear onto the cobbles while the fire roared behind them and the neighbors came with buckets and chains—

—and the girl, coughing, black-faced, clutching her rescued father, looked down at the charred scrap of paper that had fluttered to her feet. A page of a reading primer, half-burned, letters visible through the scorch.

She could read it. Perfectly. Every scorched letter. As if the fire had burned literacy itself into her.

The hum stopped. The scale pan went dark. The goddess went back to weighing nothing against everything, and the shrine was only a shrine again, inert, non-responsive, of historical interest only.

The dockhand opened his eyes and laughed — a raw, ruined, grateful laugh — and thanked the chipped statue over and over while behind him the stationer's livelihood burned and his daughter held a piece of it in her hands and read it aloud, fluently, for the first time in her life.

*A wish granted at cost.* That phrase appears forty-one times in the guild's oldest disaster ledgers. I had always assumed it was hyperbole, the way old cartographers write *here be monsters* when they mean *bad reefs*. Standing on Tallow Row with ash drifting past me like grey snow, I understood it was arithmetic. The gods had never been generous. They had only ever been exact. And exactness, in the hands of something that does not love you, is indistinguishable from malice.

* * *

The summons came by runner before dusk — not a note but a verbal order, which meant it was meant to be untraceable: *All guild members to the Map Hall. Immediately. Burn authorization pending.*

The Map Hall of the Guild of Impossible Things is the largest interior space in Saltreach, and for four hundred years it has held the most dangerous collection in the world: every map ever drawn of territory that shouldn't exist. The drowned country off the western shelf. The inverted forest. The ninety-one documented entrances to the court beneath the mountain, of which the guild officially acknowledged none. We kept them because knowing is the guild's whole purpose, and because a map of the impossible is the closest thing to a leash that the impossible will tolerate.

Master Corvane stood on the central dais beneath the great suspended orrery, and he was holding a torch.

I had known Corvane eleven years. He had signed my apprenticeship papers when no other master would take a gutter-foundling who drew maps of places she'd dreamed. He taught me that accuracy is a form of respect — that to map a thing truly is to honor it. He was not a dramatic man. So when I saw the torch, my stomach dropped before my mind caught up.

"The old gods are waking," he said, without preamble. His voice carried to the four corners of the hall without effort; he'd been trained in it, or born to it. "You will have seen the signs. Shrines responding. Prayers answered. Wish-granting in the lower quarters." Murmurs ran the length of the assembled cartographers. He let them run, then cut them off. "The guild's position is unchanged. Divine territory is unmappable, unmanageable, and historically catastrophic. Our charter obligates us to contain it."

He raised the torch. "Effective tonight, every chart marked with the red seal — every map of divine territory in this collection — is forfeit to flame. This is not destruction of knowledge. It is quarantine."

"That's the Sinking Charts," someone said near the front — old Master Ilse, whose voice shook. "Three hundred years of survey work. Corvane, those maps are the only record of—"

"Of a drowned country that has been quiet for four centuries," Corvane said. "Quiet things stay quiet when we don't poke them. The charts go. All of them. Tonight."

"And if the gods come asking why we burned their borders?" Ilse demanded. "If they take it as provocation?"

Something crossed Corvane's face then — fast, quickly buried, but I was a cartographer and reading surfaces was my trade. It was not fear. It was guilt. It lasted less than a heartbeat, and then the master's expression closed like a ledger.

"The gods," he said quietly, "will understand perfectly well why we burned them. That is rather the point."

The torch touched the first stack of charts and the fire moved down the racks like a tide coming in. Three hundred years of the Sinking Charts went up in curls of blue-edged flame. The inverted forest. The ninety-one doors. Around me, cartographers wept or turned away or stared with the hollow obedience of people watching something necessary and unforgivable happen and telling themselves it was both.

I did not burn mine. When the order came down the line — *your rack, Hartwell, your seals* — I fed it three duplicate charts of minor shrines, worthless copies I keep for exactly such purposes, and watched them curl while my true work sat folded against my ribs, warm from my own body heat, carrying every entrance to the court beneath the mountain that I had spent nine years secretly surveying.

I told myself it was instinct. Caution. The habit of a woman who trusts no institution further than she can throw its filing cabinets.

But that night, alone in my rooms above the annex, I unrolled the hidden charts across my floor and lit a single candle, and I looked at the network of passages I'd drawn leading down into the dark under the mountain, and I admitted the truth I'd been refusing since the boy with the toy boat:

The gods were answering prayers again. The contract that kept them sleeping was failing. And somewhere in the oldest ledgers of the guild I served, written in my grandmother's hand, was a debt that would come due the moment the last seal on that ancient peace gave way.

Someone knocked at my door. Three slow knocks, spaced too evenly, from a hand that did not hurry.

Through the door, a voice I did not know said: "Cartographer Hartwell. We need to talk about the page you found."
