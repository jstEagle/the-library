## 3. The Oath-Keeper's Fall

Davor Ashling had not climbed the guild stairs in eleven years, and his body remembered every one of them the way a debtor remembers interest. Two hundred and six steps from the street door to the Hall of Ledgers, each one worn into a shallow bowl by generations of clerks who believed, as he had once believed, that ascending these stairs was a form of virtue.

The rain followed him in. It pooled off his oilcloth cloak in dark crescents on marble that had been polished by ten thousand guilty feet, and the young woman behind the ledger desk looked up at him with the particular alarm reserved for men who smell of the road and old mistakes.

"I'm here for the Hartwell audit," he said. "Page forty-one thousand, the Ashling seal."

"You can't—" she began.

"Tell them Davor Ashling is asking," he said, "and watch their faces when they hear it. That will tell you everything you need to know about whether I'm expected."

She went. He waited, dripping, counting the brass lamps along the gallery rail — thirty-one, same as always; the guild never changed its count of anything — and listened to the building breathe around him. The Hall of Ledgers at night was a cathedral of paper. Shelves rose past the reach of lamplight, and somewhere above, the ventilation shafts moved air across vellum with a sound like slow breathing, like something enormous asleep on its hoard.

He had enforced contracts in this room. He had stood where Yara Hartwell now stood, at the reading table under the great lamp, and read names aloud to people who begged, and he had told himself every time that the law was the law and mercy was someone else's department.

* * *

Yara heard the name before they saw the man.

*Davor Ashling.* The clerk who brought the message whispered it like a diagnosis, and Yara set down their pen with a care that cost more effort than any map they'd ever drawn. Three days they had lived with page forty-one thousand — three days of copying it, tracing it, dreaming it, waking with their grandmother's handwriting burning behind their eyes: *one soul, sold beneath the mountain, payment deferred.*

They had not told anyone. They should have burned it. The guild was already burning maps of divine territory; what was one page among two hundred thousand?

But you didn't burn debts. You paid them, or they collected you.

Davor Ashling stood in the rain-stink of the doorway like a fence post someone had cursed — tall, grey at the temples, a face built for disapproval and currently aimed at the floor out of what might have been courtesy or shame. His left hand was gloved. Yara knew why without being told; every oath-keeper's left hand was gloved, because an oath-keeper's palm bore the sigil of every contract he'd ever sworn to enforce, and some of those sigils were better hidden.

"You're younger than your grandmother," he said.

"She died before I could walk." Yara kept their voice flat. "You'll forgive me if I don't weep for the family resemblance."

"I'm not here for grief." He stepped forward, and the lamp caught the rain on his jaw. "I'm here for the page."

"Then you've walked two hundred and six steps for nothing."

His eyes flicked up — surprise, quickly mastered. "You counted the stairs."

"It's a habit. I count things. It's my entire profession." Yara closed the ledger with both hands, as though the book were a door and they were standing in it. "You were the enforcer. My grandmother's signature is on this debt because you carried the pen to her house. So no, oath-keeper. You don't get the page. You don't get a copy, a summary, or the satisfaction of knowing I've read it. Get out of my hall."

Something moved through his face then, tectonic and slow, and Yara realized with a small cold shock that it wasn't anger. It was relief — the relief of a man who has been carrying something uphill for years and has finally been given permission to put it down.

"Good," Davor said quietly. "Refuse me properly. Because if you'd handed it over, I'd have known you didn't understand what you're holding, and I'd have taken it and let the fire finish, and that would have been the merciful thing." He pulled off the glove.

* * *

The sigils on his palm had faded almost white, but Yara could still read them — cartographer's training, the ability to find meaning in any marked surface — and the last one, lowest on the wrist, still held its ink: the coiled mountain-and-flame of the court beneath, wrapped around a word in the old divine script.

*Deferred.*

"The contract doesn't sleep," Davor said. "It waits. There's a difference, and the difference is dying tonight." He turned his palm toward the lamp, and Yara saw it — saw the sigil flicker, gutter, dim like a candle in a draft. "Dragon-fire sealed it. Every bargain written in divine law is anchored to the last true flame, the one that burns beneath the mountain, the one the old gods tended before they went silent. As long as it burns, the debt waits politely. Payment deferred. A courteous horror."

"And when it stops burning?"

"When it stops burning," Davor said, "the deferral ends. Not the contract — the *deferral*. The distinction matters. When the anchor dies, the law executes. Automatically. No herald, no summons, no grace period. Somewhere in the world, whoever holds that soul's bloodline simply ceases to be owed and begins to be *collected*." He flexed his fingers, and the sigil guttered again, weaker. "I've watched it dim for eleven years. I was there when it flared for the last time — the night your grandmother died, the night the payment rolled to her heir. To you, Hartwell. The debt has been walking downhill toward your family for sixty years, and it's nearly arrived."

Yara's hands were steady on the ledger. Their voice was not quite. "How long?"

"If it keeps gutters the way it's guttering — days. A week, perhaps. The gods coming back are drinking the air it needs. Every answered prayer is another breath taken from the flame." He said it without drama, which was somehow worse. "You've seen the shrines. Wish-granting in the Lantern District, a man healed and his shadow taken as the fee. The old powers are returning, and they are *hungry*, and the last dragon-fire is the only law older than they are. When it goes, so does every protection it anchors. Including yours."

* * *

They stood a long time without speaking, on either side of the reading table, between them a book that weighed less than either of their secrets.

"Why come to me?" Yara asked finally. "If the contract executes anyway, why warn me? Why not let it collect and wash your hands of the whole rotten line?"

"Because I signed it." He said it the way a man confesses a murder — plainly, without ornament, having rehearsed until the rehearsal wore through. "Not my father. Me. I was young, and the masters told me it was a formality, a renewal of an ancient arrangement, a signature on behalf of the guild's continuity. Your grandmother was old and frightened and alone, and I stood in her kitchen and explained that deferral was a kindness, that the court beneath the mountain was patient and honorable, that her family would never feel the weight of it." His jaw tightened. "And then I took her soul's mark in my book and walked back down those two hundred and six stairs, and I have been climbing and descending them in my sleep ever since."

"You want absolution."

"No." Flat, immediate. "Absolution is a gift and I haven't earned gifts. What I want is *work*. There is a clause in the founding law — pre-guild, pre-mountain, older than the script on my hand — and I spent eleven years in exile finding fragments of it. Debts sealed in dragon-fire may be discharged in forms other than blood. I don't know all of it. I know enough to know the answer isn't in this hall, and isn't in any hall the guild controls, and that the guild will burn this page and every copy the moment they understand what's happening — because the guild does not pay debts, Hartwell. The guild *holds* them. That is what we are. We are the vault where guilt is stored so the comfortable can sleep."

Yara looked at him — the ruined sigil, the gloved hand lying open on the table like a surrendered weapon, the eyes of a man who had audited his own soul and found the entries wanting — and felt the terrible vertigo of recognizing a fellow prisoner.

"They'll have seen you come in," Yara said. "The masters. They'll know what page you asked for."

"They knew before I reached the stairs. I'm surprised there aren't hunters in the gallery yet."

As if summoned, the great doors at the far end of the hall swung inward without a sound, and lamplight spilled across the marble in long gold blades. Silhouettes stood in it — four, five, cloaked in guild grey, and the tallest of them wore the mask of the Office of Continuity, smooth and featureless as an egg.

Yara slid the page free with one motion, folded it small, and pressed it into Davor's ruined palm.

"You said the answer isn't in any hall the guild controls," they said, already reaching for the satchel of charts and pens that hung behind the desk — the tools they had sworn never to use outside sanctioned territory. "Then we go where the guild doesn't control anything. Down."

Davor stared at the folded page, then at them. "You don't even know me."

"I know your hand," Yara said. "It's on my family's ruin. Which means you're the only person alive who owes this as much as I do." Behind the hunters, the masked figure began to walk, unhurried, certain, and Yara shouldered the satchel and nodded toward the archivists' stair — the narrow one, the unguarded one, the one that led down past the vaults to places not even the masters remembered. "Two hundred and six steps going up. Let's see how many there are going down."
