## 4. What the Guild Keeps

The vault opened only at night.

That was the first rule they taught you as an apprentice cartographer: the Guild of Impossible Things kept its truest treasures in darkness, because some things — most things — could not bear to be looked at by daylight. Yara had accepted the rule the way one accepts all guild rules, without question, for nineteen years. Tonight they questioned it with a stolen key warm in their fist and Davor Ashling's warning ringing in their ears like a struck bell.

*When the last dragon-fire dies, the contract executes itself. No court needed. No negotiation. Just arithmetic.*

The key had come from Master Ellery's own belt, lifted during the evening meal while the old woman argued about grain tariffs with Master Corvin. Yara told themselves it was not theft. It was *reclamation*. The ledgers downstairs recorded debts owed to Yara's blood; surely that entitled them to see what else was written in the guild's hidden hand.

The stairs down went further than the stairs down should have gone.

Yara counted steps out of habit — a cartographer's tic, mapping even where no map was wanted — and lost count somewhere past two hundred. The lamplight from above became a coin-sized memory. The air changed: drier, older, with a mineral sweetness like the inside of a geode, like the breath of stone that had been holding its exhalation for centuries.

At the bottom, the corridor widened into the vault proper, and Yara stopped walking because stopping was the only sane response.

They had expected treasure. Every apprentice gossiped about treasure — the original chart of the Shattered Sea, the feathered compass that pointed at whatever you feared most, the taxidermied griffin nobody would confirm or deny. There were wonders here, yes. Glass cases glimmering in the dark, shelves of bound impossibilities humming faintly against each other like tuning forks.

But the walls were what stopped Yara's heart.

The walls were covered in paper. Floor to ceiling, corner to corner, thousands upon thousands of documents, each one pinned or pasted or stitched into the stone, and every single one of them was a prayer.

Yara knew prayers the way any city-dweller did — scrawled on shrine-ribbons, murmured over candles, whispered into wells. But these had been *filed*. Sorted. Each prayer bore a small notation in the margin in a dozen different hands across a dozen different centuries: dates, names, and in the corner of each page, a tiny stamped sigil Yara recognized with a sick lurch.

The mountain seal. The seal from the ledger entry written in their grandmother's hand. The mark of the court beneath.

"No," Yara said aloud, and the vault ate the word without echo.

They moved along the wall, lamp raised, reading. A prayer from 211 years ago: *Let my daughter's fever break.* Notation: *Granted. Cost assessed. Payment deferred to line of Hartwell.* A prayer from 140 years ago: *Send rain before the blight takes the west fields.* Same stamp. Same notation. *Payment deferred.*

Deferred. Again and again, across generations — *deferred* — as though the debt were a wine too sour to drink yet, set aside in a cellar to age. Except the cellar was a bloodline, and the wine was souls.

Yara's hands were shaking. They made themselves keep reading, because a cartographer who stops surveying at the first horror never finishes the map, and unfinished maps are how people die.

Here was the mechanism, laid bare in marginalia. When the old gods fell silent — three centuries ago now, the Quiet Years every schoolchild learned about — the guild had faced a problem. People still prayed. Prayer, unanswered, curdles into despair, and despair curdles into riots, and riots burn guildhalls. So the masters had found a buyer. Or perhaps the buyer had found them; the oldest document, brittle as autumn leaf, suggested the court beneath had made the first offer, slid under the guildhall door like a bill collector's note.

*Your people wish. We answer. You choose who pays.*

And the masters had chosen. Century after century, they had taken the desperate prayers of the city — the feverish child, the dying field, the drowned sailor's widow — and routed them downward through channels Yara could almost trace in the vault's architecture, and the court beneath had answered, and the cost had been assigned, deferred, deferred, deferred, always to the same patient ledger, always accruing interest in the only currency the court accepted.

Souls. Sold. Generations of them. Yara's grandmother's entry was not an aberration. It was a *line item*.

There were other Hartwells in the files. A great-uncle Yara had been told died of marsh-fever. A cousin twice removed, lost at sea. Each with the same neat stamp, the same deferral notation, the same quiet administrative language that turned human beings into outstanding balances.

Yara sat down on the cold floor because standing had become a theory rather than a practice.

*They raised me,* Yara thought. The guild had found them orphaned at nine, tested their gift for mapping the unmappable, fed and clothed and taught them. *They raised me on the interest from my own family's sale.* The thought arrived whole and terrible, like a door swinging open onto a drop. Every warm memory — Master Ellery's dry praise, the smell of ink in the drafting hall, the belonging of it all — now had a second image printed on its reverse, visible when held to light. Complicity wearing the face of kindness.

A sound above. Footsteps on the stair, unhurried, familiar in their cadence.

Yara killed the lamp and pressed into the shadow between two cases, pulse loud enough to constitute percussion. The footsteps descended, and a second light bloomed in the vault — a hooded lantern, its glow shuttered to a blade.

Master Corvin stood among his own archives like a farmer among his fields.

He was old, but his hands were steady as he pulled a prayer from the wall — a recent one, the ink barely cured. He read it, made a small notation, and then did something Yara had no framework for: he folded the prayer into a copper tube, sealed it with wax stamped by the mountain sigil, and set it into a slot in the floor. Somewhere far below, something swallowed.

He did this eleven more times. Methodical. Tender, almost, the way a man is tender with livestock he intends to slaughter.

On the twelfth, he spoke to the dark. "You may as well come out, Hartwell. Your lamp-oil smells of juniper. It always has. You steal it from the same shelf I do."

The shadow offered nothing. Yara stayed still, breath shallow, mind racing through options and finding none worth the candle.

Corvin sighed and lowered himself onto a stool that clearly lived there, beside a desk Yara now noticed, cluttered with the working tools of exactly this task. "I've done the midnight collection for thirty years," he said conversationally. "Ellery did it thirty years before me. Her master before her. You think the vault opens only at night because of the treasures?" He gestured at the walls with their thousand filed prayers. "It's because this is the guild's real work, and real work is best done sleeping."

Yara stepped out. Denial was pointless; Corvin had known they'd come the moment the key left his colleague's belt, probably. "How many," Yara said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "How many souls."

"Outstanding? Four hundred and six." Corvin said it the way he reported rainfall. "Paid? More than double that, over the centuries. The court is not greedy. It is merely *precise*. And before you compose your outrage, consider what those prayers bought. Plagues that broke early. Floods that crested a hand's width short. Three hundred years of a city believing itself lucky." His eyes, pale and watery in the lantern light, fixed on Yara. "Stability has a supplier, child. We are simply the brokers."

"You chose who paid."

"We chose who paid *least painfully*, which is more mercy than the market usually allows." He rose, joints cracking. "But that era's ending. You've seen the shrines waking. When the old gods resume answering for themselves, the court loses its monopoly, and it will call in everything outstanding before its leverage dies. All four hundred and six. At once." He said it gently, which made it worse. "Unless the contract is unmade. And you already know the only thing that can unmake a divine seal."

"The dragon-fire," Yara said. "Which is guttering."

"Which is guttering." Corvin gathered his copper tubes into a satchel with the care of a man packing eggs. "So. Here is what happens next. In a day, perhaps two, the masters convene and decide whether to fight the court, flee it, or feed it — and I will tell you now, honestly, since you've earned honesty by breaking in: two of the five votes belong to feeding it. Starting with the newest debtors. Starting, likely, with you." He slung the satchel. "I'm telling you this because your grandmother was kind to me once, and because I am tired, and because a broker who forgets the goods are alive eventually forgets *he* is alive too."

"Why tell me anything?"

"Because the seal you're looking for isn't in a case." Corvin nodded toward the far wall, behind the oldest stratum of prayers, where the stone bulged around a shape Yara's eye had skated past. "It never left the hand that made the bargain. And that hand is buried deeper than this vault, in the one place the guild cannot follow." He smiled without warmth. "Beneath, Hartwell. Everything comes from beneath, and everything returns there."

He climbed the stairs, lantern swaying, and left Yara alone with four hundred and six souls' worth of paper and the sound of their own complicit heartbeat.

* * *

Yara did not sleep. They copied. It was the only prayer they knew.

By lamplight they transcribed the deferral notations, the dates, the lineage assignments, building in three hours the truest map they had ever drawn: a genealogy of debt, their family's branches rendered as columns in a creditor's book. When the first gray light seeped down the stairwell, the pages in their satchel weighed more than paper should.

They emerged into a guildhall already awake and already wrong. Runners crossed the courtyard at speed. The great map room's doors stood open, and from inside came the smell Yara had been ordered, two days ago, to help create: burning vellum. The order to destroy all maps of divine territory, obeyed at last, or obeyed *now*, when obedience had changed meaning entirely.

Master Ellery stood at the edge of the fire barrel, watching forty years of her own surveys curl and blacken. She did not turn when Yara stopped beside her.

"You went down," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you know we can't protect you anymore. Which means, dear child, that we never could." She finally looked at them, and her eyes held something Yara had never seen there: fear, worn openly, like a wound that couldn't be dressed. "There's a clerk asking for you at the front desk. Been waiting since before dawn. Sef — the quiet one from the archive annex. Says he has passage beneath the mountain." She paused. "Says, and I quote him exactly because it chilled me: 'Tell her I can take her below, if she agrees to forget me.'"

Behind Ellery, the fire took another map, and the borders of a country that had never existed went up in light.

Yara adjusted their satchel and walked toward the front desk, toward the strange clerk, toward beneath.
