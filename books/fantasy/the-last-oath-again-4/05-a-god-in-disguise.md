## 5. A God in Disguise

I had been Sef the clerk for sixty-one years, and I had grown fond of him the way a man grows fond of a coat worn through three winters — not because it flattered him, but because it knew the shape of him.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers on the fourth floor of a building that leaned, very slightly, toward the river, as though even architecture could be tempted by escape. My desk sat beneath the window where the light came in amber and slow, and my work was the humble arithmetic of wonder: cataloguing petitions, filing denials, stamping the word *deferred* on requests that would never be granted. Mortals asked the guild for impossible things every day. I was paid — modestly, in coin that smelled of tallow and dust — to explain why.

It was good work for hiding.

You must understand what hiding means for one such as me. It is not merely concealment. It is *diminishment*. When the temples fell and my siblings scattered into wind and rumor, those of us who survived did so by making ourselves small. We poured our names out like wine into sand. We took up trades. I chose ink because ink is patient, and paper because paper forgets nothing except, if you are careful, you.

Sixty-one years of forgetting myself, practiced daily, until the practice became the thing itself. Some mornings I woke and had to check my own hands to be sure they were mine — these soft clerk's hands, stained brown at the fingertips, no longer capable of the old gestures. Once, with these hands, I had parted a flood so a village of weavers could cross a valley dry-shod. Now they trembled when I carried too many ledger volumes at once.

I tell you this so you will understand why, on the evening Yara Hartwell came down the stairs with a stolen page hidden in their coat, I made the worst decision of my long, shrunken life.

* * *

They didn't see me at first. No one ever saw me; that was rather the point of me. I sat at my desk with a petition about rain before me — a farmer's daughter asking for water in a district the guild had mapped as permanently barren — and I watched Yara cross the archive floor like someone walking on the deck of a ship in weather.

They had the look I remembered from the oldest stories: the look of a person carrying something true and terrible, who has not yet decided whether it makes them brave or damned. Their satchel hung wrong, weighted. Their eyes kept moving to the corners of the room, cataloguing exits. A cartographer's habit. You map the way out before you finish mapping the way in.

The page they'd taken from the deep vault — I knew which page. Word travels strangely through a building; through me, faster than through walls. *One soul, sold beneath the mountain. Payment deferred.* Written in a grandmother's careful hand. The Hartwell debt. The debt my siblings' court held, the debt the guild had fed and tended like a candle they dared not let go out.

Yara stopped at the counter across from my desk, ostensibly to return a folio. Ostensibly is a fine word. It lets you describe what a thing pretends to be while admitting it lies.

"Late hour, Master Sef," they said.

"The ledgers don't sleep," I said, "so neither do I." A clerk's answer. I had said some version of it ten thousand times, and each time a small part of me died politely behind the words, which was the arrangement I had made with the world.

But Yara looked at me then. Really looked, the way they must have looked at coastlines that appeared on no other map — with that particular hunger of a person who trusts their own instruments more than anyone else's assurances. And I felt something I had not felt in six decades.

I felt *seen*.

Not recognized — recognition requires a name, and I had poured mine out. But seen, the way light sees a thing. Their gaze passed over me and snagged, just slightly, the way a cartographer's pen snags on an island that isn't supposed to be there.

"You're not what you appear," Yara said quietly. Not an accusation. An observation. The worst kind of sentence, because observations cannot be argued with, only confirmed or falsified, and I had spent sixty-one years ensuring no one would ever think to test me.

"Neither are you, apparently," I said, "a mere auditor."

We regarded each other across the counter, two false things recognizing the craft in one another.

* * *

Here is what the mortals never understood about the old gods, and what I will set down now because after tonight there may be no one left who knows it: we were never powerful the way your guild masters imagine power. We did not command. We *answered*. That was the whole architecture of divinity — prayer went out like a hand opened in the dark, and something answered it. For an age, that something was us. Then the answering stopped — we withdrew, we were withdrawn, the histories disagree and I was not present at my own retreat in any state to keep records — and the world learned to make do.

Now the answering had begun again. I felt it the way a man feels weather changing in an old wound. Across the city, shrines were waking. Wishes were being granted at rates that would have alarmed any competent actuary. And my siblings — my vast, hungry, magnificent siblings — were following the prayers home like wolves following the scent of blood to its source.

They would find me eventually. Not out of malice. Out of *bookkeeping*. I was, after all, an entry in their ledger too — a minor godling of thresholds and small mercies, unaccounted for, unaccounted-with. Divine law is not sentimental. It does not forgive an open column.

And divine law, I should say plainly, is what runs beneath the mountain. Not cruelty. Law. The court below had never done anything to Yara Hartwell's grandmother except enforce a contract to the letter — which is worse than cruelty, because cruelty can be appealed to, and law cannot.

Yara sat across from me now, in the little room behind the archives where clerks eat their suppers, and I told them none of this yet. Instead I watched them spread a pencil sketch on the table: cavern passages, drawn freehand, the lines confident but the labels sparse. *Unmapped*, the margins admitted, again and again. *Unknown depth. Unsurveyed.*

"You're going down," I said.

"I'm going down."

"Through territory no living map covers."

"I'll cover it." They said it without bravado, which is how you know a person means it. Bravado is for people hoping to be talked out of things.

"And when the guild's hunters come? Because they will come. Ashling's fall from grace bought him enemies in high places, and you—" I paused, choosing the honest shape of the sentence, "—you have discovered what the masters actually worship, and it isn't the guild."

Yara looked up. "You know about the vault."

"I file things, Hartwell. Everything passes through filing eventually." I folded my hands — my soft, useless, once-miraculous hands — and made my decision the way all gods make decisions: suddenly, and then pretending it was inevitable. "There is a way beneath the mountain that the hunters will not find, because it is not on any map, and it will never be on any map, because maps cannot hold it. It opens for me and closes behind me."

"Why would it open for a clerk?"

"Because," I said, and here the room grew quieter around my voice, the lamplight leaning in, the shadows attending, "it is mine. I made it. Long ago, when fleeing was still a skill I possessed. A threshold-god's last trick: a door that exists only between telling and arriving. Step through while the story is still being told, and you are elsewhere. Finish the sentence, and you are nowhere at all."

I had not spoken of myself in the first person plural of power for sixty years. The words tasted like a language half-remembered from childhood — sweet and faintly shameful.

Yara stared. To their credit, they did not ask *what are you*. They asked the better question, the cartographer's question:

"What will it cost?"

Everything costs. This is the first law, older than divine law, older than the mountain. I respected them enormously for knowing it.

"Forgetting," I said. "When this is done — whatever is done — I want to be forgotten again. Thoroughly. You'll have seen what I am. So will Ashling. The court beneath will know me the moment I cross its borders; they keep excellent records of deserters. If my siblings learn a godling walked a mortal thief into their court, they will pull me back into the family business, and the family business, as you've now seen, is souls." I smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth like a river stone. "So: passage in exchange for erasure. You will forget my face. Ashling will forget my name. The guild's records of a clerk called Sef will blur and smudge until no one can swear he ever existed. I go back to being no one, safely, permanently. In exchange, I walk you through the dark to the court's front door."

"That seems a small price for you," Yara said slowly, "and a strange bargain overall. Why help us at all?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question. I could have given them the clerk's answer — duty, conscience, the guild's honor. I could have given them the god's answer — debts of my own, ancient and unpayable.

Instead I gave them the truth, because I liked them, and because liking someone seemed, after sixty years, like a reason.

"Because I filed your grandmother's petition," I said. "Ninety-one years ago. A young woman, pregnant, terrified, her family drowning in a debt she didn't understand she was carrying. She prayed at a wayside shrine — one of mine, before the falling — and asked only that her child be allowed to grow up free." My hands trembled. I let them. "I was already diminished. I could not answer. Someone else answered instead. Something beneath the mountain heard a prayer meant for me, and it took her at her word, and it wrote her child into a contract, and the guild countersigned it because the guild will sign anything that keeps the peace." I looked at the unmapped caverns on the table, at the confident lines running down into darkness. "Your whole line has been collateral for ninety-one years because I was too small, that night, to answer a prayer addressed to me. I have been small on purpose ever since. I would like, once, before the end of everything, to be the right size."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, the guild masters' meeting droned on, deciding whom to feed to the mountain next.

Yara Hartwell reached across the table and did something no mortal had done to me in sixty-one years of hiding: they shook my hand. Firmly. As an equal. As though shaking hands with a god in disguise were simply the courteous thing to do when one presented itself.

"All right, Sef," they said. "One door, one descent, one forgetting. But I'm warning you — I map everything I pass through. Habit. If your door leaves a mark on my charts, I won't be able to erase it."

I laughed — a sound like a page turning — and stood, and felt the old gestures stir in my shoulders like birds remembering flight.

"Then we leave tonight," I said. "Before the fire gutters and the contract wakes. And Hartwell — bring extra candles. What's beneath the mountain hasn't been lit by anything warm in a very long time."

At the threshold of the little room I paused, hand on the frame, feeling my oldest nature rise to meet me like water finding its level.

Behind me, faint and getting fainter, I heard the shrine-bells of the city begin to ring — every bell at once, unanswered prayers finally being collected, my siblings coming home.

I opened the door that exists only between telling and arriving, and stepped through while the story was still being told.
