## 6. The Descent

The mountain did not begin at the mountain. That was the first thing the maps had never told me.

It began three days' ride south of the guild city, where the river Vell slid into a crack in the limestone and vanished with a sound like a held breath. The locals called it the Swallow. They left offerings on a flat stone beside it — bread, copper coins, once, horribly, a cage with an empty bird inside — because offerings were cheaper than questions.

I knelt at the water's edge with my satchel open and my good ink already shivering in the cold, and I understood that everything I had ever drawn above ground had been practice for this: a country with no ceiling, no light, and no one who had ever come back to describe it.

"You're smiling," Davor said behind me. He said it the way other men accused.

"I'm terrified," I said. "It looks similar."

Sef crouched at the Swallow's lip and trailed two fingers through the current, and for a moment — just a moment — the water went still around his hand, obedient, the way a dog goes still when its oldest friend whistles. Then he stood and brushed off his clerk's gray sleeves as if he'd done nothing at all.

"It remembers me," he said mildly. "Water always does. It's very sentimental."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?" Davor asked.

"No," Sef said. "Comforting would be lying to you, and I've grown fond of you both, which is inconvenient. Come along. The dark is patient, but not infinitely so."

* * *

The first hour underground, I learned that darkness is not the absence of light but the presence of weight. It pressed on my eyes until they ached. Our lantern made a bubble of amber maybe six feet wide, and beyond that bubble the stone simply stopped existing — not black, but *unwritten*, and every cartographer knows there is nothing more frightening than unwritten space.

So I did what I have always done. I wrote.

I sat on cold rock while Davor checked our rope lines and Sef listened to the water, and I charted by lantern-glow: *Chamber one. Floor of wet limestone, slope descending east-northeast, bearing roughly 40 degrees from true north as best the needle can say, though the needle has begun to wander, note that, NOTE THAT.* My grandmother taught me letters; the guild taught me geometry; and between them they had built a woman whose answer to fear was a steady hand and a fine nib. I drew the cavern walls in careful hatching. I marked the depth in fathoms. I gave names to nameless things, because a thing with a name can be found again, and found again means survived again.

*The Needle Wavers,* I named the passage. *Here the compass forgets its father.*

"Is that wise?" Davor asked, reading over my shoulder. "Naming things down here. Naming is a kind of claiming."

"So is walking," I said. "We're already guilty."

Something moved in his face — not quite a smile. "You sound like the oath-masters used to sound, before they got fat on procedure."

"And you sound like a man avoiding a conversation we've been circling since the guild gates."

The lantern flame leaned. Somewhere far above us, faint as a memory, came the baying of dogs — no. Not dogs. Guild hunters ran with *hounds of record*, beasts bred to follow the scent of ink. They would have my ledger-pages from the vault, my handwriting, my name in a hundred registers. They could not lose me unless I stopped being written, and I did not intend to stop until the work was done.

"We should move," Sef said quietly. He had gone still in that way he sometimes did, head tilted, listening to something under the world. "The water is telling the deep places we've arrived. Water gossips. Everything down here will know our shape before morning."

* * *

We walked. The caverns unspooled like the gut of some enormous sleeping animal — chambers vast enough to swallow the guild cathedral whole, then throats of stone so narrow I had to turn my satchel sideways and breathe in sips. The rock changed as we descended: gray limestone giving way to veins of white quartz that caught our lantern-light and threw it back broken, so that sometimes the darkness glittered like a sky full of mean little stars.

I charted all of it. My hand learned a rhythm: walk, measure, draw, walk. I named the chambers as we passed through them because naming kept the fear in its pen.

*The Hall of Broken Stars.* *The Throat Where Breath Is Borrowed.* *The Gallery of Hands* — that last for formations of flowstone that rose from the floor in columns uncannily like upturned palms, hundreds of them, thousands, as if the mountain were a congregation mid-prayer.

Davor walked behind me the whole way. He had insisted on rear guard, and I had let him, and only later understood why: a man walks last so no one watches his face.

It was in the Gallery of Hands that he finally spoke.

"I signed it."

My nib stopped mid-stroke. A bead of ink swelled at the tip and fell, and I watched it bloom on the stone like a small black flower.

"The original sale," Davor said. His voice in that enormous chamber came out flattened, stripped of echo, as if even the mountain declined to repeat it. "Your grandmother's soul. I was the oath-keeper attached to the contract. Young then. Ambitious. The masters told me it was a formality, that the debt was symbolic, that divine law required a witness of standing and I was standing right there." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "They handed me the quill and I thought about my career. That's the whole confession. I thought about my career for the length of time it takes to write four words. *Signed and sealed, witnessed.* And somewhere beneath this mountain, a woman who mended maps and sang off-key and loved her granddaughter before her granddaughter existed, became a line item."

I turned around slowly. In the lantern light, Davor Ashling looked older than he had in the guild hall, older than he had at the river. Shame does that. It ages you from the inside out, like rot in timber — the surface holds, and holds, and holds.

"Why tell me now?" I asked.

"Because we're deep enough that you can't leave," he said. "Because if I waited until the court and told you there, you'd think it was strategy. And because—" His jaw worked. "Because when the hounds catch us, and they will, they'll offer me reinstatement to turn on you. I need you to know what my word is worth before you decide whether to spend yours on it. It's worth this: I broke the greatest oath of my life for ambition, and I have spent thirty years keeping every small oath since, trying to fill the hole, and the hole doesn't fill. But I will stand between you and whatever comes out of that court, and I will not sign anything ever again, and if the choice comes to it, I will burn before I let them take another Hartwell."

"Hartwell," I repeated.

"Yes."

"My name. Which I'm going to need, possibly, for what's coming."

"Yes," he said again, wretched. "Which is why I'm telling you now. While you can still hate me properly."

I looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, the stone hands reached upward in their frozen congregation, and the quartz stars glittered, and the dark pressed in like a second skin.

"I don't hate you," I said at last. "I want to. It would be simpler. But my grandmother spent forty years teaching me that a person is not their worst entry in the ledger." I picked up my satchel. "Walk in front of me for a while. I'd rather see your face."

He blinked. Something in his shoulders unlocked — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but the beginning of a load shared instead of carried alone.

Then the lantern went out.

* * *

Not flickered. Went out, all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the dark came down on us complete and total and *thick*, and somewhere ahead of us in that perfect blackness, something exhaled.

I heard Davor's blade clear leather. I heard Sef say, softly, wonderingly, "Oh. Hello." And I heard, from deeper in the mountain, from many directions at once, a sound like pages turning.

Light bloomed — not ours. Cold and blue-green and sourceless, rising out of the rock itself, and I saw what breathed in the Gallery of Hands.

They were moths. Or they had been moths once, before generations of living beside the dragon-fire's distant glow had written something else onto them. Each was the size of a dinner plate, wings patterned in whorls of silver and eye-spots that dilated as they looked at us, and they hung in the air in a slow spiral, dozens of them, silent as falling snow.

"They're mapping us," Sef murmured.

And he was right. I felt it — a sensation like a fingertip drawn across the back of my neck, like being read. The moths circled, and wherever their gaze touched, I felt my own edges defined, catalogued. *One cartographer, armed with paper. One oath-keeper, armed with regret. One god pretending to be small.*

The spiral tightened. Davor raised his blade anyway, bless his stubborn heart, and I put a hand on his arm.

"Don't," I said. "You don't fight a census. You answer it."

I opened my satchel. I took out my ledger — the new one, the descent-book, filled with my careful hatching and my named chambers — and I held it up into the cold light, open, turning slowly, showing them every page.

*I am Yara Hartwell,* the pages said, in my own hand, because I had no other voice that would carry here. *I am coming down. This is where I've been. This is the truth of my path, and I have hidden none of it.*

The spiral stopped.

For one long heartbeat every eye-spot fixed on me at once, and the pressure of being read became almost unbearable — and then, all together, the great moths folded their wings and poured away down the passage ahead of us, a river of silver vanishing into the deep, leaving a corridor of faintly glowing stone in their wake.

A road. They had given us a road.

"They'll report to the court," Davor said hoarsely.

"Yes," said Sef. He was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse — awe, or pity, or both wearing the same face. "Yes, they will. And now the court knows something it didn't know before, Yara Hartwell. It knows you're honest. Down there, that makes you either the most dangerous creature to enter in a thousand years—"

He paused. Far below, through leagues of stone, something answered the moths. A sound like a forge breathing. Like fire remembering itself, thin and failing and *furious* to be failing.

"—or exactly what the mountain has been starving for," Sef finished. "Come. Walk quickly. I think your grandmother's jailer just lit lamps for company."
