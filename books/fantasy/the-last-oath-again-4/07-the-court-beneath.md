## 7. The Court Beneath

The last passage opened like a throat.

One moment Yara was crawling through a seam of stone no wider than their shoulders, Davor's breath rasping behind them and Sef's lantern-light trembling against the walls; the next the rock simply gave way, folding outward with the courtesy of a door held open by an invisible hand. Cold air poured through — not dead air, but *kept* air, scented with beeswax and cold iron and something floral that had no business existing this far beneath the world.

Yara got to their feet in a corridor of black marble veined with gold so fine it looked like writing. In fact, it *was* writing. They realized it slowly, the way one realizes a room is full of watching people: the veins in every slab of stone were lines of script, running floor to ceiling, wall to wall, in a hand so ancient the letters had softened into ornament.

"It's all contracts," Yara said quietly.

"Every surface," Sef agreed. His clerk's stoop was gone now; he walked the corridor like a man returning to a house he'd been evicted from. "The mountain is a ledger. I told you it would be."

"You said it would be *polite*."

"I said it would be *courteous*. Pray you never learn the difference."

Davor came up beside them, rolling his shoulders as though he could shrug off three days of crawling. The oath-keeper's brand on his wrist — the broken circle that had cost him his name among his order — caught the lantern light. He flexed his fingers once, twice, and Yara understood he was rehearsing something. A confession, maybe. Or a plea.

"Do not speak first," Sef murmured. "Do not accept anything offered to eat, drink, or wear. And whatever they show you—" He paused. Whatever else he meant to say, he swallowed it. "Whatever they show you, remember that everything here is true. That is what makes it dangerous."

*Everything here is true.* Yara turned the phrase over as they walked. Above ground, truth was a thing you dug for, pried loose, argued over. Here it seemed to be a substance, ambient and total, like the cold.

The corridor ended at doors of pale wood, tall as cathedrals, carved with scenes Yara refused at first to study — then could not stop studying. Figures signing documents. Figures kneeling before figures holding quills. A figure with a cartographer's satchel standing before a throne, its face worn smooth by centuries of reverent touching.

Someone had touched that carving often. Someone had waited for them.

The doors opened without a sound.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain did not look like a court. It looked like a reading room the size of a cathedral, lit by chandeliers of dragon-fire gone amber with age — the old flame, Yara realized, the first flame, banked down to embers and hoarded here like candle-ends in a miser's drawer. Desks stretched away into the gloom in perfect rows, and at each desk sat a figure writing, writing, writing, the scratch of ten thousand pens a sound like rain on a tin roof.

Between the desks moved the courtiers.

They were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: precise, patterned, and utterly indifferent to the warmth dying behind them. They wore robes the color of wet slate, and their faces were smooth except where ink stained them — lines of script curling up from their collars along their jaws, across their cheekbones, as though they had been written upon for so long the writing had become skin.

A woman at the front of them stepped forward. Her hair was bound in silver thread; her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her expression, like thunder after lightning.

"Cartographer Hartwell." Her voice filled the room without effort. "And the oath-keeper Ashling, returned at last. And—" her gaze slid over Sef with something that was almost, but not quite, amusement, "—a guest we had not expected. How wonderful. We do so rarely receive visitors who are *expected*."

"We're not here for hospitality," Davor said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You are here for a soul. Please, walk with us. Negotiations are so much more civil on the feet."

They walked. The pen-scratch rose around them like weather. Yara counted desks out of habit — three hundred, four hundred — and made herself stop when she understood what she was counting. Not desks. *Accounts.* Each one a soul, perhaps, or a fragment of one, filed and maintained and serviced like a debt that never aged because time itself had been folded into the terms.

"Your grandmother," the courtier said conversationally, "had excellent penmanship."

Yara's stomach dropped through the floor. "You know why we've come."

"We have known why you've come since before your guild had a name. The contract knew. Contracts *wait*, cartographer. That is their whole genius. Your grandmother signed in 1211 by the old reckoning. She was nineteen. The harvest had failed twice, and there was a broker in her village offering loans against things the poor were told they didn't need anyway." The courtier's tone never changed; it remained the pleasant, level voice of someone reciting a favorite recipe. "She signed for grain. Eleven sacks of barley. You have crawled three days through the dark to contest eleven sacks of barley."

"It wasn't hers to sell," Yara said.

"No," the courtier agreed. "It was not. That is what makes it binding."

They stopped before a desk apart from all the others — smaller, set on a dais of white stone, tended by no one. On it lay a single sheet of vellum under a case of glass, and above the glass hung a lamp whose flame burned low and blue and wrong, guttering in slow motion, each flicker lasting minutes.

The last dragon-fire. Even here, even hoarded, it was dying. Yara watched it dim a fraction and felt the whole room lean toward the darkness like plants toward sun.

"When that goes out," the courtier said, following Yara's gaze, "every contract sealed under divine law executes simultaneously. All debts come due at once. Do you understand what is owed to us, in aggregate?" She smiled her half-second smile. "Neither do we. We are rather looking forward to finding out."

* * *

It was Davor who broke. Yara had expected it — had been braced for it since the caverns — but nothing prepared them for the sound of a proud man kneeling on marble, armor clattering, voice cracking on the first word.

"I signed it," Davor said. "I was the oath-keeper who witnessed the sale. I was young. I believed the law was the law, that a seal makes a thing righteous. I have carried it forty years and I will carry it into whatever you make of me — take me instead. Take my service. Take my years. Let the girl's blood go free and bind me in her place."

The pen-scratch of ten thousand writers stopped.

In the silence, the courtier tilted her head with genuine, gentle curiosity — and that gentleness was worse than any cruelty Yara could have imagined.

"Oath-keeper," she said kindly, "we cannot take you. A willing sacrifice has no value under divine law. Debt requires loss. You offer us only gain." She spread her hands. "You would *enrich* us. It would be obscene."

Davor stayed on his knees a long moment. When he rose, he did not look at either of them.

"The desk," Yara said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Show me her desk."

The courtier's smile deepened by a degree. "Of course. We keep the family accounts together."

* * *

They led Yara down an aisle that grew narrower and older, the golden script in the walls thickening until the marble seemed more ink than stone. At the end stood a desk of dark oak, ordinary, human, mended in two places with carpentry that Yara recognized down to the angle of the nails — their grandmother's mending, the same careful joinery that held together the bookcases in the Hartwell sitting room, the same hands.

Behind the desk sat a woman.

She was old and she was young, both at once, the way a portrait can be both: her face carried seventy years of living, but her eyes — her eyes were nineteen, and aware, and fixed on Yara with an expression that shattered something in Yara's chest and left the pieces humming.

"Hello, little mapmaker," her grandmother said.

The sound Yara made was not a word. They gripped the edge of the desk because the room had begun to tilt.

"You have your grandfather's stubborn chin," the old woman went on, warm, unhurried, as if they were in a kitchen and not a cathedral of debt. "Don't let them see you cry. They count tears here. Everything is an entry."

"How long—" Yara's voice failed. They tried again. "How long have you been aware?"

"Oh, always. That was the trick of it, dear. The contract didn't sell my soul *away*. It sold the *use* of it. I'm still mine. I've just been... employed." She gestured at the neat stacks of paper before her, the ledgers, the endless columns of figures. "Four hundred years of bookkeeping. I'm very good at it now. I did the accounts for the famine of 1493. I balanced the war of the Broken Crown." Her mouth twisted. "I keep the ledgers for the prayers your guild sells them. Did you know they do that? Your masters. Every solstice, a new shipment."

"I know," Yara whispered. "That's why we came."

Her grandmother studied her then, really studied her, and Yara saw the nineteen-year-old and the seventy-year-old reach the same conclusion in the same instant — saw her understand, from the set of Yara's jaw, exactly how far they intended to go.

"There's a clause," the old woman said softly. "In the oldest law. Before divine writ, before seals, there was a custom — debts of blood could be commuted. Paid in name. But no one has invoked it in an age, because no one wanted to pay it. To surrender your name is to be unwritten, little mapmaker. No ledger will hold you. No record will remember you. Not your guild. Not your maps. Not me." Her voice caught — the first crack in four hundred years of composure. "You would unmake yourself, and I would be free, and neither of us would ever know the other had lived."

"I know what it costs," Yara said.

"You cannot possibly—"

"Mamai." Yara reached across the desk and took her grandmother's hand. The ink-stained fingers closed around theirs with desperate, human strength. "You taught me that a map is a promise that a place exists. You don't get to tell me I don't exist. I'll decide where I'm drawn."

Behind them, the courtier had gone very still, and very quiet, and very attentive — the stillness of a creditor who has just heard a debtor say something unexpectedly solvent.

"That clause," the courtier said, "requires invocation before the fire dies. The fire has perhaps an hour."

"Then we should hurry," Yara said, and did not let go of their grandmother's hand.

Above them, faint through miles of stone, they heard — or imagined they heard — the great doors of the court beginning, very slowly, to open.

They had not locked behind anyone.

Something else was coming down.
