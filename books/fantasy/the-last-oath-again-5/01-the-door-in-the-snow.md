## 1. The Door in the Snow

There are exactly forty-one things a person needs to survive a winter alone above the snowline, and I had thirty-nine of them.

I know because I counted. I count everything now—this is what twenty years of deliberate invisibility does to a person. You become an accountant of your own small life. Sixteen jars of preserves, seven of them plum, which is four too many plums and a decision I'd regret by Thawmonth. A sack of barley that would last until the ice broke if I ate thin. Salt enough. Tallow enough. A roof that leaked in precisely one corner, over the spot where I did *not* sleep, because the spot where I slept was chosen years ago with exactly this kind of arithmetic in mind.

The fortieth thing was company, and I had decided against it. The forty-first was hope, and I'd never been able to afford it at any price.

Outside, the blizzard had settled into its long argument with my house—the kind of storm the Thornfell mountains throw when they want to remind you who holds the mortgage. Wind found every seam in the stone and worried at it like a dog with an old bone. Snow came sideways, then upward, which is a trick of the high passes that still offends me after two decades of living here. The fire snapped and spat in the hearth, and I sat before it with a whetstone and a knife I used for nothing but bread, because sharpening is meditation for people too suspicious to pray.

The blade had no name. That mattered, once. In another life, every blade I touched had a name, or got one, or gave one back to me. But that was before. Before the purge. Before the order died screaming in a single autumn night while I—

Well. While I lived, which is its own confession, isn't it? Survival has a taste. It tastes like plum preserves in February and nobody to tell you you've made too many.

* * *

Let me tell you what I was, since no one else will and the records were burned.

I was Emrys Winterbourne, swordspeaker of the Order of the Bound Oath, fourth voice of the eastern chapter, and if you don't know what that means, good. Ignorance kept more of us alive than courage ever did. We spoke truth into steel. Not magic as your village hedge-witch understands it—no fireballs, no frog princes—but something older and quieter. A sword spoken over truly cannot lie in the hand that holds it. It cannot be swung at the innocent without singing its refusal. Kings wore oath-blades the way priests wear their gods: close to the heart, where the binding shows.

We bound crowns to their wearers. That was the phrase we used, all solemn and self-important, and it took a massacre to teach me the other reading of it. Crowns bind. Wearers get worn.

The night the order fell, I was twenty-six and full of certainty, and I survived anyway. Both facts have taken turns humiliating me ever since.

So I came north into the Thornfell, where the mountains keep secrets the way the sea keeps drowned men—thoroughly and without sentiment—and I built this house from fieldstone and spite, and I became no one. No one is a fine career, if you have the temperament. I have the temperament. I don't hunt where hunters look. I trade wool and dried herbs at the Lowmarket twice a year, always in different cloaks, always answering to a different flat name—Maren, once, and Bess, and the year I felt reckless, Widow Sorrel. I speak maybe three hundred words aloud in an average week, and I ration them like hardtack.

Twenty years. That's how long the peace lasted. I'm telling you the number so you understand the insult of what came next.

* * *

That night, with the storm gnawing the walls, I finished the bread-knife, oiled it, wrapped myself in two blankets, and permitted myself the luxury of believing tomorrow would be identical to today. This belief is the whole architecture of a hidden life. Every morning you build the wall again, brick by brick of routine, and every night you inspect it, and so long as nothing knocks—

A knock.

Three strikes. Fists, not wind—I knew the difference the way you know a scream from a creaking shutter. Weak fists. The kind of knock a body makes when the will behind it is running on fumes and stubbornness.

I went absolutely still, the way prey goes still, which should tell you something about how far I'd fallen from the woman who once faced down a war-duke with a word and a drawn blade.

Nobody knows this house. That's not poetry; that's inventory. The trail dies half a mile below at the scree slide. I haven't lit a visible fire at night in six years. The nearest settlement is a day's walk through country that kills the unprepared with a shrug. Whatever stood on my doorstep in the teeth of a blizzard was either lost beyond saving, sent deliberately, or not alive in the way we use the word.

I took down the bread-knife, which is a stupid weapon and I knew it, and then I took down the other one instead—the one under the floorboard, the one with no name that I never touch—and stopped with my hand on the wood, breathing like a woman about to dive into cold water.

Another knock. Then a sound I hadn't let myself hear in twenty years: a child crying. Not loudly. Quietly, the way children cry when they've learned that loudness costs.

I opened the door.

The blizzard came in first, as blizzards do—a white fist of wind and ice that shoved me back a step and put out two candles. And then the storm stepped aside, the way storms sometimes do, as if even weather wanted to see this clearly, and there on my threshold was a child. Maybe nine years old. Maybe younger; starvation edits age downward. Mud-caked, which meant she'd walked up from the valley roads through the thaw-ice and the slush before the storm caught her, which meant she'd walked for days. Frost in her hair like someone had tried to crown her with winter itself. Lips split and blue-grey. Eyes—

Her eyes were open and fixed on mine with a ferocity no dying child should be able to afford, and her mouth was moving. She wasn't crying anymore. She was speaking.

Not whimpering. Speaking. Syllables came out of her cracked lips in a cadence I felt in my teeth before I heard it with my ears, a rhythm like a hammer on an anvil, like a heartbeat slowed to the tempo of mountains. And I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left: I understood none of it, and I understood all of it. The sounds were foreign. The *shape* of them was not. It was the shape of language the way my hands remembered the shape of a sword—something carried in the body from a life I'd buried.

She swayed. I caught her. She weighed nothing, this child, less than my pack on a trading trip, less than the guilt I'd carried up the mountain two decades ago. Her skin burned with fever under the frost, and her mud-stiffened hand came up—slowly, with terrible ceremony, like a queen granting a boon—and pressed something into my palm. Fingers closing mine over it. Then she looked at me and said one more word, clear as a bell struck in an empty chapel, and my whole hidden life ended in the space of that syllable.

Because I recognized it.

Not the word—I didn't know the word. I recognized the *tongue*. The First Tongue. The speech before kingdoms, the language my order whispered into steel and into the ears of kings, the tongue that was supposed to have died with the last of us on the night everything burned. I hadn't heard it spoken aloud since Master Corvane closed his final lesson with it, twenty-two years ago, and told us: *This is not ours. We only keep it. Remember that, whatever comes.*

Whatever came. Something had come. It had walked through a blizzard and up a mountain that eats the unwary, and it had knocked three times on the door of the one woman in the world with reason to open it slowly, and it was nine years old and burning with fever and mute in every language I knew except the oldest one there is.

I got the door shut. I got the fire built higher. I got the child stripped of her frozen rags and bundled into my spare blanket—the good one, the one I'd been saving against a winter worse than this one—and fed her melted snow sweetened with the last of my honey, drop by drop, while she shivered and muttered in a dead language against my shoulder.

And the whole time, my hands steady, my face calm, the accountant in my head ran the figures and arrived at the answer I already knew:

Thirty-nine things a person needs to survive a winter alone. I had thirty-nine. What I did not have, what no amount of counting could stock or store, was any defense whatsoever against a child speaking the First Tongue on my doorstep in the ninth hour of a blizzard.

The peace was over. I felt it go the way you feel a blade leave your body—not the wound, at first, just the strange cold of the space where something used to be.

The child's fever climbed through the night. She dreamed, and her dreaming mouth kept talking, and near dawn she said a word that I did know—one word, the very first word of the very first lesson, the word Master Corvane made us speak before we were allowed to hold anything sharper than a butter knife.

It meant *unbind*.
