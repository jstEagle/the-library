## 2. Words Before Kingdoms

There are sounds a person learns to stop hearing. The creak of a house settling in cold. The wind worrying at the shutters like an old debt collector who won't accept that you've moved. The rasp of your own breath when the air is thick enough to chew.

The child's voice was not one of those sounds.

I had her inside and the door barred within the space of ten heartbeats—old habits, the kind you can't unlearn any more than you can unlearn walking—and I'd gotten her out of the snow-crusted coat and wrapped in my second-best blanket, which is to say the one with fewer holes, before the meaning of what she'd said finished arriving.

It arrived the way winter arrives in Thornfell. Not all at once, but inevitably, and long after you'd convinced yourself it wouldn't come this year.

She was still murmuring it. Lying on my bed pallet with her eyes half-open and her lips moving, small syllables falling out of her like water from a cracked jug. *Veth-karai. Veth-karai ilen.* Over and over, as if repetition could keep something at bay. As if she were holding a door closed from the inside with nothing but her voice.

I stood at the foot of the pallet with a cup of willow-bark tea going cold in my hand and felt twenty years fall off my life—not pleasantly, not like a burden set down, but like skin.

Because I knew those syllables.

Not the way you know a language, which is a living thing you carry around and use for ordering ale and insulting neighbors. I knew them the way you know a scar. The way your hand knows the shape of a hilt. The way a certain smell—the struck-flint smell of a forge where oaths are spoken—can drop you into the middle of a burning courtyard two decades gone and leave you there, gasping, with tea cooling in your hand.

The First Tongue.

Words older than any kingdom on the map. Words that were old when the kingdoms were young, that were old when there was only one kingdom, that were old, if the archive-masters were to be believed—and they were tedious enough about it that they had to be right—when there was no such thing as a crown, because no one had yet thought to bind authority to metal and call it holy.

That was us. That had been us, once. The Order of the Spoken Blade. We didn't make swords; anyone with a hammer and bad judgment could do that. We made promises. We took steel—good steel, honest steel—and we spoke into it until the steel learned to hold what it was told. Oaths of fealty. Oaths of justice. Oaths of protection, sworn by kings and kept by blades, because men lie but well-spoken steel doesn't.

And above all the rest, the work no one else could do, the work that made crowns mean anything at all: the Coronation Oath. The binding of a monarch to their people, spoken in the First Tongue over the regalia, so that whatever wore the crown would be held to it. Held. Bound. Answerable.

We were the hinges of the realm. And hinges are only noticed when they squeak.

They noticed us, near the end. That's how it goes.

* * *

The child kept murmuring.

I put the tea down because my hands had developed opinions of their own, namely that they wanted to shake, and I don't indulge them. I lit the lamp instead—an excuse to move—and watched the light find her properly for the first time.

Ten years old, perhaps. Eleven. Thin in the way that isn't poverty exactly but flight: the leanness of someone who has been running on less than she needs. Mud to the eyebrows. A cut above her ear, scabbed over, the kind you get from a low branch or a careless hand. Her boots were city boots, good leather, worn through at the sole. Someone had loved this child recently enough to buy her decent boots and recently enough ago that the love hadn't saved her.

And her mouth kept moving. *Veth-karai ilen.*

Except now, listening with the whole of my unwilling attention, I heard what I should have heard at the door. She wasn't repeating herself. The words were shifting, cycling through phrases, and some of them—

Some of them I knew.

*Ilen veth.* Be found. Be findable. It's what we said to lost children, back at the hold, before everything. A little blessing, three notes of it, the verbal equivalent of smoothing down someone's hair.

*Cor-vane sath.* No. That one wasn't a phrase. That was—

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the stone beneath them, through the mountain itself.

*Corvane.* My oathmaster's name, folded into the grammar of the First Tongue the way names get folded into blessings. *Cor-vane sath:* Corvane keeps. Corvane watches over. Someone had taught this child a protection-phrase built out of my dead master's name.

Which meant someone who knew him. Someone who knew me, or knew of me, or knew the order well enough to weave our dead into a child's bedtime blessing. Which meant this wasn't accident. Children don't wander blizzards by mistake. Children don't cross half a mountain range and collapse on the one door in Thornfell behind which lives the last swordspeaker of the purged Order of the Spoken Blade by coincidence.

Coincidence doesn't have that good an aim.

I sat down on the floor beside the pallet, because standing suddenly seemed ambitious, and I listened to a mud-caked child speak my order's tongue in my hiding place while outside the storm buried every track she'd left and, gods willing, every trace of her coming.

Twenty years. Twenty years I had done everything right. I had been so careful that carefulness stopped being a virtue and became a personality. I spoke Mountain Dialect in the valley town like I'd been born to slate roofs and goat cheese. I let my accent roughen. I let my hands callus over the sword-calluses until you couldn't read them anymore. I never sang the cadences, never dreamed aloud, never once—never *once*—spoke so much as a syllable of the First Tongue where wind or wall or well might carry it.

Because the First Tongue carries. That's its nature. It's not speech the way we do speech, breath shaped into noise and forgotten the moment after. It's speech the way a brand is warmth. It changes what it touches. An oath spoken in the First Tongue doesn't fade; it *settles*, into blade or crown or bone, and it stays settled until something stronger unmakes it.

And somewhere south of here, in a palace full of gilded everything, sat a crown that had been bound by my order four hundred years running—and a king whose binding had been renewed at his coronation eleven years ago.

Eleven years ago. By whom? Every speaker who could have performed the rite was ash or exile. I'd assumed—I'd *comforted* myself with the assumption—that the new king simply went unbound. Crowned by priests, blessed by bishops, sworn on books instead of blades. Hollow ceremony. A king held to his people by nothing but his own conscience, which historically is a rope of wet string.

But the child on my pallet was speaking the First Tongue like it was her mother's milk. And the First Tongue doesn't survive in the wild. It has to be *kept*. Kept by speakers. Kept by an order.

An order that was supposed to be dead.

* * *

I did what any sensible woman does when the past knocks on her door half-frozen and bleeding: I made a list.

Not on paper. Paper burns, paper gets found, paper gets read aloud in interrogation rooms by men with grey cloaks and patient voices. In my head, then, the way I'd catalogued stores and escape routes and the exact number of heartbeats between my front door and the treeline for two decades.

Item: the child speaks the First Tongue fluently, in her sleep, under duress, possibly in her dreams. Item: the phrases include protections woven around a name I buried with its owner. Item: she came north. Everyone runs south from trouble; trouble comes north looking. So either she was sent north, or she was fleeing something that moves northward, and neither option sleeps well at night. Item: her boots were bought with care. Someone equipped her for this journey. Someone chose her destination.

Someone chose *my* destination. Mine. This specific pile of stones at the edge of the maps.

Fear is a funny thing after enough years of practice. It stops being the hot animal thing and becomes almost administrative. A clerk in your chest, opening ledgers. *Very good, madam. Category: catastrophic. Let's review the relevant entries.* I reviewed them. The purge, twenty-two years back—no, twenty-three; time lies to exiles to keep them docile. Three holds burned in a single season. Forty-one speakers that I know of, and I knew most of their names, which is its own punishment. The ones who ran. The ones who hid. The one who hid best, which was me, and look how that turned out: found anyway. Found by a child, which is somehow worse than being found by soldiers, because soldiers only want your death and children want things you can't give.

Because here was the ledger's final entry, the one written in the clerk's smallest, most disapproving hand: *if the child found you, others can too.* If word of the First Tongue surviving had reached whoever sent her, then the silence I'd bought with twenty years was already spent. Already worthless. The coin was gone whether I spent it or not, and the only question left was what I got in exchange.

Duty, said a voice that sounded like Corvane and therefore could not be trusted.

Shut up, I told the voice. You're dead. Dead men don't get opinions about my ledger.

Dead men don't get children on their thresholds either, said the voice, insufferably calm, the way he'd been calm during everything, right up to and including, presumably, the moment it killed him.

I got up and checked the tea. Cold. I heated it again because the child needed fluids and because heating tea is a thing you can do with your hands while your mind screams in a dead man's cadence.

* * *

She woke at dusk.

Not gradually, the way the exhausted usually surface—she came up out of sleep like a diver, fast and fighting, and I'll grant her this: she didn't scream. Her eyes found me across the room, catalogued me—height, reach, the sword-shaped lump my blanket didn't quite hide by the hearth—and she went still in the manner of prey that has decided stillness is its remaining talent.

Smart child. Grim thought, that. Smart the way soldiers' kids and fugitives' kids are smart, having learned early that the world contains rooms where adults look at you and decide things.

"Easy," I said, in Mountain Dialect, slow and rough. "You're safe. You're at my house. You collapsed in the snow."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she opened her mouth and answered—in Mountain Dialect, accented, careful, a phrase she'd clearly been taught the way you're taught a rope bridge: tested first, crossed quickly. "Please. Are you the one called... Wren?"

Nobody had called me Wren in twenty years. Up here I was the Widow Marren, dour, unfriendly, buys salt twice a year, leaves everyone alone, let her be. Hearing my own name—my real one, my order-name, given to me at my speaking when I was barely older than her—came through my chest like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"Who taught you that name?" I asked. Quietly. The way you ask questions when the answer matters more than the asking.

Her chin trembled. She mastered it, which cost her visibly, and I recognized the mastery because I'd invented that particular exercise. Then she said, in the First Tongue, in that language I had sworn never to speak again under any sky:

*Ilen veth, Emrys Winterbourne. Sath korai ilen.*

Be found, Emrys Winterbourne. Be kept safe and findable.

A speaker's greeting. The oldest one. The one we used when a sister came home from a long road, the one Corvane used to say to me with that half-smile he thought I didn't notice, the one that means *you were lost and now the order has you back* except there was no order, there was ash and silence and a woman counting dried beans in a mountain hut pretending to be a widow, and a child had just spoken homecoming words over me like a benediction, and I discovered that twenty years of carefulness is no armor whatsoever against three syllables said gently by someone half your size.

"I can't be found," I said. My voice came out wrong. Rougher than intended. "Do you understand? There is no Emrys Winterbourne. Whoever sent you—"

"No one sent me." Also in the First Tongue. Her voice cracked on the grammar and repaired itself mid-word, which is a sign of true fluency—you break and mend without thinking, the way a native walker crosses broken ground. "I ran. They burned the—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "The place where I lived. Where the words were kept. They burned it, and a woman put words in my hand and told me to run north and find the last one, and she said—" The trembling won, briefly. She pressed her lips together and beat it back down. "She said you'd be alive. Because you were always the best at hiding."

The best at hiding. What a legacy. What an epitaph. *Here lies Emrys Winterbourne, last of the Swordspeakers; she survived; nobody clapped.*

"The archive," I said. "In Vael Corith. On the lake."

She nodded once.

The archive. The mother-hold of the order, the deep library where the First Tongue was kept in its oldest forms, where the coronation rites slept between reigns. Burned. Which meant the Regency—which meant whoever ruled through whatever passed for a king these days—had finally gotten around to the root of us. Not the speakers. The *words*.

Kill the speakers and the order dies slowly. Burn the archive and it dies forever. Someone had understood that arithmetic better than my order ever had, and had done their sums, and the remainder was lying in my blankets eating the bread I'd soaked in warm milk for her.

"They're hunting speakers," she said. Simply. The way children state horrors, because no one has yet taught them to decorate. "Grey cloaks. They found the ferryman's daughter's friend's cousin asking questions, that's how the woman knew to send me. They have a list. Your name is on it." A pause, and then, with the devastating precision of childhood: "Both of them."

Both of them. The widow and the swordswoman. Somewhere a grey cloak had a paper with both my lives on it, and I was sitting here watching the child finish my bread.

I should have turned her out. Any sensible analysis ends there: I had no obligation to her, no oath left to honor, no order to disgrace by refusing it. Twenty years of survival argued for the door, for the snow, for *I'm sorry, little one, the person you want is dead, try the next valley.*

Instead I heard myself say, in the First Tongue, for the first time in twenty years, feeling the syllables settle into the room like old dogs returning to a remembered hearth:

*Sath korai. Ilen veth.* Safe and kept. Found and findable.

Her whole face changed. It's an obscene power we had, we speakers—to say a thing and watch belief arrive in someone like dawn. She believed me instantly, completely, the way you believe steel, and I felt the old awful pride rise in me at having caused that, and underneath the pride, colder and older and far less welcome, something else stirred.

Recognition. Of the shape of things. Because I'd spent twenty years telling myself the order died with its members, but the order was never the members. The order was the *tongue*, and the tongue was a door, and doors don't die, they only wait. And somewhere south, something had walked through that door wearing a crown, and had spent eleven years doing sums in the dark, and had just now sent its arithmetic north in the body of a child—no. No, the child fled it. The child was the counter-arithmetic. Someone had thrown her north like a stone into a still pond, and the ripples were coming for all of us, and I could already feel them reaching the shore of this mountain.

Outside, the wind shifted. Turned, the way wind does before weather turns, carrying up from the south a smell that didn't belong to snow.

Smoke, maybe. Or memory. With me, those had always smelled alike.

The child watched me with her clear, unhiding eyes, waiting for the grown-up to decide the world. And the dead man's voice in my chest, calm as a level blade, said:

Well, Emrys. Now you know why you were never allowed to forget the words.

I banked the fire, and I began, very quietly, to plan.
