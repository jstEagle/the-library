## 3. What the Child Carries

*Fire first. Fire is always first.*

I have tried to put the fire in the middle, or at the end, where it belongs, but memory does not take instruction. Memory is like a cat I once fed in the archive courtyard—it comes when it wants, sits where it wants, and looks at you with an expression that says *you think you own me?*

So: fire.

* * *

The archive was called the House of Quiet Words, which sounds gentle until you learn that a quiet word can stop a heart. It stood—stood, past tense, because grammar is also a kind of ghost—in a valley south of everything, older than the roads around it, with a roof of grey slate and shelves of black oak that went up higher than any ladder could reach. The archivists used ladders anyway. They were stubborn people. Stubbornness was practically a religious requirement.

I was not an archivist. I was the thing they kept in the back room.

Not cruelly. I want to say that clearly, before the fire makes everything cruel. They gave me a bed with a quilt stitched with little birds, and honey on my bread, and Sister Maren taught me letters by drawing them in spilled flour. But there was a door at the end of the corridor with three locks, and everyone knew which room it opened onto, and when visitors came—the rare, nervous visitors who came asking about old oaths and older kings—I was moved somewhere else, like a stain covered with a tablecloth.

They told me I was precious.

Precious is a word that means *we cannot let you go*.

* * *

Here is what I remember about the night of the fire, in pieces, because that is how it comes to me—not as a story but as shards, and if you step on one barefoot you bleed for days:

Shard one: the smell. Not smoke first, but hot iron. The smell of a forge inside a library, which is wrong the way a fish singing is wrong.

Shard two: Brother Callum running down the corridor with his robes half-buttoned, shouting words I had never heard him use, and one of those words was in the First Tongue—a shield-word, a *hold*-word—and the walls themselves seemed to lean inward, listening.

Shard three: grey cloaks. So many grey cloaks. You would think grey would be easy to lose in a crowd, but these greys moved together, all in step, all silent, and silence in a crowd of forty people is louder than screaming. Their faces were ordinary. That is the part I still cannot make peace with. The men who burned the House of Quiet Words had faces like bakers. Like uncles.

Shard four: a woman's hand closing over mine. Small, strong, ink-stained at the knuckles. This was Sister Maren, who drew letters in flour, who had never once raised her voice, and she was raising it now, and what she said was not *run* and not *hide* but—

She pressed something into my palm.

* * *

I should explain what a pressed word is, except I only half understand it myself, and half-understanding is the whole condition of my life.

The First Tongue is not like other languages. Other languages describe things. The First Tongue *does* things—or rather, it did, once, through mouths trained for lifetimes, through blades forged to hold what was spoken into them. A spoken word fades like breath on glass. A pressed word stays. You take the shape of the word—you take its weight, its edges, its terrible patience—and you fold it into someone, the way you'd fold a note small enough to hide in a fist.

Sister Maren folded a word into my palm and closed my fingers over it, and her hands were shaking, and she said, "Don't speak it. Whatever happens, don't speak it. Swallowing it will hurt less."

Then she smiled at me. I keep arriving at that smile no matter which direction I approach the memory from. She was standing in a corridor filling with smoke and she smiled at me the way you smile at a child on the morning of a festival, and she turned around and walked back toward the fire, toward the grey cloaks, toward whatever they do to people who can press words, and I ran, because I am a coward, and because she spent her last courage buying exactly that: my cowardice, my survival, my small fast feet on the frost-hardened ground.

Cowardice is just love with nowhere to put itself. Someone wise probably said that. Or maybe I made it up, lying awake in barns, which is most of where wisdom gets made anyway.

* * *

North. Everything after the fire is north.

I remember it as weather more than as time. There are no dates in my head between the burning and this mountain, only weathers. A wet cold. A dry bitter cold. A cold so total it felt like being erased a little each morning, like the world was rubbing me out the way you rub out a chalk mistake.

I remember:

A hay loft, and breathing through my sleeve because below me men with lanterns were talking about a girl, about a reward, about how the Regent wanted speakers brought *alive*, and how alive was a flexible word.

A river crossing where I lost one shoe and did not cry about the shoe, which tells you something about how the rest was going.

An orchard where an old woman saw me stealing apples and instead of shouting, filled my shirt hem with them and said, "You've got a look, girl. A going-somewhere look. Don't stop anywhere with grey in it." Then she went back to her trees. Kindness, I learned, is often quick, because kindness has learned what slow attention costs.

And I remember talking. That is the strange part, the part I grieve hardest now. On that road north I still talked. Not much—I had never been much of a talker—but words came out of me when I needed them. *Please. Bread? Which way to the pass?* Small coin, but mine.

Somewhere on the road, the coin stopped coming.

It wasn't dramatic. There was no moment where my voice snapped like a string. One day I reached for a word and found the shelf empty, and the next day emptier, and by the time the mountains rose up white and enormous in front of me, I had nothing left but the First Tongue itself—and that, I could not spend. Every syllable of it burned behind my teeth like swallowed embers. Sister Maren's word sat in my palm-shaped memory, folded tight, patient, waiting.

Muteness is not silence. Silence is a choice you get to make again every morning. Muteness is a locked room where your voice lives, and you press your ear to the door some nights and hear it pacing.

* * *

Which brings us, unwillingly, to the door in the snow.

I want it noted that I did not plan anything. Plans require a self that believes in next week, and I had stopped keeping one. What I had was a body that walked, a hunger that complained, and a word in my palm that grew heavier the farther north I went—heavier the way a compass needle grows insistent near true north, tugging, tugging.

There was a light in the snow. One yellow window in all that white and dark, and smoke rising from a chimney with the particular steady confidence of a fire someone intends to keep.

I remember thinking, with great clarity: *if there is a fire, there is a person, and if there is a person, there is either rescue or death, and both of those mean stopping walking.*

That is the whole philosophy of the desperate. Rescue or death, and either way, sit down.

I knocked. I use the word loosely—my fists were so frozen they made more of a slapping sound, pathetic as a bird against glass. And then the door opened and there was light and heat and a woman with grey in her hair and a scarred forearm and eyes that went through me like a blade goes through wet paper, and I thought, distantly, *oh, she's one of us,* though "us" was a country that had been burned off the map.

I meant to say something. *Help. Please. My name is Veyra.* Any of it.

What came out instead, from somewhere deeper than my locked-up voice, from the place where the First Tongue lives coiled like a sleeping animal, were three syllables I did not choose and did not understand.

The woman's face changed. All the hardness fell away from it at once, the way ice falls off a branch in spring, and what was underneath looked like grief, and under the grief, unbelievably, looked like hope.

She caught me as I fell.

* * *

Now I am here. Wherever here precisely is—a hold, a house, a stone den built into the mountain's shoulder like a swallow's nest. There is a fire that does not go out. There is broth, salted too heavily, which I have decided is the finest food ever cooked by human hands. There are blankets with the smell of cedar and cold stone and, faintly, of steel kept clean.

And there is the woman. Emrys. She says her name like she's setting it down carefully, checking whether I'll flinch at it. She talks to me even though I can't answer—tells me which cupboard sticks, warns me about the third stair, explains the difference between the good axe and the axe that lies. At first I thought she was mad, talking to a mute child as if muteness were deafness. Now I understand: she isn't talking *at* me. She's talking *near* me, the way you leave a lamp lit in a window for someone whose arrival you're not sure of. It's hospitality performed in advance.

She recognized my syllables. I watched her recognize them—that flicker of a woman seeing her own death walk through her door wearing a child's boots. Anyone sensible would have sent me back into the storm. Instead she wrapped me in a blanket and fed me and counted her stores while looking at me like I was a debt come due after twenty years.

Because that's what I carry besides the word. I know that now. I'm not a girl; I'm a fuse. Somewhere south of here a king wears a crown that listens, and grey cloaks burn houses with baker's faces, and every mile I've run north I've been carrying the lit end of myself toward the powder.

Sometimes I wake in the night sure the fire has found me again—sure the crackle in the hearth is the archive, sure the smell of hot iron is coming up the stairs—and then Emrys's floor creaks in the other room, deliberate, loud, the creak of someone saying *I'm here* without saying it, and the fire becomes just a fire, and the iron becomes just tools, and I sleep.

Almost.

Because there is one more thing I haven't told her. One more shard I keep in my boot, so to speak, sharp side in.

When I sleep, truly deep asleep, past the fear and past the fever, I don't dream of the burning archive anymore. Lately I dream forward instead of back. I see a city I've never visited, all white towers and banners, and I see it the way Sister Maren must have seen things, the way the First Tongue sees things—from underneath, from the roots. In the dream the city is burning too. Not yet. But the fire is already in it, sleeping in the throne, patient as a pressed word, and it is dreaming of me the same way I dream of it.

Two nights ago I woke from that dream with ash on my lips—actual ash, grey grit on my tongue, though the hearth was banked and clean—and Emrys was sitting beside my pallet in the dark, watching me with an expression like a woman doing arithmetic she hates.

"Veyra," she said. My name, in her mouth, sounded like an oath being sworn. "What did you see?"

And I opened my mouth to lie, or to stay silent, or to do any of the safe things—

—and the ember behind my teeth stirred, and leaned toward her, and *wanted* to speak.
