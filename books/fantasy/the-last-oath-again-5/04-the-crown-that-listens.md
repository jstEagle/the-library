## 4. The Crown That Listens

There is a particular horror in discovering that you have excellent handwriting.

I learned this on a Tuesday. Or I believe it was a Tuesday. Time has become an unreliable narrator lately — I'll come to that, or rather, I won't, because coming to things requires that one be present for them, and presence, for me, has become a part-time occupation.

The morning began the way all my mornings begin: with someone else opening my eyes.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. It is not that I sleep and dream and wake. It is not sleep at all. It is more like being a guest at your own dinner party who has been asked, politely and permanently, to wait in the coat closet. You hear the laughter through the door. You smell the roast. Occasionally the host opens the closet, wears your face into the room, says something charming to your wife, compliments your chancellor's treasonous new tax policy, and then hangs you back up among the cloaks until next time.

I had been in the closet for — how long? Weeks. Months. There was a period when the intervals between my moments were short enough that I could track the seasons by the light in my bedchamber window. Then the intervals stretched. Now I surface like a drowning man breaking water: gasping, disoriented, given just enough air to understand that I am still drowning.

Ninety seconds. That's what I got on the Tuesday-that-was-probably-a-Tuesday. Ninety seconds of Aldric, King of Caelmere, Lord of the Thornmarch, Defender of a Faith I have never been entirely sure I believe in. Ninety seconds in which I stood at my own writing desk with a quill in my hand and wet ink on a page I did not remember beginning.

Let me tell you what ninety seconds feels like when you've been gone for months. It feels like waking from surgery. It feels like the moment after a fall when you check yourself for broken pieces. It feels, most of all, like guilt — because the first thing you do is look around at what *you* have been doing without you.

The desk was covered in state papers. Of course it was. My hand — my actual hand, ink-stained at the second knuckle the way it has been since I was nine years old and Father caned me for sloppy penmanship — rested beside a document half-finished in my own looping script. The royal seal sat warm in its dish, as though recently pressed. As though *I* had pressed it.

I picked up the page.

*By order of His Majesty,* it read, in my handwriting, with my cadence, in phrasing so perfectly mine that I might have written it in a kinder life: *the speaker known as Ferro, ferryman of the Thornfell crossings, is remanded to the Grey Cloaks for questioning regarding the child. Questioning may proceed by any means the Cloaks deem efficacious. The Crown expresses no preference.*

No preference. That was the detail that broke something in me. Not the sentence of a man I'd never met to torture at the discretion of monsters — kingdoms do worse weekly, and I had signed my share of ugly orders back when I was still allowed to hold the quill. No. It was the elegance of it. The *voice* of it. Whoever wrote those words knew me. Knew my irony, my habit of deflecting cruelty into dryness, my lifelong coward's trick of hiding behind wit so that the blade arrives wrapped in a joke.

Something wearing me had studied me the way a tailor studies a man before fitting him for his funeral suit.

I counted my breaths. That's what I do now, in the surfacing moments. Ninety seconds, roughly two hundred heartbeats if I'm calm, fewer if I'm not, and I am never calm anymore. Two hundred heartbeats to learn what I had done, decide whether I could undo any of it, and hide the evidence before the thing came back to reclaim its gloves.

*The child.* The word snagged in me like a fishhook. What child? I cast backward through the fog of my missing months and found only fragments, images that might have been memories or might have been dreams fed to me through the closet door: a burning building seen from horseback. A grey cloak spattered with something dark. My own voice — my voice! — saying, in a tone of mild administrative regret, *burn the archive, spare nothing that speaks.*

An archive. Speakers. A child fled north, into the Thornfell mountains, where twenty years ago another purge had driven the last of the swordspeakers into the snow, and where surely no survivor of *that* cleansing would shelter a fugitive, because why would history repeat itself in the same frozen hills? Except that history does exactly that. History is a dog returning to its vomit, and I have read enough chronicles to know that mine will be one of the volumes future scholars read aloud to their students in tones of horrified wonder. *How,* they will ask, *did a king fail to notice he had been replaced? How did he sign his own erasure?*

Here is the answer they will find unsatisfying: gradually, and then completely, and then not at all, because the worst of it is that some part of me never left. Some thin, cold sliver of Aldric stayed awake in the closet the whole time, watching through the keyhole while the thing wore me to masques and councils and executions. Do you know what it's like to watch yourself smile at a man you know to be innocent while guards drag him to the block? To feel your own face do things your grief cannot stop? Grief needs a body. I haven't reliably had one.

Twenty-two heartbeats. I looked at the execution order again. Ferro. A ferryman. Somewhere in the mountains, a child was running, and the first domino between her and the Grey Cloaks was a man whose crime was owning a boat.

Could I destroy it? I considered it with the giddy clarity of a man considering leaping from a burning window — knowing it wouldn't save him, doing it anyway because the alternative is standing still. But the thing kept copies. I had learned that much through the keyhole. It kept duplicates in a black lacquered chest beneath its — my — bed, and it compared them each evening with the meticulousness of a merchant counting coin. A missing order would be noticed. A noticed discrepancy would bring the Cloaks sniffing through the palace, and the Cloaks notice everything except the one thing that matters, which is that their master is not a man at all.

And there was the deeper rot, the truth I could barely look at even in the privacy of my own skull: I did not know if destroying the order would save Ferro anyway. The Grey Cloaks had already ridden. The paper wasn't the knife; it was only the receipt.

So I did what kings do best. Nothing. I set down the quill. I aligned the page with the others in the stack — neatly, precisely, my father's training holding even now, even hollowed out, God help me — and I used my ninety seconds for the only rebellion available to a passenger:

I remembered.

I stood at my window and I made myself memorize it. The mountains blue-white under morning frost. The capital below, smoke rising straight from ten thousand chimneys, the bells ringing for some festival I did not recognize — they ring for festivals constantly now; the city has never been so entertained, and I have begun to suspect that is deliberate. Bread and spectacle. A kingdom kept pleasantly drunk while something drinks *it*. And everywhere the grey cloaks moving through the crowds like needles through cloth, stitching shut every mouth that might remember the First Tongue.

Because that's what this is about. I understood it in fragments, in the way you understand a storm by the wreckage it leaves. The thing that wears me is old. Older than the crown, older than the dynasty, older than the language it hunts. It has worn kings before me — I have caught whispers of it in my lucid moments, in the private chapel where I sometimes wake with candle wax on my fingers and no memory of praying. Eleven crowns. Nine centuries. Each realm it wears, it eats — not the people, not at first, but the memory of them. Their true histories. Their languages. The words that bind. Until nothing remains but a smiling king and a population that cannot recall ever having been anything other than obedient.

It is eating my kingdom's name. Slowly. From the inside. And the last ingredients it needs are the speakers who could unmake it, which is why somewhere north of here a child is running, and why a ferryman named Ferro is about to learn what the Crown expresses no preference about.

Fifty heartbeats left. Maybe less. I could feel it coming back — not a sound, not a sensation, exactly, but a kind of pressure, like weather changing, like the moment before a mask settles onto a face. My hands were already going quiet. That's how it takes me: my hands go first. They grow calm. They grow *confident*. And then the calm pours upward like water filling a vessel, and Aldric goes back in the closet, and the king smiles.

So I spent my final heartbeats the only way I could. In the chapel — no, I didn't have time for the chapel; I spent them right there, at the desk, staring at my own treacherous beautiful handwriting, and I made myself a promise with no witness but the frost on the glass:

*I will leave a message. When I next surface, I will write it where only a friend would look, in a code only an oath-speaker would read. I don't know if any oath-speakers live. I don't know if the child survives the snow. But if one of them comes — if anyone comes — they must know what I know. They must know its hunger, its method, its patience. And they must know that the king of Caelmere did not consent.*

*Do not save me,* I thought, with the last of my ninety seconds, *if saving me costs the realm. But if you come anyway — if you are fool enough, brave enough — then look in the chapel. Look where I prayed as a boy. I will leave you the truth there, hidden inside the litany, the way my mother hid sweets inside a prayer book when I was small. Something ancient is wearing me, and it is listening—*

The pressure closed over me like deep water.

My hands grew calm. My breathing smoothed. The smile arrived — I felt it settle onto my face the way frost settles onto a window, beautiful, inevitable, mine and not mine. Somewhere far away, in the coat closet of my own skull, I watched my hand pick up the quill again, dip it, and add a postscript to the order in my own flawless script:

*P.S. — His Majesty conveys his personal regards to the ferryman.*

It signed my name. It pressed my seal. It rang for breakfast.

And I hung among the cloaks, counting heartbeats that were no longer mine, waiting — the way I always wait, the way I will keep waiting as long as one stubborn spark of me refuses to gutter — for Tuesday. For the next ninety seconds. For whatever stranger might someday open the chapel door and read what a prisoner-king scratched into the margins of his prayers.

North, I thought, into the whiteness beyond the window. Run, child. Run faster than my signature.

Somewhere in the mountains, a door was about to knock.
