## 5. Smuggler's Arithmetic

There is a particular arithmetic to hiding, and I had gotten very good at it over twenty years.

You count everything. That was the first lesson the mountains taught me, before they taught me anything gentler. You count your stores: flour enough for six weeks if I stretched it, salted goat meat for eight, dried berries and mushrooms laid in during the autumn foraging that would carry me through a winter that showed no interest in ending. You count your fuel. You count your arrows, though I hadn't loosed one at anything living since the purge—hunting was easier with snares, quieter, and quiet was the whole of my religion.

And you count your scars, though those you count differently. Not in numbers but in weather. The old sword-cut along my ribs ached when snow was coming. The burn on my left palm—the one I'd earned pressing an oath into steel at nineteen, young and certain and stupid with purpose—it ached when something worse than snow was coming.

Lately it had ached every morning.

The child slept in my bed because there was nowhere else to put her, wrapped in my spare blanket with her mud-caked boots set neatly beside the door, because even half-dead she'd had the presence of mind to line them up straight. Veyra. That much I'd gotten out of her, or rather she'd pressed it into me—not spoken, not signed, just *there*, the way the First Tongue worked, a meaning arriving behind your eyes like a guest who'd let himself in.

She hadn't woken properly in two days. Fever, mostly. Exhaustion, certainly. And underneath both, whatever it costs a body to carry words older than kingdoms in a mouth too small for them.

I sat at my table with my ledger open—a real ledger, ink on paper, because counting in your head lets you lie to yourself—and I did what I always did when the world knocked on my door. I ran the sums.

*Option one.* Take the child and go deeper. There were valleys past the Thornfell spine that didn't appear on crown surveys, places where even smugglers wouldn't walk because there was nothing to smuggle and no one to sell to. I knew three of them by heart. I could be gone within a day. The snow would cover our tracks within hours of our passing.

Costs: I had stores for one mouth, barely two. The girl was feverish and couldn't march. Deeper meant colder, and colder meant the fever deciding the argument for us. And—

And I wrote *and* in my head the way you write it in ledgers, knowing full well the entry beneath it is the one you've been avoiding all along.

And the thing she carried wasn't finished with carrying her yet.

*Option two.* Send her down alone. Give her food, give her directions to Ferro's ferry, wash my hands of the whole business, return to a life of six weeks of flour and counted arrows. This option had a beautiful clean shape to it, the kind of shape a lie has when you've polished it long enough.

I looked at her across the room—small, hollow-eyed even in sleep, her hands curled closed like two locked doors—and I thought about what it takes for a child to cross the Thornfells alone in deepwinter. Whatever had sent her north had known exactly what it was doing. It had spent her like coin.

You don't spend a child and then get to pretend you never touched the money.

*Option three.* I didn't write this one down. Option three sat under the floorboards and waited.

* * *

The nightmares started on the third night.

I want to be careful here, because I have spent twenty years telling people—I told *myself*, often and firmly—that swordspeech is dead, that the First Tongue went into hiding the same as I did, that the things we used to do with words are gone from the world like wolves from the lowlands. But there are things I saw in my order's last years that I have never managed to file anywhere sensible, and watching a sleeping fevered child speak a language through her dreams was one of them.

She didn't talk in her sleep. She *showed*. It came off her in waves, the way heat comes off a forge—not language, not exactly, but pressure, image, weight. The first night it was small: a corridor, stone, the smell of smoke. I stood at the table and felt it press against the inside of my skull like a thumb against a bruise, and I told myself it was the wind.

By the second night it wasn't wind. By the second night I was standing in the middle of my own cabin at midnight watching a city burn behind a child's closed eyes.

I knew the city. That was the worst of it. I had walked those avenues at nineteen in my order's grey-and-white, carrying a blade that sang, while crowds cheered the coronation of a young king named Aldric. The spires in her dream burned the same way festival lanterns had glowed—except dreams don't glow, they *consume*, and in her dream the great avenue was choked with running figures and the palace hill above them wore a crown of orange light, and there was a sound underneath the fire, low and rhythmic, that I recognized without wanting to.

It was a voice. Something speaking the First Tongue from inside the palace, slow and satisfied, the way a man speaks after a long meal.

Veyra woke screaming then—not aloud, she has never screamed aloud, that is somehow worse—and I was across the room before I remembered deciding to move. I put my hand on her forehead. She grabbed my wrist with both hands, grip like a drowning sailor, and pushed a single word into me:

*Still burning.*

Then she slept again, truly slept, and I sat on the edge of the bed until dawn doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because here is the sum I kept arriving at, no matter how I rearranged the columns. If the capital burns—if it *will* burn, if some part of the future had already soaked through into a child's dreams the way water soaks through canvas—then deeper valleys buy me nothing. You can hide from riders. You cannot hide from a thing that eats kingdoms. The mountains would simply be the last place I stood when it finished chewing.

And if it wasn't true—if the dreams were only fever, only fear—then I would have traded my peace for a nightmare, which is a bad trade but survivable.

But if it was true, and I ran, and the girl died anyway in some cold valley because I wouldn't spend what I had—

My oathmaster had a saying about ledgers. Corvane said: *every account balances eventually; the only choice you get is what currency you pay in.*

He'd been dead twenty years and he still made me tired.

* * *

Ferro's warning came on the fifth day, riding a bird with frost-bitten feet.

I should explain about Ferro. At the southern foot of the Thornfells there is a river, and at the narrowest point of the river there is a ferry, and for forty years there has been a man named Ferro—who claims he was named for the ferry, or the ferry for him, he tells it both ways depending on how much he's drunk—who moves people and goods across that water and asks no questions with his mouth, though his eyes ask plenty.

Twenty years ago, when a woman with a burn-scarred palm and a stolen name came down out of the mountains looking like something a wolf had chewed, Ferro looked at her exactly once, ferried her across, and never asked a question in his life. Twice a year since, one of his birds finds its way up to my ridge with news dressed as gossip: grain prices, garrison movements, which passes the crown has decided to patrol this season. He calls me *cousin* in his letters. Neither of us believes we're related. Both of us believe in the arrangement.

This bird carried a different letter. Shorter. His handwriting, normally a scandal, was careful.

*Cousin —*

*Riders on the south road. Six of them, grey cloaks, asking at every farm for a girl. Dark-haired, ten years old, mute. They show a drawing. It's good work. They pay in new silver and thank people politely, which frightens everyone more than shouting would.*

*They asked me too. I told them I ferry goods, not children. They smiled. I don't care for their smiles.*

*If you know a girl, cousin, she isn't safe south of the river, and neither is anyone who knows her.*

*F*

I read it three times. Then I fed it to the stove and watched the corners curl, because that's the protocol, and because watching paper burn gives your hands something to do while your stomach makes its arguments.

Six riders. Grey cloaks. *New silver*—minted fresh, which meant funded from the center, which meant this wasn't a local magistrate chasing a runaway. Someone in the capital wanted this child found with the patience of a creditor and the courtesy of a knife.

And they had a drawing. A good one. Which meant somewhere between wherever she'd fled and my door, someone had seen her face closely enough to commission art of it, and artists that good worked for people with resources I preferred not to imagine.

Grey cloaks, though. That word sat wrong in me, heavier than it should have. My order had worn grey and white. Grey cloaks hunting a speaker-child was either a coincidence—which I stopped believing in around the same time I stopped believing in mercy—or it meant the hunters had once been hunted, and had made their accommodations.

I went to the window. Snow to the knees outside, the pass white and silent, the world reduced to the fifty paces I could see. Somewhere south of that silence, six men were being polite at farmwives.

Behind me, the girl stirred.

"You're awake," I said, turning. Her eyes were clearer today. The fever had broken in the night, leaving her wrung out and pale but *present*, tracking me across the room with an attention that made most adults look lazy.

She nodded. Then she raised one hand, palm up, and made a small gesture—*come*—the way my order used to summon novices to lessons. My chest did something complicated about that.

I crossed and crouched by the bed. Up close her eyes were dark and old, old in a way no ten-year-old's should be, and I hated every person responsible for that, including several categories of person who didn't technically exist.

"Riders are coming," I told her, because lying to her seemed both pointless and rude. "Looking for you."

Something moved through her face—fear, yes, but also a grim little *of course*, the expression of someone who had been running long enough that pursuit was simply weather. She pointed south. Then she flattened her hand and drew it slowly level, the way you'd smooth a sheet.

*I know,* that said. *Of course they are.*

"They'll reach the pass in—" I calculated. "Three days. Four if the snow slows them."

Her chin lifted. Then, deliberately, she looked at me. Just looked. And behind her eyes I felt the question arrive without a single syllable attached, the way the First Tongue did its quiet work:

*What will you do?*

I stood up. I walked back to the window. I gave myself one more honest moment of arithmetic, because I owed my twenty years of survival that much.

Deeper valleys: cold, hunger, and a kingdom-eating thing waiting at the end of the world regardless. Down the mountain: riders, grey cloaks, a road I'd sworn off, faces I'd sworn off, a version of myself I had buried with honors and did not particularly wish to see dug back up. Doing nothing: unthinkable now, and I resented that, resented the child for making it unthinkable simply by existing in my bed with my blanket around her shoulders.

Corvane's voice, in memory, dry as old parchment: *You're stalling, Emrys. You've been stalling for twenty years. It's been a very productive stall, but the market's closing.*

"Damn it," I said quietly, to the window, to the mountains, to the ghost I refused to acknowledge was listening.

Veyra, behind me, made a sound—a small huff of breath, almost a laugh. Apparently ghosts weren't the only ones listening.

I turned around. "Here's how it is," I told her. "South of Ferro's river, you're caught. North and west, we're dead slower. So we go down. My roads—the smugglers' paths. I know them blindfolded, and the riders don't, because soldiers follow maps and smugglers follow grudges." I held up a finger. "Rules. You do what I say when I say it. You don't speak—push, gesture, whisper into my head, whatever it is you do—but nothing audible on the trail. And you eat what I put in front of you without commentary."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she held up one finger of her own and pointed at me, then at my ledger on the table, then made a walking motion with two fingers.

*You'll need to count for two now.*

"I hate that you're right," I said, and got the second almost-laugh of the day for my trouble.

We packed through the afternoon. I took less than half my stores—arithmetic again, the brutal kind—and buried the rest against a future I refused to define. I took the bow. I took the oilskin, the flint, the small hoard of crown silver I'd accumulated selling pelts in Lowmarket under a name that wasn't mine. I took down the loose board beside the hearth and looked at what lay beneath it for a long time, a long time, and then I put the board back, because some weights you carry out of the house and some you carry out of the house *last*, and I already knew which kind this was going to be.

At dusk I banked the fire. Twenty years, that fire. I'd lit it the week I arrived, and it had never fully gone out, and standing there smothering it felt like closing a book I'd expected to die inside of.

Veyra stood at the door in boots two sizes too big, laced twice around the calf, watching me with that patient old-soul attention. When I finally turned from the dead hearth, she pointed at me, then pressed her fist to her chest.

*I'm sorry,* maybe. Or *thank you.* With her, it might have been both.

"Don't thank me yet," I said, pulling the door open onto blue evening cold and a world of white. "Thank me when we're south of the river."

We stepped out. The snow took our footprints behind us almost as fast as we made them, which I supposed was the mountains' way of saying they understood the transaction: *you were never here, and now you're leaving anyway.*

Three days ahead, six riders climbed toward my empty cabin, polite and patient, carrying new silver and a good drawing.

And far to the south, under the frozen surface of a river I'd have to cross, an old man named Ferro was about to have a conversation he couldn't win.

* * *

The first day on the trail taught me I'd forgotten nothing and forgiven less.

The smugglers' paths aren't paths, properly—they're absences, gaps in the world where rock falls shadowed the treeline and game trails braided together into routes that exist only in the memory of people who needed them to. I'd learned them at twenty-two, running messages for the order through country where crown law stopped at the treeline, and my feet remembered every switchback even as the rest of me filed objections.

Veyra kept up. I'll say that for her. She stumbled, she fell twice, she went through ice to the shin at a crossing and didn't make a sound about it, and when I stopped to check her at midday she was already scanning our backtrail with an expertise that made me sad in a place I keep the sadness about children.

That night, sheltering in a hollow beneath a leaning granite slab with a fire small enough to cup in your hands, she pressed something into me while I watched the darkness. Not a dream this time. Deliberate. An offering, maybe. Or a test.

An image: the same burning city, but closer now, seen from a rooftop. And in the foreground of it—clearer than anything else in her sending—a figure on a balcony above the flames. A man in a crown, standing perfectly still while his city burned, smiling.

Smiling the way you smile when the meal is good.

I broke the contact by standing up too fast. The girl watched me calmly from the firelight, and I realized she'd shown me that on purpose, that she was telling me: *this is why I ran. This is what's coming. Decide accordingly.*

"I've decided," I said. "Twice now. Stop helping."

The huff of breath that was almost a laugh. Then she curled up against the warm stone and slept, and I sat up keeping watch, doing the only arithmetic left.

Not *whether* anymore. The whether was settled, had been settled the moment I opened the door in the blizzard and something in me that I'd thought twenty years dead had sat up and paid attention.

Now the arithmetic was simpler and worse. It counted days instead of options. Three days to the pass. Four to Ferro's ferry. And somewhere on those roads, six men in grey with a good drawing, and behind them whatever smiled from balconies.

Every account balances eventually. The only choice you get is what currency you pay in.

I checked the bowstring. I watched the snow. And on the second morning, when we came down out of the high trees to the overlook above the river valley—my valley, the one I'd sworn I'd never look at again—and saw the ferry landing below with its rope line and its lean-to and its thin thread of chimney smoke, I also saw, drawn up on the south bank, six horses, and six grey cloaks, and a man in the center of them holding a piece of paper up to the light.

They were talking to Ferro.

I put my arm across Veyra's chest before she could take another step, and we sank down into the snow at the treeline, two more shadows among shadows, and I began, with the calm of a woman whose entire life had been preparation for this exact bad arithmetic, to count the ways this could still go wrong.
