## 6. The First Blade Fragment

The floorboard under the third flagstone—no, that's wrong, there are no flagstones in my house, and if I keep mixing up my metaphors at this hour I deserve whatever the mountain does to me—was loose because I had made it loose.

That's an important distinction. Things in my house were not broken or worn or neglected. They were *engineered*. The shutter that hung crooked was crooked so that anyone prying it would find a nail where a latch should be and waste precious seconds on it. The hearthstone with the hairline crack was cracked along a line I had scored myself, so that if a stranger lifted it he'd find ash and old bones of fires and nothing else, while the real cache sat two feet to the left beneath a board so ordinary that even I forgot about it sometimes. Which, I'll grant you, is a design flaw. A hiding place you forget about yourself is just a very slow way of losing something.

I hadn't forgotten tonight. Tonight I knelt on my kitchen floor at an hour when honest hermits were asleep, with a mute child breathing in fever-dreams behind me on the bed, and I pried up the board with a knife tip the way you'd open a wound you already knew was there.

* * *

Let me tell you what I expected to feel: nothing. That's what twenty years of practice buys you. Not peace exactly—peace implies you wanted the quiet—but a kind of professional numbness, the emotional equivalent of calluses. I had built those calluses plank by plank, year by year, the way you build anything durable in the Thornfells: slowly, out of stubbornness, with bad tools.

Under the board was a wrapped bundle, oilcloth gone stiff as bark, tied with cord I had braided myself from goat-hair and spite. I lifted it out and it weighed what it had always weighed, which is to say almost nothing, which is to say everything, depending on how honest I was being with the scale in my chest.

I didn't unwrap it right away. That's the other thing they don't tell you about grief—the manuals all assume it's a wave, dramatic, salt-spray, wrecks you against rocks. Nobody warns you it can also be a tenant. It pays no rent. It sits in the corner of rooms you thought were empty and clears its throat when you're trying to sleep. And some nights, like tonight, you finally turn around and look at it, and it looks back, and neither of you says anything for a long time because what would be the point after two decades.

Veyra murmured something behind me. Not words—or not *our* words. Those syllables again, older than kingdoms, older than the mountains maybe, rolling out of a child's mouth like water over stones that predate the river. I had heard them once before in my life, properly, deliberately, spoken by someone who knew their weight.

Master Corvane, the night before the purge. Standing in the armory of our hold with his hand flat on the rack of oath-blades, saying: *If ever the day comes when steel must be unbound rather than bound, remember that binding is easy and unbinding costs. Anyone can swear. Only the sworn can take back.*

He said it like a man packing a bag he hoped never to carry. And then the next dawn came, and the king's soldiers came, and the armory burned, and Corvane burned with it—or didn't, which was worse—and I ran north into these mountains with smoke in my lungs and a single question lodged under my ribs like a fishhook: *why did he send me away?*

I untied the cord.

* * *

Inside the oilcloth lay the hilt of an oath-blade. Just the hilt. Crossguard, grip wrapped in grey leather gone dark with age and handling, pommel shaped like a closed mouth—a joke of Corvane's design, or his smith's. No blade. There had been no blade for twenty years, because a blade without an oath is just metal, and Corvane had broken the oath himself, snapped it like a man snapping kindling, the night everything ended. I had heard the sound from three ridges away. Don't ask me how; I've stopped expecting physics to behave when oaths are involved.

The order's blades had been seven. Seven swordspeakers of the First Rank, each carrying one-seventh of the Great Oath, the one we called the Unbinding when we dared name it at all, which was roughly never, because naming a thing in the First Tongue is halfway to doing it. Corvane's hilt was the first fragment. Mine had been the second, and mine was—

Mine was gone. Melted down, I assumed, in the purge. Or buried in some archive vault in the capital, catalogued by clerks who had no idea what they were filing.

So why was I holding his hilt now? Why was my heart doing that arrhythmic thing, that stutter-step, as if it had forgotten the rhythm it had practiced for twenty years?

Because of the child, obviously. Because a girl had walked through a blizzard speaking syllables that belonged to my dead order and my dead master and my dead life, and because her nightmares showed cities burning, and because Ferro's warning about riders asking questions meant the world outside my door had remembered I existed. All of that pointed one direction: down. Under the floorboards. To the thing I had sworn—privately, alone, no witnesses, no steel to speak it into—to never touch again.

I picked it up.

* * *

Cold. Of course it was cold; oilcloth isn't insulation and the mountain doesn't apologize. But it was the *wrong* cold. Metal left in a house takes the temperature of the house. This was colder than the room, colder than the snowmelt in the well bucket, cold like the space between stars that the old astronomers used to frighten each other with. Cold like a held breath.

And then, faintly, the way you hear your name called across a valley—one word, distorted, arriving late—it spoke.

Or he did. Him.

"*Emrys.*"

I want to report that I dropped it. I want to tell you I flung it across the room and made the sign against ghosts and recited the litany of small practical things—goats, firewood, the leak above the pantry—that have kept me sane for two decades. That would be the sensible account. The true account is that I sat down hard on my own kitchen floor, forty-nine years old, scarred like a smuggler's map, and pressed the hilt against my forehead and shook.

"Master," I said. My voice came out rusted. "You're dead."

"*Am I?*" The voice wasn't sound, exactly. It arrived the way swordspeech always had—not through the ears but through the place behind the sternum where an oath lives once it's been spoken into you. Faint, frayed at the edges, like a song remembered by someone who only ever half-listened. "*Define your terms, child. You were always sloppy with terms when you were frightened.*"

"I'm not frightened." A lie so fast and so reflexive that we both, presumably, enjoyed it. "I'm—this isn't possible. Oaths live in steel. You broke the blade. There's nothing left to hold you."

"*There was always something left,*" he said, and there was something in his tone I'd never heard from him in life, because Corvane in life had been granite with opinions: patience. "*A hilt remembers its grip. A grip remembers its hand. And a hand—*" He paused, and ghost though he was, I could feel him choosing gentleness, which terrified me more than any haunting. "*A hand remembers what it failed to do.*"

"Don't." I got up. My knees objected; I overruled them. I put the hilt on the table under the lamp and looked at it instead of touching it, the way you look at a letter you're afraid to open. "Don't do the confession speech. I've given it to myself every night for twenty years, and believe me, I'm better at it than you ever were. Shall I begin? 'Emrys Winterbourne, who was told to run and ran, who was told to hide and hid, who survived when eleven better speakers died, and calls that survival instead of cowardice because the alternative word won't fit through the door.'"

Silence. Ghosts, it turns out, know when to let you finish.

"*Is that what you've believed?*" he said at last. "*All this time? That I sent you south with the ledger-book because you were the fastest runner?*"

"You told me the ledgers had to survive. You put them in my arms and you told me—" My throat closed. Twenty years of composure, and it closes in four sentences. "You said, *the record outlives the recorded.* Then you went back inside."

"*Yes.*"

"And the armory burned."

"*Yes.*"

"And you expect me to have spent two decades NOT concluding that I ran while my order died?"

"*Emrys.*" My name in his mouth did what it always did, which was make me twelve years old and certain I'd misfiled something. "*I need you to listen carefully, because I do not know how long I can hold this shape, and there is less time than either of us would like. The purge was not what you think it was. The king who signed the order—look at the dates. Look at what Aldric did afterward. A man does not slaughter a hundred speakers and then spend ten years signing pardons with his left hand and executions with his right. Something has been wearing him. Something old. And the Unbinding—the real one, all seven fragments—is the only key that fits that particular lock.*"

I stared at him. At it. At the lamp flame standing perfectly still, as if even fire was listening.

"You knew," I said slowly. "Before the purge. You knew something was wrong with the crown."

"*I suspected. Suspicion is a luxury you act on too late or not at all. We chose late.*" The voice thinned. I could see—feel—the effort of him holding himself together, a man made of memory gripping his own edges. "*Which is why I broke my blade and gave you the hilt. Not as a relic. As a seed. Seven fragments, Emrys. Six were scattered by design—mine, Sable's forge-work, the drowned archive, Brannoc's quarry, and two others whose keepers I will trust to your finding. The seventh... the seventh is a harder matter. But first things first. The girl.*"

"The girl is feverish and mute and possibly mad."

"*She spoke the First Tongue at your door.*"

"She could have learned fragments. Survivors taught scraps—"

"*Did she learn them, or did they come to her?*" And now the patience in his voice had an edge, the old classroom edge, the one that preceded the question that rearranged your whole understanding of the reading. "*Think, child. Use the mind I spent fifteen years sharpening. Who carries a language in their marrow rather than their memory? Whose palm was pressed closed around a word by a dying woman in a burning archive? She dreamed it, didn't she. The burning. You watched her dream it.*"

My skin did the thing where it tries to leave without me.

"How do you know that?"

"*Because I am an oath, Emrys. Broken, yes, but oaths do not die; they wait. And the oath I swore into this hilt, twenty years ago, with my last breath and my last steel, was not a farewell.*" The lamp flame bent toward the hilt, though there was no draft. "*It was a promise that when the last speaker of the First Tongue stood at your door, I would be here to answer it. I have waited twenty years in the dark under your feet. I confess the accommodations were poor.*"

Despite everything—grief, terror, the fevered child, the riders on the pass, the whole collapsing architecture of my careful life—I laughed. One syllable, cracked down the middle, but a laugh. He'd have counted it a triumph. He always said humor was the only oath that kept itself.

* * *

I made tea. It seemed important, suddenly, to do a human thing with my hands while the dead man talked. Veyra slept on, twitching, her fingers curling around invisible syllables. I put the blanket higher on her shoulder and pretended my hands weren't shaking, and then I sat across the table from a hilt and let my master finish.

"*Six fragments,*" he said. "*Yours is lost—I grieve it, but lost is not destroyed, and the Regent is careless with what it considers trophies. Mine is in your hands. The drowned archive below Lake Verel holds the second; the lake keeps its own records, and it will want something from you in trade. Remember that lakes are honest creditors. The quarry at Keld holds two more, under Brannoc's guard, and Brannoc—*" a pause, delicate as a blade laid flat, "*—Brannoc believes things about you that are not true, and things about himself that are also not true. Be gentle with him. Or don't. You were always better at honesty than mercy.*"

"Thank you," I said dryly. "Any other flattering assessments of my old colleagues?"

"*Sable lives. Lowmarket. She broke her half-oath to survive and has hated herself efficiently ever since—self-hatred is a discipline, and she excels at it. Her forge holds more than iron. She'll deny it. Let her deny it; denial is just a door that opens slowly.*" The voice was thinning now, going the way of starlight at dawn. "*And Emrys—the seventh fragment. When you learn where it is, and you will learn soon, you will want to despair. Don't. Despair is a liar with excellent diction. There is no vessel the Regent has touched that cannot be unmade by a freely given word. Remember that. Whatever it offers you—and it will offer, it always offers—the answer is the same. What was taken cannot be bargained back. Only given.*"

"What does that mean?" I leaned forward. "Master. What was *taken*? Taken from whom?"

But the flame had straightened. The cold had gone out of the table. The hilt lay there being merely an object, old leather and closed-mouth pommel, silent as any other piece of a dead man's life.

"Corvane." Nothing. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to come back from the dead, reorganize my entire understanding of the last twenty years, and *leave before the questions.*"

Nothing, still. But underneath the silence—under the ordinary silence of a kitchen at night, wind worrying the eaves, coals ticking in the stove—something remained. Not a voice. More like the shape a voice leaves, the way a hand leaves warmth in a glove. An impression of attention. Of waiting. Of a man standing watch at a post nobody else could see, patient as stone, because patience was the whole of his religion and I was, apparently, its congregation.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I record it honestly because someone, someday, may read this and wonder how a person decides to stop hiding:

I washed my face. I banked the fire. I checked Veyra's fever—lower, thank whatever listens—and I ate half a heel of bread standing up because grief makes me forget meals and fear makes me bolt them, and tonight both had set a place at the table.

Then I took the hilt off the table, and instead of wrapping it back in its shroud and lowering it into the dark under the floorboards, I carried it to the corner where my old traveling gear leaned against the wall, gathering the particular dust of abandoned intentions. I found the sword-belt I hadn't worn in twenty years. It still fit. I'm not sure whether that's a compliment to my discipline or an indictment of my cooking.

I slid the hilt into the empty scabbard loop where a blade should hang. It rode there light as a promise and heavy as a debt, which I am beginning to understand are frequently the same object.

Veyra stirred. Her eyes opened—dark, fever-bright, ancient in a way that no nine-year-old's eyes should be—and she looked at the hilt at my hip, and then at my face, and something passed between us that needed no tongue at all, first or otherwise. Recognition, maybe. Two survivors discovering they belong to the same wreckage.

She raised one hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, then pointed at the hilt. Then she pointed at me.

"Not yet," I said aloud, my voice steadier than I had any right to expect. "But soon. And south, I think. Down the smugglers' road, past Ferro's ferry, into every trouble I spent twenty years avoiding." I looked at the window, black and rattling, beyond which the mountains held their silence and, somewhere far below, riders moved along roads I knew blindfolded, carrying questions with my name attached. "It seems the dead have opinions about my retirement."

Outside, far off down the pass, a wolf began to sing—or something that wanted very much to be mistaken for one.
