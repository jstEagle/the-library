## 7. Riders on the Pass

The river told Ferro everything he needed to know, same as it always did. Forty years poling a flat-bottomed ferry across the Greywater and a man learns to read water the way priests claim to read scripture—only the river never lied to him, which put it one up on most priests and every soldier he'd ever met.

That morning the river ran high and loud with snowmelt, and it said: *stay home.*

Ferro considered this. Then he looked at the sky, which was doing that bruised purple thing over the Thornfells that meant another storm coming down off the passes, and the sky said: *stay home too.*

Then he looked at his empty woodpile and his emptier coin purse and thought about the widow Marta's flour debt, and Ferro—who had survived two wars, one marriage, and the entire reign of King Aldric by the simple method of never being anywhere interesting—tied up his boots and went down to the ferry all the same.

Stupid, in hindsight. But then, hindsight is just foresight with the fun taken out.

* * *

He'd poled three crossings by midmorning—a tinker with more opinions than goods, a pair of pilgrims who smelled of incense and poverty, and old Mother Hesk with her goat, who paid in cheese and gossip. It was during the goat crossing that Ferro first saw the dust on the north road.

Dust in winter meant horses, and horses in numbers meant soldiers, and soldiers on the north road meant they'd come down out of the pass instead of around by the king's highway. Which meant somebody had told them about the smugglers' paths. Which meant somebody up the chain of command had finally remembered the Thornfells existed.

"Well," Ferro said to the goat. "That's not my problem."

The goat chewed thoughtfully, as goats do, unconvinced.

Six riders came down to the ferry landing an hour later. Ferro counted them from under the brim of his hat while pretending to mend a rope, because counting is free and looking like you're counting gets you hanged. Six riders, grey cloaks under their crown-tabards, and they rode wrong—not like soldiers bored on patrol but like men following a scent. Horses blown, tack crusted with road salt, the lead rider's cloak torn at the shoulder where something with thorns had objected to him passing.

They reined up at the landing. The leader was young for the job, maybe thirty, with the kind of face that had been handsome before ambition got hold of it and started rearranging furniture. His eyes did the thing Ferro hated most in officers: they looked at everything without seeing any of it, cataloguing rather than regarding. Ferro's ferry. Ferro's hut. Ferro himself, standing in plain view with a mended rope in his hands and nothing to hide.

Which was, of course, a lie. He had plenty to hide. Just not yet. Not until they asked.

"Ferryman." The officer's voice carried the flat vowels of the capital, softened by too much time shouting orders into mountain wind. "When did you last carry passengers?"

"Crossed the tinker this morning, ser. Pilgrims and Mother Hesk after. Goat's still aboard if you want to count her."

A couple of the riders laughed. The officer didn't. He dismounted, walked to the edge of the landing, and studied the far bank the way a man studies a woman who owes him money.

"We're looking for a girl," he said, without turning. "Ten, eleven years old. Dark-haired. Travelling alone or with a woman."

"River's full of girls this time of year," Ferro said. "Mostly made of ice, though. They don't travel far."

Now the officer turned, and Ferro got his first proper look at those eyes up close, and revised his estimate downward. Not thirty. Younger. Ambition aged some men fast and others slow; this one had been rushed through childhood so thoroughly he probably didn't remember having one.

"The Thornfell roads cross here," the officer said. "Or near enough. We know that. What we don't know is what crosses them, and when, and carrying what." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been installed by professionals. "So I'll ask again, and this time we'll both pretend it's the first time. When did you last carry anyone heading up into the mountains? Northbound. Anyone."

"Ser, in forty years on this river I have carried everyone heading everywhere. Smugglers, brides, corpses, one archbishop, and a bear. If I turned away every northbound soul I'd have starved before your father learned to walk. You'll have to do better than 'anyone.'"

The smile stayed. The eyes didn't. "Names, then. Faces."

"I don't collect names. Names cost extra and nobody pays."

One of the other riders swung down and started walking along the bank, reading it the way Ferro read it—which was professionally annoying. He stopped where the ground was churned soft near the reeds and crouched.

"Sergeant. Boot prints here. Small ones. And—" he paused, and Ferro felt his stomach perform a slow, dignified roll, "—a woman's boots. Two sets, heading north along the east path."

"They could be days old," the sergeant said.

"They're fresh. Frost hasn't healed them."

* * *

Here is what Ferro knew, and what he spent the next several minutes deciding whether to know out loud:

Three nights past, a woman had crossed his river going north, paying in old silver and not asking the price. Tall, grey at the temples, moving like someone whose body remembered fighting even if her mouth denied it. She'd stood on his deck with her hood back and the wind pulling at her face, and Ferro had known her.

Not her name. Her name he'd never been given, twenty-some years ago, when she'd come down the same path half-dead with frostbite and a sword wrapped in oilcloth and eyes like a door slammed shut. She'd stayed one night in his loft, drunk his bad wine, and left before dawn, and in forty years of ferry work she was one of perhaps four passengers Ferro had ever bothered remembering.

And she'd asked him, this time, leaning close as the current grabbed the hull: *"If riders come asking, you never saw me. Swear it however you swear things."*

Ferro swore things the way he did everything else: cheaply, privately, and meaning every word.

So now there were boot prints by the reeds—because the woman had come ashore there, yes, but also because Ferro himself had walked that stretch yesterday carrying firewood, and his own boots were big enough to blur anything small beside them—and there were six soldiers with a scent, and there was a question hanging in the cold air like smoke.

And here is what Ferro decided, standing there with the river talking sense behind him:

He was nobody. That had always been the plan. Stay small, stay dull, stay useful in uninteresting ways, and the great wheels of history would roll past his ferry without stopping. He'd watched kings change and orders burn and neighbors vanish, and he'd kept his head down so long it had grown roots.

But the woman had looked at him across the water with those slammed-door eyes and said *swear it*, and she hadn't offered coin for the swearing, because she'd known—both of them had—that some things you can't buy, only spend.

"Funny thing about boot prints," Ferro said aloud.

The sergeant turned.

"They go both ways on this river. Come and go, come and go. I've seen a thousand pairs since first frost, and if you want me to sort them out by size and direction and how fresh the frost says they are, I'll need a week, good light, and a payment schedule." He spat, artistically, into the current. "Or you can tell me what I get for being helpful, besides helpful."

The officer studied him for a long moment. "You get to keep your ferry."

"That's the same thing I get for being unhelpful. You'll have to sweeten it."

Something flickered behind those too-old eyes—the first honest thing Ferro had seen in them. Maybe amusement. Maybe appetite.

"You're a liar, ferryman."

"Everyone's a liar. I'm just honest about the rates."

* * *

They took him to his own hut for the questioning, which Ferro found rude in principle. A man's hut should be his castle, or at minimum his accomplice. Instead they sat him on his own stool, drank his own cider, and let the young officer pace his floor while the sergeant went through his things with the thoroughness of a tax assessor.

The first lie was bad. Ferro would be the first to admit it. They asked whether he'd ferried a woman and child recently, and he said no, absolutely not, hadn't laid eyes on either, and he said it about half a heartbeat too fast, with the particular firmness of a man reciting something rehearsed. The officer stopped pacing and looked at him the way a cat looks at a mouse that has just announced its intention to remain perfectly still.

"No," the officer repeated.

"Not a one."

"In forty years."

"Quiet stretch."

The officer sat down across from him, uninvited, and helped himself to more cider, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the only sounds were wind worrying the thatch and the sergeant finding Ferro's hidden coin tin in approximately four seconds.

"You're lying badly," the officer said, almost gently. "Do you know why?"

"My talent was always rowing."

"You're lying badly because you're frightened, and frightened men reach for the biggest lie nearest to hand." He set the cup down. "Here's what happens next. I ask again. You lie better. And if I believe you, you keep your fingers. If I don't—" he shrugged, and the shrug said everything the capital taught its instruments about consequences, "—there are places south of here where men who obstruct crown inquiries learn new trades. Shorter trades."

So Ferro took a breath, and did the arithmetic he should have done from the start, which was this:

They already knew a woman and child had gone north. The prints proved it. Lying about the crossing bought nothing—it only made him part of the story. But a story could be bent. A story could be steered, the way you steer a heavy ferry in a hard current: not against the flow, but across it, letting the river do the work.

"All right," Ferro said. "All right. Yes. I carried them."

The officer's eyebrows rose, genuinely surprised. Good. Surprise was a door, and doors swing both ways.

"Night before last," Ferro went on, and now he let the words come slow and easy, the way truth comes when you've finally decided to tell it sideways. "Woman, dark-haired, grey in it. Girl of ten or so, didn't speak a word the whole crossing—just stared at me like I owed her money. Paid in old silver, minted before the war." He shook his head. "I didn't like it. Still don't. But silver's silver, and I don't ask passengers their sins."

"Why didn't you mention this earlier?"

"Because you opened with threats, and I'm a coward, and cowards open with denials. Ask any of my wives."

"One wife," the sergeant murmured, from the corner.

"She divorced me for honesty," Ferro said. "Point stands."

The officer leaned forward. "Where did they cross to? Where were they headed?"

"Nowhere, ser, that's the thing—I mean, somewhere, obviously, but not where you're thinking." Ferro rubbed his jaw, buying a beat, watching the officer watch him. "They went downstream. South. Toward Lowmarket way. I figured her for a widow running from debt, or worse than debt. Why else pay in coin older than the girl?" He spread his hands. "I run a ferry, not an interrogation. People point, I pole. Downstream was downstream."

"Liar," the sergeant said mildly, from the corner, still going through things.

"Everyone's a liar," Ferro agreed. "But think it through, sergeant. If I were lying for them—if I'd helped a fugitive slip the crown—would I tell you they crossed at all? Would I hand you the direction?" He tapped his own chest. "Guilty men say *no one came through*. Innocent men say *yes, and here's everything*, and smart guilty men—" he held up a finger, "—say what I just said. So which am I? Take your time. I'll refill the cider."

It was, by any honest measure, a terrible gambit. He was confessing to a crossing that had happened, misdirecting its direction, and daring them to catch the bend in the current—all in one breath, with hands that wanted very much to shake and didn't because forty years of poling teaches a man to keep his hands quiet no matter what his stomach is doing.

But the officer was tired. That was the crack in the wall, and Ferro had seen it from the start—blown horses, torn cloak, days in the saddle chasing something through thaw and thorn. Tired men want the world simple. Ferro was offering them simple: a cowardly ferryman, a southbound widow, a trail leading away from the deep mountains toward a town where six riders could knock on doors and feel official.

"South," the officer said slowly.

"Toward Lowmarket. There's a drover's track leaves the south bank—you'd want the second fork, not the first, the first floods this time of year." Every word true, every word useless, the whole sentence a gift box with nothing in it. "If they kept to the track, you'd make Lowmarket by tomorrow night. If they cut cross-country—" he shrugged. "Then the river lost them, same as it loses everybody."

The officer stood. Walked to the window. Stood there so long that Ferro began composing his own eulogy, and a fine one it was, full of lies about his courage.

Then: "Sergeant. Mount up. South fork."

"And him?"

The officer looked back at Ferro, and for one naked moment all the ambition fell off his face and Ferro saw the boy underneath, exhausted and far from home and chasing something that frightened him—he was frightened, Ferro realized, actually frightened, these hunters feared what they hunted—and then the armor came back down over it.

"He comes with us," the officer said. "To show us the fork. And then we'll see whether the crown needs a ferryman on the Greywater, or whether the Greywater can learn to do without."

* * *

They let him take his own boat downriver with two soldiers aboard and the rest riding the bank, which was an insult added to injury, except that Ferro had spent forty years learning every rock and snag and shallow between his landing and the Lowmarket confluence, and a boat with Ferro at the pole goes exactly where Ferro wants it to go, weather and soldiers permitting.

The permitting was the trick.

There was a place, four miles down, where the river split around a gravel bar the locals called the Widow's Teeth—shallow, fast, and lined with deadfall that ate hulls for breakfast. In normal water it was passable on the channel side, if you knew the channel, and only Ferro and the fish knew the channel.

In rising water, with snowmelt coming down hard off the Thorns and the sky finally making good on its threat of sleet, the Teeth were suicide. Any ferryman worth his salt would put in above them and wait out the rise.

Any ferryman worth his salt.

Ferro put in below them.

He couldn't sink the boat—not with two soldiers gripping the gunwales and their swords within reach—but he could run the shallows on the wrong side of the bar, scrape hull, ship a little water, and lose an hour cursing and bailing and demanding help, and by the time they were floating clean again the light would be going and no officer alive pushes a mounted patrol down unknown drover's tracks in the dark with a storm coming on. They'd camp. They'd camp an hour short of where they could have camped, and an hour is a mile, and a mile is a life, sometimes.

It wasn't much. Three days was beyond him. But hours—hours he could steal, one at a time, like coins from a blind man's purse, and feel proud of it.

Except.

They came around the last bend above the Teeth and Ferro saw the smoke.

Thin, grey, wrong—for this stretch of river had no houses, no hearths, nothing to burn but driftwood—and rising from the gravel bar itself, where a fire had been built in the open like a signal, like an answer, like a man planting a flag on a map that said *look here.*

Two figures stood by it.

One was tall, grey-templed, arms folded, wearing patience like armor.

The other was small, dark-haired, and waving.

Waving. At the soldiers. Cheerfully. As though greeting family off a boat.

"Don't," Ferro breathed, to no one, to the river, to whatever gods supervised idiots. "*Don't you dare—*"

The officer's hand went to his sword. The soldiers behind Ferro shifted, leather creaking, and the whole world narrowed to the space between the bow of the ferry and the burning fire on the bar, where a woman stood very still and a child waved and waved—

—and then the child stopped waving, and pressed both hands flat against her ears, and screamed.

No sound came out. But every horse on the bank screamed for her, all six at once, rearing and tearing loose and bolting into the trees, and the fire flattened as if a giant had exhaled, and the gravel bar rang like a struck bell, and Ferro felt the sound go through his chest and out his spine and into the river, which shivered from bank to bank.

The girl lowered her hands. The woman hadn't moved. The soldiers were on their knees in the bottom of the boat, white-faced, and the young officer stood swaying in the stern with his sword half-drawn and his face wearing an expression Ferro had last seen on men coming out of battle: the look of someone whose understanding of the world has just been confiscated.

"What," the officer whispered, "was that."

And Ferro, who had decided three days ago to stay small and dull and uninteresting until the wheels of history rolled past, picked up his pole, looked the officer dead in the eye, and said:

"That, ser, is why the fare's gone up."
