## 8. Down the Thornfell Road

*Emrys*

The mountain does not forgive mistakes, which is why I had made a career of avoiding it.

Twenty years of knowing every goat track and deadfall on Thornfell's southern face, and I could have walked the smugglers' road blindfolded—which was fortunate, because the morning we left, the wind drove snow sideways hard enough to make seeing anything a theoretical exercise. I tied a rope between us anyway. Veyra looked at it, then at me, with an expression that said, very clearly, in whatever wordless language children perfect before they ever need words: *I am not going to fall off a mountain.*

"Humor me," I said. "I've buried enough people this decade."

She took the rope.

We went down through the pines above my cabin, past the spring that froze into a blue fist every winter, along the ledge path the old ore-runners had cut when the kingdom still bothered mining these heights. The path was barely wider than a coffin laid sideways, which is exactly how the smugglers who'd built it liked their roads: narrow enough that you couldn't bring soldiers up them, wide enough to carry out what the soldiers didn't want found. Silver once. Then people. Now nothing but me and a mute girl carrying three days of dried mutton and the end of the world in her pocket.

I talked as I walked. Not because I'm fond of my own voice—I'm not; twenty years alone cured me of that particular vanity—but because silence on a mountain makes you listen too hard to what's underneath it, and lately what was underneath it had teeth.

"The next bend is called the Widow's Elbow," I said, pointing with my chin since my hands were full of rope and cold. "Ice forms there by noon even in summer. We cross it before the sun crests the eastern ridge, or we don't cross it at all."

Veyra nodded, solemn, her breath streaming behind her like a scarf.

"And after that there's a cleft where the rock splits. You'll want to go left, because left looks easier." I glanced back at her. "Go right. The left side is where I watched a man named Doss step onto what he thought was stone and drop two hundred feet into the ravine. He screamed for eleven seconds. I counted, because I was young and stupid and thought counting mattered somehow."

She looked at the left side for a long moment. Then at me. And she raised one small hand and mimed, with terrible precision, someone falling, flailing, screaming—mouth stretched wide, no sound—and then held up fingers. Ten. Eleven.

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out rusty, like a gate hinge that hadn't turned since the last owner died.

"Yes," I said. "Exactly like that. You're a horror, child. Good. Horrors survive."

* * *

*Veyra*

The woman walked like the mountain belonged to her and she was embarrassed about it.

Veyra watched her feet more than the view. The woman—Emrys, though Veyra only knew the name from the word pressed into her palm, from the burning night, from the voice that had said *find her*—never stepped twice in the same way. On flat ground she strode like anyone. But where the rocks jutted or the ice glossed over, something changed in her, some old arithmetic running behind her eyes, and her boots found their places without her seeming to look.

Veyra tried to copy it. She failed, mostly. Once her foot slid on the Widow's Elbow—the woman had not exaggerated; the ice there was like a lie told smoothly—and the rope snapped taut, and Emrys hauled her in with one arm as if she weighed no more than the mutton.

"Eleven seconds," Veyra mouthed at her, and pointed down the ravine.

"Don't," Emrys said. Her face did something complicated. "Don't count your own seconds. Count mine instead. If I say *down*, you get low. If I say *still*, you don't breathe until I say otherwise. If I say *run*, you run downhill and don't look back, because looking back costs half a second and half a second is how everyone I ever—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Just remember the three words."

Veyra held up three fingers. *Down, still, run.* She made the shapes of each with her body, quick little pantomimes, and Emrys watched with the expression of someone being taught her own lesson back to herself.

"Good," she said finally, gruffly, and gave the rope a tug that might have meant *walk on* and might have meant something else entirely.

They descended all day. The pines thickened, then thinned, then thickened again where a stream had carved its argument through the mountainside. In the late afternoon they sheltered beneath an overhang whose floor was scattered with old tin plates, a rusted kettle, the black circle of a fire some other traveler had lit years ago. Smuggler camps, Emrys called them, and refused to say more, which meant there was a story and the story hurt.

Veyra built the fire. This she knew—not from any teacher, but from the long flight north, from nights under hedgerows and in culverts, from learning the way fire behaves when you're small and alone and cannot call for help. She stacked the wood the way fire likes: small bones first, breathing room between, patience. When the flame caught, Emrys stared at it, then at her.

"Who taught you that?"

Veyra shrugged. Everyone, and no one. Hunger taught her. Cold taught her. Fear was an excellent tutor.

Emrys fed the fire a stick and said nothing for a while. Then, quietly, as if testing whether the words would hold weight: "Show me a word. One of yours. The First Tongue."

Veyra hesitated. Words in the First Tongue were not like other words. They had weight the way stones have weight, and you did not hand stones to strangers. But the stranger had pulled her off the ice. She cupped her hands, breathed into them, and shaped the word for *warm*—not spoke it, never fully spoke it anymore, just let its shape move through her lips soundlessly—and held her open palms toward the older woman.

Nothing happened, of course. It was just a gesture. But Emrys went very still, staring at those empty cupped hands as if they held an ember, and her eyes were wet when she blinked.

"That's how Master Corvane used to do it," she said. "When he taught us. Soundless first. He said the tongue lives in the hands before it lives in the mouth." She laughed, short and cracked. "I spent twenty years trying to forget his hands. And here they are again, on a child."

* * *

*Emrys*

Here is what nobody tells you about teaching: it is grief wearing a practical coat.

Every correction I gave Veyra—tuck the elbow, test the ice twice, eat before you're hungry because hunger makes fools of everyone—came out of my mouth in Corvane's cadence, in Sable's old laugh, in the voices of the forty-one speakers who died in the purge while I ran. I was a ghost teaching a living child, and the child didn't know it, and I wasn't going to tell her.

But she learned fast. Frighteningly fast. By the third morning she was reading the mountain the way I did—pausing at the edges of ledges, checking her footing before committing her weight, listening to the wind change pitch around a corner the way sailors listen for reefs. And she taught me things too, worse luck. How to sleep curled so you keep warm with less fire. Which pine bark strips cleanly for kindling when everything's soaked. A dozen small brutal efficiencies of the road that no oath-bound speaker had ever needed, because we had always been the ones others protected.

"You're better at this than I was at your age," I admitted on the fourth evening, watching her portion out the mutton with a smuggler's fairness—not equally, but according to who'd done the most work that day, which meant she gave me the larger share and dared me with her eyes to object.

I didn't object. I was raised polite, not stupid.

That night, in the overhang camp with the wind worrying the tent-flap of our tarp, I asked her for another word. She'd been giving them freely since the fire trick—one per evening, soundless, held in her palms like offerings. *Warm. Stone. River. Home.* Each one landed somewhere in my chest and stayed.

This time she shook her head.

"No?"

She pointed at my mouth. Then at her own ears. Then she frowned and pressed her fists together, frustrated, hunting for a way to explain across the gap where her voice should be. Finally she took my hand, opened it flat, and drew one finger slowly across the palm—a long stroke, deliberate.

Then she pointed at the dark beyond the fire, toward the south, toward the capital, and made the same slow stroke through the air.

I understood, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

*A long word,* she was saying. *A word that takes your whole life to say.*

The Unbinding. The thing her burned archive had died protecting, the thing the thing-that-wore-the-king wanted silenced. It wasn't a spell you learned. It was a sentence you became. Seven fragments, Corvane's ghost had said under my floorboards, seven pieces of the broken oath, and the speaking of them would cost—

Well. I hadn't let myself finish that thought yet. Veyra had finished it for me with one finger drawn across my palm.

"Thank you," I said, which was inadequate, and she accepted it anyway, which was generous, and we slept back to back against the cold, which was neither—it was just two people keeping each other from freezing, and somehow that was the most honest arrangement either of us had made in years.

* * *

*Veyra*

On the fifth day the trees began to smell like smoke that wasn't theirs.

Veyra smelled it first—she always smelled things first now, her nose having taken over the work her voice could no longer do—and stopped so abruptly that the rope jerked Emrys around.

"What?"

Veyra pointed south. Downwind. Made a curling motion with her fingers: *smoke*. Then flattened her palm and rocked it: *a lot*.

Emrys tasted the air, and her whole body changed. Twenty years fell off her and then, strangely, piled back on double; she stood straighter and looked older at the same time, like a blade remembering both its edge and its scars.

"That's chimney smoke," she said slowly. "Woodsmoke, hearth-smoke, not forest fire. That's Lowmarket." She said the town's name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd survived. "Two days' walk. We were always going to pass near it—there's no other way down off Thornfell that isn't a cliff—but I'd hoped..." She exhaled through her teeth. "Hoped is a fool's occupation. Come on. There's a shepherd's track that skirts the valley. With luck we won't need to set foot in the town at all."

With luck. Veyra had learned what that phrase meant on the road north. It meant *the opposite, probably.*

They came down out of the high passes that afternoon into a country of grey scree and brown grass, and by evening they could see the valley below: a river, a bridge, a scatter of roofs with smoke standing straight up from their chimneys in the windless dusk. Ordinary. Peaceful. The sort of place where people believed the king loved them.

And on the bridge—small at this distance, but unmistakable in the way that trouble is always unmistakable—figures. Mounted. Moving slowly, stopping travelers, bending down to speak to them. Checking faces.

Emrys pulled Veyra down behind a boulder so fast the world blurred.

"Riders," she breathed. "Crown grey. Here." Her jaw worked. "Ferro's warning. They're asking after a girl. They're not asking anymore—they're *looking*." She was quiet a moment, doing arithmetic Veyra could almost see running behind her eyes. "The shepherd's track crosses the valley road at the ford. No way around it. And even if there were—" she looked at Veyra, really looked, with something raw and reluctant surfacing in her face, "—you need new boots, real food, and I need to find out what they know. Which means Lowmarket. Which means people. Which means someone, someday, recognizing my face."

She said it like a death sentence. Veyra tilted her head, puzzled. Emrys noticed, and gave a short, bitter laugh.

"Twenty years ago I had a life down there, child. A name people knew. Students." Her hand drifted to the floorboard-hilt hidden under her coat, the fragment that hummed faintly against her ribs like a sleeping wasp. "There's a smithy in Lowmarket. Good one. Run by a woman with burn-scarred forearms and a temper like a quenching tank." She paused. "Her name is Sable. She was my apprentice. She's the reason I got out alive, and the reason I never forgave myself for getting out alive."

Veyra waited. Emrys didn't finish the thought. Some sentences take a whole life to say.

Instead the older woman settled her pack, checked the rope between them, and looked down at the valley, at the bridge, at the riders moving among the ordinary evening like wolves deciding which sheep to count.

"We walk in at dawn, heads down, pilgrims on the road to nowhere in particular," she said. "You don't speak—you never speak, you're a deaf-mute orphan and my sister's kid and I'm taking her to an aunt in the river towns. You let me do the talking. And if I say *run*—"

Veyra was already moving downhill, quick and silent, showing Emrys her own three words in the shapes of her body.

*Run.*

Below them, one of the riders on the bridge turned his horse and began, unhurried, to ride up the valley road. Toward the ford. Toward the shepherd's track. Toward the only way down.

Toward them.
