## 9. The Town That Forgot Her Name

Lowmarket smelled the same, which was its own small miracle. Woodsmoke and wet wool, tallow and turnips, the sour-sweet breath of the cider press working overtime behind the cooper's yard. Twenty years is long enough for a town to forget a war, a plague, or a person. It is apparently not long enough to make anyone sweep their chimneys properly.

Veyra walked beside me with her hood up and her hand in mine, which she had not offered and I had not asked for. It had simply happened somewhere on the third day of descent, when the mountain paths narrowed and I needed her close enough to catch. Children are practical like that. They decide you're safe the way they decide the ground will hold them—without ceremony, without appeal, and with absolute fury if it turns out otherwise.

"You'll stay close," I said, for the fourth time since dawn.

She looked up at me with the particular patience of someone who has been told a thing four times and intends to ignore it exactly once.

"Close," I repeated.

She squeezed my hand once. *Yes.* Then, after a moment of visible internal debate, she raised her other hand and made a shape in the air—a little house, roofed with two fingers—and pointed at me.

"I don't have a house here."

She pointed again, more insistently, then at the whole street, the crooked gables, the market square ahead where a man was shouting about the price of eels as though eels had personally wronged him.

"This isn't—" I stopped. Because it had been, once. For three years, between missions, between oaths, this had been the closest thing to a house the order ever let us keep. A waystation. The brothers and sisters of the Sword-Speech passed through Lowmarket the way water passes through a mill: we came in rough and came out useful.

I hadn't been back since the purge. There were reasons. The reasons all wore grey now, if Ferro's warning meant anything, and Ferro's warnings had a way of meaning things.

* * *

We did our business the way smugglers do everything: boringly. Boring is armor. I bought salt I didn't strictly need, lamp oil, a second blanket for Veyra because the nights on the low road turned damp and she slept like something drowning. I haggled over dried apples with a woman whose hands shook too much for her to notice mine, and I kept my hood back just enough to look like a farmer's widow rather than a fugitive's ghost. Widowhood, I've found, is the great invisibility cloak of the realm. No one asks a widow her history. They only ask if she needs help carrying things, and then feel bad about asking.

It was going beautifully. Which should have warned me.

The trouble started at the forge.

You can hear a forge before you see it, and I heard this one and felt something twist under my ribs, because the rhythm was wrong—or rather, right, in a way no ordinary smith works. Three strikes, rest. Three strikes, rest. The old cadence. The one we used to set quench-water humming so the steel would take an oath evenly. Most smiths hammer until the metal tells them to stop. Oath-trained smiths count.

I stood across the lane with a basket of apples I'd already paid too much for and listened to a stranger hammer in the grammar of a dead order, and I thought: *coincidence.* I am very good at thinking *coincidence.* I'd had twenty years of practice. Coincidence had practically become my native tongue, second only to silence.

Then the hammering stopped, and a woman stepped out into the doorway to cool her hands in the autumn air, and coincidence died quietly in the gutter where it belonged.

Sable.

She was thirty-four years old and built like the anvil she'd inherited—short, broad, forearms that could crack a walnut by looking at it. Her hair was cropped short and iron-grey at the temples, far too young for grey, though forge-heat ages people strangely. There was a burn scar down the left side of her jaw that hadn't been there at fifteen. She'd been fifteen the last time I saw her, running through smoke with her half-finished oath-blade still hot in her grip, and I had shouted at her to drop it, to run clean, and she had dropped it, and the sound of it hitting the flagstones was the last thing I heard of Thornfell Abbey before the fire took the rest.

She hadn't seen me yet. She was stretching her wrists, eyes closed, face tilted toward the weak sun, and she looked—this was the worst part—she looked *content.* Settled. A woman whose life fit her the way a well-worn scabbard fits a blade, even a broken blade. Especially a broken blade.

Every sensible instinct I owned said *walk away.* I have never claimed to own sensible instincts in working condition. My feet carried me across the lane before my better judgment finished drafting its objection.

"Sable Ironhale."

Her eyes snapped open.

For a moment—one long, ringing moment—she was fifteen again, and I could see it happen behind her face: the smoke, the shouting, the blade falling. Then it passed, and what replaced it was worse than fear or hatred. It was carefulness. She looked at me the way you look at a floorboard you suspect might be rotten but which is currently holding your entire house up.

"Well," she said. Her voice had gone low and smoky from years of forge-work. "The mountain finally spat you out."

"Something like that."

"Twenty years." She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, deliberate. "Nineteen years, eleven months, if you want to be exact about it. And you come to my forge on a market day with your hood half-up like a bad painting of yourself."

"The hood's usually better."

"It's terrible." And then, because she was Sable and always had been, she jerked her chin toward the open door. "Get inside before someone with eyes walks past. You too, small one."

Veyra had gone very still at my shoulder. I felt her fingers find the hem of my coat and grip it—not fear exactly. Assessment. She looked at Sable the way she'd looked at the pass, the ferry, the first sight of the lowlands: cataloguing, deciding whether this new thing was weather or wolf.

"She doesn't speak," I said.

"So I gathered from the staring contest." Sable held out a hand, palm down, at child-height—the old greeting, the one we gave novices, level and unthreatening. Veyra studied it for a long moment. Then she placed her small palm beneath it, not quite touching, mirroring the form perfectly, and Sable's breath went out of her like she'd been struck.

"They taught you that?" she asked me, hoarse.

"No."

* * *

Inside, the forge was warm and dim and full of the smell of coal and quenched steel, and I tried very hard not to look at the rack of blades along the far wall, and failed immediately.

They were good work. That was the thing that hurt. Plain, honest, unbound steel—farm knives, wood axes, a fencer's practice sword with its edges deliberately blunted. Not one of them sang. I knew that without touching them. Whatever oath-capacity Sable Ironhale had been born with, whatever half-gift she'd carried as the order's youngest foundling-smith, she had buried it so deep that her metal came out of the fire mute. Dead steel. Safe steel. Steel that would never be taken from her and made to testify against anyone.

"You broke it," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Your half-oath."

"Yes." She poured water from a clay jug into two cups, and pushed one across the workbench to me, and gave Veyra the third cup that had clearly been waiting by the fire, because of course she'd known we'd come, some part of her, the part that counted hammer-strikes in threes. "Fourteen years ago. Cold morning, much like today. I walked out past the tannery, held the blade over the stream, and said the words backwards. Unspoke it. Took about an hour and cost me a nosebleed you wouldn't believe." She drank. "Best hour of my life. Worst hangover of my life. Both true."

"Why?"

"Why?" She laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "Because I was seventeen, Emrys, and everyone who ever taught me the words was ash, and every month I kept the oath was another month some grey-cloaked inquisitor could smell it on me. You think loyalty keeps you warm? Loyalty gets you dragged out of your bed in Lowmarket with your family made to watch." She set the cup down hard. "I unbound myself so thoroughly that when they swept through here in year three, the diviner walked past my forge and felt *nothing.* Nothing. Do you understand what that bought me? It bought me this. Coal, and iron, and a life."

"I'm not here to judge you."

"You came to my door. That *is* a judgment. People who aren't judging don't travel off a mountain after twenty years to stand in a blacksmith's shop holding a basket of apples like a peace offering from a woman who has never once in her life brought a peace offering anywhere."

The apples sat between us, absurd and damning.

"Tell me why you're really here," Sable said. "And don't tell me salt and lamp oil. I watched you buy salt. You bought salt the way a spy buys salt—examining the grain."

So I told her. Not everything—I am constitutionally incapable of everything—but enough. The child. The First Tongue coming out of a mouth that couldn't speak any other way. The king who wasn't a king anymore. Ferro's riders, asking after a girl, moving up the valley roads. The fragments. When I got to the fragments her face went carefully blank, which in Sable's language was the same as most people screaming.

"There's a fragment near here," I finished. "Corvane hid seven. I have one. The map-points put the second within three days' walk of Lowmarket, and I need a place to leave the girl while I go after it, because taking her into whatever Corvane warded that hiding place with would be—" I searched for the word. "Stupid," Veyra supplied, from her cup.

Both Sable and I stared at her.

"She talks," Sable said faintly.

"Not often. And apparently only to insult me." I turned back to Sable before the ache in my chest could show on my face. "I'm asking for a roof. Two nights, maybe three. Nothing else. I know what I'm asking. I know what walking through my door cost you fourteen years of careful nothing."

Sable stood. She crossed to the blade-rack, took down the practice sword, weighed it in her hands like she was checking it for flaws she already knew were there. Her back was to me, and her shoulders were doing the thing shoulders do when their owner is having an argument entirely inside herself.

"You want to know the worst part?" she said, without turning around. "The worst part isn't that you're here. It's that I knew you'd come eventually. Fourteen years, every time hoofbeats went past the shop too slow, every time a stranger asked about steel, some stupid loyal corner of me thought—*there she is. There's Emrys Winterbourne, last of the oath-bound, come to drag me back into the fire.*" She set the practice sword down. "I hated that corner of me. I starved it. I married it off to a good man and gave it children and a trade and a name nobody hunts. And it *still* kept the count. Nineteen years, eleven months."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She turned around at last, and her eyes were bright and furious and, underneath the fury, doing something I had not prepared for at all. "Be *useful.* You want to leave the girl? Leave the girl. She sleeps in the loft; my Tomás is away carting iron till week's end and my girls will either adore her or adopt her, likely both. You want three days? Take four. But you eat at my table and you tell me everything—everything, Emrys, including the parts you've decided are yours to carry alone, because that habit of yours got people burned, in case you've forgotten whose abbey it was that went up."

The words landed where she'd aimed them, which was directly into the oldest wound I owned.

"And afterward?" I managed. "When the hunters follow me here, the way they followed Ferro's ferry? What then?"

"Afterward," Sable said, "you take your fragments and your child and your ghosts, and you get out of my town, and I go back to being a smith whose steel doesn't sing." She picked up her hammer. Conversation over. "Now sit down and drink your water. You look like a fence post that lost an argument with winter."

Veyra tugged my sleeve. When I looked down, she was watching Sable with an expression I couldn't read—until she lifted her hand and touched her own chest, then pointed at the forge, then at Sable, and made a small, puzzled motion, like turning a key that wouldn't catch.

"What is it?"

Veyra frowned. She tried again. Chest. Forge. Sable. And then, slowly, she pressed both palms flat together and drew them apart, the way you'd show two halves of something broken—

—and Sable's hammer slipped from her fingers and rang on the anvil like a bell.

"That," Sable whispered, staring at the child, at the space between the child's hands, at the place where her own broken half-oath lived like a splinter she'd learned to stop noticing, "that is not possible."

Outside, in the lane, a horse stamped. Just one horse. Just a horse stamping.

But I had spent twenty years learning the sound of riders on a pass, and the sound of one rider dismounting slowly outside a forge, and I was already moving for the back door when Veyra caught my wrist and shook her head—*no*—and pointed at the front, where the shadow fell across the threshold, and a voice said, pleasantly, the way the Regent's people always spoke, as though reading from a menu:

"Smith. We're told a woman came in off the mountain road this morning. Tall, grey-eyed, traveling with a child. We'd like a look at her."
