## 10. Sable's Broken Oath

The forge remembers everything I try to forget.

That's the trouble with smithing. You spend your days hammering heat into shape, and the work gets into you the way smoke gets into wool—permanently, in the grain, no scrubbing it out. Every morning when I light the coals, my hands do what they were trained to do twenty years ago, and my body thinks it's back at the hold, and for one bright terrible second before I open my eyes I am still an apprentice of the Order of the Spoken Blade, and Master Corvane is alive, and nobody has betrayed anybody yet.

Then I open my eyes. Then I'm just Sable Ironmark of Lowmarket, horseshoes and hinges and plowshares, a woman who makes things that hold nothing but weight.

She'd been in my forge two days now. Emrys Winterbourne. The name alone was enough to make me want to throw something into the quench bucket.

* * *

You have to understand what she was to us. Not to the order—to *us*, the apprentices, the ones too young and too earnest to know we were being trained for a massacre with a decade's delay built in.

Emrys Winterbourne was the best of us. Not the strongest—that was Brannoc, who could speak a sword sharp enough to shave with. Not the cleverest—that was little Wren Halloway, who died at nineteen with half an oath in her mouth. But the best. The one Master Corvane pointed at when the visiting lords asked how swordspeech worked. *Watch her listen,* he'd say, as if listening were a spectacle. And she would stand there with her eyes closed and her hand flat on some old blade, and her face would go soft and terrible at once, like a woman hearing a confession she already knew by heart.

I worshipped her. That's the embarrassing truth of it. Fifteen years old and gap-toothed and so hungry to matter that I'd have walked into fire if she told me the flames spoke First Tongue.

Which is why what happened at the purge broke me in a place that doesn't set right.

* * *

It was autumn. Harvest-smoke season, when the whole valley smelled like burning and everyone pretended it was only fields.

They came at dawn. I know every account of that day says night, because night is more dramatic and also because most of the tellers weren't there. It was dawn. Grey light, mist on the training yard, the smell of porridge from the kitchen. Forty-one of us in residence. I've counted them every year since, on the anniversary, the way other people count their blessings. Forty-one.

The grey cloaks came up the mountain road without hurry. That's what I remember most—the unhurriedness of it. They had papers. Crown papers, wax-sealed, declaring the Order of the Spoken Blade dissolved for treasons unnamed, its members subject to judgment at arms. Judgment at arms. Someone in the capital had a gift for phrases that sound lawful and mean slaughter.

Master Corvane met them in the yard alone. He told us to stay inside. Most of us did, because he was Master Corvane and obedience was the first thing they taught us: *an oath unkept begins as an instruction ignored.* I stood at the dormitory window with Wren Halloway beside me, and we watched our master speak to men with crossbows, and I saw his lips moving—not arguing, not pleading. Speaking an oath. Into the ground, into the stones of the yard, into the very air, though there was no blade in his hand to carry it.

I know now what he was doing. He was buying time. Unbound speech is weak speech—it fades in minutes—but even faded, it made the grey cloaks' crossbow strings rot loose and their bootlaces crawl like snakes, and while they stumbled and cursed and cut themselves free, Corvane turned and shouted one word at the dormitory, one word in the First Tongue that meant *run* or maybe meant *live*, I've never been sure which, and both meanings were true, which is the way of that language.

Wren ran. I ran. Half the apprentices ran, out the back, down the goat trails, into the trees.

I got maybe four hundred yards before I understood that running wasn't surviving. The grey cloaks had posted riders on the lower paths. I heard the horns. I heard—this is the part I don't say aloud, the part I keep below the waterline where even I don't look at it—I heard Wren stop behind me. She'd twisted an ankle on the scree, and she called my name, once, quietly, the way you call someone when you trust them absolutely to come back.

And I kept running.

There. It's said. Four hundred yards of mountain between us, and I kept running, because I was fifteen and fast and frightened, and because some cold clerk in my chest did the arithmetic: one of us can make the treeline, and it will be me.

I made the treeline. I lived. Wren didn't, and neither did thirty-eight others, and the arithmetic has never once come out even in twenty years of recounting it.

* * *

But that isn't the oath I broke. Everyone runs; running is just fear wearing boots. The oath came later, and it came from my own mouth.

Three weeks after the purge, starving, half-frozen, I was taken up by crown soldiers on the lowland road. They knew what I was—or suspected. A girl alone, mountain-born, hands scarred in the pattern of forge-work that only the order taught. They put me in a room with a man in grey, not armor-grey, cloth-grey, the color of a sky that can't decide, and he was kind to me. That's the detail that shames me most, looking back. Not the threat. The kindness. He gave me soup. Real soup, with meat in it, after three weeks of bark and stolen apples, and I sat there holding the bowl with both hands like it was a holy relic, and I would have signed anything in blood for a second bowl.

He didn't ask for blood. He asked for words. Just this, he said: swear before witnesses that you renounce the order, its teachings, its oaths, and its masters, living and dead, and name no names of any who escaped, and you walk out of here a free woman with a crown stipend and a trade license.

A trade license. I can laugh about that now, in the flat hollow way you laugh at the shape of your own cage. They bought the last apprentice of the Spoken Blade for the price of a blacksmith's paperwork.

And I said the words. All of them. Renounce, forsake, surrender. My voice didn't shake. That's what I learned about myself in that room, the lesson underneath the soup: that I could say anything, if saying it kept me alive. There is a door inside a person, and I found mine that day, and I have never been able to close it since. I know exactly where it is. I feel it swing every time I lie to a customer about a price, every time I tell a grieving widow her husband's plow couldn't be saved when really I broke it through carelessness, every small comfortable dishonesty of an ordinary life—and behind the door, always, is that grey room, and the soup, and my own steady voice saying *renounce.*

I named no names. For whatever that's worth, and it isn't worth much. There was no one left to name by then except the ones already ash.

Except one.

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, the stone I swallow every morning with my bread: I knew Emrys Winterbourne survived.

Not for certain. But a year after the purge, a tinker came through Lowmarket with mountain-made knife blades for sale, folded steel, beautiful work, quenched in a way that sang faintly against my tongue when I held one—a maker's habit, feeling for the resonance that means a blade has been spoken to. Only one person alive worked steel like that. Only one person alive had hands that left that particular signature in a quench.

I said nothing. I finished buying two knives, haggled the tinker down, and watched him walk west toward the Thornfells, and I carried the knowledge of where she was like a coal in my chest for twenty years. Never writing it down, never saying it, but keeping it. Keeping it the way you keep a debt you have no intention of paying, because the debt is all you have left of the person you owed it to.

If the grey cloaks had ever come to Lowmarket asking, I don't know what I'd have said. That's the honest horror of it. I'd like to believe I'd have lied. But I have stood in that grey room before, in my memory, holding the bowl, and I know how steady my voice gets when staying alive is on the table.

So when she walked into my forge two days ago—older, grey-streaked, harder, but with the same way of standing like the ground owed her rent—I didn't see a legend returned. I saw a witness. I saw the one person in the world whose existence proved, daily and specifically, what a coward I am.

* * *

She asked me to come with her. Of course she did. Standing in my own forge with snow melting off her cloak, holding the hilt-fragment wrapped in oilcloth like it was a sleeping snake, she asked, in that flat reasonable voice I remembered from training drills: *Sable. The Unbinding needs seven vessels. I need a smith who knows what steel is for.*

And I said no.

I said no the way I've practiced saying it for twenty years, to every ghost that visits the forge at closing time. No, I have a business. No, I have obligations. No, I am not that person anymore. All true. All lies. The truest sentence was the one I kept behind my teeth: *if I go with you, I'll have to be brave, and I used up all my bravery at four hundred yards on a scree slope in the harvest-smoke season, running away from a girl calling my name.*

Emrys took the refusal better than I deserved. She just looked at me—she has a look, always had it, the listening look, like she could hear the oath under my skin and was noting its cracks—and she said, "Then keep the door shut, Sable. Locks are honest work." And went back to the inn, taking the child with her. The mute one. Veyra.

The child. Gods, the child. She came into the forge that first evening while Emrys was negotiating bed-space, and she stood over my anvil and touched a half-finished hinge, and her fingers moved—shaping words, silent, in the air, the way our masters taught us to practice before our voices settled enough for real speech. And the hinge, ordinary iron, cheap stock, hummed. One note, low, like a struck bell heard through a wall.

Twenty years I've quenched steel and heard nothing. The order's gone, the speech is gone, the world has moved on. And a mute child touches my workbench and my iron *remembers how to sing.*

I haven't slept properly since.

* * *

So tonight I'm doing the worst thing a coward can do, which is a brave thing done in small denominations, hoping nobody notices.

I pulled the good billet from under the floor. Folded steel, mountain ore, the stock I've been saving for twenty years against a day I told myself would never come. I've had it since before the purge—no. That's a lie, and the door swings, and behind it the grey room waits. I bought it eleven years ago, from a tinker, from the same caravan routes she uses, and I told myself it was just good stock. It was never just good stock. It was a votive offering to a guilt I refused to name. Cowards are the most pious people alive; we're always leaving offerings at altars we pretend aren't there.

I heated it slow. I worked it long past midnight, and my hands knew every pass, and the forge-light made the shapes of forty-one people in the corners of the room, and I let them watch. This wasn't a blade. Blades are for the ones who stayed. This was a scabbard—for the fragment she carries, the hilt of Master Corvane's own sword, which I recognized the moment the oilcloth fell open, because I'd polished it a hundred times as an apprentice, because every one of us had, because touching it was considered an honor and a burden and we fought over the chore.

A scabbard is a humble thing. It holds no edge, speaks no oaths, wins no glory. Its whole purpose is to keep the dangerous thing safe until the moment it's needed, and then get out of the way. I have thought, some nights, that it's the only honest ambition I've ever had: to be the thing that protects the sharp truth without pretending to be it.

So I forged the throat and the chape, and I shaped the wood core from ash—Corvane's tree, the order's tree, I had a piece of it, don't ask me why, cowards keep relics too—and I lined it with leather I'd cured myself, and at the last, when everything was fitted and the metal was cooling to that deep even grey, I held the unfinished scabbard in both hands over the anvil and I did the thing I swore in a grey room I would never do again.

I spoke to the steel.

Not First Tongue. I don't have it, never truly had it, my gift was always iron rather than language—half-oath, half-heard, the reason I was apprenticed late and ranked low. What I have is older and smaller: the smith's whisper, the one we used on blades that had to be trusted with a life. Three words of craft-speech, unbound, carrying nothing but intent. *Hold fast. Keep faith. Endure.*

The scabbard drank the words the way dry ground drinks rain. The iron warmed under my palms—one heartbeat, two—and then, faintly, from somewhere in the grain of it, something answered. Not a voice. A resonance. Like the hinge the child touched. Like every piece of order-steel I've ever handled, all of it waiting, all of it remembering, patient as buried seed.

And I sat down on my stool in the empty forge and cried like a fifteen-year-old on a scree slope, and no one came, because no one ever comes, because I built my whole life so that no one would ever again be close enough to hear.

* * *

At dawn I wrapped the scabbard in oilcloth and walked to the inn with it under my arm, and my legs wanted to go anywhere else, and I made them do as they were told, which is the one discipline cowards excel at.

Emrys was in the common room, awake, of course, watching the door the way she always watches doors. The child sat across from her, eating bread with the total concentration of the young. I put the bundle on the table between us and stepped back, hands open, showing them empty, which is what you do when you approach a wolf or an old comrade.

"What's this?" Emrys said.

"Scabbard. For the fragment. Fitted to the hilt—I measured it last night while you slept, don't glare, I put it back exact." My voice was doing the steady thing. The grey-room voice. I hated it. "Ash core. My own leather. It'll hold the piece safe against anything short of a direct fire."

She unwrapped it. Turned it over. Ran her thumb along the throat where the fit was true, and I watched her find the three craft-words in the grain, because her face changed—the listening face, the soft and terrible one—and she looked up at me and said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said.

"I'm not coming," I told her. "Don't ask twice. The answer's no, and it stays no."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were thinking it loudly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment between veterans of the same losing war. "Keep your door locked, then."

"That's the idea." I turned to go, because staying one more minute meant the door inside me would swing wide, and I'd say yes, and then I'd have to be brave for longer than four hundred yards.

"Sable." I stopped. Didn't turn. "Why forge it, if you won't carry it?"

And there it was, the question I'd been avoiding all night at the anvil, and I gave her the only true answer I had, the small one, the insufficient one, the one worth exactly what it weighed: "Because somebody should hold the sharp truth safe. Even if it isn't me."

I walked out into the blue morning and the frost squeaked under my boots, and behind me I heard the child make a sound—her first sound since arriving, not a word, just a breath, a note, like the hinge, like the scabbard, like everything in this cursed story that remembers what it was before it broke.

I got ten steps toward my forge. Then I stopped, because down the Lowmarket road, riding hard, mud to the hips, came six riders in cloth the color of a sky that can't decide—and the oldest fear of my life came home to roost, and the door inside me swung open all on its own.

I turned around and walked back toward the inn, and I was already talking before I reached the step, steady-voiced, telling Emrys Winterbourne exactly how many grey cloaks there were and which one of them, gods help me, I recognized.
