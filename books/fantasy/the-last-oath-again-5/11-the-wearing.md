## 11. The Wearing

I have learned to measure my life in the spaces between blinks.

That sounds like poetry. It isn't. It's arithmetic. When it lets me surface—and "lets" is the operative word, the way a jailer *lets* you see the sky through a slit—I get somewhere between sixty and ninety seconds before it notices I'm standing in the doorway of my own skull and slams the door. Ninety seconds. A strong man could run two hundred paces in that time. I could not run at all, the first dozen times, because my legs were not mine and my knees had been trained to a stranger's gait. Now I can move them, a little, when I'm up. I have used my ration of freedom to do such heroic things as stand in my private chapel and breathe.

Tonight the breathing is going badly, because tonight it is talking to me.

It doesn't usually. Talking costs it something—I've never understood what, exactly, but I imagine it's like a man interrupting his own digestion to compliment the chef. Most nights it simply wears me the way I wear a ring: without thought, without gratitude, and without ever once wondering how the gold feels about the arrangement. Tonight, though, something has made it expansive. Something has made it almost cheerful.

Which is, by any honest accounting, the most frightening thing that has happened to me in ten years. And in ten years I have watched myself sign death warrants with my own hand and smile while doing it.

"You're awake," it says, through my mouth, in my voice. It always uses my voice. It says this is courtesy. I suspect it says this because it enjoys the joke.

"I'm awake," I say. My voice from inside sounds strange—thinner than I remember, like a letter written by someone who used to be me. "You noticed."

"It noticed." It corrects me the way a schoolmaster would. We are standing at the window of the royal bedchamber—the real one, not the ceremonial one with the stained glass and the propaganda—looking down over the night city. Ten thousand lamps. Ten thousand people who will kneel tomorrow if I ask them to, because they believe the king never ages, and the king never ages, and therefore the king is blessed, and blessed men are owed obedience. I used to find that reasoning flattering. Then I found out why I don't age. "It has news. It thought you should hear it from itself rather than discover it later, weeping into your pillow. You do so hate surprises."

"I hate being spoken about in the third person while wearing my own face," I said. "But yes. Surprises too."

It laughed. That is the worst part, if you want to know the worst part—not the executions, not the decade stolen, not even the slow horror of watching your hands become instruments. The worst part is that it laughs *well*. Warmly. It laughs like a man who has earned his amusement honestly, and some traitorous chamber of my heart responds to that laugh as though it were mine, because acoustically it is.

"Very well." It turned from the window. In the mirror across the room, I watched my own reflection regard me with an expression I have never worn in my life—patient, ancient, faintly amused, the look of a grandfather at a child who has just discovered the stove is hot. "Ask it."

"Ask what?"

"The question. The one you have been carrying for ten years like a stone behind your breastbone. Ask it, and it will answer, because tonight it is feeling generous, and because—" the reflection smiled— "the answer no longer matters."

I should have refused. Let the record show that some part of me, the part still Aldric of the line of Vessan, wanted to refuse on principle, to deny it even the small theater of my curiosity. But principle is a luxury for men whose time belongs to themselves, and I have not had a free second since the coronation. So I asked.

"What are you?"

*What are you.* Not who. I'd stopped asking *who* years ago, after the third or fourth non-answer. What was a better question anyway. Who implies a name, a mother, a childhood. What admits of older categories.

The reflection tilted my head, considering, and then it did something it had never done. It sat down. On the edge of my bed, hands on my knees, in the posture of a soldier resting between marches, and began to talk.

* * *

"Nine hundred and eleven years ago," it said, "there was a kingdom called Sarrhenn, which you have never heard of, because that was rather the point."

I stood by the window and listened. What else does a prisoner do? I listened the way starving men listen to descriptions of bread.

"Sarrhenn had a king named Osseric. A good king, by the standards of good kings, which is to say he taxed fairly, hanged rarely, and believed sincerely in a god who did not exist. He had one flaw. He was afraid of dying badly. Not of dying—all men are afraid of that—but of dying badly, in a ditch, of a fever, unfinished. He wanted his reign to mean something permanent. So when a wandering priest came to his court speaking of a rite that could bind a king to his crown forever—a rite of speech, a word spoken into gold—he listened."

My mouth went dry. I knew this rite. Every schoolboy in the capital knows the legend of the Swordspeakers, the order that spoke truth into steel and bound oaths into crowns. The order I abolished. The order whose purge I signed.

"He listened," the Regent repeated, savoring it, "and the priest—who was not a priest—taught him the word. And Osseric spoke it into his own crown, alone, unwitnessed, unbalanced by any counter-oath, which is a thing the Speakers would never have permitted and which is precisely why they existed. And something answered."

It paused there. It likes pauses. It uses them the way a surgeon uses a clamp.

"What answered," it said finally, "was me. Or rather, what answered was what I am now, which is not quite what I was then, because I was smaller then. Hungrier. I had crossed a very long dark place to reach that crown, and I came through it thin as a knife. I poured myself into the gap his fear had opened, and I closed it behind me, and I wore Osseric of Sarrhenn for forty-one years. He was the finest king that realm ever had, in those years. Reforms. Canals. Peace. His people loved him. His people buried him weeping when at last I let the body fail—it was old by then, and I had learned everything a body could teach me—and I moved on. Down the mountain, to the next realm, where the next king was afraid of dying badly."

"How many?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded very far away.

The reflection held up my right hand, fingers spread.

"Eleven," it said. "Osseric of Sarrhenn. Maldivek of the Ashen Throne. The twin-kings Corvan and Corvus, who shared me between them for nineteen years, which was an interesting experiment and taught me a great deal about jealousy. Queen Isolda the Unlucky. Your namesake Aldric the Second, whom the histories call Aldric the Wise, and who was wise only because I had nine hundred years of other men's wisdom to lend him. And so on, and so on. Eleven kings. Nine centuries. Four kingdoms that no longer appear on maps, not because they fell, but because I ate their remembering."

"Ate their—"

"Memory," it said, patiently, as though explaining rain to a farmer. "Every realm keeps its memory in certain places. Archives. Chronicles. Songs. The minds of old women. And every realm keeps its memory most densely in its regalia—in the crown, the sword, the seal, the objects that are present at every coronation and every oath, absorbing the weight of every promise ever made in their presence. When I wear a long enough time, I drink those places dry. Not destroyed. Nothing I do destroys. Simply... emptied. A chronicle with all its pages blank but intact. An archive whose shelves hold dust and the shape of books. A kingdom of people who know they had a history the way a man knows he has lost his keys—certain of the absence, unable to describe what's missing." It spread my hands, gesturing at the city below, at the whole sleeping country. "This one is nearly finished. Another twenty years and Vessan will be a nation of well-fed orphans with beautiful empty libraries. They'll be happy. They won't know enough to be otherwise."

And there it was—the thing I had half-known for a decade and never let myself say aloud, even in the privacy of the ninety seconds. Not a demon squatting in a king. A locust. A slow patient locust dressed as a dynasty, moving from realm to realm down the centuries, and the fields it left behind weren't burned. They were *forgotten*, which is worse, because ash at least proves something stood there.

"Why tell me this now?" I asked. "After ten years of silence. Why tonight?"

Because it wanted me to understand, was the first answer. Because it was proud, was the second. But it had already told me the true one, earlier, in that offhand way it deploys like a fencer's feint: *the answer no longer matters.*

"Because something has changed," it said, rising from the bed, and for just a moment—for less than a heartbeat—the reflection in the mirror did not quite match the body making it. There was a flicker, a lag, the way a puppet's shadow moves a hair behind the hand. In that flicker I saw what sits under the shape it borrows. I cannot describe it. I have tried, in the chapel, in the margins of psalters, in letters I burned. The nearest I can come is this: it looked like a throne that had been empty so long it had begun to dream it was a man. "Something has stirred. Far away, in mountains, in mud, in places beneath its notice. A word has begun to wake. Do you know what word?"

"No."

"The Unbinding." It said the word lightly, tasting it, and I felt my own lips form it and hated that too. "There were seven blades once, made to speak it. Seven fragments now. It has spent three hundred years assuming the order that guarded them was ash, and mostly it was right. But ash holds heat longer than you'd think." The reflection smiled again, and this time there was nothing warm in it at all. "Someone has touched a fragment. Somewhere north. Someone remembered."

"And what will you do?"

"What it always does." It walked to the door, unhurried, sovereign in my body, and paused with my hand on the handle. "It has sent riders. Grey cloaks. Speakers who survived its last culling by agreeing to speak only for it—you remember the order, Aldric, you signed its destruction yourself, with this hand, smiling." It flexed my fingers. "They will find whoever is remembering, and they will bring back whatever they find, and the word will go back to sleep, and in twenty years this kingdom will slip down its throat like all the others. Unless."

"Unless what?"

It looked back at me over my own shoulder.

"Unless you interfere. Which you cannot. But it likes to leave the unless standing, Aldric. It likes to watch you look at it. Hope is such a useful seasoning." The door opened onto the corridor, onto the guards who bowed to their king without seeing that no king was in him. "Sleep now. It has a council at dawn, and a hanging at noon, and you will attend both, and you will smile."

* * *

Then I was alone.

Not free—never free—but alone in the way of a man locked in a room while his body walks the world without him. The dark came down, and the ninety seconds ended, and I sank back into the cell at the bottom of myself where I have lived for ten years, and I lay there in the black and did the only work available to me.

I thought.

Nine hundred years. Eleven kingdoms. And in all that time, in all that long grazing across the face of the world, it had never once told any of them anything. Of that I was suddenly, strangely sure. Kings before me must have surfaced as I surface, must have blinked and breathed and wept in their rationed seconds—and none of them got a history lesson. None of them got eleven names and a confession. It had given me the story of Osseric of Sarrhenn because—

Because it was afraid.

Not of me. Never of me; I am a coat it wears. But somewhere north, in snow, someone had touched a piece of seven-hundred-year-old steel, and the oldest thing in the world had felt a draft under a door it had sealed, and it had come to the one soul it owns entirely and talked and talked and talked, filling the dark with its own grandeur, the way frightened men fill silence with song.

It was afraid, and it had told me so without knowing, and that meant the Unbinding was real, and that meant the fragments were real, and that meant that somewhere out there—improbably, absurdly, against every law of the long dark—someone was coming.

I could not send word. I could not write; it reads everything my hand produces. I could not speak; it hears everything my mouth attempts. I had ten years of evidence that a passenger can change nothing.

But I had also, once, been a boy who served in a chapel, before I was a king, and I remembered that chapels keep secrets. Stones remember what is carved into them. And there was a stone in my private chapel, under the altar, that my grandfather had shown me when I was nine and sworn me to silence over, a stone with a cipher older than the Regent's arrival, placed there by a king who had distrusted his own confessor.

I could not send word north.

But I could carve it, in the ninety seconds I am given, a little at a time, night after night, in a language dead nine hundred years, under an altar, in a chapel that the Regent in its contempt has never once entered—because what godless thing needs to pray?

It thinks hope is seasoning.

Let it learn it is mortar.

Somewhere far to the north, that same night, snow was falling on a road I have never seen, on a woman I have never met, walking toward me out of the mountains with a word in her keeping. If she comes—if any of them come—they will find the chapel. They must find the chapel.

I put my hand flat against the cold floor of my cell, the way I used to press my palm against the chapel stone as a boy, and I began, silently, to count the letters of a dead king's name.
