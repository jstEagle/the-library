## 12. Hunters in Grey

The shrine sat where the smugglers' maps said it would, which was the first lie of the day, because nothing on those maps had been true in twenty years and the world had no obligation to start keeping its promises now.

It was a small thing—a ring of standing stones no higher than my hip, furred with moss the color of old copper, with a stone basin at the center that caught rainwater and held it like a grudge. Oath-speakers had built it once, back when there were enough of us to bother building things for travelers. Travelers would leave a word in the basin. Not a prayer. A word. Something true they needed to set down because carrying it any farther would break their backs.

I knelt at the basin out of habit and immediately regretted it, because habits are just ghosts you've agreed to feed.

The water held three words. I could feel them the way you feel a splinter through a glove—faint, foreign, not mine. *Forgive. Wait. Home.* Someone had come through recently. Someone frightened, probably, or grieving, or both, which in my experience were usually the same animal wearing different coats.

"You shouldn't touch it," Veyra said.

She'd taken to sitting on the low stones while we rested, legs folded, watching me the way cats watch things they haven't decided whether to kill. She couldn't speak—not aloud—but she'd developed an entire vocabulary of looks, and this one said: *you are being sentimental and sentiment is how people die.*

"I'm not touching it."

"You're listening." Her hands moved as she signed, quick and precise. We'd built a pidgin of gestures over the past days, half smuggler's cant, half something older that seemed to live in her fingers naturally. "Listening is touching. That's what your Master Corvane used to say."

"That's what Corvane used to say," I agreed, "and look how much good his sayings did him."

Bad joke. Dead master jokes are a specific genre of bad joke, the kind you make when the alternative is feeling the actual weight of the man's absence, and Veyra gave me the look that meant she knew exactly what I was doing and was letting me do it, which from a nine-year-old is a particular kind of mercy.

We'd been descending for four days. The pass road curved down toward the river valleys, and somewhere below us—two days, maybe three if the weather turned—was Lowmarket, the town where I would have to become a person with a name again. I wasn't looking forward to it. But the hilt beneath my floorboards had changed the arithmetic of everything. Six fragments besides mine. Six blades scattered like a broken sentence, and somewhere in the capital, something wearing a king's face was hunting them before I could gather them.

Ferro's warning had come by pigeon two nights back: *riders asking after a girl. grey cloaks. move.*

Grey cloaks.

I hadn't let myself think the words properly until now, kneeling at the basin, and even now I tried to keep them at arm's length. Grey cloaks meant speakers. Speakers who survived the purge and bent the knee afterward. The order had been thirty-seven strong when the fires started. Eleven had died at Thornfell. Nine had fled beyond counting. The rest—

The rest had made choices. I understood choices. I'd made one myself, the night I walked into the mountains and buried my blade under my floor and told myself survival was a virtue.

But there's surviving, and there's serving. And the ones in grey had crossed that line so thoroughly that the order's records—the few pages Sable's letters had preserved—listed them not as traitors but as *unmade*. Spoken hollow. Whatever oath they carried now, it wasn't theirs.

"They'll find us," Veyra signed. Not a question. A child doing arithmetic with deadlier numbers than any child should hold.

"Eventually. Everyone finds everyone eventually. The trick is deciding what they find when they get there." I stood, knees cracking like green firewood. Twenty years of mountain living keeps you hard but doesn't keep you young, and the descent was doing things to my joints that felt personal. "Come on. There's a shepherd's track below the shrine. It runs along the ridge instead of over it—we'll be harder to see."

Veyra slid off her stone and fell into step behind me, which was her way of saying she trusted my feet even if she didn't trust my judgment. Fair enough. My judgment had gotten her chased across half a kingdom.

* * *

The shepherd's track was exactly as miserable as I remembered, which was almost comforting. Some things stay reliably awful, and there's a strange peace in that. The path hugged the mountainside above a drop that would kill you twice—once on the way down, once on the way to caring—and clumps of gorse grabbed at our ankles with the enthusiasm of small angry dogs.

By midday we could see the valley opening below: the river a bright scar of meltwater, the ferry crossing a smudge in the distance. Ferro's territory. If Ferro had kept his word about the horses he owed me, we could be at Lowmarket by tomorrow dusk.

That was when Veyra stopped walking.

Not dramatically. She just stopped, mid-stride, one hand rising like she'd walked into a wall of air. Her head tilted. And I watched her face do something I'd only seen once before, the night she'd arrived at my door—something going out of it, some essential light dimming behind the eyes.

"What?" I asked. "What is it?"

Her hand came up. *Listen.*

I listened. Wind in the gorse. A hawk somewhere, complaining about its afternoon. Water running under the rocks. And underneath all of it—

Underneath it, faint as a word spoken through a wall, something that wasn't sound at all. A vibration in the air that my bones understood before my ears did. Steel. Carried steel, moving steel, and moving *wrong*—with that particular cadence I knew from twenty years ago, when I'd stood in a courtyard and watched men walk toward me with drawn swords and oaths in their mouths that weren't theirs.

Blades being carried by people who weren't carrying them honestly.

"Off the path," I said, and my voice came out flat and calm, which surprised me, because inside I was already running.

We went up instead of down, scrambling through the gorse toward a shelf of rock I remembered from another life—a hollow beneath an overhang where smugglers used to stash goods, back when I ran these roads with a name and a purpose. It wasn't comfortable. It wasn't hidden well. But it was high ground with a sightline to the track, and in a fight, high ground and a view beat comfort every time.

We waited.

An hour. Two. The hawk moved on. Veyra pressed against my side, and I put my arm around her, and I realized I was doing the thing I did with my sword-hand without thinking—flexing it open and closed, open and closed, like a door testing its hinges.

There is a version of this story where I tell you I spent those hours praying, or planning, or composing noble thoughts about protection and sacrifice. Here is the truth instead: I spent those hours remembering how to kill.

Because that's what it is, when you've done it before. Not learning. Remembering. Your body keeps the ledger even after your mind burns the book. Every angle, every distance, every way a body can be unmade by another body—I'd spent twenty years trying to bury all of it, and it took four hours of waiting in the gorse to dig it all back up, whole and gleaming and eager, like it had never left.

And the worst part, the part I still can't say without my stomach turning, is that some part of me was *glad*.

Glad to be afraid in a familiar way. Glad to have the ambiguity gone. For twenty years I'd lived with a shapeless dread, a sense of being hunted by history itself, and now here was the history, wearing grey cloaks and carrying stolen steel, and fear finally had a face and I could finally hate something specific.

I hated myself for the gladness. I hated it more than I feared the hunters.

Then the first of them crested the ridge.

* * *

There were five.

They came along the shepherd's track in single file, moving with the unhurried economy of people who know exactly where they're going. Grey wool cloaks, travel-stained. Hoods back despite the wind. And every one of them wore a blade, and every blade was singing the wrong song—that flat, hollow note I'd heard from across the mountainside, steel that had been *spoken to*, filled with someone else's words.

I knew two of their faces.

That's the thing nobody warns you about, surviving a purge. You assume the dead will haunt you. Nobody tells you the living will haunt you worse. The tall one in front, grey at the temples, a scar bisecting his chin—he'd been Maren's student. Quiet boy. Used to blush when Master Corvane quoted poetry. His name was Dellis. He'd been nineteen at Thornfell. He should have been forty-one now, teaching students of his own somewhere, embarrassing himself with poetry.

The woman at his right shoulder had been in my own cohort. Petra. She'd sworn her half-oath beside me in the same ceremony, same season, same rain. I remembered the exact sound her voice had made reciting the words, and I remembered thinking, *she will be better at this than I am.* She'd been right. She'd been so much better at this than I am that she'd survived by becoming whatever wore her now.

Five hunters. One of me. One child whose only weapon was a language that cost her years of her life every time she used it.

The arithmetic was bad. I'd done bad arithmetic before. The difference was that last time, the answer had been *run*, and running had cost me everything except my life, and I'd spent two decades telling myself that trade had been worth it.

This time I had a child pressed against my ribs who smelled of woodsmoke and fear, and the answer wouldn't come out as *run*. I turned it over and over in my mind and it kept coming out wrong.

Below us, the five stopped at the shrine. Of course. The basin. Words left in water leave residue; any speaker could read them. They crouched around the stones, and I watched Dellis—the quiet boy who blushed at poetry—dip two fingers into the basin and lift them dripping, and I watched his face go smooth and empty as he read whatever stranger had passed through.

He said something to the others. Too far to hear. But I saw the direction he pointed, and my stomach dropped through the mountain, because he pointed up-ridge. Toward us. Not precisely at us—toward the route we *would* take, the logical line of travel—and I understood with sick clarity that they weren't tracking footprints. They were reading intention. The Regent's corruption let them taste purpose in a place the way I tasted words in water.

"They know," Veyra breathed. Her hands barely moved. "They know we came here."

"They know someone did." My mouth was dry. "Words don't carry names unless you give them."

"You gave them a word."

Had I? I'd only knelt. Only listened. But listening leaves traces too—that was the whole doctrine of my order, the First Tongue's terrible symmetry: every act of speech changes the speaker, and every act of hearing changes the heard. I'd put my ear to that basin and the basin had put its ear to me.

Dellis straightened. Said something else. And the five of them began to climb.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next ten minutes, in the order my memory insists on, which is not chronological:

Petra's face when she saw me. Not surprise. Recognition, and then—this is the part I keep returning to, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it—*relief*. Brief, flickering, quickly smoothed away by whatever rode her, but there. Like a drowning woman seeing another drowning woman and being glad, just for a heartbeat, that she isn't alone.

The gorse tearing at my sleeves as I moved to meet them rather than wait to be found, because waiting is dying slowly and meeting is dying quickly, and between those two options I have always chosen fast.

The sound Dellis's blade made leaving its sheath—not the clean whisper of honest steel but a wet, consonant hiss, like a word being mispronounced.

Veyra's hands, signing *no no no* against her chest, and me putting my body between her and the world, which is the oldest oath there is and the only one I never broke.

And then—fighting.

I won't dress it up. Fighting is ugly, graceless work, all elbows and panic and breath you can't get enough of. Twenty years of rust fell off me in the first three seconds and what was underneath was exactly what had been there before: competent. Efficient. Trained by masters who understood that elegance is for tournaments and survival belongs to butchers.

Dellis came first, uphill, blade leading, and his form was perfect and his heart wasn't in it—I could see that, some flicker behind his eyes, the quiet boy still in there somewhere watching his own hands move. I should have used that. A word to his blade, a hook of the First Tongue, anything to reach the man inside the grey. I had trained for exactly that. Unmaking a corrupted binding was third-year curriculum.

I didn't reach for it.

I hit him instead. Pommel to the temple, hard, dropping him like a sack of grain, and even as he fell I knew why I'd done it: because words are slow and fists are fast, and because some cowardly cellar of my soul wanted the fight settled with my body rather than my voice. Because using the First Tongue meant admitting what I was, and I'd spent twenty years being no one.

Petra came second, and Petra was not holding back.

Her blade found my forearm, then my shoulder—shallow cuts, testing cuts, the work of someone reading my guard the way I read water. Good. So good. Better than me, even now, even hollowed-out. She drove me back along the shelf with a sequence I recognized from our shared training, and there was something obscene about it, fighting someone who knew the same forms, the same counters, the same rhythm drilled into us by the same dead masters. It was like arguing with my own reflection, if my reflection wanted me dead.

She had me at the overhang's edge. I felt the drop behind my heels, heard the gorse rustling far below, and Petra smiled—or the thing wearing Petra smiled, wide and wrong—and raised her blade for the finishing line.

And Veyra screamed.

Not aloud. Into her palms, into the space between us, a word in the First Tongue shaped by hands alone—and the word hit Petra's blade like a hammer hits a bell. The steel *rang*, one clear note that cut through everything, and Petra staggered, clutching her weapon, and the wrongness in her face cracked open for half a second. Half a second of Petra, my cohort-mate, my sister-in-oath, looking at me with absolute naked horror at herself.

Then the wrongness closed over her again, and she ran. Signaled the others. Three figures broke downhill, dragging Dellis's unconscious body between them, and the ridge was suddenly, absurdly quiet.

I stood at the edge of the drop, bleeding from two places, breathing like a bellows, and I waited to feel victorious.

What I felt instead arrived slowly, the way cold water finds the seams in your clothes.

I counted. That's what I did. Standing there in the wreckage of the ambush, I counted what I'd done: dropped a man who used to blush at poetry. Driven off a woman whose half-oath I'd witnessed with my own eyes. Drawn blood—mine and possibly theirs—and driven children of my own order into flight, deeper into the thing that owned them. And when the moment came to reach for the tool that might have *freed* them instead of merely beaten them, I'd chosen my fists, because my fists asked nothing of me.

I had killed no one today. That was the miracle, the fluke, the gift of Veyra's screaming hands. But I had killed in my heart, swiftly and easily, within the first three seconds, and the ease of it was the horror. Twenty years of exile and the butcher was still living in the house. She'd just been keeping the lights off.

"Emrys." Veyra tugged my sleeve. Her hands shook as she signed. "You're bleeding."

"So I am." I sat down heavily on a stone, which was less dignity and more surrender. The cuts stung with the particular honesty of shallow wounds. "Come here. Let me look at your hands."

Her palms were raw where she'd shaped the word. Blisters already rising in the exact pattern of the syllable, as if the language had branded her. Each word cost her—I'd known that since chapter one of this disaster, since the night she collapsed at my door—and I looked at her damaged hands and thought: *this child pays in flesh for every word, and I pay nothing for my violence, and somehow I'm the one who gets to call myself the protector here.*

"It worked," she signed, wincing as I wrapped her palms with strips of my shirt.

"It worked."

"You didn't use the words. Why didn't you use the words?"

And there it was, the question, delivered by a nine-year-old with blistered hands, cutting closer than any grey cloak had managed all day.

"Because," I said, and stopped. Started again. "Because using them means being what I was. And being what I was is how everyone I loved ended up dead."

Veyra considered this with the terrifying gravity of children, who have not yet learned to pretend difficult things are simple.

"But you *are* what you were," she signed finally. "You just keep hiding it in your fists."

From the mouths of babes, etcetera. I laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it—and bound the last strip tight.

We couldn't stay on the ridge. The hunters would regroup, report, return with more. Down was the only direction, down toward Ferro's ferry and the valley and Lowmarket and whatever waited for me in the town that had forgotten my name. I repacked our bundle with hands that were perfectly steady, because apparently my body had decided the crisis was over and could resume being a tool.

One thing I did before we left. I climbed back to the shrine, knelt at the basin despite Veyra's alarmed expression, and spoke a word into the water.

One word. My first in twenty years, spoken aloud, into plain rainwater where no blade could hear it and no king could wear it.

*Coming.*

Let them read it. Let the Regent choke on it. I was done hiding in my fists.

Veyra watched me do it, and for the first time since the ambush, she smiled.

* * *

We made the valley by dark.

Ferro's ferry sat moored at the crossing, lantern lit—which meant either hospitality or a trap, and after today I'd lost the ability to tell the difference. Ferro himself stood on the deck, a barrel of a man backlit by his own lamp, and even at fifty paces I could see he was agitated in the specific way of a man carrying news too heavy for his frame.

Behind him, tied at the dock rail, stood two horses. The ones he owed me.

And beside the horses, arms folded, watching us descend the bank with an expression I could read at any distance because I'd helped forge it twenty years ago—

Sable.

My former apprentice. The blacksmith who broke her own half-oath. Standing at a river crossing she had no business knowing about, at exactly the hour we needed her, in boots that had clearly walked a long way to get here.

"Ferro sent a pigeon," she called, when we were close enough. "Said you'd be coming down the ridge with trouble behind you. Said I should decide whether to be here when you arrived." She unfolded her arms. In the lantern light I could see the burn-scar on her left wrist, the one from the purge, the one she'd never explained and I'd never asked about. "Took me three days to decide. Then I heard what happened on the ridge."

My blood went cold. "You heard—"

"A peddler came through at noon. Talking about five grey cloaks asking questions at the shrine, and a fight, and—" Sable's jaw tightened. "And a child's word that rang a sword like a bell."

She looked at Veyra then, really looked, and something moved across her face that I couldn't name. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"Emrys," she said quietly. "They know about her now. What she can do. By tomorrow, every hunter the Regent has will know a word-speaker is traveling the Thornfell roads with the last swordspeaker of the old order." She stepped aside, gesturing at the horses, at the ferry, at the dark water sliding past. "So. Are you going to invite me along, or do I have to follow you the whole way to the capital like a stray?"

I stared at her. At the woman I'd last seen walking away from a burning archive, choosing survival over oath, carrying a shame she'd worn ever since like a coat that didn't fit. At the woman who had, apparently, walked three days to stand at a river crossing on the chance I'd need her.

"You broke your oath," I said. Stupid thing to say. True thing to say.

"Yeah." Sable's eyes didn't waver. "And you buried yours under your floorboards. We've both been cowards, Emrys. Difference is, I got tired of it first."
