## 13. Veyra Speaks

I have been silent for three years, and in that time I have learned that silence is not one thing. It is many things, the way snow is many things — the soft kind, the kind that buries you, the kind that squeaks underfoot like a small animal complaining.

There was the silence of the burning archive, which was really a very loud silence full of cracking wood and screaming people and my own heartbeat trying to escape through my ears.

There was the silence of the road north, which was the kind where nobody speaks because speaking costs too much.

And there was the silence I chose, after the woman pressed the word into my palm and told me never to let it out. That was the heaviest one. That one had weight and shape and edges, like a stone I swallowed and carried in my chest for three years, feeling it turn over whenever I breathed wrong.

Emrys thinks I am mute the way a person is blind — something broken, something to be worked around gently, with hand-signs and patience. She has built an entire vocabulary of glances for me. *Hungry. Tired. Hide. Run.* She's good at it. Better than she knows.

But here is what Emrys doesn't understand, standing in the shrine with her blade red to the hilt and four grey cloaks closing in around us: I am not broken.

I am *full*.

* * *

The shrine sat in a fold of the mountains like a cupped hand, all broken columns and one stubborn bell that rang without wind when it felt like it. Emrys said her order used to stop here on the long roads between oaths. She touched the doorframe when we came in, two fingers only, quick, like touching a wound to check if it still hurts. It still hurts. You could see it on her face.

Then the grey cloaks came out of the treeline, and everything got simpler and worse at the same time.

I should explain about the grey cloaks, because Emrys talks about them like weather — *the hunters, they'll find us eventually, keep moving* — and weather can't be reasoned with, so why describe it? But they're people. That's the horror of them. They were speakers once. They wear the same training in their shoulders that Emrys does, the same way of holding a blade like it might say something back. Except somewhere in the purge, someone reached into them and turned them inside out, and now the words they speak go into blades as commands instead of promises. A speaker's oath-blade sings. A hunter's blade just *obeys*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole world.

There were five of them. Emrys killed the first one before I even understood the fight had started — moved through him the way water moves through cloth, fast and total, and then stood there afterward looking at her own hands like they'd betrayed her twice.

Four left. They drove us up against the shrine wall, into the shadow of the bell, and Emrys fought the way I imagine a mountain would fight if it decided to be angry: slow to start, impossible to stop. She broke one man's knee and put another through the ribs of a fallen column. Two down.

Two left, then, and one of them was fast.

He was young. That's what I noticed, crouched behind the altar with my heart slamming. Younger than Sable, maybe younger than Emrys had been when the world ended for her. He fought like someone who'd been given his anger by somebody else and hadn't yet noticed it didn't fit. His blade was a long grey thing with no shine to it at all, and when he spoke to it — a single syllable, flat as a slammed door — it moved faster than any sword has a right to move.

It caught Emrys across the shoulder. Not deep. Deep enough. She staggered back against the bell-post, and her blade came up late, and I saw the arithmetic happen in her face: *one of me, two of them, and the girl.*

She looked at me. Just for a breath. And her glance said what her glances always say, but this time it said it clearly:

*Run.*

Here is a thing about me that Emrys has not yet learned, because she keeps forgetting to ask and I keep having no way to tell her: I do not run. I ran north for a hundred days with a word burning a hole in my palm and the smoke of my whole life behind me, and I am finished running. Running is for people who have somewhere left to be safe. I checked. There isn't one. The Regent wears the king, and the king is the realm, so the realm itself is the thing chasing me, and you cannot outrun the ground you stand on.

So instead of running, I did something much stupider.

I picked up a piece of broken column — altar-sized, heavy, the kind of stone that means well but moves slow — and I threw it at the young hunter's head.

* * *

I missed.

Of course I missed. I'm nine years old and my arm is a suggestion, not a weapon. The stone went wide and cracked against a pillar, and both hunters turned, and I saw the exact moment they understood what I was. Not a hostage. Not baggage. A girl with no voice traveling with the last swordspeaker in the world.

The older one smiled. It was a terrible smile, patient, the smile of someone who has read the report on me. *The child,* the reports must say. *The vessel. Take it alive.* The Regent wants me alive, and that protection has kept me breathing for three years, and right now, watching that smile, I wanted for the first time in my life to be worth killing.

Emrys shouted my name — my real name, the one I gave her on her floor with mud still freezing in my hair, not the false one from the road — and started forward with blood running down her arm, and the young hunter turned his blade on her, spoke his flat syllable, and the grey sword blurred.

And I understood, with the horrible clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that Emrys was going to die. Not maybe. Not possibly. The blade's obedience was faster than her skill, and skill loses to speed every time, and the mountain-anger could not stop a sword that simply did what it was told.

I had exactly one thing in the world.

* * *

You should know what the word feels like, since I'm about to spend it.

The woman at the archive — Master Ilenne, though she never said the name aloud, and I only know it because I read it off her burned badge later, in the ruins, in the rain — pressed it into my palm with her thumb, hard, the way you'd press a seal into wax. And it went in. Not into the skin. Into whatever is underneath skin, the part of a person that isn't made of meat. It sits behind my sternum like a coal that never cools. For three years I have slept on my side because lying on my back, I can feel it, and feeling it makes me dream about fire.

It is a truth-word. In the First Tongue there are command-words, which break things, and binding-words, which hold things, and truth-words, which do something worse than either: they make things be what they actually are. A truth-word spoken at a lie unmakes the lie. A truth-word spoken at a mask removes it. A truth-word spoken at a blade that pretends to be a sword —

Well.

A blade that obeys instead of sings is a lie wearing a sword's shape. That's what Ilenne taught me, in the last hour of the archive, while the shelves burned: *Every binding is a promise, little one. When men bind words into steel for cruelty instead of covenant, the steel knows. Steel always knows. It is only waiting for someone to say so.*

I have known this for three years. Knowing and daring are different animals that live in the same forest and do not speak to each other.

But Emrys was falling, and the coal behind my sternum was screaming, and I have discovered that I would rather burn up from the inside at nine than watch the only person who ever fed me porridge and pretended not to notice I was crying die on the steps of a dead shrine in the middle of nowhere.

So I opened my mouth for the first time in three years, and I spent the word.

* * *

The First Tongue does not sound like speech. I want to be honest about that, because stories make it sound like thunder or choirs or some grand ringing thing. It sounds like remembering. It sounds like the moment you wake from a dream so true that for a second the waking world seems like the copy. When I spoke, the syllable came out of me shaped like my whole life — the archive, the fire, the hundred days north, the mud on Emrys's doorstep, the porridge, the glances, *hide, run, hungry, tired* — all of it folded into one sound, and the sound went out of me and took some of me with it.

I felt it leave. That's the part no one tells you. Words in the First Tongue are not free. They are cut from you like coins from flesh, and every one takes weight and color and years, and I felt three years of hoarded silence go out of my mouth in a single note —

—and the young hunter's blade stopped.

Not deflected. Not knocked aside. *Stopped*, mid-stroke, the way a bird stops when it hits glass. And then the lie came out of it. That is the only way I can describe what happened next. The grey obedience that had been poured into the steel like tar cracked all along its length, and the steel remembered what it was before someone made it a slave — ore, and fire, and a smith's intention, and nothing else — and it came apart. Not shattered. *Unmade.* It fell off its own edge in flakes of grey ash, and the hilt dropped from the hunter's hand, and the ringing went on and on, off the columns, up into the bell, until the bell above our heads caught the note and answered it, one clear tone like the shrine itself saying *yes.*

The young hunter sat down. He didn't decide to; his legs just quit. He stared at his empty hand, and then at me, and his face did something complicated — fear first, and then, horribly, relief, like a man who has been carrying a boulder for years and finally watched someone smash it. Maybe his blade had been bound into him too. Maybe I unmade more than steel.

The older hunter did not sit down. The older hunter looked at me the way you look at a report that has just rewritten itself, and he made a choice, and his choice was to drop his blade and run, because his orders said take the vessel alive and his orders did not cover vessels that unmake weapons with a word. Cowardice and obedience look identical from the outside. Sometimes they're identical from the inside too.

And then it was quiet, except for the bell, still humming, and my own breathing, which sounded strange and enormous, because it had a voice attached to it now.

* * *

"Veyra."

Emrys was on her knees in the shrine dust, holding her bleeding shoulder, staring at me like I'd come back from the dead. Which, in a way, I had. The mute girl died on those steps. Whatever stood up in her place was going to have to learn to be a person who talks, and I already missed the old one, and I hadn't even finished standing up in the new one.

"You spoke," she said. Then, because Emrys processes shock by getting practical: "Your voice. Three years, and you — how?"

I opened my mouth to answer, and that was when I noticed.

My hands. I held them up, and they were wrong. Not hurt-wrong. *Old*-wrong. The knuckles had gone knobby. A brown spot sat on the back of my left hand that had definitely not been there that morning, and when I flexed my fingers, something in the joints crackled like dry leaves.

Emrys saw it too. I watched her see it — watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"How old are you," she said slowly. Not a question. An autopsy.

I held up nine fingers. Then, because the truth deserved company, I held up the same nine fingers and rotated my wrist so she could see the spotted skin, the stiff joints, the fine lines that had appeared around my eyes sometime in the last minute without asking permission.

One word. One word had cost me — I didn't know how much. Years? A year? Enough that my body had visibly paid it in front of a witness. The coal behind my sternum was quieter now, banked, but I could feel the hollow where it had burned hottest, and the hollow felt old. It felt like the inside of my grandmother's hands, and I had never met my grandmother, but I knew her hands somehow anyway, the way you know things the First Tongue puts in you.

I found my voice again. It came out rusty, low for a child, scraped at the edges, and every syllable cost me something small and irreversible — I could feel it, a faint graying sensation behind my eyes, like frost forming on a window — but some things need saying.

"It's the price," I said. "That's the rule. Ilenne told me. Every word out loud spends life." I swallowed. Speaking felt like spending coins I couldn't earn back. "That's why I didn't talk. It wasn't — I wasn't broken, Emrys. I was saving."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she did not wipe them, and she did not make it small, and I loved her a little bit for that.

"Three years," she said. "You've been carrying that for three years. A word that could do *that*—" she gestured at the ash that had been a sword, "—and you stayed quiet. Through the fever. Through the nightmares. Through me talking at you like you were—" She stopped. Started over. "Why didn't you use it sooner?"

Because it costs, I almost said. Because I was afraid. Because Ilenne made me swear.

But the real answer surprised me, coming out. It came out before I could weigh it, and it cost me another few frost-lines to say, and it was worth it:

"Because I was waiting for something worth it."

Emrys looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere out in the trees, the older hunter's footsteps faded toward the valley, carrying his terrible news south at the speed of terror. We had days, maybe. Hours, if the Regent listens closely — and it does; it has worn eleven kings and it listens the way a spider listens to its web.

"We need to move," Emrys said, hauling herself up with her good arm. "That one will bring everyone. But Veyra—" She hesitated, and then asked the question I could see building in her face since the bell stopped ringing. "The word you spent. The truth-word. Could you speak another one? If you had to?"

I thought about lying to her. It would have been kinder.

"No," I said, and the frost crept a little further. "Not for anything small. Maybe three more. Four, if they're short ones. After that—" I turned my hands over, old and young at once, a nine-year-old wearing borrowed decades like loose gloves. "After that there's no *after*."

Emrys nodded once, slow, filing it away in whatever ledger she keeps behind her eyes. Then she frowned, sudden, looking past me at the shrine wall — at the old carved frieze above the altar, half-crumbled, that neither of us had looked at properly since we arrived. Carved figures. Speakers, kneeling by water. A lake. And beneath the carved lake, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, a shape I recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken by a stranger.

I had seen it before. Not in stone. In the fever-dreams that Emrys thought were nightmares. A monastery under black frozen water, and something gleaming on the bottom, waiting.

"That's where the next fragment is," I said. My voice cracked on the words, and each crack aged me a hair more, and I said them anyway. "Under the lake. I've seen it. I've always seen it."

Emrys turned from the carving to me, and the bell finally went silent overhead, and she said, "Then we're going to a lake."
