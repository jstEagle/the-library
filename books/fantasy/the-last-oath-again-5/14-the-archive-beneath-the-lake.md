## 14. The Archive Beneath the Lake

The lake found us before we found it.

We'd been walking since the grey hour, following a thread only Veyra could see—her fingers twitching at nothing, her eyes going distant and glassy in that way she had when the memories pressed up against the inside of her skull. She'd point left when the path forked left. She'd stop dead when I would have gone right. Twice she made me backtrack half a mile through snow to my knees for no reason I could see, and both times I found the smuggler's cairn hidden under drift, exactly where her hand said it would be.

I'd stopped arguing with her somewhere around dawn. There's a particular humiliation in being navigated by a nine-year-old, and I'd decided to skip it.

"There," she said, and her voice cracked on the word.

I looked up and understood why.

The valley opened below us like a held breath. A lake lay in its bowl, black and utterly still, ringed by pines so heavy with snow they seemed to be bowing toward the water. And rising out of the middle of that water—or rather, standing *in* it, drowned to the shoulders—was a bell tower.

I knew it. Of course I knew it. Every swordspeaker knew the story of Vell Monastery, though we told it the way you tell ghost stories: quickly, quietly, and never after dark. The archive of the First Tongue, where the order kept its oldest records, its deepest bindings, its most dangerous words. It hadn't burned like the rest. It had *sunk*. The old texts say the monks called down the flood themselves rather than let the archive fall into the wrong hands—a whole monastery drowned on purpose, three hundred souls and every book they'd ever copied, gone under in a single night.

The stories disagreed on whether anyone had escaped.

Veyra was already walking down the slope. I caught her sleeve.

"Slowly," I said. "Tell me what you're seeing."

She turned those too-old eyes on me. "A woman," she said. "In fire. She put a word in my hand." She opened her fist as if something might still be there. "And then cold. So much cold. And singing under the ice."

*Singing.* Wonderful. Drowned monasteries that sang. My twenties were full of decisions I regretted less than this one was starting to look.

"Emrys." She said my name carefully, the way she said all her words now—each one costing her something I tried not to count. Since the shrine, since her first scream had shattered steel and years alike, every syllable she spoke aged her a little. A new line at her eye. A certain gravity in her jaw that no child should carry. I'd started doing the arithmetic without meaning to, and I'd started hating myself for it. "The fragment is there. Under the water. She showed me."

"Of course it is," I muttered. "Why would anything in this quest be dry?"

* * *

We made camp above the shoreline, in the lee of a granite shelf, and I spent an hour doing the thing I do instead of praying: taking inventory. Rope. Flints. The hilt-fragment wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, humming faintly whenever Veyra came near, like a dog hearing another dog two streets away. A hatchet. Half a wheel of cheese going hard. My own reflection in the flat of Sable's scabbard, looking older than I felt and more frightened than I'd admit.

The lake didn't freeze at the edges. That was the first wrong thing. It was cold enough that morning to crack stones, and the shallows should have worn a rind of ice like every other pool we'd crossed since Thornfell. But the water lay open and black right up to the stones, breathing a mist that didn't move with the wind, because there wasn't any wind.

"The monks," Veyra said, watching it beside me. "They're keeping it warm."

"That's not how lakes work."

"They're not really a lake anymore either."

I looked at her. She shrugged, a small, terrible gesture, the shrug of someone reporting weather.

I've been asked, since, how I could dive into that water knowing what I knew. The honest answer is: because the alternative was admitting that twenty years of hiding had been for nothing, and I would have done almost anything—including something stupid and wet and haunted—before accepting that.

The second answer is that Corvane's voice had been getting louder in my head since the quarry, and he'd started saying things like *the water is just water, Emrys; it's the dead who are heavy,* which is exactly the sort of thing a ghost says when he wants you to do something foolish.

"Stay here," I told Veyra. "If I'm not back by the time the sun clears the ridge—"

"I know. Run south. Find Sable."

"If I'm not back by then, run south anyway. Don't wait to find anyone."

She nodded. Then, because she was Veyra, she took my face in both her mittened hands and said, in the First Tongue, one soft syllable I didn't yet have translation for but understood anyway. It meant something like *come back*, and something like *you are mine to lose now*, and I felt it settle into my chest like a coal.

Then I stripped to my shift, tied the rope around my waist, and walked into the memory of three hundred drowned monks.

* * *

The cold was not cold. That's the only way I can describe it. It went past cold the way white goes past bright—it stopped being a sensation and became a fact about the universe. I am in water. The water is the absence of everything. Continue.

I swam down along the bell tower, hand over hand on the ancient stone, and the lake began to remember.

It didn't show me visions, exactly. It was worse than that. It gave me *names*. They arrived whole in my mind, unbidden, the way you remember a word you weren't looking for: Brother Aldous, who fed the poor of three valleys and drowned holding a child above the waterline until his arms failed. Sister Maren, who had argued for burning the archive instead of drowning it, and who went down last, reading. The novice Tam, fourteen years old, who had volunteered to stay because someone had to ring the bell as the water rose, so that God would know where they were.

On and on. Hundreds of them. The lake did not accuse me. It simply *told* me, relentlessly, gently, the way grief tells you things at three in the morning, and it became very clear very fast that this was the price of passage. Not blood. Not magic. Attention. The dead of Vell wanted what all the dead want—to be remembered—and the lake had become the instrument of their remembering, and I was swimming through their memorial, and I could not make it stop.

I tried to shut it out. I'm good at shutting things out; it was practically my trade for two decades. It didn't work. The names came faster, and with them came faces, and with the faces came the moment of each drowning—not as horror, strangely, but as *fact*: the last thing each of them saw, thought, loved. Brother Aldous saw the child's mother on the far shore. Sister Maren saw the page she'd been reading. Tam saw the bell rope in his hands and felt, in his final instant, enormous pride.

By the time I reached the tower door—thirty feet down, maybe more, the light gone green-black and strange—I was sobbing into my own regulator of will, gasping air I didn't have, weeping for people four centuries dead, and I want to tell you I did it gracefully. I did not do it gracefully. I clung to the stone lintel like a drowning woman, which is precisely what I was, and I heard Corvane's voice, clear as a bell across a courtyard:

*Let them be seen, Emrys. That's all remembrance is. Let them be seen.*

So I stopped fighting. I hung there in the dark water and I let them be seen, name by name, and I said—mouthed, into the water, stupidly, humanly—"I see you. I see you. I see you." Three hundred times, or however many it was, until the pressure in my skull changed from demand to... welcome. Until the names stopped arriving like blows and started arriving like hands on my shoulders, guiding me down.

The tower door stood open. Of course it did. Everything about this place was an invitation.

Inside, the scriptorium had been preserved by the cold and the dark and whatever the monks had done with their last working. Desks still stood in rows. Books lay open on them, the ink long since bloomed into grey clouds, pages furred with silt. And in the center of the room, on a lectern built for exactly this purpose, under a dome of glass that somehow still held—

—the fragment.

Not a blade this time. A *page*. Or what had been a page once: a sheet of some material that wasn't paper and wasn't metal, dense as lead and thin as a leaf, covered edge to edge in First Tongue script that moved when I looked at it directly. It sat in its glass case like a heart in a jar, and around it, the whole drowned room leaned inward slightly, the way a crowd leans toward a speaker.

I broke the case. I'm not proud of the vandalism; I suspect the monks would have understood. The moment my fingers closed on the page, two things happened at once.

First: the hilt against my chest sang—one clear note through oilcloth and wool and ribs, harmonizing with the page in my fist, and the sound in the water was so beautiful I nearly lost my grip on the rope.

Second: the lake went silent.

All those names, all that gentle relentless telling—gone. The silence was so total I panicked for a moment, thinking I'd died, thinking this was what death was: the memorial ending, the attention withdrawn. Then I understood. The dead weren't finished. They were *listening*. Three hundred drowned monks, waiting to hear what I would do with what they'd kept safe for four hundred years.

No pressure.

I kicked for the surface with the page pressed to my chest and the rope burning my waist, and the names came back as I rose—but different now. Not demands. Blessings. *Go well, swordspeaker. Go well. Go well.* And beneath them, fainter, older, in a voice I recognized with a jolt that nearly cost me the fragment:

*Well done, Emrys.*

Corvane. His voice, in the drowned dark, sounding—for the first time in twenty years—young.

* * *

I came up coughing into air that felt like a gift, and Veyra hauled me onto the stones with a strength that would have shamed a dockworker, and then she saw what I carried and went absolutely rigid.

"It's a page," I managed, teeth chattering hard enough to loosen fillings I probably no longer had. "The fragment is a *page*. From the archive itself."

She took it from me with the reverence of someone handling a relic, which, I suppose, we'd established it was. As her skin touched it, her lips moved silently, reading. Then she looked up at me with an expression I'd learned to dread: the expression of someone about to spend words she couldn't afford.

"What does it say?" I asked quickly. "Don't—don't speak it. Just tell me."

Her finger traced the moving script. When she answered, her voice came out lower than before, older, and I watched a fine new line etch itself at the corner of her mouth even as she spoke.

"It's not a spell," she said. "It's a *witness list*. Names of everyone the First Tongue ever bound. Kings. Oathmasters. Speakers." Her finger stopped near the bottom, on a cluster of marks fresher than the rest. "And here. Written recently. In the last ten years."

"Written by whom?"

She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known her, the child looked genuinely afraid—not of hunters, not of drowning, not of the thing wearing the king.

"This ink isn't ink," she said. "It's written in the same hand as the rest. Four-hundred-year-old hand. But these names are new." She turned the page toward me. "Someone has been adding to the witness list, Emrys. Someone down *here*. Recently."

The lake behind us lay black and still. The bell tower stood in it, patient as a grave.

And from somewhere beneath the water—faint, unmistakable, impossible—came the sound of a bell ringing once, deep down where no wind could reach it, and a voice that was not Corvane's saying clearly, in the First Tongue, a single word I now knew well enough to translate:

*Stay.*
