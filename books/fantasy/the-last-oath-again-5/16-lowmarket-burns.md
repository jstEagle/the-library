## 16. Lowmarket Burns

The trouble with living in a town that forgets things, I've learned, is that it also forgets how to be afraid.

Lowmarket forgot me in a week. Forgot the grey riders who passed through in autumn. Forgot the price of salt after every harvest and the name of every ferryman who ever drowned. It's a town built on forgetting—smugglers' roads will do that to a place—and for twenty years that suited me fine. Forget Sable the oath-breaker. Remember only Sable of the good hinges and honest prices.

But some things a town should never forget, and the smell of smoke is one of them.

I smelled it before I heard anything. That's the blacksmith's curse—you learn smoke like a wife learns a husband's cough. Forge smoke is dry and mineral, friendly. Hearth smoke is fat and slow. This was neither. This was thatch and timber and lamp oil, and underneath it, faint as a lie told badly, something sweeter.

Incense.

I came out of my forge with my apron still on and saw the sky over the east quarter gone the color of an old bruise, and I thought—not *they're here*, because you never think that first. You think *whose house?* You think *did anyone bank their hearth wrong?* The mind reaches for small stupid kindnesses before it lets you have the truth.

Then the screaming started, and the truth stopped being negotiable.

They came down the Low Road at dusk, six riders in grey cloaks, and they didn't ride like soldiers. Soldiers burn a town because someone ordered it and feel bad about it after, or don't, but either way there's a shape to it, a discipline. These ones rode like men doing arithmetic. House by house. Door by door. One of them carried a ledger.

A ledger. I want you to understand that. He had a book, and he checked names off while the town he'd written them in went up around him.

I didn't run toward the fire. Let's be honest about what I am: I'm a woman who ran from one purge already, who signed a paper with her left hand while her right hand shook, who traded a half-oath for a whole life. I ran toward my forge, because the forge was mine and because Emrys Winterbourne and the child had left through it three days past, and if the grey cloaks were here, then the trail led through me, and everything they'd need to follow it farther was sitting in my keeping.

The fragment. Her hilt-piece, wrapped in oilcloth under my quenching trough, singing its quiet note whenever I got too close, like a tuning fork buried alive.

And my scabbard—the one I'd forged for it, quenched not in water but in the last of my broken oath-blade's memory, though I hadn't let myself think those words until that night.

I pulled up the trough board with hands that knew the work even when the rest of me was somewhere else entirely. Oilcloth. Iron. That low hum against my palms, patient and furious at once, the way a held breath is furious.

Behind me, the Cooper's Row went up like a festival.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the purge, the real one, twenty years back—the one I don't tell, the one I paid to survive. Not the flames. Flames are easy; everyone remembers flames. What I remember is the silence afterward. How forty swordspeakers burned or broke in a single night at Corvane's hold, and how the ones of us scattered in the outer houses woke to no orders, no signal, nothing—just absence, the way you wake knowing a storm passed over while you slept. How I sat with my own blade across my knees, a half-made thing, an apprentice's work, and heard boots on my stairs, and made my choice in the space between one step and the next.

I put the blade in the cold forge. I opened the door. I said, "There's no one here but a smith."

Which was true, by then. That's the filthiest part. I didn't lie. I just rearranged myself into truth's shape and let them walk through me to everyone else.

So when I say I know retribution, know it like grain knows the millstone, believe me. And when I tell you that watching Lowmarket burn did not make me afraid—

It made me *finished.*

There's a moment past fear where the body stops negotiating. I found it standing in my doorway with a wrapped hilt in one hand and a hammer in the other, watching a grey cloak drag old Marta Voss out of the cooperage by her hair because her grandson had once sold apples to two travelers. Old Marta, who'd forgotten my name along with everybody else, and forgotten it kindly.

The rider with the ledger looked up and saw me. Saw a smith, broad and soot-dark, standing in an unburnt doorway. He started toward me, pen still in hand, and asked me the question they always ask:

"Two travelers. A woman past fifty, hard-used, and a girl who doesn't speak. Which way did they go?"

And I heard myself answer, calm as quoting a price:

"North road. Three days gone."

All true. They'd gone north. Just not the north road, and not the way he meant it. I've gotten better at truth's shape in twenty years. Practice does that.

He wrote it down. He actually wrote it down, and turned away, and that—that was the mistake that saved whatever's left of my soul. Because writing it down meant believing it, meant the ledger was satisfied, meant he gave the order to move on to the next quarter.

Which is when the cooperage roof fell in, and old Marta screamed, and something in me that had been folded up polite for twenty years stood all the way up.

* * *

My father was a smith who never swore an oath in his life. He used to say iron doesn't care about your intentions—it cares about your heat, your timing, your willingness to keep striking when the metal tells you no. I thought that was shop wisdom. Standing in my burning street, I finally understood it was the whole of the doctrine my order dressed up in ceremony.

Iron doesn't care. But the smith decides what the iron becomes.

I went back inside my forge. Not to hide. To arm.

The fragment hummed harder as I unwrapped it, as if it had been waiting twenty years for someone to stop being careful with it. Corvane's hilt—or what the purgers left of it. I'd held it once before, the night Emrys handed it to me, and felt it take my measure and find me lacking. This time I gripped it bare-handed, and this time—

This time it *sang.*

Not the low buried note. A real note, rising, a chord with no instrument behind it, and light ran up my arms like cold water running backward, and I swear before any god still taking calls that the burning street went silent for me. Just the song. Just the steel remembering what it was for.

Because here's the thing I understood in that moment, the thing that turned my rage into something with edges: the fragment wasn't answering my worthiness. It was answering my *witness*. Twenty years ago I broke my oath to stay alive, and the blade knew it, and it sang anyway—not because I was forgiven but because I was *present*. Because for the first time since the purge, a speaker's hand closed around oath-steel while innocents burned, and the old words in the old metal remembered their purpose, which was never glory. It was *this.* Standing between. Always this.

The grey cloak with the ledger came back around the corner. Of course he did. Ledgers get audited.

He saw me standing in the forge light with a dead man's sword-hilt blazing in my fist, and to his credit he dropped the ledger. To his shame, he drew steel.

"You're one of them," he said. "You're a *speaker.*"

"I'm a smith," I told him, and the song climbed my arm and out through my voice without my permission, in the First Tongue, words I didn't know I knew, syllables older than kingdoms pouring out of me like slag out a tapped crucible—and my half-broken apprentice blade, hanging on the wall where it had hung for twenty years, *answered*. Rattled in its brackets. Hummed in sympathy. Half an oath, still half-true.

The hunter's sword cracked down the middle. Just cracked, clean, like bad casting. He stared at it, and then at me, and I watched a man discover that the stories he'd been paid to end were true.

"Run," I told him, and meant it, because killing him would make me the thing they'd said we were. "Tell them Lowmarket has a smith."

He ran. Ledgers can't help you when the arithmetic changes.

* * *

Afterward—after the riders withdrew, after buckets and grief and the long gray work of counting—I stood in the ashes of the Cooper's Row holding the fragment, and it had gone quiet again. Patient. Waiting. Like it knew something I was only beginning to.

Marta Voss survived. Half the east quarter didn't. Eleven dead, and every one of them dead because a woman with my face bought bread in this town and a child slept under my roof. I helped dig the graves myself. My hands are good for that too.

Emrys will come back. She'll hear, the way she hears everything, and she'll come back to ash and graves and a town that will never forgive the harborers, and she'll carry it the way she carries everything—like a stone she's decided to swallow. And when she comes, I'm going to hand her Corvane's fragment wrapped in oilcloth, same as before.

Except now I know what's wrapped in there with it. Now I know the scabbard I forged—quenched in the memory of my own broken oath, the one sin I have that's actually *useful*—hums in harmony with the fragment. Two halves of a conversation I walked out of twenty years ago, finished at last in firelight.

Six fragments, they say, to speak the Unbinding. Seven vessels, maybe. I don't pretend to understand the doctrine anymore. But I understand iron, and I understand heat and timing and the willingness to keep striking when everything tells you no.

I'm done being a smith who makes hinges for a town that burns.

At dawn I banked my fires—for good, I think—oiled the scabbard, strapped the fragment across my back where a sword ought to hang, and walked out the north gate. Not the north road. Never the north road.

The mountains, and the woman who hates me, and whatever's left of an order I betrayed.

Maybe they'll sing for me too. Maybe they'll bury me. Either way, I go with open hands, and for the first time in twenty years, both of them are full.
