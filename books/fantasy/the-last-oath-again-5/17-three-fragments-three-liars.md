## 17. Three Fragments, Three Liars

The rain found us three miles out of Lowmarket, the kind of cold autumn rain that doesn't fall so much as seep, like the sky had given up on weather and settled for a slow leak. Veyra walked beside me with her hood up and her chin down, and every hundred paces she glanced back at the town until the trees swallowed it.

"She'll come," I said. "Sable. She'll come or she won't. Either way, looking back won't make it sooner."

Veyra didn't answer. She never did, not in words. But she pulled a stick of charcoal from her pocket—the child hoarded charcoal the way other children hoarded sweets—and wrote on a flat stone: *You looked back too.*

I hadn't, actually. I'd kept my eyes forward out of twenty years' hard discipline. But I'd listened back. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a lie and a coward's truth, and I have made a long career of knowing that difference intimately.

We made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's hut below the Grey Stair, where the smugglers' path dropped toward the Weeping Steppe. I built a fire small enough to hide behind my cupped hands if riders came, and I was feeding it a twig when I heard the footstep—deliberate, heavy, announced. Nobody announces themselves on a smugglers' road unless they want to be heard or don't care about dying.

"Emrys Winterbourne." Sable Ironhew stood at the edge of the firelight with her forge-calloused hands empty and her jaw set like something she'd quenched herself. Twenty years had thickened her, greyed her, bent her back into a smith's stoop. But I knew her by the scar splitting her left eyebrow, because I'd been the one holding her head still when they stitched it. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other twenty years," I said. "Come to the fire before the dark does."

She came. And from inside her coat she drew a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapped it with the reverence smiths reserve only for things they didn't make, and set it between us.

A blade fragment. Six inches of steel ending in a jagged break, but even broken—even rusted—it *sang*. I felt it against my teeth, a hum in the bones of my skull, the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck across a room. My hilt, Corvane's hilt, answered from my pack like a dog hearing a whistle.

"The forge sang for three nights after you left," Sable said. "Woke me up. I thought I was going mad. Then I tore my own shop apart and found this in the foundation stones, right where the old man told me to put it, back when I still called anyone 'old man' with respect." Her voice caught on the last word. She covered it with a cough. "Corvane gave it to me the night before the purge. Told me to keep it under stone and never speak of it. I obeyed half an oath for twenty years. It's more than most of us managed."

"It is," I said, and meant it as kindness, though it came out sounding like an accusation, which is a skill of mine.

Veyra reached across the fire first. Of course she did. Children touch what adults only circle. She laid her palm flat above the fragment without touching it, and her eyes went wide and strange, and then she looked at Sable—not at the fragment, at *Sable*—with an expression I couldn't read. Then she picked up her charcoal and wrote: *It knows her.*

"Fragments remember their keepers," I said carefully. "That's all."

But Veyra shook her head once, slow, and kept looking at Sable, and Sable looked away first, into the fire, and I filed that away in the drawer where I keep things I'm not ready to open.

* * *

We walked south for two days, down off the Thornfell shoulders onto the long grey grasslands, and the fragment rode in my pack next to the hilt, humming to each other like conspirators. On the second night, camped in a hollow ringed by standing stones older than any oath, Sable finally said what she'd been chewing since Lowmarket.

"I need to tell you something before we go further. Because if we're walking into whatever we're walking into, I won't do it carrying it."

I fed the fire another branch. "Then say it."

"I informed." The words came out flat, hammered flat, the way a smith flattens a seam so it holds. "During the purge. Not at the start—I didn't bring them to us. But after, when the grey cloaks were hunting survivors through the lowlands, they came to my village asking questions, and there was a woman hiding in my loft with a broken sword and a fever, and they offered me a pardon and my own forge free and clear, and I—" Her hands opened, closed. "I told them where she'd gone. Not where she was. Where she'd *gone*. South, I said. They went south. She went north. She lived, I think. But I sold her road to save my shop, Emrys, and I've been striking iron over that coal for twenty years."

I waited until I was sure my voice would come level. "Did she ever know?"

"No. That's the worst of it. Mercy without justice. She thanked me when she left."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the steppe, a nightbird made its one sad note.

"You want absolution," I said. "I can't give it. I'm not your oathmaster."

"I don't want absolution." Sable's eyes came up, and they were furnace-eyes, furious mostly at herself. "I want you to say it plainly, so it exists outside my skull. So it's a thing that happened instead of a thing I am."

"That," I said, "I can do. You informed. You chose your life over hers and got lucky enough that both survived. Now carry it. Not as a brand—as ballast. Ballast keeps a ship upright in wind."

She snorted, almost laughed. "Twenty years of novels and that's the wisdom you give me?"

"I write better than I talk. Ask anyone who's read me."

And then, because fairness is a debt I hate paying, I paid it.

"My turn," I said. "You told me yours. Here's mine, and it's worse, because mine isn't a sin of weakness. It's a sin of pride." I pulled my knees up, the way you do when the ground gets close. "The purge happened on my watch. Not Corvane's. Mine. He'd sent me to the capital archives to verify a succession-oath—a routine errand, he said, though I know now he wanted me out of the city. While I was reading dusty folios, the grey cloaks came to the chapterhouse. Forty-one speakers. Eleven children. I could have been there. I *should* have been there. Instead I spent that week arguing with an archivist about whether the Third Declension of the First Tongue could bind a living king or only a dead one."

"What does that mean, 'on your watch'? You were one speaker among dozens."

"I was the one Corvane trusted with the warning signs." I heard my voice go dry and distant, reporting from far away. "Three months before, a crown courier had asked me, casually, whether oath-speech could be *unmade*. Unasked, unprovoked. A question no honest person asks. I noted it in my journal and did nothing else. If I'd carried it to Corvane—if I'd believed it mattered—he'd have scattered the order a season early. The children would have been in the mountains." I looked at her. "So yes. I hid afterward, same as you. But I hid from guilt dressed up as caution, and I let myself believe for twenty years that staying alive was the same as staying true."

Sable was quiet a long time. Then: "The archivist. Did the Third Declension bind a living king?"

I stared at her.

"Because," she said slowly, "if it did—and there's a thing wearing our king like a coat—then your dusty week in the archives might be the most important research anyone's done in nine centuries."

Leave it to a blacksmith to find the load-bearing wall in my confession. I didn't answer. Across the fire, Veyra sat very still, charcoal motionless in her hand, watching me with those old, old eyes, and I had the sudden crawling certainty that she already knew everything I'd just confessed, had known it since the threshold, and had stayed anyway.

* * *

The fraying started the morning we sighted the Steppe.

It began small. Sable rewrapped the fragment and stowed it in *her* pack, saying, "Steel rides better with steel," meaning her smith's tools, meaning herself, and I let her, and hated letting her. Then she questioned my route—"the southern ford, really? Ferro's letter said patrols"—and I snapped that I'd crossed these paths blindfolded while she was learning to hold a hammer, and she pointed out that I'd also hidden in a hole for twenty years while the world burned, and neither of those facts made either of us right about a ford.

By noon we weren't speaking. Veyra walked between us like a wedge, glancing left, glancing right, writing nothing. The silence had teeth. Old silences always do; new ones are just awkward, but a silence twenty years in the making has had time to grow fangs.

It broke at dusk, at a ruined wayshrine where the road forked—one path to the Weeping Steppe proper, one skirting east through bandit country. Sable threw her pack down. "Say it," she said. "Whatever you've been swallowing since Lowmarket. Say it now, because I can hear you chewing."

"Fine." I turned. "You broke your half-oath to live, and I understand that, I do. But you stayed. Twenty years, ten miles from the chapterhouse ruins, hammering iron, and you never once came looking. Never asked what survived. Never tried to finish anything. You informed to save yourself and then you *settled*, and now the fragments sing in your forge and suddenly you're ready to march? Forgive me if I don't build the family altar yet."

"And what did you do?" she shot back. "You wrote *novels*. Pretty lies about brave people. You hid so deep you buried your name, and a child had to knock on your door to dig you out. At least I never pretended my hiding was virtue."

"No," I said. "You just pretended your survival was."

It was cruel and it was true and I watched it land, watched her flinch like metal under the first hammer-blow, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me, because cruelty done well is still cruelty, and I've had practice.

Veyra stepped between us.

She took charcoal and wrote on the wayshrine stone, fast, pressing hard: *Both of you are liars.* Then, underneath: *Good liars. Liars who lied to stay alive so someone could still lie tomorrow.* She held the charcoal out, first to me, then to Sable. *The Regent eats kingdoms by eating their memory. What do you think it eats first? The truth people won't say.*

The wind moved through the shrine's broken arch. The fragments hummed in our packs, faintly, together, a chord.

"Out of the mouths of babes," Sable said finally, hoarse. "And mute ones, even."

"She's not wrong," I admitted. "She's usually not wrong. It's infuriating."

Sable sat down on a fallen pillar, abruptly tired, all the furnace gone out of her. "Emrys. I didn't come because the fragments sing. Well—not only. I came because when you walked out of my forge, I lay awake all night realizing I'd spent twenty years waiting for exactly that door to open, and being terrified it would." She looked up. "You're the last oath-speaker alive who isn't wearing a grey cloak. If you're a liar, then lying is the best we have. So let's be liars together, and confess on the way."

I sat down across from her. Two women in their middle years, road-worn and rain-stained, meeting each other honestly for the first time in two decades over the body of a murdered friendship that might, with care, be raised from the dead.

"Sable Ironhew," I said formally, "you are a liar, an informer, and a coward, and you came anyway."

"Emrys Winterbourne, you are a liar, a deserter, and a proud fool, and I followed you anyway."

From my pack, muffled, the two fragments sang a low agreeing note, and Veyra smiled—the first full smile I'd seen on her, quick and crooked and young—and wrote one more line on the wayshrine:

*Found family: built on confessed sins. Strongest kind. Nothing left to hide.*

* * *

We took the eastern fork at dawn, bandit country over patrols, and by midday the Steppe lay ahead of us like a sea that had forgotten to be water—grey grass to the horizon, shivering in wind that smelled wrong, sweetish, like flowers on a grave.

That was when we saw them. Far off, maybe two miles, on our original southern line: riders. Five, six of them, grey cloaks lifting in the wind, moving at a walk along the ridge where Ferro's letter swore patrols never came.

They weren't searching anymore.

They were waiting. Arranged. As if someone had known exactly where the road would put us—and known it days ago.

Beside me, Veyra had gone rigid. She grabbed my sleeve and wrote, her hand shaking, the letters jagged:

*They knew our fork. Someone told them the eastern road.*

And behind us, faint but unmistakable, Sable's forged scabbard—empty, plain, quench-marked iron—began, very softly, to sing.
