## 18. The Regent's Offer

I have learned to measure my captivity in small units. Not years—years are the Regent's currency, and I refuse to trade in them. Seconds. Ninety of them, sometimes, when the thing that wears my body grows careless or glutted or merely bored with me. Ninety seconds in which I am Aldric of House Veylan, king of a realm I can see only through windows it opens and closes at its pleasure.

Tonight I got forty-one.

Forty-one seconds to stand at my own bedchamber window and watch the moon come up over the capital, over rooftops I had sworn as a young man to protect, over streets where children sang songs about a king who never ages. *Never ages.* They sing it like a blessing. It is the most obscene lullaby ever written.

And then the pressure behind my eyes, the familiar closing of doors, and I was folded away again—tucked into the back room of my own skull like an old coat put into winter storage, still warm, still shaped like a man, useful only when something needed wearing.

But tonight was different. Tonight, before the door shut, I felt the Regent turn its attention northward. Toward Thornfell. Toward the mountains where, twenty years ago, it thought it had killed every soul who could speak truth into steel.

It missed one. And now it knows it missed one.

* * *

Here is what no ballad will ever tell you about being worn: you feel everything.

Not the way you'd think. The Regent does not let me watch through my own eyes while it parades my hands through state functions—I am given no such theater. But the body remembers what the mind is not permitted to witness. When it smiles with my mouth, my cheeks ache afterward with the shape of the smile. When it signs an execution order, my fingers retain for hours the ghost-pressure of the quill. When it dines, I taste the wine. When it lies—and oh, it lies constantly, beautifully, in the voice of a beloved king—I feel the lie settle into my throat like a swallowed stone, and the stone does not dissolve. It accumulates.

I have been swallowing stones for ten years. I could pave the Weeping Steppe with what I've swallowed.

The court physicians say the king sleeps poorly. The court physicians are permitted to say nothing else. Three of them have been replaced over the decade—one retired to his family estate, two simply vanished, and their colleagues learned quickly that the king's health is excellent, always excellent, eternally excellent, just as the king's face is eternally forty-two, because whatever else the Regent is, it is vain, and vanity requires maintenance. There is a reason the portraits stopped changing. There is a reason the royal mirror-makers' guild received a charter and then, quietly, all its senior members did too.

You learn to find the horror funny, after enough years. Not funny the way a joke is funny. Funny the way a man falling finds the ground funny—a black, terminal humor, the last joke available. If I did not laugh inside myself, in the sealed room where I keep whatever remains of Aldric Veylan, I would go mad, and madness would be a mercy the Regent would not permit, because a mad vessel cracks, and cracked vessels do not hold.

So I laugh. Privately. In the dark. At nothing.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, though dawn means little to me now; the Regent keeps the hours it pleases with my body, and sleep is granted like alms. A steward—Hessel, poor loyal Hessel, who has aged fifteen years in ten and thinks it is grief for his late wife—knocked at my study door to inform me that the king would receive a visitor from the north road.

*I* would receive a visitor. You see how they phrase it. The grammar of my captivity is impeccable.

I sat in my own chair, behind my own desk, under my own crown's shadow—it hangs on a stand in the corner of the study when not in use, and I have spent hours of my stolen seconds staring at it, that beautiful circlet of Thornfell gold, wondering, in the early years with real dread and lately with something almost like scholarly interest, whether the metal itself remembers anything. Gold holds memory poorly, the old texts said. That was why crowns were made of it. That was why—

That was why the seventh fragment troubled the Regent so much, I would later understand. But I am ahead of myself. Forgive me. Ten years of silence makes a garrulous ghost.

The visitor arrived at midmorning. Grey cloak. Grey gloves. The hood thrown back to reveal a face that might once have been handsome and had been sanded down by decades of service into something smooth and blank as river stone. Speaker Corvel. Yes, I know the names of them—the Regent thinks its secrets are locked away from me, but it forgets that a prisoner has nothing but time, and time in abundance will pick any lock. The grey-cloaks come and go through my presence chamber, and I lie folded in the dark behind my own eyes and count them like a miser counts coins.

Corvel knelt. Even kneeling, the motion was wrong—too fluid, the way water kneels. These are speakers who survived the purge by surrendering their oaths and being refilled with something else. What pours out of a vessel leaves room, and the Regent is generous with what it pours in.

"Word from the mountain roads," Corvel said. "The girl travels with the Winterbourne woman. Two others joined them—a smith from Lowmarket and an old soldier named Brannoc."

Brannoc. The name meant nothing to me. But I felt the Regent flinch—not visibly, nothing so crude—but somewhere in the deep architecture of my possession, a hairline tremor ran through the walls, and I pressed myself against it like a child against a locked door, listening.

"The quarry elders released the fragments," Corvel continued. "Four are accounted for. Possibly five."

Silence. My mouth, my silence. I hated it, the way it used my quiet as its own.

"And the child?" the Regent asked, in my voice.

My voice. Do you understand? It asked the question in my voice, with my cadence, and somewhere in the city below a bell rang noon, and I thought: *somewhere out there, people love this voice. People remember it saying wedding vows and coronation oaths and the names of their newborn children. Every kind word I ever spoke is a coin it can counterfeit.*

"The girl grows louder," Corvel said. "Each day she speaks more freely. The hunters report her words carry weight now. One of ours lost a blade to her at the shrine."

"A blade." Something moved through the study, through me, that I can only call appetite. "Lost to a child of perhaps nine years."

"Ten, majesty. Nearly."

"Nearly ten." The Regent turned my gaze to the window, to the mountains faint and blue on the northern horizon. "Do you know what she is, Corvel?"

"No, majesty."

"She is the last unbroken well of the First Tongue. The archive burned; the order is ash; the blades are scattered tin. Everything that language once lived in, we have eaten or buried." A pause, and in the pause I felt it doing arithmetic—that cold, patient, nine-hundred-year-old arithmetic. "Except her. She is not a speaker of the First Tongue, Corvel. She is the First Tongue. Walking around in the shape of a child, carrying words older than kingdoms, and she is walking toward my throne with a dead woman's sword fragments in her pack."

Corvel's head bowed further. "Your commands?"

And here—here is the moment I want you to understand, whoever finds these words, if anyone ever does. Here is the moment the Regent did the most frightening thing it has done in ten years of frightening things.

It decided to be merciful.

* * *

Mercy. I need to tell you what mercy looks like when it comes from a thing that has worn eleven kings across nine centuries and eaten the memory of every realm it wore.

It looks flawless. That is the horror of it. Human cruelty is sloppy—cruel men gloat, they preen, they leave fingerprints of spite all over their work. The Regent does not gloat. The Regent constructs. Its mercy is architecture: load-bearing kindnesses, buttresses of forgiveness, a whole cathedral of generosity designed with one purpose—to make you walk inside and never notice there is no door out.

I lay folded in the dark and felt it compose the offer. Felt it choose each word the way a jeweler chooses stones. And the terrible thing, the thing I have not told anyone because there has been no one to tell, is that even from inside—even knowing exactly what it was, exactly what it wanted—even I felt the pull of it. For four full seconds, folded in my cell at the back of my own skull, I thought: *that is fair. That is more than fair. That is generous.*

Four seconds. Then I remembered whose generosity it was, and I laughed my black cellar laugh, and the ground came up to meet me again.

* * *

The offer went north three days later, carried not by riders but by rumor—which is faster than any horse, and harder to kill. I know its contents because the Regent rehearsed them aloud in my voice, alone in the chapel at midnight, polishing the phrasing like a blade. It thinks rehearsal is harmless. It does not know that every word it speaks in my body, I keep. I am the richest beggar in the realm; I own nothing but what passes through me, and I hoard it all.

This is what it offered Emrys Winterbourne:

Pardon. Full and formal, sealed in wax and blood-law, for every surviving member of the Order of the Oathbound. Not merely survival—restoration. The order reinstated by royal decree, its purge officially declared the work of a corrupt chancellor (dead conveniently, blameable absolutely), its survivors summoned home with honors, its confiscated holdings returned, its name struck from the lists of traitors and rewritten in the rolls of the realm's oldest servants.

And more. Because the Regent understands that pardon alone buys obedience but restoration buys loyalty, and it wanted something better than loyalty. It offered her a school. Charter, grounds, students, funding in perpetuity. An academy of swordspeech above the snowline, rebuilt on the bones of the old one, with Emrys Winterbourne—last of the oath-bound—as its founding master. Her order reborn through her. Her dead master's legacy continued through her. Twenty years of hiding answered, at last, with purpose.

All of it true. All of it deliverable. The Regent does not lie in its offers; lying is for lesser creatures. It simply omits, and arranges, and waits.

One condition. One small condition, dressed in silk.

The child.

Veyra would be taken into "royal guardianship"—those were the exact words, and I heard the Regent savor them—for her protection and education, naturally. A child of such rare gift belonged at court, among tutors, safe from the rough roads and rougher men who hunted speakers. The grey cloaks who had chased her across half the realm would suddenly become her honor guard. The hunt would end the moment the prey was delivered. Everyone would live. Everyone would flourish. The order restored, the king beloved, the child cherished, the realm at peace.

Only the First Tongue would pass into the keeping of the crown.

Only the last living vessel of the language that binds crowns to their wearers would be placed, gently, lovingly, permanently, into the hands of the thing that wears the crown.

* * *

I have thought for ten years about what the Regent actually is, and I will share my conclusions with you free of charge, since I have paid enough for both of us.

It is not a demon. Demons want souls, and souls are loud, flashy things—it has no interest in mine beyond its irritation that mine refuses to stop rattling around in here like a pebble in a boot. It is not a god; gods create, and it has never created anything in nine centuries except arrangements. No. I believe it is a hunger that learned patience. It eats realms—not their land, not their gold, those are dross. It eats *memory*. It wears a king until the kingdom's whole lived identity flows through that single body, and then it drinks, slowly, decade by decade, until the realm cannot remember its own founding, its own griefs, its own songs. The people remain. The buildings remain. But the story of the place is gone, digested, and a realm without its story is a body without a spine: upright, functional, and incapable of standing against anything.

Nine centuries. Eleven kings. Whole dynasties of the eaten, and history books that grow strangely thin whenever it has passed through.

And now, after ten years of drinking my kingdom, something has finally frightened it. I felt the fear tonight, and I confess it warmed me like brandy. Fear is proof the prisoner still matters. Fear is proof the walls can shake.

It is afraid of a mute child and a woman who has spent twenty years learning how to disappear.

It is afraid of the Unbinding.

* * *

The night after the offer went north, the Regent held audience. Ordinary business—petitions, grievances, a delegation of wool merchants from the southern valleys complaining about tariffs. It performed kingship flawlessly in my body, settling disputes with my voice, laughing warmly with my throat, and I lay in my cell and watched the petitions scroll past the edges of my awareness like fish under ice.

Then a farmer from the Thornfell foothills was ushered forward—an old man, weathered to leather, hat clutched in both fists—and he looked up into my face, into the face that has smiled at him from coins and proclamations for a decade, and his eyes filled with tears, and he said, "Majesty, they say you've never aged a day in ten years. They say it's a blessing on the realm."

And the Regent—patient, ancient, hungry—smiled my smile and said, "The realm blesses me, friend, and I return the favor. Long may we keep each other."

Long may we keep each other.

The old farmer wept with joy. The court murmured its approval. Somewhere a bard began composing the verse before the audience had even ended.

And in the dark at the back of my own skull, Aldric of House Veylan screamed, silently, with everything left of him, and the scream changed nothing, and I screamed anyway, because that is the entire art of being a passenger: you scream anyway. You refuse, anyway. You remember your own name, anyway, even when no one else is permitted to speak it.

They think I am gone. The Regent thinks it drank me dry years ago and what remains is residue. Let it think so. Residue does not count the seconds. Residue does not hoard its captor's words. Residue does not notice that the Regent, for the first time in nine hundred years, has begun to hurry.

* * *

Three days after the offer went north, I was granted another lucid interval. Longer than usual—two minutes, maybe more. The Regent spends consciousness on me the way misers spend gold, and lately it has grown either careless or contemplative, and I do not care which. Two minutes.

I used thirty seconds of them to stand in the chapel. It watches the chapel closely—there is something beneath the chapel floor it does not wish disturbed, some anchor or cache, and its vigilance there tells me the chapel matters more than any throne room in the palace. I stood among the votive candles and I thought about the woman in the mountains.

I met Emrys Winterbourne exactly once, twenty-three years ago, at a ceremony I attended as a crown prince. She was young then—younger than I was—and I remember almost nothing of her face. What I remember is the sound. When the oathmasters spoke the binding into the coronation steel, the whole hall heard it, but I swear on whatever is left of my soul that she was the only one who seemed to hear it *from the inside*, as though the words passed through her on their way into the metal, as though she were not a witness to the speech but part of its instrument. She stood very still afterward, looking at her own hands as if they had surprised her.

I have thought about those hands for ten years. Hands that speak truth into steel. If anyone alive can unmake what sits upon my throne, it is a pair of hands like that.

The rest of my interval I spent at the chapel altar, and here I must ask you to trust me, because what I did next I cannot fully explain even to myself. I took up the stylus used for signing grants and charters, and on the back of the altar's dedication plaque—in the old script they teach princes and no one else anymore, the script the Regent has never bothered to learn because it considers dead languages beneath it, which may prove the single greatest error of its long career—I wrote what I could in the time I had.

Instructions. Coded. Half-finished. The beginning of a confession and a map and a warning, scratched in a dead tongue by a stolen hand, for whoever comes.

For her, if she comes.

I had just finished the third line when the doors of my skull began to close, and as they closed I heard the Regent, settling back into me like a man settling into a favorite chair, murmur in my voice, softly, almost tenderly, addressed to no one:

"Soon, little king. Soon it will all be settled, and you can rest."

It believes that comforts me.

And the worst part—the part I laugh at, alone, in the dark—is that for one shameful instant, it nearly does.

Because the offer is good. That is what I need whoever reads this to understand, the thing that keeps me awake in my cell behind my eyes: the offer Emrys Winterbourne receives is *good*. It is generous and true and it will cost her nothing but the one thing, and every night of her twenty years of exile will whisper to her that she has suffered enough, that the child is one girl and the order was hundreds, that justice delayed has already killed everyone it was meant for and mercy at least saves the living.

I know that whisper. I hear its cousin every day.

She will be tempted. Anyone would be tempted. The Regent built the temptation out of her own griefs, measured to fit her like a gown.

And somewhere north, in the snow, a ten-year-old girl who carries the oldest language in the world is trusting her to refuse it.

Rest soon, it says.

No. Not yet. Not while I can still scratch words into altars. Not while a woman with surprising hands still walks toward me through the mountains, gathering broken blades.

Come quickly, Emrys Winterbourne. The king is running out of seconds, and the thing wearing him has just learned how to hurry.
