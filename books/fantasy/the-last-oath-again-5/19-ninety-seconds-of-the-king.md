## 19. Ninety Seconds of the King

I have learned to measure my life in ninety-second increments.

Most men measure in years. Some in harvests or reigns or the lifespans of beloved dogs. I measure in the gaps between drownings—the brief, merciful windows when the water recedes and I surface gasping inside my own skull, blinking at a world I technically rule but haven't touched in a decade.

The court physician calls them "fugues." He is not wrong, exactly. A fugue implies absence. What I experience is worse: presence without ownership. I am there for all of it. Every decree signed in my hand. every smile I did not choose. Every execution order that passed through these fingers while I screamed soundlessly behind my own eyes like a man locked in a cellar beneath his own house.

Ninety seconds, give or take. That's what I get. Sometimes less. Once—gloriously, cruelly—nearly four minutes, on the night of the solstice feast, long enough to eat half a plate of pheasant before the tide came back in. It was the best meal of my life, which tells you something about my life.

This morning's window opens without warning, as they always do. One moment I am deep underwater, watching through glass; the next, I am *here*, standing at the window of my private apartments with dawn breaking grey over Caer Alden, and my right arm is still moving, still finishing a signature on a document I cannot yet see.

I let it finish. I have learned not to fight the last motions—it wastes precious seconds, and the body completes its momentum anyway, like a cart rolling past the point where the ox stopped pulling.

Then stillness. Then *me*.

*Hello,* I think, to myself, absurdly. *Hello again.*

The document on the desk is an execution order. Of course it is. The Regent has developed a taste for them lately—or rather, it has always had the taste, but it has grown less careful about hiding the appetite. This one bears a name I don't recognize: a ferryman, by the trade listed. Ferro. Some river town in the Thornfell foothills. The charge is sedition, which in the Regent's vocabulary means *knowing something.*

I read it twice, memorizing details the way a drowning man memorizes the shape of the shore. Because that is what my lucid intervals are for—not eating pheasant, not weeping, though I have done both. They are for gathering intelligence against myself.

I am, I have come to understand, the only spy inside this kingdom who knows exactly how much treason matters.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know, since no one alive will hear it from me directly, and perhaps paper is braver than kings.

Ten years ago, on the night my father died and the crown was set on my head, something climbed into me through the ceremony itself. I felt it happen. That is the part I cannot make anyone believe, because there is no one left to tell—the priests who conducted the rite are dead, retired, or transformed into the smiling grey-cloaked things that flit through my council chamber now, and none of them would listen anyway.

It was cold. That's how it began. Cold poured down through the crown metal into my skull like winter finding a crack in a wall, and then a voice that was not a voice said, in the oldest language I know and several I don't:

*You will do.*

Not *you are chosen.* Not *long live the king.* *You will do.* The way one says of a tool, of a coat, of a horse bought cheap at year's end.

For the first weeks I fought it. I genuinely tried. I dug my nails into my palms during audiences; I bit through my own cheek until the taste of blood became the flavor of every state dinner; I once, memorably, attempted to stab myself with a ceremonial dagger during the signing of a trade accord, and my hand stopped an inch from my throat with such perfect calm that I nearly fainted from the effort of the failure.

The Regent found my resistance charming. I could feel it finding me charming, the way a farmer finds a stubborn mule charming right up until the moment he decides the mule isn't worth the feed.

Then it stopped letting me remember. And here is the mercy and the horror of it, twined together: when the water closes over you, you don't suffer between surfaces. You simply aren't. I wake in these ninety-second islands and find ten years of wreckage around me—a realm taxed into bitterness, a court full of people who flinch when I laugh, a queen who left for her sister's estates four years ago citing ill health, and whose farewell letter I found later in my own desk, answered in my own hand with three warm, hollow sentences I had no memory of writing.

The Regent wears me well. That is the unforgivable thing. It is better at being King Aldric than I ever was. Under its management, grain stores filled, borders held, the coin stayed honest. For six years the chroniclers will write—if chroniclers survive—that the kingdom prospered under my rule, and they will be correct, and I will want to burn every page.

Because prosperity was never the price. Memory was. I have pieced together enough, in my scattered minutes, to understand the shape of it. The Regent does not merely wear kings. It eats realms—slowly, patiently, the way a moth eats a tapestry, thread by invisible thread. Names go first. Then the old stories. Then the reason anyone bothers to defend a border or bury a neighbor properly. I have watched petitions arrive from villages that no longer remember their own founding days, their elders describing their ancestors' graves as "the old stones." I have watched the First Tongue vanish from every archive in the capital, page after page rendered blank by clerks who swear they never touched the ink.

Something is afraid of that language. Something wearing my face loses sleep over words older than kingdoms.

Good, I think, with the only defiance available to a prisoner: spite.

* * *

Today's window is generous. I can feel it—ninety seconds stretching toward two minutes, maybe more, the way a fever sometimes breaks kindly. I don't waste it on grief.

I walk. My body obeys me completely in these intervals; the Regent leaves the machinery unattended, confident that the prisoner cannot escape the prison. It has never considered that the prisoner might leave messages.

My private chapel is three doors down, past the guard who salutes me with eyes that don't quite focus—I've stopped wondering whether he's drugged, enchanted, or simply a man who has learned not to look too closely at his king. The chapel is small, stone, unlovely, which is why I chose it years ago for the one act of rebellion available to me.

On the wall behind the altar, beneath a century of candle soot, I have been writing.

Not in ink. Ink can be found, read, punished. I write in pinpricks—tiny holes pressed into the mortar with the point of a brooch I stole from my own regalia, a few each interval, over years. To any inspector, the wall is just a wall. But I have been building letters the way prisoners build tunnels: slowly, invisibly, one handful of dirt at a time.

The cipher is my father's. He taught it to me as a game when I was eight, a boy's amusement, the kind of thing fathers pass on without knowing they're arming their children. Every king of our line learns it; none of my line has reigned long enough to use it. It maps onto the First Tongue's alphabet—the old script, the pre-royal script—and that is deliberate, though I didn't know why when I started. I know why now. Whoever comes to undo the thing wearing me will need to read the old words, and whoever reads the old words will read my wall.

Tonight's additions, pressed while my heart hammers out the countdown of my freedom:

*IT CANNOT BE NAMED BY ITS OWN SERVANTS. IT FEARS THE FREE OATH. THE FIRST TONGUE BINDS ONLY WHAT SPEAKS FREELY—THIS IS WHY IT EATS SPEAKERS. THIS IS WHY IT WEARS KINGS: A KING'S OATH IS NEVER FREE.*

I press each hole and feel the truth of it settle. Ten years of observation, gathered in stolen minutes, distilled to a wall of dots. It cannot abide being named by an oath freely given. I learned this from its one mistake—the night it grew careless, early in my captivity, when a captured speaker was dragged before the throne and forced to pronounce a binding-word at my command. The Regent made her say it. She said it, and nothing happened, and I saw—for one flickering second—something behind my own eyes recoil. Coerced speech is empty speech. The Regent knows this. The Regent fears this. Everything it has done for nine centuries, if my fragments of history are true, flows from that single fear.

A king cannot help whoever comes. A king's oath is chained before it is spoken. But someone outside these walls—someone free—could speak the word that unmakes it.

If they exist. If any speakers survived the purge. If they can reach the throne room.

If, if, if. My whole reign has contracted into conditional grammar.

* * *

The last thing I write tonight is the hardest, and I save it for last because I nearly cannot do it. My brooch hovers over fresh mortar, and my hand shakes, and for a moment I am only a man in a cold chapel wanting desperately to live.

*I AM IN HERE. I HAVE BEEN IN HERE TEN YEARS. IF YOU CAN FREE ME WITHOUT COST TO THE REALM, DO. BUT HEAR THIS AND BELIEVE IT:*

*DO NOT SAVE ME IF SAVING ME COSTS THE REALM.*

*IF THE CHOICE IS ALDRIC OR THE KINGDOM, LET ALDRIC DROWN.*

There. It is done, and it cost more than the execution orders ever did, because those were signed by something else and these holes are bored by me alone, minute by minute, year by year, a king carving his own epitaph into the walls of his own chapel in the hope that strangers will read it.

People imagine captivity as chains and darkness. Mine is gilded, warmed, perfumed. But the truest cell is this: I have spent a decade unable to say the simplest sentence—*I am here, I am innocent, help me*—to a single living soul. The wall is my confession, my testament, and my only child. If the kingdom burns everything else I've built, let it spare the mortar.

Somewhere in the Thornfells, I am almost certain, someone survived. The Regent thinks so too—it has grown restless these past months, dispatching riders, burning river towns, hunting a child, of all things. A mute child. When an immortal hunger that has eaten nine kingdoms turns its attention toward one small silent girl, you may draw conclusions. I have drawn mine, in pinpricks, on a wall.

Which means the endgame is coming. Either the hunters find her first, and my wall becomes a curiosity for archaeologists of a dead tongue, or she comes here—with whatever battered remnant of my old order carries her—and stands in this chapel reading my father's cipher by candlelight, learning that the monster she came to kill has a hostage inside it.

To that person, whoever you are: I am sorry in advance. Sorry that freeing me will hurt you. Sorry that the face you must strike down will be mine, and that my voice will beg beautifully, because the Regent begs beautifully, it has had ten years of practice in my throat. Trust the wall. Trust the old words. Trust the child.

And if it comes to it—if the choice arrives as I wrote it—then look at the man behind the crown, and remember that he asked.

* * *

The water rises. I feel it the way sailors feel weather: a pressure behind the eyes, a coldness pooling in the jaw, the familiar sensation of my own thoughts being politely shown the door. Ninety seconds gone. Perhaps a hundred and ten. A rich interval, by my standards.

I put the brooch back in its place among the regalia. I straighten my cuffs. I compose my face into the mild, faintly amused expression the court expects of King Aldric, the king who never ages, the king who has ruled so wisely and so long.

As I turn from the altar, the last of my own mind catches one final detail—one small wrongness I file away even as the flood takes me. On the chapel floor, by the door, a smear of melted snow. Someone has entered recently. Someone the guards admitted without alarm.

Grey cloth fibers, caught on the door hinge.

They're searching the palace. They're close to something.

And then the cold pours down through my crown like winter finding a crack in a wall, and King Aldric goes under, and the thing that will do smiles with his mouth and turns toward the door, humming a tune it stole from a dead man's memory, already thinking about the mountains.
