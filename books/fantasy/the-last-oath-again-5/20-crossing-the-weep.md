## 20. Crossing the Weep

There are places in this world where the ground itself has been lied to so long that it forgets what it is. The Weeping Steppe is one of them. A hundred miles of grey grass and standing stones, named not for rain—the sky there hasn't wept in living memory—but for the way travelers come out of it crying without knowing why.

I'd crossed it twice before the purge. Both times I'd held my oath-blade against my chest and spoken my own name into the steel every dawn, the way Master Corvane taught us: *a word in steel is a word kept.* The steppe couldn't take what the metal wouldn't give up.

That was twenty years ago, when I still had a blade worth speaking to, and a name I was proud to keep.

Now I had a hilt fragment wrapped in oilcloth, a mute child with a voice like a drawn bowstring, and a blacksmith who swore like a dockhand and trusted me about as far as she could throw an anvil. And ahead of us, flat as a dead man's pulse, lay a hundred miles of forgetting.

"We could go around," Sable said, for the fourth time since dawn.

"Around adds nine days through the Greymarsh," I said. "The Regent's hunters would find us in four."

"So we fight them."

"You've seen how that goes." I didn't look at her when I said it. The shrine was three days behind us and I still woke at night smelling the blood on my own hands, hating how familiar it had felt. Two decades of peace undone in the space of six heartbeats and a grey cloak's scream.

Sable spat, which was her version of conceding a point. "Fine. But if I wake up tomorrow not knowing who I am, I'm blaming you specifically, by name. Assuming I can remember your name."

"Emrys Winterbourne."

"See, that's going to be the *first* thing to go. It's the most complicated thing about you."

Veyra made a sound that might have been a laugh. She'd taken to walking at my shoulder, close enough that I could feel the small warmth of her, which was a comfort I hadn't asked for and couldn't seem to refuse. She carried the second fragment now, the one from beneath the lake, strapped against her sternum where she said it hummed. She'd written that on her slate: *it sings when you sleep. it knows the song.*

I tried not to think too hard about what that meant.

We stood at the edge of the steppe as the sun came up the color of dishwater. The last landmark behind us was a leaning cairn with a rusted pilgrim-token nailed to it; ahead, the grass ran grey to the horizon, broken only by the standing stones—thousands of them, menhirs and markers and grave-slabs, all leaning at angles, as if the land itself had been listening to too many lies and had begun to tilt.

Veyra touched my sleeve. On her slate she wrote: *my mother crossed here. she told me: do not answer when something calls your name.*

"Sound advice," I said. "Did she say whose voice it calls in?"

The slate hovered. Then: *yours.*

* * *

The first day was almost pleasant, if you have low standards for pleasant, which after twenty years in the Thornfells I frankly do. Grey grass to the knee, grey sky overhead, wind with nothing in it but distance. We walked in single file, Sable breaking trail because she insisted, Veyra in the middle, me watching our backtrail out of habit.

By noon the stones had thickened from occasional to frequent. Most were unmarked, but some carried inscriptions worn to suggestion—names, I assumed. Names of the dead, names of travelers who'd wanted to be remembered passing through. That's the oldest human impulse after hunger, I think: *I was here.* Carve it somewhere harder than yourself.

Around midday we passed a stone with fresh carving. Fresh meaning within the year. Letters cut deep and careful:

**MAREN OTTWEIL. SHE WAS HERE. SHE IS —**

The last line unfinished. The chisel-marks stopped mid-stroke, as though the hand holding it had simply forgotten what it was doing.

Sable read it twice. "Charming country."

"They don't die here," I said. "Mostly. They just stop being anyone. Wander back out the far side and spend their lives feeling like they've left something important in a coat they gave away." Master Corvane had told me that, on my first crossing. He'd also told me that the steppe wasn't cursed, exactly. Curses require intent. This was older than intent—a scar on the world where something had fed for so long that the wound had learned to feed itself.

And everything that fed on memory, everything that ate names and histories and oaths, belonged—at however many removes—to the same appetite wearing Aldric's crown.

Veyra walked with her slate pressed flat against her chest, over the fragment, and every few hundred paces she'd glance up at me and touch two fingers to her own throat. Checking. At dusk of the first day she wrote: *still mine.*

"How does it work?" Sable asked, around a fire of dry grass that smoked more than burned. "The taking. Does it sneak up? Does it hurt?"

"It doesn't sneak," I said. "It erodes. You'll notice it in small things first. You'll reach for a word and find a hole. Then one morning you'll introduce yourself to a stranger and realize halfway through that you're guessing."

"And the stones? What are they actually?"

"Gravestones without bodies. People trying to pin themselves down before the wind takes them." I fed another handful of grass to the smoke. "Corvane used to say the steppe was the Regent's leavings. Like wax drippings under a candle. Everything it ever consumed left a residue, and the residue pooled here."

Sable looked out into the dark, where the stones stood like teeth. "Then we're walking across its droppings to go stab it. There's a metaphor in there I don't want examined."

Veyra wrote something and turned the slate toward me: *sleep. i will listen.*

"I'll take first watch," I said.

She shook her head, erased it, and wrote again, slower: *i never sleep here anyway. the singing gets loud.*

I should have argued. I was the adult; she was eleven years old and missing a voice and carrying an artifact that sang to her in the dark. Instead I looked at her face—set, stubborn, already old in the ways children get old when the world makes them carry things—and I lay down with my back to a stone that had someone's half-finished name on it, and slept.

* * *

The second day, the holes started.

Small ones, at first, the kind you can paper over. Sable called the fragment-wrapping by the wrong word three times before lunch—*sack, bundle, that cloth thing*—and got visibly angrier each time. I lost the name of the town we'd passed through two days ago, Lowmarket, gone, just a shape where a word should be, and it frightened me more than I let show, because Lowmarket was where Sable lived, or had lived, before it burned. If I could lose that—

By afternoon I'd lost the name of the mountain range I'd hidden in for twenty years. I knew it began with *Thorn*. I stood in the grey grass mouthing syllables like a woman trying to recall a dream, and finally had to ask Veyra, who wrote it on her slate and showed me with a gravity that made my cheeks burn.

*Thornfell,* she wrote. Then, underneath, smaller: *i will remember for you. all of you. both of you.*

"That's not—" I stopped. "You shouldn't have to carry ours too."

Her chin came up in a way I was beginning to know very well. She erased and rewrote: *you carried me down a mountain in a blizzard. this is smaller.*

It was not smaller. I knew what it cost her to hold words. Every word she anchored in herself was a coin out of her own purse, and the purse was a childhood. But there was no arguing with her—not because I lacked authority, but because she was right, and we both knew it, and being right at eleven years old is a burden no one should hand you and no one can take back.

So I taught her the trick instead. We sat among the stones while Sable paced off her temper, and I showed Veyra what Corvane had shown me: how to press a word into an object until the object holds it. Not swordspeech—that was gone from me, sealed away with the rest of my order's gifts—but the folk-version, the hedge-craft, the thing every grandmother knows without naming. You choose a stone. You hold it. You say the name into it, silently, over and over, giving it your breath and your warmth and your attention until it stops being a stone and becomes a *keeping-place*.

Veyra chose three stones, one for each of us. Hers she kept in her cheek-pocket, touching it with her tongue like a worry-bead. Sable took hers with a grunt—"like a rosary for a god I've met and disliked"—but I saw her thumb working over it as we walked. Mine went against my sternum, next to the hilt-fragment, and I spoke my name into it until my lips went numb.

*Emrys Winterbourne. Emrys Winterbourne. Emrys—*

It felt less like keeping a name than like bailing a boat.

* * *

The third night, the steppe called us.

I was on watch—I'd won the argument by simply staying awake when my turn ended—and the moonless dark was so complete that the stones existed only as absences, holes punched in the star-field. The grass hissed. Somewhere far off, water that wasn't there dripped from leaves that weren't there. That was the other reason for the name, Corvane said. The steppe wept sounds it had stolen from elsewhere.

Then, out of the dark, in a voice I had spent twenty years not hearing:

"*Emrys.*"

I was on my feet with a knife in my hand before thought caught up. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and dry and faintly amused, and it was his. It was Corvane's voice exactly, the ghost-voice I'd heard only once since the purge, down beneath my floorboards, and some traitor part of me leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

"*Emrys Winterbourne. Come here, child. I have something to tell you.*"

"It's not him," I said aloud, to myself, to the stones, to whatever listened. "He's dead. Dead men don't call across steppes."

"*Dead men keep their promises,*" the dark said gently. "*Come and collect yours.*"

Every step of my body wanted to obey. That's the horror of it—it doesn't drag you, it *invites* you, it offers you the exact thing you've starved for, and lets your own hunger walk you forward. My boots had moved two paces before I understood they were moving.

Behind me, Veyra sat bolt upright in her bedroll. She couldn't shout. So she did the only thing she could: she spoke into her stone, silently, and then she pressed her small hand flat against my spine, between the shoulder blades, and pushed the word through her palm into me.

It hit like cold water. One syllable of the First Tongue—*truth-root*, or near enough—and suddenly the voice in the dark was just wind, and my two paces of wandering had put me facing a stone with fresh carving on it, and the carving was my own name.

**EMRYS WINTERBOURNE.**

Cut deep, careful, recent. Below it, begun and abandoned:

**SHE WAS HERE. SHE IS COMING TO —**

I stood there shaking, knife still out, reading my own gravestone by starlight. Behind me Sable was awake now, cursing steadily and creatively, and Veyra hadn't taken her hand off my back.

"He knew my name," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "It used my master's voice and it knew my name."

Sable's curses stopped. "You're sure it wasn't—him. The ghost. You said he talks to you."

"The ghost talks to me through steel and floorboards and things that loved him." I sheathed the knife. "This thing talked through emptiness. There's a difference. Ghosts grieve. That thing *fishes*."

Veyra took her hand away long enough to write: *it called me too. before you woke. it used my mother's voice.*

The night got very quiet around that sentence.

"What did it say?" I asked.

She hesitated, chalk hovering. Then she wrote, and showed me, and her hand was steady but her eyes weren't:

*it said: give me back what you're carrying and i will give you back your voice. all of it. every word you'll ever need. no more burning years.*

"And you said no."

She nodded once. Then she added, in letters pressed hard enough to snap chalk: *it lies. voices aren't given. they're kept.*

I looked at her—this scrap of a girl, eleven years old, sitting in a field of stolen names with a monster's offer still cooling in her ears, and her answer ready before the question finished. Something in my chest that had been clenched for twenty years loosened by a single degree. Not healed. Loosened. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole road ahead.

"We anchor watches from now on," I said. "Nobody walks anywhere alone, even ten paces. And nobody—" I looked at each of them in turn. "Nobody answers. Whatever voice comes, whatever it offers, whoever it wears. We speak to each other or not at all."

Sable grunted agreement. Veyra raised her slate: *and if it wears one of us?*

The question sat in the fire-smoke between us like a fourth traveler.

"Then the other two will know," I said. "Because I'm going to teach you the test. Corvane's test. There's a question in the First Tongue that can't be answered by anything that doesn't own its own memories. Veyra, I'll need you to pronounce it for us, because my accent would get us killed."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the closest thing to daylight we'd seen in three days.

* * *

The fourth day, the steppe stopped being subtle.

The stones thickened into a forest of them, close enough that we walked a maze, turning and doubling, and every stone bore writing now—some ancient, some recent, some carved in hands I swear were still trembling. Names upon names upon names, a city of the unforgotten-by-themselves, and threaded through it all, that dripping sound, closer now, patient, like something following at the edge of hearing.

Sable forgot her mother's face at midmorning. I watched it happen—watched her stop mid-stride, mouth open, reaching for something and finding the shelf empty, and the expression that crossed her face wasn't confusion. It was grief with the details scraped off. She gripped her anchor-stone so hard it drew blood and walked on without saying a word, and I pretended not to see her wipe her eyes, because that's what you do for family, and somewhere in the last four days, without anyone voting on it, that's what these two had become.

Veyra paid for all of it. I watched her spend herself word by word—anchoring Sable's mother's name, anchoring my order's name, anchoring the word *east* when I lost east, anchoring *bread* when Sable lost bread—and with each expenditure she aged. Not dramatically. A fraction. A new fine line at the corner of her eye. Hands a shade less soft. A stillness in her posture that belongs to women past forty, arriving in a child of eleven by installments.

At the day's end I took her wrist and made her sit, and I said, "No more tonight. Whatever we lose tonight, we lose. You've paid enough."

She wrote: *but you'll forget the plan.*

"Then we'll make the plan again," I said. "Plans are cheap. You're not."

She looked at me for a long moment with those too-old eyes, and then she did something she'd never done: she leaned sideways until her head rested against my arm, and stayed there. She weighed nothing. Children should weigh more than that.

I sat in the grey grass with a sleeping child against my side and a stone full of my name against my heart, in a place built from the droppings of the thing I intended to kill, and I let myself want—for the first time in twenty years—not survival, not anonymity, not even vengeance. Just this. To be someone these two could lean on. To have a name worth keeping because someone else needed it kept.

The dripping sound, somewhere north in the dark, paused.

As if it had heard me thinking.

And then, very softly, in a voice that was neither Corvane's nor Veyra's mother's nor anyone's at all—a voice like a ledger closing—it said:

*"Almost, Emrys Winterbourne. Almost. One more day."*

Veyra's eyes snapped open. Across the dead fire, Sable sat up with her hand already around her hammer.

None of us had told it where we were going.
