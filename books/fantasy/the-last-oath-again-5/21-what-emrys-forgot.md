## 21. What Emrys Forgot

The Weeping Steppe did not weep. That was the first lie of the place, and I have since learned that everything about it was a lie, including the grass, which looked soft and was not, and the sky, which looked like sky and was actually the memory of sky, painted by something that had never bothered to look up.

We had been walking for two days into its heart. Veyra walked ahead of me with a cord tied around her wrist and mine—Sable's suggestion before she took the eastern fork toward the quarry, her scabbarded fragment wrapped in oilcloth across her back. "Tie yourselves together," she'd said. "If this place eats names, you'll want a rope to the one person who can still say yours."

I had laughed at that. A dry little laugh, the kind you make when you're laughing at the wrong thing.

By midday of the second day, I had stopped laughing entirely, because I could no longer remember what my name rhymed with.

That sounds like nothing. Let me tell you what it is like. You wake in the morning knowing there is a word that belongs to you the way your hands belong to you, and you reach for it and find a smooth wall where the word should be. Not gone—walled. You can feel the shape of it behind the stone. *E——* something. Two syllables, maybe three, with a cold edge on the front of it like a blade drawn partway from a sheath. I knew all these things about my own name and could not say it. It was like being locked out of your own house and watching through the window as someone who is not you moves your furniture around.

"Emrys," Veyra said, testing it, the way you test ice. She had been saying it every hour or so since dawn, feeding it back to me like medicine. "Emrys Winterbourne."

The first word landed. The second slid off me like water off waxed canvas. I frowned at her. "Winterbourne," I repeated slowly, and the name sat in my mouth like a coin from a country I'd never visited. True currency somewhere. Not here.

Veyra's face did the thing it did when she was frightened but refusing to look frightened: her chin came up, her eyes went flat and bright, and she became very still, a small animal deciding whether the hawk had seen it. Then she pulled the cord tighter between our wrists and kept walking.

* * *

I should explain how we got to the heart of the Steppe, because it was not a decision so much as a surrender. The Weeping Steppe lay between us and the quarry where Sable swore two fragments waited under Brannoc's paranoid guardianship, and there was no road around it that didn't add eleven days—and Ferro's warning still rang in my ears: *riders in grey move faster than grief.* So we went through. Veyra led, because the child carried words in her that predated whatever hungry old thing had soaked itself into this ground, and those words were anchors. She would stop every hundred paces, press her palm flat against the air, and speak a syllable—a true-word, she'd learned to call them—and the syllable would hang there glowing faintly, like a lantern hung in fog, and we would walk to it and she would hang another.

It worked. For a day and a half, it worked beautifully, and I let myself believe the stories were exaggerated, as stories are.

Then, on the afternoon of the second day, I looked down and realized I had been walking in a circle. Not a small circle. A perfect one, miles wide, our own bootprints crossing beneath our feet like a signature written over and over by a hand losing its nerve. And Veyra's lantern-words—the ones she'd hung that morning—were gone. Not faded. Gone, as if the air had digested them.

She stood very still when I pointed this out. Then she did the bravest thing I saw anyone do in this entire miserable affair, and I include the throne room in that count. She untied the cord from her own wrist, tied it instead around a stone the size of a fist, and set the stone on the ground.

"If we lose ourselves," she said—her voice hoarse, each spoken word costing her something visible, a fine new line at the corner of her eye that hadn't been there a month ago—"we come back to the stone. The stone doesn't need a name. The stone is just the stone."

"And if we forget the stone?" I asked.

She looked at me with the terrible patience children reserve for adults who ask stupid questions. "Then we deserve to be lost."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a dead tree that was not a tree—I knew that much even then, because real trees don't feel like they're listening—and I lay awake cataloguing what I had lost so far.

My name: mostly gone. My mother's face: blurred at the edges like a thumb-smudged charcoal drawing, though I could still see her hands, oddly, working dough. The reason I'd left Lowmarket at nineteen: intact, unfortunately; the Steppe apparently couldn't take what I most wanted it to. My oathmaster's voice: present, annoyingly, humming the training cadence in the back of my skull—*breath before steel, steel before speech, speech before self*—which is easy to hum when you are dead and have no self left to lose.

What remained was a strange clean inventory. I knew I was a woman. I knew I had been a swordspeaker, once, the last one worth the title or the only one cowardly enough to survive it, depending on the hour. I knew the child asleep beside me mattered more than I did. And I knew—with a certainty that felt less like knowledge than like weather—that something in this place was reading me. Going through my pockets. Taking the small coins first, saving the heavy ones for later, the way any careful thief does, wanting to watch how much I noticed.

Around what I judged was midnight, the wind changed, and the wind spoke.

Not in the First Tongue. In mine. In the lowland drawl I'd grown up with, the accent of the river towns, and it said, kindly, conversationally:

*You're doing well, considering.*

I sat up. Veyra stirred but didn't wake. The stars above the Steppe were arranged wrong—not badly wrong, just rearranged, the way furniture gets moved in someone else's house.

"What are you?" I asked the dark. I didn't waste breath asking who.

*An arithmetic,* the wind said, which I did not like at all. *You've spent twenty years subtracting yourself, Emrys. I'm only helping with the sum.*

"I haven't spent twenty years subtracting anything," I said, "I've spent them hiding."

*Is that what you call it.* The wind moved around me, patient as water finding a crack. *Say your name.*

"Emrys."

*And?*

"Winterbourne," I said, and the word came out wrong, flattened, a coat worn by the wrong man. Even as I said it I felt it slipping further, and I understood suddenly, with a lurch, that speaking it here was spending it. Every repetition fed it into the ground. The thing wasn't stealing my name. I was paying it out, installment by installment, every time I answered to it.

So I stopped answering. I lay back down and stared at the wrong stars and said nothing, and after a while the wind sighed—actually sighed, with theatrical disappointment, like a tutor with a dull student—and went away to bother somebody else's night.

* * *

Morning found us at the heart of the Steppe, though heart is generous. There was no landmark, no altar, no writhing nexus of ancient evil. There was just a shallow bowl in the earth, maybe half a mile across, where the false grass grew in rings and the silence had texture. Standing in the center of it was like standing inside a bell that someone had stopped up with wool. Sound happened and then was politely confiscated.

Veyra hung her last true-words in an arc around us—four of them, then a fifth, her lips whitening with effort, that new line at her eye deepening into something I refused to examine closely because examining it would mean admitting what it was. Then she sat down on the ground, cross-legged, in the middle of her little constellation, and looked up at me.

"It wants you to give it the rest," she said.

"The rest of what?"

"You know." She picked up a pebble and set it in front of her, deliberately, like a piece on a game board. "Your name is almost gone. If you walk out of here without it, it stays gone. Forever. But—" she turned the pebble over, "—it can't take what's given freely. That's why it talks. That's why it waits. It needs you to hand it over and call it a choice."

I laughed. It came out cracked. "A name for safe passage. That's a bad bargain even by ferryman standards."

"Then don't make it." She shrugged, a child's shrug, infuriatingly simple. "But Emrys—you should decide *why* you're keeping it. Because I've watched you for weeks now, and you hold your name like it belongs to somebody else. Like you're minding it for a better woman."

The silence in that bowl was so complete that I heard my own pulse stumble.

Because here is the thing, the thing I had not told Sable, not told Ferro, not told the ghost who hummed in my skull, not told myself in any language I admitted to speaking: the child was right.

Everyone assumed I hid out of fear. Fear was the story I sold, and it was a good product, plausible, even flattering—*they hunted us, I ran, survival is its own virtue.* And some of it was fear. But fear is a loud thing, and what lived under my twenty quiet years was not loud. It was small and dry and patient, like a seed of rot in the center of an apple that looks fine from the outside.

I was not afraid they would kill me. I was afraid they had been right to try.

On the night the order burned, I was three miles away. Three miles. Close enough to see the glow off the clouds, close enough to hear—this is the detail I have never once managed to bury, though God knows I've dug enough holes for it—close enough to hear the bells. Corvane had sent me out that evening with a message to a sympathetic magistrate, a fool's errand, a grandmother-and-wolves errand, and I had known it was a fool's errand even then, because Corvane only ever sent me away when he expected blood, and he always chose me to be spared, and I always let him choose. Twenty-two years old, sworn to speak truth into steel, and I rode away from the burning at a canter because galloping would have meant admitting urgency, and urgency would have meant turning back, and turning back would have meant dying among people whose deaths I could not have prevented anyway—or so I told myself, then and for two decades, in the careful lawyer's voice of the damned.

The truth was simpler. The truth was that some part of me, some part I could not evict, believed that the purge was a verdict. That the world had audited the swordspeakers—all our oaths, all our binding of kings, all our beautiful certainty that we alone knew which words deserved to rule—and had found us guilty, root and branch, and that I had survived the way vermin survive a fire: by being small, and low, and quick, and having no particular claim to the light.

So I hid. Not from hunters. From the accounting. Because as long as no one ever said my name again—as long as I was no one from nowhere with no order and no blade and no voice—as long as the ledger stayed closed, no one would ever read the entry where it was recorded that Emrys Winterbourne had heard the bells and ridden the other way.

And now a mute child with borrowed years and burned-down eyes was sitting in a circle of her own spent words, telling me she'd seen straight through twenty years of deliberate invisibility in a matter of weeks, and the Steppe's thief-wind was circling overhead waiting for me to either surrender my name or finally understand why I'd been guarding it so badly.

"You want the wound under the wound," I said aloud. My voice sounded strange in the wool-stopped silence, thin, unarmored.

Veyra just waited. Children are better at waiting than the old; they've had less practice at being disappointed by the results.

"All right." I sat down across from her, knees cracking, and put my hands flat on the false ground because I needed to touch something that couldn't judge me, even if it was lying about being grass. "Here's what I forgot on purpose, and what this place is trying to finish the job on.

"I didn't run because I was afraid of dying. I ran because I agreed with them." The words came out of me like splinters, each one leaving a hole. "Not with the killing. Never the killing. But with the *reason*. The Regent—it, whatever wore the crown and signed the orders—it told the realm we were dangerous. That men shouldn't be bound by words they didn't choose. That a handful of speakers deciding the fates of kingdoms from behind altars was tyranny wearing vestments. And lying in my bed in Thornfell for twenty years, in all that lovely safe silence, some rotten little cellar of my heart kept whispering: *they weren't entirely wrong, were they? We did bind kings. We did decide. And you, Emrys—you heard the bells and you rode away, and some honest corner of you thought, good, let it burn, let the verdict stand, we earned it.*

"That's why I hid." I was crying now, which annoyed me enormously, and I let it happen because the Steppe had already taken my dignity along with my surname and there seemed little point defending the ruins. "Not fear. Unworthiness. You can fight fear. You can't fight a verdict you secretly agree with. I let the whole order die twice—the once with fire, and then every morning after, when I woke up and chose to stay no one, because staying no one meant the woman who heard the bells never had to be sentenced. She was already serving her term."

The silence held me while I shook. Veyra crawled across the space between us—she did not walk it, she crawled, which broke something loose in my chest that crawling should not be able to break—and she took my face in both her small, chapped hands, the way Corvane used to when he wanted a student to stop performing and start listening.

"Now listen," she said, and her voice came out with weight in it, First Tongue weight, the old cadence underneath the common words, and I felt the true-word she wrapped around what came next like wire around a hilt. "Emrys Winterbourne. Last of the Oathbound. Daughter of Maren of the river towns. The woman who heard the bells and grieved for twenty years because she *cared*, not because she fled. Cowardice doesn't ache like yours does. Guilt isn't guilt unless you loved something enough for it to matter." Her thumbs wiped my cheeks, rough and ungentle and the kindest thing anyone had done for me in twenty years. "This ground eats names that aren't held by love. Yours is held. I'm holding it. Take it back."

And my name came home.

Not gradually. All at once, like a bird returning to a window ledge, like a blade sliding home into a scabbard quenched in something truer than water. *Emrys.* Cold edge on the front of it, two syllables and a half, mine, mine, worn smooth by my mother saying it across a kitchen, by Corvane barking it across a training yard, by Sable spitting it across a forge. And *Winterbourne* after it, the winter I was born in, the winter I had survived, the winter I had mistaken for a sentence when it was only a season.

I laughed and cried into the child's shoulder while the wrong stars wheeled overhead and the thief-wind circled us and found, at last, nothing loose to carry off.

* * *

We left the bowl an hour later, walking out not in circles but in a straight line that Veyra found by some sense I didn't ask her to explain. Behind us the false grass lay flat in our wake and did not rise, and I chose to believe that was exhaustion rather than sulking.

Near the Steppe's eastern edge, where true mountains began shouldering up against the false horizon, Veyra stopped so abruptly I nearly walked over her. She was staring north, past the foothills, toward the distant smudge of the quarry road.

There was smoke. Too much of it, and too black, and rising from exactly the direction Sable had gone.

Veyra's hand found mine. Her grip said everything her voice, spent from the morning, could not.

We ran.
