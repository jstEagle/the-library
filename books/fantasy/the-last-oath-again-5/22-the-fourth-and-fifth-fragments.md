## 22. The Fourth and Fifth Fragments

I'd like to tell you the quarry was hidden well, but the truth is it was hidden badly by people who had twenty years to get better at it and spent them instead getting worse at trusting.

The place sat in a fold of the Greymantle hills, two days' hard walk from Lowmarket's ashes, a scar of cut stone where men had once pried slate from the earth until the earth gave out on them. The rebels—survivors, stragglers, the stubborn residue of an order that officially never existed—had walled the old cart road with rubble and posted watchers who thought hiding behind rocks made you invisible as long as you didn't sneeze. I counted three of them before we were even in bowshot. Emrys counted five and said so quietly, which did nothing good for her standing.

"Six," Veyra said, pointing with her chin at a ledge I would have sworn was empty.

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. "Remind me to be frightened of you later."

We stood in the open, hands visible, because Emrys said that was how you announced yourself to the paranoid: you made yourself easy to kill and difficult to justify killing. A horn sounded somewhere behind the rubble wall—a cracked, uncertain note, like the blower hadn't practiced since the purge. Which, I supposed, he probably hadn't.

Then the gate of loose stones shifted aside and there he was. Brannoc.

Twenty years had done to him what winter does to an orchard. He'd been a barrel of a man when I knew him, all shoulders and certainty; now the certainty remained but the shoulders had gone to string and sinew, and his beard had gone the color of the slate they mined here. His eyes swept over Emrys first, then Veyra, then me—and stayed on me longest, which stung more than I expected, because I'd told myself on the walk up that I didn't care what the old man thought of me.

"Sable," he said. Not a greeting. An identification, the way you'd mark a body.

"Brannoc." I kept my voice flat. "You look terrible."

"You look alive," he said, "which is worse."

* * *

They took our weapons at the wall—my hammers, Emrys's plain sword, even Veyra's little belt knife—and led us down into the quarry proper, where maybe forty people lived in dugouts and driftwood shelters among the spoil heaps. Forty. I'd imagined hundreds. I'd imagined a hidden army waiting for a banner. What I found was a settlement of the tired: vegetable gardens in raised beds, a smithy with a lean-to roof, children—actual children, born after the purge, who stared at us like we were weather.

"This is what's left," Brannoc said, catching me counting.

"It's more than nothing," I said.

"It's less than it was." He walked ahead of us, not offering his arm or his history. "We were sixty-one when we came here. You can do the arithmetic on the graves if you're fond of arithmetic."

Emrys spoke for the first time since the gate. "We've come for the fragments, Brannoc."

"I know why you've come." He stopped walking. The whole quarry seemed to stop with him—people drifting closer without seeming to drift, filling the spaces between shelters, forming an audience the way water forms a level. "That's why you're going to stand in front of everyone tonight and say what you did."

"Brannoc—" Emrys began.

"Not a request, Winterbourne." His voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, into something older than anger. "You'll speak before the order. Such of it as lives. And then we'll decide whether you leave with anything at all—or whether you leave at all."

* * *

They held the trial by firelight in the bottom of the pit, where the cut walls rose thirty feet on every side and the stars came down narrow as a blade's edge. Someone had dragged out benches. Someone else had set up a brazier, and the coals threw shadows up the walls so that the gathered survivors looked taller than they were, which may have been the point.

Forty-odd faces. Some I remembered from the halls of the old hold—Corvane's teaching circle, the armory drills, the long refectory tables where we'd argued about whether oaths could bind the binder. Most were strangers who wore the order's memory the way refugees wear borrowed coats.

Brannoc opened it himself. No magistrate, no ritual beyond what memory supplied. "Emrys Winterbourne," he said, "you are accused of the betrayal at Thornfell Hold. Of carrying warning away from the hold on the night of the purge, and carrying it nowhere. Speak your account, and let the order judge it."

I watched her stand. I'd known this woman for weeks now—shared roads and rations and one very bad night of rain—and I thought I knew how she carried herself. But watching her in front of those benches, I saw a different posture entirely. She stood the way she must have stood at nineteen, when Corvane was alive and her spine was someone else's to shape.

"There's no clean version," she said. "So hear the dirty one."

And she gave it. All of it. How she'd been sent to the low villages that autumn to test a rumor of speakers in the south. How the rumor was a lure, baited by someone inside the court who'd learned how to find swordspeakers by making them come looking. How she'd written to the hold—I heard a murmur run through the benches at that, because letters could be read, letters were always read—and how her letter had been taken, turned, used to time the soldiers' arrival for the night she was due back.

"I wasn't warned off," she said. "I was meant to be here. My absence was the message that the trap was sprung. They wanted the order to know its own student had been the key." Her voice held steady, mostly. "I rode back toward the fires. I got within sight of the pass. There were forty riders holding the road, and I was one woman with a sword that couldn't speak anymore because—" she stopped. Started again. "Because I'd already failed in every way that mattered. So I turned around. I went north. I hid. Twenty years. That's my account. Judge it however you need to."

Silence, except for the brazier popping. Then a woman near the front—I recognized her vaguely, Maren? Marta?—one of the archivists, said, "Who told you the rumor was true?"

"A merchant in Duncastle. Paid in advance. I found his stall burned a week later."

"So you were played."

"I was *used*. There's a difference, and neither one is comfortable."

Brannoc leaned forward. This was what he'd come for; you could see it in the set of his mouth. "You wrote the letter. Your hand, your seal, your phrasing. Do you know what that letter cost?"

"No," Emrys said quietly. "But I imagine you're going to tell me."

"It cost eleven names." Brannoc stood, slow, joints cracking like green wood. "The couriers who moved through Duncastle on routes your letter confirmed were still live. Eleven speakers and their kin, taken in the spring after the purge. I buried four of them myself." He turned to face the benches, arms wide. "She asks you to weigh her cowardice against her trickery. I ask you to weigh it against eleven graves."

"That's not fair," I said, before I could stop myself.

Every head turned. Fine. I'd put my hand in the fire already; might as well grip the coals.

"You want to try her for surviving," I said, standing, "then try me too, because I broke my half-oath in a forge in Lowmarket and lived fat and safe under a false name while you lot ate slate dust. If cowardice is the crime, this whole quarry is guilty, and the only honest verdict is to condemn everybody and go home. Or—" I looked at Brannoc dead-on. "Or we admit that the ones who died weren't braver than the ones who ran. They were just slower."

"Sable—" Emrys started, low.

"No. He gets to make accusations; I get to make noise. That's how trials work." I turned back to the benches. "You want to know what she's done since? She pulled a mute child out of a blizzard because the child needed pulling. She walked back onto smuggler roads she swore off to keep that child breathing. She killed two grey cloaks at the shrine and cried about it after, which is more than the Regent's people ever do. Whatever she was twenty years ago, judge *that*. Or don't judge at all, and give us the fragments, because there's a king wearing a monster and a girl who speaks the First Tongue and none of it waits for your committee."

Veyra tugged my sleeve. When I glanced down she signed, quick and precise: *You're shouting.*

*I noticed,* I signed back.

*It's working, though.*

Brannoc studied me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't parse—contempt braided with something almost like appetite, the look of a man who has missed arguing and hates himself for missing it. "The forge-wife speaks," he said dryly. "The order must truly be dying."

"The order is right here," said a voice from the back. Old, female, iron underneath. An ancient woman rose from the last bench, leaning on a staff tipped with a sword-pommel worn smooth. I didn't know her name, but half the quarry bowed their heads slightly as she passed, and Brannoc's jaw tightened in a way that told me everything. "I am Ilsette. I swore the First Oath when Aldric's grandfather wore the crown, and I have outlived three kings' worth of fools. Sit down, Sable. Sit down, Brannoc. Both of you mistake volume for testimony."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the most exhausting thing I've ever witnessed, and I once spent a night negotiating with a customs officer over a crate of unmarked blades. Survivor after survivor rose to speak. Some accused. Some defended. One man simply stood, wept, and sat down again, and somehow that weighed more than any speech.

A boy of perhaps sixteen—the first generation born free, or as free as anyone was—asked the question that cut deepest: "If we punish everyone who survived by running, what are we telling the children?" He gestured at the quarry's young, watching from the edges of the firelight. "That staying alive is a crime? Then we should have all died properly at Thornfell and saved the slate."

Brannoc answered him with silence, which was itself an answer.

Near the end, Emrys asked to speak once more. "I won't defend the letter," she said. "I'll carry those eleven names until I'm dirt. But I'll say this: the Regent hunts speakers because speakers can unmake it. Every year we hide, it eats another decade of the king, another layer of the realm's memory. The fragments exist to end that. Keep them, and you're keeping a knife in a drawer while the house burns."

Ilsette raised her hand, and the quarry went quiet the way snow quiets a field.

"I have listened," she said. "Here is what I heard. A woman who failed, who knows she failed, who came back anyway knowing she'd be judged—which she has been, thoroughly, and will continue to be, because we are speakers and we never let anything go." A rustle of grim laughter moved through the benches. "I also heard a child of the First Tongue sitting quietly among us, and I heard the fragments sing in their vault the moment she crossed the wall. Stone doesn't lie, friends. The blades know their own."

She looked at Brannoc. "Bring them up."

He didn't move. For a heartbeat I thought he'd refuse outright—that we'd see the paranoid elder plant his flag and die on it. Instead he said, "On condition. She carries them, but one of ours goes with them. Watcher's right. Old law."

"Name your watcher," Ilsette said.

Brannoc's eyes found mine. "The forge-wife. She broke her oath too. Let the broken watch each other."

I laughed before I could help it. "You realize that's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"It was not intended as nice."

"Everything true sounds that way first."

* * *

They brought the fragments up from the vault at midnight—a cellar cut into the quarry floor, sealed with a door that had no handle on the outside, because the door only opened for spoken words and half these people couldn't risk speaking them. Two lengths of wrapped oilcloth, each cradled like an infant. When they unwound the first, I felt it before I saw it: a hum in my teeth, in the scar tissue of my palms, in the place behind my sternum where my own broken oath had knitted wrong.

A shard of blade, eight inches, dark metal drinking the firelight. The second fragment matched it—two pieces of a sentence, waiting for the rest.

Veyra reached toward them and both shards rang softly, one clear note, like ice settling on a pond. The nearest watchers flinched. Veyra smiled—small, exhausted, certain.

"They remember," Ilsette murmured. "Twenty years in a hole in the ground and they still remember what they're for."

Brannoc handed the wrappings to me personally, and his grip lingered a moment longer than the transaction required. "Lowmarket," he said quietly. "I heard what happened there. I heard what you picked up mid-ruin."

"Then you heard rumors."

"I heard that a scabbard quenched in oath-memory sang in a burning town." His eyes were unreadable, coal-lit. "I heard that the woman who forged it finally picked up what she threw down. Don't make me regret vouching for you, forge-wife. I've precious few vouches left."

"You and me both, old man."

We left at dawn, four of us now—Emrys, Veyra, me, and the fragments singing faintly against my back like two halves of a word trying to remember its meaning. Behind us the quarry folded itself back into the hills, and I didn't look at the graves.

We'd made maybe a mile when Veyra stopped dead on the path, went white as the frost on the gorse, and grabbed Emrys's arm hard enough to bruise. She signed with her free hand, fast and shaking:

*Riders. Grey. Behind us—they passed the quarry an hour ago. They know.*
