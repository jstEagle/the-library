## 23. Brannoc's Verdict

I have held trials before. In the old days, when the Order of the Spoken Blade still had halls worth naming and a roster longer than a funeral list, I sat in judgment on perhaps forty oathbreakers. I was good at it. Stern, fair, immune to tears. The apprentices used to say old Brannoc's face could freeze a forge fire.

That was before my face learned what it was actually for.

Now I stood in the lamplit hollow of Quarry Deep — our rebel hold carved into a dead limestone mine, all dripping stone and paranoid geometry — and looked across the trial circle at Emrys Winterbourne, and my face did what it wanted. Which was nothing. Which was worse.

"You know why you're here," I said.

"I can guess," she said. "You want someone to blame."

"I want the *truth*." My voice cracked on it like green wood. Twenty-two years I'd kept that word polished like a relic, and here was a woman who'd spent the same twenty-two years hiding under floorboards, daring to bring it into my house.

The quarry folk crowded the gallery ledges above us — maybe sixty souls, the last uncorrupted remnants of an order that once numbered four hundred. Maimed veterans, grey-bearded survivors, a handful of children we'd spirited out of one fire or another. They watched with the particular hunger of people who have been waiting two decades for a verdict and will be furious no matter which way it falls.

Sable stood beside Emrys, arms crossed, radiating the specific defiance of a woman who'd already confessed her own sins in front of these same people three days prior and hadn't liked how it felt. Veyra stood behind them both, small and silent, wrapped in a borrowed cloak too big for her, watching me with eyes that were far too old for the face they lived in.

"Read the charges," I said.

Dalla stepped forward, our record-keeper, a stooped woman with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a landslide. She didn't need the scroll she carried; she'd memorized it, as she memorizes everything, including, I suspect, the exact number of times I've lied to her. "Charge the first: that on the night of the Purge of Candlemas Hall, Emrys Winterbourne, then First Sword of the eastern chapter, failed to sound the alarm when the king's hunters breached the gate."

"I sounded no alarm," Emrys said quietly, "because there was no alarm to sound. The wards were cut from inside. By the time I understood—"

"Charge the second," Dalla continued, inexorable, "that Winterbourne alone survived the hall intact, unwounded, having fought no one."

"I fought three men," Emrys said. "I killed none of them. I wish now that I had, if killing them might have bought the novices thirty more seconds. It wouldn't have. I've done the arithmetic every night for twenty years."

"Charge the third—" Dalla paused, and I heard it, the hesitation I'd paid her well to suppress, "—that Winterbourne's testimony regarding who cut the wards has never been given, in full, to any survivor tribunal."

Silence. The drip of water off limestone. Somewhere below us, the quarry forge hissed.

"That testimony," I said, stepping forward, "is this: Emrys Winterbourne was seen speaking with Master Corvane the night before the purge. Arguing with him. And Corvane, may his traitor ghost rot wherever ghosts rot, is the man whose cipher keys opened our doors to the king's hunters. Every account agrees. The wards fell to *order* keys. And the last man known to have argued with Corvane walked out of Candlemas Hall through a servants' door that only someone who knew the building could have found — while children burned."

Emrys didn't flinch. That was the thing about her that made twenty years of hating her so damned difficult. Most people flinch. She just went stiller, the way steel goes still before it decides what it is.

"Say the rest of it, Brannoc," she said. "Since we're doing truth tonight."

"The rest of it," I said, and my mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, because I'd rehearsed this speech in the dark of my bunk for two decades, "is that I don't care what your explanation is. You want to tell me Corvane acted alone? Fine. You want to tell me you argued because you *suspected* him and were trying to talk him back? Also fine. It changes nothing. The order died and you lived, and you never came looking for the rest of us, never sent word, never lifted a finger in twenty years of hiding while the Regent's grey cloaks hunted down every survivor one by one. Marta of the Fen Reaches. Old Hollis. The twins, Ash and Sorrel, who were *fifteen*. All dead or turned. And you — the greatest swordspeaker of her generation — counted your stores and sharpened your knives in a mountain hut and called it survival."

"It was survival," Emrys said.

"It was *cowardice* dressed in sensible clothing!"

My own voice shocked me. It came out rawer than rehearsal, rawer than the frozen-face judge I'd meant to be. Above us, someone in the gallery muttered agreement. Someone else hissed them quiet.

"You're right," Emrys said.

Of all the answers I'd armed myself against, I had somehow not prepared for that one.

"You're right," she said again, and her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "I hid. Not because I feared death — death would have been simpler. I hid because I believed I deserved to lose everything, and losing everything is very comfortable once you stop struggling. I told myself I was keeping the last of the order's knowledge safe. I wasn't. I was keeping myself safe and calling it penance. If you want to condemn me for cowardice, Brannoc, condemn me. I'll agree with every word. But hear the rest first, because the rest matters more than my shame."

"The rest can wait for—"

"No." Sable's voice, flat as an anvil head. "No, old man. She gets to finish. I've stood where she's standing. You gave me three days ago exactly as much mercy as you're giving her now, which is to say a knife's width less than none. Hear her out, or I swear by my broken oath I'll start telling these good people what *you* did on purge night, and we both know I've read the same accounts you have."

The gallery went very quiet.

I should mention, since honesty appears to be contagious tonight, that Sable was bluffing about the accounts. There were no accounts of what I did on purge night. There couldn't be. Accounts require witnesses, and witnesses require someone to have stayed.

But she didn't know that. And the fear that crossed my face — I felt it happen, felt the muscles betray me like mutinous garrison troops — was answer enough for anyone watching closely.

Veyra was watching closely.

* *

Here is the thing about guilt that no one tells you when you're young and certain and holding a gavel: it doesn't sit where you put it. You bury it deep, you stack stone over it, you build a life on top and call the weight foundation. And then some night — some ordinary night, a trial, a torchlit circle, a child's eyes — the ground shifts, and you discover the guilt has been tunneling the whole time, and it's not buried beneath you at all.

It's the ground.

"Candlemas Hall," I said, and my voice came out strange, distant, like someone else's echo. "Third bell past midnight. I was master of the western watch. Four hundred and eleven souls under my key."

Emrys frowned. "Brannoc, what are you—"

"Hold." I held up a hand. It was shaking. I let it. Some verdicts you deliver standing; this one I delivered kneeling, though my knees took their time about it. "You asked for truth. Here's mine, and Dalla can scratch it into the record with her own hand, because no one else alive knows it."

The water dripped. The forge hissed. Sixty faces leaned in from the dark.

"When the wards fell, I was awake. I want that understood. I wasn't sleeping like the others — I'd been restless, some old man's insomnia, and I heard the hunters come over the wall the way you hear rain before it arrives. A softness. A wrongness. I had time. Do you understand? I had *time*. Long enough to run the east corridor and wake the novice wing. Long enough to open the escape galleries — the ones under the chapel, the ones built into the mountain itself, big enough for every child in the hall. Long enough to do the one thing a master of the watch exists to do."

I looked at my hands. Old man's hands, scarred, competent, useless.

"I ran west instead. West was my own quarters, my own books, my own blade. And the postern gate beyond. I told myself — I have told myself for twenty-two years, every night, the same lie worn smooth as river glass — that I was going west to raise the alarm. To rally the masters. And it's even true that I shouted as I ran. I shouted *to arms*, and I woke perhaps a dozen fighters, and six of them died buying minutes that saved no one, because by then the fires were lit and the galleries I could have opened were smoke-filled anyway."

"Not anyway," Emrys said softly. "The east galleries vented clean. I checked them after. I checked everything after." She closed her eyes. "They were clear. Cold air, all the way down. The children would have lived."

"They would have lived." I repeated it, tasting it, the way you taste a wound to learn its shape. "And I stood outside the postern gate with my key in my hand — my key, the master's key, the one key that opened those galleries from without — and I listened to my order die, and I turned west down the mountain, and I walked. For three days I walked. And when I finally stopped walking, I told the first survivors I found that I'd been in the archive wing when the attack came, that I'd fought my way out, that there had been no time. And they looked at me — old Brannoc, stern Brannoc, the man whose face could freeze a forge fire — and they *believed* me. Because I was the one who judged everyone else. Who would suspect the judge?"

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above, a child — one of ours, one of the ones we'd saved from later fires — shifted against her mother's side.

"So when I charge Emrys Winterbourne with cowardice," I said, "understand what you're hearing. You're hearing a man sentence his own reflection and call it justice. I built this place. I gathered the survivors. I guarded the fragments with paranoia worthy of a dragon. And I did all of it not from faith but from arithmetic — every soul saved was a coin laid against the debt of the ones I abandoned. But debts of that size don't reduce, friends. They only accrue."

"Brannoc." Dalla's voice, gentle as she ever got. "The charges against Winterbourne—"

"Dismissed," I said. "All of them. Struck. Not because she's innocent — I believe she's guilty of nearly everything I named. But because this tribunal has no standing. Cowards judging cowards. We'd need a braver court than any of us to try her, and I don't know where we'd find one." I laughed, and it came out awful. "Perhaps the child."

Every eye went to Veyra.

She hadn't moved. All this confession, all this ruin, and the girl had stood like a stone in a stream, letting the current break around her. But now she stepped forward, out of Emrys's shadow, into the torchlight, and I saw clearly for the first time how thin she was, how burn-scarred the backs of her hands were, how her silence wasn't emptiness but pressure — a held breath the size of a language.

She looked at me. Just at me. And then she opened her mouth and spoke.

One word.

I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it. The First Tongue isn't heard so much as *remembered* — it bypasses the ear and knocks directly on whatever door in you holds your oldest truths. The word she spoke had no translation I could give you. What it did, in me, was open.

And the memory came up out of the ground like water from a struck spring, undeniable, total.

Not new memory. Nothing I didn't know. That's the cruelty of it — the truth-word doesn't reveal; it *removes*. It takes away the smooth river-glass lie, the twenty years of polish, and puts the raw event back in your hand, sharp as the night it happened. I was at the postern gate. I had the key. The screaming was starting in the east wing, small voices, the youngest novices housed nearest the wall, and I stood there with the master's key warm in my fist, and I *turned away*. I felt my boots choose. I felt the gravel take my weight, step after step, each one a small vote for myself. And behind me, through the smoke beginning to pour from the windows, a child's voice calling — not for help, worse than help — calling *"Master Brannoc?"* Calling my name the way children call it, with perfect confidence, with absolute faith that the person it belongs to is coming. Ash. Little Ash, who chewed his quills and slept sitting up and had once told me he'd be a swordspeaker like me someday, gods forgive us both.

He called my name four times.

I counted. I have always counted. On the road west, in the years after, in the dark of this quarry, I counted them: four calls, growing smaller, the fourth barely a breath, and then the fire took even the counting.

The word ended. Veyra closed her mouth. And I was on my knees in the torchlight of Quarry Deep, sixty people watching, weeping the way I imagine a man weeps when a splinter he's carried for two decades finally works its way to the surface — not gently, not cleanly, with blood and gratitude tangled past separating.

"There," I managed. "There's your verdict, Dalla. Write that down instead."

* *

What happened next took longer than the confession, because grace always moves slower than judgment. Judgment is a swordstroke; grace is setting the bone afterward.

They got me to a stool. Someone pressed something hot into my hands — Dalla's bitter quarry tea, which tastes like punishment and works like forgiveness. Around me the gallery murmured, and I knew what they were murmuring: the old judge fallen, the foundation cracked. Let them murmur. A foundation built on a lie deserves its crack.

Emrys crouched in front of me. Twenty years of hiding had lined her face, but her eyes were the same eyes that had once bested me in the training yard at nineteen, quick and level and unbearably kind for someone with her record.

"You left children," she said. Not accusation. Inventory.

"I left children."

"And I left everyone. So neither of us gets to be the order's conscience anymore." She almost smiled. "Which means the job falls to the mute child and the blacksmith, and frankly the order's prospects are better than they've been in decades."

A wet, broken laugh went around the gallery. Even I managed half of one. Grief does that, sometimes — cracks you open and lets the air in.

"The fragments," I said.

Emrys went still.

"You came here for them. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. Two fragments, guarded under my key, in the deep vault where I've kept them since the year of the Fen Reaches massacre. The fourth and the fifth, if the count you've assembled holds." I looked down at my hands again. "I was going to make you crawl for them, Winterbourne. I had a whole ceremony planned. Testimony, witnesses, conditions. I was going to make you earn what I had no right to withhold. Because withholding them was the last power I had, and men like me cling to power the way drowning men cling to anything, even when it's the thing pulling them under."

"And now?"

"Now." I stood. My knees objected. I overruled them; a man should get to stand for his own disgrace. "Now a child who owes me nothing handed me the truth I owed everyone else, and charged nothing for it, and I understood — finally, at the end of a long and stupid life — that this is what the order actually was. Not blades. Not oaths. Not judgments. It was this: someone handing you the unbearable thing, freely, so you could carry it instead of hiding it. Everything else was architecture."

I turned to Dalla. "Open the deep vault. Both fragments, full escort, sealed writ. And enter into the permanent record—" my voice caught, and I made myself push through, "—the name of Ash of Candlemas Hall, novice, second year, who trusted his master, and whom his master abandoned. Let it be the first line. Before my name. Before anyone's."

Dalla wrote. Her ink-stained fingers didn't hesitate, which is the closest thing to mercy I've ever received from her.

Emrys touched my shoulder — light, brief, a swordswoman's touch, checking the edge of something. "Brannoc. What you did tonight cost more than staying silent ever would have."

"Yes. That's generally the exchange rate." I straightened. "Go. Take your fragments and your child and your blacksmith and go do whatever mad errand the First Tongue has set you. But hear my condition — my last act of jurisdiction, and I'll thank you to allow it."

"Name it."

"When you reach the capital — and you will reach it, because the child's dreams show it burning and dreams like hers don't waste themselves — when you face the thing wearing the king, you speak Ash's name into it. Along with the others. Marta. Hollis. Sorrel. All the fourth calls that never got answered. Make that ancient horror carry the weight of every name it tried to burn out of the world. Not vengeance. *Remembrance.* It feeds on forgetting. Starve it."

"I swear it," Emrys said. And I felt the words settle — felt them the way you feel a true oath, in the teeth, in the spine — and realized with something like terror that the last swordspeech I would ever witness had just been spoken by a woman with no magic left to waste, and it had bound me anyway. Perhaps that's all the First Tongue ever was. Perhaps we spent centuries mistaking the vessel for the wine.

They left at dawn. I watched them from the quarry lip until the mountain road took them — the disgraced swordspeaker, the informer-blacksmith, and the small silent girl carrying a language older than crowns — and I felt lighter than I had in twenty-two years, which is either grace or the simple physics of setting down something enormous.

Then I went below, to the vault that was empty now, and sat in the dark among the racks built for seven fragments and holding none.

That was where Dalla found me at midmorning, moving fast, breathing hard, which Dalla never does.

"Riders," she said. "On the south pass. Grey cloaks. A dozen or more — and Brannoc, they're not searching anymore." She swallowed. "They're marching. Straight for us. And the lead rider carries a standard I haven't seen since purge night."

I knew the standard before she described it. Of course I did. Some things you count forever whether you want to or not.

"Sound the alarm," I said. And this time, gods help me, I meant it.
