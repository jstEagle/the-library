## 24. Six Blades, One Silence

Six fragments of a broken order lay on Sable's forge table like the pieces of a confession no one wanted to finish.

I arranged them by feel before I touched them—old habit, older grief. The hilt from beneath my floorboards, twenty years of my hiding pressed into its leather wrap. The blade-section we'd pulled from the drowned monastery, still cold as a drowned man's hand. The third from Sable's own forge, singing when she held it because apparently guilt has a frequency. Two more from Brannoc's paranoid little kingdom in the quarry, wrapped in oilcloth and guarded by a man who'd looked at me the way you look at a debt you intend to collect. And the last—the sixth—a slim length of steel Veyra had carried against her ribs since the night she arrived at my door, though none of us had known it until the quarry.

Six. When Corvane's ghost had first spoken to me through the hilt, he'd said *seven*, the way a dying man says water. Seven fragments, seven speakers, one oath. I'd spent the road counting them like coins, and now they were all here, and the arithmetic refused to balance.

"Say it again," Sable said. She stood with her arms crossed, soot to the elbow, watching the table the way a smith watches metal that might crack in the quench. "Slowly."

"The seventh fragment," I said, "isn't missing. It was never lost." I turned the hilt over. Its tang—the stub of steel meant to run down into a grip—was sheared clean, melted at the edge into a smooth bead. "Corvane's blade was broken on the steps of the Hall of Oaths during the purge. Every account agrees. But the accounts don't say what happened after. Who swept up. What the crown did with what it gathered."

Brannoc sat by the fire with his ruined hands open on his knees. He hadn't forgiven me—he'd yielded the fragments, which is not the same thing and may never be—but when he spoke now his voice had the flatness of a man reporting weather. "They didn't destroy them."

"They couldn't." I let that hang. "You can't unmake an oath-blade. You can only change what it's bound to. So the Regent changed it. Melted the seventh fragment down and folded it into—" I stopped, because saying it made it real, and part of me had been hoping someone would contradict me.

"Folded it into what?" Sable asked, though her face said she already knew. Smiths always know. They know where things go when kings want them hidden in plain sight.

"The regalia," I said. "The crown itself. Or the sceptre. Possibly the great sword of state they lay across the throne at coronations. Something the king touches every day of his reign. Something that gets carried before him in procession so the whole realm can see it gleam." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bitter as frost. "We've been hunting a relic for months, and it's been sitting in a glass case in the capital the entire time, being polished."

Veyra made a sound—not a word, but close. Since the Steppe her voice came and went like a tide; each word cost her something visible, a flicker at the corner of her eye, a fine new line I pretended not to count. Now she signed, quick and precise: *The crown. I saw it. In the dreams. The burning archive, the woman pressing the word into my palm—behind her, above the fire, there was a crown on a pole and the fire bent around it.*

"The Regent kept it safe," I said. "Of course it did. It's been wearing our seventh piece for ten years. Wearing it the way it wears Aldric."

Nobody said anything. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind coming off the high passes made the sort of sound I used to mistake for wolves and had long ago learned was just the mountain breathing.

It was Brannoc who broke the silence, and I will give him this: he didn't soften it. "Then the Unbinding cannot be spoken here. It cannot be spoken anywhere but the throne room, with all six assembled and the seventh within reach of the king's own head. Which means you intend to walk into the seat of the thing that has spent nine centuries eating kingdoms, carrying the only weapons that can hurt it, directly to its door." He looked at each of us in turn. "I've condemned you once already, Emrys Winterbourne. I find I have nothing left to condemn you with. But I want you to say it plainly, so these two can choose with their eyes open."

So I said it plainly.

"We go to the capital. We get the six fragments past the gates—which means past the grey cloaks, which means a miracle or a betrayal, possibly both. We reach the throne room, which no one reaches except by invitation, execution, or coronation. And then I speak the Last Oath through all seven pieces at once, and the Regent is named and bound and cast out of Aldric's body." I paused. "And the price of speaking an oath freely given is the speaker's swordspeech. Forever. That's if everything goes well."

"And if it doesn't?" Sable asked.

"If it doesn't, the Regent takes the fragments, takes Veyra—who is the last living vessel of the First Tongue—and continues wearing the realm until there's nothing left worth wearing. So." I spread my hands. "No pressure."

* * *

We spent three days preparing, and I want to be honest about those days, because heroism is mostly logistics.

Sable worked the forge from dark to dark, and I finally understood what she'd been building since Lowmarket burned: not a scabbard for one fragment but a harness, a rigging of oiled leather and iron fittings that could hold all six pieces against the body, under a pilgrim's cloak, where a searcher's hands would find only a woman's ribs and a bad posture. She quenched the fittings in water she'd steeped with ash from the purge-fire she'd carried out of Lowmarket in a clay jar like a relic, because Sable believes materials matter, and who was I to argue with a woman whose half-forged oath had just taught itself to sing?

"It won't stop a blade," she told me, testing a strap until the stitching groaned. "But it'll hold. And the fragments hum when they're together—I felt it in the forge, low, like bees in a wall. If they're against your skin, you'll feel them start before anyone hears them. That's your warning. That's your only warning."

Brannoc drilled us on the roads—not the smuggler paths I knew, which were watched now, but the pilgrim routes, the ones that flow toward the capital like blood toward a wound, thick with the desperate and the devout. He knew the checkpoints. He knew which captains took bribes and which took heads. He drew maps in the ash of the hearth and wiped them away with his palm after, every time, a habit from years of trusting no one.

And Veyra—Veyra taught me.

We sat evenings by the fire while the other two slept, and she fed me the First Tongue the way you feed a fire, one word at a time, watching my mouth shape sounds older than any kingdom I'd sworn to. My order had used the Tongue the way masons use mortar: structurally, without tenderness. Veyra used it the way water uses a riverbed. She showed me how the same syllable could bind or release depending on where you placed your breath, how truth-words weren't spoken so much as *allowed out*.

"You'll need it in there," she signed, then added aloud, carefully, spending a word like a coin: "*Enough.*"

Enough meaning: enough to name him. Enough to finish it.

I practiced the refusal too. Chapter and verse of it, the formal renunciation in the First Tongue that Corvane's ghost insisted must come first—an Unbinding spoken by a speaker still bound to the old order was a contradiction, and contradictions die badly. So every night I stood in Sable's yard under the cold stars and said, aloud, in a language older than crowns: *I am not the Order's. I am not the Crown's. I am mine, and mine is what I give.*

Every night it got easier. Every night that frightened me a little, how easily surrender learned my mouth.

* * *

On the last evening, Corvane came.

He came the way he always did—not summoned, just arriving, like weather, standing in the corner of the forge where the light didn't quite reach, translucent and patient and insufferably composed, the way he'd been in life and somehow managed to remain in death.

"You heard," I said. Not a question. Ghosts hear everything within a blade's memory, and the fragments were all within arm's reach of each other for the first time in twenty years, humming their low chord on the table.

"I heard." His voice was the voice I'd carried in my head for two decades, the one that said *again* when I dropped my guard and *better* when I did well, which was rarer. He looked at the six pieces a long moment. "Do you know why the Regent melted the seventh into the regalia? Why not bury it in a mountain, sink it in the sea?"

"Because buried things get found."

"Because it wanted them close." He drifted nearer the table. "An oath-blade remembers its purpose even melted. Folded into a crown, worn by a wearer—it whispers. Every king it has eaten heard it, faintly, like a conscience. Some of them fought it longer because of that whisper. Your fragment has been the itch inside the glove for nine centuries, Emrys. The Order didn't fail. The Order has been fighting this whole time, from inside the enemy's own head."

That landed harder than he probably intended. I sat down on the forge stool because my knees had opinions.

"Aldric," I said. "The ninety seconds. The chapel cipher. You think that's the fragment? That's *him* fighting?"

"I think a man who leaves coded instructions for his own rescuers, telling them not to save him if the cost is the realm—that man has been listening to a whisper of truth for ten years and holding onto it with both hands." Corvane's gaze found me, and for a moment he looked less like a ghost and more like my master, tired and certain. "You asked me once, the night before the purge, why oaths must be spoken freely to hold. Do you remember what I answered?"

"That a compelled vow is just a cage, and cages make prisoners clever at escaping."

"Mm. The Regent has never understood that. It compels everything. It compels its hunters, its kings, its lies. And so everything it holds is waiting to escape." He smiled, faint and sad. "You are going to walk into the most compelled place in the world carrying the one thing in creation that must be given freely. Do you understand yet why you cannot lose?"

"Because everything in that palace is a cage," I said slowly, "and I'm the only thing in it that isn't."

"Better late than ever, Winterbourne."

Then, because he was my master and dead men keep their habits: "Again."

* * *

We left before dawn, the four of us—though Brannoc would peel off at the quarry fork to raise what rebels remained, a rear guard, a diversion, a promise. At the edge of the pass I stopped and looked back once at the hold, at the school-that-didn't-exist-yet, at the life I'd built out of snow and silence. Twenty years I'd kept that door shut. Now I was walking away from it with six pieces of a broken promise strapped to my chest, humming against my heart like a second pulse.

Sable fell into step beside me. "The hum," she said quietly. "Listen."

I listened. Beneath the wind, beneath my own blood: a chord, faint and steady, six voices in imperfect harmony, waiting for the seventh.

"They're not afraid," she said. "Steel doesn't get afraid. It just remembers what it's for." She glanced at me sideways, wry as ever. "Try to be more like the steel."

Behind us, Veyra signed something I didn't catch, and Brannoc laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

Ahead, far south beyond the passes, the capital sat under its winter haze, crowned and gleaming, keeping our last piece warm.

I put my hand over the harness, felt the chord answer through my ribs, and started walking.
