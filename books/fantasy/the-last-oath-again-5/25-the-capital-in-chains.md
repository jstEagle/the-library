## 25. The Capital in Chains

The road to the capital is lined with banners, and every banner lies.

That's the first thing I understand about Aurelian City, before we even pass the outer walls: a city that hangs this many flags is trying very hard to convince itself of something. The banners are white and gold, the king's colors, and they snap in the wind with a sound like applause that never stops. Emrys walks beside me with her hood up and her face angled down, which she's been doing for six days now, and I've learned to read her silences the way other people read speeches. This silence says: *don't look at them too long. Looking is how you get remembered.*

So I look anyway. It's what I'm for.

I am eleven years old, or I was eleven years old when the archive burned, though my reflection lately shows me someone older — twelve, maybe thirteen, with new lines at the corners of my eyes like a woman who has wept for decades. Every word I speak spends a little of myself. So I keep a ledger now, in my head, the way Emrys counts stores: *this sentence costs a day. This one costs a week.* Most days I spend nothing. I hoard my voice like a miser hoards coins she's afraid to touch.

But inside my own head, oh, inside my own head I am *wealthy*. Inside my own head I never stop talking.

*They're lying*, I tell Emrys, tapping her elbow twice, our code for things too expensive to say aloud.

She glances at the nearest banner — ALDRIC THE AGELESS, TEN YEARS OF GOLDEN PEACE — and her mouth does something that isn't quite a smile. *Kings don't age*, she signs back, hands low, movements small. *They just get reprinted.*

I didn't know the word *reprinted* until Sable taught it to me, back in Lowmarket, before Lowmarket burned. Sable talks about printing presses the way other smiths talk about hammers: as tools that shape whatever you put under them. A press doesn't care if the metal is true. It only cares about the mold.

Aurelian City is a city in a mold.

* * *

We come through the Pilgrim's Gate at midmorning, mixed into a river of the faithful — farmers with sun-scorched necks, grandmothers clutching votive ribbons, whole families in matching gray who have clearly walked weeks for this. The guards barely glance at us. Why would they? Pilgrims are the safest lie in the world, because no one checks whether a pilgrim believes. Belief is assumed. That's the whole trick of it.

Emrys has my hair bound up under a scarf and ash rubbed into my cheeks so I look like any travel-worn child dragged along on her grandmother's devotion. Brannoc would have hated this disguise. Brannoc, who held a gate so we could live, who died saying my name in the First Tongue like it was a door he was holding open — he'd have said disguising me as a pilgrim was an insult to actual pilgrims. He was probably right. He was right about almost everything except himself, which is the way of old men with secrets.

Sable walks on my other side, broad shoulders bent into a stoop she doesn't feel, carrying a pilgrim's bundle that holds two oath-fragments wrapped in wool and prayer-cloth. Six fragments, all told, distributed among us like pieces of a broken spine. They hum against each other when we walk close, a note so low most people would call it imagination. I don't have that luxury. I hear it in my teeth.

The gate guards wave us through, and then the city opens up in front of me, and I stop being able to think in anything but fury.

* * *

Here is what they told me about the capital, in the archive, in the years before it burned: it was loud, and greedy, and magnificent, and honest in its dishonesty. My teachers loved it and despised it in the same breath. A city, Master Corvane used to say — his ghost-self, the version of him that lives behind my eyes now along with everything else he hid from Emrys — a city is a lie everyone agrees to tell together, and the capital is simply the biggest agreement in the realm.

He didn't warn me about the *love*.

Because that's what I see in the faces streaming past us toward the palace square. Love. Real love, unforced, warm as bread. A baker has set out trays of honey-buns shaped like crowns, free to pilgrims, *in honor of His Majesty's decade of grace*. Children chase each other through the crowd wearing paper masks of the king's face, gold-painted, serene. An old man stands on a crate telling anyone who'll listen about the day King Aldric pardoned his son — *the king himself, looked right at me, boy, eyes like summer* — and the crowd around him nods along, nourished by it.

And above it all, on every wall, on every banner, painted on the sides of buildings three stories tall: the king's face. Young. Unlined. Smiling the same smile in every image, because it isn't ten images of one man, it's one image printed ten thousand times. The same smile I saw through Aldric's eyes in the moments the Regent let him surface. The smile that signed execution orders.

*It's not him*, I want to scream. *The thing you love is wearing him like a coat. He is in there. He is drowning in there. You are throwing flowers at his drowning.*

I grab Emrys's sleeve and tap her arm, fast, angry, and she reads the rhythm of it even without the proper signs. She puts one hand briefly over mine. Steady. *Not here*, the pressure says. *Not yet. Anger keeps. Anger's the one food that doesn't spoil.*

She would know. She's been keeping hers for twenty years.

* * *

We lodge that night in a pilgrims' hospice run by the Order of the Quiet Hand, which is either genuinely charitable or very good at looking like it, and possibly both. We pay in copper and gratitude. The bunks are straw and the blankets smell of lavender gone stale, and for the first time since the shrine ambush I sleep more than an hour at a stretch.

I dream of the archive burning. I always dream of the archive burning. But tonight the dream changes its costume: the flames become banners, the falling shelves become crowds, the screaming becomes singing. In the dream, thousands of people stand in the square watching the library burn, and they are *happy*, and a woman with the king's printed face moves through them handing out honey-buns, and I try to shout *stop, stop, you're cheering for the fire,* and no sound comes out, because that is the oldest fear I own, older than the burning, older than the flight north —

—and then I wake, and I realize the singing isn't in the dream.

It's coming through the hospice window. The whole city is singing. It's some kind of hymn to the Ageless King, ten thousand voices in the dark, rising off the rooftops like heat haze. I sit up in the straw with my heart slamming and listen to a city serenade its captor, and I understand, finally, why the Regent doesn't need grey cloaks and hunters anymore. Not primarily. The hunters are for the edges, for people like us. The center holds itself.

Why send soldiers when your prisoner will build his own prison and call it paradise?

*Comfortable lies*, Emrys called them once, on the mountain, in one of her rare full sentences spoken after dark. *People don't cling to comfortable lies because they're stupid. They cling to them because the truth has teeth, and they've already been bitten once.* She hadn't been talking about the capital then. She'd been talking about herself, about twenty years of pretending to be nobody. But she might as well have been describing this city, this anthem, this sea of upturned loving faces.

The difference between them and her is that she bit down on the truth eventually. Even if it took a mute child collapsing on her doorstep to make her chew.

* * *

Morning. The palace square.

We go early, three pilgrims among hundreds, to see the Decade Procession — the celebration of ten years since "the King's Renewal," which is what the printers call the day the Regent finished eating him. I need to see it. That's not strategy, or not only strategy. Emrys laid out the plan in whispers last night: the throne room, the regalia, the seventh fragment melted into the crown itself, the Unbinding spoken inside the lion's mouth. But before the plan, I need to *see*. I need to know exactly what we are walking into, and what we are walking against.

The square is a bowl of humanity. Scaffolding stands draped in white silk where the ceremony will be staged tomorrow. And there, on a platform beneath it, the royal family — or what the realm is told is the royal family. Queen Isolde, beautiful and distant, her smile a professionally maintained hedge. The young princesses, waving on schedule. And at the center, when he finally comes out:

King Aldric.

Ten years, the banners say. Ten years of golden peace. And the man on the platform looks like he walked out of a painting made thirty years ago. Not a line on his face. Not a gray hair. The crowd gasps with delight, the way crowds do, and someone near me whispers, *blessed, truly blessed, there's never been a king like him,* and the person next to her agrees, and the agreement spreads outward like ripples, and I stand in the middle of it feeling sick.

Because I can see what they can't. I have the First Tongue in my blood and the Regent's taste in my memory, and when I look at Aldric — really look, the way I looked at the burned archive, the way I look at anything I intend to remember — I can see the seams. The way his smile arrives a half-second before his eyes agree to it. The way he blinks in perfect metronome intervals, like a clock wearing lashes. The way his shadow falls slightly wrong in the morning light, a half-beat behind his body, as if even his shadow is a passenger running to catch up.

He's in there. Ninety seconds at a time, maybe less now. Somewhere behind those printed eyes, a man is pressing his face against the inside of his own skull, watching his hands sign death warrants, watching his people sing to the thing that stole him, and he cannot even scream because screaming requires a mouth and the mouth belongs to the tenant.

And they *adore* it. They adore the tenant. They would defend the tenant with their lives.

My hands are shaking. Sable notices — Sable always notices, she has a blacksmith's eye for things under strain about to fail — and puts a heavy hand on my shoulder. I shrug it off. I don't want steadying. I want the shaking, because the shaking is honest, and this square is the least honest place I have ever stood.

* * *

Here is what makes me angrier than the Regent, though I'd never say it where Emrys could hear, because she'd give me one of her looks, the ones that hold whole sermons: the Regent at least has the decency to be a monster openly, in its own fashion. It eats kingdoms. Fine. That's what it is. A wolf eating is a wolf being honest.

But these people — the baker with his crown-shaped buns, the old man with his pardon story, the children in their paper masks — they are choosing the lie. Choosing it daily, warmly, with honey on their fingers. Because the lie comes with peace, and cheap bread, and a king who never ages, and never having to ask the question *what did we lose to get this?*

I know about losing. I lost a language, a home, a woman named Maerin who pressed a word into my palm and shoved me out a window ahead of the flames. I lost my voice for two years and got it back at the price of my own face aging like wax in the sun. I am owed anger. I have receipts.

And still — and this is the thought that frightens me, the one I turn over at night in the straw like a stone with something living under it — still, I understand them. When I was fleeing north, starving, I passed through villages that had decided the purge hadn't happened. Whole towns agreeing, gently, firmly, that the swordspeakers had been traitors, that the burnings were regrettable but necessary, that the smoke they'd all seen was just weather. I could have argued. Instead I ate their bread and kept my mouth shut, because arguing gets mute children noticed, and noticed gets them dead.

Is that so different from singing to the Ageless King? Is survival's arithmetic really so different from comfort's?

Yes, I decide, gripping the stone in my pocket — yes, and no, and the distance between *yes* and *no* is exactly the size of a kingdom. They sing because the lie feeds them. I was silent because the truth was hunting me. One silence is surrender. The other is a wound being kept clean until it can be properly opened.

Ours is the second kind. Tomorrow we open it in front of everyone.

* * *

Evening. Back at the hospice. Emrys spreads a charcoal sketch of the palace on the floor — she drew it from memory today while I watched the procession, her hand moving under her cloak with the blind fluency of someone who grew up in these halls before they belonged to a monster — and Sable marks the guard rotations she counted, and I add what I saw: the seams in the king's smile, the wrongness of his shadow, the scaffold positions.

Then Emrys looks at me across the candle flame, and taps her own throat twice, and signs: *Tomorrow may cost words. Are you ready to spend?*

I look at my hand in the candlelight. The knuckles of a child. The fine new creases of a much older woman creeping in around them, bought word by word, a debt collected in skin.

*I'm ready*, I sign back.

But what I don't sign, what I keep in the wealthy country behind my teeth, is this: I saw something else today, at the procession, something I haven't told them. As the king turned to leave the platform, just for a moment, his printed smile flickered — and underneath it, for less than a heartbeat, his face did something no printer ever molded.

He looked directly at me. Across four hundred strangers. Into my eyes.

And his lips moved. No sound. Just shapes. Shapes I recognized, because they were the oldest shapes I know, the ones Maerin pressed into my palm in a burning archive:

*Hurry.*
