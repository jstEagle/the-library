## 26. Sable's Bargain

The thing you have to understand about selling a betrayal is that it doesn't feel like anything. That's the trap. I'd spent twenty years imagining that if I ever sold out the order again—if I ever became, one more time, the woman who traded other people's lives for her own skin—it would feel like swallowing broken glass. It doesn't. It feels like haggling over the price of horseshoes.

That's what frightened me most.

* * *

We'd been in Lowmarket four days when the problem announced itself in the shape of a city gate.

Emrys had laid out the plan on my forge floor with charcoal and a rag: six fragments humming in their wrappings like a basket of angry bees, one Regent sitting on a throne wearing a king like a coat, and exactly zero ways to get armed blades through the capital's gates. Because the gates had changed since any of us last saw them. There were grey-cloaked inspectors now, and they didn't check for contraband. They checked for *resonance*. A fragment of an oath-blade within twenty paces of a grey cloak would sing like a struck bell, and everyone in the queue would know precisely what was being carried and by whom.

"So we don't carry them through," Veyra said, in her new voice—that voice that cost her something every time she used it, each word a coin off her own lifespan. She rationed herself like a miser now. When she spoke, we all shut up and listened, because she was spending years to do it.

"Then how?" Emrys asked.

"The scabbard," Veyra said, and looked at me.

I should have seen it coming. Everyone always looks at the blacksmith.

Here's what I'd made back in chapter—back at the forge, before the quarry, before Brannoc and his paranoid little kingdom of survivors. I'd forged a scabbard for the first fragment, and because I'm a sentimental fool who never fully learned to stop loving the order that ruined me, I'd quenched it in oil that had steeped with oath-memory. Old blade-oil, from the purge days, soaked in the grief of everything we lost. The fragment sang sweeter in that scabbard than it ever had naked. And somewhere between there and here, without anyone saying it out loud, the plan had grown a spine made of my workmanship: the fragments would travel inside things I'd made, shielded by craft instead of magic, because craft doesn't resonate. Craft is just honest metal doing its job.

But a scabbard isn't enough. Scabbards hold swords, and we needed to walk into the throne room with actual weapons, and the gates would ring us out like liars.

"Weapons can't pass," Emrys said. "So we go unarmed."

"And when the Regent's guards close the throne room doors behind us?" I said. "You've met this thing, Wren. You've read Aldric's cipher. It invited you in. Do you trust an invitation from a thing that's eaten nine kingdoms?"

Silence around the forge fire. Veyra's eyes went to Emrys, Emrys's to the floor, and mine—I'll be honest—went to the window, where I could see the main road into Lowmarket, and the dust of riders on it.

Riders in grey.

* * *

They came into town at midmorning, six of them, and took rooms at the Drowned Bell like ordinary travelers, which was the first lie, and began asking questions like extraordinary ones, which was the second. By noon I knew their sergeant's name was Hesk and he drank porter not ale and he tipped badly and he was asking, in that soft reasonable way hunters have, after a party of pilgrims. Three pilgrims. One older woman with mountain manners, one smith, and a girl who didn't talk.

The girl who didn't talk. That was the detail that made my hands go cold on my hammer. They knew about Veyra's silence, which meant they knew about her voice, which meant they'd survived whatever she'd done to them at the shrine—and if they'd felt her speak once, they'd be listening for it forever.

Emrys wanted to slip away that night, take the river road, put forty miles between us and the grey cloaks before dawn. Sensible. Everything about Emrys for twenty years has been sensible; it's her best armor and worst sin.

"It won't work," I told her. "Hesk's already got the town mapped. He's paying children to watch the roads. We run, we run straight into the next net. There are nets all the way to the capital now—you said so yourself, after the Steppe."

"So what's your answer? Fight six hunters in a market square?"

"No," I said. "My answer is that I'm going to go have a drink with Sergeant Hesk."

Emrys looked at me for a long time. She's got these eyes that have seen too much and forgiven too little, least of all themselves, and when she looks at you like that you feel audited.

"You're going to sell us out," she said. Not a question. She'd already done the arithmetic. That's the thing nobody tells you about Emrys Winterbourne: underneath all that guilt she's the coldest tactical mind I ever met, and she will let you walk into hell if hell is the shortest road.

"I'm going to sell them something," I said. "Whether it's us depends on how good I am."

* * *

The Drowned Bell smelled of wet wool and bad decisions, which described half its clientele and all of its beer. Hesk sat in the corner with his back to the wall and his face arranged into the expression of a man doing a job rather than enjoying a calling. Grey cloaks always have that look, I've noticed. The corrupted speakers, the ones who survived the purge by kneeling—they don't have the zealot's fire. They have the employee's shrug. Somehow that's worse.

I sat down across from him uninvited and ordered two porters, because I'd watched him drink three and I do my homework.

"You're the smith," he said. "Sable Ironhew." My name in his mouth sounded like a file being drawn across a lock. "Formerly of the Order of the Spoken Edge."

"Formerly," I agreed. "Now I make hinges."

"Now you make hinges." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a precise little man performing precision. "And yet the resonance-readers in Marrowgate logged a fragment signature passing through your town two weeks ago. Faint. Shielded. Shielding takes craft." His eyes came up. "Craft takes a smith."

Here's where I tell you what selling a betrayal actually consists of, since apparently I'm the only one honest enough to say it. It consists of giving a man ninety percent truth so he'll swallow the ten percent that kills him. Hesk already knew a fragment had passed through. Denying it would mark me as either a liar or a fool, and fools get searched and liars get tortured. So I gave him the truth like a gift.

"That was mine," I said, and let my face do the thing faces do when an old wound gets pressed. "I kept a piece of my master's blade. Twenty years under my floorboards. I couldn't—" I stopped. Let the stopping do work. "I broke my oath to live. I thought keeping the steel meant I hadn't broken *everything*."

Hesk studied me with the patience of a man paid by the hour. "And now?"

"Now it's gone. Sold it, if you want the ugly version. To a woman who came through buying relics. I told myself she was a collector." I drank. "She paid in crown silver, sergeant. New-minted. Somebody in the palace is collecting oath-steel, and I don't think it's for a museum."

That was the ten percent. Crown silver, new-minted—the Regent's own coin, the Regent's own appetite, pointed away from us and toward a fiction. Let the hunters spend their days chasing a phantom collector in the palace's employ. Let them tear apart their own supply lines looking for a traitor among their masters. Paranoia is a weapon and I'd just forged it in a tavern.

Hesk was quiet long enough that the porter went flat between us.

"You understand," he said finally, "that I can't simply believe you."

"I'd think less of you if you did."

"There's a reading-stone in my saddlebag. Standard procedure."

"Standard procedure," I agreed, and held out my hands, palms up, the way you offer them when you've got nothing left to hide or you're very good at pretending. He laid the stone against my skin and it stayed dark as a dead man's eye. Of course it did. My hands hadn't carried oath-steel in weeks; everything we had was wrapped in my craft and hidden in the flour cart, thirty feet of honest iron shielding dishonest magic. But watching that stone sit cold against my palm, I felt something turn over in me—not relief. Recognition. This was the same stone they'd used in the purge days. Same procedure. Same bored men doing thorough work. I had stood in a line like this once, aged nineteen, while my friends' blades rang and mine stayed silent, and my silence saved me and damned me in the same heartbeat, and here I was again, silent, saved, damned.

"Some water finds its level," Hesk said, almost gently, pocketing the stone. "You broke your oath and lived. Most of us did the same arithmetic." For one moment his employee's shrug slipped and something tired looked out. "The collector. Describe her."

And here's where I want you to hate me properly, because I didn't hesitate. I built her whole. Tall, I said. Mountain-born. Sword-calluses on both hands, not one—a duelist's grip. Grey streak in dark hair, worn braided back. Eyes like a winter audit. I described, in other words, a woman who could exist, a woman plausible enough to launch six hunters down three false roads—and I built her out of pieces of Emrys, because the only face I could see clearly enough to describe under pressure was the truest one I knew, and then I changed it. Changed the braid to the wrong side. Made her taller. Gave her a scar she doesn't have. Every trait true except the ones that mattered.

A portrait of Emrys with the soul removed. If they catch her, they'll swear it isn't her. If they don't, they'll chase her ghost for months.

"Left for the coast road yesterday," I added. "Alone."

"Alone." He wrote it down. "And the girl? The mute one?"

"What girl?"

He looked at me. I looked at him. Somewhere in that look was the entire moral architecture of my life: the informer who informs carefully, the traitor who betrays only in form. He knew I was managing him. I knew he knew. Neither of us could prove a thing, and neither of us wanted to, because proving things means paperwork and paperwork means someone above you starts asking why a fragment signature walked through your jurisdiction and vanished.

"The reward," he said instead, "for information leading to recovery of order materials. It stands. If you remember anything else."

"I'll be sure to sell it to you," I said, and raised my flat beer, and we drank to our mutual contempt like old friends, which is what we were, sort of: two survivors of the same massacre wearing opposite uniforms.

* * *

Emrys didn't speak to me for the rest of the day. Not angry-silent—she does angry-silent like a drawn blade—but *thinking*-silent, which is worse, because thinking-silent means she's revising her opinion of you and you can't influence the draft.

It was evening before she found me at the forge, banking the coals for the night. Veyra was asleep upstairs. The fragments hummed their low note in the flour cart, muffled by my craftsmanship, patient as buried seeds.

"She had my braid," Emrys said. "Wrong side. But my braid."

"You noticed that."

"I notice everything about my own ghost, Sable. I've been living with her twenty years." She sat down on my anvil block, which no one is allowed to do, which meant she needed the disrespect more than the rule. "You gave them my shape."

"I gave them a scarecrow wearing your coat. They'll hang it in their imagination and salute it, and meanwhile we walk through the Marrowgate gate tomorrow as a smith and her two hired servants, carrying nothing but my tools—which are mine, and honest, and which they have already cleared, because I'm a registered informant now with a clean stone and a boring face."

"You're a registered informant," she repeated.

"For a false trail. Yes."

"For a false trail." She said it twice, turning it over like a coin she suspected was clipped. Then, quietly: "When they find out—and they'll find out, Sable, this thing has nine centuries of practice at finding out—what do you imagine they do to registered informants who sold them a fiction?"

"I imagine they do what they did at the shrine," I said. "And I imagine I'll be holding something I forged when they try." I banked the last coal and watched it breathe red. "Look at me, Wren. I sold the order once to stay alive, and it bought me twenty years of waking up at night listening to my own silence. I'm not going to buy our lives with a lie that cheap. This one costs. This one puts my name in their books and a target on my forge and a debt on my head that only gets collected one way. That's the difference between then and now. Then I betrayed people to save myself. Now I'm betraying myself to save people." I shrugged, because what else is there. "The form's identical. That's what makes it a real betrayal instead of a clean one. There are no clean ones. I checked."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the forge ticked as it cooled, and upstairs the child slept the dreamless sleep of someone whose voice costs more than gold.

"When this is done," Emrys said finally, "if there's an order again—if there's anything again—you'll be in it. Not because you earned it. Because you never really left."

"That's the cruelest thing you've ever said to me."

"I know." She almost smiled. It looked like weather clearing over a graveyard. "Sleep, smith. Tomorrow we carry treason through the front gate in a flour cart."

* * *

We went through at dawn, in a line of carts and pilgrims and farmers with geese, and the grey cloak at the Marrowgate checkpoint waved my cart past with the bored courtesy owed to a registered informant, and I felt the fragments humming under the flour sacks like a heart under ribs, and I smiled at the man the way you smile at a man you lied to over beer, and he smiled back the way men smile when they think they own you.

Behind us, the capital rose against the morning haze, white walls, gilded spires, banners celebrating a king who had never aged.

And ahead, on the road, waiting at the milestone like she'd been planted there—a tall woman, mountain-built, dark hair braided down the wrong side, a sword-callused hand shading her eyes.

Not Emrys. Emrys was beside me in the cart.

Someone had found my fiction. Someone had dressed it in flesh and set it on our road, smiling.

Which meant the Regent wasn't chasing my false trail.

It was answering it.
