## 27. The Chapel Cipher

The chapel of Saint Verenne smelled of candle wax and old apologies.

That was my first thought, standing in the doorway with Veyra's small hand gripping two of my fingers and Sable looming behind us like a bad decision in a smith's apron. Every chapel I had ever entered smelled the same way—incense laid over something older and less fragrant, which is guilt, if you were wondering. Guilt keeps better than myrrh. It's the one thing kings and smugglers have in common; we both store it in our holy places and pretend it's reverence.

"Cozy," Sable muttered. Her voice echoed anyway. Chapels are built to make sure your sins have good acoustics.

"Hush," I said, though she wasn't wrong. The royal chapel was smaller than I'd expected. After twenty years of imagining Aldric's palace as a mountain of marble built on the bones of my order, I'd pictured a cathedral. Instead: a modest vaulted room, six pews, a single altar, and a window of blue glass that threw colored light across the floor like spilled wine. Whoever had designed it had wanted somewhere to be small. Even kings need a room where they can remember their own size.

Veyra tugged my fingers. She looked up at me with those eyes that had learned too early how much the world costs, and she signed—quick, impatient, the way she signed when she thought I was being slow.

*Where?*

"I'm getting there," I murmured.

Because here was the problem. We knew the king left instructions—we'd known since Lowmarket, since a dying courier's pouch yielded a scrap of parchment covered in what looked like a merchant's cipher but read, when you held it to the right light and the right memory, like the marginalia of an oath-speaker's primer. Ninety seconds at a time, the reports said, the king surfaced inside his own body like a drowning man breaking water. And somewhere in those stolen breaths, he'd written us a letter. Hidden it where no grey cloak would think to look, because no grey cloak would think a prisoner could hide anything at all.

But knowing a letter exists and finding it are separated by exactly the kind of cleverness I've never trusted in myself. I break things. I speak to steel. Riddles are Sable's territory or Veyra's, and Veyra couldn't tell me what she saw, only show me, hands flying, frustration building in her shoulders like a storm front.

She let go of my hand and walked into the chapel like she owned it.

I should mention that by this point we had been in the capital for four days, disguised as pilgrims, and I hated every hour of it. Pilgrims! Me. There is a special irony in walking openly through the city that burned your order while wearing a pilgrim's badge of Saint Verenne—the very saint whose feast day the purge happened on, because the Regent has a sense of humor, and its humor is the worst thing about it. The city had draped itself in banners for the Festival of the Never-Ageing King. Children sang songs about how His Majesty's face hadn't changed in ten years, praise be. A baker sold little marzipan Aldrics. I bought one, because I wanted to know what we were eating, and then I ate the king's marzipan head first, which improved my mood by perhaps four percent.

Four days of that. Four days of watching people love their cage because the bars were gilded. And now a chapel, at dusk, while the bells rang for evening prayer and every guard in the district was counting pilgrims through the festival gates instead of counting them out.

Veyra stopped before the altar and tilted her head. Then she did something I will remember until I die, however long the silence lasts after.

She knelt—not to pray, but to look—and pressed her palm flat against the altar stone the way you'd press your ear to a door. Then she turned and beckoned, urgent, both hands.

"What is it?" Sable said. "What does she see?"

"She doesn't see," I said slowly, crossing the floor. "She listens."

Because I finally understood. Of course. You don't hide a confession from speakers of oaths behind locks and ciphers. Locks are for people who fear thieves. If Aldric had been told—if someone in his ninety-second windows had remembered the old training, the things the order whispered to its children—you don't encrypt a message for a swordspeaker. You *bind* it.

I knelt beside Veyra and put my hand against the stone.

Nothing. Cold marble, nothing more. Twenty years since I'd heard a blade speak, and stone had never been my medium anyway—oath-speech runs through worked metal, through steel quenched with intent, not through masonry. My heart did the thing hearts do when hope gets ahead of evidence: it fell, and took the rest of me down with it.

Then Veyra took my wrist and moved my hand three inches to the left.

And the stone *sang*.

Not aloud. Not the way steel sings, bright and immediate. This was deeper, slower—a vibration under the surface, like a bell struck once and buried. Someone had set a blade into that altar. Not a whole sword. A sliver, a spine of oath-forged steel laid into the marble beneath a skin of stone, and bound into it with the First Tongue was a voice.

Aldric's voice.

"He hid it inside consecrated ground," Sable breathed, kneeling on my other side. "Inside the one place the grey cloaks can't tear apart without the whole priesthood asking questions."

"He hid it where only someone who could hear oath-speech would ever find it," I corrected. "Which means he's been waiting twenty years—" I caught myself. No. He'd been waiting ten years, since the thing put him on. Ten years of ninety-second windows, and somewhere in one of them he had remembered enough of the old stories to do this. "He knew someday a speaker would come looking."

"How?" Sable asked.

I didn't answer, because the answer was Veyra, kneeling there with her hand still flat on the stone, and I didn't yet want to say out loud what that implied—that the king had known about the child-lineage, or suspected it, or been told by whatever fragment of himself still dreamed true. Some truths you unpack one box at a time.

"Can you open it?" Sable asked. "You're the speaker."

"I'm a *former* speaker," I said. "And even at full strength, unbinding someone else's oath-work takes—" I stopped. Looked at Veyra. She was already looking at me, and she nodded, once, calm as deep water.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. Do you know what speaking costs you? One word ages you a year, maybe more. We've watched it happen. I am not spending your life to crack a lock."

Her expression didn't change. That was the worst part—the patience of her, the terrible adult patience sitting in a child's face. She lifted her hands and signed, slow, so I couldn't pretend to misunderstand.

*A king wrote this. For us. It would be rude to leave it unread.*

"Rude," Sable repeated, and laughed despite herself, a short ugly bark. "Gods preserve me, she sounds like you."

"I know," I said miserably. "It's the worst thing about her."

But Veyra wasn't finished. She signed again, and this time her hands trembled slightly, and I understood that she had been carrying this thought since the lake, since the drowned monastery, since the first time she screamed a word and felt a decade of herself burn away as the price:

*Some words are worth their weight.*

I sat back on my heels. Above us the bells finished their pealing, and the colored window dimmed as the sun went down behind the palace roofs, and I thought about all the prices paid to get five strangers into this room—a ferryman in irons, a blacksmith's forge in ashes, an old man holding a gate with his body, a child burning years off her life word by word like a candle burning from both ends. Brannoc's face came to me the way it always came now, in the quiet moments. *Go,* he'd said, or rather shouted, over the ring of steel. And then his last words, the forgiveness, which I still hadn't figured out how to carry without it cutting me.

We had spent so much to get here. I would not waste it by being precious about one more coin.

"All right," I said. "One word. Choose it yourself. Make it small."

She smiled. It was the first smile I'd seen from her in days, and it was devastating, and I understood in that moment why the Regent feared her, why it had hunted the child-lineage for generations. Not because she was powerful. Because she was *willing*, and willingness is the one weapon no tyranny has ever learned to counter.

She cupped her hands around her mouth, leaned close to the stone, and spoke a single syllable of the First Tongue.

I heard it cost her—I saw it, actually; the fine lines at the corners of her eyes deepened, her jaw sharpened, a year of childhood traded away in half a heartbeat—and then the altar answered.

The slab split along a seam no eye could find, hairline-thin, and swung inward like a door. Inside was a cavity the length of a forearm, lined with lead, and in it lay a book. Small, plain, bound in grey leather. The color made my stomach turn—grey, always grey—but the stitching was wrong for the Regent's people. It was careful. Loving, almost.

I lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping animal, or a live coal.

* * *

We read it by candlelight in the pilgrim hospice, three heads bent over one table, with the door barred and Sable's hammer within reach, which is Sable's version of locking a door.

The book was Aldric's handwriting—or mostly. The script wandered. Some pages were firm and level, a king's trained hand. Others shook, letters sliding downhill, written by a man fighting for control of his own fingers. Those were the pages that mattered. I recognized the difference immediately, because I'd seen the same thing in the journals of oath-speakers resisting compulsion: the possessed hand writes smoothly, fluently, *confidently*. Truth comes out crooked.

"He fought for paper," Sable said softly, reading over my shoulder. "Ten years, and he stole scraps of himself to write this."

"He's been writing it for ten years," I said. "Look." I turned back to the first pages. Dates. Dozens of dates, each entry a few lines, some a few words. The earliest entries were desperate and confused—*I do not know what I agreed to. There is a gap between my coronation and the wedding, and Maren will not meet my eyes anymore, and I think Maren is dead.* Later entries grew disciplined, compressed, a man learning to use his windows like a soldier learns to ration water. And threaded through them all, recurring, patient as tide: instructions.

"It's a primer," I realized aloud. "It's not just a confession. It's a *lesson plan*."

"Read the middle part again," Veyra signed, and then, when I hesitated, she reached over and turned the pages for me, to an entry dated eight years past, written in the shaking hand.

*I found the old records today—the ones the fire missed. In the ninety seconds I have learned to move fast. The Regent does not watch me read about grain tariffs. But when I opened the archive of the Order of the Speaking Blade, I felt it notice. Like a cold draft through a shut door. So listen, whoever you are, whoever it sent you to find: it fears one thing. I have tested this. I insulted it once, alone, in my own skull, and it flinched. Not at the insult. At the shape of the words. They were an oath-form. I had sworn, that morning, at my daughter's grave—freely, no compulsion, no crown, just a father and a hole in the world—that I would find what killed her. And when I turned that oath against the thing inside me, it recoiled.*

*Understand what I am telling you. It has worn eleven kings. It eats realms the way rot eats timber—slowly, from the throne outward. It cannot be fought with steel. It cannot be starved or besieged. It can only be NAMED. Named by an oath freely given, spoken through a blade that carries no master's command. An oath freely given is the one language it does not speak, because everything it has ever said, it has said through someone else's mouth.*

*Its true name is in these pages. I pulled it from the oldest record, from the coronation rite of the second king it wore, before the order scrubbed names from the liturgy. Learn it. Guard it. And when you come—and you will come, because I have arranged for you to come—do not save me if saving me costs the realm. I mean this. I have written it a hundred ways. Let the man die. Free the kingdom.*

Below that, in ink of a different shade, added later, steadier:

*The name follows. I write it in parts, in case the wrong eyes find this book. Assemble it as the old order taught: crown to root, root to flame. If you are reading this, you already know the teaching. If you do not, find someone who does.*

And then, page after page, fragments. Syllables broken apart and scattered among mundane entries—grain stores, border levies, complaints about a steward—each piece wrapped in ordinary words like a jewel sewn into the lining of a coat.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The candle fluttered. Somewhere outside, festival crowds were singing the Never-Ageing song again, sweet and mindless, and it curdled my blood to hear it now, knowing what wore the smile they sang about.

"Crown to root, root to flame," Sable said at last. "That's binding-order. First Tongue grammar. Veyra?"

Veyra had gone very still. She was staring at the page of scattered syllables, and her lips were moving—not signing, moving, shaping silent sounds. Reading it. Reading it *natively*. To her, the First Tongue wasn't a scholarly puzzle; it was the language she'd been pressed into as an infant, the word a dying woman had pushed into her palm in a burning archive. She began to sign, translating as she went, and her hands slowed as she worked, the way hands slow when the water gets colder than expected.

*Crown: "Ashkarion." Root: "vel." Flame: "duneth."* She frowned. Signed again, slower. *It's not a name like a person's name. It's a name like... a sentence. Ashkarion-vel-duneth. It means—* she paused, hunting for it, hating the hunt— *"the guest who remains after the house is empty."*

"The guest," I repeated.

"The guest who remains," Sable said, "after the house is empty." She rubbed her arms. "That's the most horrible thing I've ever heard, and I've heard my own cooking described."

But I was thinking about something else. Something that had teeth.

"Sable. What did the scabbard do? Your forged scabbard, the one you quenched in oath-memory. When the fragment broke, it held the binding. Why?"

Sable blinked. "Because I made it for that purpose? Because I poured everything into it—"

"You made it freely," I said. The idea arrived whole, the way the important ones do, fully armed. "No order commanded it. No oath-bound craft. You chose it, in the middle of a burning town, because it was yours to choose. That's why it worked when the true fragment failed. Freely given work carries freely given speech." I looked down at the book, at the king's crooked, courageous hand. "And that's why it can be named. That's why it flinches. Everything the Regent says is borrowed. Every word it has spoken in nine centuries has come out of someone else's mouth. It has never once said anything that was *its own*—because it has nothing of its own. Nothing freely given. It's a guest with no house, a voice with no throat. And an oath freely given is a sound it cannot counterfeit and cannot endure."

"So we walk into the throne room," Sable said slowly, "and shout its name at it."

"We speak it," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Shouting is noise. Naming is an act of will. It has to be given—an oath, freely offered, through a vessel that carries no compulsion. Through Veyra's voice." I caught myself and softened it. "If she chooses. Only if she chooses."

Veyra was already nodding. I put my hand gently on her book-page, on the king's warning—*let the man die, free the kingdom*—and met her eyes.

"And we hear him," I said. "Whatever happens in that room. Whatever the Regent offers us, whatever Aldric begs us, whatever it looks like when the guest is finally evicted. We remember that the man in there asked us, a hundred times, in his stolen ninety seconds, to value the kingdom over his life. That's not despair talking. That's dignity. We owe him the respect of taking it seriously."

Veyra studied me for a moment. Then she signed something that undid me completely, quietly, without drama:

*He wrote to us for ten years. We will not leave him unread either.*

Outside, the singing swelled. The festival would run three more nights; the palace would be crowded, distracted, full of processions and feasting and a king displayed on his balcony like a prize marlin that never rots. Three nights. Our best and probably only window.

Sable snuffed the candle with her fingers. "Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we buy our way past the gate, and I get to go commit another betrayal, and you get to practice saying a monster's name without throwing up."

"Ashkarion-vel-duneth," I said quietly, testing it. The syllables sat strange on my tongue—heavy, ancient, faintly warm, like a stone left too near a fire. Somewhere far off, I imagined, something that had worn eleven kings felt a draft through its shut door.

Veyra touched my arm. She pointed toward the shuttered window, toward the palace heights where the royal banner hung motionless in the windless dark.

And in the silence between the distant verses of the festival song, very faintly, I heard it—the sound I had spent twenty years learning to dread. Steel, singing. Not our blades. Theirs. Many of them, moving through the night streets below, converging on the pilgrim quarter with the patient certainty of wolves who have finally picked up the scent.

"They know," Sable breathed, hammer already in hand.

"Not yet," I said, though my heart was pounding the truth at me. "But they will by morning. So we move tonight."
