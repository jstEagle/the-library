## 28. Grey Cloaks at the Gate

I have been called many things in my long and largely disappointing life—oath-breaker, coward, old fool—and I will confess that at least two of those were earned fair and square. But nobody, in eighty-one years of walking this sour earth, ever called me a hero, and I had rather hoped to keep my record clean.

The gate had other ideas.

Let me set the scene properly, because if you're going to die somewhere, you deserve an honest accounting of the scenery. The capital's pilgrim road funnels through the Weeping Arch, a crooked stone throat built by masons who clearly hated horses and possibly themselves. Beyond it, the palace district opens up like a fan—all white walls and gilded spite, the Regent's city dressed in its Sunday best. Behind us, three days hard ride back along the pilgrim road, the grey cloaks would be moving fast and quiet, the way they always did, like weather with a grudge.

Emrys wanted to push through the arch before dawn. Veyra argued they should wait for the change of watch. Sable said something unrepeatable about both plans and proposed a third involving a wine merchant's cart. And I stood there in the shadow of the arch doing arithmetic on my fingers, because that is what old men do instead of sleeping.

Three days' ride behind us. Maybe two and a half, if their horses were better than mine—which everything was. One narrow gate. Four travelers, of whom one was a child who aged a year per word spoken, one was a smith with a scabbard full of miracle, and one was a woman who hadn't slept properly since the Steppe took her name and gave it back with fingerprints all over it.

And me. Old Brannoc, keeper of two oath-fragments, hater of Emrys Winterbourne for twenty years, recent convert to the cause via the humbling mechanism of being publicly wrong in front of a nine-year-old.

"You're counting," Emrys said. She has a way of saying things she already knows the answers to. It used to infuriate me. It still does, but now it also frightens me, which I suppose is growth.

"I'm planning," I said.

"Planning what?"

"The part where we don't all die in the same place." I looked at the arch, then back down the road, where the morning fog was doing its usual impression of nothing being wrong anywhere. "They'll catch us here. Not maybe. Here. This is the only choke between the Steppe and the palace where four people can be stopped by six. Everywhere else they need twelve."

Sable spat. "So we go around."

"Around adds a day and a half through the tanneries, and the tanneries have eyes. The Regent pays for eyes the way other lords pay for bread." I scratched under my beard, which is where I keep most of my best thinking. "No. Someone holds the arch. Makes noise. Looks important. Buys the rest of you the hour you need to walk into that palace without a parade."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. You could have upholstered furniture with it.

"No," said Emrys.

Interesting thing about that word. Twenty years ago, when she said no to me across a burning courtyard, I heard it as betrayal. When Sable said no to joining us in Lowmarket, I heard it as weakness. But when Emrys said *no* just then—flat, immediate, the way you'd refuse poison offered to a child—I heard it plain: it was the sound of someone who had already buried too many people deciding not to practice.

"That's very touching," I told her, "and I'm going to pretend it moved me, because it did, but let's review your qualifications for holding a gate against grey cloaks. You're the speaker. The Unbinding needs you breathing and articulate. The child is the voice. The smith carries the seventh vessel, or whatever we're calling her scabbard this week. And I am—" I spread my arms, presenting myself the way a fishmonger presents the day's disappointment, "—an old man with two fragments, a bad knee, and a sword arm that hasn't been tested since before your parents were courting."

"Your arm held fine in the quarry," Sable said.

"My arm held fine against frightened quarry guards who mostly wanted their lunch. These are different animals." I turned to Emrys fully. "Winterbourne. Look at me. I fled the purge. I left children behind in that courtyard—you know it, the whole order knew it, and Veyra dug it out of my skull with a truth-word while I wept like a gutted pig. Everything since has been penance performed badly at a distance. This—" I tapped the stones of the arch, "—is the first time in my miserable life the bill has come due in person, and I intend to pay it, because the alternative is watching you lot get taken on the palace steps and knowing I could have stood in a doorway and prevented it."

"You don't have to buy redemption with your corpse," Emrys said quietly. "Veyra spoke your guilt open so it could heal, not so it could collect."

"Aye, well. Children say generous things. Old men know how accounts work." I smiled at her, and I felt the smile crack somewhere in the middle, because it's hard to smile when you mean something. "Besides, I'm not dying today. I'm inconveniencing people today. Dying is a rumor I've refused to confirm for eight decades."

She didn't laugh. Neither did Sable. Veyra, who had gone very still in the way she did when the First Tongue pressed against the backs of her teeth, walked over to me and took my hand—the scarred one, the one I'd broken on a door during the purge trying to get back inside to children I never reached—and pressed something into it.

A word. Not spoken. Spoken words burned her; I'd seen the cost line her face like a much older girl's. Instead she mouthed it, shaping the syllables slow, teaching me the way Master Corvane once taught us all: *listen-with-your-mouth*. And because I'd spent twenty years pretending I'd forgotten the First Tongue, and because lying to her had become impossible sometime on the Steppe, I listened with my mouth.

I won't write the word here. Some words are doors, and you don't leave doors standing open in a story that grey cloaks might read. But I'll tell you what it meant, roughly, in the tongue of ordinary people: *the debt is paid*. Past tense. Settled. Signed.

Then she squeezed my hand once, let go, and walked toward the palace district like a small person with an appointment to unmake a god.

* * *

They left me an hour's start and everything useful.

That sounds bitter. It isn't. Emrys gave me her second blade—a plain soldier's sword, no oath in it, honest steel—and Sable gave me a whetstone and a look that communicated more than most sermons. Veyra gave me the word. And Emrys, last, before she turned down the pilgrim road toward the arch's far side and the city beyond, gave me the hardest thing she owned.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Not for leaving me," I said quickly, because I couldn't have borne it if she'd apologized for that.

"For making you carry it alone for twenty years. For letting you believe the worst version of yourself was the true one." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "You weren't the only one who ran, Brannoc. I just ran further."

And there it is. Twenty years of hate, and it turns out the woman I hated had spent the same twenty years hating herself with better technique. There's a joke in there about oaths and the people who swear them, but my humor died somewhere around the second volley of crossbow bolts, so I'll let it lie.

I watched them go until the fog took them. Then I climbed the arch.

The Weeping Arch has a parapet—narrow, crumbling, built for sentries in some king's paranoid century—and from it you can see the whole pilgrim road winding back through the hills like a gray thread somebody dropped. I sat on that parapet with my borrowed sword across my knees and ate the lunch Sable had packed, which was excellent, because even facing death, a smith respects a man's need for good bread and pickled onion.

I thought about the children.

Not the ones I failed—no, that's a lie, and this chapter deserves better than my usual lies. I thought about the ones I failed *first*, and then about the ones I'd guarded after, the two fragments hidden in the quarry, the survivors I'd ruled with paranoia and called it wisdom. I'd told myself for two decades that keeping the fragments safe was keeping the faith. It wasn't. Keeping the fragments safe was keeping *myself* safe with extra steps. Faith moves. Faith walks down a smuggler's road with a mute child and a woman it forgave too easily. Faith sits on a cold parapet eating onions, waiting for riders in grey.

Faith, it turns out, looks a great deal like fear wearing better boots. The difference is only what you do when the riders crest the hill.

They crested the hill at midmorning. Six of them, then four more strung out behind, then—God help us—a third party on foot, moving with that terrible unhurried economy the corrupted ones have, as though the world were a corridor and they merely late for a meeting. Grey cloaks, faces like unsent letters. At their head rode a woman I knew.

Maren Solis. Third chair of my own training circle, once. She'd been the one who could speak a blade sharp enough to shave with. Now her sword hung at her side whispering—I could hear it from the parapet, a sound like flies in a jar—and her eyes had the flat shine of glass over deep water.

"Brannoc of the Ninth Circle," she called up. They always use your full name. The purge taught them names are handles; grab the handle, lift the person. "You have items belonging to the Crown."

"I have opinions," I called back, "belonging to myself. Come up and I'll share."

"There were five of you at the shrine," she said. "Four went into the Steppe. Where is the fifth?"

"Ah." I stood, knees cracking like green firewood, and leaned on the parapet so she could see the whole of me: old, alone, armed with a borrowed sword and a stolen hour. "You're asking the wrong question, Maren. The right question is: why does the Regent send thirteen hunters after four travelers? What could four travelers possibly carry that frightens a thing that's worn eleven kings?" I grinned down at her, and I confess I enjoyed it, which I mention as evidence that my soul required whatever came next. "It's frightened, Maren. Your master is *frightened*, and it's spending you like copper to make the feeling stop."

Something moved behind her glass-flat eyes. That's the tragedy of the corrupted—they're still in there, most of them, passengers in their own bodies, exactly as Aldric is a passenger in his. Maren had been a person who laughed at bad jokes and cried at weddings. Whatever wore her now let her watch.

"Step aside," she said, "and be judged leniently."

"I've been judged leniently my whole life," I said. "It's how I got away with fleeing. No, thank you. This gate is closed."

* * *

Here is what I learned about fighting in your ninth decade: it is mostly logistics.

I couldn't win. Any fool could see the arithmetic—thirteen against one, young against old, whispered blades against honest steel. So I didn't fight to win. I fought to *spend*. Every minute they spent climbing the arch was a minute Emrys didn't spend crossing the palace square. Every hunter I made cautious made the others cautious, because grey cloaks are brave in crowds and cowards in doorways. My job was not to defeat them. My job was to be expensive.

I was very expensive.

The first two came up the eastern stair, and I met them at the top where the parapet narrows, where one old man equals one young man if the old man knows the stones. I do know the stones. I helped rebuild this arch's parapet thirty years ago, in another life, as a favor to a mason whose name I've forgotten and whose mortar has held better than most of my oaths. A loose block here, a slick patch of lichen there, a handrail that gives way at precisely the height of a falling man's center—I had prepared the ground the way I'd once prepared testimonies, thoroughly and without conscience.

One went off the parapet into the ravine. I want to be honest: he screamed the whole way down, and I will hear it on the inside of my eyelids for whatever time remains to me, and I would do it again, and both of those facts are true, and that's war for you—no clean lines, just debts accruing.

The second I fought properly, blade to blade, and discovered to my surprise that my arm remembered everything. Twenty years of paranoia had kept my shoulder limber, if nothing else. He was faster. I was meaner. Mean wins in doorways.

Then Maren came up the western stair, and the arithmetic changed.

I could tell you the fight blow by blow, and a part of me wants to, because the vanity of old soldiers is bottomless. But the truth is the middle of any battle is a smear—you remember flashes, smells, the taste of blood that might be yours. I remember her blade whispering where it shouldn't, that fly-jar sound, and my borrowed steel singing back dull and honest, outmatched and unashamed. I remember taking a cut along the ribs that I decided, in the moment, to classify as a scratch on the grounds that classifying it otherwise would inconvenience my plans. I remember backing her to the parapet's edge and seeing, for one heartbeat, the real Maren surface in her own eyes—terrified, present, *begging*—before the thing wearing her dragged her back under.

And I remember understanding, in that gap between heartbeats, that I couldn't kill her. Not because I lacked the opening. Because she was a passenger, same as the king, same as I had been a passenger in my own cowardice for twenty years, and cutting her down wouldn't free her. It would just be one more entry in the ledger the Regent kept of everyone the order had lost.

So instead I did the only thing left in my arsenal. I grabbed her by the collar, put my face an inch from hers, and shouted the word.

Veyra's word. *The debt is paid.* I shaped it clumsy, my accent atrocious, my grammar probably criminal—but the First Tongue doesn't care about grammar. It cares about truth. And it was true. Every ruined, ragged, reluctant scrap of it was true: my debt to the dead children, my debt to Emrys, my debt to my own abandoned honor—paid, settled, signed, sealed in the oldest language there is, freely given, which is the only currency the First Tongue accepts.

The effect was—not what I expected. I expected nothing. I got everything.

Her blade stopped whispering. Just stopped, mid-stroke, like a fly dying in the jar. And Maren's eyes—her *own* eyes, wide and wet and twenty years old again—filled the whole flat glass of her face, and she made a sound I will not describe except to say I've heard it twice in my life, both times from people coming up out of deep water.

The word didn't free her. Freeing takes an Unbinding, and I'm no Emrys Winterbourne. But it cracked her. It cracked her the way frost cracks stone—one season, one word, patient as winter. She staggered back off me, clutching her silent sword, staring at it, and behind her on the stairs the other grey cloaks faltered because their leader had faltered, and that, children, is how you hold a gate: not with strength, but with a truth delivered at the correct velocity.

I bought them forty minutes. Maybe fifty. Long enough for four figures to become specks, then become nothing, swallowed by the white sprawl of the palace district. I checked between exchanges, when I could spare the breath—leaning on the parapet, bleeding generously, watching the road to the city stand empty of everything but fog.

Empty. They were through. Somewhere past that fog, past the gates and the propaganda and the adoring crowds, Emrys Winterbourne was walking toward a throne room with five fragments, a forged scabbard, and a child who carried a language older than every crown in the world.

My work was done. Everything after that point was, strictly speaking, overtime.

* * *

Maren recovered, of course. The thing wearing her stitched its composure back together and came for me again, and this time I didn't have tricks left—only the sword, the stones, and the stubbornness of a man who has finally run out of reasons to run. We fought along the parapet in the fog, and I'll spare us both the details. Take my word: I was expensive.

The end, when it came, was almost administrative. A thrust I saw coming and declined to fully evade, on the theory that evading it would prolong negotiations I'd already concluded. It went in under the ribs, which is traditional, and I sat down heavily against the parapet wall, which is also traditional, and looked out at the fog swallowing the road to the city.

Maren stood over me, blade raised, and hesitated. I choose to believe it was her, in there, hesitating. I choose to believe a lot of things; it's one of the perks of dying.

"Brannoc of the Ninth Circle," she said, in a voice that wasn't entirely hers. "Why?"

I smiled at her. Blood got in the way of the smile, but I persisted; persistence has always been my one marketable skill.

"Because a child gave me a word," I said, "and I finally had the courage to spend it." I coughed, and the cough had opinions. "Tell your master—it can't hear me, I know, but tell it anyway—that it's already lost. Not the battle. The *argument*. It offered me safety for twenty years and I took it, and do you know what it bought? Nothing. A warm room and a cold conscience. And then one girl mouths three syllables at me on a mountain road and I'd trade every safe day for the hour I just spent." I gripped the parapet stone, the good mortar, the mason's gift. "Fear keeps you alive, Maren. But it doesn't keep you *yours*. Only truth does that. Go ask the king."

Her blade came down—or the thing in her made it come down—and I looked past it, at the fog, at the empty road, at the city where a woman I had hated and forgiven and been forgiven by was about to do the bravest foolish thing since oaths were invented.

My last words were for her, wherever she was. I hope the wind carried them. Wind owes me favors.

"Winterbourne! The debt is paid! All of it—go and be *unbound*!"

* * *

They say the dying see their lives pass before their eyes. Mine must have been embarrassed by the material, because instead I saw forward. I saw a school above the snowline—don't ask me how I saw it, dying men get previews, it's in the contract. Stone halls. Children learning to listen with their mouths. An old woman teaching without powers, authority rooted in love, and a sign on the door that read, in letters any beggar could read:

ALL WHO ARRIVE ARE HEARD.

I'd like my name carved small somewhere on the lintel. Not honored. Just included. A man who fled, who guarded, who lied, who stayed—who finally, at the last possible moment, at the last possible gate, stood where he should have stood twenty years ago, and found that the door he'd been afraid to open had been open the whole time.

The fog thickened. The cold was kind. Somewhere below, faint as memory, I heard a blade begin to sing—

—and in a throne room I would never see, a door opened, and a tired woman with a borrowed sword stepped through, and said, in a voice like a key turning:

"We're here for the king."
