## 29. Throne Room of the Wearer

The last door between us and the throne was oak and iron, older than the dynasty it guarded, and it had one guard left—a boy of perhaps seventeen with a spear he clearly hoped never to use.

He looked at us the way people look at weather they've been warned about but didn't believe: four travelers in pilgrim grey, dust to the knees, Sable's smith's arms hidden under a cloak that fit her like an apology. Brannoc's blood was still on my sleeve. I hadn't had time to wash it out. I wasn't sure I wanted to.

"Chapel pilgrims," Veyra said, in a voice pitched low and steady. She'd learned in three weeks what took most speakers ten years: that language is mostly nerve. "We were told the king receives petitioners at dusk."

"The king receives no one," the boy said, and then his eyes did something complicated—flicked to the door, to the ceiling, back down—as if he were reading instructions written somewhere only he could see. "Unless," he added, very carefully, "they come about a matter of oaths."

The word landed in the corridor like a dropped coin. Everything went quiet enough that I could hear my own heart doing arithmetic.

"He knows we're here," Sable breathed.

"No," I said. "It knows. There's a difference, and I'm counting on it."

I put my palm flat against the door. Twenty years ago I would have heard the blade-speech humming in the hinges—every great door in the palace had been sworn shut by my order, bound with words so that only rightful hands could pass. That was before the purge. Before the order burned. But the bindings, apparently, had outlived the binders, because when I pressed and whispered the opening cadence—the first thing Master Corvane ever taught me, before he taught me anything else, before he taught me anything at all except how to stand—the locks answered.

*Speak, and be known,* said the door, in the voice doors have, which is the voice of everything patient.

I spoke. The bolts drew back like animals recognizing their master's tread after a long absence.

The guard stepped aside without being asked. He looked at me once—really looked, at the scar through my brow, at the hilt wrapped in Sable's scabbard-leather—and said, so quietly I nearly missed it:

"My mother sang songs about the swordspeakers. She said you'd come back someday."

"People say a lot of things," I told him.

"She also said you'd all die badly." He shrugged. "I'm hoping she got one right and not the other."

I liked him. I decided to try very hard not to get him killed, which in my experience is the fastest way to guarantee it.

* * *

The throne room of Aldric the Ninth was designed to make you feel small, and it succeeded, because architecture doesn't care that you've crossed a steppe where your own name tried to abandon you. A hundred feet of ribbed stone overhead. Windows of colored glass depicting nine kings in ascending glory, each one slightly larger and more gilded than the last, until the final pane showed Aldric himself rendered less as a man than as a golden idea wearing boots. Banners. Braziers. The particular silence of a room built for crowds that has been emptied on purpose.

And at the far end, on a throne of black oak and white gold, sat King Aldric—or the shape of him.

He was alone. No guards, no clerks, no sycophants. Just the man, seated with his hands folded on the armrests like a father waiting up past a daughter's curfew. He did not look surprised. Of course he didn't. You don't wear a kingdom for a decade without developing good hearing.

"You took the north stairs," he said. His voice was Aldric's voice—warm, a little tired, the voice of a man who has listened to too many petitions and meant well by most of them. "Clever. The east gallery has better acoustics for ambushes, and I'd hate for this to be an ambush. Ambushes are so impersonal."

Sable's hand found her hammer. Veyra stood very still beside me, and I felt rather than saw her begin the breathing Corvane's ghost had taught us both: in for four, hold for seven, out for eight. The rhythm of someone preparing to speak words that cost.

"Emrys Winterbourne," the king said, and rose.

That was wrong. Kings do not rise for anyone. It was in the etiquette manuals; it was practically scripture. But the thing wearing Aldric descended the dais steps one at a time, unhurried, its borrowed face arranged into an expression of such genuine, terrible warmth that my skin crawled.

"Twenty-three years," it said. "You were nineteen when you ran. Do you know what struck me most, all those years ago? Not your skill—you were competent, the order made competent people the way rivers make mud—but your *ear*. When you listened to a blade, you listened the way other people pray. I remember thinking: there. That one. If I ever need to be unmade properly, that's who should do it."

"Flattery," I said, "from something that eats kings."

"*Curates* them," it corrected gently. "There is a difference, and I'm counting on it."

My own words, handed back to me with the wrapper on. I decided I hated it.

It stopped six feet away. Close enough for courtesy, far enough for caution—it knew the range of a drawn sword as well as any soldier ever born, and probably considerably better than most, having watched eleven kingdoms' worth of soldiers die. Up close, Aldric's face was a map of small wrongnesses. The smile reached the mouth and stopped at the eyes, like a tide that had forgotten the shore. The eyes themselves were brown, kind, human—and behind them, deep down in whatever cellar holds a soul, something else was looking out. Something patient. Something that had practiced being human for nine hundred years and had gotten very, very good at the parts that matter to audiences.

"You came all this way," it said. "Through the Weep. Through Brannoc's paranoia—I did enjoy that, incidentally, the trial by testimony; I've always admired your order's gift for making suffering feel like procedure. Through hunters who are, I'll grant, overzealous. And why? To speak a few syllables at me?" It spread Aldric's hands, palms up, an honest broker's gesture. "Let me save us both the theater. Let me tell you what you actually want."

What I actually wanted was to drive my blade through its borrowed chest, but the blade was singing under the scabbard leather, high and hungry, and I had learned in twenty years of hiding that hunger makes poor counsel.

"I want you out of that man," I said. "Alive or otherwise, I'm flexible."

"There it is. The famous Winterbourne directness." It smiled wider, and for one flickering second the smile was *too wide*, teeth too many, before the face snapped back like a mask settling. "Here is what I offer. Not to the child—" Veyra flinched; the thing's attention touched her for half a heartbeat and moved on, deliberately, the way you'd move past a dish you were saving. "To you."

"Aldric signs orders with my hand. Aldric pardons with my voice. Tomorrow morning, the whole realm will hear their king declare the Swordspeaker Purge a crime—the crime of the age, committed by men deceived, mourned publicly, atoned for in marble. Your order restored by royal decree. Lands returned. Names carved back into the rolls from which they were scratched. Every survivor pardoned—not just you. The smith. The ferryman rotting in a cell in Thornfell. Even old Brannoc, though between us, the man is ninety percent grievance by weight."

The brazier-light moved. Somewhere in the vaulting above, a draft sighed through stone.

"And the price," I said, because there is always a price, and things like this always pretend there isn't until you ask.

"The child stays. She comes to court. Treated gently, educated lavishly, loved even—I am capable of something adjacent to love; you'd be surprised. She simply... stops speaking the First Tongue. Retires it. Lets it sleep. And you, Emrys, go home to a hall with your name over the door, and students, and a purpose, and the profound relief of never again wondering whether hiding was cowardice." It tilted its head, Aldric's head, with Aldric's gray threading the temples. "You have spent twenty years paying rent on a grave. I am offering you a house."

And here is the thing I will not lie about, not even now, not even in the privacy of my own skull where I keep the rest of my shames: for three full seconds, I wanted it.

Not the pardon. Not the marble. I wanted the *ending*. The clean, signed, notarized ending, the one where the story closes its ledger and says: this was fear, and now it's over, and here is your receipt. Twenty years is a long time to live inside a question. It offered me an answer the way a warm bed offers a freezing man—and a freezing man does not usually stop to check whether the bed is a coffin.

Three seconds. Then Veyra shifted beside me—not toward the Regent, but *away*, a small involuntary lean, the lean of prey that has learned exactly which direction safety lies—and the three seconds ended.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard every word. I want you to hear mine." I stepped forward, past Sable's warning hand, past the edge of my own fear, and drew the blade.

Six fragments, reforged along a spine of Sable's quenched scabbard-steel, hummed as they cleared the leather—a chord, not a ring. The sound filled the throne room the way water fills a footprint, and every banner stirred though there was no wind, and in the colored glass above, nine painted kings seemed to lean away from their own reflections. Behind Aldric's eyes, the cellar-thing went very still.

"You speak to me wearing a stolen man," I said. "So I will answer you in the tongue you stole him *with*. Hear it formally. Hear it witnessed."

Veyra's voice came soft at my shoulder, feeding me the cadences we had drilled across a thousand miles of road, and I let her carry the grammar while I carried the weight:

"*Na veth su'karin. Na veth il-doma.*"

*I refuse. Freely, wholly, before witness.*

The First Tongue is not a language you translate so much as one you survive. Each word has weight the way stones have weight, and when I spoke the refusal it did not echo off the vaulted ceiling—it went *down*, into the flagstones, into the bones of the building, into whatever deep foundation the palace had been raised upon back when the world was younger and more careful with its promises. I felt the oath take hold of me even as I gave it. That is the rule none of the old texts survive to explain: a refusal freely spoken binds the speaker twice as hard as the refused. From this hour, no crown could buy my speech, no fear could silence it, no offer—however warm, however reasonable, however desperately wanted—could purchase one syllable of my swordspeech ever again.

I had just made myself permanent.

The Regent stood motionless. For a long moment the only sound was the blade's low chord and Veyra's measured breathing, and I thought—foolishly, hopefully—that maybe refusal was enough, that naming the terms would end the game.

Then Aldric's face began to laugh.

It started at the edges. The mouth first, stretching past the limits of the jaw it was borrowed from; then the eyes rolling back to show whites shot through with something dark and branching, like frost seen through ice; then the whole shape of the king rippling, once, like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone. The warmth drained out of the room. The braziers guttered. In the glass above, the painted Aldric smiled down at us with the same too-wide patience as the flesh below.

"Oh, *good*," said the thing, in a voice that was no longer entirely one voice—it was Aldric's voice braided with others beneath it, older ones, voices with accents from kingdoms whose names had been eaten along with their kings. "A formal refusal. Witnessed. Binding." The head cocked, and the neck bent too far, and the smile finally, fully arrived. "Do you know how long it has been since someone refused me *properly*? Since the last of your order stood where you stand and chose the hard syllables over the easy ones?"

"How long?" Sable asked grimly, hammer already rising.

"Nine centuries," said the Regent, and its shadow on the floor began, slowly, to stand up. "And the last one took *everything I'd built* down with her. So forgive me, Emrys Winterbourne, if I seem a little—"

The king's body split open like a coat being shrugged off.

"—*enthusiastic*."

* * *

Later—much later, when I had time to be afraid properly instead of usefully—I would remember that moment as the exact instant I understood the size of what we had come to kill. Not a demon. Not a god. A *collector*. Nine hundred years of practice at being unmade, and every attempt had taught it something new.

But in that moment, standing in the falling ruin of a king's discarded shape, blade singing, oath fresh on my tongue and Veyra's hand finding mine in the dark—

In that moment, all I felt was strangely light.

I had refused. Whatever came next, it would come to someone who had chosen, out loud, in the oldest tongue there was, exactly who she was.

The shadow finished standing up, and reached for the child.
