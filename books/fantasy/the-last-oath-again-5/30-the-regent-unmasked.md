## 30. The Regent Unmasked

I have died before.

I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep of men who were worn like coats. Ten years ago—has it been ten? I lose count; the thing that lives behind my eyes steals my seasons like a thief lifting coins from a sleeping man's purse—I died for the length of a held breath, and something else put me on like armor and walked out of my bedchamber wearing my face.

Since then I have died perhaps four hundred times. Each lucidity is a small death: ninety seconds of being Aldric again, knowing what my hands have done, and then drowning again as it takes the tiller back. You learn things in four hundred deaths. You learn that horror has a floor, and that the floor gives way, and that there is another floor beneath it, and beneath that one too, all the way down to some basement where even despair gets tired and just sits.

Today I was given ninety seconds in my own skull while the Regent considered a letter. I spent them, as I always do, watching through my own eyes and hating what they showed me: my hand moving without my permission, my seal coming down on wax, my voice—my actual voice, the one my mother used to scold me in—murmuring *"the grey cloaks will manage it"* to a chamberlain who bowed to me with genuine love.

He loves me. That's the worst part, or one of the worst parts. The realm loves me. They call me Aldric the Unaging. There are songs.

Then Emrys Winterbourne kicked open the doors of my throne room, and I learned the basement had one more level after all.

* * *

You should understand what it is like to watch your own body speak when you are not the one speaking.

It is not possession in the way the priests describe it—no blackness behind the eyes, no growled syllables, none of the theater. The Regent does everything better than I ever did. It stands straighter than I stood. It remembers names—the names of every servant's children, every baron's grudges—with a warmth I never managed in twenty honest years of trying. When it smiles at petitioners, they leave the throne room glowing. I used to think, in my early captivities, *if only I had been that good a king.* That was the first knife. The second was realizing it wasn't being a good king. It was being a good costume.

So today, when the doors burst open and six people walked into my throne room—one of them carrying a sword wrapped in a smith's scabbard, one of them a child with a scarred throat—I watched myself rise from the throne with perfect courtesy and say:

"Lady Winterbourne. Twenty years, and you haven't aged a day either. We should compare secrets."

My mouth. My voice. A joke I would never make, delivered with a grace I never had. Somewhere behind my own eyes I screamed the way you scream in dreams, with no sound and no effect, and the Regent felt the scream and did something obscene: it let me feel its amusement. Like a landlord letting the tenant hear him laughing downstairs.

Emrys looked older than her years and younger than her legend, which I suppose is what twenty years in the mountains does to a person who meant to disappear. She had a sword I didn't recognize and a stance I did—every oath-speaker stands the same way when they've decided how they'll die, weight low, chin level, like the ground owes them an apology.

"You know why we're here," she said.

"Of course." I gestured—it, gestured, forgive me, I will keep correcting myself until the corrections wear grooves—with the weary magnanimity of a king hearing bad news he'd already priced in. "The girl. Veyra." My voice said the name gently, tenderly, and I wanted to tear my own throat out, because I knew what tenderness from this thing meant. "Come here, child. You must be so tired."

And here is what I saw that no one else could see, because everyone else was looking at the king and I was the only soul in the room trapped inside him: for half a heartbeat, the Regent was afraid.

Not much. A flicker. The way a great ship shudders when it scrapes something under the waterline. But I have lived inside this creature for ten years. I know its weather better than it knows itself, maybe. And when its gaze found that mute child standing between the blacksmith woman and the old traitor Brannoc's ghost-of-a-man, the weather turned.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know of what it is, gathered in fragments across a decade of captivity, most of it from its own unguarded moments—because a thing that wears kings eventually forgets its tenant can hear it humming.

It calls itself the Regent. It has worn eleven crowns before mine, across nine centuries. It told me this once, early, in the way a man tells a secret to his horse—not cruelty, exactly, just the indifference of the powerful toward their furniture. Eleven kingdoms. Not conquered. *Eaten.* It arrives in a time of crisis, offers itself to a desperate heir, binds itself into the crown and the crown into the blood, and then it rules well—for a while—and then it begins consuming. Not gold. Memory. The deep memory of a realm: its founding oaths, its true histories, its languages, its names. By the end of each reign, the kingdom it wears is a smooth-faced thing that cannot remember why it began, singing praises for a king who never ages, and the Regent moves on to the next young monarch staring down a war he cannot win.

That was me, nine years ago. Caisson's rebellion, the northern lords in open revolt, my treasury empty and my father three months dead. It came to my chapel dressed as a counselor—I never saw its true shape, not once, not until tonight—and it said, in a voice like a warm room: *A king who cannot hold his realm has no right to it. I can hold it for you. The price is only your presence.*

Only your presence. As if a soul were a chair you gave up at dinner.

I said yes. God help me, I said yes in under a minute. Some nights that minute is all I am: a man saying yes.

* * *

The confrontation in my throne room lasted less time than it takes to tell, though it felt long enough to build a cathedral in.

Emrys refused it. Of course she refused it. I heard my own voice offer her everything—restoration, pardon, her order reborn with royal charter—and I heard her answer in words I didn't know but understood anyway, old syllables rolling out of her like stones down a hillside, and the whole room changed pressure. The banners stirred without wind. My crown—it was not on my head, but I felt it in the vault below, felt it *shiver*—

And the Regent stopped pretending to be me.

I don't have the right words. I was there, inside, and I don't have the right words, so forgive a king his poverty of language; we're trained in flattery, not truth.

It shed me the way a snake sheds skin, except the skin screamed.

I felt my own shape peel away—not my body, my body stayed where it stood, knees buckling, hitting marble—but the *wearing*, the borrowed authority, the ten years of Aldric-the-Unaging that it had grown over me like pearl over grit. All of it sloughed off at once and left me visible, finally visible, a middle-aged man collapsed on the steps of his own dais, ten years older than the portraits, hair gone grey at the temples overnight because the thing had been spending my youth the whole time like a drunkard spends inheritance.

And what stood above me, wearing the space where I'd been, was—

Borrowed centuries. That's the closest I can come. It was tall the way old towers are tall, wrong-proportioned, its outline refusing to settle, and its surface moved like a crowd seen from very far away—you could almost pick out faces in it, and I understood, with a nausea that has not left me since, that those faces were real. Kings. Queens. Counselors. Whole courts, pressed into its hide like flowers in a book. Nine hundred years of people who had said yes. It wore them the way it had worn me.

Its voice, when it spoke, was every voice it had ever eaten, layered so thickly that the words arrived already mourned.

"I HAVE WORN BETTER THAN HER," it said, "AND WORSE, AND THE WORSE LASTED LONGER."

Sable—because the broad-shouldered woman had to be Sable, Emrys's lost apprentice, though I only knew the name later—swore something blistering and hefted a hammer. Brannoc planted himself like a gate. And Emrys drew her blade, and the blade made a sound I will hear until I die: a hum, a chord, six notes braided together, the sound of a broken order holding its breath.

But the Regent was not looking at any of them.

It was looking at the child.

* * *

Veyra. I knew almost nothing about her then—a mute girl, a survivor of the burning archive in the south, the last living vessel of the First Tongue. What I knew came later, from testimony, from the others' accounts. What *I* saw, from the floor of my own throne room, was this:

The Regent crossed the distance between them in a way that made the distance dishonest. One moment it stood above me; the next it had simply *arrived*, and its hand—it had hands now, assembled out of the faces in its hide, fingers that were other people's fingers—closed around the girl's arm.

She did not cry out. She couldn't. That was the whole obscenity of it, the thing that made me understand, in one white-hot instant, why it had hunted speakers for a decade and why it wanted this child alive rather than dead: it seized the last voice of the First Tongue, and the First Tongue could not cry out, because the thief was already holding her throat.

"No—" Emrys lunged. The grey-cloaked survivors it had corrupted—no, wait, those had fallen at the shrine weeks ago; what met Emrys's lunge was worse, nothing, air, the Regent simply lifting the girl clear of the reach of blades as if she weighed what a rumor weighs.

"SHE IS NOT A CHILD," the Regent said, and every dead court in its skin said it with it. "SHE IS A DOOR. AND I HAVE BEEN LOCKED OUT OF DOORS FOR TOO LONG."

I want to be precise about my shame, because precision is the last dignity available to me now. In that moment—my daughter-kingdom's throne room shaking, an ancient evil holding a child aloft, heroes arrayed against it and failing—I did not think heroic thoughts. I did not pray. I thought: *let it take her, let it take her and go, let it leave and give me back my life.*

There. Now you know what kind of king I was. Even freed, even kneeling in the snow of my own abasement a week later, I carry that thought like a stone in the boot. Let it take her and go. A child I had never met, traded in my heart for the mere hope of my own exit.

Despair is not dramatic. That's what no one tells you. Despair is small and comfortable and smells like your own bedding. For ten years I had marinated in it so thoroughly that when the moment of crisis came, my soul's reflex was not courage but arithmetic: one girl against one kingdom, and the kingdom was mine.

Except.

Except that even as I thought it—even as that filth was still moving through me—I felt something I had not felt in a decade. The Regent's grip tightened on Veyra's arm, and through our old, terrible intimacy, through the place where it had worn me so long that a scar-tissue of connection remained, I felt its fear again. Bigger this time. A hull-splintering fear.

It was afraid of a twelve-year-old girl.

And shame of my own cowardice and terror for her and something else, something new and stubborn and embarrassingly late arriving—all of it fused into the first free thought I had had in ten years:

*It is afraid because it cannot control her.*

Everything else it has ever touched, it has worn or eaten or bent. Eleven kingdoms. A hundred counselors. Me. But the First Tongue doesn't bend. Oath-language doesn't bend. It can silence a child's voice, but it cannot *wear* her, and a thing that has consumed nine centuries of everything it touches has no practice at all with the one thing it cannot consume.

Its grip tightened. Veyra's face went pale, then grey. Sable was screaming. Emrys was running, blade singing that impossible chord, and the Regent raised its free hand and the air itself went solid and threw her back across the throne room like a doll.

And I lay on the steps of my own dais, a discarded coat, and did the only thing left to a man with no body worth commanding and no magic worth naming.

I remembered.

The Regent feeds on memory—that is its hunger, its method, its whole economy. But memory runs both ways along a worn path. For ten years it had poured itself through me, and some of it had soaked in, the way rain soaks through a roof into the joists. I knew things about it that no living creature knew, because I had slept beside its thoughts. I knew the shape of its hungers. I knew the rhythm of its fears. And I knew—I had known for years and never understood what I was holding—one thing it had let slip, one night, humming to itself over my sleeping mind, careless as a rich man counting coins aloud:

It had been named before. Long ago. By someone it had failed to devour. And the naming had hurt it, had nearly ended it, and it had spent three centuries hunting down every copy of that name and burning them, and it still flinched, somewhere in the deepest stratum of its stolen centuries, at the shape of that wound.

It cannot abide being named by an oath freely given.

I didn't know yet what that meant, or how Emrys would find the true name, or what it would cost her—I learned all that in the terrible bright hour that followed. In that moment I knew only this: I had one asset, and it was ten years of unwanted intimacy with the enemy, and I would spend every coin of it.

The Regent held Veyra up like a lantern and began, in eleven dead kingdoms' voices at once, to speak to her—to offer, to tempt, to promise her back all the years her gift had burned away. I felt its attention pour wholly into her, felt it lean, felt the vast engine of its hunger turn its face from everything else in the room.

From me. From the coat on the floor. From the man it had never once, in ten years, considered worth a second thought.

Which was, I understand now, the first tactical error it had made in nine hundred years.

Because Emrys was getting up. Because Sable was pulling something from the scabbard at her hip that sang when it shouldn't. Because the child in its fist had stopped struggling and fixed it with a look far too old for her face, and her lips were moving, shaping silent syllables, gathering something.

And because a king, even a puppet, even a ruin, even a coward who thinks filthy arithmetic in his darkest hour—a king still knows one thing in his bones that no ancient devourer has ever learned:

Never turn your back on the man whose throne you're standing on.

I put my ruined hands flat against the marble steps, and I began, silently, with everything left in me, to remember harder.

* * *

What happened next belongs to Emrys, and to Veyra, and to a scabbard quenched in oath-memory, and to the Last Oath that cost more than any of us knew to ask. History will write it correctly enough, I suppose, assuming history survives the writing.

But this much is mine to tell, and I tell it because it is the hinge my whole redemption turns on: in the moment before the Unbinding began, the Regent felt me remembering, and for the first time in ten years, it spoke *to* me instead of through me.

"YOU ARE NOTHING," it said, in my father's voice, in my own voice, in the voice of every king it had eaten. "A WORN GARMENT. WHY DO YOU FIGHT?"

And I—flat on my belly on the marble of my own desecrated hall, ten years stolen, my kingdom singing praises to the thing that ate me—found, to my everlasting surprise, that I had an answer.

"Because," I said aloud, my voice cracked and rusted and entirely, gloriously my own, "you wore me instead of killing me. That was your mistake. Everything you wear remembers."

Its faces all turned toward me at once—nine centuries of the devoured, wide-eyed, as if they'd never thought of it either.

And in that heartbeat of its attention, the child in its grip opened her mouth, and the First Tongue came out of her like the sun coming up.
