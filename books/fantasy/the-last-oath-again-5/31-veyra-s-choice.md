## 31. Veyra's Choice

The Regent held me by the wrist like I was a thing it had found on a road. Which, in fairness, was true.

I want to tell you that in that moment I felt brave. I didn't. I felt small and cold and my arm hurt where its fingers—Aldric's fingers, the king's fingers, fingers that had signed bread laws and mercy writs for ten years—pressed too tight. What I actually thought was: *Emrys is going to be so angry if I die here.* Not because she'd miss me. Because dying would mean she'd failed, and Wren Galloway of the Thornfell mountains did not fail at things. She just hid from them for twenty years first.

So there we were. The throne room smelled of wax and old stone and something underneath both, like a cellar where meat has gone wrong. Sable was down on one knee near the doors, breathing hard, her broken oath-blade lying three feet away in the direction of bad luck. Emrys stood between me and the throne with her sword out and her whole body saying *no* in the old grammar, the grammar they don't teach you in any tongue that has a word for *merchandise*.

And the Regent looked at me.

I need to explain what that look was like. It wasn't looking the way you or I look. When Emrys looks at you, she sees you and also everything you're hiding, which is annoying. When Sable looks at you, she's mostly checking whether your boots are worth stealing. But when the Regent looks at you, it's like being read aloud. Slowly. By someone who enjoys the difficult words.

"You are the last vessel," it said through Aldric's mouth. Aldric's voice, warm and reasonable, the voice of a king who had never once aged. "The final well of the First Tongue. Do you know how long I have waited for a well to come to me? Nine hundred years, little one. Kings brought me their crowns and their armies and their daughters, and not once—" the smile widened by exactly the amount a smile should widen "—not once did a well walk up my own mountain."

"It climbed," I said.

Emrys made a sound. Later she told me it was meant to be a warning and came out as almost-laughter. The Regent's grip tightened, and I decided that wit was a finite resource and I should ration it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being mute for most of your life: you get very good at listening. You hear the things under things. And under the Regent's beautiful borrowed voice there was a sound like a page turning, over and over, forever—the sound of something reading a kingdom and then closing the book when it finished eating it.

It had eaten nine kingdoms. Eleven kings. It said so later, in the moment before everything broke open, but I knew it already, because Vess—because the woman in the burning archive who pressed a word into my palm—had known it, and some of what she knew stayed in my hand along with the word.

"I am going to make you an offer," the Regent said. "Not a threat. An offer. Threats are for creatures that fear loss, and I lost nothing in nine centuries but the trouble of patience."

"Everyone says that right before they lose," I said. Rationing was going badly.

The Regent ignored me, which was somehow worse than anger. It turned Aldric's head slightly, presenting the offer to the room, to Emrys, to the grey cloaks frozen in the doorway, as though the offer were a gift being unwrapped in front of witnesses so no one could pretend later they hadn't seen it.

"Child. Every word you speak costs you. I have watched you since Lowmarket. Since the shrine. You screamed one word and shattered steel, and paid for it with a year of your face." It tilted the king's head, studying me the way Ferro studied floodwaters. "You are eleven years old and you carry the hands and the bones and the tired heart of someone twice that. Speak enough of your precious Tongue and you will die old at thirteen. That is the arithmetic of your gift. I know arithmetic. Arithmetic built me."

The throne room was very quiet. Somewhere far below, water moved in the old channels, patient as everything else in this city.

"So," the Regent said. "Here is my price, and here is my gift. Stop resisting. Come stand beside the throne—not as a prisoner, never as a prisoner—and I will give back every year. All of them. You will be eleven again in truth, with a childhood ahead of you instead of behind you in ash. I will teach you the Tongue without cost; I learned its grammar before your people learned fire. You will speak until you are ninety and bury every friend you make. And this realm—" the generous sweep of Aldric's arm took in the banners, the pillars, the whole gilded cage of it "—this realm will know peace such as it has never known, because no oath will ever again be spoken against its king."

* * *

I want to be honest, because Emrys says honesty is the only weapon that doesn't rust, and she'd check anyway: for one heartbeat, maybe two, I wanted it.

Not the peace-of-the-realm part. I'm eleven, or officially I am; realm-peace doesn't fit in an eleven-year-old's head. I wanted the years. I wanted my own hands back, small ones, ones that hadn't shaken since the archive burned. I wanted to run somewhere without my lungs filing a complaint. I wanted to be a child who had never seen a woman press a dying word into her palm, who had never walked north alone through winter with soldiers' voices behind her, who had never once had to decide whether a word was worth a year of her life.

Two heartbeats.

And the Regent saw them. Of course it saw them. That's the terrible cleverness of the thing—it doesn't lie to you. It offers you exactly what you want, weighed exactly right, and lets you do the lying yourself.

"You hesitate," it observed gently. "Good. Only the honest hesitate. Liars say no at once."

* * *

Emrys spoke. Not loudly. Emrys never needed loud; her voice carried the way cold carries.

"Veyra. Look at me."

I looked. She was bleeding somewhere along her ribs—I could see the dark line of it—and her sword arm trembled with exhaustion, and her eyes were the same grey as the Thornfell sky, the sky I'd hated for months because it never stopped snowing, and which now looked like home.

She didn't tell me what to say. That's the thing about her that took me longest to understand, longer than the smuggler paths, longer than how she counts stores like a miser counting sins. Everyone else in my life had put words in my mouth. Vess gave me one word and died. The hunters wanted my silence. The Regent wanted my surrender. Even kindness kept trying to hand me scripts.

Emrys just looked at me the way Master Corvane must once have looked at her, and waited, and let me choose.

That's what oaths are, I think. Not bindings. Choices you keep making after the choosing stops being easy.

I turned back to the Regent.

"You're right," I said. "I want the years."

Aldric's face did something soft and satisfied, and beneath the satisfaction, beneath the page-turning sound, something leaned closer, hungry and attentive, the way the lake under the ice leans up at footsteps.

"But here's my arithmetic," I went on, and my voice only shook a little, and I counted that a victory. "You've worn eleven kings. You've eaten nine kingdoms' memories. You know every language that ever flattered a crown. And in nine hundred years, nobody ever gave you anything freely. Did they?"

The Regent said nothing. The silence had a texture. It was the texture of a trap noticing it might be caught.

"They gave you crowns because they had to. They gave you obedience because they were afraid. Fear isn't a gift—it's rent. And you've been collecting rent for nine centuries and calling it love." My hand hurt in its grip. I didn't pull away. "That's why you can't touch an oath freely given. That's why the chapel cipher named it. Because everything you own, you own by force, and force isn't yours, it's just weight. And weight—"

I smiled at it. A real smile. Eleven years old and gap-toothed and entirely unafraid, or close enough that the difference didn't matter.

"—weight is the one thing a well knows how to hold."

* * *

The word came up from the place where Vess had pressed it, all those months ago, into a child's palm in a burning building. It had sat there like a seed under snow. I'd spoken fragments of the First Tongue since—the anchoring words on the Weep, the truth-word that cracked Brannoc's hidden shame open—but never this one. This one I'd saved, the way you save the last of anything.

It has no translation. I've tried. Emrys asked me once, on the road, and I told her it translated roughly as *you are seen*, and she went quiet for half a mile, so maybe that was close. But really it means more than that. It means: *I see you, and I am not afraid of what I see, and I name it anyway.*

The truth-word.

I spoke it at the Regent.

Not shouted. Spoken. Clear, the way water is clear, and it cost me—I felt the years leave like heat leaving a stone, felt my knuckles sharpen and my spine settle into an older shape—but I had years to spend and the Regent had none it hadn't stolen.

And it *wounded*.

I don't know how else to describe it. Aldric's face convulsed—not the man, the man was still a passenger, poor trapped king, but the thing behind his eyes lost its footing. For a fraction of a moment the glamour slipped and what looked out through the royal features was vast and pale and layered like a wasp nest built of pages, each leaf a kingdom's remembered name, and every layer shrieked silently, *seen, seen, SEEN,* as though being witnessed were the one wound it had no armor against.

Its grip opened. I fell free and Emrys caught me, one arm, sword still up, absurd and magnificent, and I heard her begin—begin—to speak. The refusal, formal, in the First Tongue, the words that started all of this ending. The Regent reeled back toward the throne, gathering itself, and its composure came back the way ice comes back over a hole in a lake, smooth and quick and thin.

"You will regret that," it said. Still courteous. Still Aldric's warm voice. But underneath, the page-turning had become tearing.

"No," I said. My legs were shaking. My hands looked forty. "Regret is for people who had choices. You never had one. You just had appetite."

Something in the air changed then—a pressure drop, a hush, the feeling before lightning decides. The Regent's attention swung past us, past Sable struggling upright, past the dead grey cloaks, and fixed on the six fragments humming together on the floor where they'd fallen, singing their thin six-part harmony, incomplete, waiting for a seventh that did not exist.

It laughed. And the laugh was the worst sound I have ever heard, worse than the archive burning, because it was relieved.

"Six," the Regent said. "Six of seven. You have crossed mountains and buried friends and sold your years, little vessel, and you are one blade short of a prayer." It settled onto the throne, arranging Aldric's robes with terrible calm. "Come, then. Spend the rest of yourselves against my walls. I have been patient for nine centuries. I can be patient while you learn despair."

Emrys's arms were around me, and I could feel her heart going fast, and I could feel her mind going faster, running the same hopeless arithmetic we all were. Six blades. No seventh. The seventh melted into regalia decades ago, inside these very walls, guarded by everything the Regent owned.

But I had heard something, in the half-second when the truth-word tore its mask loose. Something the Regent itself didn't know it had shown me. Underneath the tearing-page sound, underneath the hunger, there had been another note—faint, metallic, *singing*. A note that didn't belong to the thing wearing the king.

A note that sounded like a forge.

I pulled at Emrys's sleeve and put my mouth close to her ear, and I spent one more year of myself on four words, because some words are worth it:

"The scabbard. It's singing."

Emrys went very still. Across the throne room, Sable—bruised, bloody, kneeling beside her broken oath-blade and the scabbard she had quenched in oath-memory in a forge that was now ash—looked up, as if the words had reached her too.

The Regent saw our faces change. And for the first time in nine hundred years, something ancient learned what it felt like to be afraid.
