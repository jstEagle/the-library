## 32. The Sixth Fragment Breaks

I have broken a lot of things in my life. A half-oath. A promise to a dying woman named Merrin who taught me the forge and deserved better than the daughter I became. Two marriages, though honestly those were mutual projects. And once, memorably, an entire shipment of the king's own ceremonial armor, which I sold piecemeal to a traveling circus because they paid in advance and the crown paid in never.

So you'd think I'd be numb to the sound of something breaking.

You would be wrong.

The sixth fragment shattered like a struck bell that had finally decided it was done being a bell. One moment Brannoc's blade-fragment—five hundred years of oath-memory humming against my hip through its wrappings—the next moment a grey-cloaked hunter's counterword hit it mid-song, and the thing came apart in my hands with a sound I felt in my teeth and my sternum and whatever organ it is that houses hope.

"No," I said. Which is a blacksmith's version of prayer. We say it over cracked crucibles and collapsed forges and apprentices who have just quenched something wrong. *No* means: this cannot be happening, and yet.

"NO!" Emrys said too, from across the throne room, which meant she'd heard it, which meant it had really happened, which meant—

Which meant six became five, and the Unbinding needed seven, and somewhere behind the throne a thing wearing a dead man's face laughed with the king's beautiful mouth.

Let me back up. Battles are chaos, but I'm a smith. We're trained to see processes inside chaos. Fire, metal, force, result. So let me give you the process.

* * *

It started well, which should have been my first warning. Things that start well make me nervous. Things that start badly at least have the decency to be honest about their intentions.

We'd come through the servants' corridor behind the chapel—Brannoc holding the gate behind us, buying us minutes with his body, though we didn't know yet that he was spending his life and not just his pride. Veyra walked in front because the First Tongue answered her now, every door-seal and ward-word softening as she passed like butter under a warm knife. Emrys carried her own blade and the hilt-fragment wrapped at her belt. I carried two fragments and the scabbard I'd forged in Lowmarket while the town burned around me, because apparently I am incapable of not working even when my world ends. Sable Ashvane: will work through apocalypse. No job too small, no fire too literal.

And then the doors of the throne room, and the Regent wearing Aldric standing on the dais in full regalia, and grey cloaks fanning out along the walls like ink dropped in water.

Emrys refused him formally. In the First Tongue, out loud, syllables that made the banners snap and the candle flames stand up straight like soldiers called to attention. It was the bravest thing I've seen a person do with only their voice, and I include the time Veyra screamed a hunter's sword into gravel.

Then everything went sideways, which is the natural resting position of all things.

* * *

Here's what I know about fighting speakers, learned in the worst possible classroom: they don't duel like soldiers. They argue. Every strike is a sentence, every parry a rebuttal, and the air between them gets so thick with half-spoken words it hums against your skin like a struck tuning fork. Emrys moved through the throne room speaking death into her own blade with every swing—short words, ugly words, words with no poetry in them at all—and grey cloaks fell or fled or crumbled where they stood, their own corrupted oaths failing them one by one.

Veyra was our artillery. The child stood near the pillars with her fists clenched and her eyes shut, and when a hunter got past Emrys she would speak—a single word, always a single word—and the word would do what a word shouldn't. Melt steel. Stop lungs. Once, horribly, unmake a man's shadow so that he simply folded up like laundry, screaming in a voice that came from everywhere.

She paid for every word. That was the arrangement nobody had made and everybody understood. Each syllable cost her years. She was nine when we found her at Emrys's door. Standing there in the throne room, shouting truth at ancient evil, she looked thirty. There were lines around her mouth that no child should own, silver threading hair that should have been brown for another decade, and she kept going anyway, because that's the kind of person she'd decided to be before anyone asked her opinion about it.

Me? I fought the way a blacksmith fights, which is to say: badly, enthusiastically, and with whatever's nearest. I had a hammer. I had forearms like bridge cables. I broke one hunter's jaw and another's kneecap and spent most of my time between the fragments and the Regent, keeping the pieces safe, because that was my job and jobs are how I keep from thinking.

The Regent didn't fight us directly. Why would it? It wore a king. It stood on its dais with Aldric's hands folded and watched us bleed for it, occasionally offering commentary in that reasonable, cultured voice—"You misunderstand your order's purpose, Wren Galloway"—while its grey cloaks did the dying.

Until Emrys reached the foot of the dais, blade raised, six fragments singing in their wrappings at her belt and mine, and began to gather the Oath.

That's when it stopped being polite.

* * *

The Regent moved faster than a king's body should move. Later, Emrys told me what it did—spoke Aldric's own coronation oath backward, unmaking the binding that held the king's flesh together as a vessel—but in the moment what I saw was this: the king blurred, doubled, became a smear of crowns, eleven different faces flickering across one skull like bad coinage, and the pressure of its true attention came down on us like a mountain deciding to sit.

Emrys held. Barely. Blade against nothing-that-was-everything, feet skidding on marble, and she shouted for the fragments—for me, specifically, because apparently twenty years of hiding hadn't cured her of delegating—and I did what I was supposed to do. I unwrapped Brannoc's fragment, the fifth piece, the one from the quarry, and held it up toward the light and toward her.

The fragment woke. They all wake near Emrys; it's like watching iron remember it used to be a star. It sang, high and clear, joining the harmony of the others, and the Regent recoiled—I saw it recoil, saw eleven centuries of stolen kingdoms flinch—and for one shining second I thought, *we might actually do this.*

Then a grey cloak I hadn't accounted for—one of the quiet ones, the ones who hang back and watch and wait, and note to self, Sable, the quiet ones are always the problem—came off the wall behind me and spoke a counterword directly into the fragment's song.

I heard the note bend. That's the thing I'll hear until I die. Not the shattering—the bending, the moment the song went sour, the exact instant where a five-hundred-year-old piece of the First Oath was forced to believe a lie about itself.

Steel believes what you tell it. That's the whole secret of smithing, and the whole horror of swordspeech, and I knew it, I've known it my whole life, and I still wasn't fast enough. The fragment rang wrong once, twice, and then came apart in my hands into a spray of glittering dust and three dull shards that hit the marble and lay there like dead insects.

The silence where its voice had been was the loudest thing in the room.

The grey cloak grinned at me. I hit him with the hammer hard enough that the grin stopped mattering, but the damage was done, the arithmetic was done: five fragments. Five. You cannot speak the Unbinding with five. You cannot bake bread with two stones and you cannot unmake a nine-century-old devourer of kings with five-sevenths of a broken oath, and the Regent knew it too, and its laughter rolled through the throne room like weather.

"Do you see?" it said, in Aldric's voice, which is the cruellest instrument I know of. "This is why your order died, little smith. Not because you were weak. Because you believed wholeness was possible."

* * *

Here's the part where I'm supposed to tell you I despaired, and here's the part where I'm honest instead: I didn't despair. I panicked. Despair is philosophical and panic is practical, and I'm a tradeswoman.

Five fragments. Need seven. No seventh exists—it melted into the royal regalia generations ago, that's why we're in this room at all. So the plan was already insane: assemble six, bring them to the crown itself, let the seventh be drawn out of the regalia by proximity and oath. Six plus the crown's buried memory equals seven. Fine. Terrible, but fine.

Now I have five plus a crown I can't reach because the crown is currently being worn by the enemy at the center of a killing field.

My hands were shaking. I noticed this the way smiths notice things—professionally. Shaking hands are a tool problem. You brace them. You change your grip. And because I am who I am, my hands, even panicking, even mid-battle, did what they always do.

They went to the work.

The scabbard. I'd forgotten I was wearing it—no, worse, I'd been carrying it without *thinking* about it, which for a smith is a sin so basic my old master would have boxed my ears. The scabbard I'd forged in Lowmarket while the smoke was still rising, hammered out in three hours of fury and grief, for a fragment that no longer existed to fill it. Quenched—not in water, not in oil.

Quenched in oath-memory.

Let me explain, because I didn't understand it myself until that moment, standing in a burning throne room doing math with my soul. When the purge came, twenty-some years ago, they burned the archive at Thornfell Hold. Everything the order was—every spoken oath, every bound blade, every recorded word of the First Tongue—went into the fire. Except fire doesn't destroy oath-memory. Any more than it destroys the shape of a sword. It changes its form. It drives it deeper.

And I—foolish, guilty, grieving Sable, who couldn't save anyone that night and has hated herself for it ever since—went into the ashes afterward. Before the hunters came back for a second pass. I knelt in the ruins of my master's forge and I gathered what was left: slag, ash, the twisted remains of oath-blades whose speakers had died holding them. I filled a crucible with the grave-goods of my entire murdered order, and I carried it north, and I told myself I was doing it for remembrance.

Then, months later, when Emrys appeared in Lowmarket looking like a ghost that had lost its haunting license, and I learned what she was trying to do—some small spiteful part of me, some part that wanted desperately to matter, to fix one thing, to hand something back to the woman whose order I'd survived by abandoning—that part picked up a hammer.

I forged the scabbard in a single night. Folded steel over steel over steel, and at the heart of it, folded in like a blessing in a loaf, the ash-metal from Thornfell. The remains of burned oaths. The memory of every blade that ever spoke true, rendered down to slag and then raised back up into form by the only hands in Lowmarket stubborn enough to do it.

When I quenched it, the quench-bath screamed. I told myself I imagined that. I have excellent hearing and a flexible relationship with inconvenient truths.

The scabbard had hung empty since. An empty vessel. A grave-marker I could carry.

Or—

Or.

* * *

"Emrys." My voice came out strange. Steady. The voice I use to quote prices. "The scabbard."

She was locked blade-to-nothing with the Regent, blood running from her nose, both ears, her eyes gone white-rimmed with effort. "What?"

"The scabbard. The one I made for the fragments. It's not empty." I pulled it off my back, and even as I said it, I felt it—felt the thing wake against my palm, the way the fragments woke near Emrys, except this was bigger and stranger and sadder. It hummed with a chord instead of a note, dozens of voices, hundreds, the whole drowned choir of Thornfell Hold singing from inside three pounds of folded steel. "I made it from the ashes of the archive. From the blades they burned. I quenched it in—" My throat closed. Twenty years of not saying it, and it chose a battlefield to come out. "I quenched it in everything they took from us. It's been carrying the whole order this whole time. I just— I never listened."

The Regent heard. Of course it heard. Eleven centuries of eating kingdoms makes you a good listener. Its head turned—Aldric's handsome face contorting through shapes that weren't his, weren't human, weren't anything that should be allowed to have a face—and it spoke my name for the first time.

"Sable Ashvane." Reasonable. Almost gentle. "The informer. The oath-breaker. You would offer *that*—a broken thing, made by broken hands—as the vessel of the Unbinding?"

And here's the thing. Here's the thing I want carved on my stone, if anyone bothers to carve one.

It was right. It was completely right. The scabbard was made by a coward out of scraps, quenched in guilt, carried for twenty years by a woman who sold information to survive and called it pragmatism. Every flaw in it was mine. Every compromise in the fold pattern, every shortcut in the tempering, every night I worked drunk and every morning I fixed it hungover—it was all in there, the way a life is all in a body.

And it sang anyway.

That's what it doesn't understand, this thing that has worn eleven kings and eaten nine kingdoms' worth of memory. It thinks perfection is power. It thinks only the unbroken can bind. But I've spent forty years at a forge, and I will tell you what I know: the strongest steel is folded, and folding means breaking it over and over and welding it back together, and every weld is a scar, and the scar is where the strength lives. Perfect steel shatters. Worked steel sings.

"You're right," I told it, walking forward, holding out the scabbard toward Emrys, toward the light, toward whatever came next. "Broken thing. Broken hands. Best offer you're getting."

The last grey cloak lunged at me. Veyra spoke one word—her voice cracking like an old hinge, her face suddenly fifty years old, fifty, gods, what were we doing to this child—and the grey cloak's weapon dissolved into red rust mid-swing.

And Emrys Winterbourne, last of the oath-bound, blood on her face and death in her grip, took the scabbard from my hands the way you take a newborn. Carefully. Like it might cry.

"Sable," she said. Just my name. But the way she said it, twenty years of silence and bitterness and the whole ruined history between us went into it, and came out sounding like something I hadn't heard from her since before the purge.

*Forgiveness*, maybe. Or maybe just *witness*. Maybe just: *I see what you made of what was taken from you.*

"It's not a blade," she said, turning it, listening to it the way only she could listen. "It's not a fragment. It's—" and then her breath caught, because she'd heard it, the chord, the choir, the whole buried order singing inside the steel I'd folded around their ashes. "Sable. It's *all of them.*"

"Yeah," I said. "I know. Took me long enough to figure out what I was carrying." I wiped my nose with the back of my hand—blood, mine, when had that happened—and gave her the only instruction I'm qualified to give. "Well. Don't just stand there admiring the craftsmanship. Draw."

Because here was the final turn of the screw, the last arithmetic, and I saw it land in her eyes the same moment it landed in mine: the scabbard wasn't the seventh vessel. Not alone. A vessel holds. A vessel needs filling. And the seventh piece—the true seventh, the melted oath at the heart of the regalia—was ten feet away, on the brow of the thing wearing the king, singing its poisoned song behind a monster's smile.

Six fragments. One scabbard built from the ashes of everything they'd burned. One crown that had swallowed the seventh piece whole.

Enough. Barely, blasphemously enough—if she could reach it, and if the reaching didn't kill her first, and if the price the Unbinding always demanded was one she was willing to pay, and I already knew, gods help me, I already knew from her face that it was, and that the price was her voice in steel forever, and that she was going to pay it anyway—

The Regent stopped laughing.

That was the worst moment. Worse than the shattering. Nine centuries old, and it looked at Emrys Winterbourne holding a coward's scabbard full of ghosts, and for the first time in its long, hungry, borrowed existence—

It looked afraid.

"Took you long enough," I told it. "Now hold still. This next part's going to leave a mark."
