## 33. The Last Oath

Here is the thing nobody tells you about the end of the world: it is mostly waiting.

The throne room doors were barred from within—of course they were; tyrants love a bolt—and so we came through the chapel passage that Aldric's coded confession had drawn for us in ink gone brown as old blood. Six of us, counting a scabbard and a ghost. Brannoc's sacrifice at the gate had bought us minutes, not hours, and every one of those minutes I could hear the palace settling around us like a beast deciding whether we were worth the trouble of eating.

Sable carried the fragments wrapped in her own forged scabbard, slung across her back like a lute she'd never learned to play. It hummed against her spine. I could hear it even through the walls—the six blades singing to each other in the dark, a chord missing its final note.

Veyra walked beside me with her hand in mine. She had stopped looking like a child somewhere on the Weeping Steppe. Not grown—*spent*. Every word she'd spoken aloud had carved years off her, and now her face held the particular gravity of someone who has read their own ending and decided to keep turning pages anyway.

"You can still stop," I told her, for the fourth time. "There's a servant's stair behind the tapestries—"

"There isn't," she said. Her voice was a dry leaf, an old woman's voice in a girl's throat. "I checked while you were being dramatic at the gate."

"I wasn't being dramatic."

"You told Brannoc you'd see him on the other side. You don't believe in an other side. You told *me* you don't."

The boy who'd taught me swordspeech used to say the same thing about my oaths: *you swear beautifully, Emrys, but you never mean the parts where you survive.* I squeezed her hand and said nothing, because she was right, and because the corridor had ended, and because the doors ahead were carved with nine centuries of kings, and the last of them was listening.

* * *

The throne room of Cael Amarth is designed to make you feel small. Vaulted ceiling painted with the dynasty's victories, pillars like petrified giants, a floor of black marble polished so that your own reflection walks beneath you everywhere you go. When I was young and stupid and sworn, I stood in this room at my ordination and felt the weight of history settle onto my shoulders like a mantle.

Now I just felt cold. The windows were dark. The braziers burned with a flame that gave off no heat, only light, the way the Regent did everything: the shape of a thing without the truth of it.

Aldric sat upon the throne—or the thing wearing Aldric did—and it had dressed for the occasion. Not in armor. In kindness. The king's face was arranged into an expression of such gentle sorrow that a stranger would have wept at the sight of him.

"Emrys Winterbourne." His voice filled the vault without effort. "And Sable of Lowmarket. And little Veyra, who has cost me three hunters and a great deal of patience. Come closer. I won't bite. The body has teeth, but they're mine now, and I take poor care of them."

"Is that meant to be charming?" Sable muttered.

"It's meant to be disarming," I said. "It works better if you're frightened."

"Then I'm safe."

We walked down the long aisle of black stone, and beneath our feet our reflections walked too—six fragments' worth of ghosts in the marble, and above us the painted kings watched with eyes that some courtier had touched up until they all resembled Aldric. Nine centuries of portraits in the gallery beyond, and I understood suddenly what Corvane had tried to tell me through the veil of death: *it doesn't conquer realms, child. It inherits them. One crown at a time, worn until the wearer is a husk, then passed along like a coat.*

The Regent rose from the throne. Aldric's body moved with the easy grace of long practice, and I hated how much it looked like him, how the tired lines around his mouth seemed genuinely tired, how a prisoner's exhaustion could be worn as decoration by his captor.

"I know why you've come," it said. "You've come to speak the Unbinding. Six fragments." It gestured, almost fondly, at Sable's burden. "Yes, I can hear them. They've been singing since the quarry. Do you know what I hear when they sing? A eulogy. Yours."

"You talk a lot," Veyra said, "for something that's afraid."

The smile didn't falter. That was the worst part—it couldn't falter; it was furniture, upholstery over something that had never needed a face until it learned to steal them.

"Afraid," it repeated, tasting the word. "Child, I have worn eleven kings. I have eaten the memory of nine kingdoms—do you know what that means? Somewhere there was a realm called Ossuvar, with bridges of woven glass, and no one alive remembers it, because I drank it. Its songs, its crimes, its names. Gone. I am not afraid of a broken sword and a mute girl."

"She's not mute anymore," Sable said.

"No," the Regent agreed, and here the voice went soft, and softness from that mouth was worse than any threat. "She spends herself. Word by word. I have watched her do it. Each syllable costs her a season of her small life, and she keeps paying, because she believes you'll win. That's not courage, Emrys. That's what I do to people. I make them spend themselves on me." It spread Aldric's hands. "But I digress into philosophy, and you came for business. So. My offer stands, and I will make it formally, before witnesses, as you deserve."

It descended the dais steps one at a time, unhurried.

"Kneel to me, and I will restore your order. Not the hollow copy—not the grey cloaks I corrupted out of your survivors, those were merely... pruning. I mean the true thing. The Swordspeakers reborn, chartered under my seal, protected forever. Every man and woman who hid, who ran, who broke their oaths to live—they are pardoned. Their children inherit honor instead of ash." Aldric's eyes found mine, and inside them, for one flickering instant, I saw something else—a man, drowning, mouthing words I couldn't yet read. Then it was gone, smoothed over like a hand smoothing bedsheets over a corpse.

"You would be the mother of a new order," the Regent said. "Instead of the last of a dead one. All you must surrender is the child. She cannot be harmed—I give you my word, and my word has outlasted empires. She will simply live out her shortened life in comfort, and the First Tongue will die with her quietly, and the world will keep turning, fed, orderly, *remembered*—"

"Stop," I said.

"—or refuse, and I will wear this king until he crumbles, and then his daughter, and then her son, and in two hundred years no one will remember there was ever anything in this land but me. Choose, swordspeaker. You've always been good at choosing. You chose to hide for twenty years while my hunters burned—"

"*Stop.*"

The word came out wrong. Not shouted—*spoken*, in the cadence my order used for steel, the deep grammar of the First Tongue, and it struck the Regent like a slap. Aldric's head turned a fraction. The smile stayed, but the body behind it recoiled, one involuntary half-step, and in that half-step I saw the whole shape of the thing: ancient, yes. Patient, absolutely.

But unaccustomed to being *named*.

"Ah," it said quietly. "There she is."

* * *

Corvane had spent forty years teaching me the theory of the Unbinding, back when it was an academic exercise, a curiosity of the founding texts, the way soldiers study obsolete weapons. Seven vessels. One voice. And a freely-given price, because the First Tongue does not recognize coercion—you cannot bind a thing with a chain you resent wearing.

"The Regent," he'd said, in the dream-logic of his haunting, "cannot abide being named by an oath freely given, because it has never been given anything. Everything it has, it took. An oath is the one currency it cannot counterfeit."

Six fragments sang in Sable's scabbard. The seventh was melted into the regalia itself—the coronation circlet, sitting on a cushion beside the throne, gold and smug and thirty feet away behind the Regent's borrowed body.

Which left the scabbard. Which left Sable, who had quenched it in oath-memory at her forge, who had folded her own broken vow into the steel like a secret letter.

"Sable," I said.

"Don't," she said. "Don't ask me nicely. Ask me like you mean it."

I looked at her—my apprentice's apprentice, the girl who'd run from the purge, who'd informed on survivors to feed her forge-fire, who'd carried guilt like a smith carries scars—and I asked her the way the First Tongue asks, which is to say honestly:

"Give me your work. Give me the vessel you made from what you broke."

Her jaw worked. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. She pulled the scabbard from her back, drew the six humming fragments from within it, and held the empty sheath out across both palms like an offering at an altar.

"It's not pretty," she said. "It wasn't made for this."

"Nothing ever is."

She pressed it into my hands, and the steel remembered her—remembered the fire, the breaking, the choice to stand back up—and the scabbard woke in my grip like a seventh blade that had been waiting its whole short life for a hand.

The Regent watched all of this with the patient interest of a cat watching mice arrange their own funeral. But it had stopped descending the steps.

"Veyra," I said.

"I know." She stepped forward, past me, small and spent and blazing, and planted herself between the Regent and me like a doorframe. "Say it. I'll carry it."

"You understand the price—"

"Emrys." Old voice, young face, eyes like the lake beneath the drowned monastery: clear all the way down. "I have been silent my whole life. Don't you dare give me a speech about loss."

* * *

So. The mechanics, for whoever asks later, though no one will, because everyone who could witness it was either dead, enchanted, or currently possessed:

An Unbinding requires seven vessels. Six fragments we had. The seventh was the scabbard, and through it I fed the six, and the six became—not whole again, nothing broken becomes whole again—but *harmonious*, a chord resolving, a sentence finding its verb.

It requires a voice in the First Tongue. Veyra opened her mouth, and what came out was not a scream this time. It was language—pure, structured, older than the stones of this palace—and as she spoke I felt each word leave her like heat leaving a dying coal, felt her dim by degrees, and hated myself, and kept going.

And it requires a speaker. A sword. And a freely-given price.

My blade was not an oath-blade. My blade was a mountain knife I'd bought from a smuggler twenty years ago and kept sharp out of habit. But I had been swordspeaker for thirty years before the purge, and the First Tongue does not live in the steel. It lives in the speaker. The blade is only the door.

I drew it, and the ghost of Master Corvane stood beside me—not visible, never visible, but *present*, the way grief is present, the way a scar is present.

"Ready?" I asked him.

*You never were,* he said. *Do it anyway.*

I raised the blade toward the thing wearing my king, and I began the Unbinding, and I offered the price.

Not my life. Death would have been easy—death is the coin every hero gets to spend, clean and dramatic, and the Regent would have happily taken it and swept up the pieces. No. What the oath demanded was the thing I actually lived by. My swordspeech. The hearing-of-steel, the speaking-into-it, the sense that had made me *me* since I was seven years old and first heard a hammer ring truth. Twenty years I'd mourned it in exile. Now I gave it away on purpose, freely, without reservation—which is the only way it counts—and I felt the First Tongue rise through me one last time like water finding the sea.

Veyra spoke. I shaped. The scabbard sang.

And I named it.

Not "the Regent." That was its mask's name, its job title, the comfortable lie it wore between crowns. The true name rose out of Aldric's hidden confession, out of Veyra's archive-memory, out of the drowned lake and the burning archive and nine kingdoms of stolen remembrance—a name in the First Tongue that means, as nearly as mortal mouths can render it: *THE ONE WHO EATS THE TELLING.*

I said it aloud, bound in an oath I gave freely, and the throne room *broke*.

* * *

I don't have words for what happened next, which is fitting, because what happened next was the defeat of a creature whose entire existence was the theft of words.

The false light in the braziers died. The painted kings on the ceiling peeled and swirled and became—for one impossible moment—nine kingdoms' worth of memory pouring home: glass bridges, woven songs, names called out across centuries to owners long dead. The black marble floor cracked in a spreading star from the throne to the doors. And Aldric—

Aldric screamed.

Not the Regent. *Aldric.* Ten years of captivity tearing loose all at once, ten years of watching his own hands sign death warrants, and the sound he made was the sound of a man surfacing from drowning, and I put everything into the last cadence, the binding-cadence, the one Corvane had drilled into me until I could speak it asleep:

*I name you. I bind you. I cast you out. By an oath freely given, by a voice freely lent, by a vessel freely forged—be named, be bound, be gone.*

The Regent came out of the king like smoke out of a collapsing house. For one heartbeat it had shape—vast, grey, a silhouette of borrowed centuries with too many hands, reaching for Veyra with all of them—and the scabbard in my grip blazed white-hot with Sable's mended oath, and the name closed around it like a fist.

It did not die. Things that eat memory don't die; they're *forgotten*, and that is worse. The name I'd spoken sealed it into itself, a creature locked alone with everything it had stolen and no way left to swallow it, and it went out of the world between one word and the next, and the silence it left behind was enormous.

The circlet rolled off its cushion and rang against the marble. Seven notes. The chord complete.

Aldric fell forward off the throne steps and I caught him—caught the actual man, sixty pounds lighter than the thing had worn him, shaking, weeping, *alive*—and behind me Veyra's knees buckled and Sable caught her, and the last thing I heard before the silence took me was the six fragments falling quiet at last, their song ended, their work done.

* * *

They tell me I knelt there holding a stranger of a king for a long time. They tell me Sable shouted my name twice before I answered.

I don't remember. What I remember is this: reaching, out of habit, for the hearing-of-steel, the sense that had been my constant companion for thirty years, the way you reach for a limb—

And finding nothing. Not pain. Not numbness. Just absence, smooth and total, like a tooth-tongue exploring a gap. My blade lay across my knee and it was only a blade. Metal. Cold. Mute as any hammer, dumb as any nail.

Twenty years I'd dreamed of this moment. Victory, vindication, the order avenged. I'd imagined weeping, laughing, Corvane's ghost saying something insufferably wise.

Instead I sat on the cracked black marble of a ruined throne room, holding a knife that couldn't hear me anymore, and felt the strangest thing of my entire strange life:

Relief.

Clean, total silence, for the first time since childhood. No blades murmuring. No oaths whispering their hungers. Just a woman on a floor, breathing, with a living king's weight against her shoulder and a child's exhausted voice saying, hoarse and human and in plain trade-speech:

"Emrys? *Emrys.* Say something, you terrifying woman."

I laughed. It came out cracked and wet and entirely ordinary, no power in it at all.

"I'm here," I said.

And the doors of the throne room burst open behind us, and torchlight poured in, and voices—soldiers' voices, courtiers', the whole waking palace come to find out why their eternal king's ceiling had just torn itself open—and I looked up at the crowd gathering at the end of the aisle, at their faces turning from fear to wonder to something I had no name for, and King Aldric pushed himself upright out of my arms, gaunt and grey and ten years older than yesterday, and did the one thing no one in nine centuries had seen a king do.

He knelt.

To me. On the broken marble, before all of them, and the whispers started like wind through wheat, and I realized—with horror, with exhaustion, with something dangerously close to hope—that the hardest part was only beginning.
