## 34. After the Naming

I remember the moment of my freeing as a kind of falling.

Not the fall itself—no, that came later, when my knees met marble and I understood what ten years of borrowed grace had cost. But before that, there was a single breath in which everything was mine again: my lungs, my hands, the small ache behind my eyes I had learned to call *mine*. The Regent had worn me so long that its leaving felt like a wound and a mercy at once, like a tooth pulled from a jaw that had forgotten it ever held anything else.

The throne room was quiet. That's the detail no one believes afterward. They expect thunder, they expect the scream of an ancient thing being unmade, and certainly there had been light—Emrys's oath had been light, terrible and clean—but what stayed with me was the silence after. The silence of a man alone in his own skull for the first time in a decade.

*You are Aldric,* I told myself. *You are Aldric Veyan Coris, third of that name, and you have not thought your own thought in ten years.*

And then, because I am apparently incapable of dignity even in my own head: *Gods. My back.*

Ten years. It had aged me ten years in a breath—not metaphorically, though the poets would make it metaphor by sundown. Whatever sorcery sustained the Wearer had been feeding on me, and when it went, the debt came due all at once. My hair, which had been brown at my last true morning, was grey at the temples. My hands shook. When I looked down at them—really looked—I saw a stranger's knuckles, a stranger's veins.

The court had watched everything. Of course it had. Half the nobility stood frozen along the walls where Emrys's oath had pinned them, and the other half were pressed against the great doors through which we'd come, and every one of them had seen their king stand up from his throne with someone else moving behind his face.

I looked at Emrys Winterbourne then.

She stood in the wreckage of the ceremony, sword bare, breathing hard, and she was looking at me the way you look at a snake you've just learned is also a victim—which is to say, with pity you're trying very hard not to feel. Behind her, the girl Veyra leaned against Sable, grey-faced and old around the eyes in a way no child should be, and Brannoc was dead at the gates, and half of Lowmarket was ash, and I had done none of it and all of it.

"Your Majesty," Emrys said. Her voice was rough. "Can you hear me?"

"I can," I said. And then, because it needed saying and because my voice cracked on it like a boy's: "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I don't—" I stopped. Started again. "I don't know how much of this is mine. That's not an excuse. I want that understood. I'm not asking anyone to decide where he ends and I begin. I'm only telling you the truth, which is that I don't know."

She sheathed her blade. Slowly, deliberately, the way you lower a weapon when you've decided not to need it.

"That's more honesty than most kings manage on their best day," she said.

"It's the only coin I have left."

* * *

They tell me I knelt.

I say *they tell me* because the next hour exists in my memory as a series of snapshots separated by gaps—the mind protects itself, or perhaps it simply refuses to record what it cannot bear. But there are witnesses, dozens of them, and the accounts agree, and so I accept the version in which the King of the Nine Valleys crossed the length of his own throne room on foot and knelt before a woman who had spent twenty years hiding in mountains because of laws signed with his hand.

His hand. My hand. The distinction mattered less every day.

I knelt on marble still scorched where the Regent had burned, and I said words. Witnesses differ on the exact wording, which pleases me; let each of them carry home the version they needed. What matters is what the words did.

First: I named the purge for what it was. Not a necessity, not regrettable policy, but murder—state murder, ordered by a crown that feared a truth it could not control. Eleven hundred swordspeakers, give or take the ones whose names were never recorded. I said the number aloud. Numbers are armor for cowards; a figure can be grieved without faces. So I refused the figure. I read names instead. Emrys's order kept rolls—we found them later, in the drowned archive—and for two hours I stood in that ruined hall and read the dead until my voice gave out, and then Veyra read, in the First Tongue, which made the names land like hammer-blows, and then Sable read, and we did not stop until every name had been spoken into a room that had once ordered their deaths.

Second: I abolished the laws. All of them. The Edict of Quiet Steel, the Registration Acts, the writs that had made a person's gift a capital charge. I did it by decree, knowing full well that decrees can be reversed by my successor, and so I did it twice—once as king, and once as a man asking the realm to hold me to it. I sent copies to every temple, every guildhall, every village well where public notices got nailed up between grain prices and lost-dog bills. Let it be read out loud in markets. Let farmers argue about it over fences. A law that lives only in archives dies quietly; a law that lives in gossip is harder to kill.

Third—and this was the part that cost me most, and therefore the part I suspect mattered most—I confessed my complicity in plain terms, publicly, without sheltering behind the possession.

Here is the thing about being worn. It is real. I will swear any oath you like: the Regent moved my hands, shaped my mouth, decided which petitions reached me and which petitioners never left the capital alive. For ten years I was a passenger watching a monster do atrocity with my smile. That is true.

And here is the other thing. In ninety seconds of lucidity, spread across a decade, I had understood enough. Enough to know something was wrong. Enough to feel the wrongness and—to my everlasting shame—not fight it. There is a chamber in the oldest part of the palace where the walls are thin to the First Tongue, and in those ninety seconds I had crawled there and scratched instructions into the stone, and those instructions led Emrys to my confession, and that confession helped kill the thing wearing me. So I was not wholly passive. I know that.

But ninety seconds against ten years. One act of defiance against a decade of complicity. When the Regent signed execution orders, some part of me—some deep cellar of myself—must have known. And I had chosen, in whatever way a prisoner chooses, not to look.

A man who does not look is still a man who benefits from not looking. I ate well while others starved. I slept safely while speakers burned. The body they used to sign the orders was fed and clothed and crowned, and some portion of the comfort of that body must have seemed, to the buried Aldric, worth the price of silence.

So I said all of this. Out loud. To the court, and then—when word spread, as word does—to the realm.

"You may ask why confess," I told the assembled lords, whose faces wore expressions ranging from horror to calculation. "You may ask what good it does now. Here is your answer: because accountability is not a door you walk through once. It is a road, and I intend to walk all of it. If the realm decides my crimes require my removal, I will go. If it decides my crimes require restitution beyond what I have offered, I will pay it. I am not asking for forgiveness. Forgiveness is not mine to request. I am asking for the chance to repair, and I acknowledge even that chance is a gift I have not earned."

Lord Casserly—old, sour, loyal to the crown the way ivy is loyal to a wall—actually spoke up. "Majesty, the realm needs stability. Confession breeds uncertainty."

"Uncertainty," I said, "breeds questions, and questions breed truth, and truth is the only foundation that doesn't rot. You want stability? Stability built on a lie is a bridge over a gorge with the planks painted on. Walk on it if you dare."

He did not dare. None of them dared, in that moment, and I understood—with a clarity that only the recently freed possess—that they were afraid of me, and that their fear was the same fear that had built the purge in the first place. Fear of power unbound. Fear of a king who might mean what he said.

Good. Let them be afraid. Fear keeps rulers honest longer than love does.

* * *

The days that followed were the hardest of my life, and I include the decade of captivity in that assessment, because captivity at least had the mercy of absence. Repair has no such mercy. Repair requires presence, attention, the daily grinding work of looking at what you broke.

I sat in judgment—not on others, but on the record. I reopened every case the Regent had closed. I read the transcripts of trials conducted under my seal, and I discovered that my signature appeared on documents I had never seen, condemning people I had never met, and that this changed nothing whatsoever about my obligation to them. The families of the condemned received letters written in my own hand, not a scribe's. Some wrote back. Most did not. One woman returned my letter with a single line added at the bottom: *"He had a laugh. You took it."* I keep that letter in a drawer in my private study. I read it every morning before I dress. I recommend the practice to any ruler who finds justice becoming abstract.

Emrys came to see me twice in those first weeks.

The first visit was formal—she came as the last sworn member of a murdered order, to discuss the restoration of legal protections and the disposition of surviving relics. We talked for three hours about scabbards, fragments, archive rights, and jurisdiction, and it was the most comfortable conversation of my new reign, because it was entirely about things and not at all about guilt.

The second visit was not formal.

She arrived unannounced, in that way of hers that suggests doors are merely suggestions, and stood in my study looking at the drawer where I kept the letter. I hadn't told anyone about the letter. She knew anyway. Swordspeakers, I was learning, could smell a penance the way hounds smelled rain.

"You're doing it wrong," she said.

I set down my pen. "Enlighten me."

"You're making it a performance. Reading names, kneeling, decrees—it's all very grand, and I believe you mean it, but grand gestures end. The purge didn't happen in one afternoon. It happened in ten thousand small decisions by ten thousand people deciding to look away. If you want to answer that, you'll answer it in ten thousand small decisions too. Appointments. Budgets. Which magistrate gets promoted and which gets quietly retired. The boring machinery of governance, turned toward repair, every day, for the rest of your life." She shrugged. "Or burn bright for a month and fade, like every reformer I've ever watched."

"You're not exactly filling me with hope."

"Hope isn't my trade. Truth is. Or was." Something crossed her face then—a flicker, quickly mastered, of the price she'd paid. I knew what she'd given up. The whole realm knew. She had spent her swordspeech buying my freedom, and now she lived in a silence she'd once described to me, in a rare unguarded moment, as "clean but enormous."

"Why did you come?" I asked. "Truly."

She was quiet a long moment. "To see if you were going to be a king or a monument," she said. "Monuments are easier. Kings are better."

Then she left, and I sat in my study feeling as though I'd been audited by a ghost, and I began, that same afternoon, the unglamorous work. Petitions. Appointments. A commission to locate the mass graves. Pensions for survivors—not charity, restitution, with the legal language carefully drawn to name it as such. The boring machinery, oiled and turned.

It is not finished. It will never be finished. That is the point. Accountability as beginning, not end—as she taught me, whether she meant to teach me or not.

* * *

There is one more scene I must record, because history will get it wrong otherwise.

The ballads already tell it differently. In the songs, the king rises from his knees transfigured, the realm weeps as one, and the wicked ministers flee into the night. In the songs, redemption is a lightning strike—one dramatic bolt and the sky is clear forever.

Here is what actually happened, one evening in early spring, four months after the Naming:

I walked, unannounced and lightly guarded, to the house in the artisans' quarter where Veyra lived with Emrys and, increasingly, with a steady stream of visitors who came seeking instruction in a language older than kingdoms. I brought no herald. I brought bread—actual bread, baked in the palace kitchens by my own request, because I had decided that if I was going to apologize to a child, I would do it with something warm.

Veyra opened the door herself. Up close, the toll of her speaking showed plainly: silver threading her hair, lines at the corners of her eyes that belonged to a woman of sixty, though she was—what, eleven? Twelve? The years she'd burned defending us all sat on her like ill-fitting armor.

"Your Majesty," she said. Not hostile. Not welcoming. Assessing. Children who have carried worlds learn to assess.

"May I come in? I brought bread."

She considered the bread with grave seriousness, then stepped aside.

We sat at her kitchen table—Emrys in the corner, watchful, arms folded, radiating the specific patience of a woman letting a student handle a lesson herself—and I spoke to Veyra directly, which no adult had quite managed yet. Everyone wanted to thank her or study her or examine her like a relic. I wanted to tell her the truth.

"When the Regent wore me," I said, "there were ninety seconds when I was awake inside my own life. In one of them, I saw you—or rather, I saw the possibility of you, because Master Corvane's designs were already in motion, and I understood that somewhere a child existed who could undo what was wearing me. And do you know what I felt?"

She tilted her head. "What?"

"Relief. Enormous, shameful relief. Because it meant I wouldn't have to save myself. Someone else—a child, no less—would carry the weight." I turned my hands palm-up on the table. "You were eleven years old, and I was relieved that you would suffer so I wouldn't have to. That is what I did with my freedom when they gave it back to me: I spent ninety seconds of it hoping a little girl would fix everything. So I am here to say that I see what it cost you. Every year on your face. And I will spend the rest of my reign making certain no child is ever asked to carry what you carried."

Veyra looked at me for a long time. Then she reached across the table, tore the loaf in half with startling decisiveness, and pushed the larger piece toward me.

"Eat," she said. "Guilt is bad for digestion."

And Emrys laughed—from the corner, sudden and startled, a sound like a lock rusting open—and I laughed too, wet-eyed and ridiculous, holding bread like a communion offering in a child's kitchen.

That, and not the marble and the kneeling and the decrees, is the scene I would carve in stone if I carved anything. A king forgiven a crust of bread by someone he had failed, and told, gently, to stop making his guilt everyone else's meal.

* * *

Winter comes early to the capital this year, or perhaps I only notice it now, having spent ten years noticing nothing.

Tonight I sit in the chapel where I once scratched my confession into the wall, and I add a new line beneath the old instructions, in ink this time, for whoever comes after me—because kings should leave maps for their successors, and mine will be a map of pitfalls:

*The crown listens. That was always true; the Regent merely proved it literal. Guard your ears. Guard your sleep. And when the weight of office whispers that you are irreplaceable, remember Aldric Veyan Coris, who believed it for ten years and woke to find himself a stranger with grey temples and a drawer full of unanswered grief.*

Tomorrow there is a council session. Three lords will move to soften the repeal of the Registration Acts—quietly, procedurally, the way rot works. I have their arguments memorized already; I've heard every version of them since the purge. And tomorrow I will defeat them, not with thunder, but with tedium, with precedent, with the patient grinding weight of a king who has learned that the distance between a free realm and a captive one is measured in meetings nobody wants to attend.

But tonight, before sleep, I do what I do every night. I open the drawer. I unfold the letter. *He had a laugh. You took it.*

I read it twice.

Then I set it down, trim the candle, and reach for the stack of petitions waiting on my desk—because the road is long, and I have only just begun to walk it, and somewhere north of the city, in a mountain hold above the snowline, a school is opening its doors to children who will grow up never knowing the fear I helped build. I intend to send them my first donation at dawn.

And if the road asks more of me than I have—if the realm decides, someday, that my repairs are insufficient and my crown forfeit—then I will go as I knelt: on my own feet, by my own choice, owing nothing to silence.

The candle gutters. Outside, snow begins to fall on a city learning, slowly, unevenly, imperfectly, to speak freely.

I pick up my pen.
