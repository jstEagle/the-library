## 35. The Empty Well

The first morning after the Unbinding, I woke up rich.

That was the joke I told myself, lying in the narrow bed they'd given me in the palace guest wing—*you're the wealthiest woman in the realm, Emrys Winterbourne; you own a king's gratitude, a child's devotion, a smith's loyalty, and the undying hatred of every grey cloak still breathing.* Rich beyond counting.

And deaf as a stone to steel.

I lay there and did what I had done every morning for twenty years in Thornfell: I reached for my blade where it leaned against the wall, wrapped my hand around the grip, and listened.

Nothing.

Not silence, exactly. Silence is a thing with texture, a held breath, a room waiting for you to fill it. This was absence—the well I'd drunk from since I was seven years old, the deep cold water of swordspeech, simply *not there*. Like reaching for your own heartbeat and finding your chest hollowed out and swept clean, someone else's furniture arranged inside it.

I'd known it would happen. I had chosen it, spoken it into the oath itself: *let my voice go where the Regent goes.* You don't get to be surprised by the price you name yourself. But knowing a thing and living it are different animals, and only one of them wakes you at dawn with your hand on a dead sword, weeping like the orphan I'd been before Corvane found me.

So that was how the great Emrys Winterbourne, last of the oath-bound, liberator of kings, spent her second day of freedom: crying into a feather pillow in a room that smelled of beeswax and other people's ambition.

Veyra found me there around midmorning. She didn't knock—she never knocked anymore, having decided sometime during the crossing of the Weep that doors were merely suggestions made of wood. She climbed onto the foot of the bed, folded herself cross-legged, and looked at me with those old-young eyes of hers, the ones that had aged a decade in the space of a single screamed word.

"You're mourning," she said. Her voice was hoarse still, and each sentence cost her something—I could see the effort of it, the way she chose words like a miser choosing coins. "For the blade."

"I'm not—" I started, which was a lie, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, and somewhere Master Corvane's ghost was probably laughing at the state of my honesty these days.

"It's all right," Veyra said. "Mama mourned things too. Before."

Before the archive burned. Before the woman pressed a word into her small palm and sent her running north through the snow. Before everything.

"The blade wasn't a person," I said, because apparently I was going to be pedantic about grief now, at my age, after everything.

"No," she agreed solemnly. "But it talked to you. That's worse. People die and leave memories. What do you do when the thing that talked to you just... stops?"

I had no answer. A nine-year-old had asked me the one question twenty years of hiding hadn't prepared me for, and I sat there with wet cheeks and an empty hand and nothing to offer but the truth.

"You sit with it," I finally said. "You sit with it until sitting becomes living again."

"Good," said Veyra, and stole the last of the bread from my plate, because comfort has limits even among the profound.

* * *

They gave me three days of official mourning, though nobody called it that. The palace simply... left me alone. Sable appeared once, set down a whetstone and a jar of her best forge-oil beside my door without a word, and left. It was the most eloquent speech she'd ever delivered, and I include the time she'd called me a coward to my face in Lowmarket, back when we were both younger and stupider about each other.

On the fourth day, Aldric asked to see me.

He was changed—you could see it from across any room. Ten years had arrived in him all at once, settling into his face like sediment into still water: new lines at his eyes, a stoop he'd have executed a courtier for noticing a month ago, hair gone white at the temples where it had been brown the day the Regent first wore him. He looked, for the first time since I'd seen portraits of him as a young man, like himself. Which is its own kind of horror, if you think about it too long. I tried not to think about it too long.

"Lady Winterbourne." He inclined his head—a full inclination, more than courtesy demanded, and we both pretended his knees didn't crack doing it. "I wanted to thank you properly. Not the public version. The real one."

"The public version had trumpets," I said. "It was very nice. I nearly wept, but then I remembered I'd already used up my week's allotment."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived before the Regent moved in. "I've been told," he said carefully, "that you can't... that the swordspeech..."

"Gone." I said it lightly, the way you'd report the weather. "Traded it for you. No refunds, no exchanges, store credit not accepted."

Aldric looked at me for a long moment, and I realized with some discomfort that this man—who had been a passenger in his own skull while executions were signed under his hand, who had watched his kingdom eat itself for a decade through eyes that weren't entirely his—was seeing me clearly. Completely. The wry tone, the deflection, the whole performance, and underneath it the raw place where the well had been.

"I'm sorry," he said simply. And then, worse: "Is it truly like losing—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Please. Everyone keeps trying to find the shape of it so they can sympathize with the correct shape. It isn't like anything. It's like *nothing*, which is the problem."

He nodded slowly. "My daughter died when she was six," he said, and the floor dropped out from under the conversation. "Fever. And afterward, people kept telling me they understood. They didn't. Nobody understands another person's nothing." He met my eyes. "I won't pretend to. But I know what it is to have a decade stolen and be expected to govern on regardless. So. If ever you want a room in this palace where no one asks you to be fine—there are several. I know them all intimately."

I should have said something gracious. Instead what came out was: "Did the Regent leave anything? Of you, I mean. Or did it take ten years and give back a stranger wearing your grief?"

Aldric was quiet a moment. "It took the years," he said. "The grief it left me all my own. Apparently even gods don't want that part."

We stood there, the two of us, king and broken swordspeaker, holding our respective nothings up to the light like appraisers at a market stall. It was, strangely, the most honest conversation I'd had in years. Possibly ever.

* * *

The trouble with being unable to hear blades is that you keep forgetting you can't.

It happened maybe forty times a day, that first month. I'd walk past a guard and reach out, reflexively, toward the song of his sword—and hit the wall. Or I'd pick up a kitchen knife to cut bread and wait, automatically, for that faint hum blades gave off near me, the way dogs lean toward people who like dogs. Nothing. Bread knife. Just bread knife.

Once—in the worst instance—I drew my own sword in the courtyard to run through the old forms, muscle memory carrying me, and partway through the third sequence my body simply stopped. My arms wouldn't finish the arc. Twenty years of Thornfell discipline said *practice keeps you alive*, and some newer, quieter voice said *practicing for what, exactly? For whom?*

I stood in the snow with a sword half-raised like a statue of somebody else's heroine, and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and then Veyra appeared—she always appeared, the child had developed an unerring instinct for arriving at my worst moments, possibly because I kept having them at predictable intervals—and took the sword gently out of my hands.

"You look like Aunt Sable," she said, "the day her forge burned."

"Sable's forge didn't burn."

"I know." She set the sword against the wall with exaggerated care, the way you'd lay out a body. "That's why it was bad. Losing a thing that's really lost is easier than almost-losing one."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I wanted to tell her she was too young to say things like that. I also wanted to write them down, because they were true, and true things were getting scarce.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the swordspeech, and what I am telling you now because someone should, someday, if the school Veyra and I mean to build ever gets its walls:

It was never quiet in my head. Not once in forty years. Every blade within earshot sang, faintly or loudly, according to its temper. Good steel hummed like a contented choir. Bad steel whined. Cursed steel *screamed*—and there had been enough cursed steel in my life, between the purge-blades and the grey cloaks' twisted oath-work, that I had learned to sleep in layers, the way sailors sleep in storms. Even in Thornfell, in my deliberate invisibility, the mountain iron in my little cottage spoke to me at night. Old mining picks. A rusted wood-axe. The knife I cooked with. All of it murmuring, all of it wanting things—sharpening, purpose, blood sometimes, mostly just *remembering*. Blades remember their making. That's the whole secret of the speech, really. We didn't put words into steel. We listened to what was already there, and gave it permission.

Forty years of that. Then the Last Oath, and the well ran dry, and—

Quiet.

Real quiet. The kind I'd never had. I lay awake the first night in Thornfell—yes, I went back, we all went back, but that's Sable's story to tell in her way and mine to tell in mine—and I heard the wind. Just the wind. And the creak of the shutters. And my own heartbeat, unaccompanied.

I had expected the silence to feel like death. Everyone assumes a gift taken back feels like a wound.

Instead it felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying. Forty years, and my shoulders had grown around the weight so thoroughly that freedom felt, at first, like deformity. I kept reaching for the ache of listening the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

By the third night, I slept nine hours. I hadn't slept nine hours since I was a girl. I woke furious about it, and then I woke rested, and then I lay there in the grey dawn light of my own rebuilt cottage—because yes, we rebuilt it, the school rose around it like a chick around a shell, but that chapter comes later, or earlier, depending on how you count—and understood something that made me laugh alone in my bed like a madwoman:

I had hidden in these mountains for twenty years calling it survival. And part of it was. But part of it was that the singing never stopped. Part of me had been exhausted since the purge, since the night the order died screaming and every blade in the compound sang their deaths to me whether I wanted to hear them or not, and I had walked north into the snow partly to escape the sound, and told myself it was strategy.

The silence wasn't my punishment.

The silence was the first honest thing anyone had given me in decades.

* * *

Of course, liberation is never as tidy as grief, and grief is never as tidy as the songs claim, and I am a novelist besides, so trust me when I say I milked both for all they were worth before accepting that they'd decided to share the house.

There were hard days. There was the afternoon I tried to teach the first students—we'd gathered three by then, mountain children, one of them a ferryman's granddaughter with Ferro's same crooked grin—and discovered that I could not demonstrate the Listening. You cannot teach a language you no longer speak. I stood before three eager faces with thirty years of knowledge and no way to hand it over except in words, plain mortal words, and for one black hour I considered walking back into the mountains and letting the whole idea freeze.

It was Veyra who solved it, in the way she solved most things: by refusing to notice it was a problem. "Tell us what it sounded like," she said. "Like Grandmother tells stories. The lake remembers drowning. Tell us what remembering sounded like."

So I did. I described the hum of good steel, the whine of bad, the scream of cursed. I described how an oath tastes going into a blade—like cold water, like a promise you mean, like the moment before a storm breaks. I described Corvane's sword, the hilt fragment that had started all of this, and how it had sung to me under my floorboards like a dog hearing its master's step after years.

And the strange thing—the thing I want carved somewhere, if this account survives—is that the teaching worked better than the doing ever had. Because when you can hear a blade yourself, you teach the student to listen for what *you* hear, and they spend years chasing your ears instead of their own. But words? Words make the student build the listening from the ground up, in their own shape.

Authority rooted in love outlasting authority rooted in magic—that was the lesson of my whole ruinous, glorious life, and I learned it finally not through power but through its absence.

Though I'll confess, quietly, here, where only the page can judge me: on certain nights, when the wind comes down off the high passes carrying the smell of snow, I stand at my window and I miss it so badly my ribs ache. I miss the chorus of the forge. I miss the particular note of Sable's work, bright and stubborn as she is. I miss the way a newly-made oath-blade sounds on its first day, like laughter in an empty hall.

I miss being needed by the metal.

And then morning comes, and a student knocks without knocking—Veyra has trained them all terribly, none of them knock—and asks a question I can answer with nothing but words and patience, and the ache settles back into its corner, and the day begins, clean and silent and mine.

* * *

It was on such a morning, five weeks after the Unbinding, that the summons came.

Not a royal summons—those had stopped meaning much to me, and Aldric had learned to send letters instead, humble ones, which was either genuine growth or very fast learning. This was a heavy cream envelope sealed with the sigil of the High Court of Inquiry, and the handwriting on the front was precise, bureaucratic, and utterly unfamiliar.

*A summons to testify,* it said, *regarding the events of the Purge of the Order of the Speaking Blade, twenty-three years past. Your testimony is required under the Reckoning Edict.*

Sable was in the yard when I came out with the letter in my fist. She took one look at my face and set down her hammer.

"They're digging up the purge," I said.

Her jaw tightened. She'd testified already—her informer past, her broken oath, all of it laid out in the open where the whole realm could pick through it. Pardoned, but not praised; that phrase had followed her around Lowmarket like a smell. "Whose testimony are they after now?" she asked. "Yours?"

"Mine." I read the letter again, and the bottom of it, which I'd missed the first time, resolved itself out of the formal script like a face out of fog. "*Witnesses cited include: E. Winterbourne, survivor. S. Marrow, survivor. And B. Hallowell, survivor, currently remanded pending trial.*"

Brannoc. Brannoc Hallowell, who had condemned me in the quarry, who had fled the purge himself and left children behind, whose hidden shame Veyra had torn open with a single truth-word, who had stayed behind at the gate and died buying us the throne room with his forgiveness still warm in the air.

Dead men don't get trials.

"Remanded," I said slowly, "pending trial. Sable. Brannoc is alive."

The hammer slipped from her fingers and rang against the anvil, one clear bright note that neither of us could hear the meaning of anymore—but that somebody, somewhere in that yard, was going to learn to listen for.

"They're putting him on trial," Sable said. "For leaving the children."

"And everyone will want to know what I have to say about it." I looked down at the letter, at my own name written in a stranger's careful hand, and felt the strange clean silence inside me shift, the way water shifts when a stone drops into a well long thought empty. "They're going to ask me whether the man who judged me deserves mercy. And whatever I answer, half the realm will call me a liar."

Sable picked up her hammer. "Then I suppose," she said, "we'd better decide what the truth is before we get there."
