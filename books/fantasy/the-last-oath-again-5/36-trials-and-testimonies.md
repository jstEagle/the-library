## 36. Trials and Testimonies

They held the trials in the great hall of Lowmarket, which was a joke in itself, because Lowmarket's great hall had burned down with half the town and what they had instead was a roofed market square that smelled of scorched timber and wet ash. The benches were grain crates. The judge's bench was a trestle table draped in the king's blue for dignity, which mostly succeeded in making it look like a corpse at a wake.

I stood at the witness mark on the third morning of proceedings, my hands cuffed—not because anyone thought I'd run, but because the clerk insisted the forms be observed. The forms. As if the forms had ever protected anybody. Twenty years ago the forms had signed off on the purge of the Order of the Speaking Blade with all the tidy enthusiasm of paperwork everywhere.

"Sable Ironhew," the clerk read out, "formerly of the Order, apprentice-ranked, oath incomplete. You stand to give testimony regarding your conduct during and after the purge of the order. Do you swear to speak truth?"

"I do."

"By what?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Every oath needs an anchor, and mine had been broken so long I'd nearly forgotten its shape. I looked down at my hands—smith's hands, burn-scarred, steady—and said, "By the work of them. By every blade I've forged since. Let them testify against me if I lie."

A murmur went through the crates. Somewhere in the back somebody said, "That's not how oaths work," and somebody else said, "Shut up, she's a swordspeaker's smith," which was generous of them, given what they knew about me. Which was more than I'd have liked.

The presiding magistrate was a woman named Odile Ferrance, sent down from the capital along with three others, because the king—Aldric himself, mortal now and visibly older by a decade in the way that made people flinch when they counted his years against his face—had decreed that the reckonings would not be conducted locally. Too many local judges owed their positions to the old regime. Too many local witnesses had neighbors on both sides of every crime.

Ferrance had a face like a closed ledger and a voice that never rose above the volume needed to fill a room exactly. "State your name and station during the purge," she said.

"You have it. Apprentice-ranked, Sable Ironhew. Seventeen years old at the time."

"And your master?"

"Master Corvane." The name still sat wrong in my mouth, like a stone I'd swallowed and never passed. "He died at Thornfell Hold. They all did. Except the ones who didn't."

"Which ones didn't?"

"Me." I let it hang there. "And one other I know of. And however many the Regent's grey cloaks scooped up afterward and turned into hunters. But those aren't survivors, are they. Those are acquisitions."

Someone laughed, one sharp bark, and cut it off fast. Ferrance didn't smile. "Describe your conduct on the night of the purge."

Here it was, then. The thing I'd rehearsed a hundred times on the road from the capital, and a hundred more times in the forge at night, hammering it into some shape I could carry without it cutting me.

"I ran," I said. "Before the soldiers came over the wall. Before the fighting even started. I heard the horn and I went out the tannery gate and I kept running until dawn." My voice came out level, which surprised me. "That's the part I've told myself for twenty years. *I ran.* Cowardice, plain and simple, and cowardice is at least honest about what it is."

Ferrance waited. She was good at waiting. Judges always are; it's the one skill the profession actually requires.

"But that's not the whole testimony," she said.

"No." My cuffs clinked as my hands tightened. "Because two days before the horn, a man came to my master's forge asking about commission work. Good boots, capital accent, paid too readily for a sample blade. He asked questions a buyer doesn't ask—how many speakers at the hold, how often Master Corvane traveled, whether the order kept its oath-blades in one armory or scattered." I made myself look at the crates full of faces. Farmers, smiths, widows, two of Brannoc's quarry folk sitting stiff as fence posts. "I answered him. All of it. Because he flattered me, and because I was seventeen, and because I wanted someone from outside to tell me I was wasted on oath-steel in a mountain hold."

The silence had a texture to it. Not hostile. Worse. *Attentive.*

"He came back once more before the purge. I told him about the tannery gate—the unguarded one. I told him which night Master Corvane rode down to Lowmarket and would be away from the hold." The words came easier now, which was the horrifying part, like a wound that stops hurting because the flesh around it has died. "Three nights later, the soldiers used the tannery gate. They hit the hold while our strongest blade was a day's ride away. Forty-one speakers died. I don't know if my answers decided it. I've spent twenty years deciding they did, and twenty years deciding they couldn't have, depending on the night."

"And afterward? Did you speak of this to anyone?"

"No. I buried it under ironwork and kept my head down and told everyone—including myself—that Sable Ironhew was just a girl who got lucky and ran. Until Emrys Winterbourne walked into my forge last year smelling of snow and old grudges, and every lie I'd quenched started cracking."

Ferrance consulted her papers. "You subsequently provided material aid to Emrys Winterbourne and her companions. A scabbard. Information. Ultimately you joined their company and fought at the palace."

"The scabbard turned out to matter," I said. "Quenched in oath-memory, or so I'm told. It became the seventh vessel when a fragment broke. So I suppose the realm owes its king to a scabbard I forged in a bad mood."

Another bark of laughter, quickly smothered. Even grief-ridden courtrooms need a laugh; it's how you know the people in them intend to go on living.

Ferrance looked at me over her ledger-face. "Sable Ironhew. This tribunal finds your testimony credible, complete, and freely given. In the matter of your informing—which contributed to the deaths of forty-one members of the Order of the Speaking Blade—you are guilty."

The word landed the way it always lands. Like a hammer you've swung yourself, coming back around on the backswing.

"In consideration of your age at the time, your subsequent decades of concealment—" here a few people shifted angrily, because *concealment* was doing a lot of charitable lifting, "—your aid to the Unbinding, and the fact that the purge itself has been ruled an act of the entity known as the Regent rather than a lawful action of the crown, the tribunal pardons you. Fully. Your oath-breaking is remitted. You may resume your trade and your citizenship without legal disability."

She paused. And here's the thing about Odile Ferrance—I'll say this much for her—she looked me dead in the eye and added, without any softness whatsoever:

"The pardon is law. It is not forgiveness. Forgiveness is not ours to give. That belongs to the dead, and to the living who loved them, and they will each decide in their own time, and some of them will never decide, and you will have to live inside that arithmetic. Do you understand?"

"I understand," I said. And I did. Better than she knew.

* * *

The trouble with a public trial is the walk home afterward.

There wasn't really a home to walk to—I'd been lodging at the reconstructed inn, sharing a room with a widow who snored like a bellows—but there was a route, and routes through a small town after dark are gauntlets whether anyone intends them or not. People stood in doorways. People didn't quite look at me. One woman did look at me, straight on, and spat into the gutter, and I nodded to her, because fair enough. Her sister had been a lay sister at the drowned monastery. The lake remembered her. So did she.

What surprised me was the man who fell into step beside me halfway down Tanner's Row. Old Hesk, who'd sold me charcoal for fifteen years and never once asked about the sword-shaped lump under the canvas in my back room.

"My brother was a soldier at the purge," he said, without preamble. "Regular crown infantry, not grey cloak. He told me once, drunk, that the hold fell too easy. That somebody'd left a door open." He shrugged. "Never knew what he meant till today."

"Hesk—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "I'm not here to forgive you. Ferrance said it right—forgiveness isn't hers to hand out like bread rations. I'm here because I've been thinking about what happens next, and I think you're going to get it wrong."

"Get what wrong?"

"This." He gestured vaguely at the town, the ash-smell, the whole slow machinery of reckoning grinding through the valley. "Everyone's going to want it clean. Pardons here, gallows there, a nice line drawn under it, done. And it won't be clean, because it can't be. Half the grey cloaks we caught were kids from villages like this one who got taken after the purge and *used*, Sable. Used the way the Regent used the king. You want to hang them? Hang the thing that wore them, sure, but it's already cast out and gone wherever such things go. What's left is a nineteen-year-old from Millbrook who killed two people wearing a stranger's obedience, and doesn't remember doing it."

We'd reached the inn. He stopped, hands jammed in his pockets against the cold.

"So what do you want?" I asked. "Mercy?"

"I want it *messy*," he said. "Messy means somebody's actually looking at each case instead of filing it. Clean means somebody's looking at a ledger. Ledgers are how we got here." He nodded at me, once, and trudged off toward his own street, and I stood in the doorway of the inn feeling like I'd been handed something heavy and told to carry it a while.

* * *

The tribunal sat for eleven more days, and I attended every session, partly because I felt I owed the room my face, and partly because Emrys had asked me to. "You've been on the wrong side of a judgment," she'd said. "Sit where the judged sit and watch how it's done. Then tell me if it's justice or just procedure wearing justice's coat."

So I watched.

I watched them try Vasken the Grey—one of the captured hunters, nineteen years old, from Millbrook, exactly as Hesk had said. The tribunal took three days over him. They brought in a speaker from the new school above the snowline—a young woman named Tessaly, one of Emrys's first students—to examine him for residual binding. There wasn't any. The Regent hadn't worn him; it had merely *paid* him, which the tribunal found somehow more damning and more pitiable at once. He'd hunted speakers for coin because the coin was good and the cause sounded righteous: *traitors who put words in kings' mouths.* They gave him ten years' labor rebuilding the burned quarter of Lowmarket, and the widow whose house he'd fire-raised got first claim on his wages, and when the sentence was read he wept like a child, and nobody in the room comforted him, and nobody hanged him either, and I sat there thinking: *messy. Hesk was right. This is messy, and the mess is the point.*

I watched them try Captain Rellis, who had commanded the soldiers at Thornfell Hold. He didn't weep. He gave orderly testimony about orders received through proper channels, and the tribunal patiently unwound those channels all the way up to a chancellor who'd been dead nine years, and then down again into the archives where the Regent's signatures sat in a row like teeth. Rellis was found guilty and sentenced to life's service—garrison labor, road-mending, the unglamorous penance of a man who'd been a gear in a machine and now had to spend his life oiling roads for the people the machine crushed. On his way out he stopped at the witness mark where I'd stood, looked at the floor as if I were still there, and said, "Your gate information was accurate. We'd have found another way in eventually." Then he left. I've thought about that sentence every day since. It was meant as absolution, I think. It worked the opposite way.

And I watched them fail to try a dozen smaller things entirely—the informers who'd never come forward, the neighbors who'd denounced, the magistrates who'd stamped the paperwork. The tribunal could only judge what walked into its hall. The rest stayed out in the world, unpardoned and unpunished, carrying whatever they carried, and I recognized the weight because I'd carried it for twenty years, and I understood at last that most of what a realm calls justice is just the cases that happened to surface. The deep water keeps its drowned.

* * *

On the last day, they called Emrys.

Not as accused—as *witness*, though everyone in the crates understood the difference was thin ice. Her role in the purge's failure had surfaced during the quarry trial, back before Brannoc's verdict; the story had spread the way stories do, growing a new limb in every telling. By the time she stood at the witness mark, half the valley believed she'd sold the order to save herself, and the other half believed she'd tried to stop it single-handedly, and neither half was right, because the truth was worse and quieter than either.

She told it plainly. How Corvane had ordered her south with the warning that never got delivered. How she'd obeyed him instead of her instincts, and the obedience had cost the order its strongest sword on the night it mattered. How she'd hidden for twenty years calling it survival when it was shame. She didn't dress any of it. When Ferrance asked if she considered herself culpable, she said, "Yes. Not legally. Truly. There's a difference and I've had twenty years to learn it."

"And does the tribunal's pardon of others bind your conscience?" Ferrance asked.

Emrys almost smiled. "No offense to the tribunal, but no. Consciences keep their own ledgers. Mine's been audited pretty thoroughly lately, though. I recommend it. Terrible experience. Improves the character."

Somebody in the crates laughed—actually laughed—and the sound broke something loose in the room, some knot of dread that had been tightening for weeks. Ferrance let the laugh live for exactly three seconds, then closed the session.

Afterward, in the muddy yard outside, Emrys found me leaning against the inn's wall with my arms crossed, watching the crowd disperse the way water disperses around stones.

"Well?" she said. "Justice or procedure?"

"Both. Neither. Depends on the case." I kicked at the mud. "Vasken gets to rebuild what he burned. Rellis mends roads. I get a pardon and a spit in the gutter and old Hesk telling me I'll get the aftermath wrong." I looked at her. "It's messier than hanging everybody. Is that progress?"

"That's the only kind there is." She leaned beside me, shoulder to shoulder, two exiles watching a town try to grow back around its own scar tissue. "Vengeance is clean because it ends. Repair doesn't end. You just keep doing it until you die, and then other people keep doing it, and that's what a peace actually is—not the absence of the debt, but a decision to keep paying it together."

"Poetic. You should write that down."

"I have a student who writes everything down. Veyra's archive is going to include the trials—all of them, testimony verbatim, including yours and mine." Emrys glanced sideways at me. "Including the parts about the tannery gate."

I felt my stomach drop and my spine straighten at the same time, which is a strange sensation, like a building settling. "Good," I said, and meant it, mostly. "Let it be in the record. Let some apprentice a hundred years from now read what I did and decide what she thinks of me. At least she'll be deciding with her eyes open."

"That's the whole idea." Emrys pushed off the wall. "Come on. Veyra wants us at the school within the month—says the archive needs a smith's input on preserving oath-blade fragments, and Aldric's sending a formal request for a demonstration of unbound forging. Apparently the crown would like to know what a sword sounds like when it isn't listening to anyone."

"A sword that carries no bindings," I said. "I've been dreaming about those since the palace. Blades that answer to the hand and nothing else."

"Then you'd better come build them." She was already walking toward the road, snow starting again, light and dry, the kind that means the worst of the winter's breaking. "Pack tonight. We ride at dawn."

I stood a moment longer in the yard, looking back at the market square where the trestle table still wore its funeral-blue drape, where forty-one names had been read aloud that morning—not charges, just names, spoken into the record so the record would have to hold them. Justice hadn't fixed anything. Justice had, at best, made the fixing possible.

But standing there in the falling snow, I found that was enough to start with.

I went inside and began packing my tools, and if my hands shook a little over the hammering tongs—well, forge-hands shake for a lot of reasons, and cold is only one of them.

At dawn we rode north, and behind us Lowmarket went on with the long, unfinished work of becoming itself again.
