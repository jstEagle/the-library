## 37. What Veyra Keeps

The thing nobody tells you about being the last living vessel of a dead language is how many people suddenly want to put words in your mouth.

For most of my life I had no words at all. Ten years of silence, sealed shut like a tomb someone had bricked over while I was still breathing inside it. Then one night on a mountainside I screamed a word that broke a sword and aged me a decade, and now everyone from here to the capital has opinions about what my mouth is for.

Aldric's opinion arrived by courier, which was at least honest of him. The letter came up the Thornfell road in early spring, carried by a rider whose horse hated the altitude almost as much as the rider did. Emrys read it first, because she reads everything first — old habit, she says, from the years when reading the wrong letter first could get your whole order killed. Then she handed it to me without expression, which from Emrys is practically a speech.

*His Majesty requests the presence of Veyra of the First Tongue at court,* it said, in beautiful calligraphy that cost someone a great deal of money and probably their eyesight. *Her gifts are needed for the treaty negotiations with the Kessarin League. A position will be created. A household. An income. The tongue of treaties.*

"They want you at court," Emrys said, unnecessarily.

"I can read," I said.

"I know. I'm stalling."

I looked at her. She looked out the window at the snowline, where the light was doing that thing it does in the mornings, turning the whole white world briefly gold, like the mountains were apologizing for something.

"Say what you're actually thinking," I told her. "You're bad at stalling. You've been bad at it since the day you opened the door for a mud-covered child instead of letting her freeze like a sensible hermit."

That earned me half a smile. Emrys Galloway's smiles are rationed like wartime bread, but they exist.

"I'm thinking," she said slowly, "that courts eat children who are useful. They don't mean to. They're not monsters, most of them. They just... feed. And you've already paid more for your voice than any person should have to pay. Look at your hands."

I looked at my hands. They were an old woman's hands on a girl's arms, knuckles swollen, skin gone papery and fine as moth wings. Every word I speak in the First Tongue takes something. Not all words — small ones, ordinary ones, cost little. But the true ones, the deep ones, the ones that make stone listen: those take years off me like a knife taking shavings from a stick. Sable says I'll be grey by thirty if I keep this up. Sable says it quietly, when she thinks I can't hear, which is funny, because hearing things people say quietly is basically my whole skill set now.

"The treaties matter," I said. "The Kessarin League matters. If the borders go wrong, people die."

"People would also die if you fell down a well. That doesn't mean you should live next to one." She turned back from the window. "Veyra. I watched a king spend ten years as a passenger in his own body because he was *useful*. Useful is what they called him right up until the end. I need you to understand that when powerful people offer you a position, they are not offering you belonging. They're offering you a shelf."

"And if I want the shelf?"

"Do you?"

And there it was. The actual question, laid out between us on the table next to the cold tea and the courier's letter, shining like a blade fragment under floorboards.

Here's what I never tell anyone, because telling it would cost more than a word: part of me wanted to say yes. Not the treaties part. The other part. The part that remembered being ten years old and mute and carried through a burning archive by a woman with smoke in her hair, and how every door after that was either locked or temporary. A position. A household. An income. A room in the palace with my name carved over the lintel, or near enough. A place where I would be *assigned*, permanently, by people with power to enforce the assignment, so that no one could ever again look at me the way the archive-burners looked at me, like a thing to be used up or erased.

Belonging by decree. Safety by paperwork.

It sounded like heaven. It sounded exactly like heaven, and that's how I knew it was lying, because I'd spent a winter learning what real heaven looked like, and it looked like a drafty hold above the snowline where a woman counted stores aloud so a child could learn numbers in a language that wasn't hers, and a blacksmith cursed at her forge and fed us anyway, and nobody's affection required a signature.

"I'm going to refuse," I said.

Emrys nodded, unsurprised. "Good."

"You're going to help me write the refusal, because I've never written a diplomatic letter and I suspect 'no thank you, your majesty, I know what shelves are' is not the correct register."

"That," said Emrys, "is nearly perfect, actually."

* * *

We sent the refusal south, and I braced for consequences, and what came back instead — two weeks later, another exhausted horse — was worse than consequences. It was understanding.

*The Crown regrets but respects your decision,* Aldric wrote, in his own hand this time; I knew because Emrys had shown me samples of his handwriting from the coded chapel documents, and this was the same tired, careful script of a man who had recently learned to hold a pen again after ten years of being held. *You owe the realm nothing further. But permit an old prisoner one observation.*

He wrote: *I spent a decade unable to choose anything, and I learned that choice is not the freedom itself. Choice is only the door. What you do in the room afterward — that is the freedom, and it is rarer, and it cannot be granted or assigned. Only built. Whatever you build, the Crown will not stand in its way. — A.*

"He's apologizing," Emrys said, reading over my shoulder.

"No," I said. "He's *envying* me. There's a difference. Read it again."

She read it again. Her breath went out slow through her nose, the way it does when she's revising an opinion and hates admitting it.

"He's right, though," she said finally. "Damn him. He's right."

So we started building.

* * *

You should understand what the drowned archive was, before you understand what we made of its bones.

The Order of the Oathbound kept its great library beneath Lake Verenhal — a monastery half-sunken on purpose, its lower vaults flooded and frozen, because water remembers and keeps what it's given. Words pressed into wax tablets and sunk in the deep cold lasted centuries. Whole lineages of oath-craft lived down there. The Regent burned the upper halls during the purge and let the lake take the rest, thinking drowned meant destroyed.

The lake disagreed.

When Emrys dove for the second fragment, back when it was just the two of us and a rope and her stubborn refusal to die, the dead spoke to her. Everyone the lake had ever taken, murmuring below the ice. She doesn't talk about it much. But afterward, on the shore, shivering blue and furious, she said one thing I've kept like a stone in my pocket ever since:

"It's not a graveyard down there. It's a library with terrible librarians."

So that spring, when the thaw came, we went back. Me, Emrys, and Sable, who claimed she was coming to "supervise the drowning risk" and brought enough forged tools to outfit a small war, which is how Sable says *I love you and I'm frightened for you*.

The monastery rose out of the meltwater like a rotted tooth — black stone, collapsed roofs, the smell of ten cold years and twenty colder ones. But the lower vaults were intact. Frozen. Sealed by the very flood that was meant to kill them.

We spent the whole summer bringing the archive back up into the air.

I'll tell you what recovery looks like, since the ballads — yes, there are ballads now, Sable collects them out of spite and corrects their inaccuracies loudly in taverns — the ballads get it wrong. Recovery does not look like glory. It looks like hauling wax tablets out of freezing water by lamplight until your fingers crack and bleed. It looks like Emrys kneeling in ankle-deep meltwater for six hours at a stretch, cataloguing fragments in a ledger, refusing help, then accepting help, then pretending she always accepted help. It looks like Sable building drying racks out of scavenged iron and grumbling that oath-masters apparently never heard of shelving. It looks like me, sitting among ten thousand sunken words, holding each tablet to the light, and reading.

Because here is the thing about the First Tongue: it isn't dead. It was *drowned*. And drowned things, given time and care, come back up changed but alive.

Most of the tablets were records. Oaths sworn and witnessed, crowns bound and unbound, treaties older than any living kingdom. Some were craft — the techniques of swordspeech, laid out in diagrams that made Emrys go quiet for entire days. And some were just... people. Letters. A student's complaint about her master's snoring. A recipe, pressed into wax by someone four hundred years dead, for honey cakes. A child's practice alphabet, wobbly letters scratched by someone who might have been me, in another century, in another life.

I cried over the honey cake recipe. Sable pretended not to notice, which is her highest form of tenderness.

"Why keep that?" she asked later, genuinely puzzled, gesturing at the tablet drying on her rack among the great oaths of dead kings. "It's nothing. Someone's baking."

"It's everything," I said. "An order is not its oaths, Sable. Any tyrant can swear. The Regent wore kings who swore constantly. What the Regent could never wear — what it ate kingdoms trying to find and never found — is *this*. The part where people wrote down cake recipes because they thought someone might want them later." I touched the wobbly child-alphabet. "This is what we're rebuilding. Not the power. The keeping."

Sable looked at me for a long moment. Then she took the tablet down, examined her rack, and rebuilt the whole structure overnight so the fragile pieces would sit away from the drips. Which is how Sable says *you're right and I was wrong*, and I have learned to accept it graciously.

* * *

By autumn we had a problem, which is how I knew we were building something real. Real things attract problems the way Emrys attracts blizzards.

The problem was students.

Word travels strangely in the mountains. It comes down shepherd paths and smuggler roads, distorted, growing stranger with each retelling — and somehow, despite all that distortion, the truth of it survived: that above the snowline, in the old Thornfell hold, the last oathmaster was teaching again. Not binding blades to crowns. Teaching the First Tongue to anyone who came honestly. Children mostly, at first. A shepherd boy who wanted to read. Two sisters whose grandmother had hummed fragments of the old cadences as lullabies and never known what they were. Then a disgraced court clerk. Then a widow who'd heard the king himself had knelt in the mud to a swordspeaker and figured the world had shifted and she might as well shift with it.

Emrys taught without powers. That's the part I still find miraculous, though she waves it off. The Unbinding took her swordspeech forever — the price she paid freely, gladly even, though she'd bite me for saying *gladly* — and so she cannot hear steel anymore, cannot press a word into metal, cannot do any of the things that made her legend. So she teaches the way I am learning to keep: with attention. With memory. With the radical, unglamorous act of listening to people completely.

"She asks what I'm afraid of," the shepherd boy told me once, wonder-struck, as if no adult in history had ever done such a thing. "Then she tells me the fear is correct and teaches me anyway."

That's the school. No crown over it. No oath binds it to any throne — Aldric offered a charter, formal protection, and Emrys declined with a courtesy so razor-edged I think he actually enjoyed receiving it. We belong to ourselves. Belonging chosen, not assigned. I finally understood the difference that winter, watching Emrys correct a child's grip on a practice stick with hands that would never again speak to steel, and seeing the child look up at her with the exact trust I'd once carried through a burning archive toward a woman with smoke in her hair.

Some inheritances skip generations. Some skip death entirely.

* * *

Which brings me to last week, and the reason my hands are shaking as I write this — yes, I write now, in three scripts, and yes, I'm showing off, I've earned it.

A rider came up through the pass at dusk. Grey cloak, travel-worn, alone. Emrys saw the cloak color before she saw the face, and I watched twenty years fall across her shoulders like snow off a roof — the hunters, the purge, the shrine ambush, Brannoc dying at a gate so the rest of us could live.

But the rider didn't draw. The rider dismounted, slowly, the way people do when their body hurts everywhere, and stood in our doorway with her hood back and her blade — an oath-blade, I could feel it humming from thirty feet, a corrupted song gone thin and desperate — held out flat across both palms.

Not pointed at anyone. Offered.

"I was at the shrine," the woman said, to Emrys, in a voice scraped raw. "Two years ago. I hunted you. I—" Her throat closed. She stood there shaking, offering the sword to the woman she'd tried to kill, in front of a schoolyard full of children who had gone very, very quiet.

Emrys looked at the blade. At the woman. At me.

And I understood, in that moment, why the archive mattered more than any treaty I could ever have brokered in any marble hall: because the question walking up our mountain was never going to fit on a government form. *Do we open the door for the guilty?* No league of kingdoms could answer that. Only a household could. Only the people who had chosen each other, badly and imperfectly, with confessed sins and rebuilt shelves and honey cake recipes pressed in wax.

Emrys's hand moved to the door.

I held my breath — an old habit, from the mute years, though now it's just what you do when the world tilts.

"Come in out of the cold," Emrys said, and stepped aside.
