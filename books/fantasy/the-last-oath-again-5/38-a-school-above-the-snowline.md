## 38. A School Above the Snowline

The first student broke my door.

Not metaphorically. Not in some poetic sense of *breaking down barriers*. The boy—sixteen, all elbows and misplaced confidence, a farm kid from the Weep's edge who'd walked three hundred miles because his grandmother told him his great-grandmother had been a speaker—put his shoulder through the latch plate at dawn on the ninth of Thawmonth and stood there blinking through the splinters like he couldn't understand what he'd done.

"You knocked," I said.

"I did! I knocked first."

"And then?"

He looked at the door. Looked at me. Looked at the door again, as if hoping one of us would explain the other. "It was very cold," he offered finally.

His name was Tam, and he had no gift whatsoever. I knew it within ten seconds of him opening his mouth—the way he spoke, words just fell out of him like coins from a torn purse, unweighted, unconsidered, with no echo beneath them. In the old days we'd have turned him away at the gate. In the old days, the gate itself would have tested him, and the mountain would have shrugged him back down into the valley, and someone would have written a kind letter to his grandmother explaining that the blood ran thin.

I hired him instead.

That's the part Corvane's ghost still hasn't stopped complaining about, when he bothers to complain at all. He drifts through the hold these days less often than he used—ghosts thin out, Veyra tells me, when they've finished their unfinished business, and whatever Corvane left undone, he seems to have decided most of it is now my problem—but when he does appear, usually at the edge of the training yard at dusk with that look of patient disappointment I spent twenty years missing and ten months remembering why I'd buried it, he watches Tam haul water or split wood or repair the third door this winter has claimed, and he says, *You are building an order without an oath.*

"I'm building a school," I tell him.

*Without an oath,* he repeats, because ghosts have never once accepted a reframe.

"Without an oath," I agree. And then, because he's dead and can't strike me: "Maybe oaths were the problem."

He doesn't argue. That's the worst part. Corvane arguing was a comfort; Corvane going quiet is a verdict.

---

Let me back up. It's easy, in month nine, to forget what month zero looked like.

Month zero was Aldric kneeling in the mud of his own courtyard, aged ten years in a breath, abolishing the Speaker Laws before witnesses while his voice cracked like a boy's. Month zero was Sable standing over her ruined forge in Lowmarket with soot in her eyelashes, saying she'd think about the mountains, and meaning *no*, and then showing up six weeks later with two wagons, four apprentices, and an expression that dared anyone to call it a change of heart. ("I'm not joining your order," she said, unloading anvils in my yard. "You don't have an order. You have a drafty building and a ghost. I'm setting up a forge where the ore is good. You happen to be adjacent.")

Month zero was Veyra asleep in the cart on the road north, her face finally, finally resting, and me watching her and understanding—with the clean, terrible clarity of a woman who has just paid a bill she'd been dodging for twenty years—that the silence in my head where swordspeech used to live wasn't emptiness. It was room.

I'd mourned it properly by then. Don't let anyone tell you surrender is quick. For the first weeks after the throne room I kept reaching for the blades the way you reach for a hand that isn't there anymore—waking in the night certain I'd heard steel singing, lying awake listening to nothing, testing myself against every edge in reach like a gambler down to her last coin checking her pockets out of habit. Grief for a thing you chose to lose is its own particular animal. It doesn't want comfort. It wants you to admit it was worth it.

It was worth it.

But grief is also a liar about duration. By midsummer I could stand in Sable's forge and watch her quench a blade without flinching. By late summer I noticed something stranger: I could hear what the students couldn't—not the speech in the steel, but the shape of their attempts. When a child held a blade wrong, I saw it. When they spoke to the metal with fear in their mouths, I heard the fear, which is the actual enemy, and always was. Corvane used to say the gift was hearing. Twenty years of silence taught me the gift was listening, and only half of listening ever required magic.

So when the first stragglers came—refugees of the purge's long tail, grandchildren of speakers, a grey-cloaked deserter named Maren who knelt in my yard shaking until Veyra brought her tea—I didn't turn them away. And by the time the snow closed the pass in Frostmonth, there were eleven of us in a hold built for thirty, and I had hung a sign over the broken-then-repaired door, and the sign said:

**ALL WHO ARRIVE ARE HEARD.**

Veyra painted it herself, in First Tongue above and Common below, and when I asked her what the Tongue version said precisely, she smiled the small private smile that means she's translated loosely on purpose.

"It says the same thing," she said.

"It doesn't."

"No," she agreed. "But it's truer."

---

Here is what I know now, nine months in, that I didn't know in twenty years of hiding:

Authority rooted in magic dies with the magic. I watched it die in me, and I'll be honest—there were nights, early on, when I wondered what exactly remained of Emrys Winterbourne if you took away the swordspeech, and the honest answer was *a tired woman with good scars and a talent for survival*, and that answer sat in my chest like a stone.

But authority rooted in love compounds. It's the smuggler's arithmetic again, which I suppose I never really unlearned: small investments, made daily, at terrible interest. Tam hauling water. Veyra sitting with Maren for four hours while the deserter finally said the name of the shrine where she'd killed her own teacher. Sable forging practice-blades with no bindings at all—"blades that carry nothing," she calls them, and gets furious if you praise them, because *they're just well-made swords, Wren, stop being mystical about metallurgy.* Me, walking the wall at dusk with whichever student had failed hardest that day, saying nothing, because nothing was the lesson.

Corvane ruled by weight. His word settled onto a room like snowfall; you felt bound before you understood the binding. I loved him. I want that on the record, since he reads over my shoulder whenever I write, and yes, Master, I know you know, write it anyway. But his love came packaged inside hierarchy, inside the oath, inside the architecture of the crown we served—and when the crown turned on us, everything built inside that architecture burned with it.

We're building outside it now. No charter from any king. Aldric offered one, twice, sent sealed letters up the pass with royal messengers who looked deeply uncomfortable at our door (Tam made them shovel snow both times; I've decided this is policy). The letters sit in a box under the floorboards where a sword-hilt once hid. I haven't refused them aloud. Refusal is a formal act in the First Tongue, and I'm saving my formal acts. But the school stands or falls on no signature but its own, and every student knows it, and knowing it makes them different. They aren't training to serve. They're learning to listen.

Veyra teaches the First Tongue to whoever has the ear for it—which, unsettlingly, is more of them than expected. She's fourteen now by body, older than that by anything that counts, and each word she speaks still costs her. We've learned to see it: the faint silvering at her temples after a hard lesson, the way she sits heavier afterward. She rations herself like water in a dry crossing. But she spends freely anyway, on the ones who need it, and when I told her once that she should hoard her years, she looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"You told me once," she said, "that a voice kept safe is a voice wasted."

"I said something wiser-sounding than that."

"You absolutely did not."

The girl argues like a ferryman. I blame Ferro, whose pardon came through in spring and who visited for one week during which he taught every student on the hold to cheat at cards and left calling it education.

---

Today was Thawmonth, ninth, again—one year almost to the day since a mud-caked child collapsed on my threshold and ended my life as I knew it, which is to say, saved it. The morning class was binding-theory, which I teach the way Corvane taught me except for everything, and Tam—he of the broken doors, still giftless, still here, now my de facto steward because the man can fix anything with a hinge—raised his hand and asked the question.

The question. Every school has one, the question that defines it whether the founders admit it or not. Ours, in the old order, was *what will you swear?* Asked at the gate. Answered with blood.

Tam asked: "If we don't bind ourselves to anything—no crown, no oath—then what holds us together?"

And the yard went quiet, eleven faces turning toward me, and Corvane drifted visibly at the wall's edge, actually leaning in, the old meddler, and I opened my mouth to give the polished answer I'd been drafting for months—

—and Veyra beat me to it.

"What held you together before?" she asked him. "Before any oath. When you were a farm kid and nobody asked you to be anything."

Tam blinked. "My family, I guess. My gran."

"Did you sign anything?"

He laughed. "No."

"There," she said, like the whole architecture of civilization fit in her palm, which, if you press a speaker far enough, it does. "Oaths don't make bonds. They *record* them. We got confused about that, all of us, for nine hundred years. We thought writing the thing down made it real." She tapped her own chest. "The bond comes first. It always came first. My mother pressed a word into my hand in a burning archive and never swore anything—she just *gave*, and I carried it three hundred miles, and that carrying was stronger than any oath ever spoken into steel."

Then she went silver at the temples, sat down heavily on the mounting block, and waved at me to continue, insufferably regal about it.

So I gave them the unpolished answer, which was: "What holds us together is that we show up tomorrow. That's it. That's the entire mystery. Show up, listen, mend what you broke—including, Tam, the door, again—and do it beside people who'd do it for you. Any kingdom can buy an oath. Nobody can buy that."

Eleven faces. Some convinced, some skeptical, one—Maren, the deserter, who still startles at loud noises—quietly crying, which by month nine I've learned is just how she takes notes.

Corvane, at the wall, wore an expression I'd never seen on him in life or death. If I had to name it, I'd say it looked like a man discovering the foundation he'd poured was sounder than the cathedral he'd built on it.

*Perhaps,* he said, to me alone, in the voice like wind through a sword-guard, *oaths were scaffolding.*

"High praise, Master."

*Don't preen. You've still only repaired three doors this winter. In my day the mountain respected its doors.*

And he thinned out into the evening light, thinner than last season, and I understood that he is finishing. Whatever he stayed to see, he is close to seeing it. Ghosts don't haunt places, Veyra says; they haunt unfinished sentences, and somewhere in the last year I became, apparently, a completed thought.

I don't know what I'll do when he goes. I know what I'll do tomorrow: walk the wall at dusk, find whichever student failed hardest, say nothing, mean everything. Then sleep, and wake, and do it again. Small investments, daily, at terrible interest.

The compounding is already visible. Come summer there will be more of us than the hold was built for. Word travels down the passes the way meltwater does—slow, then suddenly everywhere. Sable says she'll need a second forge. Veyra has started copying the recovered archive fragments onto fresh vellum, teaching two of the older students the script, a language reborn in a child's careful handwriting.

Tonight, though, tonight there is snow again, soft and unhurried, the kind that means the mountain approves of nothing and forbids nothing either. The students are at supper. The sign over the door catches lantern-light through the frost on the window: **ALL WHO ARRIVE ARE HEARD**, in two tongues, one true and one truer.

I hung that sign thinking it was for the people coming up the pass.

I'm only now beginning to understand it was for me—for the woman who spent twenty years making sure no one could hear her, and who has, against all arithmetic, become someone whose door is worth breaking down.

Tam will need to fix it again by spring. Good. That's the kind of problem I want.

Tomorrow, though—tomorrow there's a knock I'm not expecting. I hear it in the way the dogs go quiet, in the way the mountain holds its breath. Someone is climbing the pass tonight, and the snow says they're alone, and the wind carries a scrap of sound I haven't heard in a year:

Grey cloak bells.
