## 40. Oath Kept, Again

### I.

The sign took me three tries to letter, which is embarrassing for a woman who once wrote oaths into steel with her heartbeat.

The first attempt said *ALL WHO ARRIVE ARE HEARD* in the formal script of the old order—the one they taught us before they taught us anything useful, like how to hold a blade so it doesn't take your thumb. It looked too much like the doors of the archive Veyra remembers burning. She stood behind me in the snow with her arms crossed and said, "That's a door that lies," and she was right, because that script had hung over plenty of doors that did not hear anyone at all.

The second attempt was too small. The third one I carved instead of painted, deep into a plank of mountain pine Sable sent up from Lowmarket, and when I ran my thumb along the grooves I felt nothing—which is to say, I felt exactly what I always feel now, the clean flat silence where the swordspeech used to live—and somehow that made it true in a way ink never could be.

*All who arrive are heard.*

I hung it on the door of the hold that had been my prison for twenty years and was now, apparently, a school.

"You could have just said 'welcome,'" Veyra observed.

"Welcome is for people who expect guests."

"And you don't?"

I looked out past the doorstep, down the switchback road toward the valley where spring was already turning the world green while we still had drifts to our knees. Somewhere down there, travelers were making decisions about whether to climb. Some of them were running from things. Some of them were carrying things they didn't want to carry anymore. A few, if the letters were honest, were coming because they'd heard a rumor: that there was a place above the snowline where blades were studied but never bound, where oaths were spoken aloud to other people instead of hammered into metal, where the woman who unmade the Regent would listen to whatever you'd done and not flinch.

Rumors exaggerate. I've killed exactly two people since the throne room, both in defense of others, and I flinched plenty.

"I don't expect them," I told her. "But I leave the door unlocked."

Veyra smiled—that crooked, older-than-her-years smile that cost her something every time, because each word she speaks spends a little more of her childhood. She is fourteen now and has the hands of someone much older and, on bad days, the eyes too. She went inside to her archive room, where the drowned monastery's salvaged books are slowly drying out under her patient care, and I stayed on the doorstep a moment longer, doing the thing I do most mornings now:

Counting.

Old habit. Twenty years of counting stores, counting scars, counting reasons to stay invisible. But the ledger reads differently this year.

One school, barely holding its roof in wind. Four students, ranging from a ferryman's daughter with a gift for hearing lies to a disgraced crown courier who talks in his sleep in three languages. One child-archivist who is not a child in any way that matters. One ghost who drops by when he feels like being forgiven, which is to say rarely. And me, standing in the doorway of a life I never planned, holding a hammer and a sign that says the truest thing I know.

The snow began again, soft as an apology. I went inside to put the kettle on, because justice, I have learned, runs mostly on hot tea and patience.

### II.

Aldric, King of Caelmere, First of That Unfortunate Name:

Let me tell you what it is to reign mortal.

You wake in the morning and your back hurts, and this is a miracle. You eat breakfast and taste it—all of it, the burnt edge of the toast, the bitterness of the tea—and you nearly weep into your plate because for ten years you tasted nothing, you *were* nothing, a coat someone else wore to work. My physicians call the gray streaks in my hair premature aging. My physicians are wrong. Those are ten years coming back to roost, and I have decided to wear them like medals.

I abolished the Speaker Laws in my second month. It took less time than drafting them had taken my great-grandfather; tyranny is efficient that way. What took longer was the harder work: sitting in a room with survivors of the purge and letting them say what was done to their families, without guards, without interruption, without the cushioned distance of a crown. Master Brannoc's name came up often. So did names I never learned, because the Regent kept no records—it consumed memory rather than keeping it, and so the dead of the purge exist now only in the testimony of the living. Which means the living must testify. Forever, if necessary. Justice, I have come to believe, is not a verdict. It is a practice, like archery or grief, and you get better at it only by doing it badly for a long time.

Yesterday a delegation arrived from the eastern provinces demanding I restore the old regalia—the melted seventh fragment, reforged, set in the crown as it had been for nine centuries. "Tradition," they called it. They were not wrong. Tradition is exactly what it was: the Regent's leash, worn proudly by kings who never knew they were dogs.

I had the pieces gathered up publicly in the courtyard and recast into a bell.

It hangs in the capital square now. It rings at dawn and dusk, and anyone may ring it besides, and when it sounds, every oath-speaker in the city reports feeling a faint hum in their teeth—a ghost-note of the harmony the six fragments used to make. Sable wrote to me about this. Her theory is that the metal remembers being part of something whole, and wants to be again. Sable's theories are usually half right, delivered with such certainty that you accept the whole.

There are days I miss being a passenger. This is the confession I make only here, in this private page, in cipher Emrys herself taught me. On the worst days—the day the granary failed, the day the northern lords threatened secession, the day I signed an execution order myself, freely, for a man who burned an orphanage—I understand why the Regent found us so easy to wear. We *want* to be worn. Responsibility is heavy. Someone offering to carry it for you, smiling your smile, is the sweetest poison a king can drink.

So I have built the antidote into the architecture of my reign. Every edict I sign is read aloud in the square within the hour, by a speaker chosen by lot, in plain language. Every law carries its author's name—not "the Crown," but mine, Aldric, son of Marwen, the man who was absent from his own kingdom for a decade and intends to answer for it. When I die—and I will die, on schedule, like a person—my successor will inherit these practices and not my power. Power was the wound. Practice is the healing.

Ninety seconds. That's all I ever got, back then. I have spent a year spending the rest of my minutes as carefully as a miser counts coins, and I intend to spend every last one.

The bell is ringing dusk now. I will go down and stand among my people while it sounds, one face in the crowd, accountable to the sound.

It is enough. For the first time in my life, in my reign, in my body—it is enough.

### III.

Sable's hands smelled of coal and quench-water, same as always, and the forge sang the way it always sang—except now she listened to it differently.

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking an oath: the breaking doesn't end it. An oath is a shape you cut into yourself, and even abandoned, the hollow stays. For twenty years Sable had carried her hollow around like a misshapen tool, reaching for it out of habit, finding nothing, hating herself a little more each time.

Then the scabbard happened. Then the throne room happened. Then the Regent came apart like a knot untied by patient fingers, and Sable walked out of the capital with her broken oath-blade across her back and discovered, somewhere on the road home, that the hollow had changed. Not filled—healed, the way bone heals, leaving a ridge of scar stronger than the original.

She opened her forge at dawn and closed it when the light failed, and the town of Lowmarket, which had burned, rebuilt itself around her chimney smoke the way towns do: stubbornly, imperfectly, with arguments about property lines in between acts of staggering generosity.

The blades she made carried no bindings. This was not a slogan; it was metallurgy. Where the old order had quenched steel in oath-memory—whispering words into the cooling metal so the blade would remember its purpose forever—Sable quenched hers in plain water and left the purpose to the wielder. "A blade should be a question," she told her apprentices, "not an answer." They thought this was very profound. She had stolen it from Emrys, who had stolen it from Master Corvane, who had probably stolen it from someone whose name the purge ate. Everything true gets passed hand to hand like contraband.

Twice a month she rode up the Thornfell road with supplies—nails, hinges, iron stock, the good bitter tea Emrys pretended not to love—and twice a month she and Emrys sat by the fire and argued, comfortably, the way old soldiers argue, about everything except the two things they never mentioned: the informer's report Sable had filed during the purge, and the order Emrys had failed to give that might have saved the archive. Confessed sins sit quietly once they're truly confessed. It's the unconfessed ones that howl.

Today, though, something new. Sable set a wrapped bundle on Emrys's table and unwrapped it with a smith's ceremony: a blade, newly forged, plain-hilted, beautiful in the severe way of honest tools.

"For the school," she said. "For teaching. Before you say anything—it's not an oath-blade. It won't sing, won't bind, won't remember. But I held it through the whole quench thinking about the door sign. All who arrive are heard. Figured the steel could at least keep quiet and listen."

Emrys picked it up. Turned it in the light. And laughed—one short, rusty bark of a laugh, the kind she rationed like wartime sugar.

"It's perfect. It does nothing."

"It does what it's told to do by whoever holds it. Same as everyone." Sable drank her tea. Outside, one of the students was failing loudly at footwork drills, and Veyra was correcting her in the First Tongue, patiently, the way you'd teach a bird to sing. "How many have come this season?"

"Eleven. Three stayed."

"The courier still talking in his sleep?"

"Worse. Now she answers herself."

Sable grinned and looked into the fire, and let herself think the thought she'd been circling for months: *this is what redemption looks like when it arrives.* Not trumpets. Not forgiveness handed down from a throne. Just a forge, a road, a friend, a job worth doing, done well, done again tomorrow. Guilt doesn't vanish. It gets outvoted, day after day, by small kindnesses, until one morning you notice the vote has gone the other way for good.

She'd bring more iron next trip. The students kept chipping their practice weapons, and honestly, it was nice to be needed by people who hadn't known her when.

### IV.

The archive room smells of beeswax and cold stone and, faintly, of the lake.

Veyra keeps a jar of lake water on the shelf beside her desk. She cannot explain this even to herself, except that the water remembers the drowned, and she promised the dead remembrance, and a promise to the dead is the one kind you can't renegotiate. Each morning she uncaps it, breathes, and recites one name from her ledger of the forgotten—the list grows shorter every season as survivors surface with fragments of memory, and every recovered name goes into the reconstructed archive in ink mixed with that same lake water, so that the record itself carries what it records.

The First Tongue is coming back. Slowly, unevenly, like a body relearning a limb. There are forty-one speakers now, by Veyra's count—she counts everything; counting is how she holds the world together. Most speak only a handful of words. Some, like the ferryman's daughter, can hold a whole sentence. None of them bind crowns. That grammar has been deliberately lost—Veyra burned those pages herself, with Aldric's blessing and Emrys watching, and felt the language sigh with relief.

What remains is the rest of it. Words for truth-telling. Words for grief, precise as surgery. Words that force hidden memories open—used now only in courts of reconciliation, only with consent, only witnessed. The tongue that once chained kingdoms is becoming what it maybe always wanted to be underneath: a language for saying what actually happened.

Some nights the cost visits her. She catches her reflection and sees the fine lines at her eyes, hears the faint roughness age has pressed into a voice that should still be a child's, and she grieves—briefly, honestly, the way Emrys taught her to grieve, which is fully and then done. Then she remembers the hunter's blade shattering, the grey cloak falling, the moment her voice came back as a weapon, and she thinks: *every word I spend buys something. Let me choose well.*

Tonight she is cataloguing. Tonight Emrys knocks and enters with two cups of tea and the particular expression that means a student has asked a question Veyra will find interesting.

"The courier asked me today," Emrys says, sitting on the floor because the chair is buried in manuscripts, "why the First Tongue survived at all. Why a language built for binding didn't just die with its purpose."

"What did you tell her?"

"That I didn't know. That I'd ask the archivist."

Veyra turns her cup slowly. This is a question she has answered for herself a hundred times, in the dark, in the lake-smelling room, but never aloud.

"A language isn't its use," she says finally. "It's its speakers. The Regent wore eleven kings and ate nine kingdoms' memories, and it still couldn't kill the tongue, because tongues don't live in palaces. They live in mouths. In a girl's palm, pressed there by a dying woman in a burning archive. In a mute child's silence, kept safe for years." She looks up. "It survived because someone carried it who had nothing left to carry but that."

Emrys is quiet for a long moment. "And now?"

"Now it lives in forty-one mouths that mostly use it to apologize beautifully." Veyra smiles. "I think that's what it was for all along. We just had kings using the wrong vocabulary."

They drink their tea. The lamp gutters. Somewhere below, the wind tests the shutters and finds them wanting, and the mountain holds them anyway, imperfectly, the way everything worth holding is held.

### V.

Master Corvane's ghost appeared on the doorstep at first light, translucent as frost on glass, wearing the disapproving expression he'd perfected over four decades of teaching.

Emrys found him there when she went to sweep the snow off the sign. She did not startle. Twenty years ago a ghost on her threshold would have dropped her to her knees. Now it was closer to an unwelcome relative arriving early.

"You hid her lineage from me," she said. No greeting. They'd moved past greetings sometime around the Steppe.

"I seeded your rescue of her decades before her birth, yes, and I'd do it again, and I'm not going to apologize for it, so we can skip that argument." He drifted a half-inch higher, which was his version of crossing his arms. "I came because the grey cloak is two days behind me."

The broom stopped moving.

"A survivor."

"One of the hunters. One of the corrupted. She was sixteen at the purge—they took her young, turned her speech inside out, pointed her at people. She's been walking away from them for a year. She heard about your sign." His voice softened in the way it only did when he quoted someone else's courage. "She's afraid you'll close the door."

Emrys leaned the broom against the wall and looked out down the white road, empty in the early light, holding a future arrival she couldn't yet see.

"She broke her half-oath," she said. "Like Sable."

"She did worse than break it. She used it."

Emrys was silent. The wind moved the snow around like a slow animal. Behind her, inside, she could hear the students waking, Veyra lighting lamps, the ordinary machinery of a morning that believed in itself.

Twenty years ago, a door had opened for her in a blizzard, and everything since had been the long process of deserving it—or failing to, and opening it anyway.

"When she comes," Emrys said, "put the kettle on before she knocks. Nothing says *heard* like a warm room waiting."

Corvane's ghost smiled—for real, for once, without the weight of old guilt bending it—and faded with the sunrise.

Emrys swept the step, straightened the sign, and left the door unlocked.

All who arrive are heard. Even the guilty. *Especially* the guilty—because justice, she had finally learned, is not a verdict rendered and finished. It is a door, and a fire, and the daily, unglamorous practice of listening: done badly, done again, done better, until the practice outlives the practitioners and becomes simply the way a people keep faith with each other.

Down the mountain road, small and distant against the snow, a figure in grey began to climb.
