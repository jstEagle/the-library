## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The Hollow Valley did not welcome the dawn so much as absorb it.

Ada Vane had watched that absorption her whole life — thirty-one years of standing at the valley's rim or walking its floor, watching the light come down the eastern cliffs in a long grey wash that never quite turned gold. Elsewhere, she'd been told, mornings burned. Elsewhere, the sun struck the world like a struck match, all flame and color, and the air itself seemed to hum with something alive. Here the light came down flat and even, like water poured over stone, and it found nothing to catch. No glimmering wards on the doorposts. No blessing-lanterns swinging from the shrine eaves. No witchlights bobbing over the millpond at dusk, because there was no witch to light them and no pond that would hold one.

Magic could not follow a person into the Hollow Valley. That was the whole of its fame, and it was not a boast but a fact as plain as gravity. Whatever you carried of the uncanny — a hex, a blessing, a god's mark seared into the skin of your wrist — it went quiet at the border stones. It went quiet, and then it went *nothing*, and what remained inside the valley's embrace was only flesh and weather and work.

Most people called that safety.

Ada called it silence, and she had never decided which word she hated more.

She stood now at the mouth of the Kettle Pass, where the trade road dropped out of the northern hills and spilled its travelers into the valley like grain into a hopper, and she counted her cargo. Three wagons. Eleven souls, counting children. Two drivers who smelled of fear-sweat under their pipe-smoke, which was normal; one driver who kept touching the little charm knotted into his horse's bridle, which was not.

"You'll want to leave that," Ada said.

The man started. He hadn't seen her step out of the shadow of the pass's first pillar of rock, though she'd been standing there for an hour, letting the cold come up through her boots, learning the shape of the morning. He was young, round-faced, with the soft hands of someone who'd made his living in cities where doors were opened by spells and bread was baked by hearthstones that remembered warmth without being asked.

"It's just a luck-knot," he said. "My mother tied it."

"Ma'am," Ada corrected, nodding toward the older woman seated in the second wagon, wrapped in three shawls against a cold that hadn't arrived yet. "And I'm sure she tied it well. But the border stones are a half mile on, and when your luck-knot crosses them, it won't be a luck-knot anymore. It'll be string. If there's anything bound into it — anything *living* in it, a spirit, a whisper, a grandmother's ghost keeping watch — crossing will hurt it. Some things don't come back from that."

The man looked at the knot. Then at his mother. Then at Ada, taking in, perhaps for the first time, the details that marked her trade: the short sword worn high on the hip where a hand could find it fast; the long knife at the small of her back; the quilted jack dyed the color of wet slate, mended in six places, each mend done by her own needle because a mercenary who couldn't sew her own armor couldn't afford her own armor; and above all the wrist-guards, wide bands of boiled leather covering both forearms from palm to elbow, which everyone in the valley knew meant *oathsworn* and nobody in the valley asked about twice.

"You're Vane," the mother said suddenly, from beneath her shawls. Her voice was dry as seed-husks. "The escort woman. They said you were reliable."

"I'm reliable," Ada agreed.

"They say you've never lost a cargo."

"I haven't."

"Not once? In — what is it, fifteen years?"

"Fifteen years this autumn." She let the pause sit. "Not once."

The old woman studied her with eyes gone pale as frost-rimed glass, and then she smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "They also say you're the last oathsworn left in the valley. That the god you swore to stopped speaking before you finished growing, and still you wear his marks and keep his rules. They say you're faithful to a door that hasn't been answered in a hundred years."

Ada felt the familiar tightening behind her breastbone, the one that was not quite anger and not quite grief and had learned, over the years, to pass for patience.

"I'm reliable," she said again.

* * *

The border stones came up on them an hour past midmorning: seven granite pillars sunk into the earth along either side of the road, older than any record, their surfaces worn featureless by ten thousand years of rain. Travelers said the stones had been raised by the first refugees fleeing the Sundered Wars — people who wanted, more than anything in the world, a place where the wars' sorceries could not pursue them, and who had bargained with something, or simply prayed hard enough into the bones of the land, until the valley itself agreed to be empty.

Nobody knew if it was true. What everyone knew was what happened at the stones, because everyone had seen it, and seeing it never got easier.

Their lead wagon crossed between the pillars, and the world changed the way a held breath changes when it's finally let go — except here it was the reverse. The horse's breath, visible and steaming one stride before the line, stopped steaming one stride after. The faint warmth of a fire-charm banked in the wagon's footwell — the young man's mother's other indulgence — guttered out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. And the luck-knot, when the driver finally untied it at Ada's instruction and held it in his cupped hands, was only string. Braided, faded, lovingly worked string, with nothing in it anymore but the memory of having meant something.

He didn't cry. But he folded the string very carefully and put it away inside his coat, next to his heart, and Ada looked away and pretended to check the wheel-bolts.

That was the tax the valley charged. Not coin. Not blood. Just this: everything enchanted became merely itself. Everything blessed became merely kind. Everything holy became merely old. People came here to escape debts and curses and the attentions of gods and the men who served them, and the valley took them in and made them safe, and the price of safety was that they could bring nothing through the door but their own two hands.

It was, Ada thought, not for the first time, a fair bargain for most people. She had simply never met anyone for whom it was a fair bargain, including herself. Especially herself.

Because the oath-marks on her forearms did not die at the border stones. They should have. Every other scrap of the divine went silent within three strides of those pillars — she'd watched pilgrims' relics go cold, watched a hedge-witch's familiar turn into a common cat mid-leap, confused and furious about it. But the twin sigils inked into her skin when she was nine years old, the coiled vessel-shape and the waiting hand, the mark of the god called the Vessel-Who-Waits — hers endured. Dimmed. Muted, like a voice heard through a wall. But present.

The church elders had theories. The elders always had theories. Ada had stopped listening to theories around the same age she'd stopped believing the god would speak again, and those two events had occurred on the same day, though she did not let herself think about that day often, and she would not think about it today.

Today there was work.

* * *

The road south ran forty miles through the length of the valley, from the Kettle Pass to the ferry-crossing at Greywater, where the river Slough bent lazy around the valley's southern horn and the far shore began the lands where magic still lived and thrived and occasionally ate people. A routine run. Wool, salt pork, iron fittings, eleven passengers emigrating south for reasons they mostly didn't share. Bandits didn't operate in the Hollow Valley — there was nothing magical worth stealing and every farmer was armed and humorless — but wolves still operated, and desperation still operated, and the weather in the passes killed the unprepared with cheerful impartiality. An escort's job was less fighting than *knowing*: knowing which ravine funneled wind like a bellows, knowing which waystation's well ran bitter in late summer, knowing which passenger was lying about why they were leaving and whether the lie was dangerous.

By the third day Ada knew all eleven of them better than they knew she did.

There was the young man with the dead luck-knot, whose name was Pell and whose lie was that he was traveling for trade. He was fleeing a betrothal contract sealed with a binding-spell, and he flinched every time he passed a milestone, half-expecting the contract's leash to snap taut and haul him home. It wouldn't. That was the point of the valley. Ada let him believe she hadn't noticed the way he checked over his shoulder at every crossroads, and she adjusted their pace so that he was never the last wagon on the road, because a man running from a spell slept badly when he could see open country behind him.

There was the mother, Mistress Harrow, whose lie was that she wasn't dying. Ada had driven enough carts to know the particular careful way a person in pain arranged themselves around their own body, and she saw the poppy tincture bottle in the medicine roll when they repacked the wagons at the second night's camp. She said nothing. She gave the woman the seat with the least jolt and the blanket with the fewest holes, and when Mistress Harrow caught her looking — one of those level looks the dying give, testing — Ada said only, "Greywater's ferry has a covered deck. Warm in the wind." And the old woman nodded once, and that was the whole conversation, and somehow it was enough.

There were the three brothers from the valley's east farms, moving south because their land had gone sour — not blighted, exactly, just *tired*, yielding less every year, and no wise-woman could find a reason because there was no reason, because the valley permitted no enchantment to explain, only honest exhaustion and honest soil. There was a widow with a baby who cried at night in a way that made the dogs uneasy, and Ada chose not to wonder about that, because wondering led to questions, and questions in the Hollow Valley had a way of becoming accusations, and accusations had a way of becoming pyres, historically speaking, and the valley had worked hard to become boring and deserved some credit for it.

And there was the eleventh passenger, who sat at the rear of the last wagon and said almost nothing the entire journey, and whom Ada thought about more than was comfortable.

She was maybe nineteen, travel-stained, sharp-faced, with the look of someone who had walked a long way alone and gotten good at it. She boarded at the Kettle Pass with no luggage but a satchel, paid her fare in clipped southern coin, and gave her name as Merrin, which Ada privately suspected was no more her name than Ada's own cheerfulness was real. On the second evening, while the camp settled and the fires were lit — ordinary fires, wood-fires, the only kind the valley allowed — Merrin sat apart on a fallen log, turning something over in her fingers.

A book. Small, thick, bound in leather gone dark with handling.

Ada noticed it the way she noticed everything, which was to say automatically and then deliberately not at all. A traveler's book was a traveler's business. Psalter, ledger, diary, novel — none of it her concern. She had built fifteen years of reputation on the principle that a mercenary's eyes belonged to the road and the treeline, not to her clients' secrets.

But there was something about the way the girl held it. Not like reading. Like *gripping*. The way you grip a railing in a storm, or the hand of someone you love at a funeral. And every few minutes, when she thought no one watched, Merrin would glance north — back the way they'd come, back toward the pass, back toward whatever lay beyond it — with an expression Ada recognized from mirrors and bad nights.

The expression of someone who has left something behind that is not finished with them.

Ada banked the fire, checked the pickets, walked the perimeter out to fifty yards and back, and told herself the itch between her shoulder blades was just the cold coming down off the cliffs. It was, after all, the season for it.

* * *

On the fifth morning they reached Greywater.

The ferry landing sat in a shallow bowl of reeds and rope-bridges, and across the Slough the southern lands showed themselves: greener, louder, a haze over the far trees that might have been morning mist or might have been the accumulated shimmer of a hundred small workings waking with the day. Ada felt the change before the boat touched the far bank. Everyone did. It came as a pressure in the ears, a taste like copper and honey at the back of the throat.

And on her wrists, beneath twenty-year-old leather, the oath-marks woke.

They did this every time. A slow warming, then a pulse, deep and arrhythmic, like a heart heard through a wall — like a heart heard through a century of wall. The Vessel-Who-Waits, stirring in whatever sleep or absence held him, reaching for his sworn ones the moment they stepped outside the valley's hush. Reaching, and finding, and saying nothing. Never anything. A hundred years of priests praying into that silence and calling the silence holy.

Ada pressed her wrists against her thighs and breathed through it, the way you breathe through a cramp, and looked out at the far shore, and did not let her face do anything.

Mistress Harrow disembarked first, helped down by her son, who had wept quietly at the rail when the luck-knot's string stayed string and his mother had patted his arm and said, "Well. Now I know my luck was mine all along," which was either wisdom or heartbreak, possibly both. The brothers unloaded wool and argued cheerfully about freight prices. The widow stepped onto southern soil and the baby stopped crying, instantly, mid-wail, and the dogs' unease was explained and Ada chose, again, not to wonder.

Pell lingered last but one. He stood on the gangplank with his mother's string in his fist and said, awkwardly, "You could come south. Someone with your — with what you can do. You'd make a fortune down here."

"I don't want a fortune," Ada said.

"Everyone wants something."

"Yes," Ada said. "Well."

He flushed, touched his cap, went. And then it was only Ada and the girl called Merrin, standing on the ferry deck as the crew cast off the lines, and Ada realized the girl hadn't moved to disembark.

"Aren't you getting off?" Merrin asked.

"I'm paid to Greywater and back," Ada said. "Return cargo, return escort. My contract runs the full circuit."

Merrin looked at her for a long moment. Up close her eyes were dark and too steady for nineteen, and there was ink on her fingers — the stain of someone who handled books constantly, deeply, the way smiths wore soot. "Fifteen years," she said. "Carrying people back and forth across the one line in the world that keeps them safe. Never losing a single one." She smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, but it was not gentle either. "Don't you ever wonder what it would feel like to lose something? To have it matter that much?"

Ada looked at the dark book clutched against the girl's chest, and at the ink-stained hands, and at the road behind them all, forty miles north to the pass and the border stones and the silence.

"No," she said, which was a lie, and they both probably knew it.

The ferry pulled away from the southern shore. The pressure lifted from Ada's ears. The copper-and-honey taste faded. And beneath the boiled leather, her god's marks dimmed back down to their permanent ember-glow, that patient, mute insistence, the way they had glowed every day of her life inside the valley — the one thing the Hollow could never quite hollow out.

*The Vessel waits,* the priests said. *His silence is his devotion. He waits for us to be worthy.*

Ada had spent thirty-one years in the valley that magic could not follow, and she had never once managed to convince herself that she belonged anywhere — not in the valley that had raised her and muted her, nor in the loud green south where her oath might mean something if only anyone remembered what oaths were for. She was reliable. She lost nothing. She carried other people across the line between safety and the world, back and forth, back and forth, belonging to neither side of it.

The road home stretched north, flat and grey in the failing light.

Two days later, in a border tavern called the Drowned Bell, a stranger would stumble through the door clutching a century-old book, and everything she had just told the ink-stained girl would stop being true.

But that night, driving the empty wagons north under a sky the valley refused to gild, Ada Vane knew none of it yet. She only knew the silence, and the ache, and the long road — and somewhere behind her, unseen, a girl with a book full of tomorrow's dates was walking the same road south, carrying a death with her name beside it, straight toward the one place in the world that could not protect her from it.
