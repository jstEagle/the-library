## 2. A Stranger With Tomorrow's Date

The tavern at the valley's edge was called the Last Kindness, which Ada Vane had always thought was less a name than a warning.

It stood where the Hollow Road narrowed to a single cart's width, wedged between the last farmstead of the valley and the first black pines of the borderlands beyond. Travelers came to it the way pilgrims come to shrines — not for joy, but because it was the final place that would have them. Inside its walls, the air tasted like woodsmoke and tallow and the particular flatness that hung over everything in the Hollow Valley: no charm-warmed hearths, no luck-blessed ale, no hedge-witch remedies in the broth. Just fire, water, barley, and hands. In most of the world, Ada had been told, magic soaked into things the way damp soaks into stone. Here, it drained away. A spell cast at the border died before it finished being spoken. Even the gods, the elders said, could not follow you into the Hollow.

That was supposed to be a comfort. Ada had never been sure why.

She sat with her back to the wall, as always, in the corner booth beneath a shelf of chipped clay mugs, and watched the room the way she watched everything — patiently, without appearing to. The escort job had gone clean. A wool factor named Dossel and his two nervous clerks, delivered from the terraced farms of the upper valley to the border post without so much as a rude word from anyone on the road. Easy coin. Dossel was upstairs now, snoring off his celebration, and in the morning Ada would collect her fee, walk back into the valley proper, and take whatever contract the winter offered next. It was a small life. She had made it herself, which was more than most people could say, and she tried to remember that on nights when the smallness pressed against her like a hand.

The tavern was half-full and loud in the ordinary way: drovers arguing about tolls, a card game turning sour by degrees at the long table, the keeper's wife singing something tuneless while she scrubbed the counter. Rain had started an hour ago, drumming on the shingles, and everyone had the loose-shouldered ease of people glad to be under a roof.

Then the door banged open, and the room went wrong.

The stranger came in sideways, the way men come in when they've been running and their legs haven't yet been told. He was young — twenty-five, maybe, though rain and terror had stripped the years off him unevenly — with dark hair plastered to his forehead and a merchant's coat too fine for the road and torn anyway. One boot had lost its sole somewhere behind him; he left wet red prints, and Ada noted, with the detached part of her mind that never stopped working, that the blood was his own, from a gash along his calf, and not much of it. A wound like that meant he'd been moving fast through country that fought him.

Under his arm he clutched a book.

Not read — *clutched*, both arms wrapped around it the way a drowning man holds a spar. And not just any book, though Ada couldn't have said how she knew that from across the room. Something about the way he held it, close to his chest like a thing with a heartbeat, made her certain it mattered more to him than his own skin.

"Door," said the keeper, a broad woman named Marta whose voice could stop cattle. "Shut it or—"

"Please." The stranger's voice cracked on the word. He got the door shut behind him and stood there dripping, chest heaving, looking out at the room full of strangers with the expression of a man who has run out of places to run *to*. "Please, I need — is there anyone here who can read old script? Anyone at all?"

That should have been the end of it. In any other tavern in the world, someone might have laughed, someone else might have poured him a drink, and the night would have closed over him like water.

But this was the border of the Hollow Valley, and the people who lived within a day's walk of that line were the kind of people who had come *to* the line on purpose. Farmers who'd lost crops to a neighbor's hex. Families with a child born marked. Men who'd sworn vows they regretted and wanted distance from whatever heard them. They lived at the edge of the one place magic could not follow, and they had learned to flinch at anything that smelled of the other side of it.

So when the stranger held up his book, open, and said, "It has tonight's date in it," the room did not laugh.

The card game stopped first. Then the singing. The silence spread outward from the long table like ice across a pond, and Ada felt it press against her ears.

"What did you say?" asked one of the drovers slowly, rising from his bench. He was a big man with a beard going gray in patches, and Ada knew his face from years of border contracts — Harl Vetch, and under other circumstances she'd have called him decent.

"The date." The stranger turned the book toward them, holding it up like a man surrendering a weapon or presenting evidence, and his hands were shaking so badly the pages trembled. "Tonight's date. The seventeenth of Deepfrost. It's written here, and it was written a hundred years ago, and my name is beside it, and—" His voice failed. He swallowed. "And a word after it."

"What word?" That was Marta, from behind her counter, rag gone still in her hands.

He looked at her. He looked at all of them. Ada watched him decide, in real time, whether lying would save him, and watched him understand, someplace behind his eyes, that it was already far too late for that.

"*Dead,*" he said.

* * *

Afterward, Ada would try to reconstruct exactly how the mob formed, and she never quite could. Mobs don't assemble; they condense, the way fog does, out of air that had seemed clear.

Someone said *omen*. Someone else said the book must be witchwork, and that nothing written a hundred years past could name a living man unless the writing had made it so — that the book hadn't predicted his death but scheduled it, that carrying the thing had signed him, and that anything signed could sign others. A woman near the hearth began to cry quietly. Harl Vetch took a step forward, then another, and his decent face had gone strange and hard, and he said, in the reasonable tone that is the most frightening register of all, "Son, you need to give us the book."

"No," said the stranger, and backed into the door he'd just shut. "No, you don't understand, I need help, I came here because—"

"You came to the *border*," Vetch said. "With that. On this night."

Ada set down her cup.

She told herself, even as she rose, that it wasn't decision yet — just movement, just repositioning, a mercenary's habit of keeping sightlines clear. But she was already crossing the room, weaving through benches, and she heard herself say, in the flat carry she used for settling disputes over tolls, "Vetch. Sit down."

"This isn't your contract, Vane." He didn't look at her. None of them were looking at her. They were all looking at the boy and the book, and their faces had the glassy shine of people no longer entirely present in their own heads. "This is bigger than contracts."

"Everything's smaller than it looks," Ada said. "Sit down."

"He brought a death-date into the valley's shadow. You know what the elders will say. You know what the *church* will say." Now Vetch did turn, and there was something almost pleading in it, which was worse than anger. "You're one of us, Ada. You swore same as the rest."

"I swore an oath to a god who hasn't spoken in a hundred years," she said. "He's in no position to complain about my conduct."

Someone laughed — one sharp bark, immediately smothered, guilty. It broke something in the room's tension, or rather it bent it, and into that bend the stranger slid down the door until he was sitting on the floor, book still locked in his arms, breathing in short ugly pulls.

"It's not mine," he said, to nobody, to everybody. "I found it. My family — we deal in old paper, ledgers, estate records. This came in a crate from a house clearance in Karsthold. I was only reading it. I was only *reading*—"

"You read your own death out of it," said the crying woman, and her voice had gone gentle and horrified, the way people speak to the condemned. "Boy, you read yourself dead."

"I don't even believe in prophecy," he said wildly, and that, somehow, was the worst thing he could have said. Because if the book was false, then it was a lie, and lies at the valley's border on the seventeenth of Deepfrost were witchwork; and if it was true, then death had already picked its night and was standing in the doorway dripping rain. Either way, the room needed someone to do something about it, and the someone it had chosen was him.

Marta came around the counter with a poker from the hearth.

"Marta," Ada said.

"He's marked, Ada. Look at him. Anything that comes out of the mountain lands with a hundred-year book naming tonight — you want that sleeping under your roof? You want it breathing near your children?" Marta's knuckles were white on the iron. "The valley keeps itself clean. That's how it's survived. That's how *we've* survived."

"The valley keeps itself empty," Ada said. "That's different."

And maybe it was the words, or maybe it was only that she had stepped between the poker and the boy, but the room shifted — that terrible fluid hesitation mobs have, the moment when every hand waits to see what the other hands do. Ada put herself fully into the gap, arms crossed, weight settled, and gave them her professional face: the one that said she had ended better-armed arguments than this before breakfast, and had invoiced for it.

"Here's how it is," she said. "Nobody touches him tonight. Tomorrow the church post at Millbrook can send for an elder if anyone still wants one, and the elders can argue scripture over him till spring. But I've escorted cargo through this valley for eleven years, and I'll tell you what I know: bad luck follows cruelty faster than it follows omens. Hurt a terrified man in a tavern full of witnesses and you'll bring down more trouble than any book ever wrote."

"And if the book's true?" whispered the crying woman.

"Then killing him changes nothing except our luck," Ada said, "and makes it worse."

Silence. Rain. The fire popping.

Then Harl Vetch exhaled through his nose, long and disgusted, and the moment passed the way such moments do — not resolved, only released. People returned to their benches in ones and twos, muttering. Marta lowered the poker but kept hold of it. The card game resumed with the strained brightness of people pretending very hard that nothing had happened.

The stranger looked up at Ada from the floor. His face was wrecked — rain, blood, exhaustion — and underneath all of it there was something that startled her: gratitude without performance. He didn't thank her. He just looked at her like a man looking at dry land.

"Up," she said. "You're bleeding on Marta's floor, and she'll turn on you again over that faster than over the book."

* * *

She patched his leg at the corner table, farthest from the hearth and its audience, with the small mercenary's kit she carried for exactly these occasions. He hissed through the needlework and held the candle for her without being asked, angling it when she needed light, and that small competence — the instinct to be useful instead of dramatic — registered with her more than anything else he'd done.

"Corin Ash," he said finally.

"Ada Vane."

"You're a mercenary."

"Escort work, mostly. Cargo and cowards." She tied off the stitch and bit the thread. "Which are you?"

"Neither, apparently." He laughed, but there was no humor in it — only the thin, unhinged relief of a man talking to keep the fear from pooling. "I'm a records clerk. Third generation. Ash & Sons, Provenance and Estate Papers, Karsthold." He stopped. Winced. "Karsthold. Which is where the book came from. Which is probably a coincidence."

"Nothing that walks that far is a coincidence."

"You believe me, then? About the date?"

"I believe you think it's true," Ada said. She cleaned the needle, packed the kit away. "That's usually the dangerous part." She nodded at the book, still hugged against his ribs. "Show me."

His arms tightened around it — reflex, pure animal — and then, deliberately, finger by finger, he made himself loosen his grip and slide it across the table. Ada understood the effort that cost him. She'd seen men part with purses, wives, fingers, with less struggle.

It was a heavy quarto bound in gray leather gone soft with age, its corners worn round, its spine repaired once with thread that had since darkened to the color of dried blood. No title. She opened it carefully, and the pages crackled like frost underfoot.

The hand inside was old — formal, ruled, the script of someone trained to write for institutions rather than readers. Dates ran down the margins in columns, decades of them, each entry brief as a ledger line. Not poetry. Not riddling verses. *Accounts.* That was the first thing that genuinely frightened her, though she wouldn't admit it until later: the book didn't feel like prophecy. It felt like bookkeeping.

She found tonight's page.

*Seventeenth of Deepfrost.* And beside it, in the same confident century-old ink: *Corin Ash — dead.*

Below that, a second line she had to lean close to read, the letters cramped, hurried, as if the writer had been interrupted:

*Borne to the border by his own hand. The valley will refuse him.*

Ada looked up. Corin was watching her read, and his face had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing at the edge of something high.

"There's more," he said quietly. "There's a whole page after. I couldn't — I stopped reading. I couldn't make myself." He pushed the book back toward her, an inch, like a man offering a knife handle-first. "Read it. Please. You seem like the only person in this room who'll tell me the truth about it."

Ada hesitated.

Here was the moment — she saw it clearly, even then — where the smart move was to close the cover, slide it back, finish her cup, and go upstairs to bed. Eleven years of contracts had built her entire life on one rule, learned early and paid for dearly: *know where the job ends.* Take the escort from the farm to the border. Collect the fee. Walk away. Never let a cargo become a cause. The valley had raised her on that rule the way other places raise children on prayers, and it had kept her alive, and it had kept her alone, and she had told herself for a long time now that those were the same thing.

Instead she turned the page.

The entry continued in that cramped, urgent hand:

*The soul of Corin Ash is owed to the court beneath the mountain, sold before his birth, payable when the moon completes its thirteenth turn. Let none stand surety for him. Let none break bread at his right hand. Whosoever walks him out of the valley breaks a hundred years of peace, and the price shall be counted to her also.*

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere across the room, the card players groaned at a bad hand, absurdly, ordinarily, and the sound of it made the words worse.

"Thirteenth turn," Corin said hoarsely. "What does that mean? Moons don't — there isn't a thirteenth turn. There's never been a thirteenth—"

"Once a generation," Ada said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The intercalation. When the lunar calendar slips and they add a thirteenth turn to reset it. Next one is—" She did the arithmetic without wanting to. "Twelve days. Twelve days from tonight."

The color left his face completely. He put out a hand and steadied himself on the table, and the candle guttered in the small wind of his shaking.

"So it's true," he whispered. "Or whoever wrote it knew the calendar. Those are the only two possibilities, aren't they? It's either real, or it's the cruelest joke ever bound in leather, and either way—" He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and desperately, unbearably young. "Either way, in twelve days I'm either dead or I'm not, and everyone in this room already thinks I'm an omen, and I have nowhere to go, because the one direction I *can't* go is home, is Karsthold, is anywhere near the mountain, and the only other direction is—"

He stopped. Followed her gaze to the window, where the rain had slackened and the land beyond the glass lay black and featureless, the deep interior of the Hollow Valley, the place magic could not follow.

"—is in," he finished softly.

"Don't," Ada said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were about to. I watched your face do it." She stood, collected her cup, her kit, her composure, piece by piece, the way she gathered up all her belongings at the end of a job. "Get some sleep. In the morning, Marta will feed you, Vetch will pretend he didn't try to kill you, and I'll be gone. I have a rule, Ash. I don't take jobs without borders. Yours doesn't have one. Yours has a moon."

"Ada." He said her name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "The book says *let none stand surety for him.* It says whoever walks me out of the valley pays too. Why would a book warn people away from helping me unless helping me matters? Unless it *works?*"

She paused at the edge of the candlelight, and hated herself for the pause, because a pause is an answer, and they both knew it.

"Because fear writes warnings," she said. "That's all prophecy is. Somebody's terror, a hundred years downstream."

"Then walk away," he said quietly. "Prove it."

Ada looked at him — this soaked, bleeding, impossible stranger clutching a century of other people's deaths — and felt, with cold clarity, the exact shape of the trap her own life had built around her. Every road out of the valley led to contracts. Every contract ended. She had gotten very good at endings. She could not remember the last time she had started anything.

"Goodnight, Corin Ash," she said, and went upstairs.

She did not sleep. She lay on the narrow bed listening to the rain start and stop, start and stop, and to the house settling, and to the memory of a line of ink repeating itself against the dark of her eyelids: *the price shall be counted to her also.*

Counted to *her.* As if the book had known, a century ago, that she would sit at that table. As if it had been waiting — patient as bookkeeping — for the one person foolish enough to read past the warning.

Near dawn, she dressed, went downstairs, collected her fee from the snoring Dossel's purse with the keeper's witness, and stood in the doorway of the Last Kindness with the gray light coming up over the black pines and the road home lying flat and safe and familiar in front of her.

Corin Ash was asleep in the corner booth, the book open on the table beside him, one hand resting on it even in sleep, guarding it. In the morning light he looked younger and more breakable, and the bruised shadows under his eyes looked like the beginning of a death she had read in another man's handwriting.

Ada stood in the doorway a long time.

Then she swore — quietly, fluently, in three dialects — walked back inside, sat down across from him, and waited for him to wake.

When his eyes opened and found hers, confusion first, then disbelief, then something dangerously close to hope, she said:

"One condition. Before anything else — before food, before plans, before you say another word about moons — I read that book. All of it. Every page. If we're doing this, I'm not walking blind into somebody else's debt."

Corin sat up slowly, pushing hair out of his eyes, and slid the gray-leather quarto across the table to her with both hands, like a man laying down his last coin.

"There's something else," he said. "Page forty. I read it at midnight and I've been trying to forget it ever since. It's not about me."

Ada opened the book.

And there, on page forty, in the same confident century-old hand, was a name she had not heard spoken aloud since she was nine years old.

Her own.
