## 3. The Oath That Went Silent

The elders came at dusk, as they always did when they wanted something from her.

Ada heard them before she saw them — the particular rhythm of church boots on the tavern's packed-earth floor, three sets, unhurried. Men who had never once needed to hurry in their lives. She was sitting with her back to the wall, as she always sat, a bowl of barley stew cooling between her hands, and Corin Ash was across the table doing something unforgivable: talking. Quietly, quickly, his hands sketching shapes over the prophecy book he kept wrapped in oilcloth like a wounded bird.

"—and here, the marginalia, look at the ink, it's not faded the way the rest is, someone *added* this—"

"Stop talking," Ada said.

He stopped. He'd been stopping when she told him to for two days now, which was more obedience than she'd gotten out of any horse she'd ever broken, and she found she disliked how easily she'd come to rely on it.

The elders reached their table. There were three of them: Elder Maren, whose face was all soft folds and hard eyes; Elder Tullow, tall as a doorframe, with the gray stubbled scalp of a man who'd taken the tonsure late; and behind them, young, nervous, clutching a lantern that guttered in the tavern's still air, a novice Ada didn't recognize. The whole room had gone quiet the way rooms did when the church walked in — not reverent quiet, but the held breath of people hoping to be overlooked.

"Sister Vane." Elder Maren used the title like a hook. It had been years since anyone in the valley called Ada *Sister* anything; she'd surrendered the white sash at seventeen and taken up mercenary work at eighteen, and the church had pretended since then that the one thing hadn't followed the other. "You have cargo."

"I have stew," Ada said.

Maren's gaze slid past her to Corin, and the temperature of the room changed. It was a skill Ada had seen in magistrates and slavers both — the ability to make a person into an object simply by looking at them correctly. "A stranger crossed the border two nights ago," Maren said. "Carrying a text. The border wardens describe it. A book bound in black calfskin, brass corners, older than any press in the valley. Pages that name dates not yet lived." Her voice never rose. That was the trick of it. "You are harboring heretical material, Sister Vane. And the man attached to it."

"He's a merchant's son," Ada said. "Lost on the road. I've been hired to see him safe to the eastern pass."

"Hired by whom?"

Ada ate a spoonful of stew. Let the silence do its work. Maren had taught her that trick too, back when Ada was nine years old and memorizing catechisms in the chalk-smell cold of the sanctuary undercrofts — *silence is the church's first weapon, child; men fill silence with confessions.* But Ada had learned to live inside silence the way fish lived in water, and Maren's own weapon hung in the air between them, unused, until the elder's thin mouth tightened.

"You swore," Maren said quietly. "At eight years old, in the sanctuary at Hollow's Rest, you knelt on the stone and gave your voice to the Vessel-Who-Waits. Do you remember what you promised?"

Ada remembered everything.

That was the problem. That was always the problem.

* * *

She had been eight, and small for it, all elbows and scabbed knees, an orphan of the fever-winters with no family name worth speaking. The priests had lined the children up in the sanctuary at Hollow's Rest — forty of them that year, barefoot on stone floors worn smooth by a century of other bare feet — and told them that today they would be given a purpose.

The sanctuary had no windows. That was doctrine. *The Vessel-Who-Waits does not require light, because He sees inward, and inward is where the true world lives.* Instead there were lamps, hundreds of them, floating. Real floating — that was the valley's one permitted marvel, the only miracle the Hollow's magicless air allowed to survive, because it wasn't magic at all, the elders said, but *grace*. Little flames in glass globes drifting at head height through the dark like patient golden fish. As a child Ada had believed they were souls. Waiting ones. She'd believed it for years after she should have known better, because it was a beautiful thing to believe and she'd been given very little else.

Elder Ostrevan had been young then. Not young-young — thirty perhaps, with the first silver coming into his black hair — but younger than the man she'd see later, far later, across a table in a city beneath a mountain. He'd worn the same robes though, deep blue shot with thread so fine it moved like smoke, and he'd knelt down to her level, which none of the other elders ever did, and looked at her with eyes the color of wet slate.

"What is your name, child?"

"Ada." No family. Everyone knew it. The word fell flat every time.

"Just Ada." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, or she'd thought so for twenty years, and maybe it even had been kind once, before kindness got in the way of whatever he wanted. "Do you know what the Vessel waits for, Ada?"

She'd recited it. Every child did. "*He waits for the debt unpaid. He waits for the promise broken. He holds the scales beneath the mountain, and nothing given to Him is ever lost.*"

"And would you like to be His? To carry His mark, and hear His voice, and know that somewhere under the earth, something vast and patient knows your name — *your* name, just Ada, and loves it?"

Eight years old. Barefoot on holy stone. A hundred floating lamps turning their warm faces toward her like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there.

She'd said yes. They all said yes. Saying no wasn't a thing the sanctuary taught.

They'd pressed her wrist to the altar-stone — cold, colder than stone should be, cold like the bottom of a well — and the oath-mark had risen up through her skin in lines of pale silver, a circle broken by a single vertical stroke, the sigil of the scales waiting to be filled. And then —

And then the voice.

People outside the valley never believed her about the voice. They thought the god's silence meant he'd never spoken at all, that the faithful of the Hollow had spent a century kneeling to empty air and calling it devotion. But he had spoken. He spoke to every marked child on the day of their swearing, one sentence, one only, delivered straight into the marrow rather than the ear:

*I will hold what you give me, and give back what is owed.*

It wasn't thunderous. It wasn't kind, exactly, either. It was the voice of a scale balancing — precise, total, indifferent to anything except weight. Ada had wept anyway. Forty children wept that day, every one of them, without knowing why, and the elders called it joy.

For eleven years she heard him. Not often. Not words, mostly — more like weather, a pressure behind the sternum when a contract was honored, a cold drag in the blood when one broke. When she took her first mercenary contract at fourteen — guarding a wool-cart, two coppers a day, thrillingly serious — she felt his approval settle over her like a hand: *a promise made is a promise held.* When the caravan master tried to cheat her of the second copper and she stood in front of his wagon with a knife until he paid, she felt the cold drag of disapproval pass — and then, oddly, the warmth return, as if the god weighed the cheating against the payment and found the scales level again. Justice, not obedience. That was what she'd understood, at fourteen, without knowing she understood it: the Vessel-Who-Waits didn't care whether she knelt. He cared whether the weights were true.

Then, on the third night of the harvest month, in her nineteenth year, the warmth stopped.

No warning. She'd been on the border road, mid-contract, and she'd woken in the dark of a waystation loft with her wrist burning and a silence in her chest so absolute it had sound — a ringing, a vast negative space where the pressure had always been, like standing in a house and hearing the walls fall away. She'd run to the sanctuary at dawn. She'd knelt on the altar-cold stone and pressed her mark down and begged.

Nothing. Nothing, and nothing, and nothing, for thirteen years since.

The church had a word for it: *the Quiet Devotion.* The god had withdrawn, the elders proclaimed, because the faith of the valley had grown so perfect he was no longer needed. Silence was the highest praise. They rang the bells differently now, softer. They'd put out most of the floating lamps.

Ada had stopped going to services the year she turned twenty-two. She'd never surrendered the mark. She told herself it was because removing it required surgery she couldn't afford, which was true, and because a marked mercenary commanded better rates among the superstitious, which was also true. Both truths were smaller than the real one, which was that she could not bear to cut out the last place he had ever touched her, even if the touch had gone cold.

Even if she sometimes, in the worst hours before dawn, lying alone in hired rooms across the valley, pressed her wrist to her mouth and whispered into the silence: *did I break it? Was it me? What did I give you that you stopped wanting it?*

* * *

"Do you remember what you promised?" Maren asked again, in the present, in the tavern that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Ada set down her spoon.

"I remember everything, Elder." She let the mark show — turned her wrist over on the table, pale silver circle-and-stroke against the lamplight. Maren's eyes flicked to it and away, quick as a tongue touching a sore tooth. "I remember the vow of holding. *What is given to the Vessel is never lost.* So tell me —" she nodded toward Corin, who had gone very still beside his wrapped book, "— what has this man been given? By whom? Show me the writ. Show me the charge."

"The charge is heresy," Tullow said. His voice was a cellar-damp baritone. "The text names a date. Tonight's date. It names a death. These things are not permitted in the Hollow."

"They're not permitted anywhere," Ada said. "That's what makes them prophecy, isn't it? Prophecy doesn't ask permission."

"Careful, Sister."

"I'm not a sister."

"You wear the mark."

"I wear scars too. You don't get to claim those either." She heard her own voice go flat and cold, the mercenary register, the one she used when the negotiation stopped being a negotiation. Around the tavern, patrons were finding reasons to study their drinks. "Here's what happens next. My contract stands until the eastern pass. I don't break contracts. That's not stubbornness, Elder — that's doctrine. Your own doctrine. A promise made is a promise held, and the Vessel weighs it. Or did he stop weighing that too?"

Maren went very still. Something moved behind her folded face — not anger. Calculation. And beneath the calculation, briefly, something that looked almost like fear, and that frightened Ada more than anger would have, because she'd spent thirteen years telling herself the elders' serenity was faith, and suddenly it looked like a mask worn by people who knew precisely what they were hiding.

"The stranger comes with us tonight," Maren said softly. "The text comes with him. You will be compensated for the broken contract — triple rate, from church coffers. You will remain in the valley, Sister Vane. You will forget his face." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Or you will leave the Hollow with him. And you should understand what that means. Outside the valley, the mark wakes. Outside the hollow of the world, the old powers still breathe — and your god's silence may not follow you the way his silence follows you here." Her soft eyes settled on Ada's wrist. "We would hate for you to learn, at your age, what you actually serve."

The lantern in the novice's hands guttered. Somewhere in the tavern's rafters a beam ticked, settling, like the building itself leaning in to listen.

Corin moved. Just slightly — his hand came to rest on the oilcloth bundle, and Ada saw his knuckles whiten around it, saw the exact moment a man who had spent his life counting coin decided that some ledgers could not be balanced, only defended. He didn't speak. He looked at her instead. Two days she'd known him, and already he'd learned her language: the question in his eyes wasn't *save me*, it was *what will this cost you?*

No one had asked her that in thirteen years. Clients asked what she charged. The church asked what she owed. Corin Ash, a stranger with tomorrow's date written beside his name, was the first person in thirteen years to wonder what it cost *her* — and the terrible thing, the thing that made the crack open in her chest like ice shifting on a winter pond, was that she didn't have an answer. She'd never priced herself. She'd only priced her work.

Duty said: give him to them. Take the triple rate. Stay inside the valley where the air was clean of magic and the silence of her god was at least a familiar silence, a silence she'd furnished and lived in, a silence that was hers.

Conscience said nothing at all. Conscience just looked at the boy's white knuckles, and waited.

"I'll walk you to the door, Elders," Ada said, rising.

Maren's smile was thin as paper over a wound. "You'll reconsider by morning."

"No," Ada said. "I really won't. But you can hope. Hope's free — even in the Hollow."

They left. The door closed on the wind and the smell of coming frost, and the tavern exhaled, and Corin unwrapped a corner of the oilcloth with shaking fingers as if checking the book still breathed.

"They'll come back with more than words," he said.

"Yes."

"How long do we have?"

Ada looked at the window, where the moon was rising over the eastern rim — one night shy of full, fat and white and patient, beginning its slow count toward something.

"One night less than we need," she said, and began packing.
