## 4. Thirteen Turns

The book would not let him sleep.

Corin Ash had tried everything a sensible man tried: he had put it under his mattress, and felt its weight through the straw like a sleeping animal; he had set it on the far side of the room beneath his folded shirts, and heard the pages turn at some hour past midnight with no hand near it; finally he had wedged it shut with the iron buckle of his traveling belt, and the buckle had been warm in the morning — not hot, nothing so dramatic as that, just warm the way a hand is warm when it has been holding yours too long.

So on the third night he gave in. He took the book to the table by the window of his rented room above the Drowned Lantern tavern, lit the stub of tallow candle he'd been rationing like a miser, and opened it to the place where the pages wanted to go.

Outside, the Hollow Valley lay under a sky scrubbed clean of anything but stars. No moon yet — the moon would not rise for another hour, and when it did it would be thin as a paring of bone. That was one of the strangest mercies of this place, he was learning. The valley kept things out. He had crossed its border six days ago half-dead from fear, and the moment his boots touched the pale road beyond the boundary stones, something in his chest had gone quiet — a sound he hadn't known he was hearing until it stopped. Like tinnitus of the soul. In the valley there were no whispers, no dreams that weren't only dreams, no sense of being *counted*.

He should have felt safe here.

He turned the page.

* * *

The book was called, according to the cracked leather of its spine, simply *the Almanac of Turns*, though whoever had bound it a century ago had been either a madman or a poet, because an almanac was supposed to tell you when to plant barley and this thing told you when the world would end in small, specific, personal installments. The script was dense and brown-black, ink gone the color of dried blood, and it moved — he had confirmed this twice now, sober, in daylight, with Ada Vane standing over his shoulder scowling — it moved the way water moves under ice. Not quickly. Not obviously. But if you looked away and looked back, the words had shifted their order, the way a crowd shifts.

Tonight the page read:

> *In the four-and-twentieth year of the Ash line's exile-from-grace, when the moon completes her thirteenth turn upon the winter fields, the debt comes due. The court beneath the mountain will send for what was promised. The boy will be a man by then, and will not know his own name has been spent.*

Beneath it, in a different hand — newer, sharper, written as if with a stylus pressed hard enough to scar three pages deep:

> *CORIN ASH.*

And beside his name, in letters that seemed almost gentle in their smallness:

> *dead.*

He had stared at those five letters for three nights running, waiting for them to make sense, waiting for them to stop making sense. Tonight they did neither. Tonight something else happened instead, because the book, having held him at the edge of the cliff long enough, finally pushed.

The next page turned itself under his fingers before he chose to turn it.

There was a drawing. That was new. It showed a mountain — Karsthold, he knew the shape of it even rendered in a dead man's crosshatching, the twin horns of it, the city clinging to its knee like a burr — and beneath the mountain, in the space where the roots should have been, a great hall drawn in section, like a butcher's diagram of a body. Rows and rows of niches lined the walls, and in each niche sat a small flame, and each flame had a face.

He understood, with the calm that arrives sometimes at the very center of terror — the still eye of it — that the faces were souls. That the hall was full of them. That there were hundreds of niches, and the artist had grown tired or honest and left most of the lower galleries blank, which was worse, somehow, than drawing them all.

Below the drawing, the brown-black script again:

> *The court buys what men are foolish enough to sell and wise enough to regret: years, names, memories, the warmth of old loves, the strength of sons unborn. Payment is taken in kind. A soul sold whole is held in escrow until the term completes. The court does not chase its debts. The court waits. The mountain is patient because the mountain is paid.*

Corin read it three times. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he would have expected shaking, and there was none, only a strange cold clarity, the feeling of standing outside himself and watching a man at a table read his own obituary by tallow light.

Then he found the contract.

It was transcribed into the almanac the way courts transcribe things — in that terrible beautiful legal hand designed to make horror look like housekeeping. The language was old-fashioned, formal, full of *heretofore* and *in perpetuity*, and he made himself read every word of it, because somewhere in the back of his mind a merchant's son was already doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *terms, conditions, loopholes, exits.*

> *Let it be known and entered into the ledger of the court beneath Karsthold, on this day of the waning moon, that Aldous Ash, factor and freeholder of the Ash trading concern, being pressed by circumstance and creditors both, sells unto the court one (1) soul entire, being the soul of his issue and issue's issue unto the fourth generation, specifically the child yet unbegotten of his son Tomas Ash, in exchange for the sum of two hundred crowns gold, remitted forthwith, and the forgiveness of all further obligations.*

Two hundred crowns. His family's founding fortune — the number every Ash child learned along with their letters, the number carved into the lintel of the counting-house in Marrowford: *EST. UPON 200 C., GOD PROVIDING.* God providing. His grandfather used to say it as a joke. *God provided,* he'd say, tapping the lintel, *and see how He provides.*

A priest had witnessed it. That was the detail that finally made his stomach turn over. There, at the bottom of the transcription, in the list of seals and signatures, was a name and title he recognized from a hundred sermons in a hundred market towns where the church of the silent god collected its quiet tithes: *Witnessed and sealed under the rite of the Vessel-Who-Waits, by the hand of Brother M—* and then the ink had been scraped away, deliberately, carefully, by someone long ago who had wanted that name forgotten.

The god who had gone silent a hundred years ago. The god whose church had witnessed the sale of his soul before his grandfather's grandfather had even conceived the father who would conceive him.

Corin sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere below him the tavern noise rose and fell — laughter, the clink of crockery, a fiddle tuning up and thinking better of it. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where a man's soul was not a line item.

*Twelve nights.*

He counted on his fingers like a child, and hated himself for it, and did it anyway. Tonight was the third of the month by the valley reckoning. The almanac named the date of collection plainly, in a marginal note that had appeared — he was nearly sure it had not been there yesterday — beside the drawing of the mountain: *collection falls upon the thirteenth turn.* Thirteen nights from the first. Twelve remaining. The moon would rise in an hour, thin and white and beginning its slow walk toward completion, and every night she grew fuller she would be walking him toward the door of that hall beneath the mountain, where a niche waited with a small flame in it, and the flame had his face.

He got up. He walked to the window. He pressed his forehead against the cold glass and watched the stars wheel over the valley and thought, absurdly, about inventory.

That was the Ash inheritance, the one thing besides the debt they'd passed down without knowing it: when the numbers turned against you, you took stock. His father had done it the year the Marrowford floods ate the warehouse — stood hip-deep in the wreck of everything and counted aloud what remained, because a man could drown in loss or he could itemize it. So Corin itemized.

One life, twenty-four years worn, value unknown. One prophecy book, hostile property, apparently sentient. One mercenary, Ada Vane, whom he owed — what? She had pulled him out of the Lantern's common room three nights ago with a mob howling omen behind them, had put herself between him and a thrown bottle, had said *you're my problem now, and I hate problems* in a voice flat as slate, and had not once since asked him why the book bore his name. Not once. He had watched her not ask. It was the loudest kindness anyone had ever done him.

One soul, sold before his birth, held in escrow, payable in twelve nights.

His breath fogged the glass. In the fog he saw, briefly, the shape of the hall beneath the mountain, the rows of flames, the patience of it. *The court does not chase its debts. The court waits.* And it had waited a hundred years, through his great-grandfather's death and his grandfather's and the slow salt-bleached decline of the Ash fortunes, waiting for the child the contract named to grow old enough, ripe enough —

No. Not ripe. *Ready.* Ready to be picked.

He thought about running. Of course he thought about running. He was a merchant's son; flight was the first instrument in the case. Run south past the valley, run to the coast, take ship, change his name — but the book had his name, and names were exactly the sort of thing the court traded in, and besides, the valley's border stones had hummed faintly under his palm the day he entered, and Ada had said, when he asked, *magic can't follow you in here, but it remembers you at the door.* The court would remember him at the door. The court had been remembering him since before he existed.

Running was a payment plan with worse interest.

So. Itemize. What remained?

He went back to the table. He sat down. He pulled the almanac close, and for the first time since the Drowned Lantern, since the mob, since Ada's blade flashing between him and the crowd — for the first time he did not read it like a victim reading a verdict. He read it like an auditor.

The contract, he saw now, was sloppy. Oh, it wore the costume of precision, all its *hereinafters* and *perpetuities*, but underneath the costume it was a desperate man's bargain: two hundred crowns for a soul entire, witnessed by a churchman whose name someone had later scraped away as if ashamed of it. Desperate sellers wrote bad paper. And bad paper — his father's ghost murmured in his ear, in the fond exasperated tone reserved for teaching — bad paper can be challenged. Challenged on consideration. On capacity. On *consent*. Whose consent had been given? Aldous Ash's. But the soul was not Aldous Ash's. You cannot sell what you do not own. Every court in the world, mortal or otherwise, rested on that stone.

Unless the court beneath the mountain didn't care about stones. Unless law down there was only appetite wearing robes.

But the book had shown him the niches, and the niches had shown him the faces, and the faces had been orderly. Order meant rules. Rules meant seams. Seams meant him.

Corin Ash laughed — one short, cracked syllable that startled him more than the moving ink had — and the sound in the little room was the sound of a man deciding not to die quietly.

He found a fresh sheet of paper, the good rag stock he'd bought in Marrowford for invoices, and sharpened his pencil with his belt knife, and at the top of the sheet he wrote, in the neat merchant's hand that had won his father's approval exactly once: *ASH v. THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN. Assets:* and then he stopped, because the candle had gone out.

Not guttered. Gone out. Snuffed, cleanly, as if pinched by wet fingers, though the window was shut and there was no draft, and in the dark the almanac's pages whispered — many pages, turning fast, a sound like wind through wheat, like a crowd murmuring, like —

Like counting.

He did not light the candle. His hands were shaking now; the steadiness had lasted precisely as long as it was needed, and he was almost grateful to it. He sat in the dark and listened to the book count, and he understood that it was counting the same thing he had counted on his fingers, and that his twelve nights were also its twelve nights, and that it was pleased.

"Twelve," he said aloud, to the dark, to the book, to the moon climbing the eastern ridge with her bone-thin light. "You get twelve."

The whispering stopped. In the silence that followed he could feel it listening — the whole patient apparatus of it, the hall beneath the mountain, the niches, the flame with his face, all of it leaning in across a hundred miles of dark country to hear what the merchandise would say.

"I'm not paying," Corin Ash told it. "I'm renegotiating."

Nothing answered. Nothing had to. But when the candle, relit from the ember he'd kept alive in the hearth, finally threw its light back onto the table, the almanac lay closed — he had left it open — and on its cover, in the sharp scar-deep hand that was not the almanac's original scribe, three new words had been burned into the leather while he sat in the dark:

*COME AND SEE.*

Corin looked at them for a long time. Then he did the bravest and most foolish thing of his twenty-four years, braver than crossing the valley border with a mob at his back: he reached out and laid his palm flat on the cover of the book, on the burned words, and held it there, the way you hold a hand you're about to sign something with.

"All right," he said. "I'm coming."

Downstairs, the tavern noise swelled and faded and swelled. The moon cleared the ridge and laid a white road across the valley floor, straight as a ledger line, pointing east toward the mountains. And in the room above the Drowned Lantern, a man who had come here to die began, by candlelight, on good rag paper, to draw up terms.

* * *

He was still writing when the knocking came — three blows, unhurried, spaced like a heartbeat — and Ada Vane's voice through the door, low and flat as slate: "Ash. Open up. We've got company on the road, and your book is glowing through the floorboards, and I want to know why before I decide whether I save your life again."

Corin looked at the page in front of him — *Assets: 1 mercenary (disputed ownership), 1 soul (contested title), 12 nights (non-refundable)* — and then at the door, and understood that whatever he said next would spend something he could never buy back.

"The book knows how I die," he called back, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he had any right to expect. "It's got a date. And Vane — it's twelve nights from tonight."

Silence, for three heartbeats. Then, through the wood, in that flat voice that had already decided and was only informing him: "Get your pack."
