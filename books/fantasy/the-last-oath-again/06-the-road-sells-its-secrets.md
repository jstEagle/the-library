## 6. The Road Sells Its Secrets

*Ada*

The Hollow Valley let them go the way a miser lets go of coin: slowly, grudgingly, with every intention of taking it back.

For three days they walked the old drover's road east, and Ada kept waiting for the world to feel different. It didn't. The hills beyond the valley were the same gray-green as the hills within it. The wind smelled the same — wet stone, sheep fat, woodsmoke from shepherd fires. She had spent her whole life believing that outside the Hollow lay a country of wonders, because the elders said so every time they warned her against leaving. What she found instead was mud.

Mud, and Corin Ash talking.

He talked when he was nervous, she had learned. He talked when he was grateful. He talked most of all when he thought she was about to say something, filling the silence like a man shoring up a levee against a flood. By noon of the first day she knew his favorite childhood meal (his grandmother's onion pie), his worst scar (a kettle of boiling tallow, age seven), and his opinion on at least four varieties of cheese. She had told him nothing in return except *walk faster* and *don't touch my knives*, and he had accepted this arrangement with the resigned good humor of a man who had clearly grown up with older sisters.

The road bent north along a river whose name changed twice in ten miles — the Vell, then the Vellwater, then, according to a milestone so ancient its letters had been eaten by lichen, something that might have been *Vel*. They camped where a stone bridge had collapsed into the current, its arches broken like snapped ribs, and Ada built a fire small enough to hide under her coat if travelers passed.

It was there, over a supper of hard bread and river trout, that Corin finally stopped talking and started asking.

"You never said what you did," he said. "Before me. Before the book."

"Contracts."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer." She fed the fire a twig. "Someone wants something carried, or someone wants something stopped, or someone wants to know who took what from whom. I do it. They pay. There's no more story than that."

"There's always more story." He turned his fish on its stick with a delicacy that struck her as absurd — a man with eleven days left to live, fussing over a trout. "My family traded in cloth. Silk, mostly, before the war made silk expensive. And underneath the silk—" he paused, and for the first time since she'd met him, he seemed to choose silence rather than flee from it, "—underneath the silk we traded in other things. Debts. Names. I didn't know until I read the book. You don't tell a child he's collateral."

The fire popped. Downriver, something large slid into the water without a splash.

"Collateral," Ada repeated.

"Great-grandfather Aldous Ash. The book has his signature in it. A contract." His laugh was thin and wrong. "I used to think the family motto was just decoration. *Semper Solvendum.* Always payable. My father hung it over the counting house door in gilded letters. I thought it meant we paid our debts promptly. Honest merchants." He looked at his hands. "It meant we were always owed *to* someone. Or owned *by* someone. Depending which end of the ledger you stood on."

Ada said nothing. In the Hollow Valley you learned early that silence was a kind of courtesy. People filled silences with their own confessions; she'd watched it happen a hundred times around escort-fires, strangers telling her their marriages and their crimes because she was the one person who would carry it all across the valley and leave it there. She was, she realized with a small cold surprise, doing it now. Being the hollow place. Letting him pour.

"Tell me about your contracts," Corin said. "A real one. The one you're proudest of."

"I'm not proud of any of them."

"Then the one you hate least."

She should have refused. Instead she heard herself say: "There was a widow in Coldfern. Her husband died with debts to a grain-factor named Hobb, and Hobb held the deed to her house. Legal. Sealed. Witnessed." The words came out flat, rehearsed, the way she recited anything she didn't want to feel. "She hired me to get the deed back. I told her I don't steal. So I got Hobb drunk instead, and I got him to sign a new paper — a loan forgiven, witnessed by his own clerk, who hated him. Perfectly legal. Perfectly sealed. The law ate him alive when he tried to contest it."

Corin was staring at her. "That's brilliant."

"It's theft with extra steps."

"No. It's—" he gestured vaguely at the fire, the dark, the broken bridge, "—that's what merchants do. That's what *my family* did. We just called it trade." Something moved through his face, grief and recognition tangled together. "You'd have fit right in at our counting house."

"No," Ada said. "I wouldn't have." But she found, examining the feeling afterward the way you probe a bruise, that it wasn't entirely true, and that frightened her more than church hunters did.

They sat a while. The river ran. Somewhere upstream a nightbird called three notes and waited, and called again.

"My mother sold thread," Ada said, before she could stop herself. "Plain wool thread. Gray. She dyed it herself and it always came out slightly wrong — too green, too blue. Nobody wanted it." She poked the fire. "I swore my oath to the Vessel-Who-Waits when I was nine. Every child does. Hand on the stone, voice in the dark, *I will hold what must be held.* And for six years I could feel him. Not hear him, exactly. More like — warmth on the back of the neck. Like being watched by something kind." She stopped.

"And then?"

"And then one morning the warmth was gone. Just gone. Like walking into a room and finding the hearth cold though no one opened the door." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "The elders said he was testing us. The elders said a great many things. I stopped listening the day I realized they sounded relieved."

Corin was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You broke your rule for me. Leaving the valley. Why?"

Because the book named tonight's date and your death, and I've spent my whole life holding things that must be held, and you are the first thing that ever asked to be held rather than carried. She said none of it. "The mob was going to kill you."

"People get killed in front of me all the time. You said so yourself. Contracts."

"Yes," Ada said, and lay down facing away from him. "Well."

* * *

*Corin*

On the fourth day the road climbed out of the river country into moorland, and the world became wind and heather and standing stones worn faceless by centuries of weather. Corin had never seen land so empty. In the valley of his childhood, even fallow fields had been *someone's* fallow fields; here the earth belonged to itself, and he found the freedom of it intoxicating and terrifying in equal measure.

Ada walked ahead, as she always did, moving with that economy of motion that made him think of water finding its level. She had said perhaps forty words since dawn. He was learning to read her silences the way he'd once learned to read ledgers — the terse entries, the margins full of meaning.

"The stones," he said, catching up to her. "Are they significant?"

"They're old."

"That's not an answer."

"You keep saying that to me." But there was something at the corner of her mouth that might, in better light, among kinder company, have been the beginning of a smile. "Pilgrims raised them. Back when the Vessel still answered. Each one a promise somebody made and couldn't keep."

"What happened to the promises?"

"Same thing that happens to all of them." She touched one stone as she passed, briefly, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "They weathered."

They crested a rise near midday, and Corin stopped.

Below them, in a fold of the moor, stood a waystation — a long low building of dark timber with a slate roof, a stable, a well. Smoke rose from its chimney. A wagon stood outside, loaded and covered with oilcloth. Ordinary. Utterly ordinary. After four days of empty moor, it looked like salvation.

"Waystations serve anyone," Ada said, but her hand had drifted to her knife, and her eyes were moving along the treeline in a way that made Corin's stomach drop. "Food. Water. Beds. No questions."

"You don't trust it."

"I don't trust smoke that rises straight." She nodded at the chimney. "No wind down there. But the heather on the slope is bending east. Air moves everywhere on a moor except, apparently, inside that chimney." She started downslope, angling wide. "We go around. Quietly."

They were halfway across the fold when the heather stood up.

Not all of it — a crescent of it, twenty yards ahead, six shapes rising out of the ground itself, and Corin understood with a lurch that they had been lying under woven heather screens since before dawn. Chainmail glinted beneath travel cloaks. And on the breast of the foremost figure, worked in white thread on black: a closed vessel, waiting.

"Church," Ada breathed. "Down!"

The bolt came out of nowhere — a flat crack, and the wagon by the waystation exploded into splinters. Not a wagon. A ballista, manned, its crew hidden the whole time. The ambush hadn't been for travelers. It had been a net drawn tight around a fold of moor, and they had walked into the middle of it.

"*Run!*"

Ada grabbed his collar and hauled him sideways behind a standing stone as a second bolt shrieked past and detonated against the heather in a spray of peat. Corin's ears rang. His hands were shaking. Six hunters fanning out in a crescent, a ballista reloading with a sound like a giant winding a watch, and between them and escape, two hundred yards of open ground.

"We can't outrun bolts," he gasped.

"No." Ada was pulling knives. Three of them, checking each blade with a flick of her wrist, and her face had gone somewhere else entirely — calm, distant, professional, and somehow that was the most frightening thing he'd ever seen. "So we don't run. Listen to me. The two on the left flank are yours to worry about. Not fight — *worry*. Make noise, throw rocks, be a merchant. Can you be a merchant?"

"I've been one my whole life."

"Good. Be a loud, stupid, panicking one." She pressed one knife into his hand — hilt-first, an odd tenderness in it. "Don't use it unless you mean it. A knife you hesitate with is a knife they take from you."

And then she was gone, low and fast across the heather like a fox running a hedgerow, and Corin Ash, soul-debtor, silk-merchant's son, man whose name appeared beside the word *dead* in a century-old book, stood alone behind a weathered stone with a knife in his fist and two churchmen closing in.

*Worry them,* she'd said.

He picked up a rock and threw it as hard as he could, off to the left, into the deep heather. It landed with a crash. One of the flanking hunters turned toward the sound — and Corin, seized by an inspiration that felt entirely like terror, began to sing. Loudly. Badly. A counting-house ballad about the virtues of diversified holdings, the one his father used to bellow at year-end inventories, complete with the verse about wool futures.

The second hunter turned too. Both of them squinting toward the noise, crossbows half-raised, faces wearing the exquisite confusion of men whose doctrine had not prepared them for this.

Which is when Ada hit them.

She came out of the heather like the moor itself rising — one hunter dropped with a knife-hand strike to the throat before he finished turning, the other caught her across the forearm with a crossbow stock and she took the blow on bone and drove her knee into his thigh and put him down gasping. Two heartbeats. Maybe three. Corin had watched butchers work more slowly.

But the ballista crew had seen her now. The big one — a sergeant with a voice like a landslide — roared orders, and three remaining hunters converged on her position, and Corin did the arithmetic every merchant learns: assets committed, liabilities converging, and the only unspent capital on the field was himself.

He ran.

Not away. Across. Straight through the open ground toward the ballista, screaming his father's inventory song at the top of his lungs, waving the knife, waving both arms, being precisely the loud stupid panicking thing she'd asked him to be. The ballista crew swiveled toward him — hesitated, because shooting an unarmed madman singing about wool futures was the sort of thing that ended careers even in the church — and in that hesitation Ada crossed the distance like a debt coming due.

What followed took less time than Corin could properly remember afterward. There was a moment where the sergeant's sword came down and Ada wasn't where swords go, and a moment where her elbow found the ballista loader's jaw, and a moment where the last hunter standing looked at his fallen comrades, looked at Ada Vane standing over them breathing like a bellows, and made what Corin considered the first sensible decision anyone had made all day.

He fled east toward the waystation. Toward reinforcements, probably. Toward more of whatever this was.

"Can't chase him," Ada rasped, hauling Corin up by the arm. Her sleeve was torn and dark beneath, blood sliding slow down her forearm. "Can't stay. North. Now."

They ran north into the trackless heather, up and over, until the waystation was a smudge of smoke behind them and the moor swallowed them whole. Only when they'd put a mile of nothing between themselves and the ambush did Ada stop, brace against a stone, and let herself shake.

"Your arm," Corin said.

"Bolt graze. First blood of the day belongs to the moor." She sat down heavily, tore her sleeve back, and began cleaning the cut with water from her flask. "You sang."

"It's a good song."

"It's about *wool futures*."

"Diversification is a timeless theme." He knelt beside her, took the flask, and without asking began binding her arm with strips of his own shirt, the way he'd bound crates a thousand times — firm, even pressure, knots that held. She watched him work with an expression he couldn't parse. "You told me to be a merchant. Merchants improvise."

"I told you to throw rocks."

"The rocks were phase one."

Her breath came out almost — almost — like a laugh. She looked at the bandage, at his hands steadying hers, and something in her face unclenched by a single degree. "You could have run west. Alone. They weren't hunting you specifically until today. Now they are."

"Now they are," Corin agreed. "So we'd best be worth hunting together."

The wind moved the heather in long silver waves. Far behind them, faint as memory, a horn sounded from the waystation — once, twice, calling in the hunt. Ada's head came up like a deer's.

"They'll signal ahead. Every parish between here and Karsthold will know our faces by tomorrow." She rose, testing her bandaged arm, and looked north toward a horizon where, very far away, a mountain sat under clouds like a crown under a cloth. "We walk nights now. Sleep days. And Corin—"

"Yes?"

"When we reach the city, stay close. In Karsthold, everything is for sale." Her eyes went to the prophecy book's shape beneath his coat. "Including, apparently, souls. Including ours."

She turned north, and Corin followed, and behind them the horn sounded a third time — longer now, and lower, a note that rolled across the moor like the beginning of something being spoken aloud.

Somewhere beneath that distant mountain, he could not help thinking, something had just heard its name.
