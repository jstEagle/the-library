## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain announced itself three days before we saw it.

We felt it first in Corin's book. The prophecy pages began to warm against his chest like a sleeping animal, and by the second morning he carried the thing outside his coat, balanced on both palms as if it might bite him. By the third day it was humming. Not sound, exactly — more a vibration in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, the way thunder feels before it has a name.

"It knows where it's going," Corin said, and I did not answer, because I had felt the same thing in my wrist since we crossed the valley border.

Karsthold did not rise out of the earth so much as it was swallowed upward by it. The mountain was black stone veined with something pale and slow-moving, like light trapped in ice, and the city clung to its flank in terraces of grey slate — ten thousand roofs stacked like scales, each one darker than the one below, descending toward a mouth in the rock at the mountain's root that no torch ever seemed to reach the bottom of. Even from the last ridge of the pass, with evening coming down purple and cold, I could see that the city had no walls.

"You don't wall a place like this," I told Corin. "You wall things *out*. Nothing here wants out."

"That's reassuring."

"I'm not paid to be reassuring." A pause. "I'm not paid at all anymore, actually. You should remember that when you decide what we're walking into."

He looked at me then — that look he'd been perfecting on the road, the one that pretended to be wounded but wasn't — and said nothing, which was his way of saying thank you without owing anything. We had gotten good at that, in six days. Good at not owing.

* * *

We came down through the upper terraces as full dark fell, and that was when I understood why the church feared this place, and why the church, I suspected, needed it.

Torchlight everywhere. That was the first thing. Karsthold burned like a festival every night, whole forests' worth of pitch-pine going up in iron braziers along every stair and landing, because the sun never properly reached the lower galleries and the people below had made their own daylight and called it commerce. The air smelled of resin smoke, wet stone, hot metal, and underneath all of it something sweeter and wronger, like fruit going bad in a rich man's cellar.

And the market. Saints preserve me, the market.

It spilled down the terraces in tiers, following the stairs like water finding its level. And it was not a market of things. I have walked a hundred bazaars from the salt-roads to the river deltas; I know what trade looks like — bolts of cloth, cages of birds, knives laid out on wool. This was not that. Here the stalls sold *intangibles*, and everyone behaved as though this were the most ordinary thing in the world.

A woman with silver rings on every finger sat behind a table of small glass vials, each one glowing faintly a different color, and her sign read MEMORIES, FAIR TO FINE. A boy of maybe twelve stood on a crate crying out prices for YEARS OF GOOD HEALTH, GENTLY USED. Farther down, a stall draped in white sold nothing but names — rows of little lead tablets scratched with words, and customers who touched them and wept, or laughed, or went very still and walked away lighter or heavier, I couldn't tell which.

"They're real," Corin whispered. He had stopped dead on the stair above a memory-stall, and his face in the torchlight was terrible — wonder and revulsion fighting for the same ground. "Ada, they're — you can see them moving. In the glass. Like fish."

I looked. He was right. Each vial held not liquid but a shimmer, a suggestion of motion, and when the ringed woman held one up to a customer's eye and let him peer inside, his whole face changed — aged, softened, broke open — before she corked it away again.

"First rule of any market," I said quietly. "Don't touch what you can't afford. Second rule: don't let them see you want."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the trick of it. Want is the currency here. Everything else is just weight."

He gave me a look that said he didn't believe me, which was fair, because my wrist had been burning since the ridge line, and I hadn't told him that either.

* * *

We took a room in a lodging house halfway down the fourth terrace, a place called the Tallow Lamp where the beds were hard and the landlady charged extra for questions and returned none. From our window we could see the whole descending sprawl of the market, tier below tier below tier, down and down toward that unlit mouth at the mountain's foot, and above the lowest terrace, where ordinary commerce ended, a different kind of light began — not orange but white, cold, steady, the light of lamps that never guttered.

"The soul-court," Corin said. He'd said it four times since the pass, each time like a man testing whether a floor would hold him.

"The court holds market under torchlight," I said. "That's what the scribe's letter said. Neme's letter. Down there somewhere is a ledger with your name in it, signed by your great-grandfather a hundred years ago, and twelve nights from tonight the moon finishes its thirteenth turn and somebody comes to collect." I flexed my hand. The oath-mark pulsed under the wrapping, a slow wrong heartbeat, stronger here than anywhere we'd been. "So tomorrow we find the archives, we find the contract, and we find out exactly what 'payable in full' means."

"And if it means what I think it means?"

I didn't answer that. Instead I asked him something I'd been turning over since the memory stalls.

"Corin. Back there. The boy selling years of health. If you'd had coin — " I stopped myself, rephrased. "When you were a merchant's son. Did your family ever trade here?"

Something crossed his face, quick as a fish under ice. "Everyone in the trade routes knows of Karsthold," he said. "Knowing of it isn't trading in it."

"That wasn't an answer."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, and began unpacking his bag, and I let it go, because I was keeping secrets too, and mercy between liars is just courtesy.

* * *

I slept badly, which surprised me, because I have slept well on battlefields and badly only in places that wanted something from me.

In the deep hours I gave up, dressed, and went down into the night market alone. Call it reconnaissance. Call it what it was.

The market changed after midnight. The tourists and pilgrims thinned away and the true traffic emerged — robed figures moving between certain stalls without haggling, guards in livery I didn't recognize whose armor bore no sigil at all (a deliberate blankness, I realized; a uniform designed to belong to no house so it could serve every house), and buyers who came hooded, paid in silence, and left carrying their purchases pressed to their chests like infants.

At a stall near the third terrace's edge, an old woman was selling memories out of a wooden case instead of glass, and hers were different — coarser, rawer, the shimmer in them dark-edged. A young man in fine clothes stood before her counting coins, and I heard him say, "Just one. Just the one where I forgive her. I can't carry it anymore."

"One night's forgiveness," the old woman said. "It'll fade by morning. They always do. That's why you'll be back."

He bought it. He held it to his eye and his shoulders dropped and his face went slack with relief, and I watched a man purchase a feeling he already owned and lose it again by dawn, and I thought: *this is what power does when it becomes currency.* It doesn't stay in strong hands. It doesn't even stay in cruel hands. It pools wherever there's hunger, and hunger is everywhere, and so the powerful simply build channels and stand downstream.

My wrist flared so suddenly I gasped.

Not pain this time — attention. The oath-mark under its wrappings had gone hot as a coal, and beneath the heat I felt something I had not felt in twenty years, since I was seven years old kneeling on the valley floor with the elders' hands on my head:

A voice. Or the ghost of where a voice had been. Not words. More like pressing an ear to a door and hearing not speech but the shape of someone speaking — urgent, rhythmic, desperate, over and over and over, a sermon delivered through a wall to a congregation of no one.

The Vessel-Who-Waits. My god. My silent god, whose silence I had built my whole life around the way you build a house around a grave.

Here, in Karsthold, under the mountain where souls were weighed and sold and brokered — here he was not silent. Here he was *screaming*, and I was the first person in a century standing close enough to almost hear it.

"Mercenary."

I turned, hand already on my knife.

A figure stood at the edge of the stall-light: slight, ink-stained fingers wrapped around a lantern, face shadowed under a hood that smelled of dust and candle tallow. Old, I judged. Nervous, definitely. But the voice was dry and precise, the voice of someone who had spent a lifetime choosing exact words.

"You've been staring at that wrist for a quarter hour," the figure said. "Which tells me two things. One — you wear an oath-mark of the Vessel, and it's burning, which shouldn't be possible this far from the valley. Two — you don't know why." A pause. "I know why. I used to work for the people who made sure nobody else does."

"Neme," I said.

Surprise flickered under the hood. "You got my letter, then. Good. Then hear the rest quickly, because the blank-shields have marked your friend — they marked him at the gate, they mark anyone carrying paper that hums — and the court's collectors move fastest in the hour before dawn." The lantern lifted slightly, illuminating a face lined like old parchment and eyes that had seen too much ledgers. "Come with me now, mercenary, and I'll show you where your god's voice is coming from. It's the same room where they keep the contract with your friend's name in it."

Behind me, up on the fourth terrace, a window went dark. Corin, awake, waiting, trusting me to come back with answers.

Below us, past the last tier of torchlight, the white lamps of the soul-court burned on without flickering, and beneath them, beneath everything, something ancient hammered against the inside of its prison, and for the first time in my life I heard it.

I followed the lantern down.
