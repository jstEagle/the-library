## 8. A Debt With His Face

The archives beneath Karsthold smelled of cold stone and older things — vellum gone the color of tea, tallow smoke, and something sweeter underneath that Corin could not name until he realized it was the smell of preserved memory itself. The court's scribes kept their records in two forms: ink on skin-thin parchment, and the memories themselves, bottled in glass jars no larger than a thumb, each one glowing faintly with whatever moment had been pulled from someone's head and filed away like a receipt.

He had told Ada he needed an hour to rest. That much was even true. His legs ached from the descent into the mountain city, from the stairways that spiraled down past torchlit markets where names were traded in whispers and years of life changed hands for the price of a good coat. He had watched an old woman sell her wedding day — just the one day, plucked out of her past like a thread from a hem — and walk away lighter, poorer, smiling.

But rest was not what he came for. Rest was what he told himself while his feet carried him, ledger-token in hand, toward the deep stacks where the oldest contracts slept.

Neme had given them the token. *Court scribery, third rank, disgraced,* she'd said, with the flat delivery of someone describing a stain on their own sleeve. *It will open the reading rooms. It will not open the vaults. Don't be stupid about it.* She'd said it looking at Ada, but Corin had felt the words land in him like a key finding its lock.

He was being stupid about it already. He knew that. There is a particular clarity that comes with knowing you have twelve nights left — eleven now, by the moon's slow arithmetic — a clarity that feels exactly like recklessness from the outside and exactly like honesty from within.

* * *

The reading room they assigned him was a cell of grey stone lit by a single lamp of green flame that gave off heat without light and light without warmth. A clerk with spectacles ground so thick his eyes looked like coins at the bottom of a well took his token, examined it against a slate of sigils, and asked, without interest:

"Lineage?"

Corin had prepared for this question the way he prepared for customs inspections on merchant roads: with a lie polished smooth.

"Ash," he said. "Of Merrow's Crossing. Merchant family."

Something flickered behind the coin-eyes. Not suspicion. Worse — recognition.

"Ash," the clerk repeated. "Merrow's Crossing." He made a small mark on his slate. "You'll want the eastern gallery, then. Third alcove. The Ash materials have been kept together."

*Kept together.* As though there were volumes of them. As though his family — four generations of honest cloth merchants who had never once, as far as Corin knew, set foot inside this mountain — were a subject of study here.

His mouth had gone dry. He thanked the clerk. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, because everything behind his ribs had begun to shake.

* * *

The eastern gallery was colder than the reading rooms, and quieter in a way that pressed against the ears. The shelves here were not wood but bone-white stone, and the documents lay flat in shallow drawers rather than standing in books, as though even the archivists had understood that these papers should lie down.

Third alcove. The drawer was labeled simply: **ASH — DEBT ORIGINATION — SEALED LINE**.

Sealed line. His great-grandfather's name had been Tomas Ash. Corin had seen it carved on the family marker in Merrow's Crossing churchyard, worn nearly smooth by weather. Tomas had founded the business. Tomas had built the house with the crooked chimney that Corin's father still complained about. Tomas had died old, full of grandchildren, mourned by everyone.

Corin drew out the first document with both hands, the way you lift something you suspect might be holy or might be cursed and haven't yet decided which would be worse.

It was a contract. Of course it was. But contracts — real ones, the kind his father drew up for shipments of wool — had margins, clauses, the dull furniture of commerce. This document had none of that slack. It was written in a hand so fine and so black it seemed less inscribed than grown into the page, and it began without preamble:

**Let it be entered that the soul of the issue of Tomas Ash, unto all generations bearing the blood, is held in surety against sums advanced, payable in full upon the completion of the moon's thirteenth turn following the bearer's knowledge of this instrument.**

*Bearing the blood.* Not him specifically. Any of them. Whoever found out. Whoever read the page. The prophecy book hadn't chosen Corin; it had simply been the trap sprung nearest at hand. Some other Ash, in some other generation, might have opened that book instead and been walking to their death right now in his place. The thought did not comfort him. It curdled him.

And then he reached the signatures.

Tomas Ash's name stood at the foot of the contract in a shaky, human hand — an old man's hand, or a frightened man's hand, or both. Beneath it, the word *debtor*. Corin stared at it for a long time. His great-grandfather had signed this. Had walked into this mountain, or sat at his own kitchen table while men in robes explained terms to him, and had put his name to the selling of every descendant he would ever love.

But it was the second signature that stopped Corin's heart like a fist closing around it.

**Witnessed and sealed: Brother Aldous Vane, Riteskeeper of the Vessel-Who-Waits, Under-Authority of the Church of the Silent God.**

There was a seal beside the name — pressed into wax gone dark as dried blood — and Corin knew the sigil on it instantly, because Ada wore it burned into her wrist. The waiting vessel. The cup upturned, empty, patient. The mark of the god whose silence had shaped her whole life, whose voicelessness she grieved like a widow grieves.

A priest of her god had stood witness when his soul was sold.

* * *

He did not remember sitting down. He was on the floor of the alcove with his back against the stone shelf, the contract open across his knees, breathing the cold air in careful spoonfuls. The green lamp in the reading room seemed very far away. Down here there was only the bone-shelf and the paper and the truth.

*The church didn't just know.*

Ada believed her god had gone silent a century ago. She carried that silence like an injury, like a wound she kept clean and tended and showed to no one. Her whole moral architecture — the code, the contracts, the refusal to break her word even to save herself — was built on top of that grief. *My god stopped speaking. I keep faith anyway.* That was the spine of her.

And here, in black ink a hundred years old, was evidence that her god's clergy had been doing business under the altar the entire time.

Not failing to notice a soul-debt. Not tolerating it at the edges. *Brokering* it. Witnessing it. Sealing it with the god's own sigil — which meant either that the priests had stolen the authority of their silent master, or —

Or the god had consented.

Corin turned that possibility over and found it unbearable from every angle. If the priests had acted alone, then Ada's faith had been betrayed by men, and betrayal by men she could survive; she survived everything. But if the Vessel-Who-Waits had truly witnessed this — if the god she'd sworn her childhood oath to had put his seal beneath the sale of a child unborn — then her silence wasn't abandonment. It was verdict. It was the sound of a god washing his hands.

He thought about telling himself it might be forgery. He held the hope up to the lamp of the idea and watched it die quickly. The wax was period-authentic; Neme could confirm the seal-matrix in a heartbeat. And more damning than any technicality: why forge? Who would bother forging a witness-seal on a contract meant never to be read by its victims?

No one. Unless the victim's descendants someday came digging.

Unless the church feared, someday, exactly this drawer being opened.

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more. The drawer held perhaps thirty documents, and Corin, kneeling now on the cold floor like a penitent, went through them one by one because stopping was no longer possible — because this was his family laid open like an anatomy lesson.

Sums advanced. That phrase recurred. Sums advanced to whom, for what? The answer emerged slowly, in letters between the court and the church, in marginalia in different hands across different decades. In the famine winter — the one the valley histories called the Lean Years, the one that had emptied half the villages of the lowlands — Tomas Ash had come to Karsthold not as a buyer but as a beggar. The church had lent him grain. Grain enough to seed his fields and feed his village through until harvest. The price had not been interest.

The price had been *issue*. The soul of the issue of Tomas Ash, held in surety, in perpetuity, collectible whenever the court chose and the bloodline learned enough to matter.

They had bought bread with his existence. His mother had fed him from granaries paid for with a promissory note written in his own soul. Every meal of his childhood had been, unknowingly, an installment.

Corin laughed. It came out of him wrong, a dry cracked sound with nothing in it, echoing off the bone-stone. Somewhere deeper in the archives, something laughed back — or echoed, or shifted, or was only the settling of a mountain that had been eating families like his for a century and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

He thought of his father. Good, tired, decent Harl Ash, who counted stock twice and never dunned a poor customer, who had named his son for a grandfather's friend and taught him that a man's word was the only collateral worth having. *A man's word.* His family's word had been given before any of them were born, and the interest had compounded in silence for a hundred years, and the bank was a hole under a mountain, and the notary wore his best friend's god around its neck.

Best friend. Was that what Ada was? They had known each other nine days. Nine days of road dust and ambush blood and arguments about how fast to push the horses, and somewhere in those nine days she had become the fixed point his mind returned to, the way a compass needle returns north. He had not said it. He suspected she would sooner fight a bear than hear it said.

And now he knelt in the proof that the thing binding them together — the prophecy, the debt, the desperate race against the thirteenth turn — was a thing her own church had manufactured. Her god's seal on his chains. Every mile they'd fled the church hunters, they'd been fleeing the collection agency of her own faith.

How did you hand someone that truth? How did you watch her face when you told her that the silence she mourned might be the quietest part of the crime?

* * *

He copied everything. His hands steadied once the work began — there was mercy in taskwork, his father always said, and his father had been right about most practical things. He traced the signature of Brother Aldous Vane letter by letter. He sketched the seal as best he could by feel and lamplight. He noted dates, amounts, the names of three successive court factors who had managed the Ash debt like a garden: pruning here, watering there, never harvesting early, never letting the bloodline thin below viability.

*Never letting the bloodline thin.* They had protected his family. Kept them prosperous, even — there was a letter instructing the local church in Merrow's Crossing to steer favorable trade toward Ash carts, to speak well of them at market. A hundred years of small invisible kindnesses, all of it husbandry. You did not let your orchard freeze.

Every stroke of luck his family had ever celebrated. Every season the crops held, every shipment that beat the frost, every year the tax assessors looked elsewhere. Corin sat amid the papers and watched his whole inheritance invert itself, prosperity revealed as pasture, care revealed as keeping, love in his family's history revealed as feeding schedule.

The last document in the drawer was the newest — dated only twenty-two years ago, the year of his birth. A single sheet, brief as a knife.

**Note of activation: the issue born this season to Harl Ash bears the birth-mark per the origination instrument. Collection window provisionally fixed. Await the moon's count.**

Birth-mark. Corin's hand went to his collarbone without his permission, to the small pale crescent he'd had since birth, the one his mother used to kiss and call her lucky moon. He'd shown it to Ada once, jokingly, on the road — *look, I was marked for greatness* — and she had smiled the rare unguarded smile that made the whole enterprise of staying alive seem briefly reasonable.

Lucky moon. Provisionally fixed. Await the count.

He folded the copies into his coat, against his chest, where the paper rose and fell with his breathing. Then he replaced every document precisely as he'd found it, aligned the drawer's edge with the shelf, and stood in the alcove a long moment in the dark, listening to the mountain breathe around him.

Twelve nights. Eleven, now. Somewhere above him Ada was waiting — watching the door, no doubt, counting exits, cataloguing threats, guarding his life with the same ferocity she gave to every contract she'd ever sworn. Guarding a life the church itself had appraised, priced, and shelved before his grandmother's grandmother had drawn breath.

He climbed the stairs toward the lamplight and the world above, carrying the truth against his heart like contraband.

He would tell her. He had to tell her — every hour he didn't, the lie grew teeth. But he needed to know first whether the seal was genuine, whether her god's hand had truly rested on this paper, and there was exactly one person in Karsthold who could read a century-old witness-mark and tell him the truth without flinching.

*Neme,* he thought, pushing open the door into the torchlight and noise of the upper court. *I need to find Neme.*

Behind him, in the dark of the third alcove, the drawer labeled ASH slid shut with a sound almost like a page turning — and far above, through the mountain's throat, the moon went on waning toward its thirteenth turn, patient as paperwork.
