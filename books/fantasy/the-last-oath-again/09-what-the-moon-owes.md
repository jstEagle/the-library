## 9. What the Moon Owes

The scribe's shop sat at the bottom of a stair that smelled of wet stone and old ink, in the quarter of Karsthold where the torchlight from the soul-market above didn't quite reach. The sign above the door had once read something grand — I could see the ghost of gilded letters scraped away, leaving only pocked wood — and now bore a single word chalked in a careful hand: *CORRECTIONS*.

"Corrections," Corin read aloud behind me. His voice was thin. He'd been thin-voiced since the market, since we'd walked those torchlit rows and watched a woman sell her grandmother's last summer for enough coin to eat through winter. Since he'd understood, in his bones, what was owed out of him.

"It means he'll fix things," I said. "For a price."

"And if he can't?"

I pushed the door open. "Then we find someone who can, and we've lost nothing but an afternoon." It wasn't true. We'd lose more than an afternoon. The moon overhead was a shaved sliver of itself, nine turns spent of thirteen, and every hour it waned was an hour carved off the ledger that had my companion's name in it.

Inside, the shop was a throat of books. Shelves climbed the walls into shadow, stuffed not with printed volumes but with ledgers — thousands upon thousands of them, spines cracked, corners furred with damp. Some were chained shut with little brass locks no bigger than a thumbnail. The air tasted the way the valley never did: full. Charged. Every one of those ledgers hummed faintly against my skin like a struck bell still ringing, and I realized I was feeling them the way I'd felt nothing in the Hollow Valley for twenty-eight years. Magic soaked this place the way water soaks a grave.

A lamp burned on a desk buried under paper. Behind it sat a small man with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of tea left too long, spectacles perched on a nose that had clearly been broken at least twice. He did not look up when we entered.

"If you've come about a correction to your own contract," he said, "the answer is no. If you've come to buy a memory, the market's up the hill and I don't take commissions. If you've come to arrest me, the Church already did, twice, and they gave back everything except my dignity and my right to practice."

"We've come about a debt," I said.

His pen stopped.

"A soul-debt," I went on. "Old one. Generational. Payable at the moon's thirteenth turn."

Now he looked up, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd been doing for days. His gaze went to Corin — young, exhausted, alive, which by itself should have told the man everything — and then to me, and then to the oath-mark hidden under my sleeve as if he could see it burning there anyway.

"Close the door," Neme said. "And sit down. You're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

He made us tea we didn't drink, in cups chipped at the rims, and he talked with his hands the way people do who have spent their lives making marks that must be exact.

"I was a court-scribe for nineteen years," he said. "Third rank. Which meant I copied contracts but didn't draft them, verified seals but didn't forge them — that's second rank — and filed everything. Do you know what a soul-court actually is? Not the torches and the auctioneers. That's the shopfront. The court is the ledger-hall underneath. Every sale, every trade, every debt ever brokered beneath this mountain is written in a master ledger down there, and the ledger doesn't record. It *binds*. What's written in the master ledger is true. Not accurate — true. There's a difference. Accurate is what happened. True is what must continue to happen." He took off his spectacles and polished them on a rag so grey it might once have been white. "I was exiled for pointing that difference out at an inconvenient moment. A widow came in to reclaim her husband's name — he'd sold it to pay a surgeon — and she'd brought the price in full. And the clerk refused her, because the buyer refused to resell, and I said, loudly, in front of the hall, that a system where a thing can be sold but never bought back isn't a market. It's a mouth."

Corin had gone very still beside me. "Can a thing be bought back?" he asked. "That's why we're here. Can it?"

Neme put his spectacles back on. He looked at Corin for a long moment, and whatever he saw in the boy's face made something in his own face go gentler and grimmer at once.

"Yes," he said. "By substitution."

"Meaning what?" I asked, though I was already beginning to mean it myself, the way you begin to feel rain before the first drop lands.

"Meaning the ledger keeps accounts the way a scale keeps weight." He pulled a ledger toward him across the desk — a working copy, its brass lock hanging broken — and opened it flat with the ease of long habit. "Everything in a soul has weight. Not metaphorical weight; the court measures it. Memories, years, names, loves, fears, talents, the particular grief of a particular childhood — all of it has measure. When your great-grandfather sold the unborn soul in his line, the court weighed what it received, and it wrote the weight down, and that number is in the master ledger tonight, waiting, like a bowl set out on a doorstep." He tapped the page. "A soul can be reclaimed only by setting something into that bowl of equal weight. Nothing more — overpayment voids the transaction, the court is precise to the point of cruelty. Nothing less — short payment and the debt compounds, and compounding soul-debt is how you get the hollow ones, the people who walk around Karsthold with nothing behind their eyes. And—" here he paused, and his hands, which had been moving constantly, went flat and still on the desk— "it must be freely given. That's not a nicety. That's structural. Coerced payment weighs nothing. Tricked payment weighs less than nothing; it actually feeds the court. The ledger knows the difference between a gift and a theft wearing a gift's clothes. Whatever goes into that bowl has to walk there on its own legs, knowing full well what it is and what it's losing, and say *yes*."

The lamp flame leaned in some draught I couldn't find. In the silence I could hear the ledgers humming on their shelves, thousands of tiny bells, all of them ringing truths nobody wanted.

"So we find something equal in weight to a soul," I said. My voice sounded steadier than I felt. "What weighs that much? Another soul?"

"That's the obvious answer, yes. A life for a life, given freely, and the court accepts it — it's been done maybe four times in the court's history, because almost no one loves anyone that much, and the ones who do are usually dead before they can sign." Neme's eyes flicked to me, quick and sharp, and I understood he had already done the arithmetic on me. "But it doesn't have to be a whole soul. Weight is weight. A century of memories might balance a name. Thirty years of a life might balance a talent. The court will weigh whatever you bring. The problem—" he spread his stained hands— "the problem is that nobody knows the exact weight of anything until they set it in the bowl. You cannot calculate your way to salvation. You can only offer, and let the scales speak, and pray you brought enough. And if you didn't—" 

"Compounding," Corin said quietly.

"Compounding," Neme agreed. "And the window closes at the thirteenth turn. After collection, the debt is paid and the account closed forever. No substitutions, no appeals, no corrections." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "You can see why they exiled me. I kept telling clients the truth. Truth is terrible for business."

* * *

We stayed while the lamp burned lower. Neme answered questions the way a good scribe does — precisely, completely, and with visible cost, as if each answer cost him a little of the ink in his veins. Yes, he could get us near the ledger-hall; no, he could not get us in; yes, he would help, and no, he would not take payment, which frightened me more than anything else he'd said, because in my experience the people who don't charge are the ones collecting something else.

It was near the end, when Corin had gone to stand at the narrow window and look up at the moon — I watched him counting what was left of it, the way you'd count coins — that Neme lowered his voice and spoke to me alone.

"The oath-mark," he said. "Under your left sleeve. Vessel-Who-Waits, unless I miss my guess, and I rarely do — I catalogued three hundred sworn arms during my years of service."

"You can see it?"

"I can see its *weight*. It's the brightest thing in this room, mercenary. Brighter than the boy's debt." He turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. "Do you know what an oath weighs? A sworn oath to a god, witnessed and sealed — it's one of the heaviest things a mortal can carry. The clergy use sworn souls as ballast in half their greater workings. Why do you think the church hunts deserters so relentlessly? It's not doctrine. It's inventory control."

My wrist burned under the wool wrap, the way it had burned since the day we crossed the valley border and the world's magic came flooding back into me — including, wrongly, impossibly, the pulse of a god who had been silent for a hundred years. I had told myself it was an echo. A scar aching before rain.

"What are you telling me, scribe?"

"I'm telling you what you've already worked out, or you wouldn't have gone pale when I said *freely given*." He met my eyes, and there was no cruelty in his look, only the terrible kindness of a man who has filed too many contracts to lie prettily. "An oath freely given to a god is among the few mortal possessions whose weight approaches a soul's. Perhaps equals it. Perhaps — and this is the part that kept me awake after our first meeting was arranged — perhaps exceeds it, depending on the oath, and the god, and the giver." He folded his hands. "I'm telling you that you walked down my stairs carrying the answer to the boy's problem strapped to your wrist, and I am asking you — begging you — not to decide anything tonight. Freely given means freely *deliberated*. An oath sacrificed in haste weighs differently than one laid down with open eyes. The court would know the difference even if you didn't."

"I haven't decided anything," I said.

"No," Neme agreed softly. "But you've started deciding, and I've seen that look before, on better people than either of us. So hear an old file-keeper: the ledger takes what walks to it willingly. Don't start walking yet."

* * *

Outside, the night had come down cold and close, and the moon hung over Karsthold's black shoulder like a nail-paring. Four turns left. Maybe five, if the almanacs lied kindly.

Corin fell into step beside me on the climb back toward the rented room, his breath smoking in the torchlight spilling down from the market streets above. For a while neither of us spoke. The city murmured around us — the auction-bells, the criers calling tomorrow's lots, somewhere a woman laughing with the bright false ring of a newly purchased memory.

"He heard all of it," Corin finally said. "Neme. The substitution. The weighing. He looked at you when he said it, Ada. He wasn't talking about theory."

"I know."

"You'd do it." It wasn't an accusation. That was the worst of it. He said it the way a man confirms the ground is rising under him — helpless, factual, already falling. "You've known me eleven days and you'd put your god-damned oath in a bowl to weigh it against my soul."

I stopped walking. Below us the market roared; above us the moon thinned. I thought about the Hollow Valley, about the escort jobs and the empty rooms and twenty-eight years of belonging nowhere, and about the one rule I'd broken to walk out of that valley beside him, and I thought about what Neme had said — *don't start walking yet* — and understood that I had been walking since the border.

"I'd *consider* it," I said. "Which in your accounting probably comes to the same thing. But listen to me, Corin Ash. Nobody's putting anything in any bowl until we've turned over every other stone. Including the stones the church is sitting on. Your great-grandfather's contract had a witness — a priest of the Vessel-Who-Waits sealed that sale. Which means the church has copies. Records. Maybe leverage. And the High Cleric of this city has been sending runners to our inn every day since we arrived, which means Ostrevan wants something, and men like that only want things they're afraid of."

Corin stared at me. "You want to *negotiate* with them? With the people who brokered my soul in the first place?"

"I want to know what they know before they decide we're not worth knowing anymore." I started walking again, faster, because standing still under that moon felt like standing on a scale. "Tomorrow we send word that we'll meet. And tomorrow night, Corin, you're going to tell me exactly what you and Neme whispered about at that window while I was reading the contract fragments — because you forget I've spent my life reading men in bad light, and you've got the face of a man carrying a secret he thinks is protecting someone."

He said nothing. His silence followed me up through the torchlit streets like a second shadow, and high above us both, patient as a creditor, the moon wore itself away another degree toward its thirteenth turn.
