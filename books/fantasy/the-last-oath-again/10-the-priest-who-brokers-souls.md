## 10. The Priest Who Brokers Souls

I had not stood in the presence of the High Cleric in eleven years, and the smell of him had not changed: incense and ink and something older beneath both, like stone that had been prayed over so long it had forgotten how to be anything else.

The audience chamber of the Basilica of the Waiting Vessel occupied a hollow in Karsthold's upper galleries where the torchlight was permitted to be gold instead of orange. Gold light was expensive. It required mirrors ground by debtors who had sold their eyes' sharpness to the court and spent their remaining years squinting at their own handiwork. I knew this because I had been one of them once — not for my eyes, but for other things, smaller things, a memory here, a season of joy there — before I turned scribe and learned which ledgers could be quietly misfiled.

Ostrevan sat behind a desk of black karst-wood that had been part of the mountain before anyone thought to build with it. He did not look up when they brought me in. He never looked up first. Looking up was an act of acknowledgment, and acknowledgment, in Ostrevan's theology, was a form of payment.

"Neme," he said to the page of scripture beneath his fingers. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell you a great many things," I said. "Most of them are invoices."

One of his acolytes made a small sound, quickly smothered. Ostrevan let it hang there, testing whether I would flinch. I had spent eleven years among the undercity's pawnbrokers and soul-fences; I had learned flinching as a currency I could no longer afford. I stood still and let the silence do its work on him instead.

He looked up then. His face was the same as it had been when he was merely a canon of rites and I was merely a girl with too-good a memory for contract language: long, pale, composed, the face of a man who believed patience was the same thing as virtue because he had never needed to learn the difference. His eyes were gray as unlit wicks.

"You were excommunicated for forgery," he said.

"I was excommunicated for *finding* one." I kept my voice pleasant. "The forgery came pre-existing. You simply preferred the ledger closed."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Interest. That frightened me more than anger would have. Anger I understood. Interest meant he had already decided I was useful, and men like Ostrevan only ever found two kinds of people useful: the ones they intended to keep, and the ones they intended to spend.

"The mercenary," he said. "Ada Vane. And the debtor boy, Corin Ash. You've been seen with them."

"Seen by whom?"

"Does it matter?"

It mattered enormously, but I said, "No," because the question was a hook and I refused to be fish-shaped tonight.

* * *

They gave me a chair eventually, which in the Basilica's grammar meant the conversation had been upgraded from interrogation to negotiation, and I confess my hands shook slightly as I sat, though I hid it by folding them in my lap like a good little clerk. The chair was hard oak. Everything in this place was hard oak and gold light and the smell of a god who had not answered a prayer in a hundred years.

That was the open secret of Karsthold's upper city, the one everyone knew and no one said aloud in rooms with echoes: the Vessel-Who-Waits had gone silent, and the church that bore his name had continued anyway, conducting rites to a listener who no longer listened, selling absolution from a till that no longer rang. I had been a believer once. I had wept at my ordination. Then I had found the witness-seal on a century-old soul-contract that should have been signed by the god's own authority, and I had asked one careful question, and the careful question had cost me everything except my life, which they left me because taking it would have confirmed what the seal suggested.

"You know why I've called you here," Ostrevan said.

"Because your hunters lost them in the Weft Galleries and your diviners can't scry inside the undercity without paying the very brokers you'd rather didn't exist."

His mouth did the smallest possible movement, which on another man would have been a smile. "You always did have a gift for the unflattering summary."

"It's the flattering summaries that get souls collected."

He folded his hands. I watched the gesture and understood it for what it was: a performance of openness, a door left visibly unlocked so the guest would not check the windows.

"The girl, Vane," he said. "She bears the oath-mark of the Vessel. A living mark, still warm after a hundred years of silence. Do you know how rare that is?"

"She mentioned she swears by him."

"She doesn't *mention*, Neme. She *burns*. The mark has not dimmed since the day her order recorded it at her swearing-in as a child. Every other oath-mark in the valley lands went cold within a generation of the Silence. Hers stayed lit like a coal under ash." He leaned forward, and the gold light caught the gray of his eyes and made them almost silver, almost holy, almost the face of a man worth following. "Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere, in whatever dark place our god has retreated to, something is still listening to *her*."

"Or," I said, "the mark is a scar, and scars don't need anyone listening."

"That is the cynicism of a woman who was wronged," he said gently, "not the analysis of a scholar. You taught me better, once, before you taught yourself worse."

I let that pass. In the undercity we have a saying: *let the eel swim past the trap twice before you spring it.* I wanted to see the shape of the whole snare before my foot went in.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"To deliver a message. To make an offer. To be, if you will, what you once were — a broker of terms between parties who cannot safely speak." He rose, and walked to the window, and looked out over Karsthold's terraced lights, the upper city's gold bleeding down into the middle city's orange and finally the undercity's greasy blue, where people like me lived on what fell. "The church does not want the Ash boy dead."

"No," I agreed. "The church wants the *debt* honored. The boy dying is just the mechanism. There's a difference, and I suspect you're about to pretend otherwise."

He turned. For a moment — half a breath — the composure slipped, and underneath it I saw something tired. Genuinely tired. A man who had been holding up a ceiling for so long he had forgotten there was any position other than arms-raised. Then it was gone, smoothed away like a fingerprint wiped from silver, and I wondered if I had imagined it, and I decided I hadn't, and I hated myself a little for noticing, because pity is the most dangerous thing you can feel toward a negotiator.

"Here is the offer," he said. "Vane delivers the prophecy book to the Basilica. The book is church property — stolen, or soon to be claimed as such; the provenance is flexible, provenance usually is. In exchange: full absolution for herself and her companions for the violation of the Hollow peace. Safe passage for Corin Ash back to his family's holdings in the lowlands, under church seal, unmolested by the court below until such time as the moon completes its turn and the debt is settled through proper channels rather than street channels. And for you—" his gaze rested on me with terrible gentleness— "restoration. Your orders reinstated. Your name struck from the excommunication rolls. Your seat in the scriptorium returned. The work you were born for, Neme. The work you loved."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above us, a bell that no longer summoned anyone tolled the hour anyway, out of habit, out of grief, out of the sheer institutional momentum of a thing too large to admit it was empty.

And I will be honest, because a scribe who lies in her own account is worthless even to herself: for three full heartbeats, I wanted it.

I wanted the scriptorium with its south-facing light. I wanted vellum that didn't smell of mold. I wanted to sit among people who spoke in marginalia and jokes about ligatures, who understood that a well-drawn ampersand was its own small prayer. I wanted my name back. Eleven years is a long time to be a woman whose name means *disgraced* in every registry from here to the coast. Eleven years is a long time to introduce yourself as *call me Neme* and watch strangers decide you are someone who needs an alias.

Three heartbeats. Then I thought of Corin Ash's face in the lamplight of my cramped rooms, turning pages of a book older than his bloodline, finding his great-grandfather's signature at the bottom of a sale of his own soul, and asking me — voice steady, which must have cost him — *"Was there anything my family could have done? Any clause? Any appeal?"* And I thought of Ada Vane standing behind him with her arms crossed and her burning wrist held close against her ribs, like a wound she was protecting from the draft, and the way she had said, when I explained substitution law: *"Equal weight, freely given. Whose weight? Given to whom? Because those two words are doing all the work in that sentence, scribe, and I want to know who forged them."*

These were not sophisticated people, in the church's accounting. They were a sellsword with one rule and a merchant's son with a death date. But they asked better questions than the Basilica had asked in a century, and I had grown old enough, and exile-hardened enough, to know that the quality of a person's questions tells you the quality of their soul far better than any ledger.

"And the debt itself," I said. "In your generous offer, the boy goes home safe, the book comes home safe, and the debt... stands."

"The debt is lawful," Ostrevan said.

"The debt is *documented*," I said. "Lawful is a word you use when you haven't read the document closely."

His stillness now had a different texture. "Choose your next words with care, scribe."

"With care, then." I stood, because I did not want to negotiate seated; sitting puts the neck at a disadvantage. "High Cleric, I was cast out for finding a forged witness-seal. You know this. You signed the writ of my excommunication — your cipher is on it, third line, the flourish you affect on documents concerning the Vessel's authority. So before I carry your mercy to that girl, answer me the question I was ruined for asking, and answer it plainly: did the Vessel-Who-Waits consent to the Ash covenant, yes or no?"

The gold light did not flicker. The bells had finished their habituated tolling. Even the incense smoke seemed to hang straighter, listening.

"If I say yes," Ostrevan said at last, "you will call me a liar, because you believe you have proof otherwise. If I say no, you will run back to your mercenaries with a weapon I cannot permit you to carry. Either way you leave this room dangerous." He came around the desk, slowly, and stopped an arm's length from me, and lowered his voice to the register he must have used at a thousand deathbeds, the one that makes surrender feel like grace. "So I will say neither. I will say this: the Vessel is *silent*, Neme. Has been for a hundred years. And in his silence, someone must speak the law. Someone must hold the seals. Someone must keep the courts running, the debts balanced, the world from dissolving into every creditor and debtor tearing at each other in the dark. That burden fell to us. We did not choose it gladly."

"You chose it *eventually*."

"We chose it *at all*." Something flared in him and was mastered. "You think power corrupts because power is evil. Power corrupts because power is *heavy*, and men set it down wherever they're standing, and where they happen to be standing is always on someone's neck." He exhaled. "Bring me the book. Bring me the girl's agreement. Give these children their mercy, and give me back my scribe, and I promise you — I swear it on the empty throne itself — no soul will be collected that can be saved."

*Empty throne,* he had said. Not *silent god.* Empty. As if the seat were vacant rather than occupied by something bound.

I filed that away where I file everything, in the drawer that had gotten me exiled once already.

* * *

They fed me before they sent me down. This was the detail that unsettled me most, afterward: roasted quail, real salt, wine from the sun-vineyards of the middle terraces. Hospitality as anesthesia. A man who has eaten your bread argues more softly against you. I ate it anyway. Exile teaches you not to waste protein on principle.

At the door, the young acolyte who had smothered his laugh earlier pressed something into my hand — a folded paper, wax-stamped with nothing, which meant it was either worthless or priceless. I waited until I was three galleries down, in the territory of orange light and honest shadows, before I opened it.

A single line, in a nervous clerk's hand:

*The seal registers are not in the main archive anymore. She took them down. Ask about the ninth vault.*

*She.* Not Ostrevan. Some other *she*, some hand I didn't yet know, moving the records of divine consent into the deep dark of the mountain where the court's oldest debts sleep in their drawers.

I stood in the blue-lit mouth of the undercity stair, the quail still warm in my stomach, the offer still warm in my ears, and I thought about temptation, and how it never arrives looking like temptation. It arrives looking like your own handwriting. Your own name restored. Your own desk, your own lamp, your own life handed back to you by a smiling man who only asks, in exchange, that you stop asking questions.

Ada Vane would ask me what was said. She would watch my face while I answered, the way soldiers watch faces, reading the body's marginalia. And I would have to stand there — disgraced Neme, restored-almost-Neme, bought-for-three-heartbeats-Neme — and decide, in front of her, which version of myself walked down those stairs.

The paper in my hand smelled faintly of the Basilica's incense.

Below me, the undercity hummed with its ten thousand small transactions, souls and years and names changing hands in the dark, and somewhere in that noise two people were waiting for a report that could save them or sell them, depending entirely on what I chose to repeat — and what I chose to withhold.

I folded the acolyte's warning small, tucked it into my sleeve beside my pen, and started down.

By morning, I would have to tell Ada that Ostrevan had offered her everything she'd ever lost.

What I could not yet decide was whether to tell her what *he* had offered me — or what the ninth vault might be hiding.
