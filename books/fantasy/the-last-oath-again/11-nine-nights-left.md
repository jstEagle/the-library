## 11. Nine Nights Left

The undercity of Karsthold did not sleep so much as it dreamed with its eyes open.

Corin learned this on the first night, lying on a pallet in a rented room above a tannery that had stopped tanning and started renting coffins by the week. The ceiling leaked a smell of old brine and older stone, and through the wall he could hear his neighbors — debtors all, if Neme's map of the district was accurate — murmuring in their sleep. Not nightmares. Something worse. They murmured numbers. Amounts owed, years remaining, names of children they'd signed away or were saving toward. The whole floor of the city beneath the torchlit markets was an abacus, clicking softly in the dark, counting down.

Nine nights left. He had taken to saying it to himself like a prayer, except he had no god left to pray to — not really, not after what they'd found in the archives. The Vessel-Who-Waits was a signature on his family's ruin, a witness-seal pressed into wax beside his great-grandfather's trembling hand. Whatever Ada still carried on her wrist, whatever warmth she thought lived inside her oath, Corin could no longer look at it without seeing a broker's stamp.

So he prayed to arithmetic instead.

*Thirteen turns of the moon since the contract's making. Twelve nights when we arrived. Eleven now, gone with this one. The court collects at the turn of the thirteenth. Souls are payable in full, or in substitution — something of equal weight, freely given.*

He turned the phrases over the way a man turns a coin he suspects is clipped, hunting for the shave in the metal.

Neme had told them substitution was possible. That had been the whole point of finding the disgraced scribe: *there is always a door in the ledger*, she'd said, wiping ink from her fingers with the air of someone who'd spent thirty years watching people walk through doors that cost them everything. *Equal weight. Freely given. The court is greedy, but it is honest about its greed.*

Ada had gone pale in the torchlight and said nothing for a long time, which Corin had come to understand was how she screamed.

And then, two days ago, she had stopped talking about substitution altogether. She'd started talking about the ledger-hall instead. About locks, and guards' rotations, and the width of the ventilation channels beneath the Hall of Accounts. About stealing the contract outright, voiding the forged seal, burning the debt at its root.

It was a good plan. It was a brave plan. And every night when he closed his eyes, Corin saw it failing.

He saw the alarm bells — because there would be bells; a place that traded souls did not leave its vault unwarded — and he saw the soul-writ bloom across his name like frost across a window, and he saw Ada standing in a crowd of court enforcers with her sword drawn and nine hundred years of stubbornness and only one body to spend. He saw her spend it. In his worst dreams, that was all the dream was: Ada spending herself against something vast and patient and legal, buying him minutes, and the minutes running out anyway while the moon rolled fat and indifferent toward its thirteenth turn.

She would do it, too. That was the unbearable part. He had watched her cross a border that made her god's mark burn just because a stranger with a book asked her to. She had made leaving the Hollow Valley — the one rule of her whole life — look easy. If the price of his soul was her oath, her freedom, her blood, she would pay it with the flat calm of a woman settling an innkeeper's bill.

Which meant the only thing Corin Ash could give her, the only gift that mattered, was to make sure she never had to.

There was another way. He'd found it the way you find anything in Karsthold: by following the smell of money downhill.

* * *

The broker worked out of a shuttered bathhouse in the Sump, three levels below the market streets, where the torchlight never reached and the lamps burned a color Corin had no name for — a bruised amber that made everyone's face look like they were remembering a funeral. The sign over the door had been scraped blank, but the shape of the letters remained, ghosted into the wood: *YEARS, HONESTLY WEIGHED.*

Inside, steam still rose from the old bathing pools, though nothing was heated anymore. The moisture came up from somewhere deeper, somewhere the mountain sweated. Rows of alcoves lined the walls where patrons had once soaked; now they held clerks behind curtains of hanging beads, and the sound of quills scratching was constant, soft, terrible — like insects eating something slowly.

Corin gave the password Neme had taught him — *"I have weight to sell"* — and felt the words settle into his chest like swallowed stones.

The broker herself sat in the central pool, or rather on a chair set in it, hemmed above dry water. She was small and gray and neat as a ledger line, with hands folded in her lap and eyes the flat silver of coins kept too long in a pocket. When she looked at him, Corin had the unmistakable sensation of being appraised. Not his clothes, not his swordlessness, not his nervousness. His *weight.*

"You're young," she said. Her voice was dry as the pool. "Young men who sell years usually want wine money or revenge. You don't look like either."

"I need time," Corin said. "Not money."

That earned him the first flicker of interest. She unfolded one hand and beckoned him closer with two fingers.

"Sit. Talk carefully. Everything said in this room is recorded." She tapped a slate on the arm of her chair, and Corin saw that the surface wasn't chalk but something darker, wetter, that moved faintly when he looked away from it. "Whether you buy or walk out, it remembers you."

He sat on the pool's edge. The stone was warm. Everything in Karsthold was warm in the wrong places, as if the mountain itself had a fever, or an appetite.

"I'm under a collection order," he said. "Court of the Beneath. Thirteen-turn contract. Payable in full at the turn."

The broker's expression didn't change, but the beads of the nearest alcove curtain stirred, though there was no draft. Somewhere behind them, a quill scratched faster.

"Collection orders are common," she said. "Buyers of collection orders are rare. What could you possibly purchase with your own years that would save you from their loss?"

"An extension. A deferral. Anything that moves the turn further out." He made himself say the rest plainly, because hedging would mark him as a liar and liars got worse prices. "Or a substitute clause written in my favor. I've read my contract. I know the court honors equal-weight substitutions. I want to arrange one before midnight of the thirteenth turn, quietly, so that when the collectors come, the paper says paid."

The silence stretched long enough that he heard the mountain drip somewhere below them, slow as a heartbeat counting down.

"You want to pay yourself," the broker said at last. "Sell your living years to the court, and use the proceeds to discharge the debt on your soul. Years for soul. Flesh-time for forever-time." She smiled, and it was the gentlest horrible thing Corin had ever seen. "It can be done. It has been done. Do you know why it's rare?"

"Because most people can't do the math?"

"Because most people can't survive the math." She leaned forward. "Your years are worth less each time you spend them, do you understand? A year sold at twenty-five is a rich year, full and dense. But the debt on a soul accrues interest, boy, and the interest accrues in *weight*. Every night you wait, the balance grows. So you'll sell more than you think, and then more than that, and the court will always offer you one more year's worth of time for one more decade's worth of life, because that is what courts are *for*." She folded her hands again. "Men have walked out of rooms like this one owing themselves down to a single winter. Some of them thanked us."

Corin's mouth had gone dry. "How much," he managed, "to move the turn by one moon?"

The broker named a number.

Corin had grown up in a merchant family. He knew numbers the way farmers knew weather. He understood immediately, viscerally, what the figure meant: not one year. Not five. The turn of a moon, deferred once, would cost him eight years. Eight years of mornings, of rain on roofs, of Ada's voice saying his name in the particular way she saved for when she thought he couldn't hear her worry.

Eight years for thirty days.

"The rate improves with volume," the broker said, watching him do exactly the math he was doing. "Defer four moons, and the per-moon price drops by half. Defer past the winter solstice, into the new cycle entirely, and—" she spread her hands, "—the court becomes creative. Restructuring. New instruments. There are men in this city who owe themselves in installments until their grandchildren inherit the balance."

"And if I bought enough time," Corin said slowly, "we might find another way. Void the seal. Burn the contract. Then none of it comes due at all, and I buy back—" 

"No." The word fell like a coin on marble. "Nothing sold to the court is ever bought back. Read any ledger you like. That is the first law of the Beneath, older than the church, older than the mountain: *the court does not refund.*" Her silver eyes held him. "You would be buying time to find a way to keep what you already sold. Understand that before you sign. Everyone thinks they're buying a bridge. They're buying a longer rope."

Behind the bead-curtains, the quills had gone quiet. All of them. Listening.

Corin looked at his hands. Merchant's son's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, soft from ledgers, harder now from the road. He thought of Ada asleep against a wall in their safehouse, sitting up even in sleep, sword within reach, guarding a man she'd known for a handful of weeks with a ferocity that frightened him more than the court did. He thought of the page in the prophecy book — tonight's date, his name, the word *dead* — and how far past that page they had already fought their way, and how the book had never once been wrong.

If the book was never wrong, then maybe the trick was not to escape the sentence. Maybe the trick was to change what the sentence *cost.*

"I want terms," he said. "In writing. Before I commit to anything."

"Of course." The broker drew a fresh slate toward her, and the dark wet surface shivered with something like eagerness. "But first, the customary question. Freely given means freely given — the court tests intent. Are you here of your own will, uncoerced, unbargained-for, with knowledge of what you surrender?"

And here was the trap, laid so neatly Corin almost admired it. Because he wasn't, quite. He was here because of love, which some clerk in some alcove would classify as coercion the moment it became inconvenient, and then the sale would be void and the debt would stand and he'd have confessed everything for nothing. Or — worse — because of Ada, specifically, whose sleeping breathing he could almost hear from three levels up, and if he spoke her name into this room of listening quills, it went into a ledger. Into the mountain. Into reach of High Cleric Ostrevan and every hunter the church still had in the city.

"Uncoerced," Corin said. "My own will. Full knowledge."

The broker studied him a moment longer, and Corin understood that she knew, that they always knew, that *freely given* in Karsthold was a technicality like *fair trade* had been in his grandfather's warehouses — a phrase that meant precisely whatever power needed it to mean. She wrote something on the slate. The surface drank the words.

"Come back tomorrow night with your decision," she said. "Terms will be drafted. Bring no one. Speak to no one." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Especially not the mercenary. Her kind complicates valuations. Oath-marked flesh carries encumbrances."

Cold moved up Corin's spine. "You know who I am."

"I know who travels with you." The broker resumed her folded-hands repose, a gray stone in a dead pool. "Everyone in the Sump knows. The church has priced her capture at a sum that would embarrass a duke. Which means, Mr. Ash, that every conversation you have in this city happens twice — once with you, and once with whoever is listening." She smiled again, gently, horribly. "Choose your next words knowing that."

* * *

He didn't go straight back.

He walked. The Sump's tunnels branched like veins and he let them take him, past coffin-rentals and memory-brokers and a shrine where a priest of nothing at all sold candles that burned with light but no heat, and by the time he surfaced near the cistern quarter, the thirteenth moon was up — waning now, visibly thinner each night, the mountain's clock ticking in the sky where everyone could watch it rob them.

Eleven nights. No. Ten, now. Midnight was close.

At the safehouse — a disused salt cellar with one door, two beds of straw, and Neme's maps pinned across every wall like the shed skins of her former life — Ada was awake. Of course she was. She sat sharpening a knife that was already sharp, the whetstone whispering in long even strokes, and when he came in she looked up with those gray eyes that missed nothing and said:

"You're late. Where were you?"

The lie assembled itself in Corin's mouth with the awful fluency of practice. "Walking. Couldn't sleep. This city counts at you even through the walls." He held up his hands, palms out, the merchant's gesture of open books. "I needed air that didn't smell of ink."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. And it curdled in his chest the moment it left him, because Ada nodded — nodded, trusted, went back to her whetstone — and Corin realized that this was how it began. Not with grand betrayals, not with masked priests and midnight contracts. With a small false sentence spoken to protect someone, and the discovery that protection and deception weighed exactly the same in the dark.

He lay down on his straw and stared at the ceiling and listened to her breathing even out into the shallow rhythm of a soldier's half-sleep, and he did the math one more time.

Ten nights. Eight years per moon deferred, halving at volume. If he sold deep — if he sold the way the court wanted him to sell, in fat installments, rope instead of bridge — he could push the turn past winter. Past spring. Long enough for the ledger-hall job. Long enough, if it failed, to try again. And when the rope ran out—

When the rope ran out, he would be a man of perhaps forty remaining years who had spent thirty of them, and Ada would be alive, and furious, and she would call him a fool in that voice she used for fools, and he would tell her it was the best bargain he ever struck.

He fell asleep composing the speech he'd give her someday, the confession he'd make after the danger passed, when the truth would be safe and forgivable. *I did it because I loved you. I hid it because you'd have stopped me. Forgive me.*

He never heard the whetstone stop.

He never saw Ada, in the dark, watching him with the expression of a woman who had spent her whole life reading people for a living and had just caught the exact moment a client began lying to himself — and recognized, with a cold drop in her stomach, the posture of a man negotiating with something.

Outside, the thirteenth moon thinned by another hair. In a dead pool three levels down, a slate remembered his name.

And in the morning, when Corin slipped out alone "for supplies," Ada counted his steps down the stairs — and followed.
