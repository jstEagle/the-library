## 12. The Weight of a Name

The undercity of Karsthold smelled of tallow and wet stone and old bargains, and for six days it had smelled, faintly and unforgivably, of lies.

I told myself I was imagining it. That was the trouble with a place like this — a city built downward instead of upward, where the torches burned green at the wicks and every shadow had a price tag — a place like this taught you to distrust your own senses. The air itself felt negotiated. Every breath came with terms attached.

But I had spent twenty years reading men the way other people read weather, and the signs on Corin Ash were as clear as stormcloud stacking over a ridge.

He slept badly — that wasn't new; nobody slept well down here, not with the moon thinning overhead like a coin being shaved by a patient thief. But his sleeping had changed its *shape*. He'd stopped talking in his sleep, first of all. A man carrying terror mumbles. A man carrying a plan goes quiet. Second: his hands. He'd taken to holding his left palm closed, curled around something small, the way a gambler holds dice he hasn't decided to throw. And third — third was the one that made my jaw ache from grinding my teeth — he'd started being *kind* to me in a particular way. The way you're kind to someone when you're memorizing them.

Neme saw it too. The disgraced scribe said nothing, which from Neme was a shout.

So on the sixth night, while Corin went out to "walk," which no sane person did in Karsthold's undercity after the second bell, I followed him.

The galleries down there were older than the soul-court above them, older than the church, older maybe than the mountain's patience. Corridors of black rock polished smooth by feet that had been dead for a thousand years, lit at intervals by lamps that burned without oil — court-lamps, fed on something I didn't want to name. Sound behaved strangely. My boots made noise; Corin's didn't seem to. It was as if the dark down there had already priced his footsteps and found them cheap.

He stopped at a door that looked like nothing — scarred wood, no handle, set into the rock as an afterthought. He knocked four times, paused, knocked twice more. A slot scraped open. Light the color of bruised honey fell across his face, and I watched my friend — because he was my friend now, gods help me, oath or no oath — watch him lean in and speak words I couldn't catch but whose *weight* I knew in my bones. I'd heard merchants talk like that in border towns. I'd heard mercenaries talk like that outside a dying man's tent, dividing up his gear before the breath finished leaving him.

Corin Ash was selling something. And there was only one thing he had left to sell.

When the door shut and he turned back up the gallery, I stepped out of the alcove I'd chosen and let him walk straight into me.

He did what honest men do when caught: he flinched, went white, and lied. "Ada. I couldn't sleep. Just walking—"

"Show me your hand."

"Ada—"

"*Your hand*, Corin."

His left fist uncurled slowly, like something blooming wrong. In his palm lay a slip of vellum gone soft as cloth from handling, covered in court-script, the ink a brownish red that I refused to look at too directly because I suspected it would look back.

Nine lines. I counted them without wanting to. Nine clauses. At the bottom, a signature that was unmistakably his — the same careful merchant's hand that wrote his name in the margins of the prophecy book, annotating dates like a man taking notes on his own execution.

"You sold your years." My voice came out flat. Flat was worse than loud. I could hear how flat it was and couldn't stop it. "How many?"

"Ada, it isn't—"

"*How many.*"

"...Fourteen," he whispered. "Fourteen years. To a broker named Sallow-Vetch. He holds them in escrow until the thirteenth turn. If the debt collects, they collect fourteen years short. If it doesn't—" His throat worked. "If it doesn't, I buy time. Another turn of the moon. Maybe two. Enough to find another way."

I hit the wall. Not him — the wall, my palm against black stone, because if I'd opened my hand on anything softer I'd have done damage neither of us could afford. Down here, in this city, violence had a market rate. Wounds could be bought and sold. I'd seen a man in the torch-market selling the memory of a broken arm to a collector who liked the *authenticity* of it.

"You hid this from me." Still flat. Still worse than screaming. "Six days. You've been negotiating for six days while we sat in that cellar and I mapped the ledger-hall and you sat there nodding, and the whole time you were selling yourself piece by piece in the dark."

"There is no *other way*!" It tore out of him, and the echo ran away down the gallery and came back sounding like several voices agreeing. He was shaking. "You've seen the archives, Ada. You've read the contract. My soul belongs to the court beneath the mountain, signed and sealed two generations before I drew breath. Twelve nights — eleven now — and the moon finishes its turn, and everything I am gets called due. So yes. Yes, I sold fourteen years. Years I won't live anyway if we fail. I bought us *time*, which is the only thing either of us has ever needed and never had—"

"Time bought with what?" I stepped closer. Close enough to see that the green torchlight had gotten into the whites of his eyes, a faint stain that hadn't been there last week. "You know what happens to people who trade with the court, Corin? You've walked through the torch-market. You've seen them. The ones who've sold so much they can't remember their mother's face, standing in doorways trying to remember why they're sad. It doesn't come off clean. Nothing down here comes off clean. Every clause costs more than it says."

"I'm not *them*. One bargain. Fourteen years. I did the arithmetic—"

"That's exactly what they're for!" The words cracked out of me before I understood them. "That's what the whole rotten edifice *is*, Corin. It's arithmetic. It's a machine that makes decent people do sums with their own souls and call it prudence. Your great-grandfather did arithmetic — one harvest, one bad winter, one signature. Where did it get your family? A hundred years of quiet dread and a prophecy book with your name in it! And now you're doing his sums again, in the same ink, in the same dark, and you think you're different because you read the contract first?"

He stared at me. And then — this is the part I will carry longer than the rest — he smiled. Small and terrible and tired.

"That's rich," he said softly, "coming from you."

"Don't."

"A mercenary lecturing me about the corruption of selling yourself. You've got a god's mark burned into your wrist, Ada. You've spent your whole life on contract. Oath for hire, sword for hire, conscience for hire — and when the god went silent you kept honoring it anyway, because what else is there? At least I know what I'm selling mine for." He held up the vellum between two fingers. "You don't even know if yours is buying anything at all."

The gallery went very quiet. Somewhere below us, water moved through channels cut for purposes no one living remembered, a sound like the mountain swallowing.

I should have walked away. Twenty years of discipline said walk away, cool off, come back with a level head and a plan. Instead I heard myself say, low and shaking: "My oath has never once asked me to feed myself to the thing that owns me."

"No," he said. "It just took your voice, and your valley, and every road out of your life, and you thanked it for the shape of the cage because at least the cage was *yours*."

I don't remember deciding to leave. I remember the cold of the stairs going up, and I remember that behind me Corin didn't call my name, and that somehow that was the worst wound of the night — that he'd learned already, in six days of trading in the undercity, not to bid against a closed door.

* * *

Neme found me two hours later in the lamplit crawl above the cisterns where we'd been sleeping, sitting with my back against damp stone, sharpening a knife I didn't need sharpened.

The disgraced court-scribe had a way of entering rooms that suggested the room had been expecting her. She settled onto a crate across from me, folded her long ink-stained hands, and waited the way archivists wait — with the confidence that all secrets eventually file themselves correctly.

"If you're here to tell me I was right about him," I said, "save it."

"Heaven forbid." Her voice was dry as old paper. "You were both insufferable. I listened from the stairwell. Occupational habit." She tilted her head. "You fought like a married couple, for what it's worth. Married couples fight about the *thing underneath* the thing. You weren't fighting about fourteen years."

"Neme."

"You were fighting about the fact that he's willing to spend himself for you, and you've never believed anyone would, and it terrifies you more than the moon does." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to, which was to say: too long. Then she leaned forward, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and I saw something in them I'd only seen once before — the night she'd first shown us the ledger-architecture of the soul-court. The look of a scholar who has found an error in a sacred text.

"Sit properly," she said. "I need to show you something, and I need you to be angry at *me* afterward instead of at each other, because anger is useful fuel and despair is just wet wood."

I put down the knife. She unrolled a copy — hers, in her own careful hand, made from memory during our nights in the archives — of the original contract. Corin's soul, sold by Aldous Ash, great-grandfather, in the year the valley's borders were drawn. I'd read it a dozen times. I knew its clauses like scars: the consideration, the term, the collection date keyed to the moon's thirteenth turn.

"What am I looking at?"

"The witnesses." Her finger touched the bottom edge, where three seals sat stamped in wax gone dark as liver. "Every contract held by the soul-court requires three things to bind. Consent of the seller — present, genuine, Aldous Ash signed in full knowledge. Consent of the buyer — the court beneath the mountain, sealed by its registrar. And consent of the *overseeing power*." Her finger stopped on the middle seal. "Every transaction in the mortal world must be witnessed by a power competent to guarantee it. For contracts touching souls, there is precisely one such power in these lands."

"The Vessel-Who-Waits," I said. My mouth had gone dry.

"The Vessel-Who-Waits." She nodded slowly. "Whose church, conveniently, provided the witnessing priest. Whose seal appears right here." Her nail traced the sigil — the cup and the waiting hands — and then she turned the parchment over, and produced from her coat a second document, brittle, browned, and very old.

"This is a genuine witness-seal of the Vessel-Who-Waits, affixed to a grain-tithe arbitration from sixty years before your friend's contract. I stole it from the archive myself, ten years ago, the night before they stripped my rank. Look at them side by side."

I looked.

I am not a scribe. But I have spent twenty years studying the fine print of other people's promises, and I know forgery the way a shepherd knows a limping sheep — not by study but by the thing moving *wrong*. The genuine seal was deep, confident, pressed with the weight of authority; the lines of the cup radiated outward from a single unwavering center. The seal on Corin's contract was shallow. Hesitant. And the cup's rim —

"The rim breaks," I said slowly. "On the real one, the circle closes. On his... there's a gap. Here, at the top. Like the cup is open."

"Like the cup is open," Neme repeated softly. "An open vessel receives. A closed one refuses. Every initiate learns it before they learn their letters. Whoever pressed that seal — and it *was* pressed by human hands, Ostrevan's predecessor's hands, I'd stake my remaining reputation on it — whoever pressed it knew the theology perfectly and the seal imperfectly. Because you cannot press a closing cup into wax unless the closing cup consents to be pressed." She sat back. "And it didn't."

The cistern dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping a slow count.

"Say it plainly," I said. "No archivist language. Plain words."

She folded her hands again, and for the first time since I'd known her, Neme looked afraid of what she was about to say — not of the saying, but of what it would do to us.

"Plainly, then. The contract is void. Or near enough to void that the difference is academic. A soul-debt witnessed by a forged divine consent is not a binding instrument; it is theft wearing a contract's clothes. The court has enforced it for a hundred years because no one ever checked, because no one *dared* check, because the church's authority is the load-bearing wall of the entire edifice. But the law of the court — the true law, the deep law, older than the clergy and older than their prison — the true law requires that seal to be genuine. It isn't." She met my eyes. "The Vessel-Who-Waits never consented to the sale of Corin Ash's soul. Which means the debt doesn't exist. Which means the only thing standing between Corin and freedom is a piece of paper that says otherwise — locked in the ledger-hall, guarded by men whose entire power depends on that paper never being read closely."

I sat very still. Through the stone above us, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt that wrong burning from my oath-mark, the sensation that had followed me out of the Hollow Valley — the sense of something vast pressing against the inside of a wall.

*He never consented.* A century of silence, and the silence had a shape now. Not absence. Refusal. A god whose cup stayed open while his priests pressed a closed lie into the wax of the world.

"Then why," I said, "does the moon still collect? Why does the prophecy book still say his name beside the word *dead*?"

"Because the moon keeps the court's schedule, not the court's justice," Neme said. "And because paper, enforced long enough, becomes indistinguishable from truth. Unless—" and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the fear in it turned into something harder, brighter, "unless someone puts the original contract and a genuine comparison-seal in front of the Registrar of Debts before the thirteenth turn completes, and forces the court's own law to eat its own children. Which requires stealing the original from the ledger-hall. Which requires—" she glanced toward the stairwell, toward the direction Corin had gone, and something in her expression gentled by a single degree, "—which requires all three of us, including the fool downstairs who just sold fourteen years of himself to buy us the time to do it."

I looked down at my wrists. The oath-mark pulsed, faint and wrong and, for the first time, *familiar* — like a voice through a door I'd always assumed was a wall.

"Get Corin," I said. "Tell him we strike the ledger-hall in three nights, while the brokers are distracted by the pre-collection markets. Tell him—" I stopped. Started again. "Tell him the next thing he sells, he sells *with* me in the room. Both of us sign, or neither of us does. That's not an order. That's a counter-offer."

Neme rose, gathering her papers, and paused at the edge of the lamplight.

"For the record," she said, "he'll take that deal. Men like him always do. They sell themselves precisely because they assume no one else will ever offer."

She went down the stairs. Above me, through a hundred feet of mountain, the thirteenth-turn moon hung invisible and patient, and beneath my skin, in the old burnt letters of a silenced god's mark, something that was not silence at all pressed closer to the wall — as if it had heard the word *forged*, and was waiting, with the whole weight of a century, to see what we would do about it.
