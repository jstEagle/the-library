## 13. Breaking Into the Ledger-Hall

We spent two days learning the shape of our own failure before we attempted it.

Neme drew the ledger-hall from memory on a tabletop of cracked slate, her chalk-stub fingers tracing lines that seemed to ache as she made them. She had been a court-scribe for eleven years. Eleven years of copying debts into permanence, and then one afternoon she had written a clause that read too much like mercy, and the court had stripped her name from its rolls and sold it back to her at interest.

"They'll expect thieves at the doors," she said, tapping the great arched entry where torchlight pooled like spilled honey. "The Doors are a performance. Guards, gilding, ceremony — all of it meant to make you believe the wealth is kept *there*, behind the marble, so you never ask where it's really kept." Her chalk moved inward, to the heart of the plan she'd drawn. "The ledgers themselves are in the Understacks. And the Understacks breathe through the ash-chutes."

"The ash-chutes," I repeated.

"Every soul the court consumes leaves something behind. Ash, technically. Residue. The clerks call it *leavings*." She said the word the way you'd say a curse in polite company. "It's carried up through chutes in the mountain's ribs and dumped into the foundry district. Nobody guards them because nobody wants to be near them."

Nobody wants to be near them. That was the whole architecture of Karsthold, I was beginning to understand. Every cruelty in the city was built on the same foundation: people's willingness to look away from suffering if looking toward it cost them comfort.

Corin studied the map with his jaw tight. He had been quiet since Neme told us about substitution — since the word *freely given* had settled over our little band like frost. He kept touching his wrist where the debt sat invisible, the way a man touches an old scar he's decided not to talk about.

"And once we're in the stacks?" he asked.

"Find the original contract. Your family's name. Vane — no, sorry — *Ash*. The founding instrument of your debt." Neme's mouth went thin. "Everything else is copies. Copies can be argued with, amended, appealed. The original is the debt itself. Steal the original and the moon has nothing to collect against."

"And if we're caught?"

Neme looked at him for a long moment. Then at me. Whatever she saw in my face made her answer honestly, which I was starting to understand was her way of being kind.

"If you're caught in the Understacks, they won't bother with trial. A soul-writ goes out on your head within the hour. Every collector in the city is licensed to take you — not kill you, understand. Take you. There's a difference, and the difference is worse."

I folded the slate-map flat and put it inside my coat, against my heart, which felt less like sentiment than tactics. I had done eleven years of contracts and I knew how jobs died: not in the daring middle but in the sloppy edges. So I made us rehearse. I made Corin climb the foundry wall until his hands bled and climb it again. I made Neme recite the clerk-handshakes and pass-phrases until she could do them drunk, or asleep, or terrified — because on the night, she would be all three.

And I did what I always did before a job, which was sit alone in the dark and count the ways I might die, and decide, each time, that I could live with it.

There were more ways than usual this time. But underneath all of them, quiet as water under ice, ran a thought I didn't examine too closely: that some part of me had already decided this wasn't a contract anymore. Contracts end. Whatever this was, I suspected it wouldn't.

* * *

We went in during the change of shift, in the dead hour between the third bell and the fourth, when the foundry district's fires bank low and even the collectors go somewhere warm to drink.

The ash-chute mouth was exactly as foul as promised. It opened out of the mountainside like a wound, crusted black around the rim, exhaling a slow breath of cold air that smelled of burnt hair and old coins. Not smoke. Something older than smoke. I stood at its lip and felt my oath-mark stir against my wrist — that faint wrong pulse I'd carried since leaving the Hollow — and for one heartbeat it quickened, like a sleeper turning over.

*Not now,* I told it, and it subsided, sullen.

I went first. Of course I went first; that was the entire point of being me. The chute sloped down into the mountain at an angle that punished the spine, its walls slick with leavings that came away on my palms in greasy ribbons. Behind me Corin breathed through his mouth in careful, disciplined pulls, and behind him Neme whispered the way-count — *"forty paces to the junction, junction turns left, left runs level"* — because a scribe's mind was a better lantern than any flame we dared not carry.

Level, at last. We crouched in a gallery of dressed stone where the chute met the mountain's proper bones, and here Neme lit the stub of candle she'd earned the right to carry, cupping its light like a bird she was afraid would fly.

The Understacks opened before us.

Gods below. I have seen the vaults of merchant princes and the treasury of a duke who minted his own face on every coin, and none of it — none of it — prepared me for the shelves.

They rose into darkness beyond the candle's reach, iron shelving bolted into living rock, and on every shelf: ledgers. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Bound in leather gone soft with handling, spines labeled in gold that never tarnished because it wasn't gold, because nothing in this place was ever quite what it claimed. And beneath the smell of dust and old vellum there ran another smell, faint and sweetish, that I refused to name until Neme named it for me.

"That's what a soul smells like when it's been filed," she whispered. "You get used to it." She hadn't. Eleven years later, her voice still caught.

We moved along the aisles the way she directed — third gallery down, western spur, the founding-contracts. Our footsteps made no sound on stone worn glassy by a century of clerks' shoes, as if the mountain itself had learned silence from my god's long absence.

Corin found the shelf before Neme did. He stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into his back, and when I followed his eyes I understood: the ledger bore his family name stamped deep in the spine. *ASH. FOUNDING INSTRUMENTS.* His hand rose toward it and hovered, trembling, not touching, the way you hover over the face of someone sleeping whom you love and resent in equal measure.

"Ada." His voice came out strange. "It's just a book. It's just — a book, and it's my whole family in it."

"It's a book," I agreed quietly. "Books can be stolen."

That steadied him. That was the mercenary in me talking, and I hated how much it helped.

He lifted it down. It was heavier than it should have been — I saw his arms take the weight and adjust, surprised — and when he laid it on the reading-ledge and opened it, the pages fell open to a place near the front as though the book remembered being read and wanted to spare us the search.

The founding contract. His great-grandfather's signature, cramped and desperate. The sum: one soul, payable at the thirteenth turn of the appointed moon, descending through generations to the last bearer of the name. And beside it, pressed into the wax in a seal I recognized with a sick lurch —

The Vessel-Who-Waits. My god's mark, witnessing the sale.

"No," Corin breathed. "No, look at it, look at the —"

"I see it."

My god had witnessed the enslavement of this boy's line. My silent, absent, mourned god had stood witness while a desperate man signed away his descendants forever. Everything I had believed about my oath — that it bound me to something holy even in its silence — tilted, groaned, threatened to come down.

But Neme was leaning close, and her brow had furrowed in a way I'd learned to read. Scribes notice things.

"Look again," she murmured. "Closer. Ada — hold the candle lower."

I held it lower. And in the small ring of light, I saw what a century of clerks had either missed or chosen not to see.

The seal was wrong. The god's mark was right in every detail — the waiting vessel, the seven rings, the downward flow — except one. The innermost ring, the ring that in every true impression of the Vessel's seal represented consent, the god's own yes, was broken. Not worn. Not damaged. *Drawn broken.* Whoever had pressed this seal had used a forgery cut deliberately with the consent-ring open, because the true seal — the god's actual, consenting mark — had never touched this wax.

"He never agreed," Neme whispered, and her voice shook with the enormity of a scribe finding an error at the root of everything. "The church brokered the sale, but the Vessel never sealed it. The contract is — Ada, by the court's own law, the contract is voidable. It's been voidable for a hundred years. They just needed no one to ever look."

For a moment none of us breathed. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if it too was afraid to move.

Then Corin laughed — a small, cracked, disbelieving sound — and reached out to touch the broken ring, and the moment his fingertip met the wax, the ledger-hall screamed.

Not the building. The *contract*. Light poured up from the page like blood from a wound, white and total, and every shelf in the Understacks shuddered, and from somewhere far above came a sound I had only heard described in Neme's bitter stories: the tolling of the writ-bell, the alarm that meant a soul-writ was being drawn.

*"Run,"* Neme said, and it was not a whisper anymore.

We ran. The book Corin clutched to his chest like a child, and I took the lead back up the gallery because running-toward-danger-in-front was the one vow I had never needed a god to swear. Behind us, lights bloomed — clerks' lanterns swinging out of side galleries, voices raised in the flat, terrible calm of an institution that had done this ten thousand times.

"The chute's watched now," I gasped. "They'll seal it —"

"Then we don't use the chute." Neme grabbed my sleeve and hauled me sideways into an aisle I hadn't mapped. "Foundry freight-lift. It carries leavings up. Tonight it can carry us."

The lift shaft roared with wind from below, that sweetish soul-smell thick enough to taste. I got Corin onto the platform, then Neme, then dropped through the closing gap myself as the first pursuing lantern rounded into our aisle, and heard — rising past us out of the dark — the writ-bell tolling our names.

Our names. Not descriptions. *Names.* Which meant somewhere in those burning pages, our souls had been entered into the court's books as assets pending collection.

As the lift climbed toward the foundry's orange glow, Corin leaned against the cage-wall, the stolen contract crushed between us, and laughed again — that same cracked sound, but harder now, edged with something that frightened me more than the bells did.

"We have it," he said. "Ada, we *have* it. It's voidable. All of it —"

The bell tolled again, closer now, and through the lift cage's lattice I could see them gathering in the streets below: collectors, dozens of them, their lanterns converging on the foundry district with the unhurried certainty of men being paid by the hour.

"Yes," I said, and put my back to the grate, and drew my knife. "Now we just have to keep breathing long enough to prove it."
