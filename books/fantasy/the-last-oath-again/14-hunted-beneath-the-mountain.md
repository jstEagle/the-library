## 14. Hunted Beneath the Mountain

### Ada

The galleries beneath Karsthold had been carved by hands that did not fear the dark.

Ada understood this within the first hundred steps, because the dark down there was not empty. It had texture. It pressed against her skin like wet wool, and it smelled of cold stone and old tallow and something sweeter underneath — the scent of souls in storage, Neme had said, like honey left too long in a sealed jar. She had laughed when he said it. She was not laughing now.

Behind them, three levels up, the alarm-bells of the ledger-hall were still ringing. Not the sharp civic bells of the upper city that meant fire or flood; these were lower, slower, a sound she felt in her back teeth rather than heard with her ears. A soul-writ, Neme had called it as they ran. *When they sound that bell, it means someone's name is loose in the mountain. It means every debt-collector in Karsthold can touch you without paperwork.* He had said it while shoving them through a door marked with a sigil Ada didn't recognize, and then he had locked the door behind all three of them and kept running, and Ada had not had time to think about what *touch you without paperwork* meant until now, when she could hear footsteps that made no sound at all coming through stone that should have swallowed them.

Corin stumbled ahead of her, one hand trailing the wall for balance, the stolen contract wrapped in oilcloth and strapped flat against his chest like a second heartbeat. In the guttering light of her lantern his face was grey, sweat-slick, twenty years older than he'd been nine nights ago. The moon above the mountain was a worn coin now, one night from vanishing entirely — and on the night it vanished, it would turn its thirteenth turn, and the court below would come to collect what his family had sold before he drew breath.

"Left," Ada breathed. "There's a junction. Stay left."

"How do you know?"

"I don't. But right feels wrong." She touched his elbow to steer him. "Trust me. Right always feels wrong down here. That's how you know."

It was an old mercenary trick, half superstition and half sense — the body knows when a passage is watched, even if the mind doesn't. She had learned it escorting caravans through passes where bandits waited. She had never imagined using it beneath a city built out of other people's souls, hunted by clerics of the god she had sworn her life to, carrying the proof that her church had brokered a child's soul four generations before the child was born.

The contract against Corin's chest was the reason for everything now. The original. The forged witness-seal — the Vessel-Who-Waits's own mark, signed by a hand that was not his. If they could get it out of the mountain, get it to someone who mattered, the debt would collapse like a roof with its beam pulled. That had been the plan, back when the plan still had room for things like *out* and *someone*.

Now there was only down, and dark, and the silent bell tolling in their teeth.

They came to the junction. Left sloped gently downward, the walls slicked with condensation, the air warmer. Right climbed toward the upper galleries, toward daylight, toward the church quarter where Ostrevan's hunters moved in white-and-grey like fish in frosted water. Ada stood at the fork for three heartbeats, hating both options equally, and that was when she realized Neme was no longer behind them.

She turned. The passage they had come through stretched back into blackness, empty. His lantern-light, which had bobbed thirty paces back for the last hour, was gone.

"Neme," she whispered, and the word fell into the dark and did not echo. Sound behaved strangely down here. It went where it was paid to go.

*He lagged,* said something cold and pragmatic in her head, the voice she thought of as the contract-voice, the voice that had kept her alive through eleven years of jobs that should have killed her. *He's old. He's slow. He knew the risks when he showed you the loophole. Keep moving.*

But Neme had also been the one who told them about the forged seal. Who had lost his position, his name-entry, nearly his soul, for keeping records the court wanted erased. Who had looked at Corin across a guttering lamp and said, *I have spent forty years writing down what powerful people take from weak ones. Let me write one thing down that gives something back.*

"Stay here," she told Corin. "Don't move. Don't breathe loudly."

"Ada—"

"*Stay.*"

She went back into the dark alone.

* * *

### Corin

Corin counted to two hundred, and then he stopped counting, because counting made him think of turns of the moon, and thinking of turns of the moon made his chest tight enough to crack ribs.

He stood in the junction with his back against stone that pulsed faintly, very faintly, like something asleep. The whole mountain was like that, he'd realized. Alive in a way mountains weren't supposed to be. Every gallery breathed. Every shadow had weight. And somewhere far below, past floors he couldn't number, the court sat in its torchlit market buying years off desperate people, and his name sat in their ledgers like a coin waiting to be picked up.

*One night,* he thought. *One more night of the moon, and I'm theirs.*

Unless the contract was void. Unless the forged seal meant what Neme swore it meant — that no god had ever consented to hold his soul in escrow, that the whole architecture of his damnation rested on a lie written by a clerk with good penmanship and bad faith. Unless they got the paper out of the mountain and into hands that could act on it.

Unless Ada came back.

He listened. The silence down here wasn't silence; it was a held note, a chord sustained so long it had become part of the air. He strained against it, trying to separate footstep from drip, breath from draft. Once — twice — he thought he heard voices, thin and flattened, like words spoken through water. He couldn't tell whose.

His hand found the oilcloth over his heart. Through it, the contract's edges pressed against his fingers, patient as a knife.

Here was the thing he hadn't told her, the thing sitting in him like a swallowed stone: nine nights ago, hiding in the undercity while she scouted the archive district, he had gone looking for a scribe of his own. A cheap one. A gray-market one. He had asked, in careful, casual words, what his remaining years might fetch — how much time a man could sell and still walk, still talk, still be mostly himself. Enough, the scribe had guessed, to buy the court's patience. To push the collection date past the thirteenth turn. To buy Ada time to find another way, so she wouldn't have to stand between him and a debt that had teeth.

Ada had found out anyway. Of course she had. They had fought like animals in the dark of a rented room, and afterward she had held him so hard his ribs ached, and neither of them had said the true thing, which was: *I would rather be damned than watch you spend yourself buying my safety.*

And now she had gone back into the dark for Neme, and he was standing in a junction doing nothing, and the bell was tolling, and every debt-collector in Karsthold could touch them without paperwork.

He heard footsteps then — real ones, hers, quick and uneven — and relief hit him so hard his knees nearly buckled.

Then he saw her face in the lantern-light, and the relief died where it stood.

* * *

### Ada

They took him at the third stair.

That was what Neme's trail had told her, once she found it: his lantern, set down neatly on a step, still burning. A scrap of his coat sleeve, torn, not cut. A smear of blood on the wall at exactly the height of a man's shoulder, dragged sideways. He had fought. Briefly, badly, with a scholar's strength against whatever the court sent — and then the fight stopped, and the stairs were clean again except for the small honest evidence he had left her, deliberately, the way he'd left everything: a record. *They took me going up. Three of them, maybe four. Toward the ledger-hall.*

Going up. Not down to the deep vaults, where souls were stored like wine. Up, toward the halls of record, where a captured scribe who knew about forged seals was worth more conscious than otherwise. Which meant Neme was alive. Which meant Neme had hours, maybe less, before the questioning began — and the court's questioning, everyone in Karsthold knew, involved opening people the way accountants opened books.

Ada stood at the bottom of the stair and let the contract-voice make its argument. *Three of us move faster than two weighed down with rescue plans. He'd tell you to run. He'd insist. He'd probably quote precedent.*

And then, quieter, in a voice that wasn't the contract-voice at all: *You've spent eleven years keeping your word to strangers who paid you. Here is a man who gave you everything for free, lying bleeding on a stair because he helped you. What are you, if you leave him?*

Something shifted in her chest. It didn't feel like a decision. It felt like a bone setting.

She went back to Corin.

"He's alive," she said. "They're taking him to the ledger-hall. For questioning."

Corin's jaw worked. "If we go after him—"

"The writ's already on us. We're already dead men walking until the moon turns or the contract breaks. So we break it tonight, or we die tomorrow, and either way—" She heard herself, and stopped, and started again, slower. "Either way I'm not leaving him on a stair."

"Ada. The moon is *tomorrow*."

"I know what the moon is doing, Corin." Her voice came out flat and strange, and she realized she wasn't afraid anymore, and that frightened her more than anything else had all week. Fear had been her compass for eleven years. It told her when to run, when to fold, when a job was too hot. It was gone now, burned away somewhere on that bloody stair, and what was left underneath felt enormous and calm and absolutely unwilling to negotiate.

"They'll expect us to run," she said. "Down. Out. Everyone runs from the ledger-hall; that's why it works, that's why nobody's ever broken into it twice. So we don't run." She pulled the lantern up, and its light caught the junction's branching passages, and she chose the one that climbed. "We go up. We walk into the hall where they keep every stolen thing in this mountain. And we take back the scribe, the contract, and every other name on those shelves, or we die in the attempt — but we do it *on purpose*, do you understand me? Not as cargo. Not as debt. As people."

Corin stared at her. Then, slowly, something kindled behind his eyes that she'd seen once before, in a border tavern, on the first night — not hope exactly. Resolve's rougher cousin.

"On purpose," he repeated.

"On purpose."

They climbed.

* * *

### Corin

The upper galleries near the ledger-hall were different. Warmer. Lit at intervals by torches that burned without flickering, fed on something steadier than pitch. And the walls — Corin tried not to look at the walls, and failed.

Names. Thousands of names, inked directly into the stone in a script that seemed to shift when he wasn't focusing on it, each one faintly luminous, each one humming at a pitch just below hearing. Some of the names were dim, worn almost smooth, ancient entries. Some blazed fresh and new.

"That's what a soul looks like when it's owned," Ada murmured beside him. "Neme told me. The court doesn't keep souls in jars. It keeps them in the walls. The whole mountain is a ledger."

Corin walked slowly along one stretch of stone, reading names he didn't know, and felt them read him back. Somewhere in these walls was a shelf — a line, a stroke of ink — that bore the name *Ash*. Four generations of interest accruing. He put his palm flat against the rock, feeling its slow pulse, and spoke before he could stop himself, low, barely voiced:

"When we break this place open, I want every one of you to go home."

The nearest name flickered. Just once. Like a candle catching a draft. Or like something answering.

"Corin." Ada's hand closed on his shoulder. "Movement. Torchlight. Two collectors, coming around the bend — we take them quiet or we take them fast, but Neme's questioning starts at midnight bell and it's near that now."

They flattened into an alcove. The collectors passed — tall figures in court-grey, faces smooth and slightly wrong, like portraits painted by someone who had only ever had descriptions. Between them, limping, wrists bound in cord that glowed faintly gold, walked Neme.

He looked terrible. His lip was split, one eye swollen shut, his scholar's robe torn at the shoulder where the blood-smear on the stair had been born. But his head was up. And as the collectors marched him past the alcove, his one working eye swept the shadows with the practiced, cataloguing precision of a man who had spent forty years indexing things — and found them.

He didn't cry out. He didn't slow. He simply said, in a clear dry voice pitched perfectly to carry ten feet and no farther:

"Aisle nine, sub-level two. The Ash contract. Cross-referenced under *Ash*, *Vane*, and *forgery, ecclesiastical*. Everything you need is filed together. They were always too arrogant to separate the evidence from the theft."

The collectors jerked, startled by speech where there should have been silence — and in that heartbeat of inattention Ada moved.

She came out of the alcove like winter coming off the Hollow Valley, low and total. The first collector dropped before it finished turning. The second got a hand up, and golden light crawled along its fingers — collector's magic, paperwork-made-flesh, some writ of seizure half-spoken — and then Corin did the bravest stupid thing of his life and slammed the oilcloth-wrapped contract against the creature's reaching hand.

The effect was immediate and obscene. The collector screamed — a sound like tearing vellum — and came apart into drifting motes of pale light, unbound, unwritten, its own authority revoked by contact with the one document in the mountain it existed to protect.

Silence rushed back in.

Neme swayed against the wall, bound hands braced on stone, staring at the fading motes. "Well," he rasped, through the ruin of his mouth. "That's going in the record."

Ada was already cutting his bonds. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk, I can limp, and I can lecture. All three simultaneously, if required." He flexed his freed fingers, wincing. "Listen to me, both of you, quickly, because they will know within minutes and the whole floor will come. Midnight bell starts the questioning — but midnight bell also opens the hall for the *turn*. Tomorrow night the moon completes itself, and the court must conduct the collection publicly, formally, before witnesses. Protocol. Even monsters keep protocol. Which means tonight, right now, the hall is staffed thin and every clerk is preparing tomorrow's ceremony instead of watching the shelves."

"So we go now," Ada said.

"So you go now." Neme's good eye fixed on her, and something passed across his ruined face that was older than humor. "And I go with you, because I am not spending my last free hour in a corridor. Aisle nine. Sub-level two. Bring me the Ash file, and bring me the *witness register* — the master book of consents, every seal the church ever claimed the god approved. If we're proving forgery, we prove it against the original standard, or Ostrevan's lawyers will unpick us by dawn."

"Ostrevan," Ada said quietly. The name landed in the corridor like a dropped coin.

"You've met his work," Neme said. "You haven't met *him*. Pray you keep it that way." He straightened as best he could, one hand pressed to his ribs, and nodded down the gallery toward a vast door at its end, banded in bronze, breathing warm light through its seams. Beyond it, faintly, Corin could hear voices — dozens of them, clerks chanting preparations, the machinery of tomorrow's collection already turning.

"One aisle," Neme said. "One register. One chance. After you, Vane."

Ada rolled her shoulders. Corin watched her do it — watched the last mercenary habit fall away from her like a shed coat, watched what remained settle into place, still and certain and terribly bright.

"After me," she agreed.

She pushed the doors open, and the light of the ledger-hall poured over them like a tide, and somewhere far above, unseen through a mile of living stone, the moon slipped a little thinner, a little closer to gone.
