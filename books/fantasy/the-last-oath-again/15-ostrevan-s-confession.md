## 15. Ostrevan's Confession

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first insult. Ada Vane had been captured before — twice by bandits, once by a river-lord whose daughter she'd stolen back from an arranged marriage, once by her own employer when a contract went sour in ways that were not, strictly speaking, her fault. Every one of them had bound her hands, because every one of them had been afraid of them. But the clerics who took her at the mouth of the pilgrim stair simply walked on either side of her, lanterns swinging, and left her arms loose at her sides as though she were not a prisoner but a guest who had forgotten herself.

The chains they put on were elsewhere. She felt them the moment the church's wardens closed the iron collar around her throat: a cold weight that was not weight, a hush laid over her like wet cloth. The oath-mark on her wrist went silent beneath it — truly silent, for the first time since she'd crossed the Hollow's border, and she understood with a lurch that this was what the collar was for. Not her hands. Her wrist.

*You knew,* she thought at the mark, at whatever slept beneath it. *All this time, you knew where you were.*

The stair descended into the mountain in long spirals cut from living stone, and the deeper they went, the warmer the air grew, until Ada was sweating inside her leathers and the lantern-light turned amber and soft as candle-fat. Hymns drifted up from below — hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, chanting the old cadences she had learned as a child in the valley chapel, the ones that ended each verse on a held note of pure waiting. *The Vessel-Who-Waits. The Voice-That-Rests. The God-Who-Sleeps Until the World Is Ready.*

She had sung those verses every morning of her first twelve years. She had never once wondered why a god who rested needed so many people awake to sing him down.

* * *

The room they brought her to was round, and wrong.

Not grand — that was the second surprise. Ada had imagined Ostrevan's sanctum as a hall of gold and bone-white marble, the kind of place that made you feel small by architecture alone. Instead it was cramped, low-ceilinged, lit by a single brazier whose coals glowed a color she had no name for, somewhere between ember-red and bruise-violet. Every wall was covered in shelves, and every shelf was crammed with ledgers — hundreds of them, thousands, spines cracked, covers swollen with damp, some so old their leather had gone green as pondwater. The smell was dust and tallow and something underneath both, faint but unmistakable once she caught it: blood, very old, soaked into wood and dried there for generations.

High Cleric Ostrevan sat behind a desk that might have been salvaged from a fishmonger — scarred oak, ink-stained, one leg shimmed with a folded prayer card. He was smaller than he had seemed from across the cathedral nave in Karsthold, thinner, an old man in plain gray wool with ink to his knuckles and reading spectacles pushed up into thin white hair. He looked less like the keeper of a god's rites than like a clerk who had stayed too many years past his retirement.

"Sit," he said, and gestured at a stool across the desk. "Please."

Ada sat. She kept her wrists visible, her shoulders loose, her weight forward on the balls of her feet. Old habits. The stool was bolted to the floor; she noted that without surprise.

"You're not what I expected," she said.

"No one ever is." He set down the pen he'd been holding. It left a small black star on the blotter. "You expected a monster. A man in vestments heavy enough to hide a knife, a voice like a landslide. I know. I've read the reports of everyone we've brought down here. They all describe me afterward, and they're all disappointed." He smiled, and it was a tired, genuine smile, and somehow that was worse than a landslide would have been. "Ada Vane. Sworn of the Vessel-Who-Waits, marked at seven years old, oath renewed at fourteen, orphaned at nine — your mother died in the birthing fever year, yes? And your father—" He stopped himself, gently. "Forgive me. I've read your file. It's a bad habit of my office. Reading people like ledgers."

"Is that what I am? An entry?"

"You are," said Ostrevan, "a debt that has finally come due. As is young Corin Ash. As, if I am honest, am I." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve with the absent care of a man performing a ritual he no longer believed in. "But you didn't come all this way to hear me apologize for my filing habits."

"I came because your hunters dragged me here in irons."

"In a chair, actually. Warden Brannic mentioned you complained about the chair." The smile again. Then it faded, and what was underneath it made the room feel colder than any mountain dark could manage. "I'm going to tell you the truth now, child. All of it. Not because you've earned it — you haven't had time — and not because I'm brave. Because I am a coward who has spent ninety-one years being careful, and I find, at the end, that I cannot stand the weight of it alone anymore. That is the whole of my confession's motive, and I won't dress it up as virtue."

Ada said nothing. Outside the round walls, faintly, the hymn-singing rose and fell. And beneath it — so faint she might have imagined it, except that she had been hearing it for days now through the burning of her own skin — came the other sound. The one under everything. Like wind through a crack too narrow to see.

Ostrevan heard it too. She watched him flinch.

"You hear him," he said quietly.

"The pulse on my wrist. Since I left the valley. It's gotten louder every night."

"Not the pulse." His voice had gone hoarse. "The screaming."

* * *

He told it plainly, which was the only mercy in it. No incense-words, no scripture-cadence. A clerk's confession, itemized.

A century ago, the Vessel-Who-Waits had not been silent. He had been *loud*. A god of oaths and debts and justice owed — not the soft, abstract justice of hymns, but the grinding, personal kind. When a lord cheated his laborers, the Vessel spoke, and the laborers heard, and they came with torches not to burn but to *collect*. When a merchant sold a family's land out from under them, the Vessel weighed the theft aloud in the marketplace, in the cheater's own voice, so all could hear what he had done. He did not smite. He *itemized*. He made every hidden sin a public ledger-entry, and he empowered the wronged to balance it themselves.

"And do you know what happens," Ostrevan said, staring into the violet coals, "to a church when its god speaks directly to every washerwoman and ditch-digger in the land? What need have they of us? We were priests of the Voice. And then the Voice began speaking to everyone, and we discovered we were not shepherds. We were middlemen. And middlemen, Miss Vane, are the first casualties of any honest economy."

"So you gagged him."

"We *imprisoned* him." The words came out flat, dead, rehearsed by solitude. "Nine high clerics of that age. It took them eleven years to prepare. There was a rite — you'll not find it written anywhere; we burned it — that turns a god's own covenant against him. Every oath ever sworn in his name became a chain-link. Every soul marked with his sigil became a lock. Do you understand what that means?" He looked at her then, really looked, and his eyes were wet and ancient. "Your mark, girl. The mark you've borne since you were seven, the one you prayed over every night, the one that made you feel *held* — it is a shackle. You are one link among millions. Every faithful soul in the valley and beyond is a brick in his cell. We built his prison out of his own love for us."

The brazier popped. Somewhere above, the hymn swelled toward its held note of waiting.

"He fought," Ostrevan went on, softer. "For the first decades, he fought like a storm. The mountain shook. Three of the nine died holding the seal. And then..." He spread his ink-stained hands. "Then the silence. The silence everyone remembers. The silence we taught the world to call peace. He did not go quiet, Miss Vane. He went *under*. Deep enough that even we stopped hearing him. For sixty years I told myself he had forgiven us. Or forgotten. Or that gods simply sleep." He laughed, and it was the ugliest sound Ada had ever heard from a human throat. "And then, four years ago, I was cataloguing the deep archive, and I passed the seal-chamber, and I heard him."

"What does a god sound like," Ada asked, "when he screams?"

Ostrevan was quiet for a long moment.

"Like you," he said finally. "Like someone who trusted the wrong people completely, and has just enough breath left to say so."

* * *

She should have killed him then. She recognized that later, turning it over in the dark of the cell they gave her — recognized it the way you recognize a missed footing on a cliff-edge after you've already fallen past. Her hands were unbound. The collar muffled the mark but not her arms, and she had killed better-guarded men than this ink-stained old clerk with a borrowed quill. The plan — Corin's plan, Neme's plan, the fragile thing they'd built in the undercity — would have been simpler with Ostrevan dead.

Instead she heard herself ask: "Why tell me this? Why now? What do you want?"

"Want?" He seemed genuinely to consider it. "I want to stop paying the interest on my principal. That's ledger-talk; forgive me. It means—" He rubbed his eyes. "It means the court beneath this city, the soul-market, the ledger-hall your clever scribe friend has been telling you about. You think that grew up *near* our church. It didn't. It grew out of us. When we silenced the god of debts, the debts did not disappear. They only lost their referee. So we became the referee. The clergy brokered the first soul-contracts personally — including, yes, the Ash contract, including the forged witness-seal you've found or soon will; Ostrevan's own hand, three generations back, though not mine, thank every mercy, not mine — and the court we built to administer them became rich, and the church that owned it became powerful, and the power required more silencing, and the silencing required more power. A wheel, Miss Vane. We built a wheel and then climbed inside it, and it has been rolling for a hundred years, and none of us alive remember how to step off."

"And Corin? Why does the wheel need *him*?"

"Because the moon completes its thirteenth turn in two nights, and a contract signed in good faith by his great-grandfather comes due, and the court will collect, and if the court collects, the seal holds, and if the seal holds, the god stays buried another century." He leaned forward across the fishmonger's desk, and suddenly he looked every one of his ninety-one years, a paper man about to be blown apart. "Unless the seal is broken. And there are exactly two things in this world that can break it. A freely given substitution of equal weight — which the court will accept and the seal will survive — or proof, entered into the great ledger itself, that the original covenant was witnessed falsely. That the god's consent was forged." He tapped the blotter, once, over the little black star of ink. "Which is why I let you be taken, rather than killed. Which is why I am telling you this instead of handing you to the Inquisitors. I hold the last copy of the true witness-record. I have held it for forty years without the courage to use it."

His hand disappeared beneath the desk. Ada's whole body tightened, mercenary instinct singing — blade, dart, bell — but what he placed on the oak between them was neither. It was a slim folio, cracked leather, tied with a plain cord. Older than him. Older, probably, than the hymns.

"It will not open for me," he said. "I have tried. A coward's hands, it seems. It wants—" and here his voice broke, actually broke, the clerkly composure cracking down the middle like river ice, "—it wants an oath. A real one. Freely sworn, in the god's own hearing, by someone whose faith was never bought." He looked at her wrist, at the collar that silenced it. "It wants you."

Above them, muffled through a hundred yards of living rock, ten thousand voices held their long note of waiting.

And beneath them, beneath everything, faint as wind through a keyhole, the screaming changed. Ada felt it in her teeth, in the scar-tissue of her old contracts, in the mark the collar couldn't quite kill. Not rage. Not pain.

Hope.

She put her hand on the folio. It was warm, like something recently alive.

"One condition," Ada Vane said. "When this is done — when the ledger's broken and the court's burned down to its foundations — you don't get to die comfortably in this room full of your sins. You walk up those stairs and you face every debtor in Karsthold yourself. That's the price of my oath. Justice outside your law."

Ostrevan closed his eyes. When he opened them, for the first time in a hundred years of careful cowardice, they were clear.

"Child," he said, "that is the first fair contract anyone has offered me in my entire life."

Somewhere far above, the bells of Karsthold began to ring — not the hour, not the hymn-call, but the alarm. And Ada knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had read the prophecy book's next page over Corin's shoulder, that somewhere in the deep galleries below, Neme had run out of time.

The moon was two nights from its thirteenth turn. And the hunt had already begun.
