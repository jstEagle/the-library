## 16. The God Who Was Screaming

The church's cells beneath the Basilica of the Waiting Hand smelled of cold iron and old incense, and Corin Ash learned in the first hour that both smells were lies. The incense masked something sweeter and fouler — burnt sugar, almost, the scent of souls kept too long from their owners. And the iron was not for keeping prisoners in. It was for keeping something else out.

He had counted his footsteps on the way down. Forty-one steps behind a deacon whose hands never stopped trembling. Three corridors. A door with no handle on this side. Then the cell: six paces by four, a bench of black stone, a grate high in the wall through which no light came but which nonetheless breathed — slow, rhythmic exhalations of air that was warm and wrong, like the inside of a mouth.

Ada had been taken an hour before him. He had heard her fighting — heard the particular sound of her knife being knocked loose into mud, heard her snarl something profane about Ostrevan's ancestry — and then heard nothing at all. The silence afterward had been worse than any of it.

Now he sat on the stone bench in the dark and did the only thing he knew how to do with fear, which was to argue with it.

*Twelve turns,* he told himself. *You have until tomorrow midnight. You have read the book. You know what is owed.* His soul, sold before his birth by a great-grandfather he'd never met, brokered by a church that had built its whole quiet empire on such transactions. Payable when the moon completed its thirteenth turn — a moon that had been counting down since before anyone alive could remember, its ledger-keeper a god everyone believed dead.

Except Ada didn't believe it anymore. Neither did he, now. Not after what Neme had found in the contract's seal. Not after what Ada had said in the dark of the undercity, her voice low and shaking in a way he'd never heard from her: *He never went silent, Corin. He was never allowed to speak.*

And then the hunters had come, and there had been no more time for anything.

Corin pressed his palms against his eyes until sparks bloomed. Think. Ada was somewhere in these warrens. The cell doors were iron but the walls between them were older than iron — Karsthold was a mountain first and a city second, and the mountain remembered every tunnel ever cut into it. He put his ear against the wall and listened.

Stone. Cold stone. Stone that breathed.

And beneath the breathing — a sound so faint he might have invented it.

A hum. No. Not a hum. A voice stretched so thin across so much distance that it had become texture rather than sound, the way a rope becomes a line becomes a hair. It came up through the floor. Through the mountain itself. Through whatever sat chained at the bottom of everything.

Corin went very still.

*—and I said, let the ledgers burn—*

He jerked back from the wall as if it had bitten him. The words were gone as fast as they'd come, leaving only their shape burned into his mind, the way a lightning strike leaves a tree standing but hollowed.

He touched the wall again. Deliberately this time. Pressed his whole palm flat against the stone the way you'd press your hand to the chest of someone drowning.

*—let the ledgers burn, let the debts rot, let every name go home—*

The voice was enormous and exhausted and *furious*, and it had been speaking for a hundred years into stone that would not carry it, and Corin Ash, merchant's grandson, soul-debtor, coward by his own long accounting, knelt on the floor of a church cell and wept without quite knowing why.

Because he recognized it. That was the terrible thing. Under all the rage and the ruin of it, the voice sounded like Ada. Like the way she talked about the valley when she thought he wasn't listening. Like loyalty that had outlived its object and refused to die of it.

"Where are you," he whispered. "What are you."

The stone gave him nothing but the slow breath of the mountain, and somewhere far above, muffled through floors of doctrine and marble, the bells began to mark the hour. Eleven. Thirteen hours until the turn.

He had work to do.

* * *

They made one mistake with him, and it was the same mistake institutions always make: they assumed the soft one was the harmless one.

The door opened near dawn — or what he judged was dawn, by the ache in his bones — and two acolytes brought water and a heel of black bread. They were young. The taller one couldn't have seen twenty winters. They set the tray down without looking at him, because looking at heretics was apparently against procedure, and in that moment of procedural blindness Corin did three things very fast.

He took the taller acolyte's key-ring off his belt with a pickpocket's grace he had learned, shamefully, from a woman in Vell's Landing who had later married his uncle. He drove his shoulder into the shorter one's chest, sending him sprawling into the corridor. And he ran — barefoot, because they had taken his boots — toward the sound of Ada.

He didn't know how he knew where she was. He simply did. The voice in the stone had shown him, in the half-second his palm lay flat against the wall: a map drawn not in lines but in pressure, in weight, in the feeling of a place where the mountain's attention gathered like water finding a low spot. Two floors up. Past the reliquary. Behind a door marked with the seal of the Waiting Hand.

The taller acolyte recovered faster than Corin liked and came after him shouting, and the shout woke the corridor, and suddenly there were torches and feet and the whole sleeping machinery of the Basilica grinding awake around him. Corin ran anyway. He had spent his whole life running *from* things — from debt collectors, from family expectations, from the prophecy book that named his death — and he discovered, pounding up a stairwell with his lungs full of knives, that running *toward* something used entirely different muscles.

The door was iron-banded oak with a lock older than the church itself, and the key-ring held eleven keys, and he tried them with hands that shook and steadied and shook again while boots thundered below.

The seventh key turned.

Inside, chained to the wall by wrists wrapped in oath-silver, was Ada Vane.

Her head came up fast, mercenary-fast, even though her face was grey with pain. The chains weren't holding her body so much as burning her — the silver was etched with the sigil of the Vessel-Who-Waits, the cup-and-circle he'd seen carved over every door in the Hollow Valley, and where it touched her skin her oath-mark flared like a coal. Her left wrist, the marked one, was blistered raw.

"Took you long enough," she rasped.

"You're welcome," Corin managed, and dropped to his knees beside her, and started trying keys.

Three fit the manacle on her right wrist. None fit the left. The oath-silver had its own lock, and it wasn't mechanical — when he pressed a key to it, the sigil flared white and the metal *sang*, a thin hostile note, and Ada hissed through her teeth.

"Don't," she said. "It's keyed to faith. To the church's claim on the mark. You can't pick a prayer, Corin."

"Then I'll do something worse." He looked at her blistered wrist, at the oath-mark beneath the burns — the small coiled symbol she'd carried since childhood, the brand of a god who had supposedly gone silent a century ago. "Ada. When I was coming up the stairs, I put my hand on the wall. On the stone. I heard him."

Her whole body changed. Not movement — stillness. The kind of stillness she got before violence, except aimed inward, at herself.

"Heard who," she said, in a voice that already knew.

"The Vessel. He's down there. Under us, under everything — the mountain is full of him, Ada, he's *in the stone*, he's been screaming for a hundred years and nobody—" Corin's throat closed. He swallowed it down. "Nobody was listening. The church walled him in and taught everyone he'd died, and they've been using his silence to run the court. Every soul sold under his seal. Every debt. He watched all of it and couldn't say a word."

Ada stared at him. Torchlight from the corridor moved on her face, and Corin saw something break in it and reassemble into something harder and more dangerous than anything he'd seen her wear before. Even the ambush on the road. Even the night she'd learned the church had brokered her great-great-grandparents' faith into a slave contract.

"Keys," she said. "Get me out of these, and then touch my wrist."

"What?"

"Just do it. If you can hear him through stone, maybe—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Maybe he can hear you too. Maybe you're the first thing in a century that isn't church-built. Get me *out*, Corin."

The ninth key worked on the right manacle. For the left, he did what she asked: he laid both his hands over her ruined wrist, over the oath-mark, and he closed his eyes, and he did the only prayer he knew, which wasn't a prayer at all but a merchant's phrase his grandmother used to say over unfair contracts:

*I see the true terms. I see the true terms. Show me the true terms.*

The world went white.

* * *

He was standing in a place that was not a place.

It was shaped like the inside of a bell, vast and curved, and it was filled with sound the way a cup is filled with water — sound pressing against him from every side, dense enough to swim in. And the sound was a voice. One voice. Saying the same things it had been saying for a hundred years, in ten thousand variations, none of them ever answered.

*—I did not consent. I did not consent. Let the ledgers burn. I did not consent. My name is written on debts I never signed. I did not—*

In the center of the bell-space hung a figure, or the memory of one, or the idea of one: tall, robed in shadow that might once have been light, bound not in chains but in *silence* — great folds of hushed nothing wrapped around it like grave-cloths, each layer stamped with the cup-and-circle. The clergy's seal. Hundreds of seals, layered over a century, each one a prayer that was really a padlock.

The figure's head turned. It had no face that Corin could bear to look at directly, but it *attended* him, wholly, the way a storm attends a ship.

*Who are you,* said the god of the Hollow Valley, and his voice was the voice from the stones, and it was also the smallest voice Corin had ever heard, because it was the voice of someone who has called out so long they no longer expect an answer.

"Corin Ash," Corin said. His own voice felt absurdly small. "My soul is owed to the court under your mountain. Tomorrow, when the moon turns thirteenth."

*Ah,* said the Vessel-Who-Waits. *The Ash boy. I remember the signing.*

"I wasn't born yet."

*No. But names echo backward. That is the cruelty of ledgers — they reach in every direction.* Something moved in the shadowed figure, something like grief. *Your great-grandfather wept when he signed. Did you know that? He thought he was saving his family from famine. The priest told him the god approved. The god had never heard of it.*

"I know," Corin said. "We found the seal. It's forged."

*Forged,* the god repeated, and the word went through the bell-space like a crack through ice, and for one moment the whole vast chamber rang with a sound that was almost laughter and mostly agony. *One hundred years. One hundred years of watching my name sell children's souls, and the answer was in the ink the whole time, and no one — no one could reach me to tell me, and I could reach no one to tell them—*

His attention swung back to Corin, and Corin felt it physically, like a hand the size of the sky resting on his shoulder.

*You reached me. How.*

"Your mark," Corin said. "On Ada's wrist. I was touching it."

*Vane's girl.* The name landed with unmistakable tenderness. *She swore to me when she was six years old. Do you know what she swore?*

"She never talks about it."

*Neither do I,* said the god, and the sorrow in it was bottomless. *She swore to keep watch over the valley's peace until I woke or died. She was the last. After her, no one. The church stopped administering the oaths — why bind people to a god who cannot enforce his own contracts? So she has kept faith alone with a silence for thirty years, believing herself abandoned, believing her life's vow wasted on an empty sky.* A pause, and the pressure on Corin's shoulder grew heavier. *Is she well?*

"They've hurt her. The chains — the silver burns her."

The bell-space shuddered. Somewhere in its depths, stone cracked.

*Tell her,* the god said, and now the exhaustion fell away from his voice and what remained underneath was older and simpler and terrible, *tell her this: I ask nothing of her. Understand me, boy. I ask nothing. Not worship. Not rescue. Not vengeance, though vengeance would be just. I have had a hundred years to consider what I want, and I find I want only one thing.*

"Tell me."

*Release. Only release. Let me out of this bell, and I will go — wherever gods go when they are no longer wanted. I will trouble no throne. I will demand no altar. I was made a jailer of debts and I never wished the office; I will not take up another.* The shadowed figure strained against its wrappings of silence, and Corin saw, imprinted on the inside of his skull, an image: a door. Deep beneath the mountain. Sealed with thirteen locks, one for each turn of the moon, and the thirteenth lock was shaped like tomorrow.

*But hear the price,* the god said gravely. *There is always a price; I am done pretending otherwise. The thirteenth lock opens only at the turn, and only from outside, and only by a hand freely given — the church made sure of that, in their arrogance. They never imagined a hand would come. And the court will be gathering at the turn to collect your soul, Corin Ash. The same moment. The same hall. Everything will happen at once, and everything will be at stake, and I cannot tell you it will succeed.*

"And if it fails?"

*Then the debt stands, and I remain, and you are collected, and the world goes on exactly as it has — unjust, unlistening, intact.* A pause. *I would not blame you for choosing that. Intactness is not nothing. Ask anyone who has lost something.*

Corin stood in the ringing dark and thought about Ada's blistered wrist. About Neme, captured buying them time in the galleries, her scribe's hands probably already in irons. About forty generations of debtors whose names filled the ledger-hall, whose stolen souls powered the torches of Karsthold. About a valley where magic could not follow, built — he understood it now — not to protect the faithful *from* their god, but to protect the church *from* his justice.

Intactness was not nothing. But it wasn't anything, either.

"I'll open the door," he said.

*You speak for yourself alone,* the god warned. *Not for her. Never for her. Her oath is her own, and I will not spend it, even to save myself. Swear nothing on her behalf, Corin Ash. Promise me.*

"I promise," Corin said. "You'll get nothing from me but my own two hands."

*Two hands freely given,* said the Vessel-Who-Waits, and the wonder in his voice was the most heartbreaking thing Corin had ever heard, because it meant that in a hundred years, no one had offered him even that. *Then listen closely, boy. Here is what you must—*

* * *

He came back to himself on the cell floor with Ada gripping his collar and shaking him, her freed right hand bloody-knuckled, her eyes wild.

"—breathing, damn you, breathe, you absolute *merchant*—"

"I'm here." His voice came out scraped. "I'm here. I talked to him."

Ada went still. In the torchlight her face was terrible and hopeful and young, all at once, stripped of thirty years of mercenary armor. "And? What does he want? Retribution? Worship? Does he want us to raise an army, does he want the clergy broken on wheels, what does a betrayed *god* want, Corin?"

Corin sat up. His hands were steady now. Whatever else the bell-space had done to him, it had burned the shaking out.

"That's the thing," he said. "That's the whole thing, Ada. He doesn't want any of it. He wants out. Just — out. No throne, no altars, no law. He said he was made a jailer and never wanted the office." He took a breath. "He said to tell you: he asks nothing of you. Your oath is yours. He won't spend it."

For a long moment Ada didn't move. Then she turned her face away, toward the wall, and Corin had the grace to look elsewhere while her shoulders shook — once, twice — with something that was not quite grief and not quite laughter.

When she turned back, her eyes were wet and absolutely clear.

"Thirteen locks," she said. "One for each turn. And the thirteenth opens tomorrow at midnight."

"At the same moment the court comes for his soul," Corin said. "Ours. Mine. Same hall. He says a hand must be given freely, from outside, and he can't tell us it'll work."

Ada looked down at her ruined wrist, at the oath-mark gleaming through the burns, and Corin saw her flex her fingers slowly, deliberately, like a woman testing a blade.

"A hand freely given," she murmured. "He knows I'd give it. That's why he forbade me." A ghost of her old dry smile. "Clever god."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the bells of Karsthold began to toll the noon hour — and beneath the bells, deeper than the bells, so deep that only someone who had stood in the bell-space could possibly have heard it, the mountain itself seemed to shift its weight, the way a prisoner shifts when he hears, at last, footsteps on the stairs.

Thirteen hours.

Somewhere below, a door with thirteen locks waited. Somewhere above, a court gathered its ledgers and its knives. And between them, climbing toward the light with a borrowed key-ring and a god's instructions written on the inside of his eyelids, Corin Ash finally understood what it cost to be the first person in a century to answer a scream —

— because now he was the only one who knew what came next, and there was no one left to tell but a wounded mercenary with a forbidden oath burning on her wrist, and thirteen hours, and no plan worth the name.

"We need Neme," Ada said, hauling him up by the arm. "And we need the debtors. And, Corin?" She smiled, and it was the smile she wore going into a fight. "We need to steal a god."
