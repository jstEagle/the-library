## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

### ADA

The moon did not rise over Karsthold so much as it was *unveiled*.

All day the clouds had hung low and gray as unwashed wool, pressing on the mountain's shoulders, and the city below had moved through its last hours like a household preparing for a funeral — shutters closed, torches unlit, even the soul-market silent for the first time in living memory. Then, at dusk, the wind came down from the peak in one long exhale and tore the cloud cover open, and there it was: the thirteenth moon, full and white and terribly patient, hanging directly above the mountain's crown as though it had been waiting a hundred years to be looked at.

Ada stood on the aqueduct with the cold stone sweating under her palms and felt her oath-mark burn.

It had burned since they left the Hollow Valley — a faint, wrong pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — but tonight it was no longer faint. Tonight it was a fist knocking. Tonight it had *words* in it, almost, syllables of heat crawling up her forearm toward her elbow, and she pressed her wrist against the aqueduct's rail because she could not decide whether the sensation was agony or an answer to a question she'd been asking since she was seven years old.

*Not long now,* the mark seemed to say. *Not long.*

"Stop that," she muttered at her own arm.

Below her, Karsthold held its breath. The ledger-hall rose out of the city's heart like a black tooth — the court beneath the mountain kept its accounts above ground too, in that vaulted monstrosity where names were weighed and years were taxed and souls changed hands with less ceremony than a sack of flour. Every window was dark. But Ada knew what waited inside: clerks who did not sleep, scales that weighed things no scale should touch, and Ostrevan, somewhere in the deep of it, wearing his mercy like a vestment.

And beneath all of it — beneath the hall, beneath the market galleries, beneath three hundred feet of hewn dark — the prison.

She thought of what Ostrevan had confessed to her, chained in that lamp-lit cell, his voice so calm it had made her want to break his jaw just to hear him sound human for once. *We did not silence him. We built a box around his voice.* A hundred years of prayers falling into a sealed room. A hundred years of screaming folded up inside a god and called *silence*, called *mystery*, called *faith*. Her whole childhood spent listening to elders intone that the Vessel-Who-Waits had withdrawn from the world to test their devotion — when he had been shouting the whole time, and the shout had been *help me*.

Her hand found the prophecy book in her coat. Corin's death page. Tonight's date. Somewhere in the ledger-hall was the original contract with its forged witness-seal — the god's name signed by a priest's hand, never by the god himself — and a forged seal was not merely a flaw. Neme had been very clear about this, in the days before the galleries took him. A contract witnessed falsely is not a contract at all. It is a theft wearing a contract's clothes. And a theft can be undone by anyone willing to pay the price of the unmaking.

The price of the unmaking was written into the substitution clause. *Something of equal weight, freely given.*

A soul owed. A soul offered. The scales would have to balance.

"You're doing the face," said Corin behind her.

She hadn't heard him climb the aqueduct stairs. He moved quietly now in a way he hadn't when they first met; the road had taught him that, and the ambushes, and the nine nights he'd spent selling pieces of his own future in the undercity before she caught him at it. He came to stand beside her, wrapped in a borrowed watchman's cloak, his breath fogging, his eyes fixed on the thirteenth moon with the expression of a man reading his own obituary and finding it badly punctuated.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided how you're going to die and you're waiting for the right moment to tell me." He didn't look at her. "I've seen it before. You wear it like your other cloak."

"The plan doesn't work without—"

"I know what the plan requires." Now he turned, and in the moonlight his face was pale but steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than any mob ever had. "Equal weight, freely given. I've done the arithmetic, Ada. My soul weighs what my soul weighs. There's nothing else in either of our pockets that tips those scales."

"There's you," she said. "There's the fact that I swore an oath to keep you breathing, and I have broken every rule I ever had for you except that one, and I don't intend to start tonight."

"That's not weight. That's sentiment."

"Sentiment has tipped stranger scales."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, half horror — and then his hand found hers on the rail, cold fingers lacing through colder ones. Below them, far off in the debtors' quarters at the city's rim, someone struck iron against iron. Once. Twice. A third time. The signal they had spent six days seeding through the tenements and the tanneries and the mine-shafts, whispered from mouth to scarred ear: *tonight, when the moon stands at the crown of the mountain, we go to the ledger-hall, and we take back what was taken.*

The answering clangs began, scattered at first, then joining, until the whole rim of Karsthold rang like a bell being born.

Corin squeezed her hand. "Whatever happens up there—"

"Don't." She said it gently, which for her was nearly a scream. "Don't write your last words. Save them for after. I want to hear something tedious instead. Tell me about grain tariffs. Tell me about your mother's ledgers."

He looked at her a long moment. Then, softly: "After."

"After," she agreed, and did not let herself think about whether she was lying, and pulled him toward the stair.

* * *

### CORIN

The ledger-hall's doors had never been meant to open from outside.

That was the first thing Corin understood when they reached the great bronze doors at the head of the processional stair — doors tall as trees, worked over with scenes of exchange: hands giving hands, coins giving coins, a figure whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of supplicants' touches. They were not locked. They were simply *heavy* beyond mortal strength, designed to swing only from within, so that all the world might knock and only the court might answer.

But the court had not counted on four hundred debtors with crowbars, mining picks, and nothing left to lose.

The doors were already groaning when Ada and Corin arrived at the top of the stair. Torches ringed the plaza — hundreds of them, carried by people Corin had come to know by name over six desperate days: Maret the tanner, whose daughter's laughter had been sold to buy a wool contract; old Sef, who had pawned forty years of his memory one winter at a time until he couldn't remember his wife's face and had to love her from photographs and hearsay; a boy of perhaps fifteen named Illo who had inherited a debt on his own name before he was born, exactly as Corin had, and who swung a pick taller than himself with a fury that made Corin's chest ache.

The first hinge gave with a sound like a ship breaking. The second followed. And then the doors of the ledger-hall swung inward for the first time in living memory, and the crowd did not cheer — they went quiet, four hundred people inhaling at once — and poured into the dark.

Inside, the hall opened upward into vaulted blackness where the scales hung. Corin had seen them once, from the gallery, on the night he'd found his great-grandfather's contract: dozens of great brass balances suspended on chains from the unseen roof, each one weighing something invisible, swaying slowly in air that did not move. Tonight every scale hung perfectly still. Waiting.

Clerks scattered before the crowd like startled birds, clutching armfuls of writs. Someone had the sense — or the cruelty — to release the scribes' pens from their chains, and paper snowed down through the torchlight: contracts, liens, deeds of sale for voices, faces, first kisses, final words. Maret caught one out of the air, read it, and tore it in half with a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh.

"The archive vault," Ada said at Corin's shoulder. She had drawn both knives, though there was nothing yet to use them on, and her eyes tracked the balconies above. "Contract's in the fire-proof case, third alcove. We get it, we read the unmaking aloud before the scales, we—"

"We void the seal. I remember." He did remember. He had recited Neme's instructions in his sleep for two nights running. *The seal must be renounced by blood of the debtor line and witnessed by the oath-bound. Speak the true name of the false witness. Speak the word 'forged.' Let the scales hear.*

They crossed the floor together, wading through the tide of debtors, past the weighing benches where clerks had weighed lives in drachms, and Corin tried not to look at the case labels as he passed — *VOICES, ASSORTED*; *YEARS, MIXED VINTAGE*; *NAMES, GENTLE* — because if he looked he would stop moving, and if he stopped moving the moon above the mountain would keep turning anyway, indifferent as arithmetic.

They reached the archive alcove. Ada smashed the glass case with the pommel of her knife, reached in, and drew out the contract.

It was smaller than Corin remembered. A single sheet, brittle as autumn, covered in a clerk's beautiful hand. At the bottom, the signature of Aldous Ash, his great-grandfather, in ink gone brown. And beside it, the witness line:

*Witnessed and sealed under the authority of the Vessel-Who-Waits, by the hand of Anselm Dross, priest.*

Forged. All of it forged. The god had never consented, never seen, never spoken — because his clergy had already nailed his mouth shut and were selling souls under his stolen name.

Above them, without warning, every scale in the hall began to move.

* * *

### ADA

Ada felt it before she saw it — the oath-mark flaring so hot she gasped and nearly dropped the contract — and then the light changed.

It came down through the hall's high clerestory windows in a column of white: moonlight, the thirteenth moon's own light, finding the central dais where the largest scale hung. And in that light stood High Cleric Ostrevan.

He had not fled. Of course he hadn't. He stood at the center of the chaos in his gray vestments with his hands folded, and the debtors around him hesitated the way people hesitate before a snake that isn't moving, and his voice carried effortlessly through the hall as though the architecture itself had been paid to carry it.

"Children." His gaze swept the crowd and settled, at last, on Ada. "You mistake a reckoning for a robbery. The debts are real. The weights are true. What you storm tonight is not a prison — it is the only thing standing between this valley and the hunger of the thing beneath us."

"Liar," Ada said, and her voice cracked across the hall like a whip. She held up the contract. "You wrote this! Your priest, your seal, your stolen god's name — you sold this man's soul a century before he was born and you've been feeding the court ever since to keep your throne warm!"

Murmurs ran through the debtors. Ostrevan's composure flickered — only slightly, only around the eyes, but Ada had spent her life reading men across bargaining tables and she saw it, savored it.

"The court is older than the church," Ostrevan said. "The court will be fed regardless. The only question is *who*." He raised one hand, and the moonlight thickened, and the great scale above the dais began to descend — one pan sinking, the other rising, and in the sinking pan Ada could see, or almost see, a shimmer like heat off summer stone, like a person-shaped absence filling with light. "The turn completes at midnight. The collection does not require your consent, Corin Ash. It requires only the hour. Stand aside, all of you, and no one need be hurt. This is the church's final offer of mercy."

"It's the church's final offer of *itself*," said a new voice, ragged and triumphant, from the western door — and Ada's heart slammed against her ribs, because Neme walked through it.

Disheveled, limping, one eye swollen shut from the galleries where they'd taken him, but alive, supported on either side by miners, holding aloft a bundle of documents that Ada recognized with a jolt: the court's own internal ledgers, the secret accounts, the proof of everything. "We got him out," one of the miners called to her, grinning through a split lip. "He talked his way out. Took him three days, but the man can *talk*."

"Read them!" Neme cried, hurling the ledgers into the crowd. "Read what your High Cleric has purchased with your souls! Read what the court pays him! Read what he bought with the Vessel's silence!"

Papers scattered. Hands seized them. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to read aloud in a shaking voice — figures, dates, transfers — and with every number the murmurs curdled into something harder.

Ostrevan looked at Neme for a long, still moment. And Ada watched him arrive at a decision the way one watches ice decide to crack.

"So be it," the High Cleric said softly. "Let the scales weigh you all."

He spoke a word — not in any language Ada knew, a word with edges, a word that tasted of iron — and every chain in the hall snapped taut, and every scale swung, and the debtors cried out and stumbled as the air itself grew heavy, dragging at them, *weighing* them, and Ada understood with sick clarity that the scales did not merely measure. When the court collected, the scales *took*.

The central scale descended toward the dais, the shimmer in its pan brightening, gathering, becoming unmistakably, horribly *Corin-shaped* —

— and Ada moved.

Later she would not remember deciding. She remembered only the cold of the dais stone through her boots, the burn of her oath-mark climbing past her elbow, and her own voice ringing out over the groan of brass and chain, pitched to fill the whole vaulted dark:

*"Substitution!"*

The hall stilled. Even the scales seemed to pause mid-swing. Ostrevan turned slowly, and for the first time since she'd known him, the High Cleric of the silent god looked genuinely surprised.

"Clause nine," Ada said. Her heart was slamming; her voice did not shake. "Equal weight, freely given. I am oath-bound — sworn to the Vessel-Who-Waits at seven years of age, witnessed by his own church, sealed by his own rite. My life belongs to a god. That's weight, clerk. That's the oldest weight there is." She stepped between the descending scale and Corin, spread her arms, and bared her marked wrist to the moonlight. "I give it freely. Take mine. Void the seal. Let the scales hear it."

"No—" Corin's voice, raw, behind her. She did not turn. If she turned she would fail.

The mark on her wrist *blazed*. And in the blaze — for the first time in her life, in the first crack in a hundred years of silence — Ada heard her god.

Not words. Something vaster than words, a pressure of presence, a grief and fury and terrible relief pouring through the burning brand like water through a broken dam, and threaded through all of it, distinct as a bell in a landslide, one meaning, repeated, insistent:

*Not yours to give. Not yours to give. NOT YOURS TO GIVE—*

Because her life was oath-bound. Because it was already promised, already owned, already *his* — and a gift stolen from its true owner is no gift at all. It is another forgery. Another theft wearing generosity's clothes.

The scales froze.

And then, all together, with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, they reversed — every chain slackening, every pan rising — and the shimmer in the central scale dispersed like breath off a mirror, and the contract in Ada's hand grew warm, then hot, then crumbled to ash between her fingers, and through the hall rolled a single vast tolling note that was not a bell but the *word* *forged*, spoken by the scales themselves, resounding through beam and bone:

**FORGED.**

The debt was dead.

For one heartbeat, the whole hall hung in stunned silence. Then the debtors erupted — cheering, weeping, tearing contracts — and Ada staggered backward into Corin's arms, her wrist still smoking, her ears ringing, laughing at a sky that had not fallen on her after all.

Which was when Ostrevan drew his ritual knife and lunged — not at Corin, not at Ada, but at the central chain, shrieking, "*If the debt dies, the court collects from its keeper!*"

* * *

### CORIN

Corin had never tackled a priest before. He discovered, in the act, that it was mostly momentum and terror.

They went down together onto the dais, the knife skidding free across the stone, and the crowd surged in — Maret and Sef and a dozen miners, hauling the High Cleric upright by his vestments while Ostrevan fought with the frenzied strength of a drowning man, screaming scripture, screaming arithmetic, screaming that the scales must be fed, *something* must be fed, the court keeps its own accounts—

And the court agreed.

The floor beneath the dais opened. Not broke — *opened*, seams parting in the stone like eyelids, and up from the deep galleries rose the collectors: tall figures of shadow and ledger-light, faceless, patient, who had served the court since before the church existed. The debtors fell back. Ostrevan stopped struggling. For a moment he simply stared at them, and Corin watched understanding arrive in the old man's face — the slow, awful dawn of a broker realizing he is on the wrong side of the counter.

"No," Ostrevan said. "No — I fed you. A century, I kept the accounts, I kept you *fed*—"

One collector extended a hand. In its palm lay a single sheet of parchment, glowing faintly: a contract, ancient and immaculate. Corin, close enough to see, read the signature at the bottom and felt the floor tilt beneath his soul.

*Anselm Dross, priest, in surety for the seal of the Vessel-Who-Waits.*

Dross — the forger. And Ostrevan, Corin understood with a lurch, was Dross's heir in every sense that mattered. The office, the seal, the debt. The church had bound itself into the contract as collateral a century ago, in case the god's followers ever balked at payment. In case anyone ever read the fine print.

The court had always intended to collect from *someone*.

"Wait," Ostrevan said, as the shadow-hands closed on his gray sleeves. "Wait — we can renegotiate — I have access, I have *authority*—"

His voice cut off as they drew him down into the opening, and the stone sealed over him smooth as water, and the last thing the High Cleric of the silent god did in the world above was reach, futilely, toward the moon.

Then the collectors turned their faceless heads — all of them, at once — toward the dais. Toward Ada.

Toward her smoking, blazing wrist.

And the tallest among them inclined its head — not a threat, Corin realized, his blood going cold and strange. A courtesy. An *invitation*. The court knew what had just spoken through her mark. The court knew what woke below.

The floor opened again — a stair this time, descending into warm golden dark, and up from that depth came a sound that made every soul in the hall go still: a voice. A real voice. Hoarse from a hundred years of disuse and roaring with a hundred years of rage and joy and *relief*, echoing up the stair like the sun itself learning to speak:

***"AT LAST. AT LAST. WHO FREED MY VOICE — COME DOWN. COME DOWN, LITTLE OATH. WE MUST SPEAK OF WHAT YOU ARE OWED."***

Ada stood at the edge of the stair, her arm still burning, her face lit gold from below. Corin gripped her hand hard enough to hurt.

"Ada," he said. "You don't have to."

She looked at him. Then down into the light where her god waited — her silent god, her screaming god, the purpose she'd been raised inside like a blade in a scabbard.

"Yes, I do," she said quietly. "Just not the way he thinks."

And hand in hand with Corin, before four hundred freed debtors and a stunned and emptying hall, Ada Vane started down the stair into the mountain, toward the throne of the Vessel-Who-Waits — and toward the choice that would cost more than any debt ever weighed.
