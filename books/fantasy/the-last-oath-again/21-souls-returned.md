## 21. Souls Returned

The first thing Corin felt was the absence of weight.

He had lived so long under it — the soul-debt like a stone sewn into his chest, a cold he had mistaken for his own temperament — that when it lifted, in the smoking ruin of the ledger-hall, he staggered as if someone had cut a rope and left him floating. The thirteenth turn had come and gone. The moon hung above Karsthold's shattered dome, whole and white and ordinary, and he was still standing. Still himself. Still *his*.

Around him, the court was dying the way a fire dies — not all at once, but in settling embers, in the slow collapse of things that had burned too long.

The great scales of the market hall cracked down their central beam with a sound like a snapped bone. Torches guttered along walls that no longer remembered why they'd been lit. And everywhere, everywhere, souls were going home.

Corin felt them pass through him. That was the only way he could ever describe it afterward: like standing in a doorway while a crowd flows past, each one brushing him with a different weather. A grandmother's soul went by smelling of bread and river water. A child's went by laughing, wordless, bright as struck flint. An old man's dragged behind him seventy years of rain on a farm he'd sold to pay a debt he never should have owed.

They rose out of the ledger-hall's vaults like smoke finding its chimney, streaming up through cracks in the stone, out through the broken dome, scattering across the night sky toward the valley lands and every small town and hollow and riverside village where someone had once signed away something they didn't understand they owned.

"Look," Ada said beside him. She was bleeding from a cut along her jaw and her voice was wrecked from shouting orders at debtors who hadn't needed her orders half as much as she'd thought. But she was pointing up, and her hand was steady.

Above the mountain, against the moon, the streams of returning souls caught the light and shimmered — a migration, a river running backward, a sky full of salmon climbing home to spawn.

"They're going to be confused," Corin said. "Waking up with themselves again."

"They'll be angry first." Ada watched the sky. "Angry, then confused. Then—" She stopped. Her throat worked. "Then maybe something else."

* * *

They walked out of the mountain through corridors that were already forgetting how to be grand.

That was the strange thing about the court's dissolution: it wasn't just people being freed. It was *power* being returned. The torches along the deep galleries burned low and then not at all, because the flame had been fed on borrowed years and there were none left to borrow. The velvet rotted off the chairs in hours. The gold leaf curled and flaked like autumn birch. Karsthold's undercity, which had gleamed with ill-gotten splendor since before Corin's great-grandfather put ink to that cursed contract, began quietly coming apart into honest stone.

"It's like watching a lie decompose," Neme would have said.

Neme.

They found her on the third day, or she found them — Corin was never sure which, and suspected she'd arranged even that ambiguity on purpose.

She came limping into the debtor camp at the foot of the mountain, gaunt as a winter branch, wearing the rags of what had been a scribe's coat worth more than a house. Her hands, which Corin remembered as quick and dry and always ink-stained, were scarred now. The court's interrogators had been thorough in the two days they'd held her before the uprising broke their doors.

But her eyes — her eyes were wrong. Or right. Corin couldn't decide.

"You're alive," he said stupidly, when she dropped onto the bench beside him outside the tent where Ada was sleeping off a wound.

"I'm *more* than alive." Neme flexed her scarred fingers, studying them like a stranger's. "Do you know what they took from me, Ash? When I was nineteen? Not my years — I kept those, they were useful for copying contracts. They took my *want*. My wanting-anything. Oh, I wanted small things — tea, warmth, an unwatched hour. But anything large, anything that might have made me dangerous, they'd skimmed off the top before I could grow it. I spent thirty years thinking I was a modest person." She laughed, and the laugh had edges now. "I am not a modest person. I have plans. I have *so many* plans."

"That sounds terrifying."

"It is. For everyone." She said it with obvious delight. Then quieter: "I saw them, you know. While they held me. The souls still stored in the deep vaults — the ones too old or tangled to simply fly free. When the court fell, the vaults opened, and some of those poor fragments had been sitting in the dark for generations." Her voice roughened. "We're carrying them out by lantern-light. Piece by piece. There are families in the valley lands who will get back a grandfather's courage or a mother's laughter and never know where it came from."

"Do they need to know?"

Neme looked at him for a long moment. "No," she admitted. "And that's the hardest part of restitution, isn't it? Most of what we restore will be received as grace rather than justice. People will call it luck." Her mouth twisted. "I intend to write it all down anyway. Every name. Every theft. Someone has to keep the ledger now — an honest one, this time."

"The last honest ledger in Karsthold," Corin said.

"The only one," said Neme, "ever."

* * *

In the weeks that followed, the stories came filtering down from the highlands, and Corin collected them the way he'd once collected debts for his family's trading house — obsessively, item by item, except these were the opposite of debts.

A woman in Greyford woke from twenty years of a gray, dutiful sleep-walk through her own life and wept for three days without knowing why, then took up the fiddle she'd abandoned at fourteen and played until her fingers bled.

A man who had been gentle all his life woke up furious — the stolen edge of him returned at last — and used it to lead his village in burning the church tithe-records.

Children born hollow-eyed and quiet grew loud overnight. Old men found their appetites. In the valley lands, the phrase "since the moon turned" entered the language within a season, attached to every inexplicable recovery, every sudden bloom of appetite and art and stubbornness, the way other regions said "since the war" or "since the flood."

And the god—

Corin heard the reports secondhand, from pilgrims and refugees and one extremely talkative carter: a tall figure walking the roads of the valley lands with no retinue and no temple, stopping at wells and market stalls and funerals, asking questions. *Was the law fair? Was the price honest? Who went hungry so that you could eat?* He asked them gently. He asked them terribly. Priests who had spent careers administering Ostrevan's careful, corrupting mercy found they could not meet his eyes.

"He doesn't want worship," one refugee told Corin, shaking her head in wonder. "That's the strangest thing. He wants *answers*. He stands in your doorway and asks what justice looks like in your house, and then he leaves."

"And if you don't have answers?"

"Then he waits." She smiled faintly. "He's very good at waiting. It's in the name."

* * *

On a grey morning in late autumn, Corin stood at the border of the Hollow Valley and did not cross it.

Ada stood beside him. Behind them, the road wound back toward Karsthold, where Neme was rebuilding something from the wreckage — she refused to call it a government, insisting on "an accounting office," though everyone understood it would be neither. Ahead, the valley lay green and dim under its permanent hush of magicless air, the one place in the world where nothing supernatural could follow you, including, apparently, gods.

Including, once, Corin's own death. He touched his chest, where the cold had lived. Warm now. All the way through.

"Your family will be expecting you," Ada said. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the valley, at the low smoke of hearth-fires rising from the town where she'd grown up among people who had never quite claimed her. "The Ash trading house lost its heir for a season. There'll be ledgers to balance. Contracts to honor. You're good at that part."

"I'm good at that part," Corin agreed. "I hate that part."

"You don't have to hate it anymore. The part of you that hated it was probably—" he could hear the smile in her voice without seeing it— "collateral. Something the court skimmed to make you easier to manage."

"No." He turned to face her fully. "I've been taking inventory since the moon turned. Everything the debt took from me, everything that came back. And here's what I found: I hated it before they ever touched my soul. Great-grandfather's contract didn't make me miserable, Ada. It just gave my misery a deadline." He took a breath. "I'm staying."

"Staying where?"

"Near you. However you define near. The valley, the road, whatever comes next — I've watched you work since the tavern, and here's what I know: you belong nowhere, and you belong to yourself, and you're the most *whole* person I've ever met. I want to learn how that's done." His ears were burning. He kept going anyway; he'd signed a contract with hell itself, after all; he could survive this. "I'm not asking for an oath. Gods get oaths. I'm asking for — company. The kind you choose daily. The kind nobody owns."

Ada was silent long enough that the wind moved twice through the border grasses.

"You understand," she said finally, "that I freed a god and refused him. Broke a hundred-year peace, burned a court, and told a divine being, to his face, *no*. I'm not exactly safe company."

"Neither am I. I have excellent references in catastrophe."

Something happened to her face then — a loosening around the eyes, a crack in the competence she wore like armor, and underneath it, briefly visible, the girl who had once sworn herself to a silent god because she'd wanted so badly to belong somewhere. Except she belonged somewhere now. To herself. To the debtors she'd armed. To him, if he'd read the last three hundred miles correctly, and he had built a career on reading contracts correctly.

"Company chosen daily," she repeated.

"Daily," Corin said. "Witnessed by no one. Enforceable by no one. Worth everything."

She put out her hand — mercenary-fashion, contract-fashion — and he clasped it, and neither of them let go, and that was the whole agreement, and it was enough.

* * *

They crossed into the valley together at dusk.

Corin braced for the old deadness, the flat silence where magic should be — but the elders had been wrong about the valley all along, it seemed, or the world had changed beneath it. As they passed the boundary stones he felt something brush him, faint as breath: not power, exactly. *Attention.* The freed god passing far overhead on his endless walk, perhaps, noticing the place that had sheltered his oath-keeper, and choosing — as Ada had taught the whole world to choose — to leave it free.

Or maybe it was only evening. Corin found he didn't mind not knowing.

In the town, lamps were being lit. Somewhere a fiddle was playing — badly, joyfully, the way Greyford women played when they'd just gotten their hands back. Smoke smelled of bread. Neme's first honest ledger was due to arrive with the week's caravan, and Ada had promised, with the gravity of a battle-oath, to teach Corin how to drink the local cider without grimacing, and he had promised to fail.

They walked down the hillside road side by side, unhurried, unwitnessed, owing nothing to anyone but each other and choosing it again tomorrow.

Behind them the moon rose over the valley — one turn begun, twelve to go, and for the first time in a hundred years, no one counting.
