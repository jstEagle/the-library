## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The bottled memory arrived at the ninth bell, carried up from the receiving room by a boy with soot on his knuckles and a receipt he could not read, because the receipt was written in a language that had died before the guild was founded. I read it anyway. That was my work. That had been my family's work for four generations, and now it was mine alone.

*One memory, vintage unknown, provenance: a widow in Lowmarsh who says it is her wedding day but cannot say whose.*

I held the bottle to the lamplight and turned it slowly, the way one turns a wounded bird or an accusation. Inside, the liquid light swam — gold at the edges, bruised purple at its heart, and through it drifted small bright motes like pollen, like snow, like the sparks that rise from a bonfire when something beloved is burning. A good memory, well-kept. Someone had loved it enough to seal the cork with wax and press a thumbprint into the wax while it softened, which is either sentiment or superstition, depending on how much you trust thumbs.

"Appraisal?" asked the boy.

"Condition: intact," I said. "Clarity: high. Emotional residue—" I uncorked it a quarter-turn, just enough to let a thread of it escape, and let it touch my tongue the way a vintner tests a barrel. It tasted of rain on hot stone, of someone laughing with their mouth full, of hands that knew how to be gentle without being taught. My chest did something complicated and unprofessional. "—significant. The widow will want a fair exchange, not a generous one. Note it as category three, sentimental-impossible."

The boy wrote *category three* in his careful apprentice's hand and hurried away, and I sat alone again in the Appraisal Room of the Guild of Improbable Exchanges, which occupies the top floor of a building older than the country it stands in, with windows tall enough to admit the moon and shelves deep enough to hold griefs by the yard.

Below me, the guild slept. Below the guild, the city slept. And below the city, threaded through its foundations like roots through a grave, ran the border wards — old oaths made stone and salt and standing water, laid down by people who believed a border was a promise you could build. My oath-sword hung on its rack by the window, and even now, in the quiet, I could feel it the way you feel a sleeping animal in the room: a warmth, a weight, a faint hum beneath hearing. *Oath-bound*, the ledgers called me. Last of the swordspeakers. Keeper of the Voss line's vow, sworn in blood before I was born and renewed in blood every year on the Long Night, and binding me — this was the part no one said aloud anymore — to a guild whose founding members were all dead, defending a border against enemies no living person had ever seen.

I balanced the accounts instead of thinking about it.

That was the trick of a life like mine: you kept your hands busy and your ledger true, and the loneliness stayed where you put it, folded flat between columns like a pressed flower. Debits and credits. One bottled courage, withdrawn against future need. Three hours of silence, sold by a hermit who had too much of it. A debt of gratitude, compounded annually since the Flood Year, still outstanding, still accruing interest no collector dared pursue. The Guild of Improbable Exchanges traded in things money couldn't price, and I priced them anyway, because impossible things go mad without accounting. Ask any kingdom.

Outside, the stars were out in their usual numbers. I remember counting them — not all of them, just the ones over the western wall, the constellation the ward-masons called the Nine Witnesses, because they'd sworn the sky itself into the border's keeping. Eight visible tonight. The ninth, Aldera's Star, dim behind cloud. Normal. Everything normal. I want that written down somewhere: that night began as every other night of my life had begun, with arithmetic and lamplight and the small, manageable sorrows of strangers sealed in glass.

* * *

Wren Solace came in at the eleventh bell, which meant Wren Solace was avoiding sleep again, which meant I would get nothing else appraised until they grew bored.

They came in sideways, the way they did everything — through the door but also somehow around it, as if straight lines were a courtesy they extended only occasionally. Nineteen years old, guild apprentice, second year, hair the color of unpolished copper and eyes that caught lamplight and gave back more than they received. They carried two cups of chicory and set one on my ledger without asking, directly over the column I was reconciling, which I understood to be a declaration of war.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"I'm working."

"You're doing the thing where you sit in this room alone with a sword and a bottle of somebody's dead happiness and call it a life." They hopped onto the appraisal table, boots swinging above the floor, entirely without shame. Master Corvane would have flayed them. Master Corvane had retired to the coast, or possibly died; his letters had stopped coming, and I had entered him in the ledger under *assets: uncertain*. "I brought you chicory. This is courtship behavior. You're supposed to be charmed."

"I am charmed." I moved the cup off my ledger and drank it, because it was hot and because refusing it would have required a conversation about why I refused it. "Charmed is not the same as persuaded."

"Persuaded toward what?"

Toward sitting down. Toward talking. Toward the dangerous arithmetic of two people in a room after midnight, dividing a silence between them instead of each keeping their own. "Toward believing you should be asleep. Apprentices need sleep. It's in the charter."

"The charter also says the guild shall defend the realm 'against all erasures,' and nobody's done that in eighty years, so maybe we shouldn't lean too hard on the charter." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the performance dropped and what looked out at me was something older and more frightened than nineteen. "Xanthe. Do you ever wonder what we're actually guarding?"

"The border."

"The border of *what?*" They gestured at the window, at the dark, at the whole sleeping arrangement of the world. "You renew your oath every Long Night. In blood. I've seen the scar. What does the oath actually *say*? Does anyone know? Does *your sword* know?"

My sword knew. That was the trouble. Some nights, holding it during renewal, I felt less like a speaker and more like a mouthpiece — a brass horn through which some older voice said words I'd learned phonetically, in a grammar of obligation I could parse but not translate. "It says what it has always said," I told Wren. "That's what makes it an oath."

"That's what makes it a cage."

"Wren."

"Fine. Fine." They slid off the table, drained their own chicory, and paused at the door with their hand on the frame — pausing, always, just long enough to leave something behind. "For the record, I'd have renewed anything you wanted. No blood required. But you'd have to ask, and you don't ask. You balance." They tapped my ledger twice, a little knock against the paper, a little knock against everything I'd built my life out of. "Goodnight, Xanthe Voss. Try to want something before dawn. Just once. As an experiment."

Then they were gone, taking most of the light with them, and I sat with the cooling chicory and the humming sword and the bottled laughter of a dead man's wedding, and I tried, honestly tried, to want something.

What I wanted was order. I want to be fair to myself, looking back: order was not nothing. Order was a country I could defend, a ledger I could close, a duty I could carry precisely because it weighed exactly what it had always weighed and no more. My grandmother had held this office through plague and flood. Her teacher had held it through the Sundering Wars. Somewhere behind me stretched four generations of Voss swordspeakers who had stood this exact watch, felt this exact ache, and spoken their yearly blood into the same old vow without once — not once in the ledgers, I had checked — writing down whether they regretted it.

There were no living witnesses to our oath. There never had been, really; there had only been witnesses to its *keeping*, and they were all dust, and the dust didn't count, and so the vow floated free, anchored to nothing but me, and I anchored myself to it, and the circle closed, and I called it a life.

At the first bell past midnight I finished the accounts. Balanced to the copper. I signed the page — *Xanthe Voss, Swordspeaker, by oath bound* — and blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to my sword hum its low, patient note, like a struck bell that had been ringing for eighty years and could not remember how to stop.

And then, from far away, from everywhere, from underneath —

a sound like silk tearing.

Long. Slow. Enormous. The sound of something that had held for centuries deciding, all at once, to stop.

I was at the window before I knew I'd moved, and the Nine Witnesses were burning in the cold black sky —

all nine of them, cloudless and clear,

and then, between one breath and the next, they went out.
