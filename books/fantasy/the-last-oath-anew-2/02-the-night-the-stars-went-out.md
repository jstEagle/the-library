## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The tearing began, as catastrophes often do, with something small and domestic.

I was in the counting room, balancing the evening ledger by the light of a single oil lamp, when I heard the sound — faint, distant, like silk ripping somewhere beyond the edge of hearing. A long, slow pull. The kind of sound that made the small hairs on my forearms lift and lie down again, uncertain.

I set down my pen. The ink pooled at the nib's tip, a black bead trembling but refusing to fall.

"Did you hear that?" Wren Solace asked from the doorway. They had appeared there without my noticing, which was usual; Wren moved through the guildhall like a draft of warm air, arriving before you registered their absence. They were still in their apprentice grays, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink stains on their fingers from copying appraisal forms they had no business copying yet.

"Heard what?" said Master Corvane from the hall behind them, though his tone said he had heard it too.

"The wards," Wren said. "They sounded like—"

"Tearing," I finished. "Silk."

We looked at one another across the lamplit room, three people who traded in impossible things and therefore knew, better than most, how rarely the impossible announced itself politely.

* * *

The Guild of Improbable Exchanges stood on Vellum Street the way it had stood for four hundred years: squat, stone-shouldered, its windows lit amber against the dark, its cellar vaults full of bottled thunder, saint's knuckles, the last breath of a drowned queen. Above us, the city of Carreth settled into its ordinary night. Bells rang the ninth hour from the Cathedral of Measured Time. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. Somewhere a mother called her children home through streets that had carried those same calls for centuries.

I climbed to the roof because my oath-sword told me to.

That requires explanation, though I have never been good at explaining the sword. It was called Verity's Edge, and it had been passed down through nine generations of swordspeakers, each bound to the guild by the Oath of Keeping — an oath spoken in blood at nineteen years old, as mine had been, binding tongue and blade to the guild's protection until death unmade the bond or the guild released it. The sword did not speak. But it hummed. On quiet nights it hummed contentment, a low vibration felt in the wrist bones rather than heard. When lies were told near it, it sang sharp. When danger gathered, it warmed.

Tonight it was humming wrongness — a note below any note, felt in my teeth, in the roots of my hair, in the hollow behind my sternum where I kept the oath itself, folded small.

The roof was cold under my boots. I walked to the parapet and looked north, toward the border, three hundred miles away, and saw —

I saw nothing.

That is not poetry. That is the plainest accounting I can give. The stars over the northern quarter of the sky were gone. Not clouded, not dimmed — *gone*, as though a hand had wiped them from the black, leaving a smoothness where light should have been. And the smoothness was spreading southward, unhurried, patient as spilled ink finding the grain of paper.

Below that spreading nothing, where the border hills should have caught the last rose-colored memory of sunset, the horizon simply stopped. There was no line where land met sky. There was no land. There was a flatness, a blankness, a page torn out of the world's great atlas — and the tear was moving.

Behind me, boots on the roof stairs. Wren emerged first, then Master Corvane, then a scatter of guild members — old Halvard the appraiser, the twins Sef and Mira from acquisitions, Brother Anselm who kept our reliquaries and had not left the guildhall in eleven years. They crowded the parapet and made the sounds people make when language fails: small, involuntary syllables, gasps shaped like prayers cut short.

"It's the wards," Corvane said. His voice was steady, because Corvane's voice was always steady; he had negotiated for dragon eggs and dead men's shadows without raising it. "The border wards are failing. That's all. The Wardens' Circle will restore them. We hold position."

"Hold position," Halvard repeated, faintly. "Master, the sky is coming off."

"The *sky* is doing nothing of the sort." But his knuckles were white on the parapet stone, and I noticed that his eyes refused to travel north, fixed instead on the familiar rooftops of Vellum Street as though memorizing them against a loss he would not name.

Verity's Edge shivered against my hip like a frightened animal.

* * *

We went below, because Corvane ordered it, and because below there were ledgers and lamps and the comforting weight of routine. He stationed people at their posts with the calm of a man bailing water from a boat he insists is seaworthy. I took the entry hall, sword drawn, because the oath demanded vigilance and vigilance was the one prayer I had ever learned.

The guildhall felt different. That is the only word I trust: *different*. The stones underfoot seemed less certain of being stones. The lamp flames burned a half-shade too pale. When the cathedral bells rang the tenth hour, the sound arrived slightly late, as if traveling farther than it used to.

Wren found me there, of course. They carried two cups of tea and pressed one into my free hand without asking, and we stood together in the entry hall watching the door as though it might confess something.

"You feel it too," they said quietly.

"The sword feels it. I'm just holding the sword."

"That's not true, Xanthe." Their voice had that careful quality it took on whenever they skirted the edges of something larger. "You've been feeling it since sundown. Before the sound. You told me the accounts wouldn't balance — every column off by the same fraction. That's not arithmetic failing. That's the world."

I drank the tea. It tasted of ash, though the leaves had been sound that morning.

"Wren." I turned to face them. In the lamplight their face was young and old at once — young in feature, old in the watchfulness behind the eyes, a watchfulness I had never been able to source. "If the wards fall — if whatever this is reaches Carreth — what does your oath tell you?"

"To hold position." A ghost of a smile. "What does yours?"

"To keep what cannot be kept." I recited the words the way one recites a scar: *I am the blade at the threshold. What the guild holds, I hold. Where the guild stands, I stand. Until the last account is balanced.* Nine generations had spoken them. Eight were dust. I was the last, and the words sat in me like a swallowed stone.

"And if the guild is standing nowhere?" Wren asked softly.

Before I could answer, the second sound came.

It was louder now, closer — the tearing again, silk ripped from selvage to selvage, and beneath it a silence so complete it pressed against the ears like deep water. Every lamp in the guildhall guttered. Verity's Edge screamed — there is no other word; the hum became a shriek of wrongness that rattled my molars — and above us, through the high windows of the stairwell, the light changed.

Not darkened. *Changed.* It went the color of unwritten paper.

Corvane's voice came down the stairwell, still steady, still infuriatingly steady: "All hands to stations! We hold position! The guild has stood four hundred years and it will stand tonight!"

But I was already climbing, Wren at my heels, up and up into the stairwell's paper-colored light, out onto the roof where the others stood frozen at the parapet, and I saw —

The southern sky was gone now too. The whole dome of heaven, erased, replaced by that smooth pale nothing, and beneath it the horizon had stopped on every side. No hills. No distant beacon fires of the watchtowers. No moonrise. The city remained — Carreth, our Carreth, rooftops and spires and ten thousand lit windows — but it sat inside the nothing like a coin at the bottom of a dry well, surrounded on all sides by a country that had stopped existing.

And the stars. The stars were simply *not there*. I had seen cloudy nights. I had seen fog, storm, smoke. This was none of those. This was subtraction. Somewhere above us, everything that should have been shining had been removed, carefully, thoroughly, the way an auditor removes a falsified line from a ledger.

An auditor. I do not know why that word came to me, or why it fit so perfectly, or why it made my blood go cold in a way the cold wind could not.

"The roads," Mira whispered beside me. She had come up carrying her acquisition satchel as though inventory might save us. "Look at the roads going north. Look."

The great North Road ran from the city gates toward the border hills. Or it had run. Now it ran thirty yards past the gates and *ended* — not fading, not vanishing into distance, but ending, cleanly, its cobblestones simply ceasing, and beyond the cessation lay the pale smooth nothing, and the cessation was nearer than it had been when we came up.

"It's eating the miles," Sef breathed. "It's — it's unwriting them."

"We hold position," Corvane said again, but his voice cracked on *position*, and I watched the word die in his mouth.

Brother Anselm dropped to his knees and began to pray. The prayer made no echo. I noticed that. The guildhall roof had always given back echoes; now the words fell out of the air like stones into deep water, unanswered, unheard, perhaps unwritten even as they were spoken.

Verity's Edge trembled in my grip. And then, for the first time in nine generations of my bloodline, the sword spoke — not in sound, but in certainty, a knowledge poured directly into the marrow of my arm:

*The oath is fraying. The thing you swore to protect is being taken apart, and the oath will be taken apart with it. Hold, and be unwritten. Move, and perhaps remain.*

"Xanthe." Wren's hand closed over my wrist. Their pulse hammered against my skin, faster than a human pulse, faster than any pulse I'd ever felt — another of those small strangenesses about Wren Solace that I had catalogued and never questioned. Their face was lifted to the annihilated sky, and there were tears on it, but their jaw was set like someone reading a verdict. "The vaults. The ledger-vaults, the bottled things — if the country goes, everything in the guild goes. Everyone."

"Corvane says hold position."

"Corvane is holding a sinking ship because it's the only order he knows." They turned to me, and their eyes in the paper-light were not quite any color I could name. "You're the swordspeaker. The oath is yours. Not his. Yours. So tell me, Xanthe Voss — what does keeping mean, when the thing kept is already gone?"

The North Road ended forty yards from the gates now. Twenty-five. The pale nothing ate the distance between heartbeats, and beneath my feet I felt the guildhall's stones begin, very faintly, to forget themselves.

I looked at Wren — apprentice, riddle, the only person in four hundred years whose company had made the loneliness of my office bearable — and I sheathed a screaming sword, and I said the words that broke four hundred years of holding:

"Not here. Run."

* * *

We went down through the guildhall shouting the alarm, and the guild fractured around us — some obeying Corvane's iron command to hold, some grabbing children and coin and bottled miracles and fleeing into a city whose streets now curved in directions they had never curved before. Wren stayed at my shoulder, refusing the safety I twice ordered them toward, and when I rounded on them at the guildhall doors, they only shook their head.

"I'm not staying behind," they said. "Whatever's out there — it doesn't frighten me the way it frightens you. I think—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I think I know what it is. Or part of it. And I think I might know someone who can read what's happening. Someone you'll hate."

Beyond the doors, the paper-colored dark pressed close, and the last star I would ever see in the sky of my birth country went out like a snuffed candle.

Wren took my hand.

"Trust me," they said, "one more impossible time."

The doors opened onto a world that no longer existed, and we stepped through.
