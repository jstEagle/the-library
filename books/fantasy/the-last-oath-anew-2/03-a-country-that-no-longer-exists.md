## 3. A Country That No Longer Exists

Wren Solace had always been able to feel the shape of a city the way other people felt weather.

It was not a gift they had ever named aloud. It lived somewhere behind their sternum, a second sense that read streets like sentences and rooftops like signatures. In Vellharrow, the guild city of canals and copper spires, the geography had always hummed with a particular confidence — the smug certainty of a place that had stood for nine hundred years and expected to stand for nine hundred more. Every road knew where it was going. Every bridge remembered why it had been built.

Walking out of the Guild of Improbable Exchanges into the pre-dawn dark, Wren felt nothing of the kind.

The silence was wrong first. Not quiet — Vellharrow was never quiet; even at this hour the night-soil carts rattled over the cobbles and the fishwives were already quarreling down by the Saltmarket. But there was no sound now, only a hush so total it pressed against the eardrums like water. Then there was the sky.

No stars. Not clouded-over, not veiled — *gone*, as though someone had reached up with an enormous wet cloth and wiped them from the bowl of heaven. Where the constellation called the Nine Sisters should have hung above the eastern towers, there was only smooth black, the color of a page before ink.

And beneath Wren's sternum, where the city's hum usually sang, there was a wound.

"Oh," Wren whispered to the empty street. "Oh, you poor thing."

They had meant to say something braver. Something worthy of the oath-bound swordspeaker who had just stormed past them into the dark, all sharp jaw and sharper refusal, ordering Wren to stay behind in tones that made it perfectly clear staying behind was not an option Wren intended to exercise. Xanthe Voss walked ahead of them now, half a street's distance, their inherited sword drawn and humming its low wrongness, and Wren followed because following Xanthe Voss had become, without their ever deciding it, simply what they did.

But it was the city Wren grieved for. Because Wren could see what Xanthe could not: the seams.

* * *

Vellharrow's streets were intact. That was the horror of it. The cobbles sat where cobbles belonged, the gutters ran their proper courses, the lamplighters' poles still leaned in their brackets by the Tanner's Gate. A stranger would have seen a city, whole and sleeping.

Wren saw a sentence with the verbs torn out.

The Canal District looped back on itself. Wren knew it the way one knows one has been dreaming: they turned left at the Weeping Widow fountain, walked ninety paces along the Cloisterwater — and arrived again at the Weeping Widow fountain, though the fountain's moss had grown a season older between arrivals. The second time, the water in it was flowing uphill. The third time, the fountain was dry, and a ring of pale dust marked where the water had been, as if even the canal district's memories were evaporating.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice came out smaller than intended. "We're walking in circles."

"We're walking toward the Ashgate." Xanthe didn't slow. "I've done it a thousand times."

"You've done it in a country that existed."

That stopped them. Xanthe turned, and in the starless dark their face was all angles and shadow, the oath-sword casting a faint sickly gleam along the cobbles. "Say that again."

Wren swallowed. There it was, then — the moment they had dreaded since childhood, arriving not with drama but with necessity. Some lies kept people safe. This one would not keep anyone safe anymore.

"I can read places," Wren said. "Not maps — *places*. The true shape under the paving stones. I've never told anyone because—" Because I don't know what I am, they didn't say. "Xanthe, the roads aren't looping. They're *unwriting*. The country is being erased from underneath us, and what we're walking through is just... the memory of pavement. It won't hold."

"How do you know that?" Xanthe's hand had not moved toward their sword, which was either trust or shock. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I'm not entirely sure I exist," Wren said, and hated how it sounded — like a confession at a dinner party. "And things like me can tell when something else stops."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead, where stars should have been, the black seemed to deepen, attentive, listening. Wren had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the sky itself was taking notes.

"Later," Xanthe said finally, and the word was rough as a whetstone. "You'll tell me everything later. Right now you're going to show me what you see."

* * *

What Wren saw, once they let themselves truly look, nearly put them on their knees.

Vellharrow was fraying at its edges like a rug dragged backward through a doorway. At the center — the Guild, the Saltmarket, the cathedral square — the city still held its shape, dense with use and memory. But toward the borders the streets grew translucent, their stones going grey and grainy as old bone. And beyond the last rooftops there was no countryside at all. There was white.

Not fog. Not snow. White like an unwritten page held too close to the eyes, a blankness that hurt to look at directly, and it was creeping inward, patient as tide, eating the outer districts street by street.

"The wards fell last night," Wren said. They were standing on the roof of the Saltmarket granary because the ground level had begun, subtly, to disagree with itself. From up here the pattern was legible — if legible was the right word for a language made of loss. "The wards weren't just protection. They were the country's *signature*. Proof it had been written into the world. When they tore—"

"The sound," Xanthe said quietly. "Like silk."

"Like a contract being voided. Everything inside the border is being returned to draft. Unwritten." Wren hugged their arms around themselves against a cold that had nothing to do with weather. "People too, maybe. Eventually. Places go first because they're easiest to erase — no one argues with a street. But people argue. People remember being remembered."

As if summoned by the words, a light appeared below them.

It was a small, stubborn thing — a lantern in the mouth of the alley off Cooper's Row, guttering but determined, and beside it a figure wrapped in a travel cloak, hunched over something spread across a cart's tailboard. Writing. The figure was writing, furiously, by lantern-light, while around her the alley walls shimmered like heat-haze.

"She's holding it together," Wren breathed. "Look — the alley's solid around her. She's *anchoring* it somehow."

They climbed down. As they approached, the woman looked up, and Wren saw a face carved by wind and guilt: sixty years or so of it, grey hair escaping its braid, eyes the flat grey-green of a chart's open sea. Her hands were stained with ink to the wrist. On the cart's tailboard lay a map of Vellharrow — but drawn in a style Wren had never seen, every line doubled and trebled, as if she were inking the same street over and over, desperately, trying to make the strokes stick.

"Ilsa Marrow," Xanthe said, and the name came out like a blade leaving a sheath.

The woman flinched. "Swordspeaker." Her voice was smoke-rasped. "You should be dead by morning. You should all be—"

"So should you, traitor. You sold the guild's routes to the Reachmen. You drew the maps they used to breach the western passes. Forty-one guild agents died because your ink was good."

"Yes." No denial, no flinch this time. Just the word, set down like a stone. "Yes, I did. And I have spent eleven years drawing every map I could find of every place the guild ever loved, trying to fill the hole I made." She gestured at the sky, at the white horizon, at the shimmering alley. "It appears the debt has come due with interest."

Wren looked at the map on the tailboard and their breath caught. Because now, close, they could feel what the woman was doing. The map wasn't a record. It was a *tourniquet*. Every line she re-inked, the corresponding street firmed a little — the alley's shimmer steadying each time her pen crossed the paper. She was fighting the erasure with cartography, one obsessive stroke at a time, and losing, and continuing anyway.

"You're not selling anything anymore," Wren said softly.

Ilsa Marrow's grey eyes cut to them, and for the first time something moved behind them — surprise, perhaps, or hope's dangerous cousin. "No," she said. "Nothing left to sell. Only left to save. Which is more than I deserve, and less than I need." She rolled the map with hands that shook only slightly. "You're heading for the Door. Everyone who understands even a fraction of what's happening is heading for the Door."

Xanthe's jaw tightened. "How do you know about the Door?"

"Because I drew it. Thirty years ago, when I was young and the guild trusted me with its oldest charts. The Door of Anew, past the Ashfall Reach." She tied the map's cord with fingers ink-black to the knuckle. "Three days' travel, if the terrain lets you. It won't want to let you. Nothing here wants anything anymore except to stop existing, and it will pull you toward that rest like a river pulls a swimmer." She looked from Xanthe to Wren and back, weighing them, and Wren felt the weight of the look like a hand on a scale. "You need me. Hate it all you like, Swordspeaker. But I'm the only person left in this country who knows the way to the exit — because I'm the only person left who ever bothered to draw it."

The starless dark leaned closer. Down Cooper's Row, the far end of the alley had gone soft as wet paper, its cobbles fading to suggestion. The white was patient. The white had all the time in the world, which was precisely how much time they had none of.

Xanthe's sword hummed. Their face, when they turned to Wren, was a closed ledger — but Wren had learned, over two years of stolen glances and careful distances, to read the marginalia. What was written there now was grief and fury and a loneliness so old it had fossilized into duty.

"One chance," Xanthe said to Ilsa Marrow. "You betray us once more — once — and I will leave you for the white."

"Fair terms," said the cartographer, and began packing her ink.

Wren looked up one last time at the blank where the Nine Sisters should have been, and felt the country shudder beneath them like a sleeper turning over in a bed that was no longer there. Three days. One traitor. One swordspeaker who hadn't yet asked the question Wren most feared.

And somewhere past the ash, a door that demanded a price no one had yet named.
