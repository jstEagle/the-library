## 5. Ashfall Reach

The ash began at the border of what had been the border.

There was no line to cross anymore — Ilsa's maps showed roads that looped like intestines, villages that appeared and unappeared depending on which direction you squinted — but Xanthe felt it anyway, a change in the air like stepping from a sunlit room into a cellar. The sky above the Ashfall Reach was not gray so much as unfinished, a ceiling of smeared charcoal that wept fine white flakes, soft as moth wings, endless as remorse.

It fell on their shoulders. It fell in their hair. And it fell in *words*.

Xanthe saw the first one an hour into the Reach, drifting down slow as a feather: a single glyph of Old Vessian, a language dead before the guild's founding, rotating gently as it descended. It landed on the back of Wren's hand and lay there, luminous faintly, like a coal gone cold. Wren stared at it until it faded, then wiped their palm on their coat as though brushing off something alive.

"Don't read them," Ilsa said without turning around. She walked ahead with her rolled maps strapped across her back like a quiver, her limp more pronounced since the city, since everything. "That's the trick of this place. It wants you reading. It wants you *interpreting*. The moment you decide what a thing means out here, you've given it permission to be true."

"And if we don't read them?" Wren asked.

"Then they're just ash." Ilsa's voice was flat as slate. "Just weather. Just a country dying politely."

But Xanthe read them. Couldn't help it. The oath-sword at their hip hummed low and constant now, a tuning fork struck against the world's wrongness, and through that hum the glyphs resolved one by one as they drifted down: *remember*, said one. *Forgotten*, said another. A third spelled out a name — *Halveth, miller of Lowbridge* — and dissolved before it touched ground, and Xanthe understood, with a lurch, that these were not messages. They were *leavings*. The ash was made of people. Everything the unwriting erased had to go somewhere, and here it went: up into the dead sky, sifted fine, and rained back down over the only terrain left willing to hold it.

They were walking through the sediment of their own nation.

Xanthe kept this to themselves. There was no profit in saying it aloud, and Xanthe Voss had spent thirty years believing there was no profit in anything that wasn't appraisable. That was the guild way. That was the oath way. *I weigh all things, and am weighed by none.* Their father's oath, inherited whole, spoken at fourteen with a hand on the ledger and a sword flat against their tongue. They had never once thought to ask whether they'd been old enough to know what they were promising.

The first night they sheltered in the lee of a fallen milestone, huddled around a fire Ilsa built from map-parchments she claimed were duplicates. Wren slept sitting up, chin on chest, breathing in a rhythm too even to be natural — Xanthe had noticed that weeks ago and catalogued it under *apprentice eccentricities*, back when apprentice eccentricities were the strangest thing in their life. Now the list of strange things had eaten the list of ordinary ones entire.

The second night there was no shelter to find, and so they found the chapel instead.

It shouldn't have been there. Ilsa's newest map — drawn that morning, redrawn twice by noon because the ink kept crawling — showed open moor for six miles in every direction. But there it stood at dusk: a small stone chapel with a squat bell tower, its door standing open, warm light in the windows that flickered like candleflame but cast no heat when Xanthe held their hand near it.

"It wasn't here," Ilsa said.

"Neither were we," Wren said. "Technically."

Xanthe drew the oath-sword an inch and listened. The hum didn't change. Whatever the chapel was, it wasn't hungry. "We take watches," they said. "Nobody sleeps deep."

Inside, the chapel was exactly what it pretended to be: wooden pews worn smooth by generations of shoulders, a stone altar, a window of colored glass depicting some saint whose face had been deliberately scraped away. Candles burned on the altar without consuming themselves. Along the walls, hundreds of small paper slips had been wedged into the mortar — prayers, Xanthe realized, the kind the faithful wrote and posted so their words would keep working after they left. They pulled one free and read it.

*Blessed keeper, my daughter cannot sleep. She says there is something counting her. Make the counting stop.*

Xanthe put it back carefully, the way one returns a knife to its sheath.

Ilsa took the corner pew, wrapped her cloak around herself, and was asleep almost immediately — the sleep of someone who had decided, consciously, to stop spending energy on vigilance. Wren lit nothing, needed nothing lit; their eyes caught the candlelight and gave it back doubled, faintly gold, faintly wrong. They stood at the altar a long moment, looking up at the faceless saint.

"There's no god here," Wren said quietly. "I can tell. There's usually... a pressure. A listening. Even in empty temples there's a shape where the listening would go." They touched the altar stone. "This one's just a hole. Prayers fall in and nothing catches them."

"Then why do the candles burn?"

"Courtesy," Wren said. "Or habit. Buildings remember their jobs longer than people do."

They took first watch together because neither of them suggested otherwise, and because suggesting otherwise would have required knowing why.

* * *

The chapel settled into night around them the way a ship settles into water. Outside, the ash fell in slow spirals, and through the scraped-saint window Xanthe could see the glyphs catching the moonless dark, glowing faintly as they turned: syllables of languages that had died with the empires that spoke them, names of towns that no longer remembered being towns. Once, a whole sentence came down intact, drifting past the window like a leaf on still water: *WE HELD THE LINE AS LONG AS WE COULD.*

Xanthe read it three times before looking away.

"You're doing the thing Ilsa told us not to do," Wren murmured. They had taken a seat on the altar steps, knees drawn up, and Xanthe realized they hadn't been watching the ash — they'd been watching Xanthe watch it.

"I'm a guildsman. Reading is my trade. Appraising. Weighing." Xanthe flexed their sword-hand, scarred knuckles cracking softly. "My whole life has been deciding what things are worth."

"And what's this worth?" Wren nodded at the ash, the chapel, the world coming apart at its seams.

"Nothing," Xanthe said. "Everything. I don't—" They stopped. Started again, slower. "When I took the oath, I thought value was a fixed thing. A bottled memory of your mother's kitchen: forty crowns, because grief is rare and grief is wanted. A favor owed by a duke: negotiable. My word: absolute, because the guild's credit rests on it." They laughed, and it came out wrong, hollowed. "Now the guild is a building full of ledgers in a country that doesn't exist, and I keep catching myself trying to appraise the *sky*."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, gently: "What did the sentence say? The one you read three times."

"*We held the line as long as we could.*"

Wren closed their eyes briefly, as if the words had weight enough to bruise. When they opened them, they looked older than their years, and their years were already difficult to guess. "Do you think anyone will ever read us?"

The question sat between them like a third person at the watch.

"I used to think about that," Xanthe admitted. The confession came easier in the dark, in a church with no god, in the company of someone who was themselves a question mark wearing a coat. "At the guild, after closing. Balancing accounts alone at midnight. Everyone else had somewhere to be. Family, lovers, debts of the ordinary kind. And I'd sit there thinking: someday the ledger will be balanced, and the vault sealed, and I'll have kept every term of the oath perfectly, and there won't be a living soul who witnessed any of it. Perfection with no witnesses. Duty performed for an audience of paper." Their thumb traced the pommel of the oath-sword, worn smooth by four generations of thumbs. "I told myself that was the point. That faith unobserved was the purest kind."

"Do you still tell yourself that?"

"No." The word came out smaller than Xanthe expected. "Now I think I just liked having a wall between me and everyone. And called the wall a religion."

The candles guttered — all of them at once, though no wind moved — and steadied. In the recovered light, Wren was watching Xanthe with an expression Xanthe had catalogued many times over two years of stolen glances across appraisal tables and had always filed under *unreadable*. Tonight, with nothing left to hide behind, it read plainly: hope, terrified of itself.

"Can I say something improper?" Wren asked. "Given that we may die in a field of falling names tomorrow."

"You've been improper since the day I hired you."

"I didn't hire myself. You *chose* me. Out of forty applicants. I've always wondered—" Wren stopped, laughed shakily. "No. What I mean is: tonight, on this watch, I keep thinking that if this is the last night of the world, or of us, I don't want to spend it being careful. I've been careful my whole existence, and I'm not even sure I've *had* a whole existence. I might be—"

"A scrap of the unwritten," Xanthe said quietly. "Yes. I know. Or I suspect. I've suspected since the stars went out and you didn't flinch the way humans flinch."

Wren went very still. "And you kept me on anyway."

"I kept you on because when the wards fell, everyone ran for cover, and you ran toward the sound." Xanthe heard their own voice drop, roughen. "I've spent my life weighing things, Wren Solace. You are the only entry I've never been able to price. And I've finally stopped wanting to."

The silence that followed was the good kind — full rather than empty. Somewhere above, the ash kept sifting down, patient as snowfall over a grave. Wren crossed the space between the altar steps and the pew, unhurried, giving Xanthe every chance to look away, and Xanthe did not look away. What passed between them then was not a grand declaration; there was no time left in the world for grandeur. It was a hand finding a hand. Foreheads touching. The oath-sword, between them, humming — and for the first time since the wards fell, Xanthe noticed, the hum had changed pitch. Softer. Almost content.

"Guilt's going to come," Xanthe murmured against Wren's hair. "Wanting a future while the whole country burns upward. It's already queuing up."

"Let it queue," Wren said. "It can wait its turn."

They kept the watch together until the candles burned down to nothing and the light in the windows died honestly, at last, like real flames. It was Xanthe who woke Ilsa for the final shift — and found her already sitting up, her oldest map spread across her knees, her face gone the color of the ash outside.

"The Reach is done rewriting," she said. "It's started *erasing*. Look at the eastern edge — the ink isn't crawling anymore. It's fading." Her finger trembled on the parchment. "Whatever unwrote our country, Xanthe — it knows where we are. And it's coming to collect."

Outside, far across the moor, the falling ash had begun to spell a single word, over and over, vast letters of white settling on the black land:

*AUDIT.*
