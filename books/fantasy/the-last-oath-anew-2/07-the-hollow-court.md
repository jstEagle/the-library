## 7. The Hollow Court

The summons came at the hour when even ash stops falling, as if the sky itself had been told to hold its breath.

Wren felt it first in their teeth — a pressure like a word forming behind a closed door. Then the light changed. Not dimmed; *reorganized*. The gray murk of the Ashfall Reach drew itself into clean vertical lines, and those lines became walls, and the walls became a corridor of pale stone that had not existed a heartbeat before. The stones were warm. That was the worst part. Everything in this unwritten country had been cold for days, and these walls were warm as a living throat.

"Xanthe," Wren said, and their voice came out flattened, as though the corridor had already begun editing it.

Xanthe turned from the fire they'd been coaxing against a dead chapel wall. The oath-sword was in their hand before Wren finished speaking — they always drew fast now, since Ilsa, since the hollow soldiers had come out of the dark wearing names like borrowed coats. Their eyes went from the corridor to Wren's face, and whatever they saw there made them go very still.

"Don't answer it," Xanthe said.

"I haven't been *asked* anything yet."

"You have. You just haven't heard the question." Xanthe crossed the ash in three strides and caught Wren's wrist — the same wrist, Wren noticed distantly, that had been aching since sundown, since before sundown, since perhaps always. "It doesn't summon with trumpets, Wren. It summons with *paperwork*. If you're feeling it, it's already filled out your file."

Behind them, the third survivor — a ferryman named Osk who had followed them since the river that flowed uphill — made a small sound and sat down hard in the ash. He had stopped asking questions two days ago. Wren envied him that.

The corridor waited. It was very patient. Patience was, Wren understood suddenly, the Auditor's native language.

"I have to see it," Wren said.

"No."

"It showed me something already. Last night, in the fire. It's been showing me things for—" Wren stopped. Counted. The number that came back was not one they remembered learning. "I don't know how long. Xanthe, I need to know what it knows. Because I think it knows *me* better than I do."

Xanthe's grip tightened. In the gray light their face was all planes and stubbornness, the face of someone who had spent a lifetime holding doors shut with their spine. "Whatever it shows you, it will be a lie shaped like a truth. That's how it unwrote a whole country — not with armies. With *amendments*."

"Then let me read the amendments." Wren turned their wrist free, gently, the way you take your hand back from someone you love. "If I don't walk in, it walks in. You know that. It's been circling us like ink around a blot. I'd rather meet the pen than wait for it."

For a long moment Xanthe said nothing. The fire spat. Somewhere far off, the horizon made a sound like a page turning.

"Take this." Xanthe pressed the oath-sword's scabbard into Wren's hands — the sword itself stayed sheathed, humming its low wrongness, the sound it had made since the night the stars went out. "It's oath-bound, not Wren-bound. If what's in there tries to make you into a *record*, it'll object. Loudly."

"And if I'm not there to hear it object?"

"Then I'll come in after you," Xanthe said, "and I will be extremely rude to a faceless bureaucracy until it gives you back."

Despite everything — the ash, the dying country, the corridor warm as a throat — Wren laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"I'm a swordspeaker. Romance is a form of threat assessment." But Xanthe's hand lingered on Wren's a moment longer than threat assessment required, and then stepped back, and the corridor's mouth opened a little wider, as if it had been listening and had found this exchange *acceptable*.

Wren walked in.

* * *

The Hollow Court had no ceiling. Where a ceiling should have been there was sky — but not their sky, not the starless felt-gray the country had worn since the wards fell. This was the sky from *ledgers*. Ink-dark, ruled with faint lines, and moving: constellations of numbers drifting in slow formation, being counted, recounted, found correct.

Wren stood on a floor of polished slate. Before them rose a desk of black wood, and behind the desk sat the Auditor.

It had no face. It had, instead, a *filing system* — a suggestion of features the way a form suggests a name: a blank where eyes would certify identity, a smooth plane where a mouth would affix a seal. Its body was a robe of gray paper, and the paper moved constantly, pages turning over one another in a sound like distant rain. When it spoke, the voice came not from it but from *everywhere the paper rustled*, which was everywhere.

"APPLICANT," it said. "PLEASE BE SEATED."

There was no chair. Wren remained standing. "I'm not applying for anything."

"A CORRECTION, THEN." A page turned somewhere in the vast dark above. "YOU ARE CORRECT. YOU ARE NOT APPLYING. YOU ARE *BEING PROCESSED*."

"Processed for what?"

The Auditor did not answer. Instead it lifted a hand — a hand made of stacked documents, fingernails of wax seals — and placed on the desk a single sheet. It was blank. It was the most complete blank Wren had ever seen; it was not empty but *unwritten*, and looking at it was like looking into a well and hearing no bottom.

"DO YOU KNOW WHAT THIS IS?"

"No."

"IT IS YOU."

The words went through Wren like a blade going through wet paper — no resistance, just a clean cold line. "That's a lie," they said, and their voice shook. "I have a mother in Coriel's Bend. I have an apprenticeship. I have—"

"EXHIBITS," the Auditor said, almost gently, and the paper-robe rustled like a shrug. "YOU HAVE EXHIBITS. WOULD YOU LIKE TO SEE THE ORIGINALS?"

The blank sheet rose from the desk. And as it rose, it *wrote itself* — no, Wren thought, it *unwrote itself*, lines of text lifting off the page like smoke and dissolving, and each line as it dissolved was a memory: a kitchen in Coriel's Bend that had never existed; a mother's hands that had never kneaded that bread; the first day at the Guild, the smell of ink and tallow, the guild master's voice saying *you'll do, small one* — all of it lifting away, all of it *filing itself* into the drifting constellations overhead, where the numbers received it without comment.

Wren was on their knees. They did not remember kneeling.

"WHAT ARE YOU?" the Auditor asked, and it was not cruelty. That was the horror of it. It was *procedure*. "ANSWER CAREFULLY. YOUR ANSWER WILL BE COMPARED AGAINST THE RECORD."

"I'm—" Wren's mouth worked. "Wren Solace. Apprentice of the Guild of Improbable Exchanges. I'm—"

"COMPARING." A pause, in which entire galaxies of figures turned over. "DISCREPANCY DETECTED. THERE IS NO WREN SOLACE. THERE IS NO GUILD OF IMPROBABLE EXCHANGES. THERE IS NO COUNTRY IN WHICH EITHER COULD HAVE EXISTED. THE RECORD SHOWS A SCRAP. A MARGIN-NOTE. A PIECE OF THE UNWRITTEN THAT CLUNG TO THE EDGES WHEN THE PAGE WAS CLEANED, AND GREW. GIVEN FLESH BY PROXIMITY TO AN OATH TOO STRONG TO ERASE." The blank sheet drifted down until it hovered before Wren's face, and in its nothingness Wren saw — reflected, or remembered, or *drafted* — a shape like themselves, but unfinished, edges dissolving into paper-grain. "YOU ARE NOT A PERSON, APPLICANT. YOU ARE A *LEFTOVER*. AND LEFTOVERS," the Auditor said, with the terrible tenderness of a clerk closing a file, "ARE MY JURISDICTION."

The despair did not crash over Wren. It *seeped*, the way damp seeps into a map and blurs the borders — slow, total, from the inside. Because the Auditor was not lying. Wren could feel it not lying, the way you feel a plumb line hang true. Every memory they reached for had that faint paper-grain at its edges. Their mother's face: paper-grain. The guild hall's stairs, the third one that creaked: paper-grain. Even Xanthe's voice saying *extremely rude to a faceless bureaucracy* — even that, at its furthest edge, was slightly *textured*, as though it had been written rather than lived.

"WHICH BRINGS US TO THE OFFER," the Auditor said.

"I don't want an offer."

"EVERYONE WANTS AN OFFER. IT IS WHY OFFERS EXIST." A drawer slid open in the black desk, soundlessly. From it the Auditor lifted a second sheet — and this one was *full*. Dense, elegant script, margins crowded with annotations in a hand Wren knew better than their own: Xanthe's. The guild ledgers. The true ledgers, the ones that recorded every impossible thing the Guild had ever traded — every bottled memory, every borrowed year, every debt of luck — the ledger Xanthe had carried out of the burning archive on the second night, strapped to their back like a shield.

"RESTORATION," the Auditor said. "THE COUNTRY RESTORED. THE STARS RELIT. YOUR MOTHER'S KITCHEN MADE REAL — NOT REMEMBERED, *REAL*, WITH BREAD AND ALL. THE GUILD REFOUNDED. YOUR APPRENTICESHIP COMPLETED. YOU, MADE WHOLE: A PERSON WITH A PAST THAT HOLDS." The paper-robe leaned forward, pages whispering. "THE PRICE IS MODEST. THE LEDGERS. THE SWORD. AND THE OATH-BEARER'S COOPERATION, WHICH I AM TOLD CAN BE ARRANGED BY SOMEONE HE TRUSTS."

"You want me to betray him."

"I WANT YOU TO *FILE* HIM," the Auditor corrected. "THERE IS A DIFFERENCE. BETRAYAL REQUIRES LOYALTY. YOU HAVE NONE. YOU CANNOT HAVE ANY. LOYALTY REQUIRES A SELF, AND WE HAVE ESTABLISHED—" the blank sheet fluttered, almost coy, "—THAT THE SELF IS PENDING REVIEW."

And there it was. The seduction of despair, laid out on black wood with a clerk's neat hand: *you were never real, so nothing you choose is real, so you may as well choose what is offered.* Wren felt it pull at them like the river that flowed uphill, like the roads that looped home. It would be so easy. It would be *restful*. To stop being a question and become an answer, even a bad one. To be filed.

Their hand found the scabbard at their hip.

The oath-sword hummed. Not its usual wrongness — something sharper, *objection-shaped*, and through the hum Wren heard, faint and impossible, a voice like Xanthe's saying: *it'll object. Loudly.* And beneath the sword's objection, quieter but steadier, another voice — Ilsa's, the dying cartographer's, the woman who had sold maps and bought back her soul at the Ashfall's edge: *the Door doesn't open for oaths kept. It opens for oaths broken and remade.*

Remade. Wren looked up at the faceless clerk, at the drifting constellations of other people's erased lives, and felt something in them set — not a foundation, they had no foundation, but an *edge*. A margin. A place where the ink stopped and the hand began.

"Here's my answer for the record," Wren said, standing. Their legs shook. They stood anyway. "You're right. I might be a scrap. A margin-note. A leftover that grew." They took the blank sheet — *themselves* — off the air, and the paper was warm as the corridor walls, warm as a living throat, and they held it up between them like a mirror. "But a scrap that grew is still *growing*. And here's the discrepancy *you* missed, Auditor: leftovers don't fall in love. Leftovers don't carry dying cartographers. Leftovers don't stand in ash and choose. Whatever I am, I'm not in your files — because I'm not finished. And I don't file things that aren't finished."

They tore the sheet in half.

The sound was not paper. It was the tearing-silk sound of the border wards falling, the sound the stars had made going out, and the whole Hollow Court *flinched* — the constellations scattering, the desk shuddering, the Auditor's paper-robe rippling in what, on anything with a face, would have been rage.

"THAT," said the Auditor, and its voice came from everywhere at once, no longer patient, no longer procedural, "*WAS NOT YOURS TO DESTROY.*"

"Neither was the country," Wren said, backing toward the corridor's mouth, the sword humming like a struck bell. "Look how that's going for you."

The Court began to come apart around them — walls unruled, sky unlined — and through the widening tear Wren saw ash, and firelight, and Xanthe on their feet with their hand already out. Behind them, in the collapsing dark, the Auditor's voice pursued, no longer an offer but a *summons*, a warrant, a debt called due:

"THE APPLICANT WILL BE NOTIFIED. THE APPLICANT WILL BE *FOUND*."

Wren ran — out of the Court, out of the throat-warm stone, into Xanthe's arms and the cold honest ash — and behind them the sky began, at last, to fall.
