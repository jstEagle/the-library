## 10. Maps We Draw Ourselves

The stars here are wrong in the best possible way.

Wren noticed it first from the ridge above the camp, where they had gone to be alone with the enormity of having a body that still wanted things. Back home — *back in the country that no longer exists*, the thought still snagged like a burr in wool — the stars had been fixed, dutiful, arranged in constellations some ancient guild of sky-cartographers had long since named and catalogued and charged admission to view. These stars moved. Slowly, so slowly you could watch it happen if you were patient: a drift like embers rising off a fire, like seeds on wind, as though the whole sky were less a ceiling than a current, and everything in it was going somewhere.

Wren sat down on the cold grass and watched them go.

Below, the camp spread out along the shore of a lake that hadn't been there three days ago — or perhaps it had been here all along, waiting for someone to arrive who needed it. That was the other thing about this side of the Door: the land seemed attentive. When the first refugees stumbled through, exhausted and ash-streaked and weeping, springs had opened near the treeline. When the children grew frightened of the dark, the strange drifting stars seemed to gather overhead, thickening into a warm constellation shaped like nothing anyone could name but everyone somehow recognized.

Two hundred and eleven survivors. Wren knew the number because they had counted them through, one by one, while Xanthe held the Door with their blood running down their sword-arm and their old life tearing out of them like a tooth. Wren had counted every soul across the threshold, and then Xanthe, and then the Door had closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting gently, and the Auditor's hunger had been left on the far side with all that beautiful, terrible emptiness.

*You are a leftover scrap of the unwritten, given flesh.* The Auditor had said that, in its voice like ink drying. It had meant it as a wound.

Wren flexed their fingers against the grass and decided, again, for perhaps the hundredth time since, that it was not. A scrap, yes. Unwritten, once. But flesh was flesh, and want was want, and the stars above this new country did not care what Wren had been before they chose to be something else.

"Your tea's gone cold."

Xanthe's voice, behind them. Wren didn't turn around yet. They let the moment arrive slowly, the way the stars drifted — Xanthe Voss, oathless, unbound, hair still carrying the smell of ash and blood, lowering themselves onto the grass with the careful stiffness of someone whose body was keeping its own ledger of what the last weeks had cost. They pressed a tin cup into Wren's hands anyway, cold tea and all.

"You climbed all the way up here to deliver cold tea," Wren said.

"I climbed all the way up here because you weren't at your bedroll." Xanthe looked out over the camp, the scattered fires, the tarps and blankets, the little knots of people sitting close together because closeness was the only currency that had survived the crossing intact. "And I couldn't sleep."

"The stars keep moving."

"I know." A pause. "It's going to take some getting used to."

Wren leaned until their shoulder found Xanthe's shoulder. Neither of them made anything more of it than that. They had gotten very good, these past few days, at saying enormous things in small gestures — a shoulder, a cup of tea, a hand checked quietly for a pulse in the night without waking the other person. There would be time for the enormous things said enormously. For now, this was enough, and enough was a word Wren was only just learning to believe in.

"I've been thinking about names," Wren said.

"Oh?"

"Mine. What I'll call myself now that—" They gestured vaguely upward, at the sky where the Auditor's bureaucracy no longer hung like frost. "Now that nobody owns the word."

Xanthe considered this with the gravity they gave to everything, which was one of the ten thousand things Wren loved about them and occasionally wanted to shake out of them like dust from a rug. "What have you got?"

"Nothing yet. That's rather the point, isn't it? Choosing instead of inheriting." Wren sipped the cold tea and grimaced and drank more anyway. "Ilsa would have had an opinion."

The name settled between them the way ash settles — softly, everywhere, unavoidable. Below them, near the largest fire, someone had set out a stone cairn with a broken compass on top of it, because a cartographer deserved a marker even when her grave was a cairn built by people who barely knew her, on ground she'd never mapped, in a country that wasn't on any map yet either.

"She'd have hated this place," Xanthe said at last. "Unmapped terrain. No coordinates. She'd have complained for a week and then drawn the most beautiful charts anyone had ever seen."

"And sold them to the highest bidder."

"And then felt awful about it," Xanthe said, "and given half the proceeds back, badly, in secret, pretending it was an accounting error."

Wren laughed — a real laugh, surprising them both, rising out of grief the way a spring rises out of rock. "Yes. All right. She'd have loved it here, actually. Nobody to betray. Nothing worth selling." They went quiet a moment. "She bought us our exit. Three days of buying time with her body. I keep trying to make that fit inside forgiveness and it doesn't fit, it's bigger than—"

"It doesn't have to fit," Xanthe said. "Grief isn't arithmetic."

That was the kind of thing Xanthe Voss said sometimes, flatly, like reporting a balance of accounts, that undid Wren completely. They pressed their shoulder harder against Xanthe's and watched the drifting stars and let the silence hold what words couldn't carry.

* * *

In the morning, Xanthe opened a new ledger.

Wren came upon it by accident, or by the kind of accident that is secretly intention — coming back from the waterline with full skins, finding the guild master's former second seated alone at a folding table with a book bound in plain grey cloth, unmarked, no sigil of the Guild of Improbable Exchanges anywhere on it. Around the table, people had begun to gather the way people gather around warmth. Old Tomas the cooper. The twins from the tannery district. Maren, nine years old, who had crossed the Door clutching a wooden horse and refusing to say goodbye to anything else she'd ever owned.

Xanthe dipped a pen. Wrote, in the careful hand that had once balanced accounts of bottled memories and impossible exchanges:

*Maren Solace-adjacent — keeper of one (1) wooden horse, name unknown to us, to be learned.*

Maren read it upside down, frowning. "That's not my name."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "Tell me what is."

"Maren Ostrow."

"Maren Ostrow," Xanthe repeated, and wrote it, striking through the guess, and beneath it added: *worth keeping.*

Wren stood at the edge of the gathering with the water skins forgotten in their hands and felt something open in their chest that had no name yet either. This was what Xanthe had become, in the space between the old oath and the new one — not the last oath-bound swordspeaker of a dead country, but a keeper of names. Not debts. Not exchanges. Just this: a list of people, written down so that no auditor, no erasure, no hungry faceless bureaucracy could ever claim they had never existed.

Tomas gave his name. Then the twins. Then a woman Wren didn't recognize, and another, and another, the line growing patiently, each person leaving the table standing a little straighter, as though being written down were a kind of being carried.

When the line thinned, Wren set down the water skins and took the seat across from Xanthe.

"Put me in," they said.

"You're already in." Xanthe turned the book around. On the first page, in ink that had dried days ago, under the heading *Names Worth Keeping*, the very first line read:

*Wren Solace — apprentice of nothing, friend of mine, maker of their own name, pending.*

"Pending," Wren said.

"You said you were deciding."

"I've decided." Wren took the pen. Their hand hovered over the page — over the space after *pending* — and for a moment they saw it all again: the Hollow Court, the Auditor's patient voice, the truth of what they were. A scrap. A remainder. An unwritten thing that had walked out of the erasure wearing borrowed skin.

They wrote: *Wren. Just Wren. Chosen.* And below it, because honesty was a debt they intended to pay forever: *more than human, less than certain, entirely theirs.*

Xanthe read it twice. Did not argue with any part of it, including the last clause, which made heat climb Wren's throat and settle in their ears.

"That's going to raise questions," Xanthe said mildly.

"Good. I'm tired of answers nobody asks for."

* * *

That afternoon, Wren spread a fresh sheet of paper on the same table and began, for the first time since the border wards fell, to draw a map.

Not from memory. From listening. This was the gift — or the residue — of what they were: they could feel the true shape of geography the way a tongue feels the shape of a tooth. And this new country was not silent. It murmured. It suggested. To the north, ridgelines that wanted climbing; to the east, a valley that hummed with the promise of good soil; somewhere south, faint but persistent, a road that already existed in the land's imagination, waiting for feet to agree with it.

Wren drew the camp first, small and careful, at the center of the page. Marked the lake, the springs, the cairn with the broken compass. Then worked outward, tentatively, the lines loose and provisional — dotted where the land was guessing, solid where it was sure.

"What's that?" Maren asked, materializing at Wren's elbow with the fearless curiosity of nine-year-olds everywhere.

"A map of everywhere we might go."

"But where do we *go*?"

Wren looked at the page. At the dotted lines fanning out like roots, like river deltas, like fingers opening. At the small mark at the center that meant *here*, that meant *us*, that meant two hundred and eleven names in a grey ledger and one sword hung on a branch to rust and two people learning, slowly, how to stand shoulder to shoulder without an oath telling them to.

"We decide," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

They drew the first road leading south, toward the humming, and left the rest blank on purpose — a cartography of choices not yet made, of a country writing itself as it went. Above the map's empty edges, the strange living stars were already wheeling into evening, and far to the south, faint as a heartbeat, the land hummed its invitation.

Wren picked up the pen and began to draw what waited there.
