## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with living in a valley that ate magic, Felix Yarrow often thought, was that it made lying feel like honesty.

Thornvale didn't forget out of malice. It simply couldn't hold on. A charm spoken at the tree line arrived in the village square as a pleasant hum, then as nothing at all. A witch's curse hurled down from the high passes dissolved somewhere among the pines and came into the valley as a light rain, smelling faintly of ozone and regret. The elders liked to say the valley was blessed. Felix, who had spent nineteen years learning exactly how far a blessing could be stretched, preferred to think of it as deaf.

Magic came to the tree line the way travelers did — hopeful, breathless, full of promises — and Thornvale took one look and forgot why it had come.

Which suited Felix fine. Mostly.

She fed another length of iron to the forge and watched the coals take it, orange blooming up through the black like a secret told too close to a candle. The smithy sat at the valley's western edge, last building before the orchards gave way to meadow and the meadow climbed toward that unmistakable line where the forest began — dark firs standing shoulder to shoulder like a jury that had already decided. Her grandfather had built the smithy here on purpose, she'd been told. *Closest forge to the world's edge,* he used to say, when he still said things. *If magic can't follow us in, then everything we make here is ours alone. Honest iron.*

Honest iron. Felix ran her thumb along the cooling tang of a farmer's scythe and felt the truth of it settle into her chest, heavy and comfortable as an apron pocket full of nails. No enchantments lived in her work. No vows, no bindings, no whispered words that bent the world's elbow. Just steel and heat and the honest ache of her forearms at the end of the day.

That was the story, anyway. She told it so often and so smoothly that some mornings she believed it herself, and those were the good mornings.

The other mornings, she woke with words behind her teeth.

Not thoughts. Words. Shaped ones, load-bearing ones, the kind that wanted out. They gathered in her chest before dawn like water behind a dam, and if she wasn't careful — if she let even one slip into the morning air, half-dreaming, harmless-seeming — things happened. When she was seven she had looked at the neighbor's rotting fence and said, *I wish that thing would just stand up straight*, and the fence had stood. All of it. Every warped post snapping upright at once with a sound like a hundred small spines cracking, and it had stayed standing for eleven years, through floods and frosts and one memorable incident involving Tam Brackle's ox, because a wish spoken by a Yarrow was not a wish. It was a contract, and the world honored its contracts.

Her grandmother had heard the fence crack from two fields away. She had come running with a switch, which was traditional, and then sat down hard in the dirt, which was not, and held Felix's face in her rough hands and said, *Never again. You hear me, girl? Never say anything you mean in this valley. Say nothing at all, if you can manage it.*

So Felix managed. Nineteen years of managing. She spoke in the flattened, careful voice everyone in Thornvale used for anything resembling a promise — *I'll try to get to your hinge by Thursday* — and kept the true weight of her words locked behind her teeth like coins in a miser's fist. She sharpened ordinary blades for ordinary people who would forget her within a generation anyway, because that was what valleys did: they forgot. Names thinned. Stories went soft around the edges. Old Odile said it kept them safe, and everyone nodded, and Felix sharpened her blades and said nothing she meant.

It was a good life. She repeated that to herself the way other people repeated prayers.

* * *

The bell above the smithy door rang at dusk, which was wrong twice over. Nobody brought work at dusk, and nobody crossed the orchards at dusk since the year the wolves got bold, and the bell itself had been silent so long that its first note came out rusty, like a hinge complaining.

Felix set down her tongs. "Closed," she called, in her flattest voice. "Come back tomorrow."

The man who came through the door did not come back tomorrow. He came now, sideways, one hand pressed to his side, and left a smear of red along the doorframe that looked, in the dying light, almost deliberate. Like a signature. He was dressed for fast travel — dust-grey cloak, riding leathers gone soft with sweat, boots worn through at the outer edge where a man rolls his stride to save his knees. A courier, she thought. Or had been. Couriers from beyond the passes sometimes came down to Thornvale when their routes bent near the valley, mostly to laugh at the idea of a place with no mail worth stealing and no magic worth selling.

This one had no letters. He had a wound under his ribs and a look in his eyes that Felix recognized from her grandmother's stories about soldiers: the look of a man who has seen something coming for a long time and is now standing inside it.

"You're—" He got one hand on the doorframe and steadied himself. His voice was a ruin, scraped thin. "You're the smith."

"Smith's closed." She was already moving, though, because blood on her floor was blood on her floor, and there was a part of her — the inconvenient part, the part that said things it meant — that had never once managed to watch someone bleed and do nothing. "Sit down before you fall down. There's a difference between the two and I'd rather you picked it yourself."

He laughed, which cost him; she saw the pain flicker across his face like weather. "They said the valley would be quiet." He let her steer him onto the bench by the cold hearth. Up close he smelled of road dust and something else underneath, something burnt-sweet, like leaves after a fire. "They said nothing followed you in here."

"Nothing does." She fetched the clean linen, the needle, the bottle of grain spirits she kept for precisely this sort of evening, though usually the bleeding party was herself, courtesy of a slipped hammer or an argument with a burr. "Hold still. This is going to hurt more than the original injury, because I'm not gentle and I've been drinking none of this."

"Just—"

"Hold *still*."

She cut the cloak back from the wound and stopped cutting.

There was a mark burned into the skin beside the gash. Not a brand — brands had edges. This was a sigil drawn in something that had seared the flesh in fine, deliberate lines: a circle, and within it a tree with roots that branched downward like veins, and beneath the roots, a single word in a script she did not know and yet understood instantly, the way you understand a scream. Her hands went very still over the linen.

"Where did you get that?" Her voice came out lower than she intended. Careful. Flat. *Say nothing you mean.*

The courier looked down at himself, at the sigil, as if he'd forgotten it was there. "Payment," he said. "Half of it. The other half is—" He pressed his lips together. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't matter now."

"It looks like it matters considerably."

"It matters to whoever holds the paper." He caught her wrist. His grip was fever-hot and surprisingly strong. "Listen. Listen to me, smith. I've ridden four days without sleep to reach this hole in the world, and I don't have the time or the blood left for manners. Are you a Yarrow?"

The name went through her like a nail through wet wood.

"No," said Felix Yarrow.

His eyes closed briefly. "Of course not. Forgive me. Everyone here is no one, that's the whole point of the place." He sagged back against the hearthstones. "Then I'm dead either way, and I'll die in a stranger's smithy, which is better than most of us get."

Felix threaded the needle. Her hands, she noted distantly, were perfectly steady, which felt like someone else's hands. "Who's *us*?"

"Couriers." He huffed something that might have been a laugh. "We carry for the Undercourt. Down beneath the mountain, where they trade in—" He stopped. Looked at her. Really looked, for the first time, taking in the iron-dust freckles, the burn-scarred forearms, the careful flat face. "Do you know what souls weigh, smith?"

"I know what iron weighs."

"Lucky you." He winced as she set the needle to his skin. "Everything down there weighs. Every name, every memory, every year of a life. They keep ledgers. Beautiful ledgers — I've seen them, gold leaf and vellum, debts going back centuries. And every debt gets called due eventually. That's all any of this is. A debt collection." He gestured vaguely at his own ruined side. "I carried the notice. Four days hard riding to bring the call to this valley, and the ward spat me out at the tree line like a cherry pit, and I thought — good, good, it can't follow me, I'll rest one night in the quiet place—"

He broke off. Above the hearth, a kettle Felix had forgotten was on began, very softly, to sing.

"But it did follow you," Felix said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Whatever did that." She nodded at the sigil.

"It was already in me." He smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen on a living face. "You can't carry the Undercourt's paper without the Undercourt's stamp. I knew that when I took the job. Everybody knows it. We take the work because the pay is real, and we tell ourselves the stamp is just ink." He looked at the sigil again. "It's not ink."

Outside, the first true dark settled over the orchards. Somewhere in the trees at the valley's rim, a bird that should have been asleep made a short, unhappy sound and stopped.

Felix tied off the stitch. "You need rest and broth, in whichever order kills you slowest. Tomorrow you can tell me who sent you to a valley where everyone's a nobody, and then you can ride out, because whatever debt you're carrying, it isn't mine, and I keep my ledger clean."

"Your ledger." He turned his head against the hearthstone and studied her, and there was something in his gaze now that made her want to step backward — recognition, dawning slow and terrible, like frost crossing a window. "Clean. Yes." His eyes drifted, almost idly, to the iron rack beside the forge. To the half-finished scythe. To the maker's mark she punched into every blade that left this shop, because her grandfather had insisted, because pride in honest work was safe, was small, was nothing.

A sprig of rowan leaves curled around a single star.

The courier looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at Felix, and the terrible dawn finished arriving in his face.

"Oh," he breathed. "*Oh.* They didn't tell me. They never tell the couriers who the debt belongs to until it's called." He tried to sit up, and the stitches pulled, and he made a sound through his teeth and kept trying anyway, clutching at her sleeve with both bloody hands. "Girl. Girl, listen to me. The Yarrow soul is called due. Do you understand? Twenty years it's sat in their vault, collateral on a debt, and now the Regent's calling it in, and when they collect—"

"*Stop.*"

She hadn't meant to say it with weight. It came out of her like an arrow loosed in a crowded room, and the word landed in the smithy and *took*. The fire dropped an octave. The kettle's song strangled mid-note. The wind pressing at the shutters stopped as though the night outside had been told to wait its turn. Even the courier went rigid, his mouth still open around the next word, his eyes going wide and white-rimmed — not at her, but at everything, at the air itself, which had just become the kind of air where things obeyed.

Silence, total and ringing.

Felix stood in the middle of it with her heart slamming against her ribs and the taste of iron in her mouth, and thought, with a strange floating calm: *nineteen years.*

"Nineteen years," she whispered, and this time the words were only words, small and human and shaking, "of saying nothing. Of being *careful*. And one bleeding stranger walks through my door and—"

"The Yarrow soul," the courier said hoarsely, into the hush her vow had carved. "Called due. That's the message, smith. That's what I carried four days to deliver." His grip on her sleeve gentled, turned into something almost like pity. "I'm sorry. I truly am. I hoped I'd find the valley empty."

"What—" Felix's voice cracked and she hated it. "What happens when they collect? What happens to—" She couldn't finish it. Couldn't make her mouth shape the rest: *to me, to my mother's grave, to whatever piece of my family they're holding.*

But the courier wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her, toward the dark window, and his face had gone slack with a new kind of fear.

"They don't send couriers for the final call," he said quietly. "For the last mile, they send collectors. And the ward keeps magic out, but I rode ahead of mine by half a day, maybe less, and once the debt's called, girl, the ward won't matter. Debts are older than wards. Debts are older than *trees*."

The bell above the door gave a single, rusted groan — though no one had touched it — and hung still.

Felix Yarrow, who had spent her whole life saying nothing she meant, looked down at the dying man on her bench, at the sigil burning beside his wound, at her own trembling hands, and understood with perfect clarity that the quiet life was already over. Had perhaps always been borrowed.

"Tell me everything," she said. "And mean it."

The courier smiled his terrible smile. "Start with a map," he said, and reached into his cloak.
