## 2. A Map in Dying Hands

Felix Yarrow had buried three people in her life, and none of them had died on her floor.

The first was her grandmother, who went in her sleep with the good grace of someone who'd finished a long meal. The second was old Tam Broadfoot, who'd been carried to the temple because he was Tam Broadfoot and the temple was where Tam things happened. The third had been her mother, in a way that nobody in Thornvale ever spoke about, which Felix had come to understand was its own strange kind of burial.

So she knew the shape of dying. She knew the sounds. What she did not know—what no one had ever taught her, because who would teach such a thing in a valley where nothing dramatic was permitted to happen—was what to do when the dying arrived at her door at dusk, bleeding through her only good apron.

"Sit," she said, for the fourth time. "Sit *down*, you great stubborn—"

The man was trying to stand again. He'd made it two steps from her forge toward the door before his knees informed him of their resignation, and now he sat on her floorboards with his back against the anvil's base, one hand pressed to his side where something had opened him up like a letter nobody wanted to read.

"I have to—" he said.

"You have to hold still." She knelt beside him with the clean linen she used for quench-rags and pressed it hard against the wound. He hissed. "What's your name?"

"Merrin."

"Merrin what?"

He almost smiled. Blood on his teeth turned it into something else. "Just Merrin. Couriers don't get surnames. Surnames are for people who stay somewhere."

"Well, Just Merrin, you're going to stay somewhere right now, and it's going to be this exact spot, and you're going to let me work." She got her arm under his shoulder and hauled him onto the bench by the wall, which took more out of her than she liked to admit. He was lighter than he looked, the way men get when they've been running longer than they've been eating. "Who did this to you?"

"Hounds." He said it like a word from another language. "Not dogs. Hounds. They don't bark. They just—" His hand fluttered. "They count your breaths. That's how they find you. They follow the sound of being alive."

Felix's hands kept working. Her hands were very good at continuing to do sensible things while the rest of her quietly left the room. That was a skill she'd been practicing since she was six years old and first learned that some words, spoken wrong—or spoken *right*—could make the world lean in and listen.

"That's nonsense," she said. "That's festival-nonsense. Grandmother-tales."

"Yes," Merrin agreed pleasantly. "Most true things sound like that. It's a flaw in the design."

She tied off the linen and reached for the willow-bark tincture on her shelf, the one she kept for burns and the occasional mule-related catastrophe. The valley had mule-related catastrophes. That was the level of emergency Thornvale produced. A cart wheel came off. Someone fell in the millrace and came out embarrassed and cold. Once, spectacularly, a barn caught fire, and half the town had stood around discussing whose fault it was until the rain put it out.

Nobody bled into Felix's smithy. Nobody came over the tree line at all, if they could help it, because everyone knew—and everyone said it the way you say *the sky is up*—that the world beyond Thornvale was full of magic, and magic didn't cross into the valley. Magic died at the trees like a fire meeting a river. That was the whole point of Thornvale. That was why her grandmother had brought her here as a child, gripping her hand too tight, whispering *never say them aloud, never, never*—

"Drink this."

Merrin drank. He shuddered. "Awful."

"It's medicine, not wine."

"Wine is also awful. I've simply had better practice." He sagged back against the wall. In the light of her banked forge he looked, she thought, younger than she'd first guessed—mid-thirties maybe, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed for other work. His boots were ruined. His coat had been fine, once. There were silver buckles on it, and each one had been scratched with tiny marks, tallying something, though she couldn't imagine what.

"How far did you run?" she asked.

"From the border market? Four days." He laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into something wetter. "Four days ahead of them and they never lost me. You can't lose something that's counting."

"Counting what?"

"Breaths." He looked at her then, really looked, and there was a terrible clarity in his eyes, the clarity of a man who has done all his arithmetic already. "Yours are louder."

Felix went very still.

It wasn't anything anyone could have noticed—not truly. But she had spent her whole life listening for exactly the wrong kind of attention, the way other people listened for weather. And there was something in how he said it, something that wasn't menace but recognition, that made every small vow she'd swallowed over thirty years turn over in her chest like stones in a riverbed.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Merrin said kindly. "You wouldn't. They make sure of that, where you're from." He fumbled inside his coat, wincing, and drew out a leather tube, sealed with wax that had cracked sometime during four days of running. "I was told to deliver this to a smithy at the edge of Thornvale. To whoever keeps it. The instructions were specific. *A woman,* they said. *Alone. Who works metal.*"

"Who told you that?"

"A dead man." He smiled again. "Dead men give the best directions. They've stopped caring whether you like the destination."

"I'm going to need you to be less cryptic and more alive," Felix said. "Those are my only requirements."

"The first one I can manage." He worked at the wax seal with a thumbnail that shook badly. "The second—" He paused. Looked down at the linen wrapped around him, which was doing less and less work as the minutes passed, soaking dark in a way that linen should not. He seemed, oddly, unsurprised. "Ah," he said softly. "There we are."

"There we are *what*? Let me see—"

"Felix Yarrow."

Her name in his mouth stopped her hands mid-reach. Not because he knew it—half the valley called her Felix, usually while asking her to fix something—but because of *how* he said it. Like a title. Like the first line of a document.

"How do you know my surname?" she asked. "Nobody uses my surname. My surname is a plant. People call me Yarrow the way they'd call a fencepost Fence."

"Because names matter everywhere except here," he said. "Here you buried yours so deep even you forgot it was a grave." He held the leather tube out to her. "Take it."

"I don't want it."

"I know. Take it anyway. It's not for you, exactly. It's just—" he coughed, wetly, patiently, "—it's traveling to the next pair of hands, and yours happen to be attached."

She should have refused. Every instinct she owned, every rule her grandmother had carved into her—the quiet rules, the *small* rules, speak nothing, promise nothing, want nothing loudly—every one of them said: refuse the tube, put the stranger on a cart at dawn, send him back over the tree line to whatever counted-and-breathed horror had followed him, and go back to horseshoes and hinge-pins and a life so deliberately dull it was practically armored.

Instead she took the tube.

It was heavier than it should have been. That was her first thought: *this weighs too much.* Paper and leather and wax, and yet it settled into her palm like a stone, like something with intent. Warm, too. Warm as a living thing, though the evening was cold and the forge was banked to embers.

Merrin exhaled—a long, careful breath, the breath of a man setting down something heavy—and slid further down the bench.

"No," Felix said. "No, no. Stay awake. Merrin. *Just Merrin.* Eyes open."

"I'm excellent at eyes open," he murmured. "It's the rest I'm having trouble with." His gaze drifted around her smithy, taking in the racks of tools hung in careful order, the horseshoes waiting for Farmer Doss, the little tin of tea she'd never gotten around to drinking. He looked at it all with an expression she couldn't read, and then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a small, genuine, dying-man laugh.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just—I ran four days to reach the one place in the world where nothing can follow me." He closed his eyes. "And it turns out the thing following me was a delivery."

"*Merrin.*"

"The map," he said. "Open the map. Then I'll tell you what I was paid to say, and you'll know I earned my fee, and I can stop." His hand found hers—cold fingers, terribly light—and pressed the tube more firmly into her grip. "Please. A courier's last job should be finished. It's the only prayer we have. We don't pray to gods. We pray to completions."

Her hands, traitorous and steady, broke the cracked wax and drew out the paper inside.

It was a map. She saw that much immediately—the mountain country south of the valley, rendered in faded ink, the tree line marked with a border of tiny painted leaves. But beneath the ink, beneath the paint, lines moved. Slowly. Like veins under skin. Paths redrawn themselves as she watched, corridors shifting in the mountain's drawn bulk, and somewhere deep at the center of it all a mark pulsed faintly, red as the blood on her apron.

"It moves," she said stupidly.

"Maps of that place do. The place itself keeps rearranging its furniture. Very rude host." Merrin's voice was thinner now. She knelt closer. The wound had stopped bleeding, which she knew—vaguely, horribly—was often worse than the alternative. "Listen. Listen, smith-girl. There isn't time for the whole story, so I'll give you the part I was paid for, and you can hate me after."

"I don't hate you."

"You will. Everyone hates couriers eventually. We bring news, and news is always somebody's bad day." He gripped her wrist. His strength was going out of him like water from a cracked jug, but the grip was deliberate, final, a signature. "*The Yarrow soul is called due.*"

The words landed in her chest and did not land anywhere else. They just stayed there, ringing.

"I don't understand," she whispered.

"Twenty years ago," Merrin said, "a debt was made in a place under the mountain. A soul was sold to buy protection for a valley. Protection has a term. Terms end. The holder of the debt has begun calling in what's owed." His breath rattled. "The ledger says *Yarrow.* The ledger doesn't care which Yarrow. Souls in ledgers are like coins in purses. They only care about weight."

"My mother," Felix heard herself say. The words came out flat, factual, a blacksmith reading a stamp off a blade. "You're talking about my mother."

"I'm talking about what was traded," Merrin said gently. "Whether it was your mother, or whether it was you—that depends on the accounting. Ledgers are creative. That's why they frighten honest people." His eyes were losing focus, drifting toward the forge-light. "The map shows the Undercourt. Where sold things are kept. Where debts sit in jars and wait for someone brave enough or foolish enough to break the glass."

"Then someone else can be foolish. Send it to—there must be knights, priests, *anyone*—"

"Anyone who enters that place gets written into it," Merrin said. "Anyone who carries that map was chosen by it. It's been looking for you a long time, I think." He managed one more smile. "It's warm, isn't it? Maps shouldn't be warm. That's how you know."

The map pulsed in her hands, gentle as a heartbeat.

"Merrin—"

"Tell the next one I finished the job," he said. "Couriers don't get graves. We get completions. Tell them—" and here his voice finally frayed, the last thread of the professional calm giving way to something young and scared and human, "—tell them I didn't drop it."

And then he was gone. Not dramatically. The forge-light didn't flicker; the wind outside didn't rise. He simply stopped, between one breath and the next, the way a sentence stops when the writer runs out of ink.

Felix sat on the floor of her smithy holding a warm map and a dead man's hand, and did not move for a long time.

* * *

She tried to burn it at midnight.

This was, she felt, entirely reasonable. A stranger dies in her shop babbling about soul-ledgers and breathing hounds, leaves her a piece of paper that shifts like a living thing, and expects—what? That she'd pack a bag? She was a blacksmith. Blacksmiths fixed things. They did not descend into mountains after metaphors.

So she built up the forge properly, the way she did every morning of her life, raked the coals bright, and laid the map across the heat with a pair of tongs, telling herself firmly that this was practical. Dangerous documents burned. That was history's entire recommendation.

The map did not burn.

The edges nearest the coals glowed—she watched, waiting for the catch, the curl, the sweet relief of ash—but the glow sank *into* the paper instead, running inward along those moving vein-lines like water finding channels, until the whole sheet shimmered faintly and then went cool and unburnt and smug.

"All right," Felix said to it. "No."

She threw it in the trough. The hissing water steamed around it, and when she fished it out with shaking tongs, it was dry. Dry and warm and pulsing, and the red mark at its center had brightened by exactly one shade, as if it had enjoyed that.

She tried the stove. She tried burying it in the ash-bin overnight. At dawn, hollow-eyed, she tried dropping it down the well, and then spent forty minutes hauling it back up on a hooked pole because the thought of it sitting down there in the dark, *waiting*, was worse than the thought of carrying it.

By full light she sat on her step with the map in her lap, flour-dusted, forge-cold, having slept not at all, and admitted the thing she'd been circling all night the way you circle a sore tooth.

Burning it wasn't destruction. Burning it was *asking*. Everything she'd done to it, the map had answered: *no.* It was not asking her permission. It was asking her a question, over and over, patient as sunrise, and the question was: *what will you do now?*

And underneath that question, older and quieter, lay the one Merrin's words had cracked open:

*The Yarrow soul is called due.*

Her mother. Twenty years gone, mourned without a grave, mourned without a story, mourned in the particular Thornvale way of *not speaking of it*—and all that time, not gone at all. Kept. Filed. Sitting in the dark under the mountain in a jar, in a ledger, in a column of numbers, while Felix sharpened blades and swallowed vows and built a life out of smallness like a wall.

Her grandmother's voice rose in memory, low and urgent: *Never swear anything, child. Never promise. Here you're safe because here you're nothing.*

Safe. She turned the word over and found it had rotted through without her noticing. A cage built to look like a valley. And someone had been paying rent on that cage with her mother's soul, and the lease was up, and the collector was coming, and the map in her lap was warm as a heartbeat and would not burn, would not drown, would not leave.

Felix looked down the lane toward the heart of Thornvale, where smoke rose from chimneys and someone was already arguing cheerfully about a cart wheel. All of it—every roof, every mule-related catastrophe, every ordinary morning—sat on top of a debt she'd never known existed.

There was one person in Thornvale old enough to know how it began.

Felix stood, rolled the map, and tucked it inside her coat, against her chest, where it beat its slow, patient pulse against her ribs like a second heart she hadn't asked for.

She was going to go see Odile.

And Odile, Felix thought grimly, was going to wish she'd burned it too.
