## 3. What the Elders Buried

Felix did not sleep.

She told herself it was the courier's blood she kept smelling—she'd scrubbed her floor twice with lye and river sand, and still the iron tang of it hung in the rafters like a guest who refused to leave. But that was a lie, and Felix Yarrow had built her whole life on telling careful lies only to other people.

The map sat on her workbench under a leather apron, the way you'd cover a birdcage to make the bird think it was night. She could feel it anyway. It burned when she'd tried to feed it to her forge—that was the part she couldn't stop turning over. Paper didn't argue with fire. Paper did not sit in the coals for the space of ten heartbeats and come out *warm*, edges curled but unblackened, its ink gone from brown to a deep arterial red, as if the fire had been an insult it declined to take personally.

Merrin's last words kept arriving at odd hours, the way grief does. *The Yarrow soul is called due.* He'd said it like a debt collector announcing himself at a door. And then he'd looked at her—not through her, not past her, which was how everyone in Thornvale looked at the quiet smith girl—and he had smiled with bloody teeth and said, "So that's what she grew into."

He'd known her mother. That much Felix was certain of. And whatever else Merrin had been—a courier, a fool, a man running from something with teeth—he had died on her floor rather than let go of that map.

By the time the light came up gray over the eastern ridge, Felix had made a decision, and it frightened her more than the burning paper did: she was going to ask Odile.

* * *

Elder Odile Ashgrave lived at the top of the Vale Road in a house older than any three others in Thornvale, with a roof of blue slate that everyone said had never once leaked, though no one could remember anyone laying it. Her garden grew things out of season—winter roses, frost-sweet plums—and when Felix was small she had believed this was simply because Odile was old, and old people's gardens were patient enough to cheat.

Now, standing at the gate with Merrin's blood still under her fingernails in spirit if not in fact, Felix understood that nothing in Thornvale cheated except the valley itself, and everything in it was paid for.

Odile answered the door before Felix knocked. She always seemed to. She was small and straight-backed, silver hair braided into a crown so tight it looked structural, and her eyes were the pale gray of weather about to change its mind.

"Felix." Not *Felix Yarrow, the smith*. Just Felix. Odile was one of perhaps four people in the valley who used her name like they remembered it on purpose. "You look like you haven't slept."

"A man died in my smithy last night."

Something crossed Odile's face—fast, trained away, but not fast enough. Felix had spent twenty years learning to read people who were trying not to be read; concealment teaches you the grammar of it.

"You'd better come in," Odile said.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of dried lavender and bread that had gone cold out of respect. Odile poured tea without asking, and Felix wrapped her hands around the cup because they wanted to shake and she preferred they be given a job instead.

"His name was Merrin," Felix said. "He came up the north trail bleeding from a wound I've never seen the like of—burned, but from the inside, like something ate him from the middle out. He said words I'm going to say now, and I want you to listen to them the way you'd want me to listen if our positions were turned."

Odile set down her own cup. Her hands, Felix noticed, were perfectly steady, which was its own kind of answer.

"He said the Undercourt. He said there was a ledger, and a debt. And he said—" Felix made herself finish, because unfinished sentences were how valleys like this one stayed safe, and she was done being safe in the dark. "He said *the Yarrow soul is called due.*"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the kettle to cool audibly.

Then Odile said, "Where is the map?"

Not *what are you talking about*. Not *child, you've had a fright*. Where is the map. Felix felt something in her chest tilt, the way a table tilts when one leg finally gives.

"It's at my forge."

"Good. Leave it there. Tonight, put it back in your fire and hold it there until it's ash, and then grind the ash into your quench barrel and let the water take it. Do not bury it. Do not give it to the river. Fire and water, both together, or not at all."

"That's not an answer, Odile."

"That is the answer," Odile said, "that has kept every soul in this valley alive for twenty years."

Felix stood up. She hadn't planned to; her legs decided on their own. "My mother died when I was six. Everyone said fever. Everyone said the winter took her, the way winters do. I stood at her grave when I was nine years old and I swore—" She stopped, because here was the thing she had never told another living person, the thing that hummed in her chest even now, here, inside the ward where magic supposedly went to die. "I swore at her grave that I would remember her voice. And the wind stopped. For a full minute, in a valley where nothing listens, everything listened. I was nine years old and I thought God had heard me. Later I learned better. Later I learned that oaths are *mine*, that they come out of me like sparks off a grinder, and that I have spent my whole life biting them back behind my teeth because this valley doesn't allow magic and I am apparently made of it."

"Sit down, Felix."

"No." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, and she kept going anyway. "You're going to tell me what my mother sold. You're going to tell me why a dying man crossed half the mountains to bring me a map that won't burn. Because you know. I watched your face just now. You knew his words before I finished saying them."

For a long moment Odile looked at her, and Felix saw the war happen behind those gray eyes—the war between kindness and custody, between loving a child and guarding a secret, and which one had been winning for twenty years.

Then Odile rose, went to the hearth, and pressed her palm flat against the stones above the mantel. A section of rock swung inward, silent as a held breath.

"Come," she said. "And Felix—what you see in there, you will not speak of aloud. Not as a question, not as a joke, not even alone in your own smithy. Swear it first."

"I don't swear anything anymore," Felix said. "I've learned recently what oaths cost."

Odile's mouth twitched—not quite a smile. "Good girl," she said, and led her into the dark.

* * *

The room beyond the hearth was small and round and utterly impossible, because it should not have fit inside Odile's house, and Felix understood immediately that it didn't—it was one of the valley's cheats, a pocket of elsewhere, and the fact that such pockets existed inside Thornvale meant the ward wasn't a wall so much as a locked room with furniture in it.

In the center of the room stood a stone basin the size of a washing tub, filled with water so still it looked solid. Odile touched its rim and images rose in it like breath fogging glass.

"Twenty-three years ago," Odile said, "the mountain opened. Not metaphorically. There was a crack below the western ridge, and out of it came brokers—soul-brokers, memory-brokers, dealers in years and names. They set up stalls at the tree line and they bought from us. A widow sold her grief and forgot her husband entirely; she smiled all the time afterward, and we couldn't decide whether we'd saved her or lost her. A farmer sold ten years of his life and aged overnight. They paid in gold, Felix, real gold, and gold buys grain in famine years, and we were proud, and we were stupid, and by the time we understood what the Court actually was, three of our own had already signed."

The water showed her figures—hooded, elegant, wrong in ways the eye kept sliding off of.

"The Court does not steal," Odile went on. "That's the horror of it. It *trades*. Everything must be consented to, witnessed, entered in their ledger. So when we begged them to close the mountain—to leave Thornvale alone forever—they named a price. The valley's protection, permanent, in exchange for a soul freely given. One soul. Voluntary. Entered as payment in full."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "Whose?"

The water rippled. A face formed in it—young, laughing, dark-haired, with a crooked front tooth and eyes the same stubborn brown as the ones Felix saw in her own reflection every morning.

Her mother. Younger than Felix was now.

"They asked for volunteers," Odile said quietly. "No one came forward. So I went to Lila Yarrow privately, because she was the bravest person I have ever known, and I asked her. And she said yes before I finished asking. She said, 'If it keeps the valley, it keeps Felix.' She walked to the tree line carrying a lantern, and she shook the broker's hand, and she smiled at us the whole way so we wouldn't cry."

Felix discovered she was sitting down. She had no memory of sitting. The stone floor was cold through her skirts and she gripped her own knees and stared into the water where her mother's face smiled up at her, twenty-three years young, and felt the world rearrange itself around a new and terrible center.

"You buried her," Felix whispered. "There's a grave behind the meetinghouse. I pulled weeds off it every spring."

"We needed somewhere to grieve," Odile said. "Grief needs a place, or it wanders the house breaking things. We gave it a place."

"And the debt? Merrin said a debt was *called due*. Paid souls don't get called due."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them again, she looked, for the first time since Felix had known her, genuinely afraid.

"When your mother signed, the broker added a clause. Collateral. Standard practice, he said—the court likes to keep a lien on something living, in case the buyer defaults. The soul was the payment. The collateral—" Odile's voice caught, actually caught, on the word. "The collateral was the unborn child. *You.* If the valley ever broke its side of the bargain—if the ward failed, if Thornvale ever reached beyond the tree line, if any descendant of the signatory ever left the valley to seek the Court—then the collateral would be forfeit. Your mother agreed to that too. She made them write it so that the forfeiture clause could only trigger if *you* chose it. She said—I heard her, Felix, I was there—she said, 'My daughter will be free. Even freedom can cost her. But it will be her hand that pays.'"

Felix laughed. It came out of her sideways and ugly, nothing like laughter. "Free. I've spent thirty-one years being invisible. Do you know what it's like, growing up in a valley where every other child gets to dream out loud and you have to swallow every promise you make because your promises *do things*? I thought I was hiding myself. My whole life, I thought I was cleverly concealing what I was." She stood, and her legs held this time, shaking but holding. "I was the *collateral*. I was the lock on the deal. This entire valley—every festival, every harvest, every winter we survived—was purchased with my mother's soul and secured against mine. I wasn't hiding from the world, Odile. I was *stored*."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "And now you understand why I ordered you to burn the map."

Felix turned on her. "A man bled out on my floor for that map."

"Merrin Halloway was a fool and a romantic and my sister's son, and I loved him, and I am sorry he's dead." Something raw moved under Odile's composure and was caged again. "But he spent the last decade of his life digging into the Court's affairs—chasing rumors that Lila's soul was never destroyed after all, that the Court *keeps* what it's paid, files it away like coin in a vault. And maybe some of what he found is true. But Felix, listen to me. Whatever is written in that ledger, the moment you carry that map across the tree line, the forfeiture clause wakes. The Court has been waiting twenty years for a Yarrow to come looking. You wouldn't be walking toward your mother. You'd be walking into a trap baited with the only thing they ever wanted from your family."

"Then let it be a trap," Felix said. "Let them spring it. I'll swear my way out of it. I'm the last swordspeaker, aren't I? Isn't that what Merrin called me? You knew that too, didn't you. You've known what I am since the day I was born, and you let me grow up thinking I was broken."

"You are not broken." Odile crossed the room and took Felix's face in her two small hands, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "You are the most dangerous thing in this valley, child, and the kindest, and I have spent twenty years making sure those two facts never had to meet. Every elder before me made the same choice. We buried it under the meetinghouse floor and we watered it with silence and we told ourselves it was love. Maybe it was. Maybe it was also cowardice. Both things are often true at once." She released her, stepped back, and became again the matriarch, the crown of braids, the slate roof that never leaked. "Burn the map tonight. Fire and water. Then come to the autumn assembly and stand next to me and let me lie to the valley about where you've been, and live, Felix. Live long and dull and safe, and hate me if it helps, and let your mother rest."

"She isn't resting," Felix said. "She's *filed*."

Odile didn't deny it.

Felix walked to the hearth-door, then stopped with her hand on the cool stone.

"One more question."

"If I can bear it."

"The ward. The protection. If I cross the tree line and the clause triggers—what happens to Thornvale?"

Odile was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Felix almost forgave her in advance for the answer, because she could see the answer had already been living in the old woman for years, wearing grooves into her.

"The protection was bought with a soul," Odile said finally. "Souls are currency, and currencies can be *spent* by whoever holds them. Right now your mother's soul sits in the Court's vault, inert, a coin in a drawer. As long as it sits there, the ward holds. But debts get called, Felix. Ledgers get balanced. If the Court ever decides to spend that coin—if it ever finds a use for a Yarrow soul—then the protection ends, and it ends all at once, and the mountain opens again, and this time there is nothing left in the valley to sell." She met Felix's eyes. "Burning the map doesn't protect you from that truth. It only protects you from finding it out too late to run."

Felix nodded slowly, the way you nod when the ground has shifted and you're pretending you meant to move.

"Thank you," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it, mostly. "For telling me. Finally."

"Destroy it, Felix."

"Fire and water," Felix echoed. "Both together. Or not at all."

She climbed out through the hearth and into the gray morning, and behind her she heard the stone swing shut, soft as a coffin lid that had gotten very good at its work.

* * *

She didn't go home. She walked the long way, past the meetinghouse, past the graveyard where the weeds over her mother's false grave had gone to seed because Felix hadn't pulled them this spring—hadn't been able to, some instinct in her hands refusing—and out along the fence line until the trees of Thornvale thinned and she could see, far up the northern slope, the famous tree line itself: the exact border where the world's color changed, where moss grew wild and birds sang louder and everything, everyone said, got *more*.

All her life she'd assumed the valley was the shelter.

Standing there with Merrin's map heavy in her satchel—because she had never intended to burn it, not for one single moment, and now she knew why, now she knew whose voice was filed away beneath a mountain waiting for her to come claim it—Felix Yarrow looked at the tree line and understood that the valley had never been a shelter at all.

It was a display case. And she was the thing inside it.

Somewhere beyond that ridge, in the dark under the mountain, her mother was humming in a jar.

"Well," Felix said out loud, to no one, to the border, to the twenty-year lie, and felt the words land with that faint electric weight that meant the world, even here, even muffled, had heard her. "I'm coming."

Behind her, back down in the village, a bell began to ring—and it was not the assembly bell, or the chapel bell, or any bell Felix knew.

It was ringing from the direction of her forge.
