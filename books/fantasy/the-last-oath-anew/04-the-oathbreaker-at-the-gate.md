## 4. The Oathbreaker at the Gate

The rain had followed Aldric Vane for three hundred miles, and he was beginning to suspect it was not weather but judgment.

It came down in gray sheets over the tree line of Thornvale, and where it touched the valley's edge, it simply stopped—fell in a straight vertical curtain as though some patient hand had drawn a border with a ruler. Beyond that line, the air went still and ordinary. Magic, he'd been told, died at the tree line. Oaths unraveled. Curses slept.

His had not.

He could feel it even now, coiled around his ribs like a wet rope: *I swear to guard Prince Alaric with my life, until death takes one or both of us.* Twenty years old, that vow. Spoken in a chapel full of candlelight while a boy of nine beamed up at him from the altar steps. And every night since the boy had died at seventeen—since Aldric had stood three feet away, bound by the letter of his oath to protect the *prince* and not the *man*, forbidden by its cold arithmetic from stepping between Alaric and the assassin's blade because the prince had ordered him elsewhere—the vow woke him. It did not flay him in any way a physician could find. It simply reminded him, in the small hours, of what he was worth. Which was nothing. Which was less than nothing, because a knight who fails is a tragedy, and a knight who fails *correctly*, by the terms of his own sworn word, is a joke the gods tell each other.

So he had come looking for a swordspeaker. The last one. If she existed at all.

The smithy sat just inside the tree line, close enough to the border that its chimney smoke drifted up and out of the valley like a soul trying to escape. Aldric stood in the rain a long moment—he was good at standing in rain; guilt made excellent insulation—and studied the place. Small stone forge. A yard swept clean. Swords hung on an outer rack, plain blades, honest work. And above the door, carved into the lintel by someone who'd wanted the mark small enough to miss:

A yarrow flower. Six petals. Stems crossed beneath.

He knew that sigil. He had knelt before that sigil. He had watched it burned into a banner that was folded and buried with a woman whose name the court was later forbidden to speak.

"Well," he said to the rain. "Either the gods are kind or they've finally stopped pretending."

He left his horse at the tree line—the beast had refused the valley's border twice already, and Aldric didn't blame it—and walked in through the curtain of rain feeling it peel off him like a second skin he was glad to shed.

* * *

Felix Yarrow heard the knock at her door and knew, before she opened it, that her week was about to get worse.

She'd buried Merrin two days ago, under the willow behind the forge, in a grave she'd dug herself at three in the morning so no one would ask questions. The map was hidden in the flour bin, which was stupid, because everyone knew she never baked, and also sensible, because no one would ever look there. She had tried to burn it four times. It had responded to her hearth fire the way a cat responds to being put in a bath: with offended, smoldering dignity, entirely unharmed.

So when the knock came—measured, courteous, the knock of someone who had been trained to knock rather than trained to survive—she considered simply not answering.

She answered. Curiosity had always been the crack in her armor, and she resented it deeply.

The man on her step looked like a knight the way a wrecked ship looks like a ship. Tall, broad, silvering at the temples, with a face that might once have been handsome in the way cathedral doors are handsome—imposing, slightly severe. But his cloak was travel-ruined, his boots were held together with prayer and cord, and there was a rawness around his eyes that Felix recognized, because she saw it in mirrors: the look of someone who hadn't slept properly in years.

"Madam Yarrow." His voice was low and careful, like a man walking on ice over deep water. "My name is Aldric Vane. I'm told—" He stopped. Started again. "You sharpen blades."

"I do."

"And other things, I'm told."

"Who told you?"

"A dying man in a ditch outside Harrow Fen," Aldric said. "Who spoke of a courier named Merrin, and a map, and a valley where oaths go to sleep. And of a swordspeaker. The last one."

Felix leaned against the doorframe and arranged her face into the pleasant blankness she'd spent thirty years perfecting. "That's a lot of story for one ditch."

"It was a very talkative ditch."

"I sharpen blades, Sir—" She caught the title on his face, the flicker of pain. "Mister Vane. That's all I do. I don't know anything about couriers or maps or—" she gestured vaguely, "—speaking."

"You have your mother's hands."

The words landed harder than they should have. Felix kept her expression still through sheer stubborn practice. "You knew my mother?"

"No one who served the court *knew* her. She was before our time in the household. But I knew her sigil." His gaze went past Felix's shoulder, into the dim interior of the forge, and fixed there. On the wall, half-hidden by a hanging apron, was an old shield boss she'd never gotten around to selling—a relic of her mother's that Felix kept because throwing it in the river had felt too much like a second funeral. Six-petaled yarrow. Crossed stems.

Aldric stepped forward without asking, which Felix decided to forgive only because he moved like a man who'd forgotten how to intrude politely, and lifted the boss from its hook with both hands, reverent as a priest handling bones.

"She carried this at the siege of Callenmoor," he said quietly. "Before you were born. Before—" He set it down gently on the workbench, aligning it precisely with the edge, because some part of him apparently couldn't bear to leave anything crooked. "Lady Yarrow swore the ward-oath that built this valley. Did you know that?"

"The elders say the founders did."

"Lady Yarrow *was* the founder. The others signed their names to her work." He turned to face Felix fully then, and Felix saw him decide something—the way a man decides to jump. "And I think you know it. I think you've known what you are since you were a child saying words that came true, and hiding them, and hating yourself for hiding them, and I know exactly what that costs, because I've paid a version of the same bill for twenty years."

"That's quite a speech for a stranger."

"I rehearsed it. Three hundred miles of rain gives a man time to rehearse."

Despite herself—despite everything—Felix felt the corner of her mouth twitch. She killed it immediately. "Whatever you want, the answer's no."

"You don't know what I want yet."

"You're an oathbreaker. You want me to break your oath. Everyone who finds a swordspeaker wants the same thing—it's the whole appeal, isn't it? Words that unmake words." She crossed her arms. "Well, I don't do that. I don't *do* anything. I'm a blacksmith. Blades go sharp, customers go home, nobody's reality gets rearranged. Find another miracle worker."

"I can't." For the first time his composure cracked—not dramatically, just a seam letting light through. "There are no others. There's you or there's nothing, and I've lived with nothing for twenty years, madam, and I am *tired*."

Something in his voice—that specific exhaustion, the kind that has given up on sympathy and settled for endurance—snagged on Felix like a burr. She knew that voice. She'd used it, alone, to her own reflection.

Still. "No," she said, gentler this time. "I'm sorry. Genuinely. But whatever chain you're wearing, I can't take it off. Taking it off means admitting what I am, in the open, in a valley that exists specifically so I never have to. And if I do that—" She stopped. The map was in the flour bin. The Yarrow soul was called due. Some stranger in a ruined cloak didn't need to hear any of it.

But Aldric Vane was watching her with the terrible attentiveness of a man trained to read battlefields, and she saw the exact moment he read hers.

"There's already something," he said slowly. "Someone found you first."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Your forge hasn't lit in three days. Your hands are shaking. You buried someone behind the willow—I passed the fresh earth on my way in, and the ground wasn't settled right for anything smaller than a man. And you're standing between me and that shelf"—he nodded at the corner where the flour bin sat, and Felix realized with a lurch that she had, without deciding to, positioned herself squarely in front of it—"like a dog guarding a bone that's burning her mouth."

Felix said nothing. Her heart was doing something undignified.

"Map?" he asked.

"This conversation is over."

"It's an Undercourt cipher," he said. "Isn't it. Blood-pressed vellum, burns like wet wool, and it hums if you hold it near your ear at night. I've seen one die trying to destroy one. They can't be burned, drowned, buried, or reasoned with. They can only be *followed*."

Silence stretched between them, taut as wire. Rain ticked against the eaves. Somewhere in the yard, a chicken expressed opinions.

"How do you know that?" Felix asked finally.

"Because twenty years ago, I escorted the courier who carried the last one." He looked down at his hands—big, scarred, steady except at the fingertips. "Into the mountain. And I stood outside the gate when he came back out alone, without the soul he'd gone in to sell, and I watched the court's magic seal the door behind him. I was young. I thought it was trade. Commerce. Souls going where souls were needed." His jaw worked. "I was young."

Felix's arms had tightened around herself without permission. "Whose soul?"

"I don't know. Couriers weren't told names. But the sigil on the manifest—" he glanced at the shield boss, at the six-petaled flower, "—matched that."

The floor seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way it had when Odile had said *your mother's soul was the price*. Felix gripped the workbench. "Get out."

"Madam Yarrow—"

"Get *out* of my forge." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, hated the tremble, hated this whole day, this whole cursed inheritance. "You come here with your rain and your ruined face and your—your *manifests*, and you think you're the first person in two days to turn my mother into a ledger entry? She was a *person*. She sang off-key. She burned bread. She—" Felix stopped, because the next part was the dangerous part, the true part: *she loved me, and someone sold her anyway, and everyone who tells me about it keeps talking about sigils and ciphers and manifests as if that adds up to a woman.*

Aldric didn't leave. He also didn't move closer. He simply stood there in the doorway, dripping, taking her anger the way a sea wall takes waves—with patience, with structure, with the weary competence of a thing built to withstand worse.

"I'm not leaving," he said, "because you shouldn't be alone with that map. Whatever you choose to do with it, no one should carry an Undercourt debt by themselves. I've seen what happens to people who try." He paused. "Also, I have nowhere else to go, and it's raining."

Despite everything—despite grief and fury and the flour bin sitting three feet away humming faintly, smugly, like a cat that had won an argument—something in Felix's chest loosened half a notch.

"You're insufferable," she said.

"I've been told. Usually by princes."

"*Former* princes?"

"There's only been one," Aldric said, "and he's dead, and it's my fault, and we're not discussing it tonight." Something crossed his face then, quick and dark, like a fish under ice. "Tonight I'll sleep in your yard. Tomorrow I'll chop wood or mend your fence or whatever penance passes for hospitality here, and tomorrow night, maybe, you'll show me the map. Or you won't. I've waited twenty years. I can wait one more day."

"Why?" Felix asked. It came out smaller than she meant it to. "Why offer passage? Why help me? What do you actually want?"

Aldric looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin.

"When I find a swordspeaker," he said quietly, "I want her to free me. That hasn't changed. But a man doesn't ask a favor of someone he's watched drown. So first, I get you wherever you're going alive." He shrugged, an enormous, tired motion. "Consider it an investment."

"In your own freedom."

"In the only thing I have left to invest."

Felix stared at him. Then at the shield boss on her bench, six petals and crossed stems, her mother's whole life compressed into a symbol men kept using to talk about her without ever mentioning the singing or the bread.

"One night," she said. "You can sleep in the forge. Touch nothing. And if you snore, I'm sharpening something loud at dawn."

"I've slept in worse company than a hammer collection," Aldric said, and there—there it was, brief and rusty, the ghost of a smile, like sunlight finding a crack in old armor. "Thank you, Madam Yarrow."

"Don't thank me. I still haven't agreed to anything."

"No," he agreed, turning toward the door. "But you moved the apron off the shield boss last month. Someone who truly wants to forget doesn't keep dusting the evidence."

He stepped out into the rain, which received him again like an old grudge, and Felix stood alone in her forge with her mother's sigil gleaming on the bench and the flour bin humming its smug little song, and understood, with the clarity of a blade coming off the wheel, that she had just acquired a companion.

Or a complication. With knights, history suggested, the categories tended to blur.

* * *

She almost didn't show him the map.

She lay awake most of the night listening to the rain ease off and the strange rhythm of a stranger breathing in her forge—in through the nose, slow, controlled, the breathing of a man who'd taught himself to sleep lightly and wake ready, and who probably dreamed of chapel candles and a boy on altar steps. Around the third hour she gave up, lit a lamp, and fetched the flour bin down before she could argue herself out of it.

Aldric was awake instantly—of course he was—rising from the pallet by the cold forge with a dagger already in his hand, blinking at the lamp, at her, at the bundle in her arms.

"Blood-pressed vellum," he said softly. "May I?"

She unwrapped it on the workbench between them, beside the shield boss, mother's sigil and mother's debt laid side by side like an accusation. Under lamplight the map was worse than she remembered: the ink moving, faintly, the way insects move under bark. Mountain ranges rearranging themselves. A route that redrew itself each time she looked away, always ending at the same place—a cavern mouth rendered in strokes that hurt to follow, labeled in a script that wasn't writing so much as appetite.

"Harrow Fen, the toll road, the market at Graywater Crossing," Aldric murmured, tracing the route with one blunt finger. "Then the pass. Then the mountain." He looked up. "You've tried to destroy it."

"Four times."

"The fourth time is always the funny one. What happened?"

"It sulked. Is that possible? Can a map sulk?"

"With these, yes. They're not objects, they're *invitations*. And invitations resent being declined." He studied her across the lamplight, and Felix braced for pity—for the soft voice, the careful face. Instead he said, matter-of-fact: "You're going to follow it."

"I haven't decided."

"You have. You dug a grave for a man you met dying. You hid the map instead of reporting it. You've been standing at that tree line your whole life waiting for a reason." He tapped the vellum, once, gently. "You don't need my help deciding. You need my help surviving the decision. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters."

Felix looked down at the map, and the map, with what she could have sworn was satisfaction, drew itself a new road.

"The market at Graywater Crossing," she said slowly. "If I go, I'll need supplies. Rope. Food. A guide who knows what a soul-broker looks like before he's bought something of mine."

"I know what they look like," Aldric said. "They look helpful."

"And I can't carry all this alone, and I can't ask anyone in Thornvale, because anyone in Thornvale would tell Odile, and Odile would burn the map or bury me with it." She exhaled. "How far is Graywater from here?"

"Six days, if the passes are open. Five if we lie to the horses." He paused. "*Horse,* singular. Mine wouldn't cross the tree line."

"So we walk into the mountains with one horse between us and a map that sulks." Felix rubbed her eyes. "My mother sold her soul for a quiet valley, and I'm going to get eaten by a cave because of a piece of paper that won't burn."

"Lady Yarrow," Aldric said quietly, "sold her soul so that her daughter could grow up somewhere the court couldn't count her. Whatever you do next, she'd not call that a waste."

Felix didn't answer. Her throat had closed in a way that had nothing to do with speech.

Aldric rolled the map carefully, returned it to its cloth, and pushed it back across the bench toward her—an offering, a surrender of custody, a small deliberate act of trust from a man who had every reason to grab it and run.

"Sleep," he said. "We leave at dawn, if we leave. And Madam Yarrow—" he paused at the pallet, silhouetted against the banked forge, an enormous ruin of a man holding himself together with duty and spite, "—for what it's worth: I'm glad the last swordspeaker turned out to be a blacksmith. Blades and vows aren't so different. Both of them only hold if someone tends the edge."

He lay down. Within minutes his breathing slowed into that soldier's rhythm, in through the nose, ready for anything.

Felix sat alone with the lamp and the map and her mother's sigil, and outside, at the valley's edge, the rain fell straight as a drawn line—the world divided into the place that forgot and everything beyond it.

At dawn, she was going to cross it.

She blew out the lamp, and in the dark, very quietly, she began to pack.
