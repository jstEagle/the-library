## 5. The Thief with No Yesterday

The trouble with having no yesterday, Wren had decided long ago, was that you never learned anything from your mistakes.

She knew this because she'd made the same mistake twice in one evening, and both times the mistake was named Aldric Vane.

The first time, she lifted his purse outside the border inn at Harrow's Rest while he was arguing with a horse. That went fine. The second time, she lifted it again ten minutes later because she'd forgotten she already had it — forgotten, specifically, that her left boot now held two purses and that one of them jingled when she walked, which was how the knight had caught her the first time, and how he caught her the second, and why he was now holding her wrist in a grip like a manacle while she dangled her stolen goods between them like an offering to a very tired god.

"That's mine," Aldric said.

"Is it?" Wren looked at the purse with genuine interest. It was a nice purse. Cracked leather, brass clasp, the particular sag of a man who carried more coin than sense. "Huh. Doesn't ring any bells."

"Nothing rings any bells with you. I've been watching you work the yard for an hour. You stole from me twice. Twice. In the same coat."

"Well," said Wren, "in my defense—"

"Do you have a defense?"

"I was going to say I have no memory of doing it, but you've rather ruined the surprise." She smiled her best smile, the one that had gotten her out of three arrests, two marriages, and one memorable incident involving a goat. It did not appear to be working on Aldric Vane, whose face suggested a man being slowly worn down by weather. "Look. Knight. Sir. Whatever we're calling you. Let's talk about this like reasonable people. You want your coin back. I want to not be hanged. Surely there's a middle ground where everyone walks away richer than they started, or at least equally poor."

"The middle ground," said Aldric, "is that you give it back and I don't break your fingers."

"You see, most people would find that threatening, but honestly? It's the nicest offer anyone's made me all week." She turned the purses over. Both of them. His eyebrows did something complicated when he saw there were two. "Fine. Take them. But I want you to know that as crimes go, this is the least interesting thing I've done today, and if you'd caught me an hour earlier you'd have been genuinely impressed."

"I doubt that."

"You should hear about the pie."

"I don't want to hear about the pie."

"It was a *very* large pie."

* * *

Here was the thing about Wren — and she knew this the way you know the shape of your own hands, which is to say intimately and without any idea where it came from: she remembered nothing before three years ago.

Not a name-day. Not a mother. Not a hometown or a first kiss or the smell of bread or what her own voice sounded like before it settled into its current register of cheerful insolence. Three years ago she had woken up on the road south of the mountains with sore feet, empty pockets, and a single memory burned into her like a brand: standing in a dark place full of lanterns, sliding something precious across a counter, and a voice like cold honey saying, *"Paid in full."*

She didn't know what she'd sold. She only knew the shape of the hole it left.

So she'd done what anyone sensible does with an unbearable absence: she filled it with other people's things. If she couldn't remember who she was, she could at least remember who everyone else was — their habits, their hiding places, the little tells that said *this pocket is heavier than it looks*. She'd built herself a self out of other people's secrets, stitch by stitch, and if the seams showed sometimes in the dark, well. Nobody looked that closely. Nobody ever looked that closely.

Except, apparently, disgraced knights with nothing better to do than stand in inn yards judging pie theft.

Aldric marched her inside by the wrist, which was unnecessary and also slightly flattering, and sat her down at a corner table across from — Wren's eyes went wide — the girl from the smithy.

She'd seen them come in an hour ago: the big grim knight and a young woman with forge-calloused hands and the look of someone carrying a secret the size of a cathedral. Wren noticed people like that. People with secrets were predictable, and predictable people were profitable, and she'd marked them immediately as *not worth it* — too grim, too watchful, too many swords between them. And yet here she was, dragged to their table like a cat presenting a dead bird.

"Wren," said Aldric, dropping her into a chair. "Meet Felix Yarrow. Felix, meet the thief who robbed me twice."

"Felix Yarrow," Wren repeated, and then, because the name snagged on something — some instinct older than her missing years — she added, "Yarrow. Like the flower. Healing plant. Grows everywhere, hard to kill."

Felix blinked. "I... suppose?"

"Lovely name. Terrible for thieving, though. Everyone remembers a Yarrow." Wren propped her chin on her hand. "Now. Before you decide whether to break my fingers — and sir, I can see you're still considering it, which hurts my feelings — ask yourself: what could a charming, hungry, extremely talented stranger possibly offer two travelers heading east with a map they think nobody else can read?"

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Wren watched Felix's hand drift, very casually, toward the inner pocket of her coat — watched Aldric's eyes flick sideways in warning — and grinned so hard her face hurt.

"Oh, you *do* have a map. Oh, this is wonderful. You have a map with a cipher, and neither of you can read it, and you've been staring at it in public like a pair of sheep discussing algebra." She leaned back, arms crossed, radiating smugness. "Gentlefolk. I can read it."

"You cannot possibly—" Aldric started.

"Undercourt cipher. Ledger-script, third revision — the one they switched to after the second revision got cracked by a broker in Lowmarket and half the debtors' marks got forged." She said it lightly, easily, and then stopped, because she had absolutely no idea how she knew it. The words had simply arrived in her mouth fully formed, like a stone surfacing in a stream she couldn't see the bottom of. Her grin faded by a degree. "...Which I know. Somehow. Don't ask me how. Asking me how is a cruel question and I'll bite you."

Felix and Aldric exchanged a look — the long, silent, married-couple argument kind — and Wren felt something twist under her ribs that she chose not to examine. She'd seen couples do that look. She'd seen mothers and daughters do it, sisters, old friends. It was the look of people with a shared history, a whole warehouse of yesterdays they could rifle through without thinking. She'd spent three years watching other people do it and telling herself she didn't mind.

"So," she said, briskly, before the twist could become a wound. "Terms. I read your map, you feed me dinner — a real dinner, meat involved, none of this road-slop — and you don't turn me over to the constable, who I should mention owes me money, so really you'd be doing him a favor too. Deal?"

"Why," said Aldric slowly, "would you help us? What's in the Undercourt for you?"

And there it was. The real question. Wren considered lying — she was good at lying, it was practically a trade skill — but something about the way Felix was looking at her, steady and unimpressed and a little bit sad, like she already knew what broken looked like from the inside, made the lie die on Wren's tongue.

"They have something of mine," she said. "Memories. Three years' worth. Sold them — or sold *me*, I'm not entirely clear on the mechanics, the receipt was not itemized. I woke up on a road with nothing but my name and an invoice written somewhere behind my eyes." She shrugged, aiming for breezy and mostly hitting it. "So. I'm going to walk into the worst market in the world and shoplift myself back. And I need your map to get in the door."

Another silence. This one softer.

"Dinner first," Felix said at last. "Then we talk."

Wren could have kissed her.

* * *

The inn at Harrow's Rest served mutton stew thick enough to stand a spoon in, brown bread, ale that tasted faintly of the barrel, and — when Felix quietly ordered it without making a production of it — a whole roast fowl, because Wren had mentioned meat and apparently meant it as a legal requirement.

Wren ate like a woman released from prison. She was aware this was undignified. She did not care. There was a specific joy in eating food you hadn't stolen, a warmth that had nothing to do with the gravy — though the gravy helped — and she worked through the fowl with the focus of a scholar deciphering scripture while the other two pretended not to watch.

"You're going to make yourself sick," Aldric observed.

"I am going to make myself *happy*, which is adjacent." She mopped the plate with bread. "You know what I remember about food? Nothing. Nothing before three years ago. First meal I ever ate in this life was half a stolen apple, eaten running, and I cried the entire time and didn't know why. Now every meal I eat, part of me is checking — is this the taste? Is this the one I lost? Is this what my mother's kitchen smelled like?" She took a long drink of ale. "It isn't. None of them are. But I keep checking anyway."

The table went quiet. Wren winced internally. *Well done, idiot. Nothing endears you to strangers like grief over stew.*

But Felix didn't look away, and she didn't pity-pat her hand either. She just said, "My whole valley forgets things. On purpose. Magic dies at the tree line, and everything past it might as well be a story. I grew up thinking that was safety." She turned her cup slowly on the wood. "Maybe it was a cage with the door painted on."

"And now you're walking out of it," Wren said.

"Now I'm walking out of it."

"Toward a market that eats souls." Wren raised her tankard. "To terrible decisions made for excellent reasons."

"To excellent reasons," Felix echoed, and clinked, and Aldric sighed the sigh of a man who could already see where this was going and drank too.

It was, Wren thought, the first toast of her life she could actually remember. Everything before three years ago was fog; everything since had been transactions, exits, aliases. But this — warm room, warm food, two almost-strangers drinking to her terrible plan like it was a birthday — this she filed away carefully, in the front of her mind, where she kept the few things that mattered. *First toast. With the knight and the smithy-girl.* Found family wasn't a word she used. But she noted the stitch going in all the same, small and crooked, the way a seam starts.

* * *

The map came out after the plates were cleared, under Aldric's shielding forearm, spread flat on the scarred table. Up close it was worse and better than Wren expected: worse because it was bloodstained and the edges were charred, better because the cipher was crisp, deliberate, written by someone who'd known exactly what they were carrying.

Her finger traced the lines without her permission. "Ledger-script, third revision, like I said. But this header—" She frowned. The symbols swam, then sharpened, meaning arriving in her head like a fish thrown through a window. "*Account called due. The Yarrow soul answers to the Regent's seal.*" She stopped. Looked up. "Whose soul is a Yarrow?"

Felix had gone very still. "Mine," she said finally. "My mother's. Apparently." Her voice was flat the way ice is flat — smooth until you step wrong. "Twenty years ago. Before I was born. Nobody told me until this week."

Wren whistled low. "Twenty years of interest. That's not a debt anymore, friend. That's an empire."

"I know." Felix's jaw tightened. "Can you read where it's held?"

Wren bent back over the map, grateful for the excuse to stop looking at that face. The cipher unspooled under her fingers — vault levels, toll-gates, the deep archive, a mark she recognized with a lurch that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with dread:

*"Payment accepted. Paid in full."*

The same phrase. The same seal-shape from her one surviving memory, rendered here in ink instead of lantern-light. Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"This mark," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "This is the seal from my memory. The night I sold whatever I sold. Same sigil. Same office." She tapped the symbol at the map's heart. "Whatever took my three years, and whoever holds your mother — Felix, I don't think those are two debts. I think they're the same ledger."

Felix and Aldric looked at each other again — that silent married-couple argument — but this time Wren was included in it, pulled into the circle of it, and she found she didn't hate that at all.

"Three days' ride to the mountain," Aldric said at last. "We leave at dawn."

"We?" said Wren.

"You read the map."

"You fed me dinner."

"Those weren't unrelated events," Felix said, and there — there it was, the ghost of a smile, quick and real, the first crack in all that careful iron. "You're coming with us, thief. Try to rob anyone else on the way and I'll tell Aldric about the pie."

"There *was* no pie," said Aldric.

"There was definitely a pie," said Wren solemnly. "You'll understand eventually. It's a whole thing."

Later, up in the rented room she absolutely had not paid for, Wren lay on her back and listened to the inn settle, and did her nightly accounting — the only inventory she trusted, because it was the only one that fit in one head. One map memorized. Two allies, provisional. One fowl, consumed. One seal, matched, terrifying.

And underneath it all, quiet as a banked coal: *the same ledger.*

Somewhere under the mountain was a counter with her name — her real name, her whole name — scratched behind it. Somewhere down there, maybe, was the rest of her.

For the first time in three years, the hole in her didn't feel like a grave.

It felt like a door.

She fell asleep smiling, and if she dreamed of lanterns, she didn't remember it in the morning — which was, all things considered, the least of what she'd lost, and the first thing she meant to take back.
