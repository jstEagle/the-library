## 6. Crossing the Tree Line

The tree line did not look like a border. That was the first thing Felix noticed, and the thing that unsettled her most.

She had imagined it her whole life as a wall — something visible, a shimmer or a scar, the way Odile described the ward in her careful matriarch's voice: *a blessing laid over Thornvale like a blanket*. But standing at the northern edge of the valley with a pack on her shoulders and a dead man's map against her ribs, Felix saw only birches giving way to pines, ordinary trees on ordinary hillsides, and a morning so clear it felt like an accusation.

"You're stalling," Wren said.

"I'm assessing."

"Assessing." The thief hopped up onto a boulder, balanced there on the balls of her feet like she'd been born on rooftops. "You've been assessing that same clump of moss for ten minutes. I know. I counted."

"She's saying goodbye," Aldric said quietly.

Felix glared at him. The knight sat his gray mare with the patience of a fence post, his broken sword strapped across his back where a whole one should have been, watching her with those tired eyes that saw entirely too much for her comfort.

"I'm not saying goodbye," she said. "I live here. I'll be back by week's end, probably. This is a trip. An errand. A very strange errand involving soul-debt and possible regicide—"

"Not regicide," Aldric corrected mildly. "Regent-icide. Different legal category."

"Oh, thank you. That makes it much better."

Wren laughed — a bright, startled sound, like she surprised herself with it — and Felix found, annoyingly, that she liked being the cause of it. Three days out of Thornvale's inn and the thief had already developed the habit of orbiting Felix at mealtimes, stealing bread off her plate with the shameless ease of a younger sister. It should have irritated her. Instead it kept catching in her chest like a fishhook.

*Don't get attached,* said the sensible voice in her head, the one that sounded suspiciously like Odile. *You are walking toward the place that bought your mother's soul. Everyone you love is leverage.*

The problem was that the sensible voice had lost all credibility three days ago, when Felix learned that the woman who'd raised her had been the one holding the knife.

So instead she turned to face the valley one last time — properly this time, not the sideways glance of someone pretending not to look.

Thornvale in autumn was a bowl of gold and smoke. Terraced fields running down to the river, the smithy's chimney a thin gray thread among them, the longhouse roofs steaming in the early sun. Somewhere down there was Tamsin the baker, who owed her for two hinge repairs. Old Bram with his bad knee. The children who came to watch her work and asked why her hammers sang. She knew every roof. Every roof knew her, or thought it did — knew Felix Yarrow, smith, quiet, unremarkable, the girl who never left and never quite fit and never explained why.

They didn't know about the oaths. About the words she'd swallowed her whole life, pressing them down behind her teeth like stones, because in Thornvale a swordspeaker was a story mothers told to frighten children, and stories that turn out to be true get their tellers burned.

"They'll be fine without you," Aldric said. Not unkindly. "Valleys always are. It's the people who leave who change."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of," Felix muttered.

She touched the map through her shirt. It lay flat and cool now, docile, but last night by the fire she'd watched Wren trace its cipher-lines with one finger and murmur half the key under her breath before going pale and stopping. *I know this writing,* she'd whispered. *I don't remember learning it. My hands remember.* Then she'd gone quiet in a way that made Aldric pour her wine without being asked.

Felix took a breath. Turned north. Took a step.

And then another.

The moment her boot crossed the line where the birches ended and the pines began, the world *changed*.

There was no other word for it. One stride she was walking through ordinary air; the next, magic hit her like a wave breaking over her head — like plunging into a mountain lake in April, cold enough to stop the heart, vast enough to make the heart start again out of pure outrage.

She staggered. Her knees buckled. Someone shouted — Wren — and hands caught her arms, but Felix barely felt them, because every oath she had ever swallowed in twenty-three years of silence woke up at once and began to *sing*.

It wasn't sound. It was pressure. It was the memory of every vow she'd ever spoken into her pillow as a child, every promise she'd made and smothered before it could take root: *I wish I were brave. I wish my mother were here. I wish someone would see me.* Small, childish wishes, spoken carelessly in the one place where they couldn't come true — except that they hadn't died. They had waited. They had coiled up in her blood like seeds in frozen ground, and now the ground was thawing, and they were *whispering*, rustling through her veins like leaves, like moths against glass, like a crowd of small hungry voices all saying her name —

"Felix." Aldric's voice, right at her ear, steady as an anvil. "Breathe. You're drowning. Breathe slow, like you're lifting something heavy."

"I don't—" Her own voice sounded wrong to her, layered, echoing faintly at the edges. "It's *in me*, it's been in me this whole time, I thought it was just — I thought I was just *me*—"

"You are," he said firmly. "That's the trick of it. The gift isn't something added to you. It's what's left when everything else gets stripped away. Now breathe."

She breathed.

Slowly — over what might have been a minute or an hour — the roaring settled into something like a tide. Still there. Still enormous. But rhythmic. Bearable. Felix knelt in the pine needles with Wren's hand still gripping her arm hard enough to bruise and became aware, distantly, of the world around her: the smell of resin and damp earth, the chatter of birdsong that sounded somehow *denser* than valley birdsong, the light falling through the canopy in shafts that seemed to have opinions.

"Is it supposed to feel like that?" Wren asked. Her face was white. "Because you looked like someone had poured the ocean into your ear."

"Something like that," Felix rasped.

"It's worse for her than most," Aldric said. He dismounted and crouched in front of Felix, studying her face with clinical attention. "Twenty years of suppression inside a warded valley. Most swordspeakers grow up steeped in ambient magic; it dilutes the gift, teaches it manners. You grew up in a drought, girl. Everything you are has been waiting behind a dam." He paused. "How many oaths have you spoken? Truly spoken, meant with your whole heart?"

"I don't know. None. I mean—" Felix laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "When I was seven I swore to the hearth-god that I'd never eat parsnips again. Nothing happened. I assumed nothing would ever happen."

Aldric went very still.

"What?"

"Nothing happened," Felix repeated. "In the valley. Because the valley eats magic, right? That's the whole point of the ward."

"Yes," Aldric said slowly. "Yes, that's certainly the explanation I'd reach for too." But he was looking at her the way he'd looked at the map when he recognized the sigil — like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

* * *

They made camp that night beside a river, because Wren insisted that any journey without at least one riverside camp was legally a forced march. She built a fire with theatrical efficiency, produced four potatoes from somewhere on her person that no one wanted examined too closely, and declared herself cook by right of conquest.

The river ran quick and silver in the dusk, maybe fifteen feet across, chattering over stones. Felix sat apart from the fire, as she'd sat apart from fires her whole life, and tried to make sense of herself.

The whispering hadn't stopped. If anything it had organized. All day, as they rode north through country she'd never seen — real country, wild country, hills that rolled away green and gold to a horizon that simply *kept going* — the voices in her blood had sorted themselves, quiet ones sinking deep, loud ones rising. And underneath them all ran a current she could only describe as *attention*. The world was paying attention to her. Grass bent slightly toward her boots. A hawk overhead circled once, twice, and screamed something that felt almost like a question.

"This is how everyone lives?" she said aloud, to no one. "Out here. Just... swimming in this?"

"Most people swim shallow," Aldric answered from the fire. He'd drifted over, casual as anything, though she noticed he'd brought his sword and set it across his knees. "Mages dabble. Hedge-witches sip. Even the great oath-bound lords only ever swear what they can afford to keep." He nodded at her. "You're not swimming, Felix. You're the river deciding whether it wants banks."

"That is the least comforting sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once had a customer describe his boil in detail."

He smiled — the rare, crooked one that transformed his whole weathered face and vanished again like a coin dropped in deep water. Then he rose and left her alone, which was its own kind of kindness. He was getting good at knowing when she needed company and when she needed the absence of it. Felix was starting to suspect that was what Aldric Vane actually was, beneath the disgrace and the flayed-looking guilt: a man who paid attention.

Night came down. The fire shrank to embers. Wren slept curled like a cat with her stolen-potatoes belly full, muttering fragments — *not the blue one, the blue one bites* — and Aldric took first watch, sitting motionless, facing the dark.

Felix walked to the river.

It was colder down at the bank, mist rising off the water in ghost-threads, the moon laying a road of light straight down the current. She crouched. Dipped her fingers in. The water was shockingly cold, alive, impatient, going somewhere with the absolute conviction of water everywhere.

*I can't go back,* she thought. Not physically — her legs worked fine, the valley was a day's ride south. But some door had closed behind her on that tree line, and she knew it the way you know a bone is broken. Whatever she'd been in Thornvale — the quiet smith, the hidden girl, the careful, careful daughter of a careful lie — that person could not survive out here. The whispers wouldn't let her. Every mile north peeled another layer off, and underneath, she was finding things she didn't recognize.

*Who am I if I'm not hiding?*

The question scared her more than the Undercourt did.

And because she was alone, and the moon was bright, and no one could see — Felix did the thing she'd done a thousand times in the dark of her bedroom, the thing that had never once worked, the childish ritual she'd never managed to abandon:

She spoke a wish out loud.

"I wish," she whispered to the river, to the moon, to whatever was listening with such terrible attention, "that the water would hold still. Just for a moment. Just so I can see my own face."

The words left her mouth and she felt them leave — felt them as physical things, syllables with weight and edges, dropping from her tongue into the night air like coins into a well.

The river stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* Mid-current, mid-chatter, the whole silver breadth of it freezing between one heartbeat and the next into something like polished glass — but not ice, not dead, the water still moving somewhere far beneath, held, the way a singer holds a note. The mist hung motionless above it. The moon-road lay perfectly still, and in it, framed in black pine and white starlight, was a reflection Felix had never seen before.

Her own face — yes, familiar, sharp-cheeked, soot-smudged, hers. But around the face, in the held water, other things showed. Faint lines radiating from her lips like ripples in reverse, converging instead of spreading. Words she couldn't read written along them in a script that hurt to look at directly, elegant and old and unmistakably *herself*. The whispers in her blood went silent, every one, as if they too had leaned in to look.

Felix stared at the woman in the water, and the woman stared back, and for one suspended moment Felix understood — truly understood, in her bones, the way she understood hot iron and cold quench-water — what she was. Not a smith who happened to have a curse. Not a girl with a strange inheritance. A *swordspeaker*. The last one. A person whose words were load-bearing beams in the architecture of the world, and who had spent her entire life in a house built specifically to keep her from noticing.

Terror came first. It rolled through her in a great cold wave: *this is what they burned people for, this is what the valley was built to bury, this is what Odile sold my mother to contain.*

But underneath the terror, quieter, treacherous, warm as a coal cupped in both hands: *awe.*

She had made a river hold still. With a wish. With six careless words.

"Let go," Felix whispered, and her voice shook. "Please. I didn't mean — I didn't know what I was asking. Let go."

The river released. Sound crashed back — the chatter and gurgle of it, suddenly enormous, and the mist swirled, and the moon-road shattered into a thousand trembling pieces, and Felix sat down hard in the mud at the bank and laughed, high and unhinged, until tears ran down her face.

"Okay," she said to the night, wiping her eyes. "Okay. Noted. No more wishes."

"Bit late for that lesson, I think."

She hadn't heard Wren approach — of course she hadn't; the thief moved like spilled mercury. Wren dropped down beside her in the mud without hesitation, pulled her cloak around them both, and peered at the river with frank professional interest.

"So," she said. "You froze it."

"For a second."

"The whole river."

"Just the surface, mostly, I think—"

"The whole *river*, Felix." Wren turned to look at her, and in the moonlight her eyes were very wide and very bright. There was something raw underneath the awe, and Felix realized with a jolt what it was: envy. Longing. The look of a girl whose past had been scooped out of her like the flesh of a fruit, looking at someone whose self was not merely intact but *overflowing*. "Do you have any idea," Wren said softly, "what I'd give to have one true memory of myself? And you've got so many you're freezing rivers with the leftovers."

Felix's throat tightened. "Wren—"

"No, no, I'm not — that came out wrong. Mostly." Wren scrubbed a hand through her hair and laughed at herself. "It's just — sitting next to you out here is like sitting next to a bonfire. I can feel you thinking louder than I think. Is that mad? That's mad. Forget I said it."

But Felix didn't forget it. Because Wren was shivering, and not from the cold, and when Felix put an arm around her shoulders the thief let her, stiff at first and then leaning in, and something settled in Felix's chest — warm, terrifying, new. Not the whispers. Something older than the whispers. Something that felt less like magic and more like a decision being made somewhere below the level of words.

*These two,* said the wordless thing in her chest, gesturing at the fire, at the sleeping knight, at the sharp-eyed girl in her arms. *Keep these two.*

Across the camp, Aldric rose from his watch and stood looking at them — at Felix — with an expression she couldn't read. Not alarm. Something closer to grief, or to recognition. When he spoke, his voice carried clearly across the water-mist.

"You should know," he called softly, "before we go further. What you did just now — a river holds for a swordspeaker's whim, but the court will have felt it. Word travels ahead of us now. Faster than we do."

Felix went cold. "What does that mean?"

Aldric looked north, toward the black shoulder of the mountain, where the Undercourt waited with its ledger and its jars and its Regent wearing a borrowed face.

"It means," he said, "that for the first time in twenty years, something down there knows exactly where you are. And it knows what you're worth."

Beside her, Wren had gone very still — and her hand, Felix noticed, had drifted unconsciously to the cipher-map at Felix's belt, fingers tracing letters her mind couldn't read but her hands remembered.

"The blue one," Wren whispered, to no one at all. "The blue one bites."
