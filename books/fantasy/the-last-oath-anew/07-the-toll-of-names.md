## 7. The Toll of Names

The market at Grayhollow smelled of wet wool and burnt sugar, which Wren decided was the most honest smell in the world. Everything else here was a lie with a price tag.

She'd been to markets before—three years of them, at least, though her memories only stretched back so far as a rainy crossroads and a purse that wasn't hers yet. Markets were all the same underneath: stalls selling what people wanted, haggling dressed up as friendship. But this one sat on the border between Thornvale's silence and the wide world where magic still worked, and it had teeth.

"Keep your hood up," Aldric muttered, walking half a step behind them like a wall that had learned to grumble. He'd bought a plain traveling cloak in the first row and looked deeply offended by it, as if the wool had personally failed him. "And don't touch anything."

"I never touch anything," Wren said. "I borrow it briefly and then it belongs to me."

"That's not—" He gave up, which she counted as a small victory. Sir Aldric Vane, oathbreaker knight, defeated by grammar.

Felix walked beside her, shoulders drawn up around her ears. Three days past the tree line and she still moved like someone expecting the world to notice her and demand an explanation. Which, Wren was learning, it might. The map rode inside Felix's coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and every few steps Felix's hand went to it the way other people touched a wound.

"It's just a market," Wren said. "We need rope, dried plums, and something for Aldric's personality."

"My personality is fine," Aldric said.

"See? Something for it."

But even as she joked, Wren's eyes were working. That was the thing about being empty—you learned to fill yourself up with other people's details. The way a woman three stalls down kept her coin in her left boot. The boy selling skewers who watched the guard rotation instead of his customers. The fact that nobody in this market looked at the stall at the very end of the row, the one draped in gray silk with no sign and no goods displayed—and everybody, somehow, angled their walking away from it.

Wren had been many things in three years. She could not remember if she'd ever been brave, but she was curious, and curiosity did the same work with worse manners.

"We split up," Felix said quietly. "Rope and food, meet at the well. Twenty minutes."

"Twenty-five," Wren said. "There's a candied-nut man."

"There is always a candied-nut man," Aldric sighed, "and there is always you, finding him."

* * *

She lasted eleven minutes before the gray silk stall found her instead.

She hadn't meant to go near it. Truly. She'd been circling toward the nuts when a voice slid out from under the silk like smoke under a door.

"You're the empty girl."

Wren stopped. In her experience, stopping was the worst possible response to a hook, and yet her feet had opinions of their own.

The broker was a small person wrapped in gray silk several layers deep, face shadowed by a hood lined with silver thread that caught light without seeming to reflect any. On the table before them lay nothing at all—just a shallow dish of dark water and a knife with a bone handle, worn smooth as sea glass.

"Empty?" Wren said lightly. "Sir or madam or otherwise, I'm stuffed full. I've had four candied nuts already."

"The court sold you," the broker said, ignoring this entirely. Their voice was pleasant the way a warm bath is pleasant right up until you realize the door has no latch. "Three years ago. A full extraction—all of it, name to childhood. Sloppy work at the edges; they always are. You've got a hole in you shaped like a life." They tilted the hood, and though Wren couldn't see a face, she felt herself *looked through*, catalogued, priced. "I could buy you back."

The market noise went distant. Not gone—just far away, like sound heard through water.

"Buy me back," Wren repeated.

"What the court holds, the court will sell, if the price is right and the buyer is clever. And I am both." A gray-silk finger traced the rim of the water dish. "Your memories are sitting in someone's vault, little hole-in-the-shape-of-a-girl, gathering dust. I know which shelf. For a consideration—a small service, a delivery perhaps—I could retrieve them. Every yesterday you've lost. Your name. Your mother's voice, if she had one. Whether you liked candied nuts before you knew they existed."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. Her hand, entirely without her permission, had drifted to her own sternum, pressing against that hollow place she'd spent three years pretending wasn't there.

"How much?" she asked, and hated how quickly it came out.

"Oh, not coin." The broker sounded almost fond. "Coin is for people with futures. No—for you, a trifle. You travel with the swordspeaker, don't you?"

The market snapped back into focus all at once—the noise, the smells, a child shrieking over a dropped skewer—and Wren understood, with cold clarity, that she had just confirmed something with her whole face.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard my offer."

"No." Louder this time. A woman selling onions glanced over, then deliberately away. That was border-market kindness: the gift of not watching. "Whatever you want with her, the answer's no. And also I don't know anyone called a swordspeaker, and also you should invest in a sign, because your stall is very hard to find."

The hood tilted again. Something that might have been amusement moved beneath the silk.

"They'll sell her anyway," the broker said softly. "Someone always sells. The only question is whether it's you holding the coin when it happens—or standing next to her when the bill comes due." They lifted the dish of dark water, and for one vertiginous second Wren saw her own reflection in it, except the reflection was smiling and she was not. "Think about it, empty girl. I'll be at this market until the moon turns. Water remembers where you're staying."

Wren walked away. She did not run, because running would have told the broker something true about her, and she had decided, somewhere in the last thirty seconds, that this creature was owed exactly nothing true.

Her hands shook the entire way to the well.

* * *

Felix found her there, sitting on the stone rim with a paper cone of candied nuts she had no memory of buying, which was either a bad omen or proof of muscle memory deeper than memory itself.

"You're pale," Felix said, dropping onto the rim beside her. "Paler than usual, and usually you're the color of someone who sleeps indoors, which I mean as a compliment."

"A man tried to buy me today," Wren said.

Felix blinked. "Like—"

"Not like courting. Like *buying*. A soul-broker at the end of the row. Knew what I was. Said the Undercourt sold me, three years ago, everything, all at once. Said he knew the shelf my memories are sitting on." Wren turned the nut cone over in her hands. "Said he could get them back if I helped him get to you."

The silence between them stretched. Around them the market churned on—bargaining, laughing, a fiddler murdering a folk song—and Wren watched Felix absorb it, watched her friend's face do the complicated arithmetic of guilt.

"This is because of me," Felix said finally.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you make everything your fault so you can carry it instead of letting anyone help. It's very noble and extremely annoying. I've known you four days and I'm already fluent in you." Wren bumped her shoulder against Felix's. "A shark smelling blood isn't the blood's fault."

"That's a terrible analogy."

"It's early. My analogies improve after noon."

Aldric arrived carrying rope over one shoulder and a sack that smelled aggressively of dried plums, and took one look at their faces. "What happened?"

"She's being dramatic," Felix started.

"A soul-broker recognized the map," Wren said, because drama was for people with time to spare. "Offered to buy me back from the court. Wants Felix as payment."

Aldric set the rope down very carefully, the way he did everything, as if the world might break and blame him. "Did he say what the memories cost originally? Who paid?"

"He didn't get to the interesting part," Wren admitted. "I left."

"Good," Aldric said.

"Bad," Wren corrected. "Because now I keep thinking about it. He knew the *shelf*, Aldric. Someone specific owns my yesterdays. Not the court's general pile—not some ledger line in a drawer. He said *sold to someone specific* like it was gossip he'd been dying to share." She pulled a nut from the cone and didn't eat it. "Who buys an entire person's past? What did I do? Or—" and here came the fear, the one that lived under her ribs like a swallowed stone, "—what was done *to* me? What if I remember, someday, and it's awful? What if I look at you two and the first thing I remember is why I shouldn't trust myself?"

Felix was quiet a moment. Then, in the matter-of-fact tone she used for truths too large for drama:

"Then we'll hold onto you while you read it."

"That's not how memories work."

"Neither are talking rivers, and I made one hold still on Tuesday. We'll improvise."

It should not have helped. It helped enormously. Wren ate the nut.

* * *

They rented a room above a tavern called the Drowned Goose, mostly because it was cheap and partly because Wren appreciated a business honest enough to advertise its own failures. Aldric took the floor by the door out of what he claimed was habit and what Wren suspected was penance wearing armor. Felix sat cross-legged on the bed with the map unrolled across her knees, studying it by candlelight, and Wren sat beside her ostensibly helping and actually watching her friend worry the corner of the parchment like a loose tooth.

The cipher crawled across the map in looping script that resolved, for Wren alone, into readable lines. She'd never examined why. Some skills arrive pre-installed; hers simply came with no instructions.

"Here," Wren said, tapping a cluster of symbols near the mountain's root. "Toll gate. Third checkpoint before the deep court. It says—" she squinted— "'each traveler names their loss.' Poetic. Ominous. Very on-brand for underground soul capitalism."

"And after that?"

"After that it stops being words and starts being math. Prices. Exchange rates." Wren traced a column of figures. "Felix... these are souls. Listed like grain tariffs. 'Two years of vigor, per head.' 'One childhood, mint condition.'" Her finger stopped. "There's a margin note here. Different handwriting. It says: *'Yarrow stock reserved — private buyer. Do not auction.'*"

The candle flame leaned in a draft that hadn't existed a moment before.

"Reserved," Felix repeated. Her voice had gone very flat, the way it did when feeling got too big for its container. "My mother's soul has a *buyer*. Not the court—an individual. Someone walked into that place and put a claim on her specifically, twenty years ago, and the court's been keeping her on ice ever since like—" 

"Like me," Wren said quietly. "Except nobody reserved me. Nobody thought I was worth holding onto." She meant it as a joke. It came out with the stone showing through.

Felix looked at her. Really looked, the way she did—like Wren was legible, like the blanks were just another language waiting translation.

"Then whoever bought mine paid for quality," Wren said, recovering, grinning, deflecting, doing all the old tricks. "Full extraction, top shelf, mint condition. I'm apparently a luxury item, Felix. Somewhere out there is a person who owns my entire childhood and presumably displays it very tastefully."

"Fear of your own past doesn't have to mean your past is evil," Felix said. "Sometimes it just means it mattered."

"Those feel suspiciously similar from in here."

The door opened and Aldric came in shaking rain off his cloak, and whatever else he was going to say died in his throat, because the candle had gone out.

Not blown. Gone out, all three of them watching the wick sit there, cold and unlit, in a room with no draft.

On the windowsill, where no one had placed it, sat a small dish of dark water.

In its surface, faint as breath on glass, a reflection smiled up at them. None of them were smiling.

Below it, written in the water itself in letters that dissolved even as they read:

*THE OFFER STANDS UNTIL THE MOON TURNS.*
