## 8. Aldric's Broken Vow

The fire had burned down to that stage where it was more memory than flame — a bed of orange coals breathing slow, the way an old man breathes when he has decided to stay exactly where he is. Aldric sat apart from it by perhaps four feet, which was as close as his armor ever let him come to comfort. He had not removed the breastplate in three days. It was not that he feared ambush. It was that the steel remembered its shape even when he did not.

Felix sat cross-legged nearest the flames, turning a whetstone over a knife she did not need to sharpen. She sharpened things the way other people prayed. Wren lay on her back with her boots off and her hands laced behind her head, cataloguing the stars aloud in a voice that suggested she owned them all and was considering selling.

"That one's a ladle," she said. "That one's a very tired ox. That one is definitely stolen; it doesn't match the others."

"The stars aren't stolen," Felix said.

"Everything worth having is stolen," said Wren. "I'd know if I could remember anything."

It should have been a joke. Mostly it was. But there was a hairline crack under it, the kind you only heard if you had spent years listening for cracks in oaths — and Aldric had spent nothing else. He looked at the girl lying in the dirt with her stolen stars, and at the smith's daughter with her stone and her silence, and he felt the words rising in him like floodwater against a door he had been holding shut for eleven years.

He had promised himself he would tell them before the mountain. There was no sense descending into a court of sold souls while carrying a secret that heavy. Weight like that got men killed on rope bridges.

"Felix," he said.

"Mm." She did not look up from the knife.

"When we were crossing the ford today. You spoke to the water."

"I told the water to be polite." The whetstone kept its rhythm. *Shhk. Shhk.*

"It listened."

The stone stopped. Wren sat up. In the coal-light, Felix's face was carefully arranged into nothing, which was itself an admission — honest people were allowed to look startled.

"It was a small thing," Felix said. "A courtesy."

"A courtesy that made a river hold its temper." Aldric leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the armor creaked its old complaint. "I have watched oathbound knights command storms and kings command armies, and neither of them made a river *behave*. What you did today was not a courtesy. It was the smallest possible exercise of something enormous, and I think you know that, and I think that is why your hands haven't been steady since."

"They're steady."

"They're steady because you're making them be. That's different, and I know the difference intimately."

Wren, who had the unerring instinct of thieves to smell a confession coming and get comfortable first, pulled her knees up and hugged them. "Oh good," she said. "Story hour. Is there a tragic backstory? Please say there's a tragic backstory. I've got a tragic backstory-shaped hole in my head and I'd love to live vicariously."

"You may regret asking," Aldric said.

"Impossible. I have nothing to compare it to."

* * *

He took off one gauntlet. Then, after a moment that felt like unbuckling a rib, the other. He set them side by side in the firelight, palms up, the way knights laid their hands bare when swearing witness. Old habit. His hands were broad and scarred across both palms in matching lines, the mark of a sword hilt held so long and so hard it had taught the flesh its shape.

"I was knighted at nineteen," he began. "Sworn at twenty-one. Do you know what an oath of protection actually is? Not the wedding version, not the ballad version. The real thing. A knight sworn to a person's protection does not merely promise to try. He binds his own life-thread to theirs. If they die, part of him dies with them. If they die while he lives, what remains—" he turned his palms over, showing the backs now, though there was nothing to show, "—is what I am."

"Whom did you swear to?" Felix asked quietly.

"Prince Aurelian of Cael Harrow. Third son. Nobody important, which is precisely why the post existed — nobody important enough to assassinate, or so the court reasoned, so they gave the duty to a knight nobody important enough to miss." He smiled, and it hurt his face to do it, so he suspected it came out wrong. "I was twenty-four. He was nineteen. He was the sort of young man who read books about agriculture and asked farmers their opinions, which in a royal family is practically a disease. He used to sneak out of the palace dressed as a clerk to go argue about irrigation. My job was to follow him and pretend I hadn't."

"He sounds lovely," said Wren.

"He was insufferable," Aldric said, with such sudden warmth that both women blinked. "He argued about everything. He argued with me about whether knights ought to be allowed opinions. He lost that argument, incidentally. I have many." The smile faded by degrees, like a sun going behind cloud. "There was a rebellion. There is always a rebellion; the ledger of kingdoms balances itself in blood eventually. The rebels wanted his father dead, but his father traveled with two hundred swords, and Aurelian traveled with me. They caught up to us at a place called the Weeping Stair, where the road narrows along a cliff with a river far below."

The fire popped. None of them moved.

"There were six of them. Good fighters, desperate men — I can say that honestly now, though I couldn't then. I killed four." He held up his scarred hand, flexed it, watched it obey. "The fifth I wounded badly enough that he crawled away. And the sixth went for the prince, and I was ten feet away, and here is the thing about oaths that no one tells you." He looked up, and met Felix's eyes, because this part was for her, only for her. "An oath of protection binds you to succeed. It does not make you faster. It does not make the blade miss. It simply holds your soul open like a wound and says: whatever happens next, you will feel all of it."

Felix had gone very still. Even the whetstone had stopped pretending.

"He died?" Wren asked, softly, without any of her usual mischief.

"He died with my sword a hand's width from the man killing him. A hand's width and half a heartbeat. I have measured that distance every night for eleven years. Some nights it's a hand's width. Some nights it's a mile." He drew a breath that shook on the way in and steadied on the way out, the way a soldier learns to do, the way that would have horrified the boy-prince who once told him feelings were just arguments you hadn't finished yet. "The oath didn't release me. Oaths don't. When the protected one dies, the vow turns inward. Every dawn it wakes and asks: did you protect him? No. Then it flays me. Not a figure of speech, Felix. Not remorse. I mean flays. There are nights I have stood in front of a mirror and watched the marks rise across my chest, thin white lines, like a whip made of language."

"And the healers—"

"Healers see scars already healed. Whatever the oath does happens somewhere they can't put salve." He picked up one gauntlet, weighed it, set it down again. "I have spoken to priests. They tell me to forgive myself. I spoke to war-priests. They told me to die well in battle and let the gods sort the accounting. I spoke to a hedgewitch in the Marches who charged me a chicken and told me the truth: there is one kind of person alive who can unmake a vow, because a vow is a sentence spoken, and only certain mouths can unspeak sentences."

"Swordspeakers," Felix said. The word came out flat, guarded, a door pulled shut even as she named it.

"The last one died — everyone believes — twenty years ago. And then a courier bled out on your floor carrying a map with a sigil I knew from a prince's letters, and I followed a smith's daughter out of a valley where magic goes to die, and I watched her tell a river to mind its manners." He spread his empty hands. "I'm not asking tonight. I want that understood. I am not asking. I followed you to the Undercourt for your reasons, not mine. But you deserve to know what walks beside you, and why I flinch at dawn, and why I never remove the breastplate."

"Why don't you?" Wren asked. It was a gentle question, for her. "The armor."

"Because the marks are worse where no one can see them," Aldric said, "and the steel gives me someone to blame for the weight." He almost smiled. "That, and a knight without armor is just a large sad man."

* * *

Silence settled, but it was a different species of silence than the ones that usually followed him around. The coals breathed. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which Aldric found relatable.

It was Felix who moved first. She set down the knife and the stone and came around the fire on her knees, awkward, deliberate, the way people move when they are afraid of being misunderstood and have decided to risk it anyway.

"Show me," she said.

"No."

"Not the marks. I believe you about the marks." She held out her hand, palm up, mirroring the gesture he'd made earlier, and there was something in her face he recognized from the forge — the concentration of someone about to work metal, respect for the material, no illusions about who bends whom. "Your oath. Let me *listen* to it. I've never — everything I've ever spoken was small. A river. A stubborn hinge. I don't know what a real one looks like from the inside. And you're right, I should know what walks beside me too."

"You can't fix it," he said. "Not tonight, not with a look. Unbinding a crown-oath is—"

"I'm not going to fix it. I'm going to listen." Her chin came up, and for a moment he saw not the girl hiding in a valley but the woman the valley had been starving. "You spent eleven years holding a door shut. The least I can do is stand on the other side of it for a minute."

So he reached — slowly, telegraphing every inch the way you approach a horse that has been whipped — and took her hand.

The oath rose to meet her touch.

He felt her feel it. He felt her stiffen, heard her breath catch, saw her eyes lose focus as the vow poured itself through the contact: *while breath remains, I guard; while I guard, I endure; while I endure, he shall not fall.* Eleven years old and still burning, still awake, still circling the empty place where a laughing boy who argued about irrigation should have been. He watched her lips move silently, reading it the way Wren read the map's cipher, and then he watched her face change — not horror, which he had braced for, but grief, raw and specific.

"Oh," she whispered. "Aldric. It's still *loyal* to him."

"Yes."

"It's not punishing you. It's — it's standing watch over a dead man. It doesn't know he's dead. Or it knows and won't accept it." Her grip tightened. "It's waiting for orders that will never come."

"That's one interpretation."

"It's the true one." She let go of his hand and sat back on her heels, and pressed her knuckles briefly against her mouth, thinking, thinking — he could nearly hear the forge-fire of her. When she lowered her hand, she said: "I can't unbind it. You're right about that. It's woven into you deeper than anything I've touched. If I cut it carelessly I might take — I don't know. Your courage. Your ability to keep promises. Some vows are load-bearing."

"Charming image."

"But I can do something else." She hesitated, and he realized with a jolt that she was nervous — this girl who had faced down a matriarch and a map that refused to burn. "Where I grew up, when a tool broke, you didn't throw it out. You re-tempered it. Same metal, new purpose." She looked into the coals, choosing words with tongs. "I can't release you from guarding him. But maybe — maybe someday — I can help you finish it. Speak the end of the sentence. Tell the oath the watch is over. Give it a burial instead of leaving it pacing a grave."

For a long moment Aldric Vane, knight-banneret, flayer-proof, could not speak at all.

"That," he managed finally, "is the kindest thing anyone has offered me since—" He stopped. Recalculated. "No. That is the kindest thing anyone has ever offered me. Full stop. Eleven years, and the best anyone managed was absolution, which is just forgiveness delivered by someone with no skin in the game."

"Well," said Wren, from her nest of blankets, "don't get sentimental. You're no use to us weeping into your pauldrons. Also those are my blankets and you're leaking on them."

"I am not weeping."

"Your voice is doing a thing."

"My voice is *fine*."

"Your voice is doing the same thing it did when you described the prince arguing about irrigation," Felix said gently. "I noticed it too."

Aldric looked from one to the other — the thief who had no yesterday grinning at him across the ashes, the smith's daughter watching him with a forge-light patience he had done nothing to earn — and understood, with the particular vertigo of a man who has stood alone so long that level ground feels like falling, that somewhere between the tree line and this fire he had acquired people.

He cleared his throat. It fooled no one. "We should sleep. The pass tomorrow is scree and switchbacks, and the map shows the gate by nightfall after."

"Gate," Felix repeated, and the word dropped the temperature of the whole clearing.

They had not spoken much of the gate. It sat at the end of the map's ink like a period at the end of a sentence, marked only with a symbol Wren had translated with visible reluctance: *the mouth that remembers.* Wren had gone quiet for an hour afterward and eaten an entire wheel of cheese in apparent consolation.

"The gate will ask something of us," Aldric said, because someone had to say it aloud and he was, historically, the one who said the terrible things first. It was a service he performed. "Courts like the Undercourt never open for free. Before we reach it, each of us should decide — truly decide — what we are willing to name as payment. Because if you stand at the threshold undecided, the gate will decide for you. And gates like that have exquisite taste in pain."

"Cheerful," said Wren.

"I'm a knight. Cheerful is above my pay grade."

Felix fed one last branch to the coals and watched it catch, and the light moved across her face, and Aldric thought — with the oath still humming its vigil inside him, and the memory of her hand still warm on his — that whatever waited at that cave-mouth had no idea what it had invited in. A thief full of borrowed stars. A broken knight full of unfinished sentences. And a swordspeaker learning, one small vow at a time, the size of her own voice.

He slept that night without the dream. For the first time in eleven years, he slept without the dream — and woke before dawn anyway, sitting bolt upright with his heart hammering, because the silence where the flaying should have been was somehow louder.

Across the dead fire, Felix was already awake. Watching him. Her expression said she had felt it too — the oath, stirring, restless, like a dog that had heard its master's footsteps at last.

"We're close," she said quietly.

The mountain, black against the graying sky, agreed.
