## 9. The Mountain's Mouth

The mountain had been watching us for two days before we saw it.

I know how that sounds. But after a week on the road with Aldric and Wren, I'd stopped apologizing to myself for the things I noticed. The suppressed oaths that whispered in my blood since we crossed the tree line had grown from murmurs to something like conversation—low, insistent, the way water talks against the hull of a boat—and they all leaned in the same direction. Toward the peak ahead of us. Toward the dark.

"It's called Grayhollow," Aldric said, when we finally stood beneath it. He said it the way other men named battlefields.

"Cheerful name," Wren said. She was sitting on a rock with her boots off, rubbing her toes, because apparently even approaching the gates of hell required proper foot maintenance. "Very welcoming. Very 'come in, mind the drips.'"

"The drips are real," Aldric said. "Everything under there drips."

"Wonderful."

We had come up through pine forest gone sparse and strange, trees twisted as if combing their hair away from something. No birdsong. That was the first thing. Then no wind, though the branches above still moved. Then, at the tree line—which was lower here than any tree line had a right to be—the oaths in my blood went quiet all at once, like children shushed by a look from their mother.

I didn't like it. Silence, in my experience, was never neutral anymore.

"There." Aldric pointed with his chin, hands staying near his sword out of habit. "There's your mouth."

* * *

It wasn't a cave so much as a wound. The mountainside split open in a jagged seam wide enough to drive a cart through, its edges smooth in a way stone should not be—melted, almost, or licked, as if something enormous had once tasted the rock and found it wanting. The dark inside didn't fade with distance the way darkness ought to. It just went on being perfectly, completely black, like the mountain had swallowed a piece of night and kept it.

Frost crept outward from the entrance in long fingers across bare stone. In summer.

"Well," Wren said, hopping down from her rock and pulling her boots back on. "Lovely venue. Terrible lighting."

"You don't have to come," I said. It came out more gently than I meant it to. Or maybe exactly as gently as I meant it to, because Wren had been quiet all morning in a way that wasn't her usual quiet. Her usual quiet was the quiet of a person counting exits. This was the quiet of someone walking toward an address she half-remembered.

Wren snorted. "Felix Yarrow, if you think you're going down there to rob an entire civilization of soul-brokers without me, you're insulting us both." She slung her pack over one shoulder. "Also you can't read the cipher. Also I want my past back. Also—" she paused, and some of the flippancy drained out of her voice, leaving something smaller behind, "—also whoever has my memories is down there, and I've decided I'm done letting them keep them."

Aldric said nothing, which was how he agreed to things. He simply began checking his gear with the methodical care of a man who expected to die soon and wanted to be tidy about it.

We made camp fifty yards out, because Aldric refused to approach the mouth after sundown. "Gates talk more freely at night," he said. "You don't want to be within earshot."

"Gates *talk?*" I asked.

He looked at me with an expression I was beginning to catalog as *you'll see.*

* * *

They talked at dusk.

We were eating—Wren had produced a suspicious quantity of dried plums from somewhere in her pack, and when questioned, said only "found them"—when the wind changed. Except there was no wind. But something moved through the hollow anyway, a breath drawn over the rocks, and the mouth of the mountain exhaled.

"Felixssss Yarrow."

My name came out of the dark in my mother's voice.

I dropped my plum. My whole body knew that voice before my mind caught up—knew it the way a scar knows weather. Low and warm and slightly rough at the edges, the voice that had sung me awake for six years before it went silent forever. It came sliding out of the black seam of the gate and wrapped around me like a hand on the back of my neck.

"Mama?" The word left me before I could stop it, small and stupid and six years old.

"It does that," Aldric said quietly. He hadn't reached for his sword. There was nothing to swing at. "It speaks you. Whatever names you carry, it finds them and wears them. Don't answer it."

But it was already moving on. "*Aldric Vane,*" it breathed, and now the voice was older, male, clipped with authority—a voice I'd never heard but that made Aldric flinch as though struck. "*Oathbreaker.*" Then, savoring: "*Perjurer-to-be.*"

Aldric's jaw tightened. "Charming," he said, to the mountain, in the tone of a man refusing to rise to a bait he very much wanted to bite.

"*Wren,*" said the dark, and here it faltered—actually faltered, the way a reader stumbles over a word printed too faintly. "*Wren. Wren. W—*" The name repeated itself, thinner each time, like a bell rung until the bronze wore through. "*Empty vessel. Sold girl. She who was once—*"

"Once what?" Wren demanded, on her feet, plum forgotten, eyes bright and furious in the firelight. "*Once WHAT?*"

The gate said nothing more about her. It moved on to reciting our heights and weights and the contents of our packs, which somehow felt worse, and then it fell silent, satisfied, a merchant who had finished taking inventory.

Nobody slept well. We sat up around the fire, three people arranged back to back, listening to the mountain breathe.

* * *

In the gray light of morning, Aldric laid out the rules like a man dealing cards he already knew were bad.

"The gate takes an oath of entry," he said. "From each of you. Spoken aloud, witnessed by the court below. You must name what you will surrender to pass."

"Surrender," I repeated. "Like a toll."

"Exactly like a toll. Except the toll-taker decides whether your payment is honest, and if it isn't, you don't get turned away." His eyes flicked toward the black seam. "You get taken. The gate collects what it's owed however it likes. Usually from whatever you value most."

"Great system," Wren said. "No notes."

"What did you pay?" I asked him. "When you came through before?"

Something crossed his face—quick, old, buried fast. "That was twenty years ago, and I paid in a coin I haven't missed yet." Which told me everything and nothing. He stood, brushing off his knees. "Think carefully before you speak. A false payment is perjury, and you've seen what perjury does." He looked at me when he said it, meaning: *you'll see what it does to you, specifically, soon enough, if the Regent's bargain goes the way bargains go.* I looked away first.

"And if we refuse?" Wren asked.

"Then we turn around and Felix's mother stays in a jar forever, the Regent unravels Thornvale whenever she pleases, and we've walked three hundred miles to admire the scenery." Aldric shrugged. "So. We don't refuse."

* * *

We approached the mouth at midday, because Aldric insisted daylight made the gate lazier, and I chose to believe him because the alternative was believing the gate was never lazy.

Up close, the dark had texture. Not depth—texture. It hung in the entrance like a curtain of woven shadow, and standing before it felt like standing before a door with someone on the other side, waiting politely to hear why you'd knocked. The cold rolled off it in waves. Frost crackled under my boots. And beneath everything, faint as a pulse, I felt my own oaths stirring again—not whispering now but *leaning*, straining toward the dark like dogs toward a familiar scent.

The gate spoke first.

"*Three come down,*" it said, and the voices braided together—my mother's rough warmth, Aldric's dead prince's clipped command, and a third, thin and worn, that made Wren go rigid beside me. "*Three come down. Name your price. Speak true, or be spoken.*"

Aldric stepped forward first. Of course he did. He drew himself up the way he probably had in front of kings, shoulders square, and I realized I'd never actually watched him swear anything before. For a man whose life had been ruined by oaths, he stood before one with remarkable steadiness.

"I am Sir Aldric Vane," he said. "Once of the King's Shield. I enter the Undercourt of my own will, for my own purpose. What I surrender—" and here he paused, and I saw the pause cost him, saw him weigh things on a scale only he could read. When he spoke again his voice had gone quieter. Rougher. "What I surrender is my hope of forgiveness. Let it stay at the threshold. I won't need it where I'm going."

The dark considered him. Frost crept a little further out, touched his boot, retreated.

"*Paid,*" said the gate, in all three voices at once. "*Pass, oathbreaker. The court remembers you.*"

"That's what worries me," Aldric muttered, and stepped through the shadow-curtain and was gone—no scream, no flash, just swallowed, the way a stone goes into deep water.

Wren went next. She bounced on her heels twice, which was her version of a war drum, and planted her fists on her hips facing the dark like it owed her money. Which, I supposed, it did.

"Right, you great gloomy doorway," she announced. "I'm Wren. That's the whole name, don't wear it out. What I surrender—" She stopped. Her bounce died. For a moment she looked young, and lost, and I wanted to grab her hand and tell her to walk away, that no memory was worth this, that we'd find another way.

Then she lifted her chin.

"What I surrender is my fear of what I'll find," she said. "Whatever I was before—I leave the dread of it right here. I'll take what's mine. But I'm done being afraid of myself before I've met her."

The gate hummed, low, almost thoughtful. "*Paid,*" it said at last. "*Pass, empty girl. The court kept your change.*"

And then there was me.

I stepped forward alone, frost biting through my boots, and the dark leaned in—I swear it physically leaned, the shadow-curtain bowing toward me like a listener cupping an ear. Because it knew. Whatever the court was, whatever the Regent was planning, the gate knew what I was. The last swordspeaker. Collateral on a twenty-year debt. The thing this whole mountain had been waiting for.

"*Yarrow,*" it whispered, in my mother's voice, gentle as bedtime. "*Speak.*"

I thought about what I could afford to lose.

My mother's face, worn by a stranger? Already promised to the Regent, or so she'd claimed. My name? It was already fading at the edges—I'd seen it happen since the false oath, strangers' eyes sliding off me, innkeepers calling me "the tall one" without knowing why. What was left that was mine?

Aldric's silence at my back. Wren's laugh over breakfast. The smell of the forge in Thornvale. The valley itself, magic-dead and stubborn and the only home I'd ever known.

I opened my mouth to name something small. A trinket. A memory of a rainy day. Everyone lies a little at tolls.

But the gate was looking at me with my mother's voice, and I remembered Odile's face when she admitted what they'd sold, and I understood suddenly that this gate would know a lie, and that perjury had a timer on it already, and that if I walked into the Undercourt already marked as a liar I would never leave it.

So I told the truth.

"What I surrender," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it, "is my old life. All of it. The hiding. The smallness. The girl who sharpened other people's blades and swallowed every vow she ever wanted to speak." The words were coming faster now, and they burned coming up, the way splinters burn leaving skin. "I surrender the daughter Marin Yarrow lost. I surrender the last swordspeaker of the old line. Whatever I was before today—let it stay on this threshold. Because whoever walks out of this mountain, if anyone walks out, won't be her."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Then the frost bloomed. It spread out from the gate in a rush, silver ferns racing across the stone, up over my boots, around my ankles—cold, but not cruel, cold like recognition—and all three voices spoke together, layered, harmonized, and for one terrible heartbeat the topmost thread was unmistakably my mother, saying:

"*Paid in full. Pass, Yarrow. Your mother's voice is kept for you below.*"

And the dark parted like a held breath released.

I looked back once. Aldric stood in the shallows of the shadow, watching me with something I'd never seen on his face—not pity, not respect, but the particular grief of a man watching someone else make the same mistake he made, younger and better. Wren's hand found mine and squeezed hard enough to hurt, which was her way of saying everything she couldn't.

"Together?" she said.

"Together," I said.

We stepped through, and the mountain closed its mouth behind us, and somewhere far below, in lantern-lit streets where souls were stacked like coin, something ancient stirred in its seat and smiled behind a stolen face.

The last thing I heard, echoing down the throat of the world, was my mother's voice—warm, and rough at the edges, and singing.

I knew the song.

So did Wren.
