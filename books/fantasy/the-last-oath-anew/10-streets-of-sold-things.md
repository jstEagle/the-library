## 10. Streets of Sold Things

Wren had stolen things her whole remembered life—three years of it, anyway—and she'd thought she understood the shape of theft. Theft was quick. Theft was a lifted purse, a palmed ring, a door left unlatched by someone careless. You took, you ran, you laughed about it later over cheap wine.

She had never imagined theft could be an architecture. A whole city built out of it.

The Undercourt opened beneath them like the throat of something vast and patient, and Wren stood on a ledge of black stone looking down into streets that had no sky. Lanterns hung everywhere—thousands of them, strung between crooked towers and sagging across alleys like fruit on impossible trees—but they didn't burn with fire. Each one glowed a different color, and each one pulsed, slow and rhythmic, and after a moment Wren realized with a crawling feeling at the base of her skull that every lantern was beating in time with something. A memory. A year. Someone's name.

"They're not lamps," she said quietly.

"No," Aldric said beside her. His hand rested on his sword hilt the way it always did now, as if the blade might remember its old loyalty and come back to him. "They're what's for sale."

Felix stood on Wren's other side, pale in the lantern-light, the map folded away in her coat. She hadn't spoken since the gate had said their names—since it had said *Maren Yarrow* in a dead woman's voice, and Felix's face had gone the color of the valley's winter ash. Wren wanted to say something to her. She'd never been good at that part. Comfort was like lockpicking; she knew it was possible, she'd watched other people do it, but her own fingers always fumbled the tools.

So instead she said, "Right. Ground rules."

Aldric looked down at her. "Ground rules?"

"We're thieves entering a market. There are rules." She held up fingers. "One: nobody buys anything without showing the rest of us first. Two: nobody sells anything either, especially not pieces of themselves, because I guarantee you everything in this city has a price tag and half the prices are *you*. Three—" she pointed at Aldric, "—you don't draw that sword unless something is actively eating someone. Four: we find the archive, we get what we came for, we leave before anyone learns our faces."

"Too late for four," Felix murmured. "The gate knows our names."

"The gate knows your names." Wren jerked her chin at the glowing city below. "I intend to be extremely forgettable. It's my one natural talent."

It wasn't, quite. But it was close, and saying it made her chest hurt less than the truth, which was that standing above a city that traded in memories felt like standing above an open grave and hearing scratching.

* * *

The main street was called the Lanternway—or rather, everyone called it that, and there were no signs anywhere, which Wren found both practical and sinister, because a city without signs is a city that assumes you already know where you are. The crowd moved around them in a slow river of shapes: hooded figures, masked figures, figures with no discernible edges at all, just a suggestion of personhood wrapped around a shopping list. Nobody ran. That was the worst part. In Thornvale's market, if you dropped a coin, three people dove for it. Here, if you dropped something, it stayed where it fell, because everyone understood that anything loose might be worth more than they could afford.

The stalls began almost immediately.

Wren had expected—she didn't know what. Cages? Jars? Something obviously horrible, something you could point at and hate cleanly. Instead the first stall sold years. Not time in the abstract—a merchant sat behind a low table displaying small glass phials on velvet, and inside each phial a scene turned slowly like snow in a globe. A child running through wheat. A wedding feast, tiny figures raising cups forever. An old man on a porch, watching rain.

"Fresh stock," the merchant said to a passerby, in the tone of a man selling apples. "Good vintage. Nineteen years of a miller's life, well-kept, minimal grief. The grief's been decanted separately, if you want it—it deepens the flavor."

Wren kept walking. Her hands were fists in her pockets.

The second stall sold names. There was a ledger open on the counter, and a woman behind it was writing with a pen that scratched like a beetle in a wall, and whenever she finished a name, somewhere in the crowd a person stopped mid-stride, looked briefly confused, and then kept walking with a slightly emptier face. Beside the ledger, on little wooden stands, hung nameplates waiting for owners. Wren read one as she passed: *TOMAS*, it said, in cheerful letters, *GENTLE WITH HORSES, AFRAID OF THE DARK.*

"That one's popular," the name-seller said cheerfully to a customer. "Very balanced. Some fear gives a life texture."

"Don't look," Aldric rumbled, putting a heavy hand on Wren's shoulder and steering her past. But she had already looked. Of course she had. Looking was what she did.

"It's not the selling that gets me," she said, once they were clear. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "People sell things. People sell terrible things, I've fenced enough of them. It's that everything here is *labeled so nicely.*"

"Fear gives a life texture," Felix repeated flatly. "Gods below."

"Probably literally," Wren said, and was rewarded with the ghost of a smile. Small victory. She'd take it.

* * *

They found the archive district by following the lanterns that didn't pulse—the cold ones, white and steady, hanging over buildings that all had the same severe architecture of a place where nothing was meant to be enjoyed. They marked it, agreed on a plan for tomorrow—because even Felix, wound tight as a bowstring, admitted that walking into the court's records exhausted and unprepared was a way to die politely—and found a lodging house that rented rooms by the night and asked no questions, which in this city meant it probably collected answers instead.

Their room had three cots and a window that looked out onto a wall of living rock veined with lantern-glow, green and gold light sliding slowly down the stone like honey poured over the world.

Wren couldn't sleep.

This surprised her not at all. She lay on her cot listening to Felix breathe in the steady rhythm of genuine exhaustion and Aldric breathe in the careful rhythm of a man who slept the way he did everything else, out of duty, and she stared at the ceiling and felt the city humming through the floorboards. All those lanterns. All those lives, lit and labeled and priced.

And underneath the humming, quieter, a thought she'd been carrying since the border market, since the soul-broker had looked at her with those hungry, knowing eyes and said *the empty girl*:

What if my past is worse than the blank?

Everyone assumed the Undercourt had taken her memories as payment. Fine. But people paid with things when they had no coin, and people who had coin usually paid with something small, and her blanks went back *all the way*. Every year. Every name. If she'd sold her childhood, she must have needed something enormous. Debts. Crimes. Or—

Or someone had taken them. Sold them out from under her, the way the name-seller sold TOMAS off his little wooden stand. And whoever holds your memories knows exactly who you are. Knew her better than she knew herself. Was possibly, right now, somewhere in this lantern-lit city, wearing her.

She got up. Quietly, the way she did everything, and slipped out into the streets, because Wren had learned long ago that the only cure for a thought that size was motion.

* * *

The market changed after midnight. Or she assumed it was midnight; there was no way to tell, underground, and time here seemed less like a river and more like a hallway you walked up and down. The crowds thinned. The merchants grew stranger. And the stalls grew *personal*.

That was how she thought of it later. The big stalls sold categories—years, names, generic joys in bulk. But the small stalls, tucked into alcoves and stairwells, sold specifics. One sold last words. One sold the smell of a particular kitchen. One, run by a figure whose face was entirely wrapped in gray cloth, sold lullabies, and the sound drifting from its little brass horns made Wren stop dead in the street, because one of them was a song she didn't know and her body knew anyway—her feet had gone still, her breath had gone shallow, some animal part of her had recognized it before her mind caught up.

Her heart hammered. She approached the stall slowly.

It wasn't the same song coming from the gray-wrapped seller. That one dealt in melodies by the dozen, tinny and secondhand. But next to it, wedged into an alcove barely wide enough for a blanket and a folding table, was a smaller operation entirely—an old woman with clouded eyes and a tray of memory-shards laid out on black velvet like a jeweler's nightmare. Each shard was a sliver of glass no bigger than a fingernail, and each one held a faint image, turning, turning.

"Looking for something specific, dear?" the old woman asked. Her voice was kind. That was somehow worse.

"Just browsing."

"Mm. Browsers have a look about them." The old woman's milky eyes drifted over Wren's face, and Wren had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the woman was seeing straight through her, past her, into the hollow space where a person should have been. "You've got an absence about you. A vacancy. Good bones, though. Someone built you well."

"I'm not for sale."

"No?" The old woman smiled with too few teeth. "Everything here was somebody once. Have a look. You might know the merchandise."

And Wren should have walked away. She was a professional. Rule one, rule two, the rules she'd invented herself not six hours ago. But her feet didn't move, and her hand reached out over the velvet tray as if it belonged to someone braver or stupider, and she looked.

Shards of ordinary lives. A boy falling out of a boat, laughing. A woman braiding another woman's hair by candlelight. A harvest dance, arms swinging, dust golden in torchlight.

And then—third row, near the edge—a shard that showed a kitchen. Just a kitchen, stone-floored, copper pots, a window full of rain. And in the kitchen a woman with dark hair and a smudge of flour on her cheek was singing while she kneaded bread, and the shard didn't hold sound but Wren could *see* the song in the set of the woman's shoulders, the way her head tipped, and—

The bottom dropped out of Wren's stomach.

Not because she recognized the woman. Because her body recognized *everything else*. The height of the window. The chipped blue rim of the bowl beside the flour sack. The way the rain hit the glass in fat, slow drops, and the smell—gods, the shard carried *smell*, yeast and woodsmoke and wet stone—and her throat closed and her eyes burned and she stood in a lantern-lit alley in a city of sold souls with tears running silently down her face over a piece of glass, because it was hers. It was *hers*. A Tuesday morning, some unimportant, perfect morning, kneading bread with a woman whose name she would never know, and someone had pried it out of her and put a price on it.

"That one's old stock," the old woman said softly. "Been in the tray a long while. Most folk want the dramatic pieces. Deaths, betrayals, first loves. Nobody wants bread." She tilted her head. "You're crying over bread, dear."

"How much."

"Now, don't be hasty—"

"*How much.*"

The old woman studied her, and whatever she saw made her sigh, and the kindness in her voice turned tired and real. "For that one? A memory of yours. Something recent. Nothing precious—I won't take precious from a girl who's already been picked clean." She tapped the tray. "Trade fair. That's my rule, even here. Especially here."

Wren dug through three years of self. What did she have? The inn at the border, the taste of stew, learning dice games from a carter named Hobb, the first time she'd picked a lock and felt the tumblers give like a small miracle. Felix laughing—no. Not that. Not any of them. She wasn't trading the family she'd just barely found.

In the end she gave the old woman a market day in a town whose name she'd never known anyway—banners, roasted chestnuts, a pickpocket she'd watched get caught and decided not to become. It cost less than she feared. The extraction was a cold tick behind her ear, a brief gray flicker, done.

The old woman wrapped the shard in a scrap of velvet and put it in Wren's palm and closed her fingers over it. "Whoever she was," the old woman said, nodding toward the glass, "she sang the same song every morning. I've had that shard eleven years. Heard it through the glass a thousand times." Her cloudy eyes were almost gentle. "It's a good song. It sounds like someone who was loved."

Wren couldn't speak. She nodded, pressed a fist to her mouth, and walked away before she came apart entirely in front of the merchandise.

* * *

She sat in an alcove between two cold white lanterns for a long time, holding the velvet bundle, not opening it. Around her the city hummed its stolen light.

When she finally unwrapped it and pressed the shard to her temple, the way the broker at the border market had once demonstrated with a sample, the kitchen bloomed around her—not sight exactly, more than sight. Warmth. Flour under her nails. Rain on glass. And the song, finally, *finally*, the actual sound of it, rising out of her own chest in a voice that wasn't hers and was: a low, sweet melody with words in no language she knew, except that she knew every word, the way you know the shape of your own hands.

She was crying again. She let herself. Nobody in the Undercourt spared a tear a glance.

When it faded—shards only held a minute or so, the old woman hadn't lied about that—she sat in the dark and did the math she'd been avoiding since the border market. The shard was eleven years old. Eleven years ago, she'd been in a kitchen, kneading bread, loved, singing. Three years ago, she'd woken up empty. So the taking happened somewhere in between, and it hadn't been a trade she'd made. You didn't trade away mornings like that. You didn't sell the song.

Someone had taken it. And the old woman had said *old stock*, had said it had been in the tray a long while, which meant it had passed through hands, which meant somewhere upstream there was a seller, and sellers keep records, and records live in archives.

The cold white lanterns hung above her, steady as stars that had never seen a sky.

"Tomorrow," she whispered to the kitchen-woman, to the flour-smudged stranger who might have been her mother, her aunt, no one at all, everything at all. "Tomorrow I break into the biggest archive in the world, and I'm going to look up a thief."

She wrapped the shard carefully and tucked it against her heart, and headed back through the sleeping streets toward the room where Felix and Aldric were dreaming, unaware that their thief had just acquired the only thing she'd ever truly wanted—

and that wanting it was going to get them all killed.
