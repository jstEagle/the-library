## 11. The Ledger of Yarrow

The archive smelled the way old promises smell—dust and iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in a locked room.

Felix had imagined many things about the Undercourt's heart. She had imagined vaults, certainly, and guards, and perhaps a throne room where some vast and terrible creature counted souls like a miser counting coins. What she had not imagined was a library.

It sprawled through the cavern like a fossilized forest: shelves of black wood rising out of the stone floor at angles that defied any carpenter Felix had ever met, curving where walls should be straight, branching where ceilings should hold them up. Lanterns hung from nothing. The light they gave was amber and slow, and it pooled on the floor in shapes that looked almost deliberate—like the court wanted its records well lit, because what was the point of keeping accounts no one could read?

"Remind me," Wren whispered beside her, "why we're robbing a *library*."

"Because libraries are where people hide the truth," Felix said. "It's the first place anyone looks for lies. Nobody checks the truth."

"That is either very wise or very stupid."

"I've been told it's both, usually by the same person, usually Aldric."

Aldric, for his part, stood at the archive's mouth with his hand resting on his sword hilt—not drawing it, just touching it, the way a man touches a talisman against bad weather. His armor had been left behind two days ago, wrapped in oilcloth and buried under stones outside the mountain; down here, steel sang wrong notes, and he'd said so without explaining how he knew. Without the armor he looked less like a knight and more like a tired farmer who had wandered very far from his fields. The disguise did not entirely work. He still walked like a man expecting an executioner.

"There," he murmured. "The desk."

There was indeed a desk—or something desk-adjacent—a slab of green stone set into the floor near the center of the cavern, and behind it sat a figure that Felix's eyes kept sliding off of. Not invisible. Just uninteresting. Her gaze touched it and slid away the way rain slides off oiled canvas, and she understood, with a small cold shock, that this was what she must look like to most of Thornvale: present, forgettable, a smudge in the corner of the eye.

*They built their whole world out of the same trick they used to cage me,* she thought. *Of course they keep a librarian.*

"We don't want the librarian," Wren breathed. "We want the stacks. The cipher on your map matches the spines—look." She pointed with her chin, careful not to move her hands. Three years of thieving had taught her that thieves' hands were watched even when their faces weren't. "Every shelf is numbered in the same script. We need the section marked with—" she traced a shape in the air, a circle crossed twice, like a wheel with a broken axle— "*that.*"

"And that means?"

"Debts." Wren's mouth twisted. "I didn't know I could read it until I saw it written down. Isn't that a lovely feeling? Knowing things you can't remember learning."

Felix squeezed her shoulder. It was all she had to give, and Wren took it the way she took everything—lightly, quickly, as if acceptance itself might be stolen from her if she lingered.

They moved along the outer edge of the cavern, one shelf at a time, while the uninteresting figure behind the green desk turned pages that made no sound. The shelves nearest them held ledgers bound in leather gone soft as cloth, and when Felix brushed one with her fingertips, it *hummed*—a faint, fretful note, like a struck tuning fork remembering its pitch.

She snatched her hand back.

"They're not empty," she whispered.

"No," said Aldric quietly, from behind. "I don't think anything in this place is empty."

He was right. Now that she listened—really listened, the way she listened to metal, listening past the ring to the grain beneath—the whole archive breathed. Every ledger carried a murmur inside it, thousands upon thousands of tiny voices layered so fine they blended into something like wind through wheat. Names. Fragments. The sound of lives filed neatly by subject.

Wren had gone pale. Felix pretended not to notice, which was its own small kindness, and Wren pretended not to know she was pretending, which completed it.

The broken-axle mark appeared on a shelf deep in the western curve, where the lanterns burned a shade redder, and the murmuring here had a different quality—not wind-through-wheat but *grinding*, slow and patient, like millstones. Debts, Wren had said. Of course debts would grind.

The ledgers here were larger, bound in grey hide stamped with seals Felix didn't recognize and one or two she did—she stopped cold at the sigil of Thornvale's elders, the twin thorned branches she'd seen carved over the meeting hall her entire life, and felt it like a slap.

"Aldric," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Help me pull this one."

The ledger weighed as much as a child. That was her first thought, absurd and immediate: *it weighs like a child.* She set it on a reading stand shaped like a folded pair of hands—of course it was—and opened it.

The pages were not paper. They were thin sheets of glassy material, cool to the touch, and the writing on them shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it, rearranging itself the way a dream rearranges when you try to describe it aloud. But the headings were stable. Headings always were. Accountants were accountants in every world, apparently, and every accountant since the dawn of time had loved a good heading.

*HOLDINGS — ENCUMBERED*, read the first page. *Collateral Pledged Against Outstanding Obligations.*

Below it, entries. Hundreds. A soul named Corvane Ash, pledged against a bridge never built. A soul named Ilsette Mourn, pledged against a marriage that failed. On and on, each entry bearing three columns: the debtor, the debt, and the holder.

Felix ran her finger down the debtor column, looking for—

Her finger stopped.

She didn't find the name so much as the name found her. The letters seemed to lean toward her skin, warm as a cheek.

*Yarrow, Sera. One (1) soul, whole and unspent. Pledged as collateral by Odile Thren, First Elder of Thornvale, against obligation outstanding twenty years, four months, eleven days. Holder: THE REGENT OF THE UNDERCOURT.*

The cavern went very quiet around Felix, though of course it didn't—not really. The millstone murmur ground on. But something in her chest shut like a door, and behind that door was silence.

Twenty years, four months, eleven days. The count was still running. Somewhere beneath the ink, whatever kept these books was still tallying the days her mother had spent as somebody's security deposit.

"She never paid," Felix heard herself say. "Odile never paid the debt. My mother has been sitting in a drawer for twenty years because Odile couldn't—or wouldn't—finish paying for her own valley."

"Felix." Aldric's voice, low and careful, the voice he used near spooked horses. "Keep reading. There's more."

"Why would there be more?"

"Because there is always more. That is the first lesson of oaths, and the second lesson of ledgers."

She kept reading.

The entry continued below the pledge line in smaller script, the shifting kind, and she had to hold her thumb against the margin and force her eyes to move slowly, catching words as they settled:

*Note: Yarrow blood carries the swordspeaker inheritance. Collateral of this lineage is not inert. A soul sworn-and-sold may be conveyed across wards it could not cross living. Holder's intent, per attached instrument: conveyance of collateral to Thornvale wardline upon default, effecting dissolution of said ward. Land to revert to open claim.*

Felix read it again. Then a third time. The words didn't get better with familiarity. They got worse, the way a wound gets worse the more you use the limb.

"They're not keeping her soul," she said. "They're *saving* it. Like a key under a mat." She looked up at Aldric and found him already gray-faced, already understanding, because he had spent a lifetime among men who thought in instruments and clauses. "A Yarrow soul can pass the ward. Because my mother sold herself—because Odile *sold* her—the court can walk her right through the one border magic can't cross. And when she touches it—"

"The ward unravels from the inside," Aldric finished. "No siege. No breaking. The valley simply... stops being protected. And a valley where magic dies is worth more than gold to a court that deals in it. Empty land. Unclaimed land. Land where their writ runs and no elder's oath can argue back."

"They're going to use my mother as a crowbar."

"Yes."

The lantern above them flickered, and Felix realized distantly that her hands were shaking, and that the shaking had traveled up into the ledger, and that the glassy pages were trembling in sympathy—or perhaps she was trembling in sympathy with *them*. Twenty years, four months, eleven days. Her mother had been arithmetic for two decades. A line item. A *note*.

And Odile had known. Odile, who had taught her letters. Odile, who had given her honey cakes on festival mornings and told her, every year, with that grave warmth, *"You belong here, Felix. Thornvale holds its own."*

Thornvale held its own. Apparently it also pawned them.

"There's something else," Wren said softly. She had drifted to the neighboring shelf while Felix read, because Wren could no more stop exploring than she could stop breathing, and now she stood before an open folio with an expression Felix had never seen on her—stripped of all mischief, all quickness, just a girl looking at something that hurt. "Look at the holder column. Look at who holds *everything* in this section."

Felix wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and looked.

Corvane Ash: holder, the Regent. Ilsette Mourn: holder, the Regent. Entry after entry after entry, down the page and up the next—the same designation repeated until it stopped being a word and became a fact of geology. The Regent of the Undercourt held the collateral on nearly every defaulted debt in the archive. Whole shelves of them. Whole caverns of murmuring glass and humming hide, and behind every one, the same name, patient as water.

"It's not a court," Felix said slowly. "Courts have factions. Courts squabble. This is—" She gestured at the grinding dark. "This is one person's strongbox. Everyone in this city who trades in souls, they're all trading *through* someone. And the someone is—"

"The Regent," Aldric said. "Whom we were rather hoping to rob quietly." He exhaled through his nose. "I've served kings, lad—I mean, *lady*—I've served kings whose wealth I mistook for institutions. It is a common error. Power likes to dress as permanence. But strip the robes away and half of all kingdoms come down to one person in a room, holding everyone else's promises."

"So we steal one ledger-entry's worth of promise," Wren said, "from the person who owns the room."

"When you put it that way—"

"Oh, I can put it worse ways, if you'd prefer."

Felix almost laughed. It came out crooked, but it came out, and she was grateful to Wren for that—for the way she could wedge a joke into a crack in the world and lever it open just enough to breathe through. Found family's first stitch, she'd thought once, at an inn table, watching Wren steal bread off Aldric's plate. She hadn't known then that the stitching would have to hold a mother's soul and a valley's survival together.

She looked back down at the entry. At the bottom of the glass page, beneath the holder's intent, one final line had surfaced—new ink, still wet-looking, as if the ledger had only just decided to write it:

*Default declared imminent. Conveyance scheduled. Summon the Yarrow heir for audience.*

"*Summon,*" Felix repeated. "Summoned how? I haven't been summoned anywhere. I'm standing right here, robbing you politely."

As if in answer, every lantern in the western curve brightened at once.

Not much. A breath's worth. But the amber light gathered itself toward the reading stand like a tide deciding, and the murmuring in the shelves changed pitch—rose, sharpened, became almost *attentive*—and somewhere far off in the cavern, beyond the green desk and the uninteresting figure, a bell tolled once. Not loudly. The way a house bell tolls when dinner is ready.

Or when an appointment has arrived.

"Ah," said Aldric.

"Don't 'ah' me. What does 'ah' mean?"

"It means," he said, drawing himself up with the weary dignity of a man who has been right about disasters before, "that in my experience, when a powerful party summons you, refusing to attend does not cancel the audience. It merely makes the audience shorter. And louder."

The uninteresting figure at the green desk rose. Felix's eyes finally caught on it properly now that it moved—a tall shape in layered grey, faceless, or faced with something her mind declined to record—and it turned toward the western curve with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been surprised.

Three paths, Felix thought wildly. Run, fight, or—

Or walk forward. Walk up to the desk in the heart of the enemy's library, with her mother's name warm under her fingernails and the whole terrible architecture of the trap laid out plain before her, and see what the thing wearing the Undercourt's crown wanted with the last of the Yarrows.

Wren's fingers found hers. Cold, quick, steady.

"Together?" Wren asked.

"Together," Felix said.

And because she was who she was—because she was, whether Thornvale liked it or not, the last swordspeaker—she discovered that the word had weight when she meant it. The lanterns leaned in. The shelves hummed. Somewhere in the grinding dark, a ledger turned its own page.

The grey figure bowed, and gestured toward the depths of the court, and Felix Yarrow walked toward her audience with the Regent.
