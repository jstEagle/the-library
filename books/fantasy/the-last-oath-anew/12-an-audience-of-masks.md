## 12. An Audience of Masks

They came for Felix at the hour the Undercourt called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep. The lanterns along the corridor brightened by degrees, a false dawn performed for her benefit, and two attendants in grey robes arrived at her cell door without knocking, because doors here were apparently a suggestion rather than a rule.

"Lady Yarrow," said the taller one.

"I'm not a lady."

"The Regent insists," said the shorter one, who had the toneless voice of someone reciting from memory. "All guests of the court are styled according to their ledger entry. Your entry is quite distinguished."

Felix considered pointing out that she had not agreed to be a guest, that guests could generally leave, and that she had been locked in a room with excellent bread and no window for what she estimated was a day and a half. She decided against it. In Thornvale she had learned that arguing with people who held all the cards mostly taught them where your temper lived.

Instead she said, "Is there tea?"

The taller attendant blinked. "There is tea."

"Then this is looking up."

* * *

They walked her through corridors that hurt to look at directly—not because they were ugly, but because they were *wrong*, angles bending slightly away from where her eyes expected them to be, as if the whole court had been drawn by someone working from a description rather than the thing itself. Servants glided past with trays of candles and jars. Felix made herself not look at the jars. She had seen the archive's outer shelves during their capture—had heard the murmuring—and she was saving the rest of that grief for later, when she had a use for it.

Aldric and Wren had been taken somewhere else. She'd shouted herself hoarse about it, and a clerk with spectacles had patiently explained that separate accommodations were standard for persons of independent ledger interest, and would she like the tea now or after.

She thought about Wren's face when the guards came—furious, then terrified, then doing that thing Wren did where she turned fear into a joke mid-sentence: "Try the wine cellar first, I hear it's lovely." She thought about Aldric going quietly, too quietly, his jaw set like a man walking toward something he'd been promised.

And she thought: whatever happens in this audience, do not swear anything true.

Her mother had taught her that much, or tried to, in the years before the tree line took Felix's gift and buried it. *Words are load-bearing beams, little bird. Once you set them, the house stands on them.* Felix had been seven. She hadn't understood. She understood now, walking through a city built entirely out of other people's words.

* * *

The throne room—or audience chamber, or whatever the Undercourt called the place where it did its worst business—was smaller than she expected. That felt deliberate. Grandeur was for courts that needed to impress. This court only needed you to understand that it already knew everything about you.

The Regent sat on a chair of black glass, plain as a farmer's stool, and wore robes the color of deep water.

And wore her mother's face.

Not a likeness. Not a mask shaped like a face. It was Maren Yarrow's face, exact down to the small scar over the left eyebrow from a forge accident, down to the way the eyes crinkled first at the outer corners when they smiled—which they were doing now, warmly, terribly.

Felix stopped three steps in. Her body simply declined to continue, and she let it.

"You have her chin," the Regent said, and the voice was almost right too—almost, with something underneath it like a second voice speaking slightly out of sync, an echo lagging half a heartbeat behind. "I wondered if you would. Ledgers don't record faces well. Ink is unkind to cheekbones."

"Give it back," Felix said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"It isn't mine to give. It's collateral." The Regent rose—Maren Yarrow's easy rise, the smith's roll up onto the balls of the feet—and descended the shallow steps. "But you know that. You read the ledger. I'm told you wept on page four hundred and eleven. The archivist was very moved. He doesn't get moved often; he sold the feeling in 1603."

Felix's hands wanted to shake. She kept them still the way she kept a blade steady at the grindstone—by thinking about something else entirely. The tea she hadn't gotten. The bread in her cell, which had genuinely been excellent.

"You're wearing her like a coat," she said.

"I'm wearing her like a key." The Regent circled her slowly, and Felix fought every instinct not to turn, because turning meant following that face with her eyes and her eyes were not trustworthy right now. "Do you know what a Yarrow soul is worth, Felix? Do you know what it can *do*?"

"No. And I notice you haven't offered tea."

Something flickered across the borrowed face—amusement, genuine or expertly counterfeited. "Your mother asked the same question once. Different context. She was negotiating the price of horseshoes with a man who was cheating her, and she smiled exactly the way you're smiling now, and he gave her fair prices for twenty years out of pure terror." The Regent stopped in front of her. Up close, the wrongness was worse—the echo-voice, yes, but also the eyes, which never blinked quite enough. "You've never met me, and yet you keep meeting pieces of me. Your valley. Your silence. Your whole careful invisible life—that was my design, child. You've been living inside my paperwork."

The floor seemed to tilt. Felix thought of Odile's stricken face in the elder hall, of the confession wrung out like a confession always is—too late and incomplete. *The ward required a soul of unspoken oaths. The court required collateral. You were both.* She had hated Odile for it, righteously, for a full day and a half.

Now she hated the thing wearing her mother's face instead, and hatred, she found, organized the mind wonderfully.

"Say what you want," Felix said.

"What I want is already in motion. What I *offer* is simpler." The Regent gestured, and the air between them opened like a book—a shimmering pane on which words appeared, inked in silver, each line settling into place with a faint sound like a bell being wrapped in wool. Felix read it before she could stop herself. Reading things was a habit she'd need to cure.

**THE COURT OFFERS:**
**One (1) soul, recorded as MAREN YARROW, condition used but intact, currently held in Vault Seven, jar forty-nine.**
**In exchange for:**
**One (1) soul, recorded as FELIX YARROW, condition oath-bound, the last of her kind, valuation pending.**

"Jar forty-nine," Felix repeated. Her throat was dry. "You number them."

"We number everything. Numbers are how we love." The Regent's smile widened by a degree. "Here is the shape of it, plainly. A swordspeaker's soul, freely sworn into our keeping, is worth more than any vault in this court. More than the mountain. With it I could buy the valley's ward outright—no theft, no unraveling, no messy collapse. Your mother walks out under the sky. The ledger closes clean. Everyone goes home."

"And if I say no?"

"Then in ninety days the debt comes due, and the court collects its collateral by force, and your mother's soul goes into an instrument of the court's choosing, and the valley's ward comes apart anyway—slowly, painfully, with everyone inside it awake to watch. You'll have gained ninety days and lost everything else."

Felix looked at the contract floating between them. She looked at the face above it, her mother's face, patient and warm, waiting for her daughter the way Maren Yarrow had waited every evening at the forge door.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it—it wasn't even a hard bargain. One soul for another, hers for her mother's, and Felix had spent twenty years believing her own existence was the price paid for a valley's safety. What was one more payment? She'd been collateral since birth. At least this way she'd finally be *worth* something.

"That's the speech," Felix said. "You've done this before."

"Eleven thousand times. I'm very good at it."

"And how many took the deal?"

The pause was tiny, but Felix had spent a lifetime reading people in a valley where nobody lied well, and she caught it. "Enough," the Regent said.

"How many *kept* it?"

The echo underneath the Regent's voice stuttered—one heartbeat of dissonance, like a singer losing the note—and the mask-smile did not move at all.

"There it is," Felix said softly. "That's the flaw in the paperwork. Souls freely given, freely traded—but nobody reads the fine print until they're already in the jar. And then they spend eternity knowing they were cheated." She folded her arms. "My mother haggled horseshoe sellers into honesty. You think she wouldn't have read every word?"

"She did," the Regent said. "That's why she signed. To protect you. The ward, the valley, the silence around your gift—all of it purchased with her consent. Your entire life is a contract your mother honored. Are you so much better than she was?"

It landed. Felix would give the thing credit—it landed, right in the soft place under her ribs where she kept all her oldest guilt. For three long breaths she stood in the audience chamber of the Undercourt and genuinely considered selling herself, and the only reason she didn't say yes immediately was a memory that arrived sideways, the way important ones do:

Wren, three days ago, at the border inn, stealing bread off Felix's plate and saying, with her mouth full: *You're allowed to want things, you know. That's not a crime anywhere except in your head.*

And Aldric, gruffly, pushing his own untouched portion toward her: *Eat. Oaths are heavy work. So is stubbornness.*

Found family. She hadn't had the phrase yet then. She had it now, and it changed the arithmetic completely, because her soul wasn't just hers to spend—it was braided into two other people now, and the Regent's ledger couldn't see the braid.

"I won't trade today," Felix said.

"Today is not required." The Regent spread her hands—Maren's hands, callused correctly, *how*, how had they gotten the calluses right—"The offer stands ninety days. But the court does require a token of good faith. A formality. A small vow of intent: that you will return within the term to settle the matter honestly, one way or the other. No sale. Just... attendance."

"A promise to show up."

"A promise to consider. Even your knight knows the weight of such things. He swore lighter vows than this and they've been flaying him for thirty years." The Regent tilted her mother's head, sympathetic, understanding, horrible. "Refuse, and I must assume bad faith, and bad faith changes the terms of everything—your friends' accommodations, chiefly. Accept, and we part as honest parties. Say the words, Felix Yarrow. They cost nothing. Words are free."

*Words are load-bearing beams.*

Felix looked at the contract, at the silver ink, at jar forty-nine waiting somewhere in the dark with her mother's soul humming inside it. She looked at the face she had missed for twenty years, wearing an expression her mother had never once worn in her life, because her mother had never in her life looked at anyone the way a creditor looks at collateral.

And Felix Yarrow—who had hidden for two decades, who had sharpened ordinary blades and swallowed extraordinary words, who knew better than anyone alive what a spoken vow could build—decided to do the one thing a swordspeaker should never do.

She decided to lie.

She let her shoulders drop. She let her eyes go wet—grief was real and abundant, that part took no acting at all. "Ninety days," she said. "I'll come back. I swear by my name and by my mother's memory: I will return to this court before the debt falls due, and I will settle it honestly, with my own hand, as a daughter should."

Every word of it was technically true except the one that mattered. She would return. She intended to settle it. She simply did not intend to *pay*—and the vow, carefully built, carefully hollowed, said nothing whatsoever about paying.

The silver contract dimmed. The Regent extended her mother's hand.

Felix shook it. The calluses were perfect and the grip was cold as well-water and beneath her skin she felt something settle into place, a hair-fine thread tying itself around her wrist, light as a bracelet, patient as a snare.

"Pleasure doing business," the Regent said.

"Funny," Felix said. "Everyone in this court keeps saying variations of that, and none of you seem to know what pleasure is anymore. Sold it, probably. Sixteen-oh-three."

She turned and walked out, spine straight, escorted by her two grey attendants, and she did not let herself breathe fully until the throne room doors had closed behind her.

Only then did she look down at her wrist.

There, circling it like a tattoo written in wet ink, was a thin line of script—her own vow, transcribed in the court's hand, glowing faintly. And as she watched, the last three words began to darken. *As a daughter should.* The ink of them was turning the color of rust, of old blood, of a bell wrapped in wool, and somewhere far below her sternum Felix felt a small cold weight take up residence, the way frost takes up residence in a wall: quietly, and with every intention of spreading.

Perjury, the thread said, in a language older than words. Perjury, perjury, perjury.

"Well," Felix said aloud, to the empty corridor, because humor was also load-bearing, "that could have gone worse."

She had absolutely no evidence for this claim. But she had ninety days, two friends in separate cells, a stolen map, a plan barely past the drawing stage—and now, apparently, a countdown etched into her own skin, ticking toward the erasure of everything she was.

First, she thought, walking faster, she needed to find Wren.

Because Wren could read ciphers. And Felix had a terrible, growing suspicion that the cipher that mattered most was written on the inside of Wren's missing years.
