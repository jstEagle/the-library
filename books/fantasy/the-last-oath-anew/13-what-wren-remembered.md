## 13. What Wren Remembered

The memory came back to her in a bathhouse, of all places.

Not that the Undercourt had proper bathhouses—nothing so generous as steam and hot water and someone scrubbing your back for coin. What it had were condensation rooms carved into the living rock behind the tallow-market, where the cavern's own heat wept down the walls and you could pay two coppers to sit in the wet dark and pretend, briefly, that the world above still knew your name.

Wren sat on a stone bench with her knees drawn up, watching water gather in beads along her forearms, and told herself she wasn't afraid of the little glass phial in her palm.

She'd been carrying it for three days now. The fragment—a sliver of amber no bigger than a thumbnail, bought from a stall whose owner had smiled too many teeth at her—sat warm against her skin like a sleeping bird. The stall-keeper had said fragments were best swallowed in quiet, where the mind had room to unfold them. He had not said what to do if the mind turned out to be a room you'd been locked out of for three years.

"You could wait," Felix had offered that morning, in the careful voice she used whenever Wren's past came up, as if it were a wounded animal that might bolt. "Until after. Until your mother's soul is free and we're somewhere with better lighting."

"Better lighting," Wren had repeated. "You want me to remember my childhood by candlelight. Like a séance."

"I just meant—"

"And what if there is no *after*? What if the Regent feeds us to something with too many mouths tomorrow?" She'd held up the phial then, letting lantern-light pool inside it. "I've spent three years being an empty jar, Felix. I'd like to know what I was full of."

Felix hadn't argued after that. Felix was learning, slowly, that Wren did not want gentleness about this. She wanted it done. Aldric, who understood things about broken people that he never said aloud, had simply nodded and asked if she wanted company or space.

She'd said space. Then she'd gone and found a condensation room, because apparently what she actually wanted was company of the damp and anonymous kind.

"Right," she said to the empty room. Her voice came back off the wet stone, smaller than she'd sent it. "Hello, whoever I was."

She tipped the fragment onto her tongue.

It tasted the way all court-glass tasted—of cold iron and honey and something older underneath, something like the smell of a house you can't quite place—and then it dissolved, and the world folded inward like a hand closing.

---

She was small. That was the first thing. Small enough that the world arrived at her chin, and everything above it—the vaulted ceilings, the hanging banners of woven shadow, the tall figures gliding through corridors lit by lanterns the color of bruises—loomed like weather.

She was running. Bare feet slap-slapping on polished obsidian, breath hitching, because she'd done something wrong again. She was always doing something wrong. Father's clerks said so. The masked stewards said so. Even the servants said it kindly, which was worse: *Little mistress, you mustn't wander, little mistress, you mustn't ask.*

But she'd heard the crying. Down past the ledger-hall, in the corridor where the debt-jars lived, something had been crying—not a person-cry, exactly, more like a song that had forgotten its words and was grieving the loss—and she'd followed it the way children follow anything sad, with the terrible directness of the very young.

The corridor was cold. The jars were cold. And in the smallest jar, on the lowest shelf, there was a light. Not fire-light. A warmer thing, a hearth-colored thing, and it pressed against the glass like a moth against a window, and when she picked it up with both hands it hummed.

A lullaby. Half-remembered. A woman's voice, worn thin by years of glass.

"*Hush now, little ember, the mountain's asleep—*"

Wren—younger, smaller, seven years old maybe, with hair the color of tarnished silver instead of the plain brown it would be later—Wren held the jar against her chest and decided, with the absolute authority of a child, that the crying was going to stop.

She didn't know how she did it. That was the horror and the wonder both, remembered from inside: she simply put her lips to the glass seam and said, in the way that things in the Undercourt were *said*, "You're let go." Three words. A child's grammar. But the Undercourt ran on speech the way rivers ran on rain, and a daughter of the Regent's house carried weight in every syllable whether she meant it or not.

The seal cracked like spring ice.

The light poured up through her fingers, through her ribs, out through the high vents toward the distant gray suggestion of the surface—and the last thing it did before it went was brush against her cheek, warm as a thumb, grateful as a goodbye.

Then: screaming. Not hers. The ledgers screamed. Every clerk in the hall came running with their styluses raised like knives, shouting about a breached account, a Yarrow collateral, twenty years of interest evaporating through a child's carelessness—

And then Father came.

---

The memory went strange here, the way memories do around the moments that matter most. Details sharpened while everything else went soft as wool.

She remembered his boots. He wore boots of black glass, always, and they made no sound at all, which was somehow more frightening than sound. She remembered kneeling on the ledger-hall floor while clerks circled her like crows, and she remembered being more puzzled than scared, because she'd fixed it, hadn't she? The crying had stopped. Wasn't stopping the crying good?

Father knelt in front of her. Up close, behind the lacquer of his half-mask, his eyes were tired in a way she'd never seen eyes be tired before.

"Do you know," he said quietly, so only she could hear, "what you've cost me?"

She shook her head.

"Neither do I." And then—he looked over his shoulder at the clerks, at the stewards, at the whole breathing machinery of the court that watched him always, that measured him always, that would use any crack in him the way water uses any crack in a wall—and when he looked back at her, something in his face had closed like a book.

"The girl has damaged a holding," he announced, in the voice he used for judgments. "Her penalty is assessed."

"Father?" she said. Because that word had always been a door that opened. It didn't open now.

"The penalty is her remembering."

---

The memory bloomed its final petal, and it was the worst one, and it was also—God help her—the kindest thing she had ever seen.

They took her to the forgetting-room, where the air was thick as syrup and the walls were lined with jars of other people's yesterdays. The stewards prepared the extraction: her childhood, her name, her face's history, all of it to be decanted neatly into glass and shelved, because a Regent's daughter who freed souls on a whim could not be allowed to remain a Regent's daughter. The court required it. The ledgers required balance.

And Father stood in the doorway, and he could not stop it—she saw that now, understood it with her adult mind laid over her child's terror: he ruled nothing, really. He was the court's face and the court's fist and the court's prisoner, and the machine he fronted would grind him next if he flinched.

So he did the only thing left to a man with no power except the power of arrangement.

He waited until the stewards' backs were turned, and he crossed the room, and he took his daughter's face in his gloved hands, and he whispered:

"Listen to me. You won't remember this. So I'm putting it somewhere they can't take it." His thumbs wiped her cheeks, though she wasn't crying yet. "There's a valley above. There's a woman there named Yarrow whose child I couldn't save from the ledgers—no. Whose child I *did* save, once, years ago, and the court never knew, and tonight you paid for it without knowing why. Hear me, little bird. If you ever find your way to the surface—" his voice cracked, one hairline fracture, instantly plastered over, "—find the Yarrow girl. Find her and stay near her. Whatever else you lost, you'll have found the only clean thing I ever did. Consider it... inheritance."

The stewards turned back. The extraction began. And the last thing Wren-the-child saw, as the amber rose to swallow her, was her father turning away so she wouldn't watch his face break.

---

Wren came back to herself sitting in cold water, and she was screaming.

Not loudly. A thin, continuous sound, the scream of someone who doesn't know they're doing it, and it took her several seconds to find the off-switch, and several more to notice that the condensation room was no longer empty.

Felix crouched in front of her, soaked to the knees, hands hovering an inch from Wren's shoulders like she was afraid of startling a wild thing. Behind her, filling the doorway with shoulders and guilt, stood Aldric, dripping, a knife already in his fist though there was nothing in this room to stab but grief.

"I heard it through the wall," Felix said. "Everyone heard it through the wall. Are you—are you hurt? Did the fragment—"

"My father," Wren said.

Her voice came out in pieces, like something dropped down stairs.

"My father is the Regent."

The silence in the wet stone room was total. Somewhere far off, the market roared its endless market-roar, selling pieces of people to other people.

"No," Felix said—but softly, the way you say *no* when some part of you has already said *yes*. "No, Wren, the Regent wears masks, the Regent is—it's an office, it's not a—"

"It's a man in black glass boots." Wren laughed, and the laugh was awful, wet and cracking. "It's a man who calls his daughter *little bird*, and then sells her whole life to cover for the one decent act of his entire rotten reign." She pushed wet hair out of her face with shaking hands. "Do you want to know what the decent act was? Do you want to know what I'm worth? One soul. One Yarrow soul, slipped out of a jar years before either of us could walk. His one mercy. And the price of hiding it was *me*."

Felix had gone very still. Wren watched it land on her in stages—the arithmetic assembling itself behind those sharp smithy eyes—and watched her arrive, appalled, at the sum.

"A Yarrow soul," Felix said. "Years ago. Before I—" She stopped. Started again. "My mother told me stories about when I was a baby. She said I was sick for a whole winter. Fevered past sense. And when I woke up, she swore I was different. Warmer. She called it a miracle."

"It wasn't a fever," Wren said. The knowledge sat in her like a swallowed stone. "Or it was, but the cure was my father opening a ledger-line and pouring something back into a dying child in Thornvale. Something the court was owed. Something he stole *back*." She wrapped her arms around her own knees, rocking slightly. "He freed you, Felix. Before I ever met you, before the inn, before any of it—I was ordered, by my own father's last whisper, to find you and stay near you. My whole—" Her voice gave out. She tried again. "My whole life since the inn, I thought I chose you. Both of you. I thought finding family was the one thing nobody had taken from me. And it turns out it was written into me like a line in a contract. I'm not even sure my loving you is mine."

That was the sentence that broke Felix Yarrow's famous composure.

"Oh, Wren." She grabbed Wren's shoulders, water sloshing, and shook her—gently, but firmly, the way you shake someone to keep them in their body. "Listen to me. Listen. When you picked my pocket at the border inn, did your father's ghost guide your fingers?"

"...No?"

"When Aldric snored like a landslide and you stuffed a sock in his mouth, was that in the contract?"

A watery, involuntary snort. "He deserved it. Twice."

"When you shared your food with me the night I couldn't stop shaking after the tree line—when you lied to the soul-broker about my name to buy me time—when you bought a piece of your own childhood with money you needed for boots—was any of that his doing?" Felix's grip tightened. "He pointed you at a door, Wren. Walking through it was yours. Staying was yours. Loving us was *yours*. Nobody writes love into a ledger. Believe me—I've read this court's ledgers, and they don't stock it."

Wren wanted to argue. She was built for arguing; it was one of the few possessions the court hadn't confiscated. But Felix's hands were steady on her shoulders and her eyes were fierce and certain, and Aldric had sheathed the knife and was standing there with the expression of a man who had once made terrible oaths and had learned, at ruinous cost, to tell true ones.

"She's right," Aldric rumbled. "For what it's worth. Which, coming from me, is usually nothing."

"Immense comfort," Wren croaked.

"He hid the truth inside you because it was the only safe place left," Aldric said. "I know something about being made into an instrument. The difference between a tool and a person is whether you get to choose what you're used for." He paused. "You chose. Every day since the inn. That's the part they can't sell."

Wren breathed. In, out. The condensation beaded and fell. Somewhere in her chest, the new-old memory settled into place beside the three years she did have, and it hurt, and it fit, the way truth does.

"So," she said at last, wiping her nose with the back of her hand, dignity in ruins and past caring. "Let me make sure I have the shape of our disaster. My father runs the court. Your mother's soul is his favorite collateral. And the plan we're carrying into the vault tomorrow was designed by the man who—" she stopped, recalculated, started over— "who already sacrificed his own daughter once to do one merciful thing, and got away with it for twenty years."

"Yes," Felix said slowly, and Wren watched the same cold realization move across her face like frost across a window.

"And he knows we're here. He always knew. He *sent* the courier, didn't he? 'The Yarrow soul is called due'—that wasn't the court talking. That was a man arranging for his little bird to fly home."

Felix didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence answered, and it sounded like boots of black glass making no sound at all on obsidian floors.

Wren stood, water streaming off her, and felt something crystallize in her that was colder and harder than fear. Three years of emptiness, and now it turned out the emptiness had a purpose, and the purpose had a father attached, and the father had a mask she was going to take great personal pleasure in removing.

"Well then," she said. "Tomorrow we rob him blind. And afterward—" her mouth curved into something that was not entirely a smile, "—afterward, I'm going to knock on his door and introduce myself. Family reunions should be memorable."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of mountain, the Undercourt's bells began to ring—and not the market-chime they'd learned these past days, but the deep, arrhythmic tolling Wren had heard only once before, in the memory, in the forgetting-room.

The sound of the court preparing a penalty.

Felix's face drained white as paper. "That's for us," she whispered. "Wren—that's for *me*."
