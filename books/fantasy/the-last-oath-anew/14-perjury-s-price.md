## 14. Perjury's Price

Aldric Vane had spent eleven years learning what a broken oath looked like from the inside. He knew the way it started—a faint itch behind the sternum, like a splinter working toward the heart. He knew the middle stages: the dreams that weren't quite dreams, the way your own name began to sound like a stranger calling for someone else in a crowded market. He knew, better than any man alive should, how it ended.

So when he caught Felix Yarrow staring at her own reflection in the rain barrel outside their roadside camp and watching it *hesitate*—a half-second delay, like an echo arriving late—he felt something he hadn't felt since the prince died.

Hope, of all the cursed things. Because she still had time. And time was the one thing he'd never had.

"You see that too," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

He came to stand beside her. The water in the barrel was brown with pine needles, but it served. Her face floated there, pale and sharp-eyed—and then, as they both watched, the image flickered. Not vanished. Just… wavered, the way a candle flame wavers in a draft, as though the water wasn't sure whether it was obligated to hold her at all.

"The court marked you," Aldric said. "Perjury's brand. You swore a false oath in front of the Regent, and the Undercourt keeps books the way misers keep coin. Every lie you told it is now a debt, and it means to collect."

"Collect *what*? My soul? I thought that was my mother's department."

"Your name first. Then your face. Then your voice." He kept his tone level, the way you'd talk someone down off a ledge—or talk yourself up onto one. "Then everything else, in whatever order the ledger finds convenient."

Felix pulled back from the barrel and shook out her hands, which were trembling only slightly, which was better than yesterday. "Wonderful. So I'm being repossessed."

"In a manner of speaking."

"By a cave."

"A very old, very wealthy cave."

She laughed—short, bitter, but real—and Aldric found himself absurdly relieved by the sound. Three days since the audience with the Regent. Three days since Felix had smiled into that mask of her mother's stolen face and sworn, sweetly and sincerely-sounding, that she would deliver herself to the court within a fortnight, no tricks, no flight, no counter-oaths. She'd walked out of the Regent's hall on steady legs. She'd made it nearly half a mile before her nose started bleeding.

Wren had bandaged her by firelight without a word. That was Wren's gift: she could do terrible necessary things in silence, as if her missing past had taught her that some moments were better left unmarked.

"Show me," Aldric said now.

"Show you what?"

"Whatever it took from you today. Don't dress it up. I need the true count."

Felix hesitated. Then she held up her right hand, palm out, and said, "My name."

Aldric waited.

Nothing happened. No word came. Her lips moved around the shape of it—the shape was fine, the shape was easy—but the sound wouldn't come. It was like watching a woman try to lift a door that had been nailed shut from the other side. Her jaw worked. Color rose in her cheeks. Finally she exhaled through her teeth and dropped the hand.

"It's not gone," she said quickly, too quickly. "I can *feel* it. It's just—" She gestured vaguely at her own throat. "Behind glass."

The cold that went through Aldric then had nothing to do with the mountain air. He knew this too. Gods, he knew this stage. There had been a month, after the prince's funeral, when he'd tried to say *I swear I loved him like a brother* at the graveside and produced nothing but breath.

"How long?" he asked. "Since you noticed?"

"This morning. When the innkeeper asked who was paying for the room." Her mouth twisted. "Wren covered for me. Said my name was 'Smith.' The innkeeper believed her instantly, which I choose to find insulting rather than alarming."

"Take the insult. Alarm comes later." He turned away from the barrel before his expression could betray him, and busied himself with the fire. "Pack light tonight. We walk at dawn."

"Dawn? We're two days from the vault route Wren mapped. If we go early, we'll be exhausted when we hit the wards—"

"We are not going anywhere until I teach you something," Aldric said, "and I cannot teach a woman who dissolves in her sleep."

That stopped her. Good. Fear was useful fuel; he'd burned a decade of it himself.

"What do you mean, *dissolves*?"

"I mean the oath doesn't take you all at once. It takes you in installments. And it takes the quietest parts first—the parts nobody would miss until they're gone." He fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "Tomorrow, before we move, you will stand where I can see you and speak one true vow. Aloud. Small. Something you actually intend to keep. And every dawn after that, another. Until the vault. Until the end of it."

"That's it? Poetry lessons?"

"That's the oldest sword-form there is, girl. Older than steel." He looked at her over the flames. "You want to know what a swordspeaker is? Everyone thinks it's the big oaths. Rivers stopping. Mountains listening. But the power doesn't live in the size of the vow. It lives in the *truth* of it. A small vow, perfectly true, is worth more than a grand one full of cracks. Your perjury opened a hundred cracks. We seal them one truth at a time."

Felix stared at him. In the firelight, her reflection in her own eyes seemed—mercifully—to arrive on time.

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then we improvise. I've gotten very good at improvising. It's the only thing left that I'm good at."

"That's not true," she said, and then blinked, as if surprised by her own voice. "You're good at cooking. Badly, but with confidence."

Something in Aldric's chest loosened a notch. "Confidence is nine-tenths of cooking."

"And the other tenth?"

"Bread. Which is a lie anyway. Bread is mostly patience wearing a crust."

She laughed again—longer this time—and went to tell Wren they'd be moving at dawn. Aldric stayed by the fire and did not sleep much, because he knew what was coming for her in the dark, and he knew there was only so much a man could stand between a friend and the night.

But he would stand there anyway. It was, at least, an oath he could still keep.

* * *

Dawn came grey and cold, and Felix stood in the frost with her arms crossed like a woman awaiting sentencing.

"All right," she said. "One true vow. Small. What counts as small?"

"Anything you can't lie about." Aldric paced a slow line in front of her, hands clasped behind his back in his old drill-yard fashion, which Wren had already mocked twice and would mock again. "Not 'I will free my mother.' Too large, and half a hope. Not 'I will survive.' You can't intend that; nobody can. Start with something so humble it's almost embarrassing."

"Like what?"

"'I will eat breakfast.'"

Felix gave him a look. "You're joking."

"I have never been more serious in my life, which for me is saying something." He stopped pacing. "Say it like you mean it. Say it knowing you'll be annoyed with me later for making you waste magic on porridge."

She rolled her eyes, drew a breath—and spoke. "I vow I will eat breakfast."

The words came out flat. Nothing happened. A bird somewhere kept doing whatever birds do at dawn, unimpressed.

"Well?" she said. "Should the river stop? Should the mountain bow? Because I feel ridiculous."

"Again. Mean it this time. You're performing a vow instead of speaking one. There's a difference, and the world can hear it."

"I *am* going to eat breakfast!"

"Then why does it sound like a hostage statement?"

She opened her mouth to argue—closed it—glared—and then, visibly, let something go. Her shoulders dropped an inch. Her breath came slower. And when she spoke again, it was quiet, almost conversational, the way you'd tell a friend a small true thing across a table:

"I vow I'll eat breakfast. With both of you. Because I don't know how many mornings I've got left, and I don't want to waste this one."

The frost around her boots melted in a neat circle, six inches wide, perfect as a coin.

Aldric felt the hair rise on his arms. Eleven years since he'd stood near a spoken vow and felt the world *lean in*. The circle of bare earth steamed faintly. Felix looked down at it, then up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious about it.

"It worked," she whispered. "Why did *that* work? That was nothing. That was less than nothing."

"That was everything." His voice came out rougher than intended. "The false oath is eating the Felix the court knows—the collateral, the Yarrow heir, the last swordspeaker. All the titles. But it can't touch what you chose just now. It has no claim on breakfast."

From the bedroll, Wren raised her head, hair a glorious catastrophe, and said, "If the magic ritual involves food, I'm legally required to participate. Also, is there food?"

"There's hard bread and harder cheese," Aldric said.

"Then I vow," Wren announced, sitting up with tremendous dignity, "to eat all of it."

Nothing happened, obviously. Wren wasn't a speaker. But she said it with such conviction that Felix laughed through her tears, and Aldric privately decided that counted. Truth was truth, whatever mouth it came out of.

They ate. The frost-circle stayed bare and green-edged all day, a small stubborn signature written in the ground, and whenever Felix glanced back at it—which was often—she straightened a little, like a woman remembering her own spine.

By evening, though, the tide had come back in.

It happened at the border inn, the second one, where a carter's wife chatted with them while the stable boy saw to the horses. Pleasant woman, broad-faced, missing a tooth, the kind of person whose kindness costs her nothing because she's never once calculated it. She liked them immediately. She pressed dried apples on Wren and asked Felix her name.

And Felix opened her mouth, and nothing came out.

Not this time the silence of a sealed throat—worse. This time she *said* it. Aldric watched her lips form the syllables, watched the air move. But the sound arrived wrong, thinned, like a voice heard through a wall. The carter's wife frowned politely, tilted her head, and said, "Sorry, dear?"

"*Fe—*" Felix tried again. "*Yar—*"

"Ferryman's daughter?" the woman guessed helpfully.

"Yes," Felix said, and the relief in her voice was a knife.

Afterward she sat on the inn's bench with her hands wrapped around an untouched cup of cider, and Aldric sat beside her and said nothing, because there was nothing yet to say that the dawn-vow couldn't say better.

"She didn't hear me," Felix finally said. "Or she heard something else. Or—" Her thumb traced the rim of the cup, around and around. "Aldric. How long do I have?"

He considered lying to her. Considered it seriously, the way you consider jumping off something high just to know what the falling feels like.

"The man who taught me—the knight who found out what I was, before everything—he described it as a candle burning at both ends. The court lit the second end when you lied. But here's what he didn't know, because I learned it after: a candle burns faster in wind. Alone, in the dark, telling no truths—you'd be gone inside a month. Maybe less."

"And with the vows?"

"With the vows?" He turned to look at her. Her face was still fully hers, in this light, at this hour. He memorized it, the way you memorize things you might be asked to describe later under oath. "With the vows, we might buy you the fortnight. Enough to reach the vault. Enough to finish this."

"Might."

"I'm not in the business of promises anymore."

"No," Felix said softly. "You're in the business of teaching people to keep their own." She drank the cider at last, decisively, as if defying it. "Then we do it every dawn. Even the stupid ones. *Especially* the stupid ones."

"That's the spirit."

"And tomorrow," she added, with the ghost of her old crooked smile, "you're doing one too."

Aldric went very still. "I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me. One true vow, aloud, every dawn. You think I haven't noticed you flinch every time I say the word *oath*? You think I haven't noticed you never once say your own name?" She set down the cup and faced him fully. "You said a small true vow is worth more than a grand one full of cracks. Fine. Prove it. Show me a cracked man sealing one crack."

"Wren put you up to this."

"Wren suggested it. I'm the one threatening you with sincerity."

Somewhere behind them, Wren called out, "I regret nothing!"

Aldric looked at Felix Yarrow—who was disappearing by inches, who had every right to spend her remaining days clutching her own erasure like a wound, and who had instead chosen, on the worst week of her life, to start handing out homework—and felt the second emotion in eleven years he had no armor for.

Gratitude. Worse than hope. Hope at least kept its distance.

"Fine," he said. "One vow. Tomorrow. But I pick mine, and if either of you laughs—"

"We would never," said Felix.

"We absolutely will," said Wren.

* * *

The third dawn was the worst one, and Aldric knew it before Felix even woke.

He knew it because the bedroll beside hers was empty-looking—not vacant, she was in it, breathing—but *unremarkable*, the way furniture is unremarkable. His eyes slid off her twice before his mind overruled them and forced him to look properly. And when she sat up and pushed the hair from her face, the morning light did something obscene: it passed through the edge of her, just slightly, the way light passes through smoke. Only at the edges. Only for a moment. Then she was solid again, ordinary, glaring at the cold.

But Wren was sitting bolt upright, staring, both hands pressed over her mouth.

"I heard her snore all night," Wren said, muffled. "I heard her. Why did I forget she was there? Twice. I got up to check the horses and forgot we were four and not three."

"Because the world is starting to round you down," Aldric said grimly. "Fractions first. The ledger trims what won't be missed." He stood, joints cracking, and pointed at the patch of open ground between the dead fire and the frost-line. "Up. Both of you. Dawn service starts now."

Felix climbed out of the bedroll slowly. In the grey light, without the fire to prop her up, her edges looked… thin. Not transparent. Just *economical*, as though someone had begun sketching her from memory and running low on charcoal.

"Your turn first," she rasped. "You promised."

"So I did." Aldric planted his feet. He had rehearsed exactly nothing, because rehearsed vows rang false, and because some part of him had hoped she'd forget. She had not forgotten. Of course she had not forgotten. She was the last swordspeaker; she collected debts of the tongue the way other people collected coins.

He breathed in. The mountains stood around them, patient as creditors.

"My name is Aldric Vane," he said, and the words scraped coming out, rusted from disuse. "I was a knight of the realm, and I broke the only oath that ever mattered to me, and I have hated myself cleanly and thoroughly for eleven years." He paused. Steadied. "I vow—I intend—that when this ends, however it ends, I will not run from the result. Whatever Felix becomes, or stops becoming, I will stand where I can see it. I am done looking away."

The silence afterward had weight. Not magic—his speaking days were done, everyone knew it—but the older kind of silence, the kind that follows a true thing said out loud.

Wren wiped her eyes with her sleeve and pretended she hadn't. "Nobody laugh," she ordered. "I'm serious. Nobody laugh."

"You're crying," Felix said gently.

"I'm *serious* crying. It's different."

Then it was Felix's turn, and she stood in her half-faded edges, and Aldric braced for something grand—for the oath that would call rivers or shake stone, the kind of vow that had made her bloodline legend.

"I vow," Felix said, voice thin but level, "that I am Felix Yarrow. Daughter of Maren Yarrow. Friend of a thief and a fool. And I am *still here*."

The frost steamed in a wide ring. The dead fire stirred. Somewhere upslope, a whole hillside of pines bent together in a wind that touched nothing else—and then everything settled, and Felix gasped, and grabbed Wren's arm, and laughed, ragged and bright.

Her edges were solid. For now. For this hour.

"Again tomorrow," Aldric said quietly.

"Again tomorrow," she agreed.

They broke camp fast after that, moving with the particular urgency of people racing a candle. By midday they'd reached the switchbacks below the vault road, and Wren was crouched over the cipher-map, tracing routes with a charred stick, muttering about ward-stones and guard rotations she shouldn't know and did. Felix sharpened blades that didn't need sharpening, her hands needing work the way other hearts need beating.

It was Wren who looked up first. Not at the map. At the ridge.

"Aldric," she said, in a voice with no humor left in it at all. "How many riders did we expect to be following us?"

"None. Why?"

"Because there are six," she said, "and they're flying the Regent's colors—and the one in front is carrying something on a pole, and I really wish I hadn't recognized it."

Aldric followed her gaze. The banner snapped in the high wind, white on black: the sigil of the Undercourt's Regent.

And beneath it, borne like a trophy or a summons, hung a mask. Her mother's face, smiling.
