## 15. The Vault Beneath the Court

The Undercourt never slept, but it did, occasionally, blink.

That was what Wren called it—the blink—a stretch of maybe forty heartbeats in the smallest hours when the lantern-tenders changed shifts and the great lamps along the Processional guttered all at once, like a city closing one eye to rub it. She'd learned the rhythm during her three blank years here, or so she guessed; her body knew the timing even if her mind had sold the lesson along with everything else.

"Forty heartbeats," she whispered now, crouched beside Felix in the shadow of a buttress shaped like a kneeling giant. "Thirty-seven if the tender on the east stair is old Tammas, and he's always old Tammas."

"You remember his name?" Felix kept her voice low. In the Undercourt, names were currency, and spending them carelessly was how people ended up on shelves.

"I don't *remember* him." Wren's grin flashed white in the lantern-dim. "I just know things. Like I know the vault wards cycle on a seven-count and the third count has a gap wide enough for a lie. It's infuriating, honestly. My head is full of other people's secrets and not one single memory of my own birthday."

"Your birthday could be worse knowledge," Aldric murmured from Felix's other side. The knight was a wall of shadow and quiet discomfort, as he had been since they'd entered the cavern city two days ago. He kept touching the hilt of his sword as though checking it hadn't turned into something else.

"Everything is worse knowledge down here," Wren said cheerfully. "Come on. The blink starts when the bell with the crack in it rings wrong."

They waited. Above them, the cavern ceiling stretched away into darkness hung with ten thousand lights—soul-lanterns, some of them, though most folk in the markets below preferred not to think about that. The bell rang. It rang *wrong*, a cracked, wheezing note that made Felix's teeth ache, and then the great lamps dimmed in a slow wave, street by street, like a tide going out.

"Now."

They moved. Wren led, quick and certain, darting through alleys between market stalls shuttered for the night—stalls that had sold, Felix knew from their grim survey, secondhand childhoods and bottled years and the names of dead men still warm enough to wear. Felix followed with her mother's map pressed flat against her ribs, and Aldric brought up the rear, broad enough that anyone glimpsing him would assume he was architecture.

Three turns. A drainage channel they waded through, thigh-deep and smelling of cold stone. Then a door that wasn't a door—Wren pressed her palm flat against blank rock and muttered something under her breath, something with too many syllables to be any living language, and the rock sighed open onto a spiral stair descending past the range of any lantern.

"How do you know these words?" Felix asked, as the dark swallowed them.

Wren was quiet for a moment. "I think," she said at last, "they're mine. Or they were. Somebody taught me, before—" She waved a hand vaguely at her own skull. "Before the yard sale."

The stair went down farther than Felix liked. Her knees kept a running commentary about it. But the air grew warmer as they descended, which was wrong—caves should grow colder—and there was a sound beneath the sound of their footsteps, faint and layered and ceaseless.

Murmuring.

"Do you hear that?" Felix asked.

"Yeah." Wren didn't look back. "You will hear it more. Try not to listen to individual voices. That way lies standing in the middle of the market at dawn crying over a stranger's grandmother. Speaking from experience. Presumably."

* * *

The vault doors were glass.

Not framed in glass, not reinforced with glass—*made* of it, two panels taller than Aldric, thick as a man's forearm, and through them Felix could see the light within: a soft gold glow, sourceless, the color of candlelight through honey. Carved—or grown, nothing down here looked carved—into the glass were words in the same too-many-syllable language, and beneath them, in trade-script everyone in the markets read fluently:

**THE LEDGER HOLDS ALL. ALL HELD IS OWED.**

"A cheerful place," Aldric said.

"It gets better." Wren pressed her hand to the glass again and closed her eyes. "Third count. There's a gap. Watch."

She spoke the many-syllabled words again, slower, shaping each one like a person handling fragile crockery. Somewhere inside the doors, something clicked—not a lock opening, Felix thought, but a *count* losing its place, a ledger-line briefly smudged. One panel swung inward a hand's width, silently, obediently, as if it had merely been waiting for someone polite enough to ask.

Felix stared at her friend. "How do you know the vault's own counting-words?"

"Like I said. Infuriating." But Wren's grin had gone thin at the edges. "Felix. When we get in there. If you see something that used to be mine—"

"We're here for your mother," Aldric said quietly. "One jar. In and out."

"One jar," Felix agreed, and hated how much the promise cost her. Because the vault beyond the doors was full of jars—thousands upon thousands of them—and every single one held somebody's whole world.

* * *

Inside, the vault was a cathedral.

That was the only word for it. Pillars of pale stone rose into darkness overhead, and between them, tier upon tier upon tier, shelves climbed toward a ceiling no lamp reached. And on the shelves, glass jars. Round-bellied, narrow-necked, each one cradled in a stand of woven silver wire, each one glowing faintly from within—gold, blue, green, one distant shelf burning sullen red—and each one *murmuring*.

The sound filled the space the way water fills a well. Not words, exactly. Fragments. A woman laughing at something. A man repeating a child's name over and over, gentle as rain. Someone humming. Someone arguing. Somewhere far off, a whole choir of voices singing a harvest song slightly out of tune, the way real people sing it, the way no one sings it after they've been poured into glass.

Felix stopped walking.

"Felix." Aldric's hand landed on her shoulder, heavy and steadying. "Breathe."

"They're *in there*," she said. "All of them. They're not sleeping, they're—they're still *in there*." A jar on the nearest shelf glowed a soft evening blue, and from it came a voice, small and clear: *"—and then we'll plant the beans after the frost, I promised you, didn't I? I always keep—"*

The voice looped. Began again. *"—and then we'll plant the beans after the frost—"*

Felix's eyes burned. "This is where Odile sent my mother. This is what twenty years of Thornvale's protection cost. Not coin. *Shelving.*"

"And why we're taking one back," Wren said gently. "Just the one. Tonight." She'd produced, from somewhere in her impossible coat, a slip of paper covered in her own cramped cipher-notes. "The archive said Yarrow collateral, Regent's holding, sub-level seal. Sub-level means down, and down means—"

"That stair." Aldric pointed.

It spiraled down from the cathedral floor's center, guarded by nothing—which was somehow worse than guards. Things this valuable didn't need guarding, Felix supposed. They needed *accounting*. As they descended, the murmur of the upper shelves faded behind them, replaced by something quieter, older, deeper. The jars down here glowed differently. Steadier. These souls had stopped hoping someone was coming.

Felix walked faster.

* * *

The lower gallery was smaller, rounder, its ceiling low enough that Aldric had to duck. Dozens of jars, not hundreds. Each stand bore a little plate of etched metal, and Felix made herself read them, one by one, because skipping felt like a betrayal of every name she passed.

*Held against debt of Corvin Hale. Term: perpetual.*
*Held against debt of the House of Nine Lanterns. Term: until paid.*
*Held against debt of Odile, Matriarch of Thornvale. Collateral: one soul, surname Yarrow.*

There.

Felix's breath left her in a rush she hadn't authorized. The jar sat on an ordinary shelf on an ordinary stand, and it looked like all the others except—it didn't. The others' glows flickered with their looping fragments, restless, repetitive, trapped in the last moments of the lives they'd been taken from. This jar glowed steady and warm, amber-gold, like bread baking, like lamplight in a kitchen window, and the sound that came from it wasn't a loop at all.

It was a song.

Felix knew the melody before she knew she knew it. Three notes rising, two falling, a little turn at the end like a shrug, like a laugh. She had thought it was something she'd invented as a child, a tune she hummed while working the bellows, and had never known where it came from. Now a thief's bought memory came flooding back—a woman's voice singing in a kitchen, flour on her hands—and Felix understood that the song had lived in her all these years the way a seed lives in frozen ground.

Her mother was in the glass, and her mother was *singing*.

"Oh," Felix said. It wasn't a word. It was just the sound a person makes when the floor of the world holds.

"Felix." Wren's voice, very soft. "Is that—?"

"She's singing." Felix reached out and stopped herself, fingers hovering a hair's breadth from the glass. "Twenty years in a jar and she's *singing*. Does she—is she in there? Does she know—?"

"The broker at the market said the good ones stay coherent," Wren said, and then winced, because there was no gentle version of anything anyone said down here. "Sorry. Bad phrasing. What I mean is—she might hear you."

Felix laid both palms flat against the glass.

It was warm.

That undid her more than anything else. Not the glow, not the song, but the simple animal warmth of it, like pressing your hands to a sleeping person's back. The jar was warm the way a person is warm, and Felix stood there in the cold cathedral of sold lives with her hands on her mother and felt twenty years of careful, practiced invisibility tear straight down the middle.

"Mama," she whispered. "It's Felix. It's—your name is Sera, and you make terrible stew and you sing off-key on purpose because you think it's funny, and you gave up everything to buy me a valley where nobody could ever do this to me, and I'm here to take you *home*."

The song faltered.

Just for a heartbeat—one skipped note, one held silence—then resumed. But the quality of it had changed. The little turn at the end, the shrug-note, came twice now. Three times. Deliberate. Repeating, the way a trapped voice repeats when repetition is all it has.

Felix's skin prickled. "Wren. Is she—is she trying to say something?"

Wren leaned close, head cocked, listening with her whole body the way she listened to locks and bells and the rhythms of cities. Her face went slowly, thoroughly strange.

"It's a warning," she breathed. "Or—I think—it's the same phrase, over and over. Felix, I think she's saying *not me*. Over and over. *Not me. Not me. Not—*" Wren straightened, and Felix saw real fear arrive in her eyes, fear with nowhere to hide, because Wren's face had never once managed to hide anything. "Why would your mother warn us away from her own jar?"

"Because it's exactly what I'd do," Felix said slowly. The warmth under her palms suddenly felt less like comfort and more like a hearth before a house fire. "If I were bait. If I were the trap the whole court built around the last swordspeaker, and I knew my daughter was stubborn enough to come anyway—" She pulled her hands back. The song went on, patient, urgent, *not me, not me, not me*. "Wren. The archive entry. Read it to me again. All of it. Every line."

Wren fumbled out her notes with fingers that weren't quite steady. "*Held against debt of Odile, Matriarch of Thornvale. Collateral: one soul, surname Yarrow. Holder: the Regent's own seal. Terms: redemption permitted by bearer of Yarrow blood—*"

"*Bearer of Yarrow blood,*" Felix repeated.

The word *bearer* sat in the air between them, doing its quiet, horrible work.

"Not *heir*," Aldric said. His voice had gone flat, the voice he used when the flaying-oath was near the surface. "Not *daughter*. *Bearer*. As in the person who carries something. As in—" He looked at Felix, and Felix watched him arrive at it half a heartbeat after she did. "As in a vessel. They don't want to sell her mother's soul, girl. They want to send it home. Inside whoever comes to claim it."

The jar sang on, sweet and warm and desperate. *Not me. Not me.*

"No," Felix said—but even as she said it, she felt it, the faint pull at the edges of her, the thing she'd been too hopeful to notice: the way her oaths had been whispering louder since they entered the vault, the way her fading reflection in the glass seemed to *lean* toward the amber light, the way the perjury-mark on her soul itched like a hook seeking purchase. Her mother's soul in the glass. Felix's blood on the outside. Two halves of one transaction, and she had walked herself across the counter.

"It doesn't matter," she said aloud, and was surprised by how steady her voice came out. "Whatever they planned. Whatever trap this is. I am not leaving her on a shelf." She wrapped both hands around the jar's neck, where the silver wire met the glass, and began, very softly, to speak—an unbinding, a countersong, her mother's own melody turned inside out to loosen the seal. The glass warmed further under her hands. The song shifted, quickened—

And above them, through the cathedral of murmuring souls, a bell began to ring.

Not the cracked blink-bell. A clean, hard, urgent peal, rolling down through tier after tier of glowing jars, and every soul in every jar went silent at once, thousands of voices cutting off mid-word, mid-laugh, mid-name, until the only sounds left in the vault were the ringing, and the ringing, and the ringing—

—and Aldric Vane, on his knees on the stone floor, screaming.
