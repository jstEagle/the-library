## 16. Betrayal in Glass

The jar sang.

Aldric had heard a great many things in his forty-odd years—war drums, funeral bells, the specific wet sound a promise makes when it breaks—but he had never heard a soul sing before. It was a small sound, barely more than breath, coming from the shelf of glass at the cathedral's heart: a woman's voice humming a lullaby through the walls of her prison, patient as water wearing down stone.

Felix stood before that shelf with both hands pressed flat against the glass, and she was crying without seeming to notice.

"That's her," Wren whispered beside him. "That's the song. From my memory. Aldric—that's the *song*."

"I hear it," Aldric said.

What he did not say—what he could not make his tongue say, though he tried, though the words climbed his throat like men escaping a burning house—was *we have to go*. The vault was too quiet. He had spent twenty years in service to a crown and ten more running from it, and he knew the particular silence of a trap that has already sprung. This was not the silence of an empty room. This was the silence of held breath.

"Felix," he managed. "The wards on these shelves. Look at them."

She looked. Anyone else would have seen nothing—the vault's magic was old and smooth as river stone—but Felix Yarrow had been born reading oaths the way other people read weather, and after three weeks outside the valley she read them fluently now, the way a drowning man reads currents.

"They're not keyed to keep people out," she said slowly. Her fingers traced the air above the nearest shelf, following lines of binding only she could see. "They're keyed to keep something *in* until—" She stopped. Her face went very still, the way it did when she was about to swear something, which was exactly what made Aldric's blood run cold.

"Until what?"

"Until a swordspeaker touches them." Her hand was still hovering above the shelf where the singing jar sat. "Aldric, these wards aren't built to hold souls. They're built to hold *me*. Or someone like me. The whole shelf is one enormous conditional clause." She laughed, short and unhappy. "Whoever built this expected me."

*No*, thought Aldric. *Not expected. Arranged.*

And then the old oath woke up.

---

He felt it first in his knees. That was how the crown's oaths always took him—not in the mind, where a man might argue, but in the body, where there was no argument to be had. His knees hit the vault floor hard enough to crack the flagstone, and the words came up out of him like bile, like a confession, like a blade being drawn slowly from a wound twenty years unhealed:

*I swear by crown and iron and the life in my breast to bear the last swordspeaker to the Undercourt's door, and to see the debt of Yarrow collected, whatever the cost, whoever the payer.*

He had never spoken those words. He would have remembered; you do not forget the shape of your own damnation. But they lived in him anyway, stitched into the oath he *had* sworn at nineteen with wine and flattery in his blood—I will serve the crown's interest in all things, unto death—and some clerk of the court, some lawyer of souls, had found the loophole years ago and waited for it to matter.

*Unto death.* Oaths were literal things. Aldric had learned that lesson over a dying prince's body, and here it was again, arriving fashionably late.

"Aldric?" Wren's hand was on his shoulder. "Aldric, what's happening? You've gone white as bone."

"Get away from me," he said, and his voice came out doubled, his own and something older underneath it, something with the cadence of a court clerk reading terms aloud. "Both of you. *Run.*"

"No," said Felix, because she was Felix, because she had refused every sensible thing he'd ever suggested from the moment they met at the valley gate. "Aldric, look at me. What is it? What's in you?"

"The oath." The words ground out between his teeth like millstones. "There's a second oath under mine. Sleeping. It's—it's waking up. Felix, I think I was sent to find you. Not by accident. Not by my own choosing. Twenty years ago, when I swore myself to the crown, someone wrote a *rider* into my service, and the rider says—" His jaw clenched so hard he heard it click. The compulsion seized his right arm, dragged it toward his sword hilt, and he fought it with everything he had, which suddenly felt like almost nothing. "It says I'm the delivery. I'm the *pack mule*, Felix. I carried you straight to the door they needed opened, and now the door needs opening, and the key is—"

His hand closed around his sword hilt.

He had not told it to.

---

Here is what no ballad ever tells you about being an oathbreaker: the breaking is the easy part. Any coward can break an oath. What the songs call disgrace, what Odile's elders called shame, what Aldric had carried across three kingdoms and ten bitter years—that was not the breaking. That was the *carrying*. Because an oath does not leave you when you break it. It stays. It becomes a splinter in the soul, and every day it works a little deeper, and every night it reminds you of the exact shape of the promise you failed, and you learn to live around it the way a tree learns to grow around a nail.

But this—this was different. This was an oath that had never been broken, working exactly as designed, and there was no living around it. There was only the iron of it, pouring down his arm, turning his own sword arm against the two people who had—gods help him, he'd never say it aloud, knights didn't, not even disgraced ones—who had become, somewhere on the road, in shared fires and stolen bread and Wren's terrible jokes, the closest thing to home he'd had since a prince died with Aldric's name half-spoken on his lips.

"*Wren,*" he gasped. "Take Felix and *go*."

"She can't," Wren said. Her voice was shaking but her feet were planted, and she had put herself bodily between Aldric's rising sword and Felix's back, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing Aldric had ever witnessed, and he had witnessed a great deal of both. "If she leaves the vault without the jar, everything we—"

"Wren, I am *asking you*—"

"You don't ask," Wren said. "You order. And I've never once taken an order from you, old man, why start now?"

He loved her for it. He hated her for it. Both at once, and no time to sort the feelings, because the compulsion found his legs and stood him up like a puppet, and the vault's great glass cathedral blurred past as his body began to march.

---

The wards were not on the shelves. He understood that now, with the terrible clarity the body has when the mind has been evicted. The wards were on the *doors*—the four tall archways of black glass at the compass points of the vault, each sealed with script older than the court itself. A conditional clause, Felix had said. Until a swordspeaker touches them.

Or until a swordspeaker's bound servant breaks them.

His sword came up. It was a good sword; he had maintained it faithfully through disgrace and exile, oiling the steel in inns and ditches, and he hated it in this moment more than he had ever hated anything, because it was obedient, because it went where the oath pointed it while he screamed behind his own eyes.

The first blow rang off the eastern door like a bell tolling for a funeral. Cracks spidered out from the impact, fine as hair, glowing sullen red.

"Aldric!" Felix's voice, raw. "Counter-oath me! Say something true, break the chain—"

"I *can't*—" The words cost him blood; he felt them tear loose on the way up. "It's not *my* oath, girl, it's the crown's, it's got the weight of—of every knight who ever swore—"

"Then fight it! You fought for a prince who was already dead—you fought the whole court's judgment with nothing but your stubbornness—fight *this*!"

The second blow. The cracks widened. Somewhere far above, through miles of mountain, an alarm began—not a bell, not a horn, but a sound like a ledger page tearing, like a thousand voices remembering themselves at once, and Aldric knew the court was waking, knew guards and brokers and things worse than both were already descending, and none of it mattered, none of it could stop his arm.

"Listen to me." Wren had her face an inch from his, her hands clamped on his sword wrist with a thief's grip, trying to drag the blade off-line, and her eyes were bright and furious and terrified. "Whatever they wrote into you, whatever they planned—they didn't plan *us*. They didn't plan a thief with no yesterday or a smith who swears rivers still. People are not *terms*, Aldric. We are not clauses. So you get to be a person for five more seconds, and a person *chooses*—"

The third blow shattered the eastern door.

Black glass burst inward in a bloom of shards, and every ward in the vault lit at once, and every soul in every jar began to scream—not in pain, Aldric thought wildly, in *alarm*, a chorus of the sold and the shelved all crying out together—and the singing stopped. The lullaby cut off mid-note, and that, of all things, was what broke something loose in him: the image of Felix's mother's song ending, unfinished, because of him.

He got one word out. Just one, torn from wherever he kept the things that were still his own:

"*Run.*"

Then the oath took the rest of him, and Aldric Vane—oathbreaker, kingslayer-in-name, keeper of a dead prince's memory—turned his sword on the last swordspeaker, and the fourth blow fell toward the western door with Felix standing in its path.

---

Later—he would have later, he told himself, he *had* to have later—he would remember it wrong. Memory is a dishonest clerk. In the moment it was not a series of events but a single terrible chord: Wren screaming his name; Felix not running, never running, her mouth already moving around words that bent the air; the western door's ward flaring to meet his blade; and beneath it all, faint and clear as a lullaby through glass, the sound of the whole vault waking.

Felix did not dodge. Of course she did not dodge.

She stepped *into* the swing, inside his reach, close enough that the sword's arc carried it harmlessly over her shoulder, and she caught his face in both her hands—his scarred, disgraced, oath-ridden face—and she spoke directly into his eyes, where oaths keep their throne.

"Aldric Vane. Hear my vow." Her voice was quiet, and the vault went quiet to listen, every jar, every shard, every screaming soul. "I am Felix Yarrow, daughter of Lira Yarrow, whose soul sings in a jar on your shelf, and I swear to you by the last of my name: *you are not their sword.* You never were. Whatever they wrote into you, it ends tonight, and I will pay the ink. Now sleep."

The compulsion did not end. But it *hesitated*—and a hesitation, in an oath, is a crack.

Through that crack, Aldric saw the western door, unbroken. Saw the eastern one, shattered, and the shapes already pouring through it: court guards in lacquered masks, soul-brokers with ledgers chained to their belts, and behind them all, tall and unhurried, a figure wearing a face Aldric knew from a locket Felix kept against her heart.

"Ah," said the Regent, in a voice like Felix's mother's, warmed and ruined. "There you are. Right on schedule."

The alarm was still ringing. The vault was full of enemies. And Aldric Vane dropped to his knees in the wreckage of his own loyalty, his sword finally, blessedly silent in his fist, knowing—with the certainty of a man who has broken one oath and been broken by another—that the lowest point of his long, low life was not behind him.

It was here, in the glass, and it was still falling.

* * *

The guards did not rush them. That was the worst part. They spread along the shattered doorway in a practiced line, masks gleaming, and simply *waited*, the way you wait on a debt—patient, entitled, certain. The Regent folded her hands. Felix's mother's hands. Felix made a sound Aldric had never heard from her before, small and young, and Wren's arm went around her shoulders, and the three of them stood in the center of the vault with a hundred singing jars at their backs and nowhere left to run.

"Sir Aldric," the Regent said pleasantly. "Twenty years of service, and you arrive precisely when promised. The crown does reward patience." Her masked gaze—her mother's gaze—moved to Felix. "And the collateral comes home of her own accord. The ledger does love an honest entry."

"You're not her," Felix whispered.

"No," the Regent agreed, and smiled with a dead woman's mouth. "But I was. Once. Shall I show you the receipt?"

Aldric's hand found Wren's sleeve, and Wren's hand found Felix's, and the three of them stood linked in the ringing dark—knight, thief, swordspeaker—while the court they had come to rob closed around them like a ledger closing over a name.

Somewhere above, through miles of stone, the mountain itself seemed to lean in to listen.

And Felix Yarrow, last of her line, began—very quietly, very clearly—to speak.
