## 17. Every Oath at Once

The bells of the Undercourt did not ring so much as scream.

They hung from the vaulted ceiling of the great cavern like a hundred brass throats, and every one of them had found its voice the moment Aldric shattered the vault's wards. The sound came down through the lantern-lit streets in waves, rattling the glass jars in the market stalls, rattling Felix's teeth, rattling something deeper than her teeth—the oath she'd sworn false in the Regent's hall, which had been eating her name bite by bite ever since.

*Run,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had spent twenty-six years keeping her head below the tree line of everyone's attention.

So they ran.

* * *

Wren took the lead because Wren always took the lead when running was involved—something about having no past to weigh you down, she'd once joked, made you very light on your feet. She vaulted a stall selling bottled lullabies, scattered a broker's display of other people's childhood summers, and shouted over her shoulder, "Left! No—my left! Your right!"

"Your left is my left," Felix gasped. "We're facing the same direction!"

"Then left!"

They plunged into an alley between a memory-broker's shop and a stall where someone was auctioning off the last year of a dead man's life. Behind them, boots. Many boots. The court's wardens wore masks of smooth white porcelain with no features but mouths, and when they spoke, their voices came out layered, three or four speakers deep, saying things like *halt* and *surrender* and *the swordspeaker is called due*, and each phrase landed on Felix's skin like a hook seeking purchase.

Aldric brought up the rear, sword drawn, and he was fighting beautifully, the way a man fights when the alternative is thinking. But even from behind, Felix could see it—the wrongness in his shoulders, the way his head twitched toward the wardens' voices as though each one were a hand on his collar. His old oath. The crown's chain in his blood. *Deliver the swordspeaker.* He'd held it back for three streets now through sheer bloody-mindedness, and she could see the sheer bloody-mindedness running out.

"Alley!" Wren yelled, and ducked beneath a hanging lantern into a gap barely wider than her shoulders.

Felix followed. The alley smelled of lamp oil and cold stone, and it opened—mercifully, impossibly—onto one of the court's terraced walkways overlooking the central chasm, where the city's lights spilled downward like an inverted sky. For one breath there was nothing behind them but noise.

Then a warden rounded the corner, featureless mask tilting, mouth-mouths opening—

Aldric moved.

Not toward the warden. Toward *Felix*. His sword came up and his face was a war, grief and horror and compulsion all fighting for the same ground, and he said, in a voice scraped down to the nails: "I can't—I am *sworn*—"

"I know," Felix said.

And then she did the thing she had been afraid of her entire life, more afraid of than dying, more afraid of than the dark under the mountain: she opened her mouth and let the truth come out.

* * *

Here is what no one had told Felix Yarrow about her own gift, because no one in Thornvale had known enough to tell her: oaths are not commands. She had spent her whole life believing that if she ever spoke a vow aloud outside the valley, reality would simply obey her, and that this would make her a monster—or a weapon—and both of those things frightened her equally.

What she learned in the space between Aldric's raised sword and his falling blade was different. Oaths are not commands. Oaths are *arguments*. Reality is a great stubborn creature, and a swordspeaker does not seize its leash; she stands before it and makes a case so true, so completely and perfectly true, that the world cannot help but agree.

The problem was that she was trying to win an argument while running out of words.

Because her name was going. She felt it go the way you feel warmth leave a room—a little less of herself every hour since the false oath in the Regent's hall. *Felix.* It had been thinning all day. Strangers in the markets had looked through her. Wren had called her "hey" twice without noticing. And now, as the wardens closed in and Aldric's oath dragged him around like a compass needle finding north, Felix understood the arithmetic of her situation with terrible clarity:

She had maybe a handful of hours of *Felix Yarrow* left in her.

She could spend them running. Or she could spend them here.

"Wren!" she shouted. "Get behind me and do not let me fall over."

"You're going to do the voice thing," Wren said. It wasn't a question. Her face, for once, had no joke queued up. "Okay. Okay. I've got you." And she caught Felix's elbow and braced, small and strong, the way she'd braced at every bad moment since the inn, and something in Felix went quiet and certain at the touch. Found family. The phrase arrived in her mind whole, as if it had been waiting years for permission.

Aldric lunged.

Felix raised her hand—not the sword, her hand, palm open, the way you stop a horse—and spoke.

* * *

"Aldric Vane."

Her voice did not echo in the cavern. That was the first strange thing. The cavern was built for echoes—it ate whispers and returned them as thunder—but her words fell straight down and stayed where she put them, as if the air itself knew better than to interrupt.

"You swore an oath to the crown of Callowmere: to guard the prince Aldemar with your life, to obey the crown's command above your own heart, unto death." The words came easily, which frightened her—they came the way water comes to a downhill channel, as if they had been carved into her long ago and she'd only now found them. "That oath was used tonight. Not honored. *Used.*"

Aldric stopped mid-stride. Not because she commanded it. Because his oath, the actual living thing coiled around his bones, turned its attention toward her voice the way a dog turns toward a whistle.

Behind him, the wardens poured onto the terrace, white masks catching lantern light, layered voices rising: *"The swordspeaker speaks. Silence her. The debt requires—"*

"An oath given under deception," Felix said, louder, "is not broken by refusing the deception. It is broken by *seeing it.*"

And oh, that landed. She saw it land. Aldric's whole body shuddered, and the compulsion in him—the thing that had turned his sword against her in the vault, the thing that had flayed him nightly for fifteen years—shuddered with him. Because it was true, and oaths, even cruel ones, are made of truth, and truth recognizes truth the way two tuning forks find each other across a room.

But it wasn't enough. Truth alone was a key; the lock still needed turning, and turning a lock that size required force. It required *payment*.

She understood then what the gift actually cost. Not blood. Not pain. A swordspeaker paid in *self*—every vow drew on the speaker's own substance, her history, her weight in the world. That was why her mother's soul had been worth a valley's protection. That was why the Regent wanted her. A Yarrow oath wasn't powerful because of some accident of birth.

It was powerful because Yarrows had always been willing to be spent.

"Felix," Wren whispered against her shoulder. "You're doing the thing where your outline goes fuzzy. I hate the fuzzy thing."

"Hold on to me," Felix said. "Both hands. If I start to disappear, say my name. Loudly. As many times as it takes."

"What name?"

And there it was—the cost, arriving early, wearing its cruelest face. Wren's eyes went wide with dawning horror. "Felix. *Felix.* I remember your name. I remember it. Say something else quick, give me something to anchor—your favorite color, the thing you burned in the smithy that time, anything—"

"Green," Felix said. "The color. And I melted Odile's good ladle making a horseshoe and blamed the goat." Her voice cracked on a laugh that wasn't entirely a laugh. "Now hold on."

* * *

She reached for her name the way you reach for a splinter buried deep in your own hand.

It hurt. Of course it hurt. *Felix Yarrow*—daughter of Maren Yarrow, who sang while she cooked and hummed even the song Felix couldn't remember learning; apprentice to a forge; keeper of a garden her mother might never recognize; the girl who hid; the woman who stopped hiding. All of it rose up in her like heat off iron, and she gathered it, and she began to pour it into words.

"Aldric Vane," she said again, and her voice was changing now, taking on harmonics it shouldn't have had, the river-holding-still feeling multiplied a thousandfold until the whole cavern seemed to lean in. "I do not release you from your oath, because it is not mine to release, and because a man should not escape his debts—he should *pay* them honestly. So hear the accounting."

The nearest warden lunged. Wren yanked Felix sideways and the warden's blade scored the stone where they'd stood, but Felix didn't stop speaking. She couldn't stop. Stopping mid-oath, she understood instinctively, would be worse than never starting.

"You swore to guard a prince, and you guarded him with everything you had, and he died anyway, and the oath has punished you every night since for a failure that was never yours—" Aldric made a sound like a man being unmade, and she pressed on, gentle and merciless, "—because the oath was written badly. It asked you to do the impossible and called it duty. I am rewriting it. Hear the new terms."

She lifted her free hand and laid it flat against his breastplate, over his hammering heart.

"You will guard what remains to be guarded. You will obey your own conscience above any crown, above any court, above any mask. You will keep faith with the two people standing in front of you—one who has no yesterday, and one who is spending hers right now to save you. This is the whole of the charge. Everything else is released."

"*No*—" one of the wardens said, all four of its mouths at once. "*The oath is crown property. The oath is collateral. The swordspeaker has no authority—*"

"She has every authority," Felix said, "because she is paying full price."

And she paid it.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to describe what it looked like, and she never managed it well. She said it was like watching someone pay a debt in candle flames—one after another after another going out, except the candles were memories, and the person paying was smiling, which was the worst part.

Felix gave the oath her first memory of snow. It left without protest; she watched it go and felt the shape of its absence, a small pale hole where wonder used to live. She gave it the smell of the forge at dawn. She gave it her tenth birthday, her mother's hands shaping dough, the particular creak of the third stair in the house she'd grown up in. Each one went out of her and into the words, and the words grew heavier and truer and brighter, and the compulsion in Aldric's blood fought her like a drowning animal fights a rope—

—and lost.

It happened all at once. Fifteen years of nightly flaying ended between one word and the next, and Felix felt it break the way you feel a knot come loose in your own stomach, secondhand, through her palm against his chest. Aldric dropped to his knees. The sword rang on the stone. He looked up at her with his face wet and said, hoarsely, "You absolute fool of a girl. Do you know what you just spent?"

"Ask me again when I'm done," Felix said.

Because she wasn't done. The false oath was still in her, the Regent's bargain, the perjury mark, and it had been feeding on her name all this time—and now, flushed with the momentum of a successful argument with reality itself, Felix Yarrow turned her attention inward to the lie sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She could not take it back. A spoken oath could not be unsaid.

But it could be *amended*.

"To the Regent of the Undercourt," she said, and her voice carried down into the chasm, up the cavern walls, into every listening jar and every porcelain mask, "I swore a bargain I did not intend to keep. Hear my correction: I lied. I have always intended to rob you. Consider this statement my formal notice, my confession, and my declaration of war, all in one, freely given and therefore *true*—and let the truth of it burn the falsehood out of me root and branch."

The perjury mark shrieked. She felt it go—felt the coal crack apart, felt her name, or what remained of it, settle back into her bones like a bird returning to a roost. Not all of it. Never all of it again. She could feel the edges of herself gone soft and missing, whole years worn thin, and she knew that somewhere in the ledger of the court, the account of Felix Yarrow now read like a book with pages torn out.

But there were pages left.

"Felix?" Wren's voice, very small. Both her hands were gripping Felix's arm hard enough to bruise. "Your eyes were glowing. Just so you know. For future reference. Glowing."

"Good to know," Felix said, and swayed, and Wren caught her.

* * *

Aldric got to his feet slowly, like a man testing whether the ground still wanted him. He picked up his sword, looked at it, and threw it off the edge of the terrace into the chasm, where it fell glittering for a long time and made no sound at all.

"Fifteen years," he said. "Every night. And she ends it in ninety seconds and pays with—" His jaw worked. "With pieces of herself. That's not how it's supposed to work. That's not how *any* of this is supposed to work."

"It's exactly how it works," Felix said. "I just didn't know the exchange rate." She tried to smile. It came out crooked, because half her smiles were missing. "How many fingers am I holding up?"

"Three." He frowned. "Why? What number is it?"

"Three." Relief loosened something in her chest. Small mercies. The countable things remained. "Then I'm mostly here."

The wardens had stopped advancing. They stood in a ragged crescent at the mouth of the alley, masks tilted, layered voices murmuring to one another in a language of ledgers—she caught fragments: *unauthorized amendment, asset devaluation, the Regent must be informed*—and Felix realized, with a tired, fierce joy, what she was hearing.

Fear. The court was afraid of *her*.

"They think in debts," she said quietly. "That's all this place is. A ledger pretending to be a city. Every soul here is a line item, every warden is a collection agent, and the Regent—" She thought of the mask of her mother's face. Her stomach turned. "The Regent is the biggest thief of all, and thieves are always most afraid of being robbed."

"So we rob her," Wren said.

"We already did. We have my mother." The thought steadied her like a hand on a rail. The jar was gone—smashed in the fight, the murmuring light within it fled somewhere, freed or scattered, she couldn't know yet—but her mother's soul was *out*, uncollateralized, beyond the ledger's reach unless the Regent caught them first. Which meant the Regent would come herself. Which meant—

Which meant the trap was now pointed the other way, and everyone in the cavern city knew it.

"The bells stopped," Aldric observed.

He was right. Somewhere in the middle of the oath, the screaming brass had fallen silent, all hundred throats at once, and the silence was worse. In it, Felix could hear the Undercourt breathing—a vast slow respiration of stone and lantern-light, the whole cavern city gathering itself, every sold thing within it turning its attention toward one fading girl on a terrace above the dark.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the mouths of every mask and the murmur of every jar, a single voice spoke. It was her mother's voice. Of course it was. The Regent had worn it so long she'd forgotten she owned others.

*"Come and finish the argument, little oath-speaker. I'll be waiting where you first heard me sing."*

Felix's blood went cold, and then hot, because she did know where that was. Wren's bought memory—the stolen fragment, the woman's voice singing—had come from somewhere, and now the source was calling them home to it.

"She knows we're coming," Aldric said grimly.

"Good," said Felix Yarrow, who had maybe half a name left and all of a reason, and started walking toward the center of the dark. "I'm done with sneaking."
