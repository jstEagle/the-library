## 18. The Regent Unmasked

The throne hall of the Undercourt was not what Felix had imagined, and she had imagined it often in the past weeks—usually at three in the morning, heart hammering, while Wren snored beside their dying fire. She had pictured something vast and cold. What she found instead was almost domestic: a chamber the size of Odile's meeting house, its walls ribbed with pale stone like the inside of a shell, lanterns of green glass hanging low enough to touch. It was the warmth that frightened her. This was a room built to make you comfortable before it took something from you.

And on the dais at the far end, seated in a chair of black glass that might once have been a tree root, sat the Regent.

They came to a stop twenty paces out. Aldric's breathing was ragged—the counter-oath Felix had poured into him in the vault corridors still held, but it was holding the way a dam holds, groaning at every seam. Wren stood close at Felix's left shoulder, her hand nowhere near her knives because they both knew knives would be useless here. The alarms had woken the whole court. They should have been surrounded by soldiery, by brokers, by things with too many joints. Instead the hall stood empty except for the Regent, which was somehow worse.

"You brought me all this way," the Regent said, "through my markets, past my ledgers, down into my own vault. I confess I expected more subtlety from a Yarrow."

Felix's stomach dropped through the floor. *I confess.* Her mother said that. Had said that. Over burnt bread on winter mornings, over mended hems, over everything: *I confess, love, we're out of honey again.*

She made herself look at the Regent properly. Tall. Robes the color of deep water, embroidered with ledger-lines in silver thread—debt written as decoration, wealth displayed as obligation. Hands folded in the lap like a patient teacher. And above it all—

A mask.

It was porcelain-white, fine as an eggshell, and it was her mother's face.

Not approximately. Not in the way a sculptor captures likeness. It was *exact*: the slight asymmetry where the right eyebrow sat a fraction higher, the small scar through the left one that her mother had never explained, the laugh lines around eyes the mask did not bother to give, because masks do not laugh. Someone had taken a face and made a copy of it so lovingly that wearing it was either worship or mockery, and Felix could not tell which, and that uncertainty hurt worse than anything the court had done to her yet.

"Take it off," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"Should I?" The Regent tilted her head, and the mask moved with the head, seamless, horrible. "You've come so far to see what's underneath. But consider, child, what most people find when they look beneath a face. Would you rather keep the version of your mother you can blame?"

"I know what's under there." Felix stepped forward. Aldric's hand twitched toward his sword and she waved him still. "You've got her jar in your vault. Jar forty-one, humming a lullaby. Whatever you are, you're not her—you're just wearing her the way you'd wear a coat."

"Do you think so?" Something shifted in the Regent's voice—not menace. Curiosity. It was worse. "Then why does the mask fit?"

*Because you stole the measurements,* Felix wanted to say, but her mouth had gone dry, because the truth was the mask didn't just fit. When the Regent moved her hands, Felix recognized the gesture. When she paused before speaking, gathering words the way her mother always had, sorting them like coins before spending them—Felix recognized that too. You could not counterfeit twenty years of someone's habits unless you had lived inside them.

Wren's fingers found hers and squeezed once. *Careful*, the squeeze said. Wren, who three days ago had learned that her own blankness was her mother's mercy, who understood better than anyone alive what it meant to be rewritten by this place.

"The Yarrow soul is called due," Felix said. "That's what Merrin died telling me. So let's skip the part where you pretend to be cryptic. You want a Yarrow soul to break Thornvale's ward. My mother's soul wasn't payment—it was the key, and Odile never finished paying, and now the debt comes due, and the only soul left in the ledger with our name on it is mine. That's the whole game, isn't it? That's why you let us walk in."

The Regent rose. The black-glass chair seemed to sigh as she left it, and she descended the dais slowly, robes whispering against stone, until she stood close enough that Felix could smell something faint and impossible: woodsmoke and rosemary. Her mother's soap. Her mother's kitchen.

"You are your mother's daughter," the Regent said softly. "She also insisted on knowing the whole shape of a thing before she signed it. Do you want the whole shape of this, Felix Yarrow? Or would you prefer the story where I'm simply a monster, which will cost you less to carry home?"

"I'll take the truth."

"A dangerous purchase in this market." The masked face turned, considering Aldric, then Wren. "Very well. Your valley's ward was not bought, child. It was *mortgaged*. Twenty years ago your elders came to us terrified—the border wars were reaching even their forgotten fold, and oath-magic cannot defend a place where oath-magic dies. So they traded. A soul of Yarrow blood, freely given, to stand surety for a ward that would keep magic out forever." The Regent lifted one hand, and the air itself seemed to open like a ledger. Pages of light turned somewhere behind the world. "Your mother walked down that mountain road on her own feet, kissed her daughter's hair, and told no one where she was going. She believed the debt would be honored. It never was. Odile paid the interest in silence and called it protection."

Felix's vision blurred. She thought of Odile's face in the meeting house—guilt, yes, but also something older and colder underneath it. Relief that the secret had stayed buried. "And you kept her," Felix managed. "Twenty years. In a jar."

"We kept her *soul*." The Regent's voice gentled into something that was almost kind, and that was the cruelest instrument in the room. "Her body went into the ground outside your valley, unmarked, because your elders could not bear a marker that asked questions. But souls are patient things. Souls wait. And a surety-soul, unclaimed for two decades, accrues to the holder. The ward weakens as the debt sours—did your matriarch tell you that? Every year Thornvale forgets a little more of how it was protected, because protection deferred is protection denied. Soon the valley would lie open. And when it did—" the mask inclined toward Felix, "—the court intended to send the Yarrow soul home. Carried in a living vessel. Through the tree line. Into the very heart of the ward."

"A Trojan horse," Aldric said quietly. He understood oaths; he understood instruments. "You don't break a ward from outside. You carry the debt inside and let it detonate."

"Just so, Sir Vane. Though you were meant to be the courier, weren't you? Sent to fetch a swordspeaker, sworn to deliver her whether you wished it or no. I do apologize for the compulsion. I find it works better than asking."

Aldric's jaw worked. Felix watched him swallow the shame—he'd already confessed it once around a fire, the prince he'd failed, the vow that flayed him nightly—and she watched him plant his feet wider anyway, positioning himself between the Regent and Felix without being asked. Broken oaths, she thought. Even broken, they pointed true.

"But here is what the ledger did not anticipate," the Regent continued, and now she came closer still, close enough that Felix could see her own reflection warped faintly in the porcelain cheek. "Here is what even I did not anticipate. The last swordspeaker walking into my court with her name half-eaten by perjury, carrying a thief who is my own daughter, trailed by a knight whose oath I hold like a leash. You are not the plan, Felix Yarrow. You are the plan arriving *early*, in the wrong hands, with the wrong heart. And plans in wrong hands are how courts fall."

"Then take the mask off," Felix whispered. "If you're going to tell me truths, show me your face while you do it."

The Regent was silent a long moment. The green lanterns hummed. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the dark of the mountain, counting seconds the way such places do.

"As you wish," the Regent said. "I confess—I never could refuse you anything."

Her hands rose to the edges of the mask.

And Felix, in the last instant, understood—and could not speak fast enough—that a woman who had lived twenty years beneath this mountain, whose soul had been collateral and whose body had been buried, did not need a Trojan horse delivered to the tree line. Did not need a vessel. Because the Regent had just told them, in her careful ledger-keeper's cadence, exactly where the Yarrow soul had been all along:

*Walking. Talking. Signing.*

Porcelain cracked like ice on a spring river.

Beneath it was her mother's face—not the mask's flawless copy but the real one, older, thinner, gray streaking hair that had been brown, eyes the color of the ones Felix saw in her own reflection every morning. Eyes that looked at her now with twenty years of buried grief and something else beneath it, something worn smooth as river stone by two decades of servitude, and Felix felt the world tilt sideways, felt Wren catch her arm, felt Aldric step forward, felt all of it from very far away.

"Mama?" she said. It came out the way it had when she was four years old and woke from nightmares. Small. Stupid. True.

"Hello, love," said her mother. "I confess—we're going to be late if you keep standing there."

And Felix realized, with a horror that arrived whole and complete as a falling star, that her mother had not been freed by the cracking of a mask. Her mother had been *sent*. That the Regent's plan had never needed a jar carried up a mountain, because the Regent and the Yarrow soul had shared a body for twenty years—and the only reason she was standing here now, speaking to her daughter with her own stolen mouth, was that the court had finally decided it was time to go home.

Behind her mother's eyes, patient as a mortgage, the debt began to wake.

* * *

"No," Felix said. "No, that's not—Mama, look at me. Look at *me*."

Her mother was looking at her. That was the unbearable part. She was looking at her with such fierce, ordinary tenderness that Felix's knees nearly went, and underneath the tenderness, visible now that Felix knew to look, ran something else—a second current, a puppeteer's patience, the ledger-light flickering behind the iris like coins turning in a dark purse.

"She's still in there," Wren breathed. "Both of them. Oh, Felix. Oh, that's *awful*."

"It's efficient," her mother said, and her mouth smiled while her voice did arithmetic. "One soul, one vessel, one delivery. I confess, I argued for a simpler mechanism, but the court does love its symmetry. A Yarrow must carry the debt home. A Yarrow must knock upon the door. And the ward—" her mother's hand rose, touched Felix's cheek, warm, real, calloused in the places Felix remembered, "—will open for family. It always opens for family. That was its flaw, love. That was always its flaw."

"Fight it." Felix grabbed her mother's wrists. The skin was cool. Twenty years cool. "You're a Yarrow. We're stubborn as knotwood, everyone says so, Odile says so—fight it. You fought before. You hid me from the ledger, didn't you? Wren told me. You pulled a child out of the books and nobody noticed for years. You can fight."

Something crossed her mother's face—quick, real, a fish flashing under ice. "I did," she whispered. "For you. And it cost—" Her jaw clenched. The current surged. When she spoke again the tenderness was still there but it was arranged now, displayed, like goods in a market stall. "It cost nothing worth mentioning. Felix. Daughter. Come home. Bring your friends. The valley is lovely this time of year, and the ward is *so tired*, and all you have to do is walk."

"Never."

"That," her mother said sadly, "is what the ledger anticipated."

The hall changed. It happened without violence and that was the horror of it—the green lanterns brightened by one shade, the ribbed walls flexed like the inside of a throat, and doors Felix hadn't noticed swung open along the chamber's curve, and through them came the court. Not soldiers. *Customers.* Brokers with their trays of bottled years, clerks with their pens, the soul-broker from the border market with his rings and his smile, dozens of them, filing in and arranging themselves along the walls with the placid patience of people watching a transaction they had all invested in.

Aldric drew his sword. It sang out of the scabbard and the sound was very small in the shell-shaped room.

"They won't fight you," her mother observed. "Why would they? Violence is for people who lack paperwork." She turned back to Felix, and now she extended her hand, palm up, in the oldest gesture of bargain-making in the world. "One last offer, daughter, and then the ledger speaks and we are done with offers. Give me your oath. Freely spoken—that's all the court ever needed, freely spoken, that's the whole trick of your bloodline. Swear to carry me home. Swear it as a swordspeaker, and the debt rides your word through the tree line, and the ward opens to a daughter bringing her mother home, and no one is harmed at all. The valley keeps its forgetting. The court keeps its due. Everyone goes home."

"And Thornvale?" Felix's voice cracked. "What happens to Thornvale?"

"What happens to any mortgaged house," her mother said gently, "when the bank arrives."

The hall waited. Felix could feel the weight of every gaze, could feel Aldric trembling at her shoulder—not with fear, she realized, but with the strain of his old leash, the crown-oath waking in his bones now that its maker stood near, whispering *deliver her, deliver her, deliver her.* Could feel Wren's hand in hers, steady, furious, hers. Could feel, beneath all of it, her own name thin as onion skin, half-eaten by perjury, barely holding the shape of her.

And in that moment Felix understood something the ledger had not written down.

Her mother was asking for her oath. *Freely given.* Which meant the court could not take it—which meant the court could not compel her, could not trap her, could not force a single word past her teeth. Everything, all of it, the entire cathedral machinery of jars and ledgers and leashes, came down to this: a woman in a ruined mask's shadow, holding out her hand, waiting for her daughter to say yes.

They could not make her swear.

But oh, they had made her want to. That was the masterpiece. That was the whole architecture of the place. Because it was her mother's hand, and her mother's voice, and her mother saying *come home, love*, and Felix wanted nothing in the world—not freedom, not her name, not her own soul—as much as she wanted to take that hand and walk up the mountain road with her, the way they used to, before.

"Mama," Felix said, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because hiding had been her whole life and she was done with hiding. "I'm going to get you out of there. Not the way they planned. Not the way the ledger wants. My way."

"Felix—"

"I'm a swordspeaker." She stepped back, out of reach of the offered hand, and raised her voice so it filled the shell of the hall, so it reached the brokers and the clerks and the patient customers, so it reached the ledger itself wherever it turned its pages of light. "And everything I say comes true. So listen carefully, all of you, because I am about to say something true."

Her mother's face went very still. Behind her eyes, for one clear instant, the current faltered—and in that instant her mother mouthed two words, soundless, urgent, shaped only for Felix:

*Don't. Please.*

Too late. Felix filled her lungs with the cold deep air of the Undercourt, air that had been traded and sold and owed for centuries, and reached for the only currency she had left—the fraying scrap of her own name—and began to swear.
