## 19. The Last Oath Anew

The mask smiled at her with her mother's mouth.

That was the thing Felix could not stop seeing, even as the Regent's voice poured out of it—even as the court's lanterns guttered and the vault alarms still rang somewhere above them like a thousand small bells being tortured. The face beneath the silver was Maren Yarrow's face. The same crooked eyebrow. The same scar on the chin, the one from falling off the mill roof when she was nine and laughing about it for a week after.

Felix had spent twenty years without that face. She'd built whole days out of not thinking about it. And now here it was, wearing a crown of black glass, telling her in a voice like warm honey over cold stone:

"Take the jar. Take her home. Walk her through the tree line and watch the ward bloom open like a wound. That is all you must do, little oath-bound one. You may even believe you're saving her."

"You remade her," Felix said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "You hollowed her out and put your plan inside her like—like stuffing in a bird."

"I *improved* her. Souls are so inefficient on their own. All that wanting. All that grieving." The mask tilted. Behind Felix, Wren made a sound—not quite a word—and Felix heard Aldric shift his weight, armor groaning, and she knew without looking that he was still fighting himself, that his old oath to the crown was coiled around his spine like a wire, waiting for the Regent to pull.

Felix looked at the jar in her own hands.

Her mother's soul sat in the glass like a candle flame behind frosted panes, warm and gold and murmuring. Murmuring the song. *Down by the water where the willows lean,* the same four notes Wren had bought at the market stall, the same four notes Felix had hummed to herself her whole life without knowing why, because grief is patient and finds its way into lullabies.

She had come all this way for this jar. Through the tree line, past the toll of names, down the mountain's throat, through streets of sold things, through perjury eating her alive bite by bite. Her reflection had gone thin as smoke. Strangers in the market streets had looked straight through her twice today, their eyes sliding off like rain off oilcloth. She had perhaps a day left before the court finished collecting on her lie. Maybe less.

And the Regent knew it. That was the trap inside the trap. Free her mother, carry the soul home, and the Trojan horse rides into Thornvale wearing Felix's own hope. Refuse, walk away, and the Regent keeps everything—the ledger, the debt, the valley's future, and eventually Felix herself, perjury-flayed and nameless and easy to collect.

"There is a third option," the mask said softly, and Felix realized with a jolt of horror that the Regent could see her thinking. Of course it could. It wore her mother's face; some part of her mother's patterns were probably stitched into its judgment. "There always is, for your kind. Swear something else. Unmake my claim. But understand what it costs, swordspeaker. Your name is already half-spent. What remains of you is collateral—the last unclaimed Yarrow soul in existence. If you bind yourself elsewhere, truly bind yourself, my ledger cannot touch you. But neither can anything else. No archive will hold you. No memory will keep you. You will be an oath with legs, nothing more." The mask smiled again, gently, terribly. "You would be trading your mother's freedom for your own erasure. Is that not what you've been doing your entire life? I'm merely offering to make it permanent."

The lantern light wavered. Somewhere above, the bells stopped ringing, which was worse than the ringing.

"Felix," Aldric said. Low. Careful. Like a man approaching a spooked horse, or his own execution. "Whatever you're considering—"

"She's right," Felix said. "It's the only door out."

"It is *not*—"

"It's the only door that doesn't end with one of us carrying the other one home as a weapon." She turned to look at him, and then at Wren, who stood clutching the bought memory against her chest like a talisman, eyes huge and wet in the lantern light. "I've been doing the math since the vault. There's no version where we all walk out clean. So I get to choose which dirty version we take."

"That's not choosing," Wren said fiercely. "That's just losing slower."

Felix laughed. She couldn't help it—a cracked, exhausted sound, but real. "You know what my mother used to say? Before. Odile told me a piece of it once, when I cornered her. My mother said: *an oath is just love with a spine.* Everyone in this court thinks oaths are chains. Chains, cages, collars. They build ledgers out of them, they sell each other by the syllable. But they're wrong." She lifted the jar a little higher. The flame inside leaned toward her, the way a plant leans toward light. "Love has a spine. Love can *stand up*."

"The poetry is lovely," the Regent said. "Swear, then. I'll wait. I have waited twenty years; I can wait five minutes more to collect an empty girl and a hollow woman."

But Felix wasn't listening anymore. Because here was the thing the Regent had miscalculated—the thing the whole Undercourt had been built too old and too proud to see.

An oath needed two things: words and witnesses. And the court had spent this entire descent teaching her exactly who her witnesses were.

"Aldric Vane," she said.

He went very still. "Felix. Don't."

"You swore to protect a prince and watched him die, and every night since, the oath has flayed you for it, because it was a promise made to a title, to a crown, to a dead boy's bloodline—and bloodlines die, Aldric, that's the whole problem with them. Blood is just inheritance. Inheritance is just other people's choices wearing your skin." Her voice was shaking now, and she let it shake. "I don't want that. I've had twenty years of other people's choices. I want mine."

Wren stepped forward. "What are you doing?"

"Choosing my family." Felix's eyes stung. "Twice, if I have to. As many times as it takes."

She set the jar down on the vault floor, gently, the way you lay down something sleeping. Then she drew her sword—not the fine blades she sharpened for farmers, but her father's old sword, plain steel, honest weight—and drove it point-down into the stone between the three of them. The ring of it went out through the cathedral dark like a bell tolling in reverse.

"I am Felix Yarrow," she said, and the words came up out of her the way the river had risen when she first spoke outside the valley—cold water, bright iron, a self she had never been allowed to be, rising all at once. "Daughter of no one's design. Smith of Thornvale. Last swordspeaker, and glad to be the last, if this is what the speaking is *for*."

The court's magic gathered around her like a held breath. She felt it pressing in, hungry, attentive, the whole cavern city leaning close to listen.

"Hear me, ledger. Hear me, court. Hear me, whatever old hungry thing built these streets out of sold selves." She put her left hand on the sword's pommel. She reached out her right—one to either side, palms up, an offering, a bridge. "Aldric Vane. Wren. I cannot give you my blood, because my blood was spent before I was born. I cannot give you my history, because it was sold off in jars before I could speak. But everything I have *chosen*—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—everything I have chosen is standing in this room. So I swear it anew."

She looked at Aldric. "I swear to stand between you and your oath when it turns on you, as you stood between me and the court when it turned on me. Not because you are owed. Because you *asked*, and asking was the bravest thing you ever did."

His breath broke in the middle. This knight who had crossed a kingdom with his guilt strapped to his back like armor, this man who flinched from kindness like it was a blade—he took her hand like it might burn him, and then he didn't let go.

She looked at Wren. "I swear to be your yesterday. Every morning, until you have enough mornings that you don't need borrowing. I swear to remember you out loud, so the world can't forget you quietly."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and grabbed her hand hard enough to hurt, which Felix understood was the thief's way of saying *yes, yes, a hundred times yes.*

"And I swear," Felix said, and now her voice rang off the glass jars, off ten thousand murmuring souls, off the vault's ancient wards, "that my soul belongs to *this*. To these hands holding mine. To the table we eat at and the road we walk and the smithy I will rebuild and the garden my mother will plant if it takes her twenty more years to learn my name. Bind me there. Root me there. Let no ledger claim what is freely given. Let no court collect what was never collateral. My life is not a debt. **My life is not a debt.**"

The words went into the stone like roots into soil.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The lanterns burned. The jars murmured. The Regent's masked face regarded her with something almost like patience.

Then the court *recoiled*.

Felix felt it happen the way you feel a great wave suck back from the shore—the whole cavern's magic drawing away from her in one vast offended motion, every ward and chain and claim in the Undercourt trying to find the edges of her soul and finding instead Aldric's grip on one hand and Wren's on the other, finding a knot tied between three people that no contract could unpick, because it had never been a transaction. It had never been *for sale*. It was the one currency the court did not mint.

The ledger screamed. Not in words—in arithmetic, a shriek of failing sums echoing up through the vault floors, debts collapsing into each other like snow sliding off a roof. Somewhere in the archive, she knew, her mother's entry was burning itself blank. Collateral released. Claim voided. *Account closed.*

"No," the Regent said, and for the first time the honey had gone out of the voice, leaving only cold stone underneath. "No—you cannot—you are *bound*, girl, you were *born* bound—"

"To whom?" Felix asked. "Show me the paper."

The mask's hand rose—her mother's hand, elegant and terrible—and light gathered around it, the court rushing to obey its master, to seize what remained, to salvage something—

And the jar on the floor *cracked*.

Not shattered. Cracked—one clean line running up the glass, breathing out golden light like dawn coming through shutters. The flame inside stood up straight for the first time, and the humming changed. The four notes of the song unfurled into eight, into sixteen, into a whole verse, a whole voice, a woman's voice singing clear and strong and *free*, and the crack spread, and the light spread, and Felix dropped to her knees beside it with her hands still locked in her friends' hands, unable to gather it in, able only to witness.

"Mum?" she whispered. "Mum, I'm here. I'm—I found you. I'm sorry it took so long, I'm sorry, I—"

The jar burst into light.

Not fire. Light—warm and gold and everywhere, flooding the vault, washing over ten thousand jars until each of them glimmered like stars answering a sunrise, and out of the light stepped a figure: a woman, solid as bread and rain, barefoot on the stone, mid-laugh, mid-song, hair full of river-light. Maren Yarrow. Whole. Herself. Looking around the vault with the bewildered blinking of someone waking from a long dream in a strange bed.

Her gaze found Felix.

And Felix saw it—the flicker, the reaching, the mother in those eyes straining toward her daughter across twenty years of stolen time—and she also saw the moment the light couldn't hold, the moment the Regent's remaking pulled taut, and her mother's brow furrowed and her head tilted and she said, in a voice rough with disuse and wonder:

"Do I... know you, little smith? You feel like..." Her hand drifted to her own sternum, pressed there. "You feel like Sunday mornings. You feel like *home*."

The words landed in Felix's chest like a knife made entirely of tenderness.

"Not yet," Felix managed. Her tears came anyway, hot and unstoppable, and she laughed through them because apparently that was allowed, apparently joy and grief could share a table after all. "Not yet. But you will. We've got a garden. And a valley. And time—we've got time now, Mum. I made sure of it. I *swore* it."

The court shuddered around them. High above, deep in the Regent's tower, something enormous and old began to fail—the ledger's collapse rippling outward through every pillar of the Undercourt's power, every contract wobbling, every claim dissolving. The mask on the Regent's face split down the middle, silver peeling away from her mother's stolen features, and beneath the mask the woman staggered, suddenly just a woman, suddenly mortal, suddenly *empty*—the plan broken, the instrument freed, the Regent reduced to someone whose entire empire had been built on the assumption that love could always be priced.

Felix stood. She picked up her mother's hand, and her mother—confused, blinking, but trusting, the way you trust warmth—let her.

"We're going home," Felix told her. "All of us. I know you don't remember me yet. That's all right." She squeezed the hand. "I remember enough for both of us. I always did."

Behind her, Aldric let out a long, shaking breath, and she felt the wire around his spine go slack—the compulsion snapping with the ledger that had held it, the old oath dying with the debt that owned it. He sank down onto the vault floor like a man setting down a mountain he'd carried for fifteen years.

Wren was staring at her own hands, at the memory-clutch pressed against her chest, and when she looked up at Felix her face was doing several things at once, none of them orderly.

"You swore to be my yesterday," Wren said. "You absolute idiot. You beautiful, reckless—do you have any idea what you just—"

"Yeah," Felix said. "I do."

And far overhead, faint through miles of stone, the first crack of the mountain beginning to come down rumbled like the world clearing its throat.

Aldric's head snapped up. "*Run,*" he said.
