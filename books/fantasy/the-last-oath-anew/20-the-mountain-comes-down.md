## 20. The Mountain Comes Down

The first sound was not an explosion. Aldric had expected explosions—he'd imagined the Undercourt's fall as thunder, as a great shattering of glass and pride all at once—but what came instead was a *sigh*, long and low, rising up through the stone beneath his boots like the last breath of something enormous finally allowed to rest.

Then the jars began to sing.

They stood in the vault's ruined antechamber, where Felix's new oath still hung in the air like heat above a forge fire. The ledger—that vast iron-bound book whose claim had chained them all in one way or another—lay open on its pedestal, its pages fluttering though there was no wind. And all around them, on shelves that climbed into darkness beyond sight, the glass jars holding sold souls were lighting up from within, one by one, each with a small warm glow, each humming a different note.

"It's working," Wren whispered. She clutched her own recovered memories against her chest—a leather satchel of vials and papers she'd snatched from the archive in their flight—and her eyes were wide and wet. "Aldric, it's actually working."

"I know," Aldric said. "That's rather the problem."

Because he could feel it too, through the soles of his feet and the ache of his old oath-scarred bones: the Undercourt ran on debt. Every lantern, every stair, every arch of that cavern city was mortared together with obligation—the weight of ten thousand souls held as collateral, the gravity of every promise made and kept and broken down here in the dark. Felix hadn't just freed her mother. She'd defaulted on everything.

The sigh became a groan. The groan became a crack.

"RUN," Felix shouted.

They ran.

* * *

Aldric Vane had spent thirty years learning how to move in armor, and twenty of those years learning how to move in armor while something terrible happened behind him. It was, he reflected grimly as he pounded up the shaking stair, the single most marketable skill of his entire disgraced career, and no academy had ever thought to teach it.

Behind them, the vault's great doors burst outward in a geyser of light. Souls poured out—not bodies, not yet, but shapes, presences, murmuring bright things streaming upward through the cavern city like salmon going home. Where they passed, the markets below erupted into chaos. He caught glimpses as they climbed: a soul-broker clutching his ledgers as the ink lifted off the pages in curling ribbons; a stall of bottled years toppling, its contents evaporating into silver mist; a woman standing very still in the middle of the Lantern Quarter with her hand pressed to her mouth, whispering *my name, I remember my name* over and over.

"The stairs!" Felix called from ahead. She had her mother by the arm—the woman who wore Yarrow's face but carried nothing of her, who walked obediently wherever she was pointed because obedience was the only thing the court had left in her. She moved like a sleepwalker. She did not run when told to run; she had to be pulled. "Aldric, help me—she doesn't understand—"

"I have her." He swept the woman up in his arms as if she weighed nothing at all, which she nearly didn't. Souls, it turned out, were heavy, but hollowed women were not. She stared up at him with mild gray eyes and asked, politely, "Are we going somewhere?"

"Yes, my lady," Aldric said, and his voice cracked on it. "Home."

"Oh." She considered this. "I don't know that word anymore. But you say it kindly. We may go."

Above them, the ceiling of the great cavern split like bread.

* * *

Stone came down in slabs the size of oxen. Aldric threw himself and his burden sideways behind a fallen pillar as the stair ahead of them simply ceased to exist, crumbling into a chute of rubble and dust.

"Wren!" he bellowed.

"I see it!" Her voice came from somewhere in the murk, thin and furious. Then: "Felix, catch!"

Something arced through the dust—a coil of rope, snatched from some dead merchant's cart, because of course Wren had been looting during the apocalypse. Aldric would have laughed if laughing wouldn't have cost him the breath needed to keep a woman's soul-body from being crushed.

They tied off and climbed. That was the whole of the next hour: tie off, climb, duck, climb again. The Undercourt died around them in stages. First the lanterns guttered, because lantern-light down here was paid for with memory-tallow, and nobody was paying anymore. Then the market stalls folded in on themselves like paper left in rain. Last came the mountain itself, shrugging off the architecture that had burrowed into it for a thousand years, settling its shoulders, closing its wounds.

At one landing, half-drowned in dust, they found a child.

She couldn't have been more than six. She sat in the middle of the shattered stair with her knees drawn up, untouched by the falling rock in a way that made Aldric's skin crawl, and around her drifted three small lights—the soft gold of souls newly unbound, circling her like moths.

"They're going up," the girl said, in the flat tone of someone reciting. "Everyone gets to go up now. But I don't know which one is mine."

Felix knelt. Aldric saw his friend—the swordspeaker, the last oath-bound soul in creation, the girl who had just unmade an empire with a sentence—go down on her knees in the ruin and take a stranger child's hands in hers.

"Then we'll walk you up until you find it," Felix said. "Nobody gets left in this mountain tonight. That's not a vow. It's just true."

The smallest of the three lights drifted down and settled against the girl's chest, and she blinked, and suddenly she was crying, suddenly she was a child again instead of an inventory item, and Wren scooped her up onto her back without a word.

They went up. All five of them. Six counting the mother, who hummed a song she did not know she knew.

* * *

They surfaced into night air near dawn, coughing, bleeding, alive.

The cave mouth that had demanded oaths of entry stood behind them—or rather, it *didn't*. The gate had collapsed inward, and from deep in the mountain came a sound like a cathedral full of bells falling down a well, and then silence. Not empty silence. Settled silence. The silence of a thing that has finished.

Wren set the child down on the grass, where the girl immediately fell asleep as only children can—as if the world were a bed she'd been waiting all night to lie down in.

"Someone will come for her," Wren said. Her voice was hoarse with dust. "Souls going home means families getting them back. Someone's family is missing a six-year-old and they're about to have a very good morning."

"You're crying," Felix observed gently.

"I'm not. It's dust." Wren wiped her face with both hands. "There's an enormous amount of dust."

"There is," Aldric agreed solemnly. "Terrible conditions. Practically a sandstorm."

She laughed—one cracked, hiccupping laugh—and then sat down hard on the grass beside the sleeping child and put her face in her hands and cried properly, and Felix sat on one side of her, and Aldric, after a moment's consideration of what a disgraced knight's dignity was worth these days (nothing; it had never been worth anything), sat on the other.

Between them, Felix's mother watched the sunrise over the tree line with the polite interest of someone watching weather.

"She keeps asking me questions," Felix said quietly. "Small ones. What season it is. Whether the sun always rose in that direction. She asked me my name an hour ago and I—" Felix's jaw worked. "I told her. And she smiled and said it was a nice name, and asked if we'd met before."

"Felix—"

"No. Don't. Don't do the comforting voice yet." She wasn't angry; her voice was too tired for anger. "We got her out. That was the whole—everything I swore, everything it cost, we got her *out*, and she's free, and freedom apparently looks like this. A stranger wearing my mother's face, learning the world from the beginning." She looked at her hands. "The Regent used her up. Twenty years of being the court's instrument. There wasn't much left by the end, and the oath I swore took the rest."

"The song," Wren said suddenly.

"What?"

"When she was humming, in the vault. In the stair. She's been humming it since." Wren dug into her satchel and pulled out the little vial she'd bought in the market days ago—the fragment of her own childhood, the woman's voice singing. "It's the same song. The one in my memory. The one your mother sang to me before—" Wren stopped. Started again, slower. "Before whatever happened. Before the Regent sold me away and hid what she'd done. Your mother knew me, Felix. She sang to me. Which means some part of her might still—"

"Might still know the song," Aldric finished softly, "even if she doesn't know why."

They all three turned. The woman in the grass had stopped watching the sunrise. She was looking at Wren with her head slightly tilted, the way one looks at a melody one almost remembers, and after a moment she patted the ground beside her—an invitation, gracious and empty and somehow more heartbreaking than grief.

Wren went. Of course Wren went. She sat down beside the stranger who had once sung her to sleep, and the woman leaned close and said, conspiratorially, "I don't know your name either, but I think you're one of mine. Isn't that funny? I seem to have collected people."

"Yeah," Wren managed. "Yeah, you really have."

Aldric looked away to give them the moment, and found Felix looking at him instead—with that expression she'd been wearing since the vault, since the moment his old crown-oath had seized his limbs and turned him against her and she had answered not with a sword but with words, pouring her fading name into a counter-vow until his body was his own again.

"You're staring, Sir Vane," he said.

"You're hurt."

"I'm forty-eight years old and I just climbed a mountain. Hurt is my natural state."

"Aldric." She touched his arm, right over the place where the compulsion had burned him from the inside. "When it took you. When the Regent's leash yanked—you fought it. For a full minute you fought it, and every other man I've seen under oath-compulsion just... walks. What did it cost you?"

He considered lying. It was, historically, his best skill after walking-away-from-explosions. But she had given her name to the dark to save him, and a man owed better than lies to that.

"Everything I had left of the prince," he said. "The oath flayed me every night for twenty years, but the pain was... anchored, do you understand? It was tied to the memory of him. Guilt needs something to be guilty *about*. When I fought the compulsion, I felt the anchor tear loose." He shrugged, and was appalled to find his eyes stinging. "I tried, on the stairs, to remember his face. His name comes easy—princes' names are carved everywhere—but the face is gone. Just gone. I traded it for about ninety seconds of disobedience, and I'd do it again, and I think that's the part that frightens me."

Felix said nothing for a long moment. Below them, the sun cleared the ridge, and the valley beyond—the valley that had forgotten her—caught fire with morning light.

"Then we'll rebuild the anchors," she said at last. "All of us. You with the prince. Me with—" she gestured helplessly at her mother, at Wren, at the whole improbable wreckage of her life. "New ones. Better ones. Oaths we choose." A ghost of a smile. "I'm told I'm rather good at those."

* * *

They walked down out of the mountains for three days, because the Undercourt's collapse had taken the roads with it, and because none of them could bear to hurry.

The child—they'd learned her name was Pip, recovered along with her soul—turned out to be a relentless talker and an enthusiastic forager of mushrooms, approximately half of which were poisonous, a fact Wren corrected with increasing desperation. Aldric carried the mother when her feet grew tired, and she told him stories the whole way, rambling, gentle fictions about people she couldn't name in places she'd never been, and he began to understand that these weren't memories returning. They were seeds. Whatever the court had left in her, whatever Felix's final oath had spared, was starting—slowly, blindly—to grow toward the light.

On the third evening, camped within sight of Thornvale's tree line, Wren sat bolt upright by the fire.

"It's back," she whispered.

"What's back?" Aldric reached for his sword on pure reflex.

"My yesterday." She was trembling. "One of them. Just one—I woke up and it was *there*, sitting in my head like it had always been." She pressed her palms to her temples. "I'm in a kitchen. There's flour on my hands. Someone's singing—the song—and I'm maybe eight years old, and I'm happy, Aldric, I'm so *happy*, and there's a woman with dark hair telling me I've got to stop eating the batter or there won't be any cake left for—"

She stopped. Her breath hitched.

"For who?"

"For Felix's mother." Wren looked up, and the firelight made her eyes enormous. "Aldric, I remember being *her daughter*. Before the court. Before the Regent gave me away to hide what she'd done for the Yarrow child—I wasn't just hidden, I was *raised*, I had a mother and a sister and a kitchen with flour in it and I chose—" Her voice broke clean in two. "I chose to forget. I sold it myself. To survive what was coming. And now I have one day of it back and I don't know whether to celebrate or—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Across the fire, Felix's mother had gone very still, her head cocked at that listening angle again, and when she spoke her voice was soft with wonder.

"That song," she said. "You're the girl from the song."

And far above them, at the crown of the silent mountain, something shifted—a faint golden shimmer rising off the peak like heat-haze in reverse, like ten thousand souls finding their roads home at last. One light among them paused, high over the tree line, and hung there a long moment, as if considering.

Then it drifted down toward the fire.

Toward Felix.
