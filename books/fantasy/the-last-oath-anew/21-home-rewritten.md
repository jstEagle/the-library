## 21. Home, Rewritten

The tree line of Thornvale looked different from the outside.

Wren had crossed it once before, three years ago, running in the wrong direction with empty hands and a head full of holes. She remembered the border as a wall of pines that simply *ended*, like a thought cut off mid-sentence. Now she came at it from below, from the mountain road, with ash in her hair and stone dust on everything she owned—which amounted to one knife, one stolen lantern (returned, mostly), and a jar that no longer held anything but glass and a faint smell of lavender.

The valley held its breath as they approached. Wren felt it do so, which was strange, because she'd never felt a place breathe before. But the ward hung over Thornvale like a held note now, thicker than memory, and when they stepped beneath it—

Nothing happened.

That was the miracle of it. Nothing happened. No gate demanded their names. No ledger reached for them. The magic slid off Felix's shoulders like rain off oiled leather, and Aldric walked through with his broken oath still flaying him nightly, and Wren passed under those old trees feeling exactly as unremarkable as she'd always suspected she was.

"It should have caught us," Felix said for the fourth time. She kept touching her own chest, as if checking that her heart hadn't been confiscated somewhere on the road home. "The debt dissolved. The court collapsed. The ward should be *weaker*—"

"Or," Aldric said, "the ward was never a wall. It was an unpaid bill." He said it wearily, the way he said most things these days, but there was something underneath it—wonder, maybe, wearing an old man's clothes. "Odile bought your mother's soul to power it. That debt is gone now. Paid in full, by other means. And a paid-for thing stands on its own."

Wren nudged him with her elbow. "You've been saving that speech since the river crossing."

"I've been *workshopping* it since the river crossing. There's a difference."

"There is," Felix admitted. "One of them has a point."

They came down into the valley in late afternoon light, and Wren watched Thornvale appear the way you watch someone sleep—carefully, tenderly, not wanting to wake it. Smoke from chimneys. A dog barking itself hoarse at nothing. Wheat going gold in the long fields. It was, by any honest measure, a small place. It had cost them a mountain to save it.

Felix stopped walking.

She stood in the middle of the road with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, and Wren saw her face do something complicated—the look of a woman coming home to a house that had lied to her. Twenty-two years of invisibility, and here was the valley that had made her invisible, sitting pretty in the evening sun like it had never done anything worse than grow wheat.

"Hey," Wren said quietly. "You don't have to go back in."

"Yes I do."

"That's not what I asked."

Felix looked at her then, and whatever she found in Wren's face must have helped, because some of the knot went out of her shoulders. "I know what you meant." A breath. "But my mother's in here. Or—she was. Whatever's left of her is about to walk into a town that remembers selling her, and she doesn't remember any of us. Somebody has to stand between those two facts until they work out how to coexist."

"And that somebody's you?"

"Apparently I'm good at standing between things." Felix almost smiled. "It's the whole swordspeaker job description. You should read the fine print sometime."

* * *

Her mother did not recognize the smithy.

Wren watched from the doorway as the woman who had been the Regent—who had been Mara Yarrow, once, before the court wore her down to an instrument—walked the length of the forge with careful, curious steps, trailing her fingers along the cold anvil like a traveler in a museum. She was relearning her own hands. She kept looking at them as though they belonged to a stranger who'd been kind enough to lend them.

"She hums," Wren told Felix later, on the step outside, sharing the last heel of a loaf because neither of them wanted to admit they were done eating. "Your mum. When she thinks nobody's listening. Same song."

"The song from the stall." Felix's voice did something careful. "From your memory."

"Yeah. Turns out I bought a piece of your childhood instead of mine." Wren shrugged, aiming for lightness and landing somewhere near it. "Typical. Even my stolen goods aren't really mine."

Felix laughed—a real one, short and startled out of her—and then pressed the back of her hand to her mouth like she could put the laugh back where it came from. "I keep waiting for it to hurt more. That she doesn't know me. Everyone keeps looking at me like I should be grieving harder, and I *am*, I do, every morning—but there's also this—" She gestured helplessly at the forge, at the garden fence her mother had already begun repairing without being asked, because apparently some things lived deeper than memory. "She fixed the hinge. The one that's squeaked since I was nine. She didn't know why. She just said it was bothering her."

"Some things you can't sell," Wren said. "You'd know. You tried to burn that map twice."

"Three times."

"Three times," Wren conceded, "and it burned you back. Honestly, the map had more personality than half the people we met underground."

Inside, Mara Yarrow paused at the hearth, frowned at nothing, and began humming.

Felix closed her eyes.

Wren looked away and gave her the privacy of it, watching the lane instead, where the town was doing what small towns do with catastrophe: slowly, in groups, carrying bread.

* * *

Odile came on the third day.

Wren heard the cart before she saw it—one mule, one elderly driver, and the matriarch of Thornvale sitting straight-backed among her shawls like a queen visiting a country she no longer ruled. She got down from the cart in front of the forge with the care of a woman whose joints had opinions, and then, before anyone could speak, she did the thing Wren would think about for years afterward.

She knelt.

Right there in the road, in the dust, skirts pooling around her, in front of Felix, in front of Mara Yarrow's puzzled face in the doorway, in front of the growing crowd of neighbors pretending very hard not to watch. Odile Vess knelt in the dirt of the valley she had governed for forty years and bowed her head.

"Mara," she said, and her voice cracked on the name like old ice. "I sold you. I signed the paper myself. I told your daughter it was mercy. It was arithmetic." She lifted her head, and her eyes were terrible and dry. "I won't ask forgiveness. Forgiveness is a debt, and I'm done trading in debts. I'll only say: I was wrong, and I knew I was wrong while I was signing, and I will spend what's left of me being wrong in public, where everyone can see it, so no one ever does it again believing it's kindness."

The silence went on long enough for the mule to stamp.

Mara Yarrow came down the forge steps slowly. She looked at the kneeling old woman with the polite bafflement of a stranger—and then something flickered across her face, some deep stratum of self that even the Undercourt hadn't managed to strip out. She crouched down, stiffly, until she and Odile were nearly eye to eye.

"You knew my singing?" she asked. "Everyone here seems to know things about me. Did I sing? Was I any good?"

"You were," Odile whispered, wrecked completely, "the best in the valley."

"Oh," said Mara Yarrow. "Well." She patted Odile's hand, twice, the way you comfort a frightened animal. "Then I expect I'll sing again, and you can tell me if I've gotten worse. That seems fair."

She stood, and went back inside, and left the matriarch of Thornvale kneeling alone in the dust, crying with her whole body, silently, while a town watched and learned.

Wren found Felix at the corner of the forge, white-knuckled against the wall.

"You okay?"

"No." Felix wiped her face briskly, furious about it. "Yes. Ask me tomorrow." A wet, disbelieving laugh. "*Did I sing? Was I any good.* Twenty years, and she asks that like—" She shook her head. "The Regent took everything and couldn't find that. Couldn't touch that. She walked out of a cathedral of sold souls and the first thing she wanted was to know if her singing held up."

"Found family," Wren said. "Found *self*. Apparently it works either direction."

Felix looked at her then—really looked, the way she'd started doing lately, like Wren was a page worth rereading. "Speaking of which."

"Don't."

"Wren."

"I know that tone. That's the tone of someone about to make a speech."

"It's a *short* speech."

"All speeches are short right up until they start."

* * *

But it was that night, at the table, that Wren made her decision—not because of any speech, but because of soup.

Mara Yarrow had insisted on cooking, despite remembering nothing of the kitchen, and had produced by pure stubborn instinct a soup that made Aldric close his eyes and say, quietly, "I haven't tasted thyme used properly since the prince's table." Mara had glowed. Felix had pretended not to notice her mother glowing. Aldric had eaten three bowls with the disciplined dignity of a man refusing to be moved, and been moved anyway.

And Wren sat there with her bowl and thought: *three years ago I woke up in a ditch with no yesterday, and this is the first time since that a table has felt like mine.*

She set down her spoon.

"I'm staying," she announced.

Aldric raised an eyebrow. "We hadn't asked you to leave."

"Well, I hadn't formally committed. There's a difference. Ask Felix; she'll back me up on the difference between deciding and workshopping."

Felix choked slightly on her soup. Mara looked between them all with the bright, hungry curiosity of a woman collecting context clues about her own life, and finally said, "Staying where, dear? Not that I object. I just like knowing the shape of things."

"Here," Wren said. "This valley. This table. This—" she waved her spoon at all of it, the forge and the repaired hinge and the matriarch doing penance in the lane and the knight who flinched at his own reflection and the swordspeaker who'd sworn herself to two people she'd known for a season, "—whatever this is. I found you lot twice, you know. Once before I could remember it, and once after. Seems rude to waste a streak like that."

Mara reached over and squeezed her wrist, brief and warm. "Then stay, dear. We'll want help with the garden. I'm told I'm good at gardens. I intend to find out."

"You're not," said Felix. "You're terrible. You planted turnips in the shade."

"I planted them *optimistically*."

"That's not a horticultural strategy."

"It worked for twenty years of me, apparently."

Aldric cleared his throat, and when he spoke, the flaying-guilt rasp was gone from it, or at least quieter. "There's the watchtower on the ridge. Falling apart. I thought—I might stay as well. If the valley will have an oathbreaker mend its walls." He said it carefully, testing each word like thin ice.

"The valley," said Wren, "will have you fixing the roof too. Have you *seen* the inn?"

"I have seen the inn."

"Then it's settled."

Later, on the step outside, Felix sat down beside her in the dark. The ward hummed overhead, invisible and paid-for, holding. Somewhere down the lane, Odile's cart creaked past on its way back to a house that no longer commanded anything.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Felix said. "The court sold memories and names and years, and called it economy. And the whole time, the thing it couldn't price was—" she gestured at the lit window behind them, at the sound of Mara humming over dishes, "—this. Three people and a stranger at a table. Worthless as collateral. Completely worthless." Her voice caught and steadied. "Best trade I never made."

Wren leaned her head briefly against Felix's shoulder. "For someone who talks for a living, you're not bad."

Inside, the humming stopped. Then Mara's voice, calling pleasantly through the door:

"Felix? There's someone at the gate. Says he's come a long way, and that you'll want to hear what he has to say about the tower on the eastern ridge—he says it isn't falling apart at all. He says someone built it that way."

Felix went very still beside her.

"...Built it *how*?" Wren asked.

But Mara only smiled, warm and unknowing, and held the door open on the night.
