## 22. The Smithy at the Tree Line

The forge woke before Felix did, which was the way she liked it.

She'd learned that in the first weeks after they came home—learned to rise in the blue-grey hour when Thornvale was still holding its breath, when the mist lay along the valley floor like a cat too comfortable to move. She would bank the coals, coax them back to life with the bellows, and stand there letting the heat press against her face while the rest of the world decided whether to exist yet. It was the only hour of the day when nothing was asked of her. Not her name, not her oaths, not her terrible and miraculous voice. Just a woman and a fire, and iron waiting to be argued into shape.

She was hammering the spine of a farmer's scythe when the bell above her door jangled.

"Open," said a voice she knew, muffled through the wood. "I know you're in there. There's smoke coming out your chimney like you're signaling an army."

"It's six in the morning," Felix called back, not stopping her work.

"Exactly. Prime thieving hour. Nobody suspects anything at six." The door swung open anyway—Wren had never once waited for permission in the year Felix had known her—and Wren slipped inside carrying two paper parcels and an expression of elaborate innocence that had never once fooled anyone.

"You're supposed to knock," Felix said.

"I did knock. With my elbow. The door's just badly latched." Wren hopped onto the workbench, scattering a careful arrangement of tongs, and set one parcel on the anvil. "Breakfast. And don't say I never bring you anything, because today I brought you everything. There are plum buns in here, Felix. *Plum.* Old Tam's cart came up from the lowlands for the first time since the thaw, and I may have acquired several."

"*Acquired.*"

"Bought," Wren said primly. "Bought with money. Mostly. The baker gave me the last three out of respect."

"Respect."

"For my negotiation skills." She tore into the second parcel herself, and Felix let it go, because that was the shape of Wren: a person who arrived unannounced, ate half your breakfast, and stayed until you were glad of it. A year ago the sight of her had been a stranger's face across an inn table. Now it was as ordinary and necessary as the forge's warmth.

Felix quenched the scythe blade and hung it to dry. Steam curled up toward the rafters. Outside, past the smithy's open door, the tree line stood exactly where it always had—a dark hem of pines stitching the valley shut against the wider world. But it looked different now. Everything about Thornvale looked different now that Felix knew what the silence beyond it had cost, and what it had failed, in the end, to protect her from.

The ward still held. That was the strange part, the part the elders still argued about in low voices down at the meeting hall. When the Undercourt's ledger shattered—when every sold soul in that glass cathedral went spinning free—the debt that had purchased Thornvale's protection dissolved with it. By every law Odile had ever taught, the ward should have collapsed like a dropped stitch. Instead it had *tightened*, woven somehow into the valley itself, no longer a bargain struck over her mother's soul but something older and quieter. Odile claimed it was the debt's dissolution. Aldric suspected it was the Yarrow oath, rewritten. Felix privately thought it might simply be that some promises, once truly kept, didn't need coin anymore.

She hadn't told anyone that theory. Some things you keep warm in your chest like coals overnight.

"Eat," Wren commanded, kicking a stool toward her. "You've got soot on your chin and you're doing the thing where you stare at the tree line like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me money," Felix said, taking a bun. "It owes me twenty years."

"Yeah, well. Get in line behind my entire childhood." Wren said it lightly, the way she said most true things, and bit into her own bun. But her eyes flicked to the tree line too, just for a moment. They both knew what lived out there now—or rather, what didn't. The Undercourt was rubble and silence, its markets scattered, its brokers fled to whatever dark corners had room for them. Somewhere in the world there were other courts, other ledgers. Aldric said so sometimes, in the flat voice he used for truths he didn't enjoy. But not beneath this mountain. Not again.

Wren swallowed. "Tonight?" she said. "Same as usual?"

"Same as usual," Felix agreed.

* * *

Her mother's garden was visible from the forge door if you leaned out and looked left past the rain barrel: a square of turned earth behind the cottage where Felix had grown up, bordered with river stones, green now with the first stubborn lettuce of spring.

Maren Yarrow worked it every morning that weather allowed, sleeves rolled, grey-streaked hair pinned back with a pencil because she could never find the actual pins. She hummed while she worked. Always the same song. Felix had stopped flinching at it sometime around midwinter, though it still landed somewhere deep and complicated—the tune Wren had bought in a lantern-lit market stall for the price of three coins and a piece of her heart, the song from a jar in a vault, the sound of a soul remembering itself. Her mother hummed it without knowing any of that. She hummed it because it was hers, worn smooth by decades of use, the way a stair goes hollow in the middle.

Maren remembered almost nothing of her daughter.

The court had remade her, hollowed her, worn her like a mask for years before Felix tore it away. What came back up the mountain with them was a woman with her mother's hands and her mother's laugh and a great echoing absence where twenty years of loving Felix should have been. The healers said the memories weren't destroyed, only scattered—that they might return in pieces, like light through a cracked shutter. In a year, a few had. Maren remembered a house in another valley. Remembered a man named Yarrow whom she spoke of softly and rarely. Remembered, once, waking at dawn with tears already drying on her face and the absolute conviction that she had dreamed of a small girl teaching a wooden sword to fight the rain.

But mostly, each morning, she met her daughter's eyes across the breakfast table with courteous warmth and no recognition at all.

They were learning each other anyway. That was the thing Felix hadn't expected—that love could be built forward instead of recovered backward. Her mother knew she liked honey in her tea. Knew she burned bread when distracted. Knew, in the way of someone paying close attention to a stranger she'd decided mattered, that Felix went quiet when she was thinking hard and hummed back, unconsciously, when Maren sang. It wasn't the love they should have had. It was its sibling, born late, growing fast.

"Felix." Her mother waved a trowel at her from the garden as Felix carried out the wash water. "Come tell me if these rows are straight. You have an honest eye."

"They're crooked."

"That's why I asked you. Crooked rows make me feel less alone." Maren grinned, dirt-smudged, and Felix laughed despite herself, and stood a while in the morning sun while her mother pointed out seedlings with the pride of someone who had only recently learned that things could grow for her. Once, Maren Yarrow had sworn oaths that bent the world. Now she made lettuce grow the slow way, and seemed, in her quiet moments, more content than Felix had ever seen a person be.

"I'm teaching Wren tonight," Felix said.

"Mm. The small vows?"

"The smallest ones."

"Good." Her mother pressed a lettuce start into the earth and patted it firm. "Small vows are the honest ones. It's the big promises that get people hurt." She said it plainly, without weight, a gardener's wisdom—but Felix felt it settle into her anyway, alongside all the other things her mother kept teaching her without knowing she was teaching them.

* * *

Aldric could be found, most days, halfway up the eastern ridge, restoring the old watchtower stone by stone.

It had been a ruin for longer than anyone living could remember—a broken tooth on the ridge, home to crows and ivy and the occasional adventurous goat. He'd started on it in the first autumn, without asking anyone's leave, hauling mortar up the trail in sacks he'd stitched himself. When Odile asked him why, he'd said, "A knight should hold something," and gone back to work, and Odile—who had spent the year kneeling in penance at every door in the valley, who had given up her seat and taken up a spade for the common fields—had simply nodded.

He was different now. Not healed—Felix doubted oaths like his ever fully healed, any more than hers did—but *held*. The nightly flaying had stopped the moment her counter-oath unbound the compulsion woven into his old vow; what remained was the memory of it, and the guilt, and a scar tissue of habit around both. He'd traded his broken sword for a mason's trowel without ceremony. His hair was going grey at the temples. He whistled off-key.

Felix climbed up to see him on afternoons when the forge was slow, and they would sit on the unfinished parapet with their feet dangling over the drop, sharing water and watching the valley breathe below them.

"You missed the third course of the wall yesterday," he said today, when she arrived. "It's slightly crooked."

"So I hear from my mother. Crooked runs in the family."

"Crooked is fine. Crooked has character." He handed her the waterskin. Below, tiny figures moved through the greening fields; further off, the meeting hall's roof caught the sun. "Odile wants me to take students. Watchtower maintenance. She says the valley needs a standing guard again, even a small one."

"What do you want?"

He considered it the way he considered everything now—slowly, honestly, like a man who had spent enough years being lied to by himself. "I want to finish the tower," he said. "After that, we'll see. I'm told it's acceptable to not know." A pause. "Your thief tells me you're teaching her oaths now."

"She asked."

"A thief with a swordspeaker's ear for vows." He shook his head, but he was smiling. "The world isn't ready. Neither, frankly, am I. Be gentle with her, Felix. First oaths are like first loves—they mark you where everyone can see, if they know how to look."

"I know," Felix said quietly. "Mine marked me."

He looked at her then, steady and kind. "Yes," he said. "And look how well you wear it."

* * *

Night came down soft over the valley, and the forge filled with lamplight and the smell of coal-smoke and plum bun crumbs, and Felix taught Wren oaths the way her mother must once have taught her: slowly, gently, one true word at a time.

They sat on either side of the anvil, which served as their desk. Between them lay a strip of copper, a candle stub, and a bowl of river water—small objects for small vows. That was the whole curriculum. Nothing grand. Nothing that could bind reality or break kingdoms. Felix had learned, at great and permanent cost, the difference between a vow and a weapon, and she intended to pass on the difference before she passed on anything else.

"Again," she said. "From the top. What's the rule?"

"Never swear what you won't keep," Wren recited, ticking fingers, "never swear bigger than the truth, and—" she squinted— "always mean the boring ones most."

"The boring ones most," Felix confirmed. "Any fool can mean a dramatic vow for five minutes. The test is whether you can mean *this blade will cut true* every single day you use it. Now. Tonight's practice. Pick something small. Something true. Say it to the copper, and we'll see if it takes."

Wren frowned at the strip of metal, chewing her lip. This was the fifth week. So far she'd managed *this candle will burn till morning* (it had), and *the water will stay sweet* (it had), and one memorable disaster involving a vow about eggs. She took a breath, held the copper up to the lamplight, and said:

"This will remember being mine."

Nothing thundered. No wind rose. But the lamplight shifted, just slightly, the way it does when a candle leans—and the copper warmed under Wren's thumb, faintly, unmistakably, the way metal warms in a living hand. Wren's eyes went wide.

"It took," she whispered. "Felix. *It took.* Did you feel—"

"I felt it." Felix was smiling. She couldn't help it. A year ago, this gift had been a secret that ate her alive, a thing to hide in a valley built on hiding. Now it was a lamp she could hand to someone else, lit carefully, flame trimmed small. "That's a good one, too. Honest and a little vain. Very you."

"Thank you, I cultivate it." Wren turned the copper over, watching the light move across it, and for a moment her quickness went quiet. "Do you think," she said, in the smaller voice she only used at night, "that someday I'll swear something big? Like you did? Something that matters?"

"Wren." Felix reached across the anvil and tapped the copper in her hand. "You already did. You swore it just now. *This will remember being mine.*" She held her friend's gaze. "A girl with no yesterday, deciding she owns tomorrow. That's not small. That's the biggest oath I ever heard."

Wren blinked hard, twice, and then grinned wetly and stole the last plum bun by way of recovery, which was also very her.

* * *

Later, when Wren had gone home to the loft over the tavern she'd somehow talked her way into renting, and the forge had cooled to a red heart in its bed, Felix walked out past the tree line.

Not far. Never far. Twenty paces into the pines, to a flat granite shelf she'd cleared of needles, where the ward's edge shimmered faintly if the moon was right—no longer a wall, exactly. More a doorway that chose to stay closed.

She sat down on the cold stone and looked out at the dark world beyond: mountains, forests, roads, the wide restless lands where magic ran free and dangerous and real. A year ago she had crossed that line with two companions and a dying map, and everything she'd been had burned away in the courts below the world. She'd come home with less than she left with—no name her mother recognized, no inheritance but a forged one—and somehow with more than she'd ever owned.

Because that was the secret the whole year had taught her, the one she wanted to carve into the lintel over her forge door if she could find words small enough: identity wasn't a thing you found. It was a thing you *forged*. Hammer-blows, heat, quenching, mistakes, repairs. You didn't recover yourself from some vault. You made yourself, daily, out of who you chose to stand beside.

Her mother was learning to love her from scratch, and it counted. Wren had built a self out of scraps and stubbornness, and it counted. Aldric was laying stone over old wounds one course at a time, and it counted. Even Odile, on her knees in the fields, planting what she'd helped sell—it counted, or it would, if she kept at it long enough, and Felix believed she would.

Felix stood. She brushed the pine needles from her clothes. And there, on the granite shelf at the edge of everything, she swore her last new oath—not to the court, not to the ward, not to any power listening anywhere in the wide dark world. Just a woman's voice, quiet, in the trees.

"I'll stay," she said. "Tomorrow, and the day after, and all the ordinary days after that. I'll stay."

No thunder answered. No ledger shivered. The vow simply settled into her like a key turning in a lock she'd forgotten she owned—small, true, boring in the best way. Meant most.

She smiled, and turned back toward the lights of home: the forge banked and glowing, the watchtower's scaffold silhouetted on the ridge, a lamp in her mother's window, and—just visible, moving quickly and guiltily away from the bakery side street with a familiar sack over one shoulder—a figure she'd have sworn she'd locked out of the plum supply hours ago.

Felix laughed into the dark, shook her head, and started down the hill after her.

Some oaths, she thought, you keep by chasing a thief across a moonlit valley, laughing the whole way.

And some mornings, she suspected, the tree line would look different again. Roads went places. Maps got redrawn. Somewhere out in that wide world there would always be another court, another ledger, another soul worth finding—and someday, maybe soon, one of those roads would call to all three of them at once, and they'd go together, sworn and ready.

But not tonight.

Tonight, home.
