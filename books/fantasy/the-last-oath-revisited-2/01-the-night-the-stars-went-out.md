## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mule hated the pass, and Silas Blackwood had learned long ago to trust the animal's opinion over his own.

"Easy, Bess," he murmured, one hand flat against her sweat-dark neck. The wind came down off the Thornfell peaks carrying that particular cold that meant winter was thinking about arriving early, and somewhere above them, snow was already sifting through the black teeth of the ridgeline. Below, the valley of Lower Marrow lay scattered with lamplight, small and warm and utterly unaware of what his panniers held: forty pounds of cold-iron nails, three ward-plates stamped with the Regent's own seal—stolen, naturally—and a bundle of salt-charm ribbons that would sell for more than a farmhand earned in a year.

Iron-warded goods. The bread and butter of every smuggler who worked the border, because everyone on both sides of it wanted protection from the other side, and nobody wanted to pay honest prices for it.

Silas knew the passes the way a priest knows his psalms. Eleven years since Maren had turned him out of her cottage with nothing but a pack, a knife, and the words *don't come back until you're someone*, and in those eleven years he had become someone indeed: the man you went to when you needed something carried through Thornfell without the Crown's eyes seeing it. He had a room above a tavern in Marrow Cross, a reputation that opened doors and closed mouths, and enough coin buried in three separate places that no soldier's search would ever find all of it.

It was a good life. Lawless, but good. He'd built it himself, stone by stone, and not one piece of it owed anything to the hedge-witch who had raised him and then, without explanation, thrown him away.

Bess stopped dead at the bend below Widow's Crag and would not be persuaded forward.

"Bess." He tugged the lead. She planted her hooves and rolled an eye at him, white-rimmed, and Silas felt the first cold thread of real fear slide down his spine—not because of the mule, but because of the silence. The high passes were never silent. There should have been wind in the scree, the far-off chatter of the ward-stones along the border road, the low hum that travelers felt in their back teeth and never thought about until it stopped.

The hum had stopped.

He looked up, and the sky was wrong.

Not clouded—there wasn't a cloud from the crag to the peak—but empty. The stars were gone. All of them. Every light he'd steered by for eleven years of night crossings, wiped away as if a great hand had passed across the heavens and rubbed them out. Where stars should have burned there was only a smooth, dead blackness, like the bottom of a well.

"No," Silas said softly. "No, that's not—that isn't weather."

He'd seen storms eat the sky. He'd seen the auroras the old folk called the fae-fires dancing green over Hollowmere's edge. He had never once seen a sky with nothing in it.

Then the wards died.

He didn't see it happen; he felt it, the way you feel a tooth break. Along the whole length of the border, the standing stones that marked Thornfell from Hollowmere—the covenant-stones, older than the kingdom, older than any kingdom—went dark. Silas couldn't have said how he knew they'd gone dark; he'd never been able to see magic in his life, not properly, not the way hedge-witches did. But he felt it go out like a hearth-fire dying in a sealed room, and every hair on his arms stood up, and Bess screamed.

Not whinnied—*screamed*, rearing so hard she nearly pulled the lead from his grip, and down in the valley the dogs began to howl, dozens of them, every dog in Lower Marrow at once, and under their voices came a sound like a bell struck underwater.

Silas did the thing eleven years of smuggling had taught him to do with catastrophe: he got moving. He hauled Bess around by the lead, off the open road and onto the goat-track he used when Crown riders patrolled the pass, up into the rocks where a man and a mule could vanish. His heart was hammering but his hands were steady, because that was the trick of it—your hands stayed steady even when the rest of you wanted to run screaming, and if your hands were steady you could work, and if you could work you could live.

Halfway up the track, he heard footsteps on the road below. Running ones.

A figure burst out of the dark toward him—a man, stumbling, arms pumping, and even before the moonless gloom resolved him Silas knew the gait. Courier. One of the relay-runners who carried dispatches between the border forts, lean and fast and half-mad with exhaustion. This one wasn't fast anymore. He ran like a man being chased by something inside his own body.

"Help—" The word tore out of him. "You—you're Blackwood, aren't you? The smuggler. I've seen you on the lists."

Silas stepped out of the rocks, knife loose in his hand out of habit rather than intent. "Easy. Who's chasing you?"

"Nothing." The courier collapsed against the trailside boulder, and in the faint grey seep of starlight-less dark Silas saw why the man ran like that: there was blood on his lips, blood soaking through the front of his dispatch coat. "Nothing's chasing me. That's the whole—the whole horror of it. Nothing's chasing me and I'm dying anyway." He laughed, wet and terrible. "The wards broke, and I'm a warded courier, do you understand? We're all keyed to them. Every runner on the line. When they went out, it went through us like—like a blade."

Silas crouched beside him, already pulling the roll of linen from his pack, though some cold instinct told him bandages were beside the point. "Who did it? Crane's people? Or the fae?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's why—" The courier seized his wrist with shocking strength. His hand was freezing. Not cold—freezing, like riverbed stones in deep winter. "That's why you have to take it."

"Take what?"

The courier fumbled inside his coat and brought out a stone.

It was smaller than a fist, roughly oval, and even in the lightless dark it seemed to hold a glow of its own—not shining, exactly, but *present*, the way an ember is present in ash. It was warm against the night's dead cold. As the courier pressed it toward him, Silas saw fine lines crawling across its surface like veins, like script, like something written in a language that moved.

"This is—" Silas started.

"An oath-stone." The courier's voice dropped to a rasp. "One of three. It fell out of the covenant-line at Duskwatch when the wards failed. It came *to me*, do you understand? Rolled uphill against the wind to my feet. They choose. And it's chosen—" He shuddered, a full-body convulsion that left blood on his chin. "Gods, it's chosen someone, and it isn't me, and I've run six miles trying to make it not be true."

"Chosen who?"

"You." The courier shoved it into Silas's palm and folded his fingers over it, and the warmth of the thing sank through skin and muscle and into the bone, and Silas felt it settle behind his sternum like a second heartbeat. "I don't know why. I don't want to know why. But it's singing your name, Blackwood, and I mean that as near as makes no difference literally—"

"It doesn't know my name."

"It knows more than your name." The courier's grip tightened, and his eyes—wide, fevered, going glassy—fixed on Silas's face as if he were reading something written there. "There's a name under yours. Older. A woman's name. *Maren*. Do you know a woman called Maren?"

The world tilted.

Eleven years, and the name still landed like a blow. Maren, who had found an abandoned infant on her doorstep in a winter exactly this cold. Maren, who had fed him willow-bark tea and taught him which mushrooms lied and which herbs kept secrets. Maren, who had sat with him by the peat fire and never once spoken of where he'd come from or why the wards along her garden wall hummed louder when he walked past them. Maren, who had put him out of her life with two sentences and no reason, and whom he had spent eleven years telling himself he hated.

"What about her?" His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"She's written into it," the courier whispered. "Under the binding. Under everything. Whatever that stone is, whatever it opens—she's at the root of it. And now it's yours, and gods forgive me, I'm glad to be rid of it, because things that choose are things that *collect*—" He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it finished he sagged against the boulder, and Silas caught him, and felt how little of him was left.

"Stay with me. There's a shepherd's hut two miles—"

"No." The courier smiled, and it was almost peaceful. "You'll take it where it needs to go. That's what chosen means. I'm just the postman." His eyes drifted up, to the empty sky. "Look at that. No stars. My grandmother used to say the stars were the old covenant's witnesses, pinned up where everyone could see. If they've gone out..." He looked back at Silas, and now there was pity in his face, and that was worse than the blood. "Whatever you were, Blackwood, before you were a smuggler—it's not done with you."

His grip loosened. Slid free. And the courier of the Duskwatch line was gone, sitting upright against the stone as if resting, his breath making no more mist in the freezing air.

Silas knelt there a long moment, the oath-stone burning gently against his palm like a sleeping bird.

Down in the valley, the church bell of Lower Marrow began to ring—no pattern, no alarm he recognized, just frantic, clanging chaos, the sound of a village waking to a world with no wards between it and Hollowmere for the first time in four hundred years. Somewhere out in that dead-black sky, doors that had been locked for centuries were standing open.

And the stone pulsed against his hand, patient as a heartbeat, and in the deepest layer of its slow-turning script he could almost read a name, and the name was hers.

"Maren," he said aloud, and the wind took it, and Bess pulled nervously at the lead. "What did you do?"

The stone gave no answer. It only grew warmer, the way a hand grows warmer when you finally take it—like something that had waited a very long time to be picked up, and was not inclined, ever again, to be put down.

Silas looked north, toward the cottage in the high hollow where an old woman had raised a boy she'd bound to something enormous, and made the choice he'd sworn for eleven years he'd never make.

He turned the mule toward home.

* * *

He was three miles along the ridge when the first snow began to fall—dry, silent, wrong-smelling, snow that fell without clouds beneath a sky without stars—and it did not melt where it landed, and it did not stop falling, and Silas Blackwood rode into the gathering of the long winter carrying a dead man's errand and a name he had spent a decade trying to forget.

Behind him, far to the south, he never saw the signal fires kindle one after another along the Regent's road, nor the riders spurring hard through the night, all of them hunting the same thing that warmed his hand.

But the stone knew. And it hummed.
