## 2. What Maren Never Taught Me

The oath-stone had not left Silas's hand since the courier died.

He'd tried to put it in his pack. It ended up back in his palm. He'd tried wrapping it in cloth, and the cloth had slid off as if the stone shrugged. By the time the grey light of a starless dawn crept over the pass, he had stopped fighting it. The thing was warm, faintly so, like a river-smoothed pebble held too long, and when his thumb crossed its surface he felt a pulse that was not quite a heartbeat—slower than one, older.

*Thornfell*, the courier had said. Not the border town everyone called Thornfell, but something else. A name spoken like a key turning.

Silas knew only one person who spoke names that way.

Maren.

He told himself it was practicality. He was a smuggler; smugglers did not run toward danger, they ran along routes around it, and Maren's cottage sat three days off his usual line through the Ashen Fells—a route he'd walked since he was eleven years old with bundles of iron-warded lace and cold-forged nails strapped under his coat. Three days of hard walking on paths no soldier knew. That was all this was. Practicality.

But beneath the practicality, something colder coiled: the dying man had said her name the way you say the name of someone you loved or feared, and Silas could not decide which frightened him more.

The world without stars was wrong in ways his body understood before his mind did. Travelers on the low road moved like sleepwalkers. In the village of Harrow's Edge he'd watched an old woman stand in her garden at midnight, staring upward, whispering *come back, come back* to nothing. Children cried without knowing why. Dogs would not stop howling until someone beat them silent. And every ward-stone he passed—every little carved marker that had guarded crossroads and wells for as long as anyone remembered—sat dead and grey as ordinary rock.

He had built his life on those wards. Iron-warded goods, warded passes, wards against fae mischief and human law alike. All of it dead now, all of it meaningless, and Silas Blackwood, who had spent ten years being nobody in particular, suddenly carried the one object in two realms that apparently mattered.

He kept to the high trails. He did not look back.

* * *

He smelled the cottage before he saw it: char and wet ash, days old, softened by frost.

Maren's cottage had stood in its clearing for longer than Silas had been alive—thatch gone silver, walls leaning together like conspirators, a garden that grew what it liked regardless of season. He had spent eight years of childhood there. He knew the exact creak of the third floorboard, the smell of her drying herbs, the way she hummed when she worked charms into wool.

Now the door hung by one hinge. The herb racks were slashed to ribbons. Someone had taken a blade to the walls themselves, gouging long furrows through plaster and lath, hunting for hollows, hiding places. The great hearth had been torn apart brick by brick. Her cauldron lay dented in the yard, and the garden—the impossible garden that bloomed in snow—was black and trampled to mud.

"Ransacked," Silas said aloud, just to hear a voice. "Properly ransacked."

His own voice sounded thin in the dead air.

He stepped inside. The floorboards groaned their familiar groan, and the sound hit him harder than the destruction had. This had been home once—not by blood, but by choice, hers and his both, before he'd stormed out at seventeen with a stolen apprenticeship contract and a heart full of grievance.

She had never come after him. That was the wound he'd nursed for a decade. She had let him go as though he were nothing worth keeping.

He searched anyway. Smuggler's habit: check the obvious places fast, then the clever ones slowly. Under the collapsed bed. Behind the loose stone by the chimney—he checked, though whoever came before him had clearly found it first; the cavity gaped empty. In the rafters, where she'd kept jars of preserved light that now glowed for no one. Nothing.

Then he saw the hearth.

They had torn it apart looking for something, yes—but in tearing it apart they had missed what they made. One brick, the keystone of the arch, had cracked down the middle, and inside the crack something pale showed. Silas pried it out with his knife. A folded packet of oilskin, wedged where only a hand that knew the cottage's bones would ever think to look—or a hand that had laid the bricks itself.

Inside was a journal, small and swollen with damp, bound in green leather he recognized. He'd seen it on her worktable a hundred times. He'd assumed it was receipts.

The first pages were ordinary enough. Herb notes, weather charms, complaints about a neighbor's goats. Then, dated the winter of his birth, in a hand pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*The child is born tonight. The covenant is failing faster than the elders will admit. I have done what the old texts demand, what I swore to do before I understood what swearing costs. He is bound. He is the keystone. May the gods forgive me, because the stones will not.*

Silas read it four times. The words refused to rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

*He is bound. He is the keystone.*

Bound. Like a ward. Like a lock. Like cargo strapped beneath a smuggler's coat.

He turned pages with hands that had gone strangely steady—that smuggler's calm, the one that came over him when everything burned. Fragments followed, years of them:

*He walks the border roads and does not know why he has never once been troubled by the old wards. Of course not. They know him.*

*The boy asked me today why I never taught him the deep craft. I told him talent. I am a coward and a liar and I will answer for it.*

*I watched him leave. I let him go. A keystone set in a wall must be allowed to sit where it sits. And perhaps—if he is far from the border when it fails—*

The last entry was recent. Days old. The ink still sharp:

*If you are reading this, then the stars are gone and my time is spent. I'm sorry, Silas. For all of it—for the binding, for the silence, for letting you believe yourself unloved because love was the one thing I couldn't afford to give you plainly. Take the stone to Thornfell. Trust neither crown nor court. Trust the oaths you keep. — M.*

He sat down in the wreckage of her kitchen and laughed, once, a horrible sound.

So that was it. His whole childhood—the hedge-craft she wouldn't teach him, the rules, the curfews keyed to moonrise, the way she'd flinch when soldiers rode past, the strange tenderness that always stopped just short of warmth—all of it was architecture. He hadn't been a boy raised by a witch. He'd been a stone set in a wall and taught to walk.

And yet.

*Trust neither crown nor court. Trust the oaths you keep.*

Even now, even betrayed, she was steering him. And underneath his fury, treacherous and unwelcome, lived the memory of her hands bandaging his knees, her voice reading to him through fevers, the night she'd stood between him and a wolf on the fell road armed with nothing but a stick and a word. You did not do those things for a keystone. Or maybe you did. Maybe he no longer knew the difference, and that was the worst of it—that he could not untangle the lie from the love, and suspected he never would.

Outside, a jay screamed. Once. Twice. Then cut off mid-note.

Silas went very still.

Birds didn't cut off mid-note unless something had taken the note from them.

He moved to the window, slow, and looked out across the ruined garden to the tree line. Horses. Four of them, picketed in the shadows, and men moving among the trees with the particular economy of professionals. Red-and-black livery. Crane's colors—the regent's own. Not tax collectors, not press-gangs. Ward-hunters, if the stories were true, the regent's new company formed within a day of the stars going out, riding the border like carrion birds.

One of them turned his head, and even at forty yards Silas saw the moment he spotted the broken door.

No time to pack. No time to think. The journal went into his coat beside the stone—*the stones,* he corrected himself, *there will be others*—and Silas went out the back, through the gap in the bramble fence he'd worn smooth escaping chores at fourteen, and dropped into the ravine behind the cottage.

* * *

The soldiers reached the clearing above him as he reached the streambed. He heard their voices, flat and carrying: *"Door was broke when we got here—" "Search the ravine. He'll have gone to ground."*

They knew someone might come. Which meant Maren's name was already in their mouths, which meant she was alive when they took the cottage, which meant—

He shut that door in his mind and ran.

Not north, not south, not anywhere a tracker would look. Silas ran the smuggler's country, the secret geography stitched into his feet by ten years of illegal trade: the dry wash that looked impassable and wasn't, the badger sett wide enough for a thin man and his fears, the limestone sink where the hill swallowed a stream and gave it back a mile on. He moved through the fells like water finding cracks, and the land, dead-warded and starless, seemed almost to lean toward him—as though it remembered whose bones anchored it.

Twice he heard horses above him and froze in a hollow with his heart hammering and the stone pulsing against his ribs like a second heart answering, and twice the riders passed on, drawn elsewhere, as if something had nudged their attention aside. He chose not to wonder about that.

By full dark he had put six miles of impossible country between himself and the cottage, and he allowed himself to stop in the mouth of an abandoned quarry, back to cold stone, breath steaming.

The journal lay open on his knee. He read the last line again by feel more than sight, the letters memorized now, branded in.

*Take the stone to Thornfell.*

But Thornfell was a name out of a dying man's mouth and a witch's book, and between him and it stood the regent's hunters, a court of fae he'd been raised to fear, and a question that gnawed worse than hunger: if Maren had bound him at birth, what else had she bound? What else was he?

He closed the journal. Ahead, far to the east, where the border met Hollowmere, the darkness had a texture to it—an absence with weight, like a held breath. The wards were dead, the border open, and every instinct that had kept him alive as a smuggler said: *stay away, stay small, stay human.*

Every instinct except the stone in his hand, which pulled gently, patiently, toward the dark—like a compass needle remembering north.

Like a keystone remembering its arch.

Silas stood, shouldered his pack, and turned his face toward Hollowmere.
