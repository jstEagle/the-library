## 3. The Court of Hollow Bells

The border between Thornfell and Hollowmere had never been a line you could see. Maren had told him once, in one of her rare instructive moods, that it was a *feeling*—a coldness at the back of the neck, a sense of being watched by something patient. Silas had crossed it a dozen times as a smuggler, iron-warded goods on his back and a charm against notice in his pocket, and he had never felt anything but the ordinary dark of the passes.

Now the wards were dead, and the border was not a feeling. It was a wound.

He knew it before he saw it. The air changed three miles out from the old crossing—a pressure like the moment before thunder, but constant, unrelenting, pressing against his ears and his teeth. The trees grew wrong past that point: alder where pine should be, their bark pale as bone, their leaves turning slowly though there was no wind. And everywhere the silence. Not emptiness—*silence*, deliberate and total, as if the whole forest were holding its breath to hear what he would say next.

Silas walked anyway.

He had considered every other option through the long grey day. Go back to Thornfell and let Crane's soldiers find him—no. Take the oath-stone and bury it somewhere no one would ever dig—his hand kept closing around it in his pocket anyway, warm as a sleeping bird, and something in him recoiled from the thought as if he'd considered drowning a kitten. Find another hedge-witch to read the journal—but the journal was written in Maren's shorthand, the private cipher she'd invented for her own recipes, and Silas suspected he was the only living person who could read it fluently. He had spent his childhood transcribing her grimoires as punishment for small sins. She had called it education. He'd called it slavery. Now the cipher was the only inheritance he had, and it said things like:

*The keystone must not learn what it carries until the carrying can no longer be undone.*

And beneath that, scratched in a different ink, hurried, almost angry: *I was wrong to wait this long. Forgive me, little wren.*

Little wren. His childhood name, the one she'd used when he was six and feverish and afraid of the dark. Seeing it in her handwriting did something to his chest that he refused to examine.

So: Hollowmere. Because the courier had died whispering her name, because the stone had grown warmer with every mile east, because—if the journal's wild fragments could be trusted—the covenant had two ends, and whatever broke it had broken from both sides at once. Someone in Thornfell. Someone in Hollowmere. And Silas Blackwood, smuggler, liar, runaway apprentice, was apparently standing on the seam.

He crossed at dusk, or what passed for dusk under a sky still scummed with cloud. There was no threshold, no shimmer, no wall of thorns. One step he stood in Thornfell's dead ward-light; the next, the world tilted like a ship changing tack, and the smell of the air went green and old and sweet, and he understood why people had once needed wards to keep this place out.

Hollowmere did not look hostile. That was the trap of it. It looked like home—the same rolling country, the same grey stone, orchards heavy with fruit too perfect, a village ahead with lamplit windows and woodsmoke rising straight as drawn thread. Everything was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: composed, intentional, watching him back.

The bells began as he reached the first cottage.

They came from everywhere at once—from the well, from the orchard, from inside his own skull—a peal of deep bronze notes with no church and no tower to explain them. Each toll pressed down on him like a hand on the crown of his head. By the third note he was on his knees in the lane, the oath-stone burning through his coat, and the villagers' doors had all opened at once, and the people stepping out of them were smiling.

That was worse than the bells. They smiled the way the trees turned their leaves—with intention—and none of their eyes moved as they walked toward him across the frostless grass.

"Thief," said one pleasantly.

"Smuggler," said another, correcting, "which is a kind of thief with better posture."

"Iron-carrier," said a third, and now there was nothing pleasant anywhere in the lane. "Iron-warded goods through the passes, year upon year. Did you think we didn't feel every ounce?"

Silas got one knee under himself and stopped there, because standing seemed suddenly like a very bad idea, and because his hands were full of stone-light. The oath-stone had begun to glow through the wool wrapping, a gold like candlelight through honey, and every fae in the lane had gone still around it the way water goes still around a dropped coin.

"Well," said the first villager, and her smile finally reached her eyes, which were the colour of pond ice. "*There's* a thing we haven't seen since the wards fell."

* * *

They did not drag him. That was somehow more frightening. They walked him—gently, courteously, offering him wine from a flask he refused—up out of the village along a road that hadn't been there when he chose his direction, through country that rearranged itself behind them so that he could never have found his way back alone. The bells tolled overhead at intervals, each toll making the stone against his chest pulse in answer, call and response, question and question.

The court, when it resolved out of the evening mist, was not a castle. It was a ruin wearing a castle's memory—towers of black glass that cast shadows pointing the wrong way, halls open to a sky in which the stars should have been and weren't, courtyards hung with bells of every size, all of them silent now, waiting. Fae filled the galleries in shapes mostly human and occasionally not: tall figures with antlers of white coral, children who cast adult shadows, an old woman whose gown was made entirely of keys.

At the centre of the great hall, on a dais of drowned marble, sat a lady on a throne of woven willow.

She was younger-looking than any queen Silas had imagined and older-looking than the hills, both at once, in the way that hurt to look at directly. Her hair was the silver of birch bark; her gown moved like slow water. She did not wear a crown. She wore instead a thin band of iron at her throat—an irony so naked and deliberate that Silas stared at it before he could stop himself.

"You notice," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way the bells had. "Good. Most humans see only the face. Iron, gentlemen and ladies of my court, worn willingly, in mourning for what our grandmothers built. Lady Vesperine of the Hollow Bells. And you are the keystone's carrier."

"Not its keeper?" Silas managed.

Her expression flickered—something quick and sharp and almost human, surprise maybe, or delight. "Ohh. You've been reading your witch's book." She rose, and the whole court rose with her, a sound like wind through wheat. "Bring him light. Bring him food he can safely eat. Bring him—" she paused at the foot of the dais, studying him with eyes the grey-green of deep lake water, "—the truth, for once, since apparently no one in either realm has tried that in a hundred years."

* * *

They fed him in a side chamber hung with more of the silent bells, small ones here, each engraved with names. Vesperine dismissed her court and sat across from him herself, pouring wine from a jug into two cups, drinking from hers first without ceremony.

"The covenant," she said, "was never a wall. Whatever your Thornfell histories tell you—we are not the flood they held back, and they were not the sheep behind the fence. It was a marriage. Two realms, one root system. Our magic bled south to keep their crops alive; their iron discipline and their stubborn mortal order bled north to keep us *anchored*. Without them we drift. You may have noticed the weather."

"The winter," Silas said.

"Our magic is unravelling and running everywhere at once, like a loom with the warp cut. Frost in high summer. Stars snuffed out because the night sky itself was part of the binding—your witch-mistress helped stitch that, did you know? A hundred years ago. She was young then. She is very old now, for a hedge-witch." Vesperine turned her cup slowly. "The wards didn't fail, Silas Blackwood. They were broken. From both sides. On the same night. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That someone in your court worked with someone in Thornfell."

"Yes." Something moved behind her composure then, brief and bitter. "Someone of mine. Someone I trusted enough to give a task of great delicacy, believing their grievance smaller than their loyalty." She set the cup down. "I don't know who yet. The bells know—they always know—but the bells have gone quiet since the stars went out, and I cannot make them sing again. But the stones remember everything. Every oath ever sworn over them, every hand that shaped the covenant, every traitor's name spoken within earshot of the binding."

"And you need a human to carry them," Silas said slowly, "because the stones won't answer fae hands anymore. Not while the bridge is down."

Vesperine smiled, and this time there was nothing pleasant in it at all, and he liked her enormously for not pretending otherwise.

"You *are* your mistress's student. Yes. Three oath-stones, scattered when the wards collapsed—one came south with a dying courier, one lies in a chapel that flooded forty years ago and has been flooding further ever since, and one—" she watched him carefully— "is wherever Maren hid it. Which means it is wherever Maren is. And Maren, keystone-carrier, is in Thornfell, in the keeping of your regent, who wants the stones for reasons I promise you are worse than anything your smuggler's imagination has produced."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, one of the great bells gave a single soft note, unbidden, like a dog dreaming.

"So here is my offer, and I will make it honestly because I am told honesty disarms humans: help me retrieve the second stone from the drowned chapel, and I will give you safe passage back to Thornfell, my protection against Crane's soldiers, and every scrap my court knows about the binding—including how a keystone might survive the reforging. Refuse, and I keep you here comfortably for as long as it takes you to change your mind. My court is very comfortable. Ask anyone." She leaned forward. "But decide quickly, Silas. The chapel lake froze last night for the first time in living memory. In a week the freeze reaches the bottom, and the stone goes beyond anyone's reach—mine, yours, anyone's—for a hundred years."

Silas looked down at the oath-stone in his lap. Warm. Patient. Almost breathing.

"Why me?" he asked quietly. "You have a whole court. Why does it have to be a smuggler who can't swim particularly well?"

Vesperine's face did something complicated—grief and amusement fighting to a draw.

"Because the stone in your pocket has been calling your name since it left Thornfell," she said, "and I have half a hundred listeners in this room, and every single one of them heard it before you did. It isn't asking you to carry it, Silas Blackwood. It's asking you to come *home*."

Outside, the starless dark pressed against the black-glass towers, and far off across the drowned country, faint as memory, the chapel bell began to toll.
