## 4. Drowned Chapel, Rising Water

The lake did not look like a place where anything holy had ever happened.

It lay in a bowl of grey hills, skin already stiffening with ice at the edges, the color of old pewter and old grief. Bramble-ashe stood on the shore with his arms crossed and his thorn-crowned shadow stretched long behind him, and he regarded the water the way a man regards a debt he has been avoiding for thirty years.

"The chapel went under before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath," he said. "The monks built it on a spring they should have left sleeping. The spring drowned them for their pride. Now it keeps what they kept."

"And the stone is inside."

"Inside." The fae scout's mouth twisted. "You say that word as if the chapel has an outside still. The water took the walls, the bells, the bones. There is no inside anymore. Only down."

Silas crouched at the shoreline and touched the ice. Thin here — a finger's thickness, maybe two — but the cold coming off the lake bit through his glove like teeth. Three days ago he had never heard of oath-stones. Two days ago a dying courier had bled one into his hands along with Maren's name. One day ago Lady Vesperine of the Court of Hollow Bells had smiled at him with too many teeth and said, *bring me the second stone, smuggler, and we shall see about keeping you alive.*

He was beginning to understand that everyone in this war intended to keep him alive only so long as he remained useful.

"How deep?" he asked.

Bramble-ashe shrugged, a gesture like branches shifting in wind. "Deep enough that light forgets the way. Deep enough that I will not follow you. My people and drowned places have an old disagreement." He looked away when he said it, and Silas marked the flinch but did not yet understand it.

"You're my guide," Silas said. "That was the arrangement."

"I guide you to the lake. The lake guides you further, if it's minded to." Bramble-ashe's green eyes flicked to him, then away again. "Which it may not be. The stones answer to oaths kept, not power held. Remember that, if you remember nothing else from me."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," the scout said softly, "that the stone in your pocket chose you, and I do not think it was because of your strength."

* * *

Silas built the dive rig the way he'd built everything in his life: out of stolen parts, smuggler's cunning, and stubbornness. A lead weight belt taken off a dead courier's horse. Greased leather to hold what warmth a body could hold. A rope tied around his waist with three pulls meaning *haul me up, I'm done*, and a knife at his hip in case the rope itself became the problem.

Bramble-ashe watched all of this with something that might have been pity.

"When the freeze comes," Silas said, "how long do we have?"

The scout tilted his head back. Above them the sky hung low and colorless, and somewhere past the hills the long winter was walking toward them — Silas could feel it now, a pressure behind his eyes like a storm building in his own skull. The wards were dead; the covenant between the realms was dying; and the winter that had begun as frost on windowpanes was gathering itself into something older and crueler.

"A day," Bramble-ashe said. "Perhaps less. When the lake seals, it seals whole. Ice a man could march an army across. Nothing goes down. Nothing comes up." He paused. "I would think on that, if I were you."

"I've thought on it."

"And?"

Silas pulled the first oath-stone from his shirt, where it hung against his sternum on a cord. It was warm. It had been warm since the courier died holding it, warm in a way that had nothing to do with heat — warm the way a hand is warm, the way a promise is warm. Maren's name had been the last word the man spoke. Maren, who had raised him. Maren, who had lied to him for nineteen years, or loved him truly, or both, and Silas no longer knew which truth frightened him more.

The stone pulsed faintly against his palm now, like a heartbeat quickening.

"It wants to go down there," Silas said.

"Of course it does. They were three once. What was sundered remembers being whole." Bramble-ashe crouched beside him, and for a moment his wild face was almost kind. "Listen to me, human. The chapel is not empty. Drowned things keep their offices even after death — that is what makes them drowned things rather than merely sunken ones. The monks swore oaths in that place, and the water holds the shape of every vow ever spoken under its roof. If you go down lying to yourself, it will know. And it will let you drown politely, without any fuss at all."

"That's comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be true." The scout rose. "Go. Before the sky finishes forgetting how to make daylight."

* * *

The cold hit him like a wall of hands.

Silas went under at the lake's center, where Bramble-ashe swore the bell tower had stood, and the water closed over him with a sound like a door. For ten heartbeats he could do nothing but hang in the grey and fight his own body — the spasm of the lungs, the animal screaming need to breathe — until the smuggler's discipline he'd spent nine years forging finally took the wheel. *In through the mouth, slow. Count. Move.*

He hauled himself down the rope, hand over hand, into the dark.

The chapel found him before he found it. First a suggestion of geometry below — angles where angles shouldn't be — then the bulk of it rising out of the murk, walls furred with pale silt-weed, arched windows glowing faintly with some light that had no source. Fish hung motionless in the doorways like congregations turned to stone. And everywhere, everywhere, the feeling of being *watched*, patient and sorrowful and vast.

His rope ran out forty feet down, short of the chapel roof. Silas untied himself.

It was the bravest and stupidest thing he had ever done, cutting loose from the world above, and later he would understand that this was the moment the stone began to judge him — not his strength, not his magic, which was small and half-asleep and bound up in whatever Maren had done to him at birth, but his choice. He let go of the rope. He sank.

Through a broken window, into the nave.

The interior was a cathedral of suspended sediment, motes drifting in shafts of sourceless gold. Pews stood in rows, soft-rotted, and above the altar — intact, impossibly, hanging upside-down from a vault that should have fallen centuries ago — swung the great bronze bell, silent, its clapper bound with chains of living iron that gleamed like wounds in the green water.

And beneath the altar, set into the stone itself, a hollow shaped exactly like the stone against Silas's chest.

He swam toward it, and the cold reached into him then, deeper than flesh. Not the lake's cold. An older cold. The water thickened around his limbs like syrup, like grasping fingers, and far above him the last coin of surface-light shrank to a pinprick as the freeze came early — he felt it, a tremor through the whole lake, the sky slamming shut over his head — and panic rose in him black and total.

*You are alone in a drowned church under sealed ice and you are going to die here and no one will ever—*

The stone burned hot against his chest. And into the roar of his terror, quiet as a hand laid on a shoulder, came a voice that was not a voice:

***What do you keep?***

Not *what do you want*. Not *what can you offer*. The monks' question, the water's question, the question the covenant itself had been built to ask: what promises have you made, and which of them have you honored?

Silas hung in the freezing dark, lungs burning, and searched himself with terrible honesty.

He had kept his word to fences who paid him. Kept his routes secret when torture-by-hanging waited for informers. He had carried a stranger's package through a blizzard rather than abandon it, because he'd said he would. Small vows. Lawless vows. But kept — kept, kept, kept, a ledger of small faithfulness written in a life he'd thought was worth nothing.

And one vow broken: he had promised Maren he'd come home by midwinter. He had run instead.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, and did not know if he meant it to her or to the water or to himself. *I'm going back for her. Whatever she did to me — I'm going back. That I swear. Even if she never asks it of me.*

The cold released him all at once, like a fist unclenching.

The hollow beneath the altar filled with light. The second oath-stone rose out of the stone as gently as a bubble rising, drifting to meet the first where it hung against Silas's heart — and when the two touched, the sound went through him like a bell struck underwater, a single pure note he felt in his marrow, in his teeth, in the binding Maren had laid on him at birth, which stirred for the first time in nineteen years like something rolling over in sleep.

*Two,* the not-voice seemed to say. *One remains. And winter does not wait for the worthy.*

* * *

He broke the surface into air gone knife-bright and utterly still.

The lake had sealed. A lid of new ice stretched shore to shore, milky and thickening even as he watched, and beyond it the hills wore white to their waists. Bramble-ashe stood on the bank with both fists pressed to his mouth, and when Silas dragged himself out of the water hole he'd shattered climbing — ice had cracked upward around him like blooming flowers, like the lake itself deciding, at the last possible breath, to let him live — the fae scout did something Silas had never seen his kind do.

He bowed. Low. To a mud-covered, half-frozen human smuggler.

"You went down lying to yourself," Bramble-ashe said, "and came up honest. I have seen knights fail that test." His eyes dropped to the two stones at Silas's throat, burning together like twin coals, and something moved across his face — grief, or guilt, or fear — too fast to name. "The third stone lies in Thornfell. In Crane's country. Behind Crane's walls."

Silas got his breathing under control. His hands wouldn't stop shaking, but his voice, when it came, surprised him by being steady.

"Then we'd better move fast. Because I just realized something, standing in that church." He met the scout's eyes. "The regent doesn't want the stones. He wants *me* — the keystone. And Vesperine knows it. She didn't send me to find the second stone." He touched the coals at his throat, warm as a held promise. "She sent me to be bait. And I walked straight into the trap wearing it."

Above them, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall.
