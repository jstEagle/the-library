## 5. The Regent's Bargain

They took him at the ford at Graymoss, where the river ran thin as wire under the new ice and the smugglers' path crossed the king's road for forty yards of open ground. Silas never saw the soldiers until the nets came down—weighted nets, iron-threaded, the kind used for snaring ward-beasts, not men.

He fought anyway. He was proud of that later, in the way a man is proud of a scar: it proved he had tried. He got one arm free, got his knife into someone's thigh, felt the second stone through his shirt like a coal against his skin—and then a gauntleted fist found the base of his skull, and the world went white, then black, then nothing at all.

* * *

Regent Aldous Crane read reports the way other men drank wine: slowly, with appreciation, alone.

He sat in his study at Fort Marrow with the shutters open to a sky the color of dirty pewter, snow drifting past the sill in long silent curtains, and he read. The wards along the Thornfell border had failed in a single hour on a single night—the work of a decade, undone in an evening—and half his council wanted to call it catastrophe.

Crane called it opportunity. Opportunity simply required patience, and patience required paper.

The report on his desk concerned a smuggler named Silas Blackwood, twenty-three years of age, apprenticed at seven to one Maren Collier, hedge-witch, deceased or fled, whereabouts unknown. The boy had been taken at Graymoss carrying two items of interest: an iron-warded courier's satchel, and an oath-stone of pre-Covenant make, warm to the touch, which had burned the sergeant who handled it barehanded.

Crane set down the page and looked out at the falling snow.

There had been a time—he was young then, a captain on the border, before the regency, before the crown had been pressed into his hands like a hot coal—when Aldous Crane had stood on the wall at Hollowmere's edge and watched a fae knight bow to a human warden across a table no wider than a coffin lid. Mutual survival, the old covenant said. Two realms bound together because neither could live without the other.

He had believed it once. Then he had buried three hundred soldiers in a single autumn when Hollowmere's court decided its obligations were negotiable, and he had stopped believing, and he had started planning instead.

The wards had been a wall. Walls kept things out—but they also kept you blind behind them, waiting for the next breach, the next autumn, the next three hundred graves. What Crane intended was different. A covenant reforged as a weapon pointed one way only: every drop of magic flowing from Thornfell into Hollowmere severed, reversed, harnessed. Let them feel what dependence meant. Let them starve politely at a locked door for a change.

All it required was the stones. And, apparently, a smuggler.

"Bring him up," Crane said to the guard at the door. "And have the kitchen send something hot. If we mean to buy a man, we oughtn't let him freeze while we haggle."

* * *

Silas woke on a cot in a room with a fire in it, which frightened him more than a cell would have.

Cells were simple. Cells said: you are caught, endure. But this room had clean linen, a basin, a plate of bread and stewed pears going cold, and a window with a view of the fortress courtyard where soldiers drilled in the snow like patient clockwork. Somebody wanted him comfortable. In Silas's experience, people who wanted you comfortable wanted something worse.

The door opened without a knock, and a man entered whom Silas knew by reputation the way you know a storm front: Regent Aldous Crane, tall and gray and precise, dressed plainly enough to be insulting, with eyes the color of a winter river—calm on top, and moving very fast underneath.

"You're the keystone," Crane said, without greeting.

"I'm a smuggler."

"You're a smuggler," Crane agreed, drawing the chair to the fire and sitting with the ease of a man who owned the chair, the fire, and possibly the cold itself. "You're also the living anchor of a covenant older than Thornfell's crown, and you've been carrying one of the three oath-stones around your neck since the night the stars went out. Shall I go on? I can. I've had eleven days and a great deal of tea."

Silas kept his face still. It cost him. *Keystone.* The word landed in him like a hook finding meat.

"What do you want?"

"The truth, first, so we understand each other." Crane leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the regency fell away and there was just a tired soldier talking across a fire. "I broke the wards, Blackwood. Not the whole failure—that was the fae traitor's doing, though I suspect you know more about that than you think—but the timing, the weakness I seeded in them over six years. I needed them down before I could rebuild them properly. You've been told, I imagine, that this makes me a monster. I'd ask what the wards ever did for you. Did they feed the villages east of the pass last winter? Did they stop the tithe wagons? A wall is a promise that the suffering stays on the other side. I intend to end the arrangement entirely."

"And if they starve?"

"They should have thought of that before they treated us as a larder." No heat in it. That was the terrible thing; there was no heat anywhere in the man. "But you didn't come here to debate policy. You came here because you want answers. So here is my offer."

Crane counted it off like coins laid on a table.

"Legitimacy. A pardon under the great seal—everything you've run through those passes is ash, legally speaking, and everything you will run is mine to protect. Safety. Fort Marrow has walls no fae trick has ever cracked, and inside them, no court, human or otherwise, touches you. And third—" the pause was surgical, "—Maren Collier."

The name went through Silas like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"She lives," Crane said, watching him with those fast gray eyes. "She is held somewhere you will never find without me, in a condition you would not enjoy seeing but might be able to change. She spent herself shielding you, boy. Since the day you were born, apparently, which is a story I confess I'd very much like to hear myself. She is dying of it now, slowly, in the dark, and I am the only man in either realm who knows exactly where 'the dark' is."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, boots crunched in snow, keeping their clockwork rhythm.

"All of it," Crane went on softly, "for the stones. Bring me all three, help me raise the new covenant, and walk away a free man with a clean name and the woman who raised you alive in a house with a garden. Refuse—" he spread his hands, almost apologetic, "—and I take the stones off your corpse, because you will not leave this mountain otherwise, and Maren dies in the dark having gained nothing by her sacrifice. I don't say that as a threat. I say it as arithmetic."

Silas looked at the bread going stale on the plate, at the fire, at the window full of falling snow, and understood with awful clarity that Crane had built this offer the way he built everything: load-bearing in every direction. Every piece of it true. Every piece of it good. Legitimacy. Safety. Her.

*Say yes,* whispered some cold practical smuggler-part of him, the part that had kept him alive through nine winters of passes. *Say yes, get her out, worry about the rest after.*

But the rest did not go away. He thought of the drowned chapel, the water pressing in, the second stone singing against his chest when he swore—not power, oaths; the stones answered oaths. And Crane wanted a covenant built as a weapon, a door barred forever from one side, and he wanted Silas to be the hand on the bar. To swear it. Keystone. Living anchor. His oath would be the mortar.

*You'd be saving her,* said the cold voice, *into a world where everything she spent her life building is a cage.*

"You're honest," Silas said at last. "That's the worst part. I almost wish you were lying."

"Honesty is cheaper than you'd think, once you stop needing to be loved." Crane rose, buttoned his plain coat. "You'll be given a proper room tonight. Locked, but warm. Think it through. Sleep on it. I find men choose better rested." At the door he paused, and something almost human crossed his face, quickly, like a fish under ice. "For what it's worth—I met her once, years ago. Your hedge-witch. She told me a wall made of fear always falls, and asked me what I'd build instead. I never had an answer I liked. Perhaps you'll do better."

The door closed. The lock turned.

* * *

In the study, Crane poured himself a measure of the good brandy he saved for victories and near-victories alike, and allowed himself the luxury of confidence.

The boy would break. They always did, when the lever was love rather than pain; pain only taught men to hide their treasures, but love made them carry the treasure out into the open themselves, wrapped in both arms. Three days, perhaps four. The stones would come to Fort Marrow, the rite would begin, and the age of mutual anything would end.

He was halfway through the glass when Captain Verrin knocked—twice, quick, the wrong rhythm.

"Regent. Two problems." Verrin's breath steamed; he'd run up from the gatehouse. "First: the fae scout who came in with the prisoner's party—Bramble-ashe, the one in the reed-cloak—has vanished from holding. Left no tracks. Second—" the captain hesitated, and Crane found, distantly, that he already knew what was coming, the way one knows thunder after lightning. "Second, the woman. Maren Collier. The physician says she's failing faster than she should. Whatever's shielding the boy is burning backward through her now that they're close together again. She has days. Maybe less."

Crane stood very still.

Days. His arithmetic, so elegant on the page, had developed a flaw: the collateral was spending itself. Which meant the leisurely siege of a young man's conscience was a luxury, and luxuries, in Aldous Crane's long experience, got people killed.

"Move her," he said quietly. "Tonight. Out of the deep cells, up to the infirmary wing—light, warmth, a physician who isn't a butcher. And put word of it where the prisoner can hear." He turned back to the fire, and his reflection in the darkened glass wore the expression of a man setting a beautiful, terrible machine in motion. "Let the boy see exactly what his hesitation costs, hour by hour. Grief makes faster decisions than love."

Below, in the courtyard, the drills went on in the snow, patient as clockwork.

And above the fortress, past the shuttered window, the stars were still missing from the sky—and in their place, faint as a bruise, a green light was rising over the northern peaks, moving toward Thornfell's summit, where three roads and three armies and three truths were converging on the same broken ground.
