## 6. Ashes of the Hedge-Witch

The fortress of Regent Aldous Crane sat on the river like a black tooth in a gray gum, and Silas left it the way smoke leaves a snuffed candle—through the crack nobody watched.

He had spent three days in Crane's guest quarters, three days of honeyed offers and iron-gated corridors, and one night learning the rhythm of the guards' boots on the stair. They changed at the fourth bell. They always changed at the fourth bell; soldiers were the same everywhere, smuggler country or regent's keep. He went over the wall in the laundry cart, dropped into the moat ditch, and ran north through the reed beds with the second oath-stone burning cold against his chest like a chip of winter that refused to melt.

Behind him, horns. Ahead, the fens.

He had meant to run all the way to Hollowmere, to Vesperine, to anyone who could tell him what he was. But the stones had other ideas. Both of them—he'd pried Crane's confiscated map from his own sleeve and confirmed it—pulled toward the same point on the horizon, northwest, into the hills where Thornfell crumbled into wilderness. Toward a cottage he had sworn never to see again.

*What Maren never taught me,* he thought bitterly, wading a black stream up to his thighs. *She taught me everything except what I am.*

By the time the fortress horns faded behind him, he was no longer running from Crane.

He was running toward her.

* * *

The hills took two days. The long winter had reached them first; the heather stood sheathed in glass, every stalk cased in its own thin coffin of ice, so that the whole slope chimed when the wind moved through it. Silas climbed through fields of frozen bells. Twice he saw wolves watching from the ridgelines—not ordinary wolves, not with eyes like banked coals—and twice they let him pass, which frightened him more than an attack would have.

The third stone's pull grew stronger as he climbed, a tugging behind his sternum, gentle and terrible, like a hand closing around his heart from the inside. The second stone answered it, humming against his ribs. When he crested the last ridge in the blue hour before dawn, both stones sang—a single high note only he could hear—and below him, in the fold of the valley where he had learned to walk and read and lie and love without admitting it, sat Maren's cottage.

It had been ransacked months ago. That much he'd known. What he hadn't known was that someone had rebuilt part of it. The roof was newly thatched over the eastern half. Smoke curled from the chimney, thin and gray against the snow.

And around the whole clearing, sunk to their shoulders in the drifts, stood warding posts carved with hedge-signs he knew better than his own name.

His feet knew the path down. His whole childhood knew the path down. He walked it with his heart hammering out a smuggler's warning—*trap, trap, trap*—and knocked on the door anyway.

"Come in, Silas." Her voice, thinner than memory, frayed at the edges like old rope. "You always did knock too politely for a thief."

* * *

She was dying. There was no mistaking it, no polite pretending otherwise. She sat propped in the bed beneath the eastern eaves, wrapped in blankets despite the peat fire, and she had gone translucent—not pale, *translucent*, as if she were being erased from the world in slow strokes, the firelight showing faintly through the flesh of her hands.

"You," he said, and couldn't find anything after it.

"Me." She smiled, and it was Maren's smile exactly, crooked and unrepentant, and something in his chest tore cleanly in half. "Shut the door. The cold gets in through the cracks now. Most things get in through the cracks now."

He shut the door. He stood there with his back against it like a boy caught out past curfew, twenty-three years old and nine again in the same breath. "They said you were taken. Crane said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Crane said he'd give me your location. That means he doesn't have it. You escaped."

"I walked out of his fortress the way you walked out of it tonight, love. Smugglers make poor prisoners; we've had practice at doors." A cough took her then, deep and rattling, and when it passed she lay back among the pillows looking smaller. "Sit down. Please. I haven't the strength to chase you around this room, and we've years to catch up on and perhaps an hour to do it in."

He sat. The chair was the one by the hearth, the one where he'd learned his letters, and the realization hit him like cold water: she'd kept the room almost as he'd left it. As if she'd expected him to come home.

"You're dying," he said.

"I'm *spent*." She lifted one transparent hand, turned it in the firelight. "There's a difference, though I grant you the destination is the same. Do you know what a keystone is, Silas? Truly?"

"A stone at the top of an arch. Take it out and everything falls."

"Clever lad." Her eyes glittered. "Now do you know what a keystone does while the arch stands?"

He frowned. "Nothing. It just—holds."

"*Holds.*" She laughed, and it became another cough. "Yes. Holds. Every day. Without rest. The covenant between Thornfell and Hollowmere was bound at your birth—bound through a living soul, because living souls can bend without breaking, and dead stone cannot. And every day since the hour you were born, some portion of the strain of that covenant has passed through you. Into you. And someone has been standing between you and it, taking the worst of it onto herself, so that a babe wouldn't be crushed, so that a child wouldn't wake screaming, so that a boy could grow up thinking himself merely unlucky, merely restless, merely—"

"*Merely yours,*" Silas said. His voice came out strange and flat. "The apprenticeship. The smuggling. Me running away."

"You didn't run away from me, love. You ran away from the weight, though you couldn't name it. No one could blame you. I built the life you fled—I made it small enough to escape, because I thought a small cage would hold until you were strong enough for the truth." She closed her eyes. "I was wrong about the timing. The wards fell before you were ready. I'm sorry. I've been sorry for twenty-three years, and it's the only part of it I'd undo."

"The binding." The words scraped coming out. "At my birth. Was that Crane? Was that the court? Who—"

"Me." She opened her eyes and looked at him straight, the way she'd looked at him over every broken rule and every hard lesson, and did not flinch. "It was me. I chose it. I performed it. I bound you, Silas Blackwood, to the covenant of the two realms, and I would do it again, and I will burn for it gladly, and none of that will change what I'm about to tell you, so listen."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the frozen heather chimed.

"I was young," she said. "Younger than you are now. I served the old Warden of the Pass, and I loved her the way you love a teacher who sees you truly, and she was dying, and the covenant needed a new keystone or both realms would have torn each other apart within a generation. The stones choose. They don't ask. And they chose a child not yet born—a child of the border itself, born in the pass, of blood from both sides of the old wall." Her voice gentled. "My sister's child. My sister married a Hollowmere ferryman, against every law on either side of the water, and they died crossing the ice one winter, and I was left holding you. Half of here. Half of there. All mine."

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "My mother—"

"Was fae-blooded, three generations deep. Your father was a Thornfell carter who never knew. You were the only keystone the stones would accept, and they would not wait, and I—" For the first time her voice broke, clean through. "I had just lost everyone. I could not lose you too. So I swore the binding myself, alone, unwitnessed, and I poured my magic into the shield-spell that same night, and I have poured it in every night since. Every night, Silas. Twenty-three years. It's why I never taught you the deep craft—it wasn't cruelty, it was that I had nothing left to teach with. Everything I was went into keeping you whole."

"Why?" The word came out cracked down the middle. "Why not let the stones take someone else? Why not refuse them?"

"Because they'd already chosen, and refusal would have killed you slower and worse. And because—" She reached out, and her transparent hand found his, and it weighed nothing at all. "Because you were mine. Not the covenant's. Mine. And I have never once regretted it, and I have always known it was unforgivable, and I have waited every day since you left to say so to your face."

* * *

He didn't remember standing. He was kneeling by the bed with her hand pressed to his forehead like a blessing, and he was crying, ugly and silent, the way he hadn't cried since he was small enough to be held.

"I hated you," he whispered.

"I know."

"For years. I hated you for the smallness of it. The chores and the rules and the lies about my parents."

"I know, love."

"And I missed you every single day, and I told myself it was just habit, and it wasn't, it never was—"

"I know." Her thumb moved across his brow, leaving no weight, only warmth. "Loyalty and anger make poor bedfellows but stubborn children. You get it from me."

He laughed through the tears, and she laughed too, weakly, and for a little while neither of them said anything at all, and the fire burned, and the stones hummed their single high note, and it sounded almost like forgiveness already given.

Then she drew her hand back and worked it beneath her pillow, and brought out the third oath-stone.

It was smaller than the others, worn smooth as a river pebble, and it glowed with a light like the last coal of a banked fire—and Silas understood, in one sick lurching instant, that the glow was hers. That the stone had been drinking her for years, or she had been feeding it, or there was no longer any difference.

"They'll want it back," she said. "All three. At the peak, at the old covenant site, under whatever sky is left. Vesperine will tell you the same if you trust her, and you should—you're a better judge of fae than you think; you got it from your mother." She pressed the stone into his palm and folded his fingers closed over it, her hands shaking now in earnest. "Listen to me. The stones answer oaths, not power. Remember that above everything. Crane will offer you strength. Bramble-ashe—or whoever the court sends—will offer you freedom. Neither of those is an oath. An oath costs the swearer. Make sure whatever you swear, you can pay for."

"What will you—" He couldn't finish it.

"I'll be ash by morning, love, stone or no stone. I gave the last of me to the shield last night, when I felt you coming over the ridge. I wanted to be awake for this." She smiled again, and now he could see the fire through her cheekbone, and he made a sound he wasn't ashamed of. "Don't waste it grieving. Grief is fuel, same as anything else—burn it hot and get where you're going."

"Maren—"

"One more thing." She pulled him close, her lips at his ear, her voice barely a breath. "Whatever they told you the covenant was—a wall, a weapon, a chain—it was none of those things, once. It was a promise. Two realms saying *we will not let each other fall*. Someone will try to make you swear it small again. Don't. Swear it the size it was meant to be."

She kissed his forehead, and the warmth of it sank all the way through him like sunlight through water.

"There's my boy," she whispered. "Go and be the bridge."

* * *

He buried her at dawn beneath the rowan tree she'd planted the year he was born, digging through the frozen crust with his bare hands because no spell of his would move it, and laying her down wrapped in her own blanket because she'd asked for no shroud and no marker but the tree. The wolves came to the ridge line and stood watching, coal-eyed and still, and he was too empty to fear them.

When he stood, stiff and salt-faced, both stones in his pockets, the pull had changed. It was no longer a tug behind the sternum. It was a road. Northwest, uphill, through the freezing fog, to the peak where the old wall's foundations still crawled along the spine of the world like the root of a fallen star.

Far off in that direction, faint through the murk, he saw lights. Many lights. Torches, ranked and ordered, moving in columns.

An army. Crane's army, marching for the covenant site—and between Silas and the peak lay half a night's climb and a winter that was deepening by the hour, the cold pressing down now like something alive and hungry and *deliberate*.

Silas put Maren's stone in the pocket over his heart, beside the others, and started climbing.

Behind him, the rowan tree blossomed—out of season, out of nowhere, white flowers opening in the killing frost—and he did not see it, because he had already turned away.
