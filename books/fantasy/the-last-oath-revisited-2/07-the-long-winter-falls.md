## 7. The Long Winter Falls

The snow came sideways on the third day out from Hollowmere, driven by a wind that had learned to hate. Silas pulled his scarf over his mouth and squinted into the white, where the world had been reduced to a gray blur and the mountain was only a rumor above them.

"Two days," Bramble-ashe said beside him, voice pitched low under the wind. "Perhaps three, if the pass holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

Bramble-ashe smiled the way he always did—too many teeth, too much ease—and said nothing. Silas had stopped trusting that smile somewhere around the drowned chapel, and yet he still walked beside him, because the fae scout knew these mountains the way Silas knew smuggler routes: bone-deep, blood-true.

Behind them, Vesperine moved like a shadow given purpose, her court-gown long abandoned for riding leathers the color of dead leaves. She had not slept since they'd left the drowned chapel's shore. Fae did not need sleep the way humans did—or so she claimed—but even her kind were thinning now, he could see it. The hollow places under her eyes had gone from shadow to bruise. The long winter starved both realms alike; magic bled out of Hollowmere with every day the covenant lay broken, and what fed the fae was bleeding fastest.

"The villages below," Vesperine said suddenly, drawing level with him. Her voice carried strangely in the wind, as if the air itself leaned in to listen. "You hear them?"

Silas shook his head.

"Exactly." Her eyes, green as deep water, held something he had never seen there before. Not contempt. Not curiosity. *Fear.* "Silence. In Thornfell's valleys there should be dogs barking, hammers ringing, children shrieking at the snow. There is nothing."

* * *

Tamsin Hale had buried two of her children by the time the church bell cracked.

Not buried—there was no digging ground soft enough for graves anymore. She had wrapped them in quilts and laid them in the tithe barn with the others, because the frozen earth refused the spade and the priest had run out of words weeks ago. Nine souls in the barn now, from Millbrook alone. The whole village was down to forty-one, and every morning the number was smaller.

She stood at her window watching the snow bury the last of the turnip patch and thought about the story her grandmother used to tell, about the old days when the border wards were strong. When the fae left bread on doorsteps in hard winters and the humans left cream out at midsummer, and nobody quite admitted the arrangement aloud but everyone kept it. Grandmother had called it *the quiet bargain*. Tamsin had thought it a fairy tale.

Now the fairies were dying on their side of the hills, and the bread had stopped coming, and her children were in the barn.

Her surviving boy, Wat, seven years old and sharp-boned as a winter sparrow, tugged her sleeve. "Mum. There's a man on the road. A lot of men."

She looked. Down through the falling white came a column of soldiers, banners stiff with ice, torches burning a sickly green-white that made her stomach turn. At their head rode a man in a fur cloak, and even at this distance she knew the face from the tax notices nailed to the church door: Regent Aldous Crane, the regent who had never once come to Millbrook in twelve years of rule.

He reined up before her gate. His breath steamed. His eyes, when they found hers, were the warm brown of a well you could drop a stone down forever without hearing it land.

"Woman," he called. "How many mouths in this village?"

"Forty-one." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Thirty-nine after tomorrow, if the fever takes the Cooper girl like it took her brothers."

Crane nodded slowly, as if she'd reported grain yields. "And if I told you forty-one more would live through spring, would you help me?"

"With what?"

He gestured behind him, at the army, at the frozen valley, at the dead gray sky where stars should have wheeled at night and did not. "There is a mountain above your village. On its peak sits a thing older than your church, older than your kingdom. It is broken, and its breaking is killing your children. I am going to fix it." He leaned down from the saddle, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "I only need one man to reach it first. A smuggler. Silas Blackwood. Have you seen him?"

Tamsin thought of the Blackwood boy who'd grown up half-feral on the passes, who'd once carried her sister's medicine over the border when no lawful man would touch it. She thought of her children in the barn.

"I've seen him," she said. "Heading north, three days back. Toward the peak."

It was a lie and Crane knew it was a lie, and he let her believe he didn't, which was its own kind of message. He pressed a coin into her palm—gold, real gold, warm as a living thing—and moved on down the street, and his soldiers went with him, and the green torchlight slid away into the snow.

Wat looked up at her. "Why did you lie, Mum?"

"Because," Tamsin said, closing her fist around the coin until it bit, "whatever that man is building on the mountain, it isn't for us."

* * *

They found the first starving fae at dusk, folded beneath a rowan tree like a puppet with cut strings.

Vesperine knelt beside it—a youth, barely more than a sapling, skin gone gray as ash, the leaf-patterns faded from his cheeks. She touched his brow and closed her eyes, and Silas saw her lips move in what might have been a name or a prayer or an accounting.

"He gave his light to the orchards," she said quietly, rising. "Trying to keep them alive. Our people are spending themselves to save trees and wells and hearths, and it buys days, not seasons." She turned to face him, and the wind tore at her dark hair, and for a moment she looked less like a lady of the hidden court and more like something exhausted and ancient and very much alone. "You should understand what we race toward, Silas Blackwood. If the covenant is not reforged before the last frost-moon wanes, Hollowmere does not suffer. Hollowmere *ends*. And Thornfell follows within a generation—the wards were not a wall to us, whatever your histories say. They were lungs. We breathed together, your realm and mine, and someone strangled the breath."

"Bramble-ashe says severance would free you."

Something flickered across Vesperine's face—too fast to read, too slow to miss. "Bramble-ashe says many things."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the mountain showed itself for the first time: Thornfell's peak, black against a sky the color of a dead hearth, wearing its crown of snow like a shroud. And far below, tiny as ants, torches. Green-white torches. Moving up the southern road.

Silas counted, and then stopped counting because the number made his hands shake.

"Crane's not sending scouts this time," he said. "That's an army."

"Then we climb faster than an army marches," Vesperine said, and started upward.

* * *

They climbed through the night, and the night fought them.

The cold had teeth now—it was not weather but intention, as if winter itself had been set to guard the peak against all comers. Ice glazed the rocks so that every handhold was a gamble. Bramble-ashe ranged ahead, finding paths no human eye could see, and twice he came back for Silas with a rope and a grin and hands that were warmer than any fae's had a right to be.

"You're shaking," the scout observed on the second night, crouched beside their miserable fire in a hollow of stone. The fire burned green, fed on a twig of Hollowmere rowan, and gave little heat. Little was enough. "Your kind was not built for this altitude. Or this season. Or, frankly, this quest."

"My kind built the covenant," Silas said. "Someone of my kind can rebuild it."

"Mm." Bramble-ashe fed another twig to the flame. His face, lit from below, was unreadable in the dancing light. "And when it's rebuilt—what then? Your regent wants it a weapon. My lady wants it a chain, however gilded. Have you asked yourself what *you* want it to be?"

Silas looked into the fire. He thought of Maren's journal, and the word *keystone*, and thirty years of a life that had been shaped around him like a wall around a foundation stone. He thought of Tamsins everywhere, on both sides of the border, laying their dead in barns because the ground would not take them.

"A bridge," he said, not knowing why the word rose, only that it felt true in his chest like a struck bell. "I want it to be a bridge."

For just a moment—one heartbeat, no more—something moved behind Bramble-ashe's eyes. Grief, or guilt, or a door closing on both. Then the grin returned, easy and bright and wrong.

"A bridge," the scout repeated softly. "Get some sleep, smuggler. Tomorrow we make the summit ridge."

* * *

On the last morning, the storm broke open like a wound and showed them the world.

From the summit ridge, Thornfell fell away on either side: to the south, the human valleys, smokeless and silent, fields lying dead under ice; to the north, Hollowmere's silver woods, gray-frosted, dimmed like coals going out. Two realms, holding their breath, waiting to see who reached the peak.

Below on the southern switchbacks, the army climbed. Banners cracking in the wind. Torches burning green. And at its head, small with distance but unmistakable, a figure in a fur cloak, climbing steadily, patiently, like a man who had already won.

Vesperine stood at the ridge's edge, hair streaming, and spoke a single word in a language that made the stones hum.

"How long do we have?" Silas asked.

"An hour," she said. "Perhaps two. The covenant site is just beyond—that ring of standing stones, there, where the snow will not settle. You can see it refuses the winter." Indeed, in the heart of the peak, a circle of ancient monoliths stood bare and black amid the white, untouched, waiting, patient as bones. "But Silas—" she turned to him, and her voice was stripped of all its courtly music, leaving only the raw truth underneath, "—when we begin, whatever happens, the stones will demand a price for the reforging. They always do. Oaths of that magnitude are never free. Are you prepared to pay without knowing the cost?"

Silas thought of Maren, dying in a cell so he could stand here. Of a bridge, in his chest, like a struck bell.

"No," he said honestly. "But I'm going anyway."

He started forward across the ridge—and stopped.

Because Bramble-ashe was no longer walking beside them. He stood apart, near the edge, facing the army below with his back to the summit, and when he turned, the grin was gone from his face at last, and what remained underneath was older and sadder and infinitely more dangerous.

"Before we go any further," Bramble-ashe said, drawing a slim knife that gleamed like moonlight on water, "there is something you both should know about the night the wards fell."
