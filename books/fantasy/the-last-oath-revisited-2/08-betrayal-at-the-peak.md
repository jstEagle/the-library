## 8. Betrayal at the Peak

The covenant site had no name that anyone remembered. The old maps called it only *the Seat*, a crown of black stone at the summit of Thornfell's peak where the mountain itself seemed to have been split by some ancient argument and then persuaded, grudgingly, to heal. Two thrones of rock faced each other across a chasm no wider than a grave — one of grey granite, human-cut; the other of living wood, its grain still warm after centuries. Between them, the chasm breathed.

That was how Silas thought of it now, standing at the edge with the three oath-stones heavy in their satchel against his chest. The border was not dead. It was breathing — ragged, arrhythmic, like a man with a knife between his ribs who has not yet decided whether to die.

"You feel it too." Vesperine stood on the wooden throne's side, her court-finery traded for riding leathers the colour of frost-bitten heather. Even stripped of ceremony she moved like winter given a spine. "It is trying to remember what it was."

"It remembers," Bramble-ashe said.

Silas turned. The fae scout stood at the chasm's far lip, on the human side of the divide, which was itself strange — Bramble-ashe never crossed lines without reason. His hazel eyes caught the dying light, and there was something in his face Silas had never seen there before: not the scout's easy half-smile, not the soldier's watchfulness. Something older. Something like grief that had finished grieving and hardened into purpose.

"The stones answer to oaths kept," Silas said slowly. "Vesperine told me that in the drowned chapel. But an oath needs two voices, doesn't it? A bridge needs two banks."

"Yes." Bramble-ashe drew a breath that seemed to cost him something. "And I am sorry, Silas Blackwood, for what I must do while you work that out."

He stepped back off the human throne — and the ground took him.

Not swallowed him. *Took* him, the way a lock takes a key. Roots erupted from the granite like veins surfacing in a wrist, wound once around his ankles, and sank. The stone beneath the throne lit from within with a green-gold light Silas knew intimately, because he had seen it twice before: once in a dying courier's fist, once in a flooded chapel held in the hands of a drowned priest. Oath-light. Living covenant-light.

Bramble-ashe stood inside it as though it were water up to his knees, and he did not struggle.

"No." Vesperine was across the chasm in three strides that covered thirty feet, frost blooming and shattering under her heels. "No, no — *Bramble.*"

"Fourth stone," Bramble-ashe said quietly. "There was always a fourth, my lady. You knew that better than anyone. You simply chose to believe it was lost." He looked at Silas across the divide, and his smile came at last — the old one, the crooked one, the one that had shared smuggler's bread with Silas in a frozen pass and taught him which stars lied about the weather. It broke Silas's heart more cleanly than any blade could have. "You carried three. Someone had to carry the fourth. Someone had to be the fourth."

"What are you doing?" Silas's voice came out low and dangerous, a stranger's voice. "Bramble. What are you *doing?*"

"Finishing it." The scout spread his arms, and the light climbed him like ivy. "The night the wards fell — you've been hunting the traitor through every chapter of this miserable winter, haven't you? Crane thinks Crane did it. Vesperine suspects her own court. Maren blamed herself." His voice roughened. "It was me. I unbound the keystone-wards from the inside. I have been a warden of this site for two hundred years, and on the night the stars went out, I let go."

* * *

The wind rose around the peak, moaning through the chasm, and for a moment nobody spoke.

Silas found he could not move. He kept seeing it — the pass, the first night, the courier dying with Maren's name on his lips, and Bramble-ashe finding him in the snow afterward. *Lucky thing I was patrolling,* the scout had said. Lucky. Every step since. Every rescue, every warning, every fire shared on cold ground — arranged. Guided. Herded.

"You used me," Silas said. "From the start."

"I *protected* you." For the first time, anger cracked through the scout's calm. "Do you know what Crane would have done if I hadn't been there when the courier fell? Do you know what Hollowmere's own hunters would have done to a human holding an oath-stone? I walked you through every trap this winter, Silas. I got you to Maren. I got you out of Crane's fortress. Everything I did, I did so that you would arrive here alive, with all three stones, at the exact hour they could be used."

"To be used for *what?*"

"For severance." Bramble-ashe turned to Vesperine, and now his voice gentled, which was worse. "My lady. You felt it yourself, in the drowned chapel. The covenant was never a marriage. It was a hostage exchange. Hollowmere's magic bleeds into your soil to keep your harvests alive; Thornfell's iron and belief bleed into ours to keep us *real*. Neither realm can end the arrangement without both bleeding out — and so we have spent a thousand years bound wrist to wrist to a people who burn our forests, fence our paths with cold iron, and call us monsters when we object."

"We have a treaty—"

"We have a *cage* with very old bars." He shook his head. "I loved this land enough to be its warden. And I watched it grow thinner every century. Fewer children born true. More of us fading at the edges like old embroidery. And all because we cannot survive without leeching from a realm that barely remembers we exist except as stories to frighten children."

"And severance fixes this?" Vesperine's voice was glacial. "Cutting the artery we both share? Half of Hollowmere dies with the connection, Bramble. The outer courts. The wild folk. The ones who cannot pull back toward the deep heart. You know this."

"They would die *free*." The words rang off the black stone. "Better one clean death than a slow draining forever."

"That is not your choice to make for them!"

"No," Bramble-ashe agreed softly. "It was mine to make alone. That is what wardenship means, my lady. You taught me that."

* * *

Silas looked down at the satchel. Three oath-stones, humming faintly against his ribs like caged birds. Above them, the sky was the colour of dirty pewter, and the long winter's breath fogged from his mouth in plumes. Somewhere below, faint but unmistakable now, came the sound he had been dreading all the climb: drums. Not fae drums. Human ones. Square, disciplined, patient.

Crane's army was coming up the mountain.

"Bramble." Silas forced himself to speak evenly, the way he'd learned to haggle over contraband with men holding knives. "If you take the fourth seat and sever the covenant, what happens to Thornfell? To the villages? I've seen them starving. I've buried two of them myself this month."

"I know. I helped dig." No flinch. No apology. "Yes. Humans will suffer. Many will die. And here is the truth that keeps me awake, Silas, the truth I will say plainly because you deserve it plain: your realm has taken ours for a thousand years and never once starved for it. When the ledger finally balances against you, you call it atrocity. Your grievance and mine are written in the same ink. Only the hand differs."

Silas wanted to hate him for it. He reached for hatred the way a man reaches for a familiar tool in the dark —

— and found his fingers closing on understanding instead. That was the worst of it. Bramble-ashe was not wrong. Not entirely, not even mostly. A thousand years of quiet theft dressed up as mutual survival; a border built as a wall and called a covenant; a boy bound at birth as a *keystone* without his knowledge or consent, because someone had decided his life belonged to an architecture. Maren had made that choice out of love, and it had still been made *for* him. Hollowmere's elders had made theirs out of necessity, and it had still been made *for* the fae who bled for it.

Everyone in this story had been bound by someone else's oath.

"You're right," Silas said.

Bramble-ashe blinked. Vesperine went very still.

"You're right about the theft. About the wall. About all of it." Silas opened the satchel. The three stones' glow spilled up across his face, gold and silver and the deep blue-green of chapel water. "But you're wrong about the ending. Severance isn't freedom, Bramble. It's just another binding — binding both realms to a corpse. I didn't climb this mountain to choose whose cage we die in. I climbed it to break the cage open."

"There is no third door," the scout said. "There is the wall, or there is the wound."

"There's a bridge."

Below them, the drums grew louder. Through the thinning snow Silas could see them now — torchlight snaking up the mountain road like a burning vein, hundreds strong, pike-points glittering. And at the head of the column, unmistakable even at distance by the white fur of his cloak, Regent Aldous Crane rode at the pace of a man who believed the summit was already his.

Vesperine drew herself up beside Silas, frost crackling along her knuckles. "Whatever you mean to do," she said quietly, "do it before those torches reach us. Or after. I confess I no longer know which serves us better."

Bramble-ashe looked at Silas across the breathing chasm, and something in his face wavered — the warden's certainty cracking just enough to show the friend underneath, the one who had shared bread in the snow.

"Three stones, Silas. Four seats. The fourth is mine, and I will not yield it. Whatever you swear, you swear *around* me."

Silas lifted the stones out of the satchel, one by one, and felt the mountain hold its breath.

"Then I'll swear around you," he said. "But you should know — the stones don't answer to power held. They answer to oaths kept. And you, my friend, have just broken the oldest one of all."

The chasm exhaled. The torches below rounded the last switchback. And the first stone began, very softly, to sing.
