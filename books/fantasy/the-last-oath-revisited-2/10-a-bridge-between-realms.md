## 10. A Bridge Between Realms

Spring came to the pass like a rumor that refused to die quietly.

Silas felt it first in the stones of his cottage—warmth seeping into walls that had been raised by a hundred hands, human and fae alike, in the weeks after the Reforging. The mortar between them was strange stuff: grey stone from Thornfell's quarries, white chalk from Hollowmere's cliffs, and a binding of living ivy that Bramble-ashe had planted with his own thorn-browned fingers, muttering apologies to each root as he set it. In winter the ivy slept against the walls like a held breath. Now it woke, and green ran up toward the eaves, and Silas stood in the doorway with his morning tea going cold and watched it grow.

He could not have said, a year ago, that he would end his life as a keeper of anything. He had been a smuggler once. A boy with quick feet and quicker hands who carried iron-warded goods through mountain passes and asked no questions because questions cost coin. He had built himself out of not-knowing, brick by careful brick.

Then the stars went out, and every wall he'd ever built came down at once.

He drank his tea anyway. It had gone bitter. Most things did, now, when he thought about them too long.

* * *

The pass itself had changed.

Where the old wards had stood—a wall of woven oaths older than either kingdom, invisible until the night they died—there was now something else entirely. Travelers called it the Gate, though it was no gate at all, only a stretch of open ground between two leaning standing-stones, one granite-grey, one moonstone-pale, their roots grown together beneath the earth so that no one could say which realm owned which half.

Anyone could cross. That was the whole of it. That was everything he had paid for.

Farmers from Thornfell drove carts of barley south into Hollowmere's valleys, where the long winter had killed three harvests running. Fae weavers came north with cloth that shifted color like water over stone, and children of both kinds chased each other across the line without ever knowing where one world ended and the other began—which, Silas thought, watching them from his window, was perhaps the truest miracle of all. He remembered what it had been to be a border. He knew better than anyone that borders only existed for those old enough to fear them.

The covenant hummed under the pass day and night. He couldn't hear it anymore—not with magic—but he could feel it in his teeth, in the soles of his feet, the way a man feels a river through the planks of a bridge. Three oath-stones sat in a cradle of silver wood at the center of the crossing, and twice a day someone came to tend them: a hedge-witch from the villages, or one of Lady Vesperine's bell-keepers, taking turns as if the two realms were learning to share even their rituals.

They always nodded to him when they passed his door. Keeper, they called him. Not because he kept anything, exactly. Because he was the reason there was something left to keep.

* * *

In the afternoons he walked the pass.

It was the only place he could walk. That was the shape of his exile, precise as a knife-cut: the covenant lived in him still, in whatever thread of himself had been left behind when his magic drained away, and neither realm would hold him. Step south past the last stone of Thornfell and his boots slid on nothing, the world refusing him purchase. Step north into Hollowmere and the air itself turned him gently back, like a host too polite to say *you are not wanted*, saying it anyway.

He had tried it four times in the first month. On the fourth attempt he had stood at Hollowmere's threshold until dusk, close enough to smell the bluebell woods and hear the drowned chapel's bells ringing soft across the water, and then walked home to a house built for him by people he could never live among.

But on the pass itself—the narrow spine of the world between—he was solid. Here he was both things at once, or neither, and either way the ground held him.

Bramble-ashe found him there most days. The fae scout had never gone back to the court. Vesperine had offered him pardon—had offered him more than pardon, honor, a place at her right hand—and he had knelt in the mud of the peak with frost still in his hair and refused it. *A traitor should not keep honors,* he'd said. *Let me carry stones instead.* So he carried stones, and planted ivy, and taught Thornfell's farmers which fae herbs would grow in their thin soil, and every afternoon he climbed the pass to sit beside Silas on the flat rock above the Gate and say very little.

Today he brought honey-cakes wrapped in a leaf.

"You're staring at the boundary again," Bramble-ashe said.

"I'm allowed. I own it."

"Own half." The fae bit into a cake. His skin had lost its winter pallor; green was coming back into it the same as into the ivy. "You gave the other half to everyone else."

Silas almost smiled. "How is she? The Lady."

"Blooming. Literally—she has flowers growing out of her crown now, and pretends to find it undignified. She holds court on the border every full moon. Human petitioners, fae petitioners, one queue, one law." He paused. "She asks after you."

"What do you tell her?"

"That you're stubborn and drink your tea cold." Bramble-ashe looked at him sidelong, and there was something careful in it, the care of a friend walking around a wound. "And that you don't remember her. And that maybe that's a kind of mercy."

"It isn't," Silas said.

"No," Bramble-ashe agreed softly. "I don't suppose it is."

That was the arithmetic of it, the ledger Silas read every night before sleep took him: he had bought two worlds with one memory, and the memory had been Maren—all of her, every season of her, the woman who had bound him at birth and lied to him for twenty years and loved him hard enough to spend herself to ash shielding him from Crane's soldiers. He knew the shape of the truth the way you know the shape of a room you've been carried through blindfolded. He knew she had existed. He knew she had mattered. There was a hole in him cut exactly to her proportions, and some mornings it ached like a tooth, and he could not even remember what he was mourning.

Regent Aldous Crane had died on the peak, caught in the unraveling of his own weapon-wards, and no one mourned him either. But Maren—

Maren he would have liked to mourn properly. Instead he tended her grave.

* * *

Her cairn stood on the Thornfell side of the pass, an hour's climb, within sight of the Gate. He went up every seventh day with a bundle of rowan sprigs, because something in his bones—some ghost of habit older than memory—insisted on rowan.

He talked to her sometimes. Told her about the spring. About the first caravan of Hollowmere apples coming north, how the children had eaten themselves sick. About the letters that arrived now from both courts, sealed with oak leaves and moth-wing sigils, addressed to *the Keeper of the Pass*. About how he still couldn't sleep under a roof without checking the exits, some smuggler's instinct outliving its purpose.

"And I'm told you'd laugh at that," he said to the cairn, "which means somewhere in me there's a version of your laugh I've forgotten, and I hate that most of all."

The wind moved through the rowan trees. He let it.

On the way down, he passed a party of travelers heading south—Thornfell wool merchants, and with them a fae woman carrying a human infant wrapped against her chest, and the baby reached out toward Silas as he passed, the way babies reach at everything bright. He didn't know why it made his chest hurt. Everything made his chest hurt lately, in ways he couldn't name. He lifted one finger and the child gripped it, briefly, with astonishing strength, and laughed.

"There," the fae mother said. "He'll grow up thinking the border is a person."

"He'll grow up thinking the border is a bridge," Silas corrected, and meant it, and felt the covenant hum under his feet like a purring cat.

* * *

The stranger came on the last evening of the thaw.

He heard the footsteps at dusk—light ones, unhurried, stopping at his gate—and by the time he opened the door there was no one on the path but the fading light and a small bouquet of flowers lying on his doorstep.

Woodland flowers. Bluebells, primroses, a spray of hawthorn blossom, and one stem of something pale and star-shaped that he did not recognize and that no book in his cottage could name—though his hand trembled holding it, though his throat closed around a word that wasn't there.

He stood in the doorway a long time, looking down the empty road, at the pass glowing gold and violet in the last light, at the standing-stones leaning together like two people sharing a secret.

He didn't know why he wept. That was the truth of it, plain and unadorned. He didn't know whose hand had cut these flowers, or why they'd chosen this evening, or why the small star-shaped bloom undid him like a key turning in a lock whose door had been bricked over years ago. He wept the way the snowmelt wept down the mountainside—without decision, without shame, because the season had turned and something frozen was finally moving.

He brought the flowers inside and put them in water on his table, and sat with them while the dark came on, and the covenant sang low under the floorboards, and somewhere south a child grew up believing borders could be bridges.

Worth it, he told the ache in his chest. Whatever you were. Whoever you were. It was worth it.

Outside, the first star of the evening rose over Hollowmere, and then another over Thornfell, and the sky filled slowly with lights that had gone out once and been given back. Silas lit his lamp, and set two cups on the table out of a habit he couldn't explain, and poured tea into both—one for himself, and one for whoever had left the flowers, and one, perhaps, though he would never count it, for a woman whose name the wind knew and the stones remembered, and who had loved him first, and longest, and best.
