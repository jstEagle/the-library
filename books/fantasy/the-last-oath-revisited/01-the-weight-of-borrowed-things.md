## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The Hollow kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins—clutched close, counted often, never spent. Mara Nystrom had lived there twenty-six years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one thing worth learning: what people would trade to forget.

She sat in her back room with the shutters drawn, the afternoon light coming through the gaps in slats like knives laid flat. On the table before her, arranged in a careful row, sat six glass vials no bigger than a thumb, each one stoppered with wax and wrapped in a twist of cloth so they wouldn't clink. Six memories. A good week's work.

The first held a farmer's memory of watching his barn burn—the one he'd set himself, for the insurance, though the Hollow had no insurance and the fire had spread farther than he'd meant it to. He'd paid well. People always paid well to be rid of their worst nights.

The second was a widow's last conversation with her husband, which she'd sold not because it hurt but because it hurt *too much*, because she wanted to love him again from the beginning instead of drowning in the ending every time she closed her eyes. Mara had taken it gently. Some thefts were closer to mercy.

The third through fifth were small things: a drunkard's shame, a debtor's terror of the man he owed, a midwife's memory of the one birth that went wrong in thirty years of right ones. Small cruelties, small prices. The Hollow's economy ran on them the way other places ran on grain.

The sixth vial was empty. It was always empty, and Mara kept it on the end of the row as a reminder—a promise she'd made herself at fifteen and had never once broken.

*Never steal from children.*

Not never take payment from them. Not never accept what they offered. Never *steal*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Adults came to her with their hands out, asking, bargaining, sometimes begging. Children didn't know yet what they'd want back someday. A stolen childhood memory left a hole shaped like itself, and holes had a way of growing.

So: never children. She'd carved the rule into herself deeper than any oath, and in twenty-six years she had kept it.

Mara picked up the widow's vial and turned it against the light. Inside, the memory swam like a minnow in dark water—a smear of gold and grey, the shape of a kitchen, the sound of a voice she couldn't hear but could feel pressing against the glass. That was how stolen memories worked. They weren't pictures. They were weather. Hold a vial up and you could feel what it felt like to be someone else for an hour or a lifetime.

She could have sold it in town. Old Sef at the trading post would give her three days' flour and a bolt of wool for something that rich. But Mara had learned long ago that the real money wasn't in selling memories forward—it was in keeping them. A woman who owned two hundred lives could be anyone. A woman who owned none was only herself, and Mara had never been sure that was enough.

Her own memories fit in a single drawer. Birth in a house she couldn't picture anymore. Parents who were shapes, then names, then nothing—dead when she was four, the elders said, of the fever winter, though no one could quite remember the fever winter either. The Hollow forgot easily. That was its nature and its shelter. Magic could not cross the valley's rim; the old stories said the Oathkeepers had made it so, a place where the great powers of the world went hungry. What the stories didn't say—and what every child raised in the Hollow learned by watching—was that forgetting was the tax you paid for safety. Memories thinned here like soup stretched too far. Whole years went soft. The elders called it peace.

Mara called it a leaky roof, and she'd spent her life patching it with other people's rain.

A knock at the front door. Two knocks, pause, one—Hesta's pattern, the miller's wife who fancied discretion but telegraphed it like a festival drum.

"Coming," Mara called, and stowed the vials in the floor cache beneath a loose board, all but the empty one, which stayed on the table where she could see it.

* * *

Hesta stood on the step wringing her apron, which meant bad news wearing good manners.

"Master Aldric," she said. "The cooper. He wants a thing taken."

"Then Master Aldric can come tell me himself."

"He can't." Hesta glanced over her shoulder at the empty lane, the low stone houses with their smoke rising straight in windless air. "He won't say why. But he sent this." She pressed a copper token into Mara's palm—an old coin, worn smooth, stamped with a sigil Mara didn't recognize. Not one of the Hollow's marks. "He says it's from before. From his grandfather's time."

Mara turned the coin over. Cold, despite sitting in Hesta's warm hand. Colder than metal had any business being in a valley where cold things couldn't follow.

"When?"

"Tonight. His cellar. He says—" Hesta lowered her voice as if the walls had ears, and in the Hollow, they might. "He says it's been whispering."

*Whispering.* Mara had heard every euphemism in the trade. Whispering meant a memory too strong to sit still in a head. Meant a thing that talked back. Meant trouble with a price tag attached, and Mara's rent was due and her stores were thin and the snows would come early this year, everyone said so, the birds had gone quiet in the hedges.

"Tell him I'll come after dark."

"Mara." Hesta caught her sleeve. Her face, in the fading light, looked older than it had that morning. "Be careful what you take tonight. Some doors don't shut again."

"They always shut," Mara said. "I'm very good at doors."

But walking home through the blue dusk, past the meeting hall where the elders were lighting lamps and arguing in low voices about something she was deliberately not listening to, she found herself turning the strange cold coin over and over in her pocket, and for no reason she could name, she thought of her parents. Not remembered them—you couldn't remember what you didn't have—but reached for them, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. Shapes. Names, maybe. *Nystrom,* which was half the valley's name and therefore no name at all.

The feeling passed. Such feelings always passed, in the Hollow. That was the whole point of the place.

* * *

Cooper Aldric's cellar smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil and, underneath, something older—stone-sweetness, like water over minerals, like the air inside a cave that has never seen sun. Mara noticed it the moment she descended the stairs, and her hand went instinctively to the empty vial in her coat pocket. Habit. Protection. The vial was her talisman of restraint; touching it reminded her who she chose to be.

Aldric sat on a barrel with a candle guttering beside him, and he looked ten years older than he had at market last week. His hands, which shaped barrel staves for a living, were shaking.

"You came." Relief and dread in equal measure.

"You knew I would. Rent doesn't pay itself." Mara set her satchel on a crate and looked around the cellar. Barrels in ranks. A drain in the floor. One small window at ground level, black now. "Show me."

"It's my father's memory. Maybe my grandfather's—I don't know whose, exactly. It's been in the family." He swallowed. "In the family the way you keep things in a family. Passed down. And lately it's been—" He rubbed his arms. "Loud. At night. My wife hears it talking through me. In a language nobody speaks. I wake with my mouth full of words I don't understand and a taste like iron."

Memories didn't do that. Memories were meat and light; they sat where you put them. Unless—

Unless they weren't really memories. Unless they were messages wearing memory's clothes, waiting generations for someone to open them.

"Where is it?"

Aldric pointed at his own temple with a trembling finger. "It's in me. Has been since I was a boy. My father gave it to me before he died—he said someone would come for it, eventually. Someone with your gift. He said I'd know you by—" He stopped.

"By what?" Mara asked, though part of her already didn't want the answer.

"By the fact that you'd ask."

The cellar got very quiet. Somewhere above, a floorboard creaked—Aldric's wife, pacing, or just the house settling. In the Hollow, houses settled constantly. Everything here settled, sank, softened. Nothing was supposed to arrive.

"I'll need you still," Mara said, falling back on procedure because procedure was solid ground. "Still and silent. I take only what we agreed—the loud thing, nothing else. You won't miss it. You may feel lighter. Most do."

"And if it hurts you?" Aldric asked suddenly. "My father said—there are things in this world that bite the hand that reaches for them."

"Your father also said someone would come asking, which means he knew more than most dead men." Mara drew out the extraction kit: a shallow silver dish, a needle-thin stylus of black glass, and the empty vial. "Sit still. Count backward from a hundred if it helps."

Aldric counted. Mara touched the stylus to his temple, found the thread of the foreign thing—it was easy to find, blazing like a coal among banked fires—and pulled.

It came fast. Too fast. Extraction was usually slow, delicate work, coaxing a memory loose like a splinter. This thing leapt for the stylus, eager, as if it had been waiting decades in a cooper's head for exactly this hand.

And then it was in her, and Mara understood why Aldric's wife heard whispers.

It wasn't a memory. It was a message, compressed and folded a thousand times, addressed—not to Aldric, not to his father or grandfather, but to a name. And the name was hers.

*Mara.*

Not spoken. Worse than spoken. *Remembered*—the way you remember something you've never known, the way the Hollow forgets and forgets until the forgetting itself becomes a shape, and the shape has edges, and the edges spell a word. The word filled her skull like cold water filling a boot. She felt the cellar tilt. Felt the candle flame lean without wind. Felt, distantly, Aldric shouting her name—her actual name, his mouth this time—as she swayed and caught herself on a barrel stave.

The message folded itself small and slid, uninvited, into the empty vial in her hand. The wax seal she pressed over it frosted white instantly, blooming crystals like fern leaves.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

"What did it say?" Aldric whispered.

"Nothing," Mara lied, because the truth was worse: it hadn't said anything. It had *called*. It had used her name the way a key uses a lock, and somewhere beyond the valley's rim, whatever had mailed this letter across generations now knew the letter had arrived.

She took Aldric's payment—too little, she didn't argue—and walked home through streets gone utterly silent, under stars that seemed colder than usual, past the meeting hall where the elders' argument had stopped mid-word.

In her room, she set the frosted vial on the table next to the others and told herself the unease crawling up her spine was just the night chill. Told herself doors always shut, that she was very good at doors.

Outside, the first snow of the season began to fall—early, silent, and wrong somehow, each flake drifting down as if it were being placed rather than dropped.

Mara blew out her candle and lay in the dark, listening to the snow land without sound, and waited—for sleep, for morning, for whatever had whispered her name to make its next move.

It didn't take long. Just past midnight, someone knocked at her door.
