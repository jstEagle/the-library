## 2. The Child at the Door

The snow came wrong.

I knew it before I saw it, the way you know a stranger's footsteps on your stairs. The Hollow had gone nine years without snow—nine years of grey drizzle and mud that froze only in the shallow ditches where even the valley's stubbornness wore thin. Old men in the tavern swore the last real winter had died with the last real king, and nobody argued, because arguing about weather in the Hollow was like arguing with a wall. The wall always won.

But that evening, as I was locking up my strongbox after trading away a cooper's memory of his first wife—she'd run off with a tinker, and he wanted to stop flinching whenever he smelled beeswax—the light through my shutters went white. Not the pale nothing of overcast. White-white. A clean, deliberate white, like a page turned.

I opened the door and the first flakes were already falling, fat and slow, each one perfect as if cut by a jeweler who had never once been rushed.

Snow doesn't fall like that. Snow falls tired. This fell *intentional*.

I stood in my doorway with the cooper's coins warm in my pocket and watched the valley fill up with something that had no business being here. The Hollow kept things out—that was its whole nature, its whole reason for existing in the maps nobody drew anymore. Magic couldn't cross our boundary stones. Weather with intent couldn't either, or so the elders said when they'd had enough cider to talk about such things. And this snow had intent the way a knife has an edge.

The village below me was lighting lamps early. I heard dogs start up, then quit all at once, which was worse than the barking. Dogs know things. Dogs had known about me for years, back before I learned how to be unremarkable.

I should have shut the door. I want that on the record, wherever records go when there's no one left to read them. I stood there a long moment, and every instinct I owned—the ones I'd bought and bartered from other people, the sharp ones, the survival-shaped ones—all of them said: close the door, bank the fire, pretend until morning.

Instead I stepped out into the snow.

* * *

My house sits above the village on a shelf of granite, at the end of a path that forks three ways and none of them signposted. You don't get to my door by accident. People come because they've heard what I do, or because they're desperate, or both. The path was filling with white, erasing itself behind me as I walked down toward the gate, and I remember thinking that the snow was doing deliberately what I spent coin and craft to do to my own tracks: making the past impossible to follow.

That should have been my second warning. I didn't take it. I was too busy looking at the shape standing at my gate.

It was small. Child-small, though the falling dark made it hard to judge. It wasn't moving, and it wasn't shivering, and those two facts together sat in my stomach like swallowed river stones. Everything alive shivers. Even the Hollow's half-starved cats shiver, dramatically, professionally, the way beggars hold out cups.

"Hello," I said, because apparently I was going to do this.

The shape didn't answer. It held something up instead—a little higher than its head—and the thing caught the lamplight from the village and threw it back wrong. Lamplight is orange. What came off the object was the color of deep ice under pressure, blue so far into blue it went cold just to look at, and it lit the child's face from below.

A girl. Six, maybe seven years old, thin as a winter branch, wearing clothes that had been fine once and a hundred years ago—cut in a style I'd only seen in tapestries, fastened with buttons shaped like leaves. Her hair was white. Not grey, not bleached. White as the snow settling on it, so that for a heartbeat I couldn't tell where the child ended and the weather began.

Her mouth was open. She was trying to speak. Nothing came out but breath, visible and ragged, and her face worked through shapes—words, clearly words, a whole sentence's worth of effort—and produced silence. Mute, I thought. Or struck dumb. There are fevers that do it, and worse things.

And she was holding the shard.

I say "shard" now because that's what we all came to call it, but in that first moment it looked less like broken glass than like a piece torn out of the sky's underside. Maybe as long as my forearm, irregular, faceted along lines that didn't agree with any geometry I knew—it seemed to have more edges when I wasn't looking directly at it. Cold light moved inside it like water finding its level.

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing that made me open my gate instead of calling the elders: I recognized it.

Not the object. I'd never seen the object. But I recognized it the way you recognize a voice through a wall—you can't place it, you can't name it, but some animal part of you is already moving toward the door of the house where it lives. The shard hummed against something in me. Something old. Something I had never once examined, the way you never examine the ground you stand on.

I told myself it was nothing. I'm very good at telling myself things. It's most of my trade.

"You're freezing," I lied, because she plainly wasn't, and opened the gate.

* * *

Inside, I did the sensible things. Fire built up, blanket around shoulders, bread and honey set out untouched on the table while she sat with her hands folded in her lap like a small duchess waiting out a boring sermon. She wouldn't eat. She wouldn't drink. She watched me with eyes the grey-green of thawing ponds, and she did not blink nearly as often as a person should.

I talked, because talking is how I take the measure of people, and because silence in my house has always felt like debt.

"What's your name?" No response, just that pond-water stare. "Where did you walk from? Nobody walks the high passes in—" I gestured at the window, where the wrong snow kept up its patient work. "Nobody walks them ever, honestly. The passes eat people."

She looked at the window too. And I swear on whatever I still owned that her expression changed—not fear, not wonder, but *recognition*, the look of someone seeing their own handwriting on a note they don't remember leaving.

Then she raised the shard and pointed it at me, and spoke.

The language was nothing. That's the only honest description. It wasn't the trader's cant from the coast, wasn't the church-tongue the priests mumble, wasn't the mountain speech the shepherds use to curse their sheep. The sounds were older than sounds have any right to be—consonants like stone settling, vowels held long past comfort, a rhythm that made me think of doors closing in empty houses. Three syllables, maybe four. My skin crawled up my arms in a wave, scalp to wrist, like the time I'd pulled a memory from a drowning man and gotten his last thirty seconds thrown in free.

"I don't understand you," I said, and hated how thin my voice came out.

She tried again. Same words. Same cadence, exactly, repeated with a precision no child has—and then, when I shook my head, her face crumpled with a frustration so adult, so *specifically mine*, that I actually took a step backward and knocked the poker off its hook.

It clattered on the hearthstone. The girl flinched. And the shard—

The shard answered me.

I don't know a better way to put it, and I've had forty chapters' worth of time to find one. When the metal rang against stone, the light inside the shard stuttered, and a sensation went through me like a finger drawn down my spine from the inside. Not sound. Not quite feeling. It was the sense of being *addressed*. Of something enormous turning its attention, slow as a lighthouse beam, onto the exact spot where I stood.

And then, clear as a bell rung underwater, it said—or showed, or meant—my name.

*Mara.*

Not spoken. I need you to understand that. Nothing in the room made a noise. But the knowledge arrived in me fully formed, the way memories arrive when I pull them: complete, sourced, undeniable. My name, in that dead old tongue, pronounced correctly—which is more than anyone alive had ever managed, since Nystrom got flattened into something Danish-sounding generations back. Underneath the name, a weight of meaning I could only catch the edges of. Oathkeeper, maybe. Or oath-breaker. In that language, I suspected, the difference was a matter of tense.

The girl watched me take this in. Her hands tightened on the shard until her knuckles went bloodless, and she pushed it across the table toward me—an offering, a dare, I couldn't tell which.

Every rule I lived by said no. Don't touch cursed goods. Don't handle what you can't price. Never steal from children, and never let a child hand you anything, because children are the one kind of client whose memories come back on you sideways. I had rules the way other people had religion, and they had kept me alive and mostly whole through nineteen years of a trade that eats the careless.

I picked up the shard anyway.

Cold. Gods, the cold—it went through my palm like a splinter of midnight, straight to the bone, and it *burned*, is the paradox I can't fix, burned the way frost burns, and somewhere in that burn my whole nervous system lit up like a struck match. I heard myself make a sound. The room tilted. The firelight bent toward the shard as if leaning in to listen.

And the shard said, in the same silent, total way:

*Mara Nystrom. Daughter of Eirik and Solveig. Heir of the Sundered Gate. We have been waiting for you to pick us up.*

Or that's the translation my mind scrambled together afterward, because what actually entered me was older and bigger than sentences. A gate with its doors torn off their hinges. A winter that had been falling for longer than my whole civilization. Three realms holding their breath. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, a thread of recognition running both directions: the shard knew me, and I—I knew the shard, down in whatever cellar of myself my parents had walled up before abandoning me in the only valley magic couldn't enter.

Because that was the thought that surfaced as I sat there with my hand frozen to a piece of the sky, and it was worse than all the rest: my parents had fled to the Hollow. Everyone said it—quietly, the way you mention a family disgrace. Fled *here*, of all places, the one spot in creation warded against everything uncanny.

People don't hide from something by running to the one place it can't reach unless they are specifically, personally afraid of it.

Unless it's specifically, personally looking for them.

I dropped the shard on the table. It didn't clatter. It landed without a sound, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet, and its light settled back to that deep patient blue, and outside the window the snow kept falling with its jeweler's patience, filling my path, filling the road, filling the valley, like something being poured.

The girl slid off her chair, took the shard back, and tucked it inside her coat against her chest, where it glowed faintly through the fabric like a second heart. Then she looked at me—straight on, pond-grey eyes steady, no blinking—and pointed one small finger at the space directly over her own breastbone, then at me.

Same gesture twice. The grammar was obvious even without words. *You. Me.* An equals sign drawn in air.

I sat down slowly across from her, my hand still aching with borrowed winter, and listened to the snow bury the world outside my door, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever came next had already started—that it had started, probably, years ago, in a valley designed to keep it out, and I had simply been the last person in the Hollow to be informed.

By morning, the elders would come asking questions. By morning, the whole village would know a strange child had crossed the boundary stones that nothing strange had ever crossed.

But that night there was only the fire, and the silence, and the girl watching me across the table like a mirror that had finally decided to show me something true.

She opened her mouth. Tried once more, patiently, to give me her name.

No sound. Just breath. But the shard against her chest pulsed once, gently, like something knocking politely on a door it already owned—and I reached over, almost without deciding to, and covered her small cold hands with my burnt one.

Outside, the snow fell on.
