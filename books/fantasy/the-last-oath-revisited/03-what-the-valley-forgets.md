## 3. What the Valley Forgets

The elders came at dusk, which was their habit. Old men and older women, wrapped in wool the color of ash, shuffling up my lane with the solemnity of people who had never once in their long lives been surprised by anything—and intended to keep it that way.

There were five of them. Mother Hesk walked first, as she always did, leaning on a staff of blackthorn she'd cut from a tree that hadn't grown in the valley for sixty years. Behind her came Aldous Vane, whose family owned half the terraced fields and all of the good opinions; then the twins Sela and Sorrel, ancient sisters who finished each other's sentences and, I suspected, each other's thoughts; and last, trailing like a dog that hoped no one would notice it, old Tam Wicker, who had been my father's friend once, back when fathers were things I had.

They arranged themselves in my kitchen without being asked, because asking was for people with less authority.

I put water on to boil anyway. It gave me something to do with my hands besides hold them.

The child was asleep in the loft above us. She'd eaten half a loaf of dark bread and an entire jar of honey—devoured it, actually, with the desperate efficiency of someone who had gone hungry longer than she could remember—and then curled into the straw near the window like a fox going to ground. The shard was tucked against her chest even in sleep. Even now, in the dimming light, I could see its faint glow through the weave of her blanket, blue-white, steady as a heartbeat.

"She should be given to the snow," Mother Hesk said.

Not a greeting. Not *how do you do, Mara, sorry to trouble you.* Just that. The Hollow's way of talking: skip past everything human, get to the arithmetic.

I set two cups on the table. Then a third, out of stubbornness, though none of them drank.

"The snow doesn't want her," I said.

"Then she'll learn to want the snow." Hesk's voice was dry as seed husks. She was ninety if she was a day, and her eyes were the pale washed-out gray of a thing left too long in weather. "You know what she is, girl. You felt it when you touched that stone."

I had. That was the problem.

*It answers you,* I'd told myself the night before, lying awake while the child slept below. *A shard of dead magic answering a thief. That means nothing. Stones answer heat. Doors answer wind. Everything answers something.*

But it wasn't true and we both knew it, sitting there across from each other, the old woman reading my face like a ledger with the numbers showing.

"Magic cannot enter the Hollow," Aldous Vane said, in his pulpit voice, the one he used at harvest-tithe when he wanted the poor fields assessed generously. "That is the covenant our founders struck. That is why our grandparents crawled into this ditch of the world—to be beneath its notice. And now one of you drags its notice in through our door."

"I didn't drag anything," I said. "She walked. On her own feet. Through the pass, in the first snow this valley has seen in living memory, to my door specifically." I let that sit a moment. "Ask yourselves why."

"Chance," said Sorrel—or Sela; in the firelight they were interchangeable.

"There's no such thing," I said. "Not in the trade I practice. Every memory has a reason it surfaces. Every debt has a shape. A child walks three days through weather that shouldn't exist to reach the one house in the Hollow where the door opens for her? That's not chance. That's intent. Something wants her here, or wants her somewhere only she can find, and either way giving her to the snow tells it nothing except that we're afraid."

"We are afraid," Tam Wicker said quietly, and I almost loved him for it. He was the only one who wouldn't dress the word in finer cloth. "That's what we are, Mara. We're afraid, and we've earned the right to be. Your grandmother remembers—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "The old records remember what it was like before the founders sealed this valley. What came sniffing at the edges every winter. The Hollow exists so that we never have to know those things again."

The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. I took it off the heat rather than let it whistle. Some part of me was aware, distantly, of how loud everything sounded tonight—the tick of the stove, the settle of the roof-beams, the small wet sound of the child turning over in the straw overhead. As if the whole house had leaned closer to listen.

"Give her to the snow," Mother Hesk repeated. "At the boundary stone, at first light. The heralds—" She caught herself. Her thin hands tightened on the blackthorn staff. "If anything is waiting at the edge of the valley, it can have her there. Clean. Outside our lines. Magic cannot cross into the Hollow, but nothing says it cannot wait at the border, patient as winter. Let it take what it came for and go home."

*Patient as winter.* The phrase went through me like a splinter finding a nerve, though I couldn't have said why.

"And me?" I asked. "Am I given to the snow too? Since I'm the one who opened the door?"

"You opened the door," Aldous Vane said, "because you are what you are, Nystrom. A taker of memories. A dealer in other people's heads. Half this council has sold you something they'd give their teeth to buy back, and the other half lives in terror of the day you decide they're worth robbing. Don't mistake our tolerance for affection."

"No," I agreed. "I never have."

He flushed. Sela patted his sleeve, the way you'd calm a horse.

Here is what nobody in the Hollow ever said out loud: they needed me. Not liked, not trusted—needed. In a valley where nothing could be remembered truly well and nothing magical survived a season, I was the closest thing to a wonder they permitted themselves. Grieving widows bought back a hour of their husbands' voices. Guilty men paid me to forget what they'd done. The dying traded whole childhoods for a season of bread. I kept the valley's conscience in jars, and everyone hated the tax collector and paid him all the same.

So when Mother Hesk looked at me across my own table and said *she should be given to the snow*, what she was really saying was: *we will do this ugly thing, and you will help us carry it, because carrying ugly things is your trade.*

And some cold clear voice in the deepest cellar of me—the one I didn't visit often, the one that had no name and no history I could recall—said: **no**.

Not *that's unwise*. Not *the elders will object*. Just: *no*. Flat and absolute and older than any argument, the way a hand says no to a flame.

I've wondered since where that voice came from. I have my suspicions now. But that night, standing in my own kitchen with four cups of untouched tea cooling around me, I only knew that my body had decided something before my mind caught up, and that the decision sat in my chest like a second heart.

"No," I said.

The room went very still.

"Excuse me?" said Aldous Vane.

"I said no. You heard me the first time. Say it again and I'll say it again, and we'll both die of old age before either of us gets bored." I turned my back on the council of the Hollow—which is not done, which has possibly never been done—and busied myself banking the stove, because my hands needed work and my face needed somewhere else to be. "She stays under my roof. She's mine to look after until she can tell me otherwise, and since she can't talk, that may be a long time. Get out of my house."

"Mara." Mother Hesk's voice changed. Softened into something worse than anger—into pity. "Child. Sit down. Listen to sense. Whatever blood runs in that girl, whatever purpose carried her here—it isn't yours. You are a woman of the Hollow. Whatever your parents were, they're twenty years gone, and what they left you is a trade and a roof and nothing else. Don't die for a stranger's errand."

And that—that—made me turn around.

Because she'd said *whatever your parents were* the way people say *whatever that noise was*, dismissing it, filing it away, and something about the phrasing snagged on me like a fishhook. Twenty years I'd lived in this valley and no elder had ever once spoken of my mother and father in any register but grief. Dead young, dead sadly, dead in the rockslide on the west scree, everyone had always been very clear and very kind about the rockslide.

But Hesk hadn't said *dead*. She'd said *gone*. And her hands had flinched on the staff.

"What were they?" I asked.

Hesk's face did something complicated and closed, like a shutter coming down over a window that had been open just a breath too long.

"They were Nystroms," she said. "And they're gone. That's all any of us need remember."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one the valley keeps." She rose, slow as a door swinging, and the others rose with her, a tide of gray wool and disapproval. At the threshold she paused, and for one moment the pity in her face looked almost like fear wearing pity's clothes. "First light, Mara. The boundary stone. If the child is not there, the council will come for her, and we will not come gently, because gentle is how valleys die. Whatever is hunting her will take this whole place apart looking, and we both know it won't stop at your door."

"It's already stopped at my door," I said. "And it knocked."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't expected them to.

Tam Wicker lingered last, the way he always did, fumbling with his cap. Up close he smelled of pipe smoke and sheep lanolin and, faintly, of something else—something green and cold, like the memory of a forest, which made no sense and which I filed away with the hundred other nonsenses of the evening.

"Your mother sang," he said abruptly, not looking at me. "That's all. I just—you should know somebody remembers that. However it ended. She sang."

Then he was gone down the lane, hurrying after the others, leaving me holding a sentence like a hot coal.

*Sang.* My mother sang. I had known—I think I had known—some fact shaped like that, filed under *mother, deceased, details unavailable*, the way you know a number you've never counted. But hearing it aloud cracked something. For one dizzying instant I was standing in a kitchen that wasn't this kitchen, smelling woodsmoke that wasn't this woodsmoke, hearing a melody I could not begin to hum and would have known anywhere—

The sensation passed. It always passes. That's the trick of the Hollow: it forgets things for you, kindly, whether you ask it to or not.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

I climbed the ladder and found the child awake, sitting bolt upright in the straw, the shard clutched in both fists, watching the door with wide dark eyes. She hadn't heard the argument—she'd slept through thunderstorms before, this was no different—but she had heard the silence afterward, the particular quality of a house that has just been threatened.

"It's all right," I lied, in the tone I used on clients waking from taken memories. "Nobody's taking you anywhere."

She looked at me. Then she held up the shard.

I don't know how to describe what happened next without sounding like a madwoman, so I'll say it plainly and you can believe me or not. When she raised it, the light inside it shifted—not brighter, not dimmer, but *aimed*, like a lamp being turned to face something. And the glow fell across the floorboards between us in a shape. A pattern. Deliberate.

Three lines crossing. A gate, maybe. Or a knot.

Or a letter, in an alphabet older than any kingdom, spelling out a word I could not read and somehow understood anyway:

*Wait.*

The child watched me watch it. Then she pointed at the window—toward the pass, toward the edge of the valley, toward whatever waited at the boundary stone with the patience of deep winter—and shook her head, hard, twice. *No.*

Then she pointed at me.

And lay back down, pulling the blanket to her chin, and closed her eyes with the terrible trust of someone who believes the person watching over her has already decided.

I banked the fire. I checked the latch twice. I sat in the chair by the window with my knife across my knees and watched the unnatural snow fall straight down, silent as sifted flour, and I thought about the voice in my cellar that had said *no* before I knew why.

Somewhere out past the tree line, past the terraces and the boundary stones and the invisible line magic could not cross, something waited. Patient as winter.

Come morning, it would learn that patience cuts both ways.

Because I had decided something too—and I still couldn't explain it, not to the council, not to myself, not to the pale-eyed dark—but the deciding felt less like a choice than like a remembering. As if some buried part of me had heard the elders' arithmetic and answered with the only law it had ever kept:

*Nobody gets given to the snow. Not while I draw breath to stand between.*

In the loft, the child slept on, glowing faintly, keeping her secrets.

And far off, at the rim of the world, the winter waited for morning.
