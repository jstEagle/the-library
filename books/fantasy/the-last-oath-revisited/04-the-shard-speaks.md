## 4. The Shard Speaks

Cold is the first thing. Cold before name, cold before mother, cold before the word for cold.

The child knows cold the way other children know breathing.

But this cold is different. This cold lives in her hands. It came through her palms and up her arms and into the place behind her ribs where a heartbeat should be loud, and it made a sound there — not a sound exactly, more a *knowing*, the way you know a door is locked without touching it.

The shard sits in her cupped fingers like a splinter of night.

It is not pretty. Pretty is firelight on snow, pretty is the red berry that means *do not eat*. The shard is none of those things. The shard is a hole shaped like a piece of something, and the something it was cut from is so large the child's mind slides off it every time she tries to see it whole. She tries anyway. She tries every night, lying in the loft above the woman-with-the-tired-eyes' hearth, holding the shard beneath the blanket where no one can see it glow.

It glows the color of deep water under ice. It glows like the last light in a window when everyone has already gone to bed.

And when the house is quiet enough — when even the mice have stopped their small arguments in the walls — the shard speaks.

Not with a voice. Voices are for mouths, and the shard has no mouth. It speaks the way dreams speak: all at once, sideways, true.

*Come*, it says, without saying anything at all. *See.*

*

The first vision comes like falling.

She is standing nowhere. Not in the loft, not in the Hollow, not anywhere with a floor she recognizes. Beneath her feet there is stone, and the stone is broken, and the brokenness goes down forever. She looks up — up is a guess, here, but she makes it — and she sees the gate.

The gate is the size of grief.

Two pillars of white stone, each one taller than any tree the child has ever climbed or imagined climbing, and between them an arch that once held doors, though the doors are gone. Not open. Gone. Torn away, the way a tooth is torn from a jaw, leaving the socket behind. Light pours out of the empty arch — no, not light. She squints into it, this child who has never had words for weather beyond snow and not-snow, and understands: it is *winter* pouring out. Winter as a substance. Winter as a flood. It spills through the broken gate the way water spills through a cracked dam, silent and total and patient, and somewhere far below the frost is eating fields, roads, rivers, names.

There are people at the gate.

She cannot see their faces. Their faces are the one thing the vision will not give her, blurring like breath on glass every time she looks. But she can see what they are doing, because some things do not need faces. They are breaking something. Together, all of them at once, they are bringing something down — a great shape above the arch, a keystone or a crown or a heart, and it is cracking along lines the child somehow already knows, lines like a river delta seen from very high up, and from the cracks comes a sound—

She wakes with her own scream caught behind her teeth, because her teeth do not open, because the woman downstairs told her never to cry out in the night, and the child keeps the rules. Keeping rules is how she stays.

Her palms are bleeding. She does not know when they started bleeding. The shard lies beside her pillow, glowing softly, satisfied, like a dog that has brought back something dead.

She picks it up again anyway.

She always picks it up again.

*

The second vision is winter itself, but winter as a person.

She stands inside the cold and the cold stands around her like a court. There are shapes moving in the white — tall shapes, thin shapes, wearing crowns of frost that grow and regrow like antlers — and they are hungry. That is the whole of what she learns about them, the way you learn everything about a wolf by seeing its eyes: they are hungry, and they have been hungry for a long time, and their hunger has a throne at the center of it.

On the throne sits a woman made of the moment when a fire dies.

The child has watched fires die. In the Hollow, fuel is counted like blood, and sometimes the hearth goes dark and everyone pretends not to notice until morning. That moment — the last coal going grey, the warmth leaving the room faster than you thought possible, the dark coming in like a tide — that moment has a face, and the face is on the throne, and the face is beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful, which is to say: from far away, briefly, right before everything ends.

The queen opens her eyes. They are the eyes of someone counting.

She counts the child. Her gaze crosses the impossible distance between throne and nowhere-stone-floor and lands on the girl holding a shard of a broken promise, and the queen's mouth moves, and although there is no sound the child understands her perfectly, because understanding without words is the only language the shard allows:

*Mine*, says the queen's mouth. Or possibly: *Why?* Or possibly both at once, because from where the queen sits, from the top of a kingdom of hunger, those two words have come to mean the same thing.

Then the frost reaches for her — long white fingers unfolding out of the air like ferns in fast-forward — and the child does the only thing she has ever done well.

She runs.

She runs through the vision the way she ran across the mountains to the valley's mouth, barefoot, breathless, pursued by a cold that wanted her back. And just as the frost-fingers close on the collar of her dream-self, the vision tears —

— and there is fire.

*

Fire should mean safety. Fire means hearth, means soup, means the woman-with-the-tired-eyes humming while the kettle mutters. But this fire is wrong, this fire is everywhere, this fire is the world.

The child stands in burning.

Flames rise off the ground in slow columns, gold and orange and a green she has no name for, and nothing screams, and nothing burns *down* — the fire simply is, the way rain is, the way snow is, a weather made of ending. Ash falls upward. Somewhere in the roar there is singing, or weeping; at this distance they are the same sound.

And in the middle of the fire, standing where the throne stood in the other vision, where the broken gate opened in the first —

a woman.

She has Mara's face.

The child knows this the way she knows her own heartbeat, which is to say: completely, and with terror, and without knowing why. She has known the woman upstairs for three days. Three days of bread crusts and pointed silence and the woman watching her sidelong like one watches a door that might open onto anything. Three days, and already the woman's face is carved somewhere permanent inside the child, the way the shard is carved into her palm.

The woman in the fire has that same face. Older, maybe. Harder. There is a scar over one eyebrow that Mara Nystrom does not yet have, and the sight of it does something to the child that she has no word for — a lurch, a recognition, a grief arriving years too early, mailed to the wrong address.

The woman in the fire is not burning.

The fire bends around her. It parts for her the way water parts for a keel. And she is looking directly at the child — through the fire, through the vision, through whatever distance the shard has folded up between them — and her expression is not triumph and it is not despair.

It is apology.

That is the worst part. That is the part the child carries back up out of the vision like a swallowed stone. Whatever the woman did, whatever is coming, whatever price is standing in the road ahead waiting to be paid — the woman in the fire is *sorry* for it, and she is sorry the way you are sorry for a thing you are going to do anyway.

Her lips move. No sound comes, but the child reads the shape of the words the way she reads everything now, in the language below language:

*Remember for me. Remember for me. Remember—*

*

She wakes before dawn with the shard hot enough to sting and her cheeks wet and her throat aching with all the screaming she did not do.

Below her, the house creaks. The woman-with-the-tired-eyes is awake — the child can tell by the particular quality of the silence, the held-breath quality, the listening quality. Mara Nystrom lies in her narrow bed and listens to the floorboards and pretends, probably, that she is not listening at all.

The child lies in the loft and holds the shard against her sternum and lets its cold pulse against her heartbeat until the two rhythms find each other. Under her palm, the fragment hums its one long note of *come, come, come*, and threads its images together for her one more time, patient as winter, insistent as blood:

A gate, broken. A queen, starving. A woman, burning, sorry, familiar.

Three visions. Three pieces. And the shard wants — the shard *needs*, with a need bigger than the child's whole small body — for her to make the woman understand. To put these pictures into Mara Nystrom's stubborn, guarded, memory-trading head. To make her look south, past the valley's rim, toward the place the frost comes from. To make her *move*.

But the child has no voice. The child has never had a voice in this life; her tongue remembers words the way her feet remember the mountain passes, but the words come out as air, as nothing, as the sound of snow falling on snow. She tried, the first night. She stood in the doorway with the shard blazing in her fist and opened her mouth and the old words came pouring out — the high cant, the oath-language, syllables like struck bronze — and the woman's face went white and shut like a ledger, and nothing got through, nothing ever gets through, and the child had stood there mouthing prophecy into a silence that swallowed it whole.

Words do not work.

So she does what she can do. When Mara comes up the ladder in the morning with bread and that flinty don't-ask-me look, the child takes her hand — quick, before the woman can pull away, the way she takes things, because taking is the one inheritance she can feel working in her own small fingers — and presses the shard into her palm, and holds her hand closed around it, and looks up into the tired eyes with everything she has.

For half a heartbeat, the shard flares.

The woman gasps. Her pupils blow wide and black, and behind them something flickers — the child watches it land, watches the images strike home like arrows into a door: *gate, winter, fire, face, your face, your face* — and then Mara wrenches her hand back as if the shard bit her and the shutter comes down and she says, roughly, "No. Whatever you're doing, no," and turns away, and the moment closes like water over a dropped coin.

Almost.

Almost through.

The child sits on the edge of the loft and hugs her knees and watches the woman below move through her morning — banking the fire, counting coins, checking the seals on her windows the way she checks them every day now, since the snow fell wrong, since the child arrived — and the shard pulses its patient, terrible pulse against the girl's chest.

*Soon*, it says, without saying anything. *She will listen soon. Or the ones in white will arrive, and she will have no choice left at all.*

Outside, the unnatural snow keeps falling, soft and wrong and endless, and far off — at the valley's rim, where magic waits like a wolf at a fold in the hill — something begins, very quietly, to walk downhill.

The child feels it before she hears it: a pressure change, a hush, the specific silence of the valley elders gathering in the square below to decide what must be done about her.

She presses the shard tighter to her heart and starts, silently, to practice the old words.
