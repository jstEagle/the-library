## 5. Old Debts

Corvess came to my door the morning after the elders' council, and he did not knock.

He never knocked. Forty years of living above his shop had taught me that doors were for strangers and Corvess considered himself furniture in my life—present whether or not I acknowledged him, useful, occasionally creaking. So when I opened the door to fetch water and found him already standing on my step with snow in his grey hair and a wrapped bundle under one arm, I only stepped aside and let him in, the way you let in weather.

The girl was asleep in the corner behind the curtain, curled around the shard like a dog around a bone she'd stolen from a bigger dog. She hadn't let go of it since the night it arrived. When she slept, it dimmed to a dull ember-glow, just enough light to read a face by. I had spent two nights reading hers.

"Still here," Corvess said. It wasn't a question, but it was an accusation dressed as one. He set the bundle on my table—a loaf, black rye, still warm, which meant he'd baked it himself at an hour no honest man bakes—and unwrapped his scarf finger by finger, taking his time about it, which was also how he took his time about everything, including telling the truth.

"The elders voted," I said.

"I heard. I was there."

"You were there." I looked at him. He met my eyes the way he always did, steady as a wall, and something in the steadiness of it snagged on me like a fishhook. "You didn't speak up."

"No."

"Why not?"

He sighed and lowered himself into my one good chair, the one with the woven seat that bowed under weight, and folded his hands over his belly the way he did before lessons, back when I was nine and learning the difference between a memory's texture and its taste. *Bitter memories sit forward in the mind,* he'd told me. *Sweet ones sink. You have to reach deeper for the sweet ones, and reach carefully, because they bruise.* I had thought, all these years, that everything he taught me had been practical. Trade craft. Survival. Now, looking at those folded hands, I understood that some of what he'd taught me had been preparation for this exact moment, and I hated him a little for the patience of it.

"Mara," he said. "Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit down anyway."

I sat. The bread smelled wonderful and I hated that too, because it meant he'd planned this, planned it down to the warmth of the loaf, the way you plan a difficult conversation for a child—which is exactly what he was doing, which meant whatever came next was going to be bad enough that even Corvess thought I might need comfort to swallow it.

"The girl," he said. "Tell me again. Everything. From the first snowfall."

I told him. The snow falling upward for three breaths before it fell down. The small bare feet on my threshold, blue with cold but not frostbitten, not touched by the cold at all when I thought about it later, because the Hollow kills cold things gently and quickly and she should have been dead twice over. The shard burning cold in my palm instead of hot. The way it had answered me—not words, nothing so kind as words, but a pressure against the inside of my skull, a recognition, the feeling of your own name spoken by a mouth you can't see.

When I finished, Corvess was quiet for a long time. Outside, the unnatural snow kept falling—the fourth day of it now, snow that didn't melt, didn't drift, just accumulated in flat white silence like the valley was being buried alive at a courteous pace. The Hollow hadn't seen snow in living memory. Magic couldn't enter the valley; everyone knew that the way everyone knows the sun rises. Snow was weather, and weather was natural, and therefore—

"There's no such thing as natural anymore," Corvess said, as if I'd spoken aloud. Sometimes I think after forty years he simply heard my thoughts before I made them. "That's the first thing you have to understand. There hasn't been for a long time. The valley isn't a place magic can't follow, Mara. It's a place magic agreed not to look. And agreements—" his mouth twisted, "—agreements can be renegotiated."

"What are you talking about?"

"I'm talking about your parents."

Three words. I had heard them maybe five times in my whole life, usually from drunk traders passing through who claimed to remember a woman with my jaw, a man with my hands, gone before anyone could ask where from. I had built my entire self out of not asking. That was the deal I'd struck with the world: you don't get parents, but you get Corvess, and Corvess is enough. And now here he was saying the words casually, terribly, the way you'd say *it's raining*, and every stone I'd ever stacked felt suddenly loose.

"My parents," I repeated. "Dead fifteen years. Before you took me in."

"They weren't dead," Corvess said. "They were hiding. There's a difference, and the difference is the reason you're sitting in this chair right now instead of standing in a court of ice swearing oaths you don't understand."

He talked for a long time. I'll give it to you the way it came to me—not clean, because it didn't come clean, it came in pieces with his pauses between, and each pause was worse than the words, because Corvess only paused when he was deciding how much truth a moment could hold.

There had been an order once. Not a legend, not a fireside story—he said the word *order* with the flatness of a man naming a trade guild. The Oathkeepers. They kept a covenant, a binding thing, a stone-and-word contract that held three realms in a single shape. And they had broken it. Not lost it, not fumbled it—broken it, deliberately, on purpose, in one night, rather than let it be used the way it was drifting toward being used.

"One realm wanted to be first among equals," Corvess said. "Winter. Winter is patient and winter keeps excellent records, and over centuries the covenant's language drifted, the way language does, until clauses that meant *mutual obligation* meant *submission*. The Oathkeepers watched it happen for three hundred years. Then they stopped watching and started breaking."

"And my parents were—"

"Oathkeepers. Both lines, back generations. Your mother carried the blood of the First Nine. Your father was a warder—one of the ones who stood at the Gate while the others worked. When the covenant shattered, the Court sent heralds. Not to punish. To *collect*. An oath doesn't die when you break it, Mara. It goes looking for someone to hold it. And the bloodlines that broke it—" he spread his hands, "—the blood carries the debt. It always has. That's what an oath is. A debt with a heartbeat."

"So they ran."

"They ran. And they ran here, of all places, because your mother was clever in a way that frightens me even to describe. Everyone knows the Hollow is dead to magic. What nobody asks is *why*. Why does a valley exist where the impossible politely declines to enter? Who negotiated that?" He leaned forward, and the chair creaked, and his voice dropped the way it dropped when he taught me the dangerous trades—the memory-stealing itself, the ethics of it, the rules. "Your grandmother negotiated it. Before you were born. She bought this valley's blindness with something I was never told the price of, only that it was paid in full and non-refundable. The Hollow isn't a hole in the world, girl. It's a blindfold. Someone put it on the world's eyes so that one family could raise children without the sky noticing."

I sat very still. Behind the curtain, the girl breathed slow and even, and the shard glowed its dull ember-red, and I found I was afraid of the sound of her breathing, which was a new and unwelcome sensation.

"And me," I said. "My trade. Stealing memories. You trained me to steal memories."

"Yes."

"In a valley where magic can't—"

"Can't enter," Corvess said quietly. "But it was already in you. It was in you when your mother carried you across the boundary line, screaming, both of them half-dead from the crossing. The valley mutes what comes in from outside. It has never once been able to mute what was born inside it." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for the first time since I'd known him—since I was seven years old and he'd caught me lifting memories off travelers for bread, and instead of turning me out he'd said *if you're going to do crime, do it with technique*—for the first time he looked old. Truly old, old the way winter is old, not decrepit but patient, ancient, waiting. "Every lesson I ever gave you was a cage, Mara. Every rule. Never steal from children—that wasn't ethics, that was camouflage, because a gift like yours shows its true nature around the young. Keep your takings small, trade them away fast—that wasn't discipline, that was keeping the fire banked. I built you a small life on purpose. A controlled burn. Because if you ever grew into what you actually are, the world outside would feel it. The way a sleeping thing feels a fly walk across its face."

"The Court," I said. My voice sounded strange to me. Distant, like someone else's memory. "It feels us now. That's why the snow."

"That's why the snow."

"And the girl."

He was quiet long enough that I turned fully in my chair to look at the curtain, at the small hump of her under the blanket, the shard's glow pulsing faintly through the fabric like a heart seen through skin.

"The shard answered you," Corvess said. "You told me that yourself. Cold in your hand, and then—it recognized you. Do you understand what that means? The Oathstone doesn't recognize people. It recognizes debt. It woke up in that child's hands and went looking for the debtor, and it found you." He exhaled. "Either you are more your mother's daughter than either of us knew, or the covenant is closer to healing than anyone dreamed, and either way, Mara—either way—the safest thing you can do, the only thing, is exactly what your parents did. Nothing. Stay small. Stay blindfolded. Give the child to the snow like the elders want, grieve for a week, and go back to trading in small bitter memories until you die old and unremarkable in this chair."

The room was very quiet. Snow ticked softly against the window, if snow can be said to tick; this snow did, faintly, like fingernails, like patience.

"You said my parents hid here," I said finally. "And then?"

The pause was the longest one yet. I watched him decide. I watched him lose.

"Heralds found them," he said. "Fifteen years ago. They came to the edge of the valley and they couldn't cross—the blindfold held—but they didn't need to cross. They stood at the rim and they spoke your parents' true names into the air, and an oath hears its own name anywhere, even here." His voice had gone rough. "Your mother walked out to meet them so your father could run with you. He made it four miles. He left you at Corvess's door—my door—with a note and a locket, and the note said *keep her small* and the locket was empty, and I burned both within the hour because keeping them would have been keeping evidence. Then I raised you and lied to you every day for fifteen years, and it was the right thing to do, and I would do it again, and I am telling you now only because the snow has reached my doorstep too and lying has stopped working."

The locket. I had a sudden, total recall of a locket—I had never seen a locket in my life, and yet the image arrived complete, warm metal, a hinge, an emptiness inside shaped like a portrait that was never made—and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't my memory at all. It was leaking off Corvess like heat off a stove. Fifteen years of carrying it, worn smooth from handling, and my gift had picked it out of the air between us without my even trying.

I could take the rest. It would be easy. He was sitting three feet away, defenses down, full of the whole story—what my mother's face looked like, what the note's handwriting slanted like, what my father's last words to him were. One touch. I'd done it a thousand times to strangers.

I didn't move. Some part of me understood, with terrible clarity, that if I stole the rest of this from him, I would never hear any of it freely given again. And something in me—something older than my training, older than the cage—wanted it given. Wanted to be trusted with it. Wanted, absurdly, to earn it.

"Say their names," I said. "Their true names. If speaking them calls the debt, then I'm owed the risk of knowing them."

"No."

"Corvess—"

"*No.*" It cracked out of him like ice splitting, and behind the curtain the child stirred, and the shard flared bright enough to throw our shadows huge against the wall. Corvess stared at the glow, and I watched him arrive somewhere he'd been dreading, watch it land on him like a verdict. "Mara. Look at it. It heard me refuse you and it flared. It's listening through her. It has been listening through her since the night it arrived." He stood up, joints cracking, and crossed to the window, and stood with his back to me looking out at the flat white burying-snow. "There's one more thing. The reason I came today and not tomorrow. A trader came through last night from the eastern pass. Half-frozen, half-mad, wouldn't stop talking. Said he'd seen figures walking the rim of the valley. Tall. White. Moving without bending the snow."

Heralds.

"They can't enter," I said. "You said the blindfold holds."

"It held for your mother." He still wasn't facing me. "But your mother's blindfold was bought by her mother's sacrifice, and sacrifices spend down, Mara. Everything spends down. And whatever is standing on that rim right now has been waiting a very long time for the balance to run low enough." He turned, and his face was the face I'd known all my life and also completely new, the face of a man who had been lying professionally for fifteen years and had just, catastrophically, run out of material. "Stay away from the edges. Stay away from the elders—they'll come for the child within days, mark me. Stay in this house. Bake bread. Be small." He pulled his scarf back on, wound it twice, and at the door he stopped, and for a moment he looked like he might say something else—something with love in it, maybe, or apology.

What he said was: "The bread's still warm. Eat it while it lasts."

Then he was gone, and I sat alone in the ticking snowlight with a warm loaf and a sleeping child and the shape of a locket in my head that I had never owned, and I understood that the ground under my whole life had not merely shifted. It had been moving for fifteen years, patiently, under Corvess's careful feet, and he had known, and he had kept us balanced on it anyway, and we were now, both of us, standing on the last inch of solid ground he had left.

Behind the curtain, the girl woke. I heard her sit up, heard the soft clink of the shard against the floorboards, and then—faint, cracked, the first sound she had made since the night she arrived—her voice, speaking that dead tongue in a whisper, over and over, one phrase, urgent as a pulse.

I didn't know the words.

But I swear to you, on the empty locket, on the burned note, on every memory I had ever stolen: I knew exactly what she meant.
