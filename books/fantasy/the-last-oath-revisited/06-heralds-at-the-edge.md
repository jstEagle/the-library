## 6. Heralds at the Edge

The snow at the rim of the Hollow did not fall so much as wait.

Corvess had lived sixty-one years in the valley and had walked its boundary more times than he could count—first as a boy chasing rabbits to the very edge of the frost line, then as a young man escorting the dying out for burial in ground that would hold them, and lately, alone, because an old man's legs needed reasons and the rim was as good as any. The boundary was not a wall. It was a seam, visible only when you stood close enough: on one side, the brown grass of the Hollow, stubborn and magicless; on the other, a white that had no business existing in autumn, rising in a slow curtain like breath held by something enormous.

He had never seen it snow at the edge before this year. Snow belonged outside, with everything else they'd traded away.

He told himself he'd come up to look at it. That was the story he'd brought with him, the one he'd rehearsed on the climb—the concerned elder, checking the weather, guarding his people. It was even true, in the way most comfortable lies are true: he was concerned. He was checking. He was guarding.

He just hadn't decided yet what he was guarding, or from whom.

* * *

The herald was already there when he arrived.

It stood—or she stood; Corvess could never decide, and suspected the indecision was the point—at the exact seam where Hollow met winter, so precisely placed that one boot heel rested in each world. White robes without hem or stain. A face like a coin left too long in a river, features worn smooth, eyes the blue of deep ice under pressure. Corvess had seen drawings of them in books he wasn't supposed to have kept, woodcuts from the century after the Breaking: *heralds of the Winter Court, messengers of the long season.* In the woodcuts they were beautiful. In person they were worse than ugly. They were patient.

"You are Corvess," it said. Not a question. Its voice was the sound of a lake freezing—one long, fine crack, then silence.

"I'm no one," Corvess said. "A farmer. I walk here sometimes."

"You are Corvess Ansel, called Vess, who came into the Hollow forty years ago carrying a child not yet two winters old." The herald tilted its head, and the motion was wrong—not stiff, but too fluid, like water deciding to be a neck. "You buried your name at the boundary stone and took up turnips. You have been diligent. Diligence is a kind of prayer, among your kind."

Corvess's mouth had gone dry somewhere around *not yet two winters old.* He made himself breathe through his nose, slow, the way he'd taught Mara to calm a frightened animal. "You've mistaken me for someone else."

"Have I?" The herald smiled. It had all the teeth and none of the warmth, which was somehow exactly right and exactly terrible. "Then you won't mind answering a simple question from a stranger. There is a woman in your valley. Dark-haired, sharp-eyed, trades in things that aren't hers. And there is a child who arrived at her door speaking the old tongue, holding fire that burns cold."

"The valley has many people," Corvess said. "And no children like that."

"No," the herald agreed softly. "Not yet."

The word hung between them like the snow itself. *Yet.* Corvess looked past the herald, out over the white country beyond the seam, and saw—for the first time since childhood, since stories—shapes moving in the far distance. Tall shapes. Unhurried. The kind of shapes you see at the edge of sleep and pretend were trees.

"What happens if I say nothing?" he asked. "If I turn around now and go home and pull turnips until I die?"

"Then we wait," the herald said. "Waiting is our whole art. The winter does not knock down doors, Corvess Ansel. It stands outside them until the house grows cold on its own, and then the door opens, and whoever opens it believes it was their idea." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "We have waited eleven hundred years. We can wait for one old man to finish dying."

* * *

He should have turned around then.

He knew it the way you know a floorboard is rotten—you feel it give under your weight and understand, in the half-second before the crash, that you always knew. Instead he heard himself ask, "What do you want with her?"

"With the child?"

"With Mara." The name came out of him raw, and he hated how much it gave away. Sixty-one years of discipline, forty years of practiced anonymity, undone by one girl's name spoken too fast.

The herald regarded him for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had changed register—lower, gentler, the voice of someone settling in beside a sickbed. Later, Corvess would understand that this was the moment the trap closed, and that the trap had never been the threat. The threat was only the frame. The trap was sympathy.

"You love her," the herald said.

"She's my student. My responsibility."

"You found her parents' bodies at the boundary and you carried their daughter inside and you raised her on lies sized for a child and truths rationed like wartime bread. You taught her your trade and your rules and you watched her become better at both than you ever were. And every year on the anniversary of the night you carried her in, you walk to the boundary stone and stand there like a man visiting a grave." The herald's smooth face did not change, but somehow it managed pity anyway. "That is love, Corvess. Ours is simply older than yours."

"Answer the question."

"The Court wants the heir. The child is a key, and keys are useful, but the hand that turns them—" the herald spread one white sleeve toward the Hollow, toward the smoke of hearths below, toward a girl who was at this hour probably pretending not to worry while she burned bread, "—the hand matters more. The last blood of the Oathkeepers. The covenant broke because her family broke it. What remains broken can only be remade by what broke it. Or by what remains of it."

"And if the hand refuses?"

"Then the hand is removed, and we take the key anyway, and the winter comes into the valley the way water comes into a cracked bowl. Slowly. Then all at once." The herald let that sit. "Your magicless sanctuary has been safe because it was beneath notice, Corvess. She has made it noticeable. Her safety ended the night the shard crossed your threshold. The only question left is whether it ends badly or gently."

The wind moved along the seam. On the winter side, the snow bent and straightened. On the Hollow side, nothing moved at all, and Corvess felt the full weight of the difference—the whole gray valley behind him, every life in it, all of it held together by nothing stronger than being overlooked.

"There is a third option," the herald said.

Of course there was. There was always a third option; that was how these things worked, in the old stories he'd read by candlelight with the shutters barred. He almost laughed. Forty years hiding from the Winter Court and here he was, negotiating with it like a man haggling over seed grain.

"Say it," he said.

"The child. Given freely, at this place, before the first hard frost. Freely is important—we cannot enter, and we will not take; taking leaves wounds, and wounds leave trails, and the Court dislikes messes. But a gift—" the herald's smile returned, thinner, "—a gift is clean. A gift binds the giver as much as the given. Give us the key, and the hand becomes unremarkable again. One memory-thief among hundreds, in a valley beneath notice, for the rest of her short mortal life. We withdraw. The snows stop. Your people keep their fires."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we do not withdraw, and we do not stop, and one night—and it will be a night you cannot predict, because we are very good at nights—the valley learns what waits at its edge has never once blinked." The herald stepped back, one heel leaving the Hollow soil, and the gesture had the finality of a door closing. "You have until the first hard frost. Three days, perhaps four. Consider what you owe her, old man. And consider what you owe yourself. Guilt makes poor company for a lifetime. So does grief. But guilt you can spend. Grief only spends you."

* * *

He didn't go home. He couldn't have said why—he walked instead along the rim, south, to the boundary stone where he'd buried his name four decades ago, and he sat down against it like an old dog returning to a kennel it hated.

His name had been different then. His life had been different then. He had been a junior archivist in a court that no longer existed, a minor functionary of a major betrayal, one of the dozens who had helped the Oathkeepers scatter the shards of the covenant and then scattered themselves, running from a winter that punished them by refusing to end. He'd been given the baby by a dying woman at the edge of a burning waystation—*take her where magic can't follow, take her where the Court is blind*—and he had done it. He had done the one heroic thing in his entire careful, cowardly life, and he had spent forty years telling himself the story ended there.

But stories don't end. They just change narrators.

Because he'd known. Even then, half-dead with fear, fumbling a screaming infant through the pass in the dark—he'd known what she was. He'd known whose daughter she was. He'd known that carrying the last Oathkeeper into the Hollow wasn't rescue so much as postponement, that the bill would come due someday, addressed to whoever stood closest when the collector arrived.

And now he was standing closest.

"That's not fair," he said aloud, to the stone, to the dead, to nobody. "I fed her. I taught her to write. I sat up with her through the fever when she was six and she cried for parents she couldn't remember and I lied to her face and held her anyway. That counts. That has to count for something."

The stone said nothing. Stones are honest that way.

He thought about the herald's voice doing its sickbed gentleness. *Consider what you owe her.* As if debt were arithmetic. As if forty years of bread and lessons and lies balanced against delivering a child—a mute child, a child who had looked at him yesterday across Mara's kitchen with eyes old enough to make his skin crawl—into the hands of the thing that had hunted her family for eleven centuries.

He tried to imagine handing her over. Tried it honestly, the way he tried everything: weighing, measuring, pricing. He saw the little girl at the seam, one small hand in his, the other holding cold fire. Saw the white shapes coming across the snow, unhurried, patient, forever. Saw her face when she understood—

He couldn't finish the image. It broke apart somewhere around her eyes, and what replaced it was Mara at nineteen, feverish, gripping his wrist, saying *you're all I have, old man, don't you dare die before I forgive you,* and he still didn't know what she'd meant, and he was suddenly, terribly afraid he'd never find out.

So he did what he had always done with unbearable arithmetic.

He changed the sum.

*It's mercy,* he told himself, and the thought slid into place with the horrible ease of a key into a lock it was cut for. *They'll take her regardless—that's certain, that's weather—but freely, gently, a gift instead of a wound? No hunt. No trail of emptied villages. No winter seeping into the valley while everyone sleeps. One child, who may not even suffer—they said a gift binds, they said clean—who knows what the Court wants her for, maybe nothing cruel, maybe just custody, guardianship, and in exchange: Mara. Mara alive, Mara hidden, Mara safe for the rest of her natural life.* He could save one. Everyone always said it—save who you can, grieve who you can't, that was the whole grim catechism of surviving bad centuries. He'd spent his whole life believing he'd get to choose which one.

Except he already had chosen, hadn't he. Forty years ago, at a burning waystation. He'd chosen the baby. And choosing was a habit like any other, and habits were just graves you dug in advance and lay down in slowly.

"No," he said to the stone, louder, and a bird went up out of the frost-killed grass. "No. That's not—I raised her. She's mine to protect. That's the whole point of me. That's the *only* point of me."

The stone said nothing. The snow at the seam fell and fell, patient as interest accruing.

He sat there until the light went copper and then gray, and he ran the bargain through his mind the way a thief runs stolen goods through his hands, feeling for flaws. And the flaw was always the same one, surfacing wherever he pressed: *freely.* The gift had to be free. Which meant Mara could never know it wasn't. Which meant he would have to engineer it—engineer *her*, the sharpest thief in the Hollow, the girl who could spot a lie in a man's pulse from across a room. It would require misdirection. It would require the one thing he'd sworn on the boundary stone he would never do again.

It would require making sure she was elsewhere when it happened. Making sure she trusted him enough to be elsewhere.

He got up, joints cracking, and brushed the frost from his coat, and faced down the valley toward the lights of home, toward a kitchen where a woman was probably feeding a child who shouldn't exist soup and pretending not to be afraid. Three days, the herald had said. Perhaps four.

He began to walk down.

Behind him, at the seam, the snow kept falling, and something in the white distance stopped moving, and waited, and did not blink.
