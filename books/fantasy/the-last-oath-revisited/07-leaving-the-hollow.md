## 7. Leaving the Hollow

The knock came at the hour when even the Hollow's lamps gave up pretending.

Mara was awake—she was always awake now, sleeping in snatches with the shard wrapped in wool beneath her floorboards and the child curled in her bed like a comma in a sentence no one dared finish. The girl had been with her eleven days. Eleven days of snow that fell without wind, of elders muttering behind closed shutters, of Corvess's visits growing longer and his eyes growing stranger.

She opened the door a hand's width, knife low against her thigh, and found Old Wrenna standing in the dark like something the night had coughed up.

Wrenna had been old when Mara was young. She kept the valley's birth-records and death-records in the same ledger because, she said, they were usually the same event. She had never once bought a memory from Mara, which made her either the most honest person in the Hollow or the least interesting, and Mara had never decided which.

"You need to leave tonight," Wrenna said.

Not *good evening*. Not *child of Nystrom*. Just that, delivered flat as a coin on a counter.

"Come inside."

"Can't. Won't. Listen." The old woman's breath fogged in air far too warm for fog. "The vote's already done, girl. They'll tell you tomorrow it's still being weighed, but I've counted hands my whole life and I know when the counting's over. Dawn after next, they come for her. Bram Halvard's boys. Six of them, and Bram's got a new axe he's been showing off at the tavern like it's a christening."

Mara felt the cold arrive in her stomach before it reached her face. "They voted to give a child to the snow."

"They voted to give a *problem* to the snow," Wrenna corrected. "It sounds better that way. It always does." Her eyes, milky as boiled glass, flicked past Mara's shoulder toward the loft where the child slept. "You should have given her up the first night. I'd have respected that. Hard choice, clean hands. But you didn't, and now you're going to do the other hard thing instead, and I've come to tell you how."

"Why?" Mara asked. "Why warn me? Why care?"

Wrenna smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was an old one, worn smooth like a river stone. "Forty years ago a woman came through here with a baby and a look on her face I'd never seen before or since. Terror and certainty, both at once. I kept her name out of the ledgers. First lie I ever told, and I've told maybe six since." She tapped the doorframe once with a knuckle like a root. "Your mother thanked me by never coming back. So consider this account settled between our families, and let me finish talking, because we haven't got the whole night."

* * *

She talked for a quarter of an hour, and Mara listened with her heart climbing steadily into her throat.

The valley's boundary wasn't a wall. That was the first thing. Everyone in the Hollow spoke of the rim as if it were stone—the way you speak of things you never want to examine—but Wrenna, who walked the high pastures gathering herbs nobody else would touch, knew better. It was a threshold, and thresholds could be crossed. Had been crossed. People left sometimes, though the Hollow forgot them so fast it amounted to the same thing as dying.

"Northwest," Wrenna said, tracing it on the frost of the window with one cracked fingernail. "Past the shepherd's cairn, past where the pines give out. There's a place where the grass grows wrong—all bent the same direction, like it's listening to something downhill. That's the edge. You feel it before you see it. Like walking into a spiderweb you can't find."

"And on the other side?"

"On the other side is everything your parents ran from." Wrenna turned from the window, and for a moment her blind-seeming eyes were very sharp. "Magic doesn't enter the Hollow, child. But blood remembers what the valley makes it forget. You cross that line, whatever sleeps in you wakes up. And things wake up *hungry*."

"I don't have anything in me to wake."

"Then why did the shard burn cold in your hand and not hers?" Wrenna said softly. "Why did it answer *you*?"

Mara had no answer. She had stopped asking herself the question around day four, when the asking started keeping her up past even her usual hours.

"The heralds," she said instead. "Corvess told me about them. White riders at the rim. If magic waits at the edge—"

"Then you go through the gap while the waiting things are looking elsewhere, and you pray." Wrenna shrugged, ancient and unbothered. "I'm eighty-one years old. My advice has never once included a guarantee."

* * *

Packing took twenty minutes, because Mara had spent her whole life ready to be run out of somewhere.

Memory-vials went into the false bottom of her satchel—the good stock, the ones worth food and passage: a fisherman's thirty years of storms, a bride's whole wedding day sold twice, a soldier's homecoming. The shard went last, unwrapped from its wool, and she held it a moment in her bare palm. Cold. Patient. Faintly, at the very edge of feeling, it pulsed against her skin like a second heartbeat running slow.

*Hurry,* it seemed to say. Or perhaps she only wanted it to.

The child woke when Mara lifted her, but did not cry. That was the thing about the girl that unsettled people most, Mara thought—not the dead language, not the glowing rock, but the silence. She watched everything and spent nothing. Now her dark eyes tracked Mara's hands as they moved through the room, and when Mara slung the satchel across her chest, the child reached up and gripped two fistfuls of collar, and held on.

"No going back after this," Mara murmured to her. "You understand that? Whatever's out there."

The girl stared at her with an expression too old for her face, and nodded once, gravely, like a judge accepting terms.

* * *

The Hollow at midnight was a skull wearing a mask of snow.

Mara moved through lanes she had walked ten thousand times and tried to see each one as if for the last time. The tannery's reek, frozen mid-stink. The shrine with its blank-faced god, the only idol in any kingdom carved without eyes, because in the Hollow you did not want things looking back at you. The tavern where she'd sold the miller his own wedding night back to him at triple price, and he'd wept with gratitude.

Her whole life fit in one satchel and a sleeping child, and the strange part—the part that scared her more than Bram Halvard's axe—was how little that hurt. How much it felt like exhaling.

She cut up past the shrine, past the last lamp, onto the goat-track that switchbacked toward the high pasture. Snow came down soft and constant, filling her footprints almost as fast as she made them. Behind and below, the valley lay quiet. No torches yet. No alarm. Wrenna's hands were steady; the ledger of the Hollow had one more lie in it.

At the shepherd's cairn she allowed herself one glance back.

The Hollow looked exactly as it always had: small, lamplit, safe, a cupped hand against the world. And for the first time in her life Mara saw it clearly for what it was—not a sanctuary but a *held breath*. A valley holding itself empty so nothing could get in, and in doing so making sure nothing ever got out either. Her parents had fled into this place and shrunk themselves to fit it, and then died anyway, and the valley had eaten their names within a season.

She thought of Corvess—of his face this afternoon, when he'd spoken of her parents, of the Oathkeepers, of staying hidden, staying small, staying *safe*, the word coming out of him like a prayer worn smooth from use. He loved her. She believed that the way you believe in weather. And love, in the Hollow, meant the same thing it meant everywhere: *be less. Be less, and I can keep you.*

The child stirred against her shoulder. Below them, faint as a struck match, a light appeared in Corvess's window.

He was awake too. Of course he was. He'd known her since she was seven; he probably knew she was leaving before she did.

Mara turned northwest and climbed.

* * *

The pines gave out an hour before dawn.

After them came scrub and scree and a wind with no valley walls to blunt it, and the snow changed character—thicker, wetter, falling as if hurled rather than released. Mara's lungs burned. Her legs shook. The child had long since transferred herself to Mara's back, arms locked around her neck, and Mara had long since stopped noticing the weight, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat until it stutters.

And then the grass began to bend.

All of it, every blade and stalk in the wide pale meadow ahead, leaning the same direction—downhill, westward, combed flat like fur on a fleeing animal. Wrenna had described it precisely, and still it raised the hair on Mara's arms, because it was wrong the way the child's language was wrong: old wrong, deep wrong, a rule being enforced rather than obeyed.

She stopped at the meadow's lip, breathing hard, breath tearing away sideways in the wind.

Beyond the flattened grass the land simply *continued*—more hill, more dark, more snow—but she could feel the difference from here, the way you feel a stair that isn't there. A pressure. A held line. Twenty paces of bent grass, and on the far side of it, everything.

The child slid down off her back and stood beside her in the snow, small hand finding Mara's. The shard, strapped beneath Mara's coat, pulsed faster now. Eager.

"If I cross," Mara said—to the child, to the shard, to the dark, she didn't know—"and it kills me, you keep walking east until you find someone kinder than me. There's supposed to be people like that. I've just never met any."

The girl squeezed her hand. Once. Firm.

Mara laughed—a short, cracked sound, half terror, half something with no better name. Then she stepped into the bent grass.

The first step was cold. The second was cold. On the third step, the world remembered her.

It came up through the ground into her boots, through the air into her skin, through her open mouth into her lungs—magic, raw and total, flooding into a vessel built to hold it and sealed for twenty-three years. It was pain the way birth is pain: total, structural, and somehow, underneath everything, *correct*. Every stolen memory in her satchel lit up at once, every vial singing a different note, and beneath them all something vast turned over in her blood and opened one eye.

Mara went to her knees in the snow.

She was aware of screaming—her own voice, torn out of her—and of the light pouring off the shard through her coat like water through a net, and of the child kneeling beside her with both hands pressed flat against Mara's chest, steady, anchor-steady, humming three low notes of a language older than kingdoms.

The flood crested. Held. Began, slowly, to recede—not gone, but settling, finding its level, a river learning its new banks.

Mara knelt in the snow beyond the Hollow and breathed, and the breath tasted like iron and lightning and, absurdly, like spring.

She looked down at her hands. They were shaking. Beneath the skin of her forearms, faint as veins of ore in dark stone, lines of pale light rose and fell with her pulse.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, that's what I am."

The child beamed at her—beamed, the first full smile Mara had seen on her face—and pointed north, down the dark slope, to where the world went on forever.

Behind them, far below and east, a bell began to ring. Once. Twice. A third time.

The Hollow had noticed the empty bed.

Mara stood, pulled the child up onto her hip, and ran north into everything.
