## 8. First Magic

The boundary crossed us before we crossed it.

One step I was in the Hollow's gray hush, where the air sat flat as old water and nothing hummed. The next step my skin lit up like a struck bell.

I went down on one knee in the frost-burnt grass with a sound tearing out of me that I didn't recognize as mine. It wasn't pain exactly. It was *hearing* — after twenty-six years of silence, every sense I owned had been deaf, and now the whole world was shouting through them at once. The grass screamed its slow green patience. The wind carried ten thousand small messages from everywhere it had been. The child's hand found my shoulder and her touch rang through me like a coin dropped down a well.

And underneath all of it, under everything, something was waking up in my blood.

I'd felt echoes of it inside the valley — the way memories came loose for me when other people's wouldn't, the way I could slide into a mind like a hand into a glove. But that had been trickery. Sleight of thought. This was different. This was a furnace door swinging open in my chest, and everything I was standing too close to it.

"Mara." The girl said my name. Not in her dead language. In mine. Flat, clear, careful — the way you'd say a word you'd only just learned but already owned.

I looked at her. Her eyes were the color of the shard, and the shard was out of her fist now, hovering an inch above her palm, turning slowly, shedding cold light across both our faces.

"You knew," I rasped. "You knew what would happen when we crossed."

Her expression did the thing it always did — closed like a book being set gently face-down. She held out the shard instead of answering, which was answer enough.

I didn't take it. I couldn't. My hands were shaking too badly to hold anything, and some animal part of me understood that if I touched that stone while the furnace was open, there was no telling what would pour through.

So I knelt in the dead grass at the edge of a dying world and shook, and the child stood beside me and waited, patient as winter, until I could stand again.

* * *

We walked north because the road ran north, or what passed for a road out here — two ruts of frozen mud stitched together by cartwheels and hope. The Hollow sat behind us under its dome of gray nothing, and ahead the land fell away in long brown folds toward the river country, all of it wearing the wrong season like a stolen coat. Frost in high summer. Fields gone white and brittle. A farmhouse with its roof caved in under snow that shouldn't have existed.

Magic lived out here. I could feel it now, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. It pooled in low places and drifted along hedgerows. It made the frost patterns on fence posts spiral in shapes no water ever froze into. And it kept looking at me. That was the only word I had for it. Every thread of power within half a mile turned its attention my way like a room full of people noticing a stranger walk in.

"Stop staring," I muttered at a hedge. The hedge, naturally, did not stop.

The child walked close at my side, and where she walked the pressure eased, as though her presence gave me somewhere to hide. She'd done that since the first night. I'd told myself it was coincidence. Out here, past the boundary, with the furnace open in my chest, I was running out of ways to call things coincidence.

Near midday we heard the soldier before we saw him — a jingle of tack, a wet cough, the rhythm of a horse walking lame. We got off the road, into the shadow of a leaning barn, and watched him come around the bend.

He wore no livery I recognized: boiled leather gone gray with road salt, a spear slung across his back, a sword he carried more out of habit than threat. Young. Twenty, maybe. Face chapped raw by cold that had no business existing in harvest month. He reined in at the ruined farmhouse, dismounted stiffly, and stood looking at the collapsed roof for a long moment with an expression I knew from a hundred faces in the Hollow — the look of a man taking inventory of a loss.

Then his head came up, and he looked directly at our barn.

I should have stayed still. I'd spent my life staying still. But the furnace was open, and the man was afraid, and fear has a smell that magic apparently finds delicious, and before I understood what was happening something reached out of me — not my will, not quite, more like my hunger given legs — and touched him.

He swayed in the saddle. His horse stamped.

And his life poured into me.

Not a memory. Not a scene. His *whole life*, uncompressed, unfiltered, every year of it arriving in the space between two heartbeats —

— a boy on a river dock learning to gut fish because his father's hands shook too badly, the knife slipping once, twice, the scar he still carries; a recruit yard where a sergeant broke a wooden practice sword over his back and called it training; the first man he killed, a farmer with a pike and no armor, the sound that farmer made, the sound *he* made afterward in the latrine trench where nobody could hear; his mother's funeral, the priest's lies about peace; a woman named Sera who wrote him letters he could barely read and kept every one anyway; the order that had brought him onto this road, a lord's levy chasing rumors of a witch-child, burn orders folded in his coat pocket that he'd read six times and hadn't decided whether to obey —

I came back to myself screaming.

Or trying to. What actually came out was a thin cracked sound, because my throat had locked. I was on my hands and knees in the dirt and I was *him*, for one endless second I was Tammas Reeve of Millbrook, twenty years old, and I knew the weight of his mother's coffin and the taste of Sera's letters and the exact temperature of the blood on the farmer's pike, and I could not find the edges of myself anywhere, could not remember my own name, could not —

Hands. Small hands, gripping my face, forcing my eyes to hers.

The shard blazed between us, cold as a plunge through ice, and the cold cut me free of him the way a blade cuts a rope.

I gasped. The barn. The frost. The girl's face inches from mine, fierce and ancient and eleven years old, maybe, if you measured her body instead of whatever was looking out through it.

"Breathe," she said. "Name five things."

"What?"

"Five things. Real ones. Now."

"The barn," I croaked. "The road. Your hands. The — the horse. The soldier's name is Tammas Reeve and he's twenty and he doesn't know yet whether he'll obey the burn order in his coat, and I know that, I know *everything about him*, how do I know —"

"Because you took it." She said it without cruelty. That was somehow worse. "Let go of the rest. Keep the name if you want. Let the rest go home."

"I can't —"

"Yes you can. You've been giving memories back your whole life. Do it backward."

She was right, and I hated that she was right, and I squeezed my eyes shut and did the only thing I knew how to do — the trader's work, the careful handling, except in reverse. I gathered the flood of Tammas Reeve into something like shape and pushed. It resisted. It *wanted* to stay; some part of me wanted it to stay, wanted his mother's kitchen and Sera's letters the way a starving woman wants bread. I shoved harder. Felt the current turn, flow back along whatever invisible cord bound us, and snap.

On the road, the soldier straightened in his saddle, blinked, frowned at the barn as if trying to remember why he'd been looking at it, and rode on. He never checked his coat pocket. He never learned what had brushed him.

I stayed on my hands and knees a while longer anyway.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, walls half-standing, a hearth that drew well enough. The child built the fire — she was better at fire than I was, better at most practical things — and I sat against the wall holding my own hands like they belonged to someone else.

Which was, I was beginning to understand, closer to the truth than comfort allowed.

In the Hollow I'd been a thief. A good one, careful, disciplined, governed by rules I'd carved myself: never from children, never more than needed, never leave a person less than I found them. I'd told myself the gift was small. A parlour trick with a market value. The elders said people like me were stunted — magic's leavings, the dregs that even a dead valley tolerated.

But the valley hadn't been suppressing my gift. It had been *containing* it. And now the lid was off, and what was inside me wasn't a parlour trick. It was a thing that could reach across forty feet and swallow a stranger entire. It had happened without my consent. Without my *wanting*, mostly. Mostly. And the part that horrified me most, sitting there in the firelight cataloguing what remained of Tammas Reeve inside my skull — the name, the scar on his thumb, the melody his mother used to hum, fragments that hadn't flowed back, that I'd kept without deciding to keep — the most horrifying part was how natural it had felt. Like a door I'd always had, finally opened to its full width.

"How much of him did I keep?" I asked.

The child fed a stick to the fire. "Little. You'll lose the rest in sleep."

"And if I hadn't let go? If I'd held on?"

She looked at me across the flames, and whatever was behind her young face considered the question honestly, because it always did. "You'd have had all of him," she said. "And he'd have had none of himself. He'd have walked to the next village and forgotten his own name by the gate. It's happened before."

"To Oathkeepers."

"To Oathkeepers." A pause. "That's what the Court tells stories about, in the places where they still tell true ones. Not the breaking of the Gate. The keeping of it. What the keepers became when they stopped being careful."

My stomach turned over. "Corvess said my parents fled the Hollow to hide their bloodline. He never said what the bloodline could *do*."

"He probably didn't know." The child poked the fire flat, businesslike. "Or he did, and loved you too much to say it."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. Comfort is for after." She settled back against the opposite wall, the shard dimming against her chest to a faint coal-glow. "Sleep, Mara. You'll need it. And when you wake, practice."

"Practice what?"

"Reaching on purpose. Small things. A bird's song. A rabbit's fear. You're going to meet worse than a tired soldier on this road, and next time it might be better if the door opens when *you* choose."

I lay down on the hard floor and pulled my cloak around me and stared at the rafters, where frost had grown in spirals no water ever froze into.

Fear of what I was becoming sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. But underneath it, quieter, in a place I wasn't ready to look at, sat something else — the memory of the flood itself, the vastness of another life pouring in, and the terrible, undeniable fact that some part of me had *loved* it.

I slept. And dreamed, for the first time in my life, in someone else's dreams — a dock on a brown river, a woman humming, a knife slipping twice.

* * *

Dawn came up bruise-colored, and with it, smoke.

A thin column of it, rising from the pass two miles north where the road bent through the hills. Too much smoke for a chimney. Not enough for a town. The amount a patrol makes burning a farmstead, or a farmstead makes burning its own furniture to survive the wrong season.

The child was already standing at the wall gap, watching. The shard pulsed against her chest in a rhythm I was starting to recognize — quickening when trouble came, steadying when it passed. Right now it beat fast.

"Heralds?" I asked.

"No." She tilted her head, listening to something beyond hearing. "Worse. Hungry people."

I joined her at the gap. The smoke column bent eastward in the morning wind, and beneath it, tiny with distance, figures moved on the road. Armed. Organized. Moving with the particular economy of men who had stopped wasting motion weeks ago.

Tammas Reeve's levy. Chasing rumors of a witch-child. Carrying burn orders one of them hadn't decided whether to obey.

"They're looking for us," I said.

The child didn't answer, which was answer enough. She turned from the gap and looked up at me, and the shard threw her shadow long and strange across the ruined floor.

"Then we decide," she said, "who they find."

Down the slope, the smoke thickened, and the first of them crested the hill onto our road — and stopped, every man at once, as if the wind had told them something. As if the land itself had pointed.

At the barn. At us. At the place where, I realized with a cold drop through my middle, my footprints in the frost were steaming — glowing faintly, melting the white around each print, writing a trail across the fields for anyone with eyes to follow.

Magic was leaking out of me with every step I took.

And I had left a trail straight to our door.
