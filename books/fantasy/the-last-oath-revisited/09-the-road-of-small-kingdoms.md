## 9. The Road of Small Kingdoms

The first thing you learn outside the Hollow is that the world is not one world. It is a hundred small ones, each with a man at the center of it who has decided, usually through violence, that he is its weather.

We crossed three of them in nine days.

The child rode on my hip or my shoulders or, when my arms failed, slung against my back in a blanket I'd bought from a widow with a memory I'd paid for. She never spoke where strangers could hear. She watched everything. She had a way of looking at a warlord's gate the way other children looked at sweet stalls, and it unnerved me more than the gates did.

The first kingdom was a river bend called Tallow, ruled by a man named Brannock who had once been a sergeant and had decided, when the army that made him a sergeant dissolved, that dissolution was a state of mind. He held the only bridge for forty miles, and his toll was not coin. Coin he had. What Brannock wanted was the past—specifically, the past of a woman named Sefa, his late brother's widow, who had witnessed something at his brother's death that Brannock needed to un-know before it un-made him.

"I don't want it changed," he told me, in a hall that smelled of tallow and wet dog, because everything in Tallow smelled of tallow and wet dog. "I want it gone. From her. She looks at me across the supper table and she *knows*, and knowing is worse than the knife."

I'd been in the Hollow too long. My first instinct was the old trade—take what's offered, give nothing back, keep your own ledger clean. But we were three days from anywhere, the child needed meat, and the heralds were behind us. I could feel them the way you feel a storm through a wall: pressure, not yet weather.

"Memories don't come out clean," I told him. "I take one, I take the edges with it. She won't just lose what she saw. She'll lose the shape of the room, the smell of the rain that night, maybe the sound of your brother's voice. Grief is a woven thing. You can't pull one thread."

Brannock's jaw worked. "She'd want that," he said finally. "She told me once she'd cut it out with a fish knife if she could reach it."

That, I believed. That, I could work with.

I sat across from Sefa in a side room while Brannock waited outside like a man outside a birthing chamber, and I asked her twice if she was sure, and she said yes twice, and the second time her voice cracked in the middle like ice under a boot, which is its own kind of yes.

Taking a memory willingly given is a different animal from theft. In the Hollow I'd always worked the unwilling ones—brushing against a stranger at the market, a fingertip to a sleeping drunk's temple, quick as pickpocketing. Willing memory comes slow and warm, like drawing thread from a spool that wants to unwind. Sefa held my hands and wept without sound and gave me the night her husband died: the bridge, the argument, the way Brannock had stepped back when the knife came out instead of stepping in. The way he'd let it happen. The way he'd then inherited the bridge and the tolls and the widow both.

I understood, holding it, why he needed it gone. Not from her.

From himself. He wanted her eyes emptied because his own were full.

I took it. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left that nobody's counterfeited. I took it, and I gave her in exchange a memory of her own choosing—her wedding day, which she'd offered, which was hers to give—and I walked out past Brannock without looking at him, because if I'd looked at him I'd have seen a man buying his own acquittal, and I'd have had to decide whether that made me a healer or a hired eraser.

The child looked up at me as we crossed the bridge.

"You took a bad thing," she said. Not a question. Her voice, when she used it, was careful, each word placed like a stone in a ford.

"I took a true thing," I said. "From someone who wanted it gone."

"Is that the same as a bad thing?"

"I don't know yet."

She nodded, satisfied, as if *I don't know yet* were an answer she'd expected and could file away. She was always filing things away. Sometimes I caught her watching me the way I watched marks, and I wondered what she was learning that I'd have to un-teach later, or worse, that I wouldn't.

* * *

The second kingdom taught me the other direction.

His name was Orlaz and he held a pass through the hills where the road narrowed to a cart's width, and he was not subtle. He'd heard what I'd done at Tallow—word travels faster than people in the small kingdoms; rumor rides the wind and doesn't pay tolls—and he wanted what Brannock had been too squeamish to ask for.

"There's a man in my dungeon," he said. "He led a revolt. Killed eleven of my men. I'm going to hang him tomorrow, and then I'm going to hang his brother next week, and his cousin the week after, because that's how you end a revolt—root and branch. But the man himself, he's got something I want. He knows where the revolt's gold is buried. He won't say. So you're going to take it out of his head."

"And then?"

"Then hang him. I keep my word on the small things. It's how people learn to trust me on the big ones."

I stood in Orlaz's hall with the child's hand in mine and I did arithmetic I hated. We had food for two more days. The pass was the only way east. Behind us, at a distance I could no longer pretend was comfortable, the winter was coming down the map like a lid closing, and somewhere in that whiteness were heralds in white who did not need passes or roads or permission.

"He's a murderer," Orlaz said, reading my hesitation as negotiation. It was, a little. That's the part I keep circling. It was, a little. "Eleven men. You think he dreams of them? You'd be doing him a kindness, taking what's in his head. Let him hang empty."

That was the argument that got under my skin, and it got under it because it was *almost* right. I knew what it was to hold a memory that poisoned everything it touched. I'd spent my whole life in a valley that refused to hold memory at all, and I'd told myself it was sanctuary, and some days it was. So yes: a man about to die, carrying eleven deaths and a buried treasure and a cause, might be better off hollow.

But better off hollow for whose sake?

I went down to the dungeon alone. The child waited at the top of the stairs, which she never did, and I understood that too: she didn't want to see what I was about to decide, whichever way it went.

The man's name was Doss. He was maybe thirty and looked fifty, and when I told him what I'd been sent to take, he laughed until he coughed.

"Take it," he said. "Take all of it. Take the gold's location, take my brother's name, take my mother's face while you're at it. Take everything. What's it matter? I'm dead tomorrow."

"And your brother and your cousin? They hang after you. Orlaz told me."

Something moved behind his eyes—the last thing a man about to be emptied still owns, which is worry for someone else. "Then don't take the gold," he said. "Take it, but don't give it to Orlaz. Bury the knowledge somewhere he can't dig. Let him hang us all and never find his prize. Let the revolt cost him something even losing."

I could have done that. I want you to know I thought about it for exactly as long as it takes a candle to gutter, and then I did something worse, or better, or both.

I took the gold's location, and I gave it to Orlaz, because a bargain's a bargain and I had a child to feed and a pass to cross and a war to lose slowly at the far end of the world. But I took it *last*. First I took Doss's memory of the eleven men he'd killed—willingly, gratefully, he wept while it left him like a man passing a kidney stone—and then his memory of the torture, and then, because he asked, his memory of his mother's face, so he could hang without her watching.

And then I sat in that cell with a dead man's whole life stacked in my head like stolen plates, and I gave one thing back before the guards came: his brother's name. Just the name. So the last thing in him wasn't nothing.

Orlaz got his gold. Doss hanged empty except for a name. We crossed the pass by noon.

That night the child wouldn't eat. She sat across the fire with her knees drawn up and looked at me across the flames, and finally she said: "You gave him back one word. Why one?"

"Because one was all I could spare without Orlaz noticing."

"That's not why."

I fed the fire instead of answering.

"You kept the eleven deaths," she said. "You could have dropped them in the river. You kept them."

"I keep everything I take. That's the trade. That's the whole—" I stopped. "Yes. I kept them. I don't know why I kept them."

"I do," she said. "So someone remembers them. Orlaz wants them gone. Doss wanted them gone. So now they're only in you." She looked at me with those eyes that were older than her body had any right to. "That's what you are now. The place where gone things go."

I didn't sleep that night. I lay under a lean-to with a dead man's eleven deaths folded into me like letters I'd never send, and I understood that the child had named my condition more precisely than I ever had. Every thief becomes a warehouse eventually. The question I couldn't answer, staring up at a sky the color of dirty wool, was whether a warehouse is a service or a sickness.

* * *

The third kingdom was the smallest and the worst, because it was run not by a man but by a grief.

Her name was Maren—no relation, the coincidence sat in my chest like a swallowed stone—and she had lost her son four years ago to a fever, and she had responded by annexing the valley town around her house with forty hired spears and a tax on everything that moved. Her toll was simple: travelers wept at her son's grave. That was it. She sat by the headstone in all weathers and made the world mourn with her, and if you couldn't produce tears, her spears produced them for you.

We might have wept and walked through. But as we passed the grave, the child stopped. And Maren looked up—this gaunt woman with frost in her eyebrows and four years of winter in her face—and the child looked back at her, and something passed between them that I have no clean words for.

"She's warm," Maren said. Not to me. To the child. "Why are you warm? Everyone's cold. Everyone's been cold since he—"

"She can't talk to you," I said quickly. "She's mute."

But she wasn't, and Maren's eyes went from the child to the shard at her neck, which was glowing through the cloth, faintly, the way coals glow under ash, and Maren's spears were suddenly very close and very interested.

"You're the memory woman," Maren said. "Tallow. The pass. I hear things." She stood, and up close she smelled of winter and unwashed wool and something underneath that was almost sweet, like fruit going bad. "Take it," she whispered. "Take the four years. Take the fever night. Take him dying in my arms. I'll pay you anything. I'll give you the town. I'll give you the *spears*. I just want to stop waking up with his last breath in my ears."

Here is what I should have said: no. Here is what a good person says: grief is not a wound to be cut out; it's the scar tissue of love, and cutting it out kills the love too. Here is what I actually felt, standing there with the shard warming against my collarbone and the heralds somewhere behind us and a child who needed walls around her more than she needed my virtue:

I felt the pull. The market-pull. The old Hollow reflex, the one that had fed me for twenty years: *here is a woman with more memory than she can carry, and everything she'd pay to be rid of.*

And beneath that, worse, a colder thought, the thought that made me sick at my own roots: *a town. Walls. Forty spears. Safety for the child, bought with one surgical theft.*

I could have done it. I could have taken the fever night and left Maren standing at a grave that meant nothing to her, free, light, hollowed, and grateful. She'd have thanked me. That's the part that turns my stomach even now—she'd have *thanked* me, and meant it, and the child would have watched the whole transaction and filed it away in whatever ledger she keeps behind those eyes.

Instead I took Maren's hands, and I did the one thing in my power that wasn't theft.

I gave.

I reached into the hoard I'd been carrying since the pass—Doss's eleven deaths, Sefa's bridge, the widow's wedding, a soldier's whole life from the boundary road—and I found the one thing in all of it that was grief-shaped and love-shaped and true, and I pressed it into Maren's mind like a coal into a banked fire: a memory that was not hers. A mother singing. Not her mother. Not her song. But a mother, and a song, and the specific unbearable tenderness of a voice that will one day stop.

I know what you'll say. That it was a lie. That a borrowed grief is counterfeit coin. But Maren sank down against the headstone and wept—real weeping, the first in years, she told me later, that didn't taste of fever—and when she stood up she said, "Who was that?" and I said, "A woman I stole from," and she said, "Then she's in you, and now she's in me, and that's two graves singing instead of one."

She let us through the town. She gave us bread and a room with a lock. And in the morning she did something I didn't expect: she sent her spears' captain to me with a message. There were white riders on the south road, she said. Three of them. Asking after a woman and a child. Asking in a voice that made the tavern dogs go silent.

* * *

We left before dawn, north and east, away from the south road, toward the one place on my map that had a name older than any warlord: Vellum, the archive-city, where the small kingdoms gave way to something older and stranger—libraries with walls thicker than fortresses, and scholars who traded in knowledge the way Orlaz traded in fear.

The child walked beside me with her hand in mine, and the frost came down the hills behind us like a tide with a schedule, and I turned her words over and over as we walked. *The place where gone things go.*

Because here is what I hadn't told her. Here is what I'd barely told myself.

The hoard was getting heavy. Not in the body—in the *self*. I could feel Sefa's bridge leaning against my own memories of the Hollow like a stranger in my house. Doss's eleven deaths had opinions. The soldier's whole life from the boundary road had *children*, and sometimes when the child laughed, a grief that wasn't mine rose in my throat, and I had to stop and stand very still until it passed through me like weather.

I was becoming a crowd.

And somewhere behind us, three riders in white were following a trail that wasn't footprints. It was me. Every theft left a seam, I realized—every memory pulled from an unwilling or a willing mind left a thread hanging, and the heralds could *feel* the threads. I'd been leaving a rope of myself across three kingdoms, bright as blood on snow, and I hadn't known, and now the child had stopped at the crest of a hill and was looking back south with an expression I'd learned to dread, because it was the expression of someone reading a sentence she'd read before.

"They know the road we took," she said.

"How?"

"Because you left yourself on it." She looked up at me. "Every place you took something, you left a little of you holding it. They're not following us, Mara. They're following your leavings."

The wind came down off the hills and it was the wrong wind, the wind from the wrong season, and far to the south, at the edge of seeing, the sky had gone the white of a page with nothing written on it yet.

Vellum was six days east. The white riders were three days south and closing.

"Then we stop leaving leavings," I said, and heard how it sounded, and the child heard it too, and neither of us said the rest of it out loud: that a thief who stops thieving in a world of tolls and walls and winters has exactly two currencies left.

What she carried.

And what I remembered.

The child's hand tightened in mine, and the shard against my collarbone pulsed once, cold and bright, like a word at the start of a sentence I was going to have to finish.
