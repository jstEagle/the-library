## 10. A Language Older Than Kings

The problem with living forty years inside an archive was that Aldous Wren had forgotten how to be surprised.

He knew this because he had catalogued his own surprise, once, as an exercise—a young man's vanity project, *An Index of Wonder*, twelve pages of things that had made his heart stumble: the first time he read the Vellum Concordance aloud and felt the room change temperature; the night a fire in the west stacks revealed a sealed door nobody had recorded; the taste of plum wine shared with a visiting cartographer who kissed him badly under the astrolabe. He had kept the index in a drawer for decades, taking it out on bad days like a physician consulting old symptoms.

He had not added an entry in eleven years.

Now he sat in the Hall of Dead Tongues with a cup of tea gone cold beside him and a transcription that refused to be nothing, and he felt the drawer calling to him.

The transcription had arrived three days ago with the autumn caravans, one sheet among four hundred, folded into a satchel of gate-tolls and gossip. The gate-scribes of Vellum were supposed to record anything anomalous—new coinage, new plagues, new gods—and most of what they recorded was merchants cheating each other with inventive arithmetic. But Master Ellery at the Southgate had a careful hand and a cautious soul, and he had written:

*Woman and child, road-worn, paid passage in traded recollections (see attached protocol breach report). Child speaks no trade-tongue. Attached are the sounds as best I could mark them, using the Fenwick notation.*

Fenwick notation. Nobody used Fenwick notation. Fenwick notation had been designed two hundred years ago to capture a language from the drowned coast, a language of clicks and breath that no living throat produced anymore, and it survived only in the Hall of Dead Tongues because Aldous Wren was the last person alive who could read it fluently.

He looked at the sheet again. He had been looking at it for three days the way a man looks at a door he is afraid to open.

*Attached are the sounds as best I could mark them.* Fourteen phrases. Ellery apologized twice for his accent-marks. And then, in a postscript that had kept Aldous awake two nights running:

*The mother—I assume the mother—understood me when I spoke Vellumi. She answered in it. But she answered slowly, like a woman translating in her head, and when the child spoke she listened the way scholars listen. I have served this gate nineteen years. I know what languages sound like. This did not sound like any language. It sounded like language remembering how to be language.*

Aldous set down the sheet and rubbed his eyes.

He had spent his life on dead tongues. It was not a heroic life. It was a life of marginalia, of cross-references, of being the man consulted when a tomb inscription defeated someone else. He had married the archive instead of a person, and the archive had been a faithful if chilly spouse. He knew, with the intimacy of long habit, exactly how languages died: not all at once but in pieces, grammar outliving vocabulary, vocabulary outliving song, until the last speaker died mid-word and the tongue became marks on vellum, which became marks on stone, which became nothing, which became a rumor that there had ever been marks at all.

Fourteen phrases in Fenwick notation sat in front of him, and they were not nothing.

They were not even close to nothing.

He read them aloud for the hundredth time, quietly, because the Hall of Dead Tongues was empty at this hour and the acoustics were kind. His voice was reedy now, but it still remembered how to shape the Fenwick breath-marks, the swallowed clicks, the vowels that started in the chest and ended somewhere behind the teeth.

"*Seth-ka-vael. Seth-ka-vael, iorun-dath.*"

And the hall answered.

Not literally—he was seventy-one years old, not a fool—but the acoustics of the vaulted ceiling took the phrase and returned it softened, rounded, and in that echo something clicked into place that had been sitting loose in him for forty years. The stress pattern. The way the second syllable leaned on the first like a child leaning on a parent. He had seen that stress pattern before. Not in a dead coastal tongue. In the *forbidden* shelves.

There were three locked cabinets in the Hall of Dead Tongues, and every archivist learned about them the way children learn about wells: don't, and don't ask. Officially they held heretical grammars confiscated during the Purist Reckonings—languages deemed politically dangerous because their speakers had been dangerous. Unofficially, in forty years, Aldous had opened two of the cabinets exactly once each, out of scholarly duty, and found them mostly full of tedious polemics and worse poetry.

The third cabinet held a single manuscript. He knew because he had dusted it for decades without opening it, and because the catalogue entry—which he had written himself, young and thorough—read:

*Cant fragments, provenance unknown. Recovered from the Sundered Gate excavations, year 214. Oathkeeper origin alleged; unverified. Access restricted by order of the Archivist-Prime. Note: the notation system employed appears to predate Fenwick and may represent its ancestor.*

Oathkeeper cant. The secret speech of the order that had shattered the Oathstone and ended the world's warm age. Every schoolchild knew the story the way schoolchildren know all their monsters: the Oathkeepers, sworn to hold the covenant between the three realms, had broken it instead—pride, or fear, or treachery depending on which kingdom told it—and the Sundering had let the long winter pour in like water through a cracked dam. Nine hundred years of frost. Nine hundred years of the realms turning inward, walls rising, roads dying, magic retreating to the strong and starving the weak.

Nine hundred years, and not one word of the traitors' own tongue had survived. Scholars had looked. Aldous had looked, in his arrogant twenties, and found ash and rumor, and had written a monograph titled *The Linguistic Silence of the Oathkeepers* that had made his reputation precisely because it proved a void.

A void did not walk up to a gate-scribe at Southgate in the body of a small mute girl and speak fourteen phrases of perfect, breathing grammar.

His hands were not steady as he unlocked the third cabinet.

* *

The manuscript smelled of cold, which was impossible and true. Paper taken from a frozen ruin sometimes kept the frost in it for years, and this fragment had been recovered from the Sundered Gate itself, where winter exhaled eternally through the half-sealed wound in the world. Aldous laid it flat under the reading-lamp with the care owed to a relic, though he told himself it was merely old.

It was a scrap. Half a page. Someone had begun teaching the cant to a learner—there were glosses in Old Vellumi in the margin, the teacher's hand neat, the student's practice-lines wobbling below like fledglings. And at the top, in that older notation, the ancestral cousin of Fenwick's marks, a single line repeated three times, each repetition steadier than the last:

*Seth-ka-vael. Seth-ka-vael, iorun-dath.*

Aldous sat back, and the cold tea, and the lamp-hiss, and forty years of scholarship all fell away at once, and he was simply an old man in an empty hall with his heart going like a boy's.

It was the same phrase. Word for word. Stress for stress. The gate-scribe had heard a child at Southgate repeat, over and over—for Ellery noted this too, *she says it constantly, like a prayer or a name*—the very first sentence some long-dead learner had practiced nine centuries ago at the foot of a broken gate.

He began the translation the way he'd taught a generation of students to begin: phonetics first, then structure, then meaning, then doubt. The margin-glosses helped; the old teacher had translated this exact phrase. *Seth-ka-vael*: the gloss rendered it clumsily, *stone-of-the-speaking*, but the morphology was richer than that—*vael* carried a reciprocal root, a doing-together. Not *speaking stone*. *Stone that speaks with*. And *iorun-dath*—the gloss said *return-path*, but *dath* in compound form meant obligation, debt, the thing owed forward.

*A stone that speaks with us. The road we owe our return upon.*

Or, looser, the way a child might say it, saying it over and over to herself on a long cold road:

*The stone will talk with us. We owe it the way home.*

Aldous wrote the rendering out twice, crossed it out once, and then sat very still, because the next thought arrived uninvited and would not leave.

The child wasn't reciting a prayer. She was *reading*. She was holding a shard of something—the gate-report mentioned a glowing object, logged with the bored precision of a man logging everything (*one item, crystaline [sic], warm despite weather*)—and the shard was speaking to her, and she was repeating what it said, and what it said was a sentence from the first lesson ever given in the Oathkeepers' secret tongue, nine hundred years ago, at the Sundered Gate.

Which meant the shard remembered the lessons.

Which meant the shard had been there.

He was aware, distantly, of how late the hour had grown, of the lamps needing oil, of his supper untouched. He worked anyway, the way he had not worked since he was young enough to forget meals, and by the time the bell rang for the midnight watch he had verified the phonology against three separate sources, confirmed the stress-pattern match beyond statistical accident, and drafted—then burned, then redrafted more carefully—a preliminary note titled:

*On the Survival of Oathkeeper Cant: A Living Speaker at Southgate.*

He stopped before writing the fourth word of the second sentence.

Because here was the thing Aldous Wren understood about knowledge, the thing forty years in the archive teaches you if you're honest: knowledge is never neutral. A word can be a key or a knife depending on who holds it. He had spent his career believing that truth was safe as long as it was *accurate*—that scholarship's virtue was precision, and precision harmed no one. But he was holding a precision now that could get a child killed. The gate-report named no names, thank the founders; Ellery had described a woman and a girl and a glowing stone, but the woman had paid with traded memories, which meant she was a thief of the Hollow kind, and the Hollow kind left traces, and traces could be followed.

Who else would read this and understand it? He ran the list the way you run a hand along a shelf in the dark.

The Archivist-Prime, whose masters sat in the Duke's court, and whose court would want a relic of the Oathstones the way dogs want meat. The Collegium of Applied Thaumaturgy, whose funding had tripled every year the winter worsened, and who would want the shard for study, which was a gentler word for vivisection. And—the thought arrived cold, colder than the manuscript—

The Winter Court.

Everyone knew the heralds rode south of here; the caravans talked of little else. White riders, asking questions. And everyone knew what the Court wanted, because the Court had never hidden it: the heir. The lost bloodline of the Oathkeepers, the last throat able to swear the covenant whole again—or broken further, depending on the teller. For nine hundred years the search had been a fairy tale, a reason for the Queen's riders to knock on doors. Fairy tales didn't usually involve a mute child speaking the covenant-tongue at a customs gate while carrying a stone that remembered the Gate itself.

Aldous looked at his draft. *On the Survival of Oathkeeper Cant: A Living Speaker at Southgate.* Twenty copies went out with the morning couriers if he filed it. Twenty copies, to twenty desks, and he controlled perhaps three of the hands at those desks.

Scholarly wonder, he thought, has always been weaponized by whoever pays for the paper. He had known this abstractly for forty years. Now he knew it the way you know a blade: by the edge.

* *

He did not sleep. Instead he did the three things an old archivist does when he finally remembers how to be afraid.

First, he copied the gate-report and his translation into cipher—the old Wren family cipher, useless against professionals but useful against clerks—and hid the original transcription of the cant inside the binding of his worst monograph, *The Linguistic Silence of the Oathkeepers*, where irony would keep it safer than any lock. If the silence of the Oathkeepers was going to end, let it end hidden inside its own obituary.

Second, he went down to the courier registry and read the dispatch logs. This was technically within his rights and definitely against custom, and it confirmed what the caravans had whispered: three separate messages had gone south over the past fortnight from parties unknown, marked with the snowflake seal that no Vellum official would touch without gloves. Interested parties were already interested. Whatever he decided tonight, the board was not empty; it was merely waiting for someone to move a piece.

Third, he wrote two letters.

The first was short and honest and addressed to Master Ellery at Southgate, thanking him for his diligence and requesting—no, *begging*—that should the woman and child return or pass through again, he extend them every courtesy, lose their description in his paperwork, and send Aldous no further reports whatsoever. *Some findings are safest unwritten,* he added, and hoped the careful-handed, cautious-souled gate-scribe would read between the lines correctly.

The second letter was harder.

Addressed, ultimately, to no desk in Vellum—it would go by private courier, costing Aldous his winter coal money, to a former student named Sef who had abandoned academia for the road and now did work of a flexible nature for people who needed things found or un-found. Sef owed him. More importantly, Sef was the only person Aldous knew who moved faster than official mail and owed allegiance to nothing larger than a purse.

*Sef,* he wrote. *You once asked me why anyone spends a life on dead languages, and I gave you the answer old men give. Here is the real one. Because the dead keep secrets, and sometimes the secrets stand up and walk through a customs gate wearing a child's face. There is a tongue in this city tonight that has not been spoken in nine hundred years. It came in on the road from the west, on the heels of a woman and a girl, and it is going to bring the white riders down on this city like wolves on smoke. I am too old to follow the road and too stubborn to stay silent. Find them before the others do. Tell them an old man in the Hall of Dead Tongues knows what the child is saying—and that what she is saying, translated as best I can render it, is:*

*The stone will talk with us. We owe it the way home.*

*Burn this.*

He sealed both letters with plain wax, no signet. Then he sat for a long while in the lamplight among the dead tongues, listening to the building settle, thinking about the index in his drawer.

He got up, retrieved it, and uncapped his pen.

*Entry thirteen,* he wrote, beneath eleven years of blankness. *Tonight I heard a dead language breathe. Tomorrow, probably, I will help kill it or save it, and I no longer believe those are different acts. Wonder, it turns out, was never the opposite of dread. They are the same sensation, reported to different authorities.*

He blotted the page, put the index away, and stood at the tall window of the Hall of Dead Tongues watching the eastern sky consider dawn.

Vellum woke below him—cart wheels, gull-cry off the canal, the first smoke of ten thousand stoves. A city of paper, he thought. A city that believed ink was safety. Somewhere out on the western road, a woman and a child were walking toward this city because cities meant anonymity, and anonymity meant survival, and they did not yet know that in Vellum, anonymity lasted exactly as long as it took a gate-scribe's careful hand to reach a scholar's hungry eye.

The couriers would leave at first bell. His letter to Sef would move fast; the twenty copies of his draft would move faster, unless he burned it.

Aldous Wren stood with the draft in his hand for a long time—longer than the lamp-oil lasted, longer than his tea stayed warm, long enough for the dawn to arrive gray and cold on the glass.

Then he fed the draft to the stove, watched the title curl and blacken—*A Living Speaker at Southgate* becoming fire, becoming smoke, becoming nothing—and felt, in the same instant, both a coward's relief and a scholar's grief, because burning the report protected the child and silenced the greatest discovery of his lifetime, and he could not honestly say which motive had weighed heavier on the scale.

No matter. What mattered now was speed. Sef would ride today. The white riders were somewhere south, and dispatches flew, and wolves talked to wolves.

In the quiet hall, in the cold light, Aldous gathered his coat and his courage and went down to the courier registry to pay for the fastest rider money could buy—pausing only at the door, because the morning bell was ringing, and beneath it, faint and impossible across the city's noise, he could have sworn he heard the acoustics of the empty hall behind him still holding the shape of a child's voice, repeating its one sentence, patient as frost:

*Seth-ka-vael. Seth-ka-vael, iorun-dath.*

The stone will talk with us. We owe it the way home.

"Coming," Aldous said—to the hall, to the dead, to the road—to no one—and went to spend his winter coal on a race he was terrified of losing.
