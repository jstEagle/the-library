## 11. The Hunt Begins

Vellum smelled like dust and money. The archive-city rose out of the frost-plain in tiers, every surface crusted with paper—prayer strips nailed to doorframes, contracts pasted over older contracts until the walls were a palimpsest three centuries deep. Even the gutters ran with ink when it rained. It hadn't rained in weeks. The frost-plain saw to that.

Mara kept her hood up and her hands in her sleeves and taught herself the city the way she'd learned to pick locks: by touch, by listening, by pretending to be exactly as bored as everyone else.

The child rode her hip in a sling made from a blanket Ilsa had traded for. To anyone watching, Mara was a tired pilgrim woman with a sick daughter, one of dozens shuffling through Vellum's lower tiers toward the charity houses and the soup queues. Sick children were invisible here. Grief was the city's native language.

The shard she'd moved.

That had been the hardest lesson of the road—the one she hated most, because it worked. Wrapped against her own skin, under her shirt, the shard hummed its cold note all day long, and every hedge-witch and wandering priest within half a mile felt it like a toothache. But bound flat against the child's back, between her shoulder blades where no one touched a stranger's child, it went quiet. Almost quiet. The girl never complained about it. She only pressed back against it sometimes, the way you lean into a wall that's holding up your house.

*They're looking for a woman carrying something bright,* Mara reminded herself. *Not a mother with nothing.*

She was learning to think of herself as a mother with nothing. It was good camouflage. It was also becoming less of a lie than she wanted to admit, which was a problem for another day.

* * *

The trouble started at the scriptorium market on the third morning.

Vellum sold everything written or writable—quills cut from frozen goose-wing, ink ground from soot and beetle shell, letters of credit, love spells, last wills. Mara had come down for lamp oil and bread and to trade one small memory to the pawnbroker on Copper Row: the taste of a specific stew she'd eaten once in a border town, rich with marrow and pepper. The pawnbroker paid well for tastes. People were starving in Vellum that winter, and a vivid memory of a full belly went for more than silver.

She was counting the coins when she heard them.

Not their voices. Their boots.

Everyone in Vellum shuffled. Pilgrims, scholars, porters, beggars—all of them moved like people conserving heat, shoulders in, eyes down. These four walked like the cold didn't apply to them. Even steps. No hurry. The particular unhurried gait of men who had never once needed to run.

Mara turned her head a fraction and looked through the crowd at a stall selling wax tablets.

White.

Four figures in unblemished white, moving through the grime of the market like a knife through bread. Not robes—cloaks, close-cut, hemmed above the boot, the white so clean it hurt to look at. Frost did not cling to them. That was the detail that stopped her breath. Everything else in Vellum wore a skin of grey ash and road-salt, but the white of those cloaks came off the eye like fresh snowfall, untouched, *deliberate*. They weren't dressed for the city. They were dressed to be remembered.

Heralds. She knew it without being told, the way you know a snake's shape before your mind supplies the word. Something old in her blood stood up and took notice.

They stopped at the mouth of the market square, where the notice-board stood—the great board where the city posted bounties, proclamations, plague warnings. One of them produced a sheet of paper so white it seemed to glow against the weathered wood, and pressed it flat, and stepped back.

No nails. No paste. It simply held.

Then the tallest of them spoke—not loudly, but his voice carried anyway, sliding under the market noise the way cold slides under a door.

"We seek a woman traveling alone," he said, "in company of a child who does not speak. She may call herself by any name. She will not look like a thief. Thieves do not have her eyes."

Mara's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery, and then resumed beating.

*Her eyes.* What did that mean? Whose eyes?

Around her, the crowd did what crowds do: leaned in, murmured, began composing the gossip they'd sell by nightfall. A woman with a mute child. Half the city would remember seeing one by tomorrow whether they had or not. That was the cruelty of it—you couldn't hide by being ordinary anymore. Ordinary was now the description.

Mara bought her lamp oil without shaking. She was proud of that later, in the way you're proud of small survivals. Then she walked, unhurried, out of the square, and did not look back, and did not breathe properly until she was three streets gone.

The child, against her chest, was rigid as a drawn bow.

"It's all right," Mara murmured, knowing it wasn't, hating that she said it.

The girl's hand crept up inside the sling and found the edge of the shard through the cloth. Her eyes, when they lifted to Mara's face, held something Mara had never seen in them before.

Fear. And beneath the fear, unmistakably, an apology—as if the danger were her fault. As if she'd brought this down on Mara specifically, personally, and had known all along that she would.

*You have,* Mara thought grimly. *Whatever you are, whoever sent you, you've known since the door.*

But that wasn't quite fair either, and fairness was starting to matter to her in ways she distrusted. The girl had been six years old and alone in the snow. Whatever had put her there, whatever war she was a piece of—that wasn't a child's doing.

Still. *A woman in company of a child who does not speak.*

They know us, Mara thought. They know us precisely.

Which meant someone had told them. Someone who'd seen them on the road, or in a village, or—

Or someone who'd known before they ever left the Hollow.

She put that thought away in the locked room where she kept thoughts she couldn't afford yet, and climbed.

* * *

Their room was on the fourth tier, over a dye-shop, reached by an exterior stair that groaned like an old man. Ilsa was there when they got in, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her staff across her knees, and one look at Mara's face made the hedge-witch go still.

"You saw them."

"Four. In the square." Mara set the girl down; the child went immediately to the corner where they kept the chalk stubs and began, silently, to draw. She drew constantly now—maps mostly, spiraling roads and river-lines, the Sundered Gate rendered again and again in her clumsy, insistent strokes. Ilsa said it was instinct. Mara said nothing, because she suspected it was instruction.

"Four is a delegation," Ilsa said. "One herald is a messenger. Four means they expect to find what they're looking for."

"They won't. Not today." Mara crouched, began repacking the bag with the economy of long practice. Blanket, chalk, bread, the pawnbroker's coins sewn into the lining. "We leave tonight, north gate, before the watch changes. There's a carter—"

"And go where? North is the Sere. The Sere is ice to the horizon." Ilsa's voice was dry as her magic these days, thinner every week. Mara could see the failing of it in her hands, in the tremor she tried to hide by gripping the staff. "Girl, listen to me. You can't outrun the Winter Court. Nobody outruns winter. Winter doesn't get tired."

"Winter doesn't get tired," Mara agreed, lacing the bag. "But it has to send messengers, and messengers have to sleep somewhere eventually. Everyone sleeps somewhere."

Isla looked at her for a long moment. "What are you planning?"

Mara hadn't fully formed it yet. It arrived in her mouth already whole, the way her best plans always did—born in the same place her thieving instincts lived, just below thought.

"I'm going to read one of them."

Ilsa's face went through several expressions and settled on horror. "Read a *herald*. Girl. Those aren't men in white cloaks. Those are the Queen's own—what they carry in their heads isn't for—"

"I pulled memories from soldiers and smugglers for ten years. I know what I'm doing."

"Do you?" Ilsa's voice cracked, and she hated it, and pushed on anyway. "You told me yourself what happened with the soldier on the road. His whole life, all at once. You were on your knees in the ditch. Now imagine a mind built by the Winter Court. Imagine what they keep in there instead of a life."

Mara imagined it. She'd been imagining it since the square.

"That's why," she said quietly. "I don't need their lives. I need one thing: what they actually want. The herald in the square talked about my *eyes*, Ilsa. About how I'd look. That's not a bounty hunt. Bounty hunters want a body. This is something else." She glanced at the child, bent over her chalk map in the corner, small shoulders curved like a comma. "And I'm done running blind. If we're going to die, I want to know the sentence first."

The dye-vat below them burped and sloshed. Somewhere a bell marked the hour. Ilsa rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, and when she lowered her hand she looked twenty years older than the last time Mara had seen her face.

"If it goes wrong—"

"Then you take her and run, and you don't look back, and you tell her—" Mara stopped. Started again. "Tell her she did enough. Tell her it wasn't her debt."

"What debt? What does the child owe anyone?"

"I don't know," Mara said honestly. "But she thinks she owes it. I see it every time she looks at me."

* * *

She found him alone at dusk, which surprised her. She'd assumed the heralds slept in shifts, guarded each other, breathed in unison. But the white-cloaked man came down the tier-stairs to a chandler's shop near the third-tier landing, alone, and bought candles. Dozens of them. Plain tallow, the cheapest kind. He paid without haggling and stood a moment in the shop doorway after, looking north, toward the frost-plain, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that Mara recognized from ten years of reading strangers in markets.

He was homesick.

Well. Whatever the Winter Court's equivalent was. Whatever a creature like that missed, when it stood in a dirty human city buying cheap candles.

She came up behind him on the stair, in the narrow dark between the lanterns, and let her fingers brush the bare skin of his wrist the way a hurried stranger might—one light accidental touch, apology in the gesture, already moving past.

His memory opened like a door blown wide.

She'd braced for cold. For a mind like the inside of a glacier, all pressure and blue dark. What she got was worse.

She got *order*.

The herald's interior was vast and immaculate and almost empty—a cathedral with nothing on the altar. No childhood. No mother's kitchen, no first snow, no name he'd been called before he was a title. Where a life should have been there were instructions, layered like sediment, going down further than she dared follow: protocols, geographies, lists. Cities catalogued. Roads. Border crossings. And threaded through all of it, everywhere, the same recurring image, worn smooth from repetition the way a coin wears smooth:

A gate, broken. And a stone, and a socket, and a turning motion. Over and over, the image of the shard seated in the Gate's lock, and the sense—urgent, reverent, *hungry*—of what happens when it turns.

And around that central image, orbiting it, the mission itself. She snatched at it as the contact broke, as the herald stiffened under her hand, as his head began to turn:

*Not the child. The child is a vessel, a courier, a convenience. Find the vessel, yes—but the prize, the command that sits above all commands—*

*the heir must swear willingly.*

*The oath must be given freely or it will not hold. Bring the heir to the Court whole and unbroken. Let her understand. Let her choose. The Queen does not want the shard. The Queen wants the last Oathkeeper kneeling at the ice throne, of her own will, speaking the words—*

The connection tore away.

Mara stumbled back down two stairs. The herald had turned fully now, and he was looking at her, and his face—

His face was the worst part. Not anger. Not alarm. *Compassion*, calm and terrible, the way you'd look at a moth battering itself against a window you knew wouldn't open.

"There you are," he said softly.

Mara ran.

She took the stairs in leaps, shoulder-checking a scholar aside, vaulting a stack of delivery crates, hearing behind her not pursuit—he didn't pursue, he didn't need to—but his voice, level and unhurried, rising after her through the stairwell like smoke:

"The Court knows you took it. It changes nothing. We were always coming for you, Mara Nystrom. Daughter of Elin and Tomas. Last heir of the Oathkeepers. Come willingly, and the child walks free into any future you choose for her. Come unwillingly, and we take you both, and the choosing happens anyway—in a colder room."

She hit the fourth-tier landing, wrenched open the dye-shop door, slammed it, threw the bar.

Then she stood in the dark among the reeking vats, chest heaving, and understood—with a clarity so complete it felt like falling—that she had just learned the single most dangerous thing anyone had ever known about her.

They knew her name. Her parents' names. Her bloodline, confirmed.

And they didn't want to kill her.

They wanted her *consent*.

Somehow that was infinitely worse. A blade you could dodge. A bargain you could refuse. But they had just offered her the one currency she couldn't counterfeit and couldn't steal: her own free will, priced against the child's safety, payable at the ice throne.

Below the vats, faint through the floorboards, she heard the chime of the shard—and the child screaming.

Mara took the inner stairs three at a time.

The girl was in the corner, chalk snapped in her fist, both hands pressed flat over the shard's hiding place between her shoulder blades, and her eyes were fixed on Mara with an expression that needed no translation, because it was the same expression Mara had seen in the herald's stolen mind, worn smooth from repetition:

*The gate. The stone. The turning.*

"No," Mara said out loud, to the child, to the Court, to the whole white machinery of winter. "Nobody chooses anything tonight. Tonight we run."

From the window, far below on the frost-plain, she could see them: four white figures walking north out of the city gates in no hurry at all, unhurried as falling snow, as if the road ahead of them were already theirs.

As if they knew exactly where she would run next—and were content to arrive first.
