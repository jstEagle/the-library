## 12. Corvess Returns

He came out of the tree line at dusk, walking like a man who had never once been in a hurry in his life.

I knew his silhouette the way I knew my own hands. The slight forward lean of a man carrying an invisible pack. The way he paused at every rise to look around before committing himself—old scout's habit, or old coward's habit, depending on who was telling the story. His coat was wrong, though. New. Grey wool, good buttons, the kind of coat you didn't buy with Hollow money.

The child saw him first. She'd been walking ahead of me on the frozen ruts of the old cart road, arms out for balance, and she stopped so abruptly that I nearly walked over her. She turned and looked back at me, then at the man emerging from the pines, and her whole small body went rigid as a drawn bow.

"It's all right," I said, which was a lie, because nothing about my chest had been all right since we crossed the valley's edge three days ago. "It's Corvess. He raised me."

She looked at me the way she sometimes did when I spoke—as if my words arrived a half-second late, as if she were hearing them twice. Then she stepped behind my skirts and gripped a fistful of wool, and I felt the shard through her pocket, warm against my hip where she pressed close.

Corvess stopped ten paces off. Up close the new coat told more lies than it covered. He was thinner. There was grey at his temples that hadn't been there in autumn, and his eyes had the glassy brightness of a man running a fever he refused to acknowledge.

"Mara." My name in his voice, low and rough, like a door opening onto a room I'd spent twenty years living in. "You shouldn't be out here."

"No," I agreed. "I shouldn't."

* * *

We made camp because there was no sense pretending otherwise. He built the fire—he always built the fire, quick and clean, feeding it from the bottom the way he'd taught me—and the child watched him do it with an expression I couldn't read. Not fear exactly. Recognition of something wrong, the way a dog watches a man whose smell is off.

"You crossed the boundary alone," Corvess said, settling onto his heels by the flames. "At your age. With no protection. Do you know what the magic does to a body that hasn't touched it since childhood?"

"I know what it did to mine." I held up my hand. Three days later the scars from the crossing still showed, thin silver lines across the knuckles, like frost-cracks in old paint. "It woke up. It's very rude about it."

"That's not funny."

"Nothing's ever been funny to you, old man."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Corvess—he had taught me to steal memories, to read a mark, to walk a crowd without being remembered, and through all of it he'd kept some dry, stubborn tenderness banked down inside himself like coals under ash. Twenty-two years. He'd found me half-dead at the valley's edge, a toddler abandoned with the winter's dead, and he'd carried me into the Hollow and never once told me why he'd been at the edge that day, or why a man who hated magic above all things would take in a child of obvious blood.

"Where's the girl's people?" he asked now. Too casual. I marked it.

"She doesn't have people. She has me."

"Mara—"

"She came to my door during the first snowfall in the Hollow's history. Carrying a piece of the Oathstone. Speaking a language older than any kingdom alive." I fed a stick into the fire and watched him not react, which was itself a kind of reaction. "And the elders wanted her given to the snow, and I said no, and I don't fully know why, except that saying no felt like the first true thing I've done in years. So now we're going to the Sundered Gate. Both of us."

"The Gate's a ruin. A story."

"So was the snow."

He looked at me across the fire for a long moment. The light moved in his eyes, and I thought: *he already knows. He came knowing.* And then I thought: *no. You're tired and paranoid and you want everyone to have betrayed you so the world stays simple.*

That was the trouble with my gift. I'd spent my life taking other people's certainties and I had none of my own left.

"There are things following you," Corvess said quietly.

"Heralds. I know. We lost them at the ford."

"They don't lose people at fords. They lose people when they choose to." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. I've come to help you. But helping you might mean turning you around. The Gate is death, girl. Whatever the shard whispered to you, whatever the fire-visions told you—the Gate eats Oathkeepers. That's what it's *for*. Your parents—"

"My parents." I hadn't meant to say it. It came out sharp. "You knew them. Chapter five of my life, Corvess, the one you never let me open. You knew them, and you never said a word, and now you walk out of the winter wearing a coat you couldn't afford and tell me to turn back?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere off in the dark, ice cracked in a tree—a sound like a knuckle popping, like something small breaking.

"I loved them," Corvess said at last. "Your mother and father. They were the best people I ever failed."

"And now?"

"Now I'm trying not to fail their daughter."

The child shifted against my side. I put my arm around her without thinking, the gesture worn smooth by three weeks of nights. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and cold. Under us the ground was iron-hard; above us the stars burned with the terrible clarity they get in deep frost, each one a nail hammered into black cloth.

"Eat," I said. "Sleep. In the morning you can help us or watch us go. Those are your choices."

He nodded slowly, and I told myself I believed him.

* * *

I woke once in the black hours, the way I always did, some thief-instinct keeping its own watch even while I slept. The fire had burned to red eyes. Corvess sat awake on the far side, coat wrapped around him, staring into the coals.

And he was crying.

Silently. No hitch in his breath. Just tears sliding down into his beard, his face slack and old in the ember-light, looking at nothing—or at something only he could see. I lay still with the child's breath warm on my neck and felt my heart crack along a seam I hadn't known was there, because whatever Corvess was carrying, whatever had brought him out of the safe gray stone of the Hollow into this killing cold, it was eating him alive.

*Guilt,* I thought. *Or grief. Or both. Let him have his night. Ask in the morning.*

I slept again. That was my mistake. That was the last easy hour I would ever spend loving him.

* * *

Morning came white and silent. We broke camp, and Corvess helped—he took the child's hand to lead her over a frozen streambed, and she went stiff as a plank but went, and I remember thinking how strange it was to see his big scarred fingers folded around hers, the two halves of my life touching for the first time.

It happened at midmorning, on a stretch of road where the pines crowded close and the shadows lay blue as bruises.

He reached for her again—to lift her over a fallen trunk, a perfectly ordinary kindness—and she whirled on him with a speed that belonged to no child, and bit him.

Bit him hard, on the wrist, teeth sinking in past the glove. Corvess cried out and flung his arm back, and she stumbled against me, and I caught her, and for one heartbeat everything hung suspended—his blood welling dark between his torn glove and skin, her mouth stained red, her eyes blazing with something ancient and furious.

Then a drop of his blood struck the shard.

She'd pulled it out in the struggle—it must have been in her hand, or her fist had opened, I never got the sequence straight afterward—and the drop fell from her lip onto the crystal and did not bead. It sank. The shard flared, cold-white, and light poured upward off it like smoke running in reverse, and the light hit my face and the world

changed.

* * *

I have stolen thousands of memories. I know their texture better than anyone breathing—how they taste going down, how they sit in the mind like stones in a riverbed, smooth after enough years. What came off that shard was not a memory. It was a *witnessing*. Blood remembers what its owner will not say. The shard read Corvess the way I read marks, and it read him aloud to me.

I stood in a room I had never seen, beneath the Hollow, in the deep cellars where the elders keep the old records nobody reads anymore. Torches. Cold stone. Corvess, younger, kneeling. And standing over him—

White.

A herald, but not like the ones I'd seen in visions. Older. Vast. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the voice of a court that existed before courts:

*"The heir lives in your house. The heir sleeps in your debt. Bring word when she moves, and the valley keeps its peace. Refuse, and we come in over the wall, and we take her, and we take the town, and the snow does not stop falling until there is nothing left to bury."*

And Corvess—younger, proud, terrified—Corvess said, "She's a child. She's nothing. She's mine."

*"Everything is yours until it is ours. You have until she leaves the valley."*

The vision tore. Another one came, closer, rawer—three days ago. The cellar again, but now the herald wore flesh, a tall figure in white with a face like weather, and Corvess stood before it wringing his hands like a beggar.

*"She has crossed,"* the herald said. *"You have until the next moonrise. Bring her to the Waystone at the river bend, delivered willing, or the bargain breaks and the valley burns. You know what we promised. You shook on it with your own mouth."*

And Corvess—my Corvess, the man who taught me to gut a fish and pick a lock and never, ever take from children—Corvess bowed his head and said:

"I'll bring her. She trusts me. She'll come willing."

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in the snow.

The pine shadows had barely moved. It had taken a heartbeat, maybe two. Corvess was clutching his bitten wrist, staring at the shard in the child's hand, and his face—his face was the worst part. Not guilt. Not even shame. *Relief.* The relief of a man whose lie has finally been found out, who can put down a weight he has carried so long it has grown into his spine.

"Mara," he said.

"Don't." My voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Don't say my name. You don't get to say my name right now."

"It wasn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, slower, the words dragged up like splinters. "Twenty-two years ago they found out what you were. Who your parents were. They gave me a choice, and I chose you. Every year since, they've come back, and every year I paid a little more to keep them out. Information. Boundaries. Where the wardens walked. Small treasons, Mara, small ones, to buy you a quiet life—"

"To buy me a *cage*."

"Yes!" It burst out of him, ragged. "Yes, a cage! A warm cage, a safe cage, a cage where nothing with wings could ever reach you! You think I didn't know? You think I let you grow up ignorant out of cruelty?" He was crying again, openly now, tears freezing in the creases of his face. "I stole your history to save your life. I'd do it again. God forgive me, I'd do it again tomorrow."

"And now?"

"Now the price came due." He looked at his wrist, at the bite, at the blood. "Now the choice isn't your safety or your freedom. Now it's your freedom or the valley. Four hundred people. Elder Sef. The baker's kids. All of them, for one girl who was always going to die at the end of that road anyway—because that's what the Gate does, Mara, that's what I tried to tell you, the Gate *eats*—"

"I know what it costs." I stood. My legs held. I was surprised by that. "I've known for days. Longer than you know."

"Then turn around. Come back with me. Give them the child if you must—I'll argue for her, I'll fight for her, I swear it—but you come home, and we find another way, there's always another way—"

"The Waystone at the river bend," I said.

His mouth closed.

"Moonrise tonight," I said. "Delivered willing. That was the phrase, wasn't it? *Delivered willing.* Because the Court needs my oath, not just my body—they need me to kneel on my own. And you were going to walk me there with stories. Turn-back stories. Death-at-the-Gate stories. You were going to frighten me home like a child chasing a ball toward traffic, and then hand me over at the border of your own conscience and call it love."

"Mara—"

"The coat," I said, and my voice cracked at last, and I hated it. "The grey coat. Did they give it to you? Is that what four hundred lives costs these days—one coat, one errand, one old man's broken heart?"

He flinched like I'd struck him. Behind me the child made a small sound—not triumph, nothing so simple—something more like sorrow, the sorrow of a person who has seen this exact scene before, in a future I was only beginning to guess at.

I picked up our pack. My hands were steady now. Steadier than they had been in weeks, in years, in my whole careful caged life. Something had finished breaking in me and the break had come out clean, the way green wood snaps when you finally commit.

"Here is what happens," I said. "You go to the Waystone tonight. You tell them whatever keeps the valley safe a little longer. And we go north, and we reach the Gate, and we do what has to be done—and if the Court comes for the valley because of what we do, then the Court will find the valley defended by a covenant older and truer than any bargain struck in a cellar. That's the only apology I'll ever make you, Corvess. I'm sorry I won't be able to hate you properly. It would be easier."

"Mara, please—"

"*No.*" The word came out with something behind it, something that wasn't mine alone—the shard humming in the child's grip, the air itself leaning in. Frost bloomed across the pine bark around us in spreading ferns. Corvess stared at it, and at me, and for the first time in the twenty-two years I had known him, he looked afraid of me.

Good, some cold thing in my chest said. *Be afraid. Be as afraid as I am.*

"We're leaving," I said. "Don't follow."

* * *

We walked north fast, faster than wisdom allowed, the child's hand welded into mine, the shard burning cold through both our pockets like a second heartbeat. Behind us Corvess stood in the road and did not follow, and did not call out, and I did not look back, because I knew—if I looked back and saw his face I would go soft, and soft was a luxury the dying world could no longer afford.

By afternoon we heard them.

Not hoofbeats. Not voices. A sound like a great many people walking in perfect unison through deep snow, far off to the east, keeping pace with us—too regular, too patient. Heralds. More than the two we'd shaken at the ford. A company of white, converging.

They weren't hurrying. They didn't need to hurry.

Ahead of us the land climbed toward the river country, and somewhere on that road, at a bend marked by a leaning stone, Corvess would stand tonight under the moon and wait for a delivery that was never coming—and when it didn't come, the Court would stop bargaining and start hunting, and every mile between us and the Sundered Gate would fill with white.

The child tugged my sleeve. She pointed east, at the sound of the marching snow, then north, at the darkening hills, then she looked up at me with those old, old eyes.

Her lips shaped two silent words. I couldn't hear them. But I understood them anyway, the way I was beginning to understand everything about her:

*Faster. Please.*
