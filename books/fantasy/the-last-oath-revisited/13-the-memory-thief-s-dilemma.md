## 13. The Memory Thief's Dilemma

The barn smelled of wet straw and old rot, and Corvess stood in the doorway with his hands open like a man showing he carried no weapon.

That was the first lie.

Mara knew every version of those hands. She had watched them for twenty years—rolling cigarettes she never smoked, kneading dough on festival mornings, signing the elder's ledgers with that careful, clerkish script. Hands that taught her which memories kept their color and which faded to gray. Hands that had never once struck her, not even when she was small enough to deserve it.

They were empty now. That was how she knew he'd come to take something instead of give.

"You look tired," he said.

"I wonder why." She kept the child behind her, one hand on the girl's shoulder, feeling the tremor there like a plucked string. Outside, the rain had stopped an hour ago, and the silence it left behind was worse than the noise. Silence meant listening. Something was listening.

"Put down whatever you're holding," Corvess said. "We should talk."

"We're talking."

"We're *posturing*." He stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind him, and the dark came down like a lid. "Twenty-two years I've been talking at you, girl, and you've learned nothing except how to answer me sideways."

The shard pulsed against the child's chest where it hung wrapped in flannel, a heartbeat under the cloth. Mara felt it the way she felt weather changes in her teeth. It didn't want her to run. It wanted her to stay and hear this out, which was almost worse.

"The heralds," Mara said. "How many are coming?"

Corvess paused. He was good at pauses—he'd taught her that too, taught her that the space before an answer told you more than the answer. But she'd had twenty years of practice reading him, and this pause stretched a half-second too long, and in that half-second the whole shape of the night rearranged itself around her.

"That's not why you're here," she said quietly. "You're not warning me."

"Mara—"

"*You're not warning me.*"

He sighed, and it was the sigh of the man who had bandaged her knees and corrected her grip on the thief's knife and sat up with her through fevers, and hearing that sigh now made her want to be sick. "Sit down," he said. "Please."

"No."

"Then stand. But listen." He moved to the edge of the straw pile and lowered himself onto it with the groan of a man whose joints had started keeping their own ledgers, and for a moment he was just old, just her old teacher, just tired. "I met them at the rim of the valley. Two days before you ran. There was a woman in white who didn't leave footprints, and she made me an offer, and God forgive me—I took it."

The words landed in the barn and lay there.

"What offer," Mara said. Not a question. A door being opened whether he liked it or not.

"They want the child. Only the child. And in exchange—" his voice did something complicated "—they leave you. Whole. Unmarked. You keep your valley, your trade, your life. Everything stays as it was."

"As it was." Mara laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "As it was before a mute girl showed up on my doorstep carrying a piece of the sky? Before the snow started falling in a valley where snow can't fall? You think anything is going back to *as it was*?"

"I think dead women don't get to complain about the weather." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and in the dim light leaking through the boards his face was all hollows. "I raised you, Mara. I found you in that burned caravan when you were four years old, screaming at things nobody else could see, and I carried you into the Hollow because the Hollow is the only place in this world where people like us can be *nothing*. No magic. No blood-price. No heralds at the door. I built you a quiet life out of other people's leftovers, and it worked for twenty years, and I will not stand here and watch you throw it away for a child who speaks a language that died before your grandmother's grandmother."

"She's not *throwing away* anything," Mara said. "And neither am I."

"Aren't you?" His voice cracked open, and underneath the crack was something rawer than anger. "Where are you going? To the Gate? You've heard the stories same as I have—the Sundered Gate eats Oathkeepers, that's what the Gate *is*, it's a mouth and it's been hungry for three hundred years—"

"You know about the Gate." The realization arrived cold and complete, like ice water finding the seam of a boot. "You know about the Gate, you know about the heralds, you knew my parents were Oathkeepers. What else do you know, Corvess? How long have you known what I am?"

And there it was again. The pause. Too long.

The child's fingers closed on the back of Mara's shirt.

Mara had spent her whole life taking things from other people's heads. She knew the architecture of a mind the way a carpenter knows a house—where the load-bearing walls were, where the hidden rooms lay, where the locks were cheap and where they'd been welded shut by shame. Corvess had taught her that too. He had also taught her the one rule above all rules: *you don't steal from family.* He'd said it when she was nine, when she'd reached for his thoughts out of curiosity, and he'd caught her wrist mid-air, and his face had been so frightened—not angry, frightened—that she'd never tried again.

She tried now.

It wasn't like opening a lock. It was like breaking into a house where she'd grown up, where she knew every creaking stair, and finding all the doors standing open because he wanted her to see—or because some part of him had stopped guarding years ago. She slipped past his surface thoughts, past the lit rooms of his immediate attention, and went down.

Down into the cellar of him.

There was a night, nineteen years ago, when two riders came to the valley rim in the rain. A man with burn-scarred hands and a woman with Mara's own dark hair plastered to her face, both of them bleeding, both of them carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that hummed like a struck bell. They'd come to Corvess because Corvess was the only person in the world they trusted—a former Oathkeeper archivist gone to ground, keeper of secrets, keeper of a baby girl they'd left with him three years earlier. They'd begged him to hold the thing in the oilcloth. They'd said, *if we don't come back, never tell her. Never let her touch it. Let her be ordinary, let her be safe, let her forget.*

And Corvess had promised. And they hadn't come back. And he had buried the oilcloth bundle beneath the floor of his own house, and he had lied to her for nineteen years, and every year the lie had gotten heavier, until it weighed exactly as much as love weighs, which is to say everything—

Mara surfaced gasping.

"You buried it," she whispered. "Under your house. Under my bed, when I lived with you. It was *under me* the whole time."

"It doesn't matter now—" 

"It matters *enormously*—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated that he could still do this to her, reach through twenty years and squeeze. She steadied herself against the barn wall. "What else. What did the herald offer. All of it. Because I saw the shape of it in your head just now and there was something else, something underneath, and if you make me dig for it I'll dig, Corvess, and you won't enjoy what else I find down there."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then, softly, like a confession wrung out drop by drop: "They asked for you too. Not your body. Your oath. The woman in white said the heir must swear *willingly* or not at all—and if you refused, if you fought, then they would take the child and simply wait. Winter waits well, Mara. It's the only thing winter does better than anything alive."

"So you brought me to them. Willingly. So I wouldn't be taken unwillingly." Bile rose in her throat. "That's not mercy. That's just doing their work for free."

"It's the only version of you surviving!" He was on his feet now, and the old authority was back in his voice, the classroom voice, the ledger voice. "Listen to me. I have read the archives. I was an archivist before you were born, before the purge. The covenant is broken, yes, but broken things cut whoever picks them up. You are a memory-thief from a nowhere valley with a knife and a stolen shard and a child who bites. Against the *Winter Court*. You cannot win. You can only choose how you lose, and I am begging you—begging you—to lose slowly, somewhere warm."

The child moved.

Not toward the door. Toward Corvess. Mara grabbed for her and missed, and the girl crossed the straw in three quick steps and stopped in front of the old man, looking up at him with those eyes that were too old and too familiar, eyes Mara had been refusing to examine since the first night. Slowly, deliberately, the child pulled the shard from its wrapping.

It lit the barn like dawn.

In that white light Corvess's face was stripped of everything—every practiced expression, every careful pause—and what was left underneath was grief so old it had fossilized into something resembling calm.

"Yes," he said to the child, quietly. "Go on, then. Look at me. See what I am."

The girl held up the shard. And bit him.

Her teeth found the meat of his thumb, quick as a wren striking, and his blood welled bright across the stone's faceted surface—and the light changed. Went deep. Went red-gold, ember-colored, and the barn filled with images that weren't images, that poured directly into Mara's skull the way memories poured when she took them:

A frozen shore at the valley's rim. A woman in white without footprints. Corvess kneeling in the snow, and his voice, thin with cold: *Take the child, spare the girl, and I'll bring her to you myself.* The herald's smile, patient as frost forming. And then—the part he hadn't said aloud, the part buried deeper than bargains—a memory of a burned caravan, a screaming four-year-old, and a promise made over a small body: *no one will ever hunt you again, little one, I swear it on what's left of me.*

The vision folded shut. The shard dimmed. Blood dripped from Corvess's thumb onto the straw, black in the afterlight.

Everyone stood very still.

"Well," Corvess said at last, and his voice was almost steady. "Now you know."

"Now I know." Mara's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "You sold us."

"I traded," he said. "For you. For the only thing I have ever actually wanted, which was your survival, which apparently makes me a monster, which—yes. Fine. I'll wear it." He held up his bitten hand, almost curious about it. "She drew truth out of me with her teeth. That's not a child, Mara. Whatever she is—"

"She's mine." The words came out of Mara before thought, before permission, with the certainty of something that had been waiting a long time in a locked room. "Whatever she is, she's mine. And you—" 

And here it was. Here was the moment she'd rehearsed in a hundred forms since the valley rim, since Vellum, since the herald's white figure first entered her nightmares. Cornered, betrayed, with the man responsible standing close enough to touch, and her gift awake in her hands like a drawn blade.

She could erase him.

It would be easy. Easier than anything. One touch to his temple and she could reach into the house of him and clear it room by room—burn the archive, sweep the ashes, unmake twenty-two years. He would wake knowing only that he was an old man alone in a barn with strangers, and he would never follow them, never report them, never ache for her again. Clean escape. Total victory. The kind of clean that leaves nothing living underneath.

No one would ever know. Except her. Except her, forever.

The child tugged her sleeve. When Mara looked down, the girl was watching her with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue, living or dead: *I know what you're thinking. I'm watching what you choose.*

Mara breathed out.

"No," she said aloud, though no one had asked the question. "No. I'm not going to do it."

Corvess blinked. "Do what?"

"You know what. It's what I'm *for*." She crossed the straw toward him and he flinched—actually flinched, the great careful archivist, the keeper of everyone's secrets, flinching from a girl he'd raised—and something in her chest tore along an old seam. "Hold still. This is going to hurt less than you deserve."

She put two fingers to his temple.

She went in gently, the way she'd never bothered to be gentle with any mark in twenty years of thieving. Past the lit rooms, past the archive halls, down into the cellar where he kept the thing that had rotted everything: the bargain at the frozen shore. She took hold of it the way you take hold of a splinter lodged near bone—firmly, without tenderness—and she pulled.

Corvess cried out. His knees buckled; she caught his weight against her shoulder, an old man suddenly heavier than he'd ever been, and lowered him onto the straw. His eyes were wide and wet and searching her face like a man looking for his own reflection in dark water.

"The shore," he rasped. "There was—I knelt in the—I offered them—"

"Gone," Mara said. "That memory is mine now. I'll carry it. Somebody should have to." She straightened. "Everything else stays. Every lesson. Every fever you sat up with. Every time you caught my wrist and told me never to steal from family. You keep all of it. You get to remember exactly who you were to me—and exactly what you did—and you're going to live inside both of those facts for whatever years you've got left. That's not mercy, Corvess. That's the sentence."

He stared up at her. And slowly, horribly, he began to laugh—a dry, cracked sound, half sob. "God. *God.* You've become a better thief than I ever was, do you know that? I taught you to take memories. Nobody taught you to leave a man his conscience." He pressed his bitten hand over his eyes. "It's worse. What you left me is so much worse."

"I know," Mara said. "That was the point."

The child took Mara's hand. Small fingers, cold from the shard, laced through hers with the absolute trust of someone who had, apparently, done this before—in another life, another winter, some future Mara couldn't imagine and wasn't sure she wanted to.

"Come with us," Mara said, and surprised herself. "Turn yourself in to the elders. Testify. Help us. I'll tell everyone it was coercion if—if that helps. Or don't, and live with it. Either way, we're leaving now, and the heralds arrive by morning, and I'd rather not be here when they find their bargain has walked off the table."

Corvess lowered his hand. His face was wrecked and open in a way she had never seen it, and would probably never see again.

"The Gate will kill you," he said hoarsely.

"Probably."

"And even if it doesn't, the Court will never honor any terms you write. They're *winter*, Mara. Winter doesn't negotiate. Winter arrives."

"Then I'll steal something winter can't afford to lose." She pulled the child toward the door. "Your last lesson, old man. Watch what a thief does with the whole world as her mark."

Outside, the clouds had torn open, and the stars over the dying lands were hard and close and cold as chips of the Oathstone itself. Somewhere east, beyond the ash roads and the frozen river, the Sundered Gate waited in its wound of ruin. Somewhere north, white figures moved without footprints, and one of them had begun, very quietly, to count the hours until morning.

Mara turned east, took the child's hand more tightly, and walked.

Behind her, in the barn, an old man sat in the straw holding a memory-shaped wound, and did not call after her—and that, too, was an answer of a kind.
