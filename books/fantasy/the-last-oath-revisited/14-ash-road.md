## 14. Ash Road

The cold has a sound.

I did not know this when I was only the shard's hands. Now I am learning words again—*cold*, *road*, *Mara*—and each word is a door opening in me, and behind every door there is more cold, and the cold makes a sound like a long breath drawn through teeth. It is the sound of a world holding still so it will not be noticed by winter.

We walk south. Mara says *south* like it means salvation. I know it does not mean that. I know where south ends. But my mouth cannot make the sentence yet, and so I walk beside her and hold her coat hem with two fingers, which she pretends not to notice, which is how I know she needs it too.

* * *

The first village had a name once. The sign at its edge was carved wood, and frost had eaten the letters into soft gray ghosts. Mara read what remained: *—ollow —ill.* She said, "Another Hollow," and laughed, and her laugh came out wrong, like something breaking politely.

The people of Hollow-hill burned their beds that night.

I stood in the doorway of the inn—I do not sleep much; sleep is full of the falling gate—and watched them carry their furniture out into the square one piece at a time. A table. Chairs. A wardrobe with a painted door that opened on nothing, because the family who owned it had sold the house years ago and kept only this beautiful useless door. An old man fed his grandmother's rocking chair into the fire with both arms, the way you would feed a sick animal you loved.

No one wept. That was the part I could not stop looking at. They were past weeping. They burned their histories to boil water for tea, and they drank the tea, and they thanked us for sharing their fire because guests meant luck, or maybe because guests meant someone would remember this village existed after the frost took the sign.

Mara gave them nothing. Not because she is cruel. Because the woman who ran the inn asked her to take a memory—"any memory, take the day I got married, I can't afford to keep it warm"—and Mara looked at her for a long time and said, "Keep it. It'll keep you warmer than tea." And then she paid for our beds with coin she didn't have, promising the ferryman's cousin downriver would settle it, which was a lie, and the innkeeper knew it was a lie, and took it anyway, because a lie about tomorrow is also a kind of fire.

I sat near the flames and felt the shard under my shirt go quiet, the way it does around real human warmth, as if it is ashamed.

* * *

That night I dreamed the gate again, but closer now, and in the dream there was a river under it, frozen mid-splash, fish hanging in the ice like ornaments. When I woke I found the charcoal stub from the hearth and drew the river on the floorboards before the dream could finish leaving me.

Mara crouched over it with her morning face—the one that hasn't decided yet whether to trust the day.

"What's this?"

"River," I said. My voice is still strange to me. Small and rusted. But it works.

"There's no river for three days yet."

"Sere," I said, and her whole body changed. The way a rope changes when something on the other end pulls.

"The Sere's frozen solid," she said. "Everyone says so. Has been since autumn. Nobody crosses."

I put my finger on the drawing and dragged it downstream, east, to a place where I made a small jagged mark. I don't know why I know the mark belongs there. That is the terrible thing about what lives in me now. The knowledge doesn't come with reasons. It comes the way hunger comes.

"That's not the crossing," Mara said slowly. "That's the old ford. Under the ice. Nobody's used the ford in—gods, in generations."

She looked at me the way she looks at locked boxes.

I drew the mark again, harder, until the charcoal snapped.

* * *

After that she gave me paper.

Not real paper—a roll of birchbark she traded from a trapper for one hour of a memory he'd been trying to drown in drink for years. The memory of his son's face. He cried when it left him, and thanked her after, and I hated both of those things equally, and I think she did too, and neither of us said so, because saying so wouldn't feed us.

I filled the bark with lines.

Roads. Rivers. The white scars of the heralds' passages, which look different from roads—they are straighter than anything men build, and they avoid high ground the way a wound avoids salt. Villages as dots. Some of the dots I crossed out before I understood why, and later we passed the places and found them empty. Not dead-empty. *Gone*-empty. Doors standing open. A cradle with frost inside it shaped exactly like a baby.

Mara stopped sleeping well after the third crossed-out dot.

"You're drawing things you've never seen," she told me, not as a question. We were walking. Her breath came out in flags. "How?"

I shrugged, which is a word the body knows even when the mouth fails.

"It's the stone," she said. "It's showing you."

I shook my head. This is important to me. Maybe the most important thing I own. I touched the shard through my shirt and then touched my own chest, twice, and then pointed at the map.

Her. Me. Same chest. Same knowing.

She stared at me for ten steps, twenty. Then she said, quietly, almost to herself: "You're not reading the stone. You're remembering something you never lived."

Yes.

Yes, and if I could say more I would tell her the rest: that the maps are not visions, they are *instructions*, that somewhere behind my eyes a version of me already walked this road and failed, and the failing left grooves, and I am rolling along the grooves like a marble in a channel, and every day my mouth gets better at words and worse at explaining why.

Instead I said: "Faster. If we go faster."

"We're going as fast as we go."

"Faster," I said again, and pulled her sleeve, and she let herself be pulled, which is a kind of love.

* * *

*

* * *

The second village was bigger, and had stopped pretending.

They had burned everything wooden weeks ago and moved on to coal and peat and finally to the pews of their own chapel, and the chapel stood open-roofed like a broken tooth, and the god it used to house had been a mild green god of growing things whose little shrine statue now wore a scarf knitted by some practical soul who couldn't bear to see any holy thing freeze.

An old woman stopped me in the lane. Her eyes were the pale washed blue of thin ice.

"You're the one," she said. "The child from the north road. They say you're going to the Gate."

Mara stepped between us. "Who says that?"

"Everyone says that. Everyone's known since the birds went quiet." The old woman leaned around Mara's shoulder to look at me. Her gaze had weight. "Are you going to fix it, little one?"

I wanted to say yes. The yes was right there in my mouth, fat and easy, and I understood suddenly why prophets are dangerous—not because they lie, but because the truth is too heavy to carry and a lie shaped like hope weighs nothing.

So I said the true thing instead: "Someone will."

The old woman smiled with all her gums. "Good answer. Fixers die. Someone-lasts."

She pressed something into my hand—a button, bone, worn smooth, carved with a spiral that I recognized with a jolt so hard I nearly dropped it. Oathkeeper work. Old cant spirals, the same ones that circle the shard's socket-script. I looked up and she was already walking away, and when Mara called after her—where did you get that, who gave it to you—the old woman waved without turning and said:

"Everything here comes from somewhere else. That's what ash means."

* * *

That evening, in the shelter of a burned-out granary, I drew while the others slept. Deserter Brannic snored in the doorway out of habit, guarding nothing. Ilsa's staff lay across her knees even in sleep, her fingers curled around it like a child's around a mother's braid. And Mara sat up watching me draw, chewing the inside of her cheek, which I have learned means she is composing a question she isn't sure she wants answered.

Finally she asked it.

"When you draw the Gate"—she tapped the far edge of the bark, where I had drawn, without deciding to, a wound in the world rendered as two broken arcs and a darkness between—"what do you see? Really see. Not the shape. The... the rest of it."

I set down the charcoal.

This is the hardest thing. Words are nets and what I know is water. But I owe her more than shrugs now. She gave me her mother's song to a ferryman. I watched her pay it. I know what it cost, because I know what songs cost, because I am built partly out of one.

"Ice," I said. "And under the ice, a door. And the door is not shut. It is *holding*. Like a person holding a heavy thing. And the person is tired."

"The Gate's tired," she repeated flatly.

"Not the gate." I touched my chest again. Her eyes went wide in the dark.

"You," she whispered. "Something's holding the winter back and it's *tired*, and you can feel it because—"

Because I am the same kind of thing as whatever holds it. Because the covenant did not shatter clean. Because a piece of it stayed behind, and a piece of it went into the shard, and a piece of it went into a girl sent walking backward down her own life, and all the pieces ache in time with each other like brothers' heartbeats.

"Yes," I said, because it was shorter than all that.

Mara wrapped her arms around her knees. For a long time the only sound was Brannic snoring and the wind testing the granary's broken roof like a thief working a lock.

"My mother used to sing a song about holding," she said at last. "I don't remember it anymore. I paid it away. But I remember that it was about holding—that was the whole song, I think. Something about how everything that lasts is held by somebody." She laughed, that polite breaking laugh. "I paid away the one song about carrying things and now I'm carrying the world's heaviest child across a frozen country. There's a joke in there. The gods have lousy timing."

I crawled over and put my head against her arm. She stiffened—she always stiffens first—and then settled, and her heartbeat came through her coat, quick, quick, slowing.

"Mara," I said.

"Hm."

"Your name is mine."

She went very still. "What did you say?"

But I was already asleep, and dreaming of the river, and in the dream the ice over the Sere sang one clear note like a struck glass, and beneath the note, faint as a pulse, someone was singing about holding.

* * *

We reached the bluffs above the Sere on the ninth day.

The land had gone fully white by then—not snow-white but *frost*-white, every blade of grass sheathed in glass, the trees bent and glazed like waxwork. Even the crows had stopped coming this far. The silence had a texture. It pressed on the ears.

And below us lay the river.

The Sere should have been a mile of gray ice with current muttering underneath. Instead it was a mile of white silence so deep and perfect that it did not look frozen. It looked *stopped*. As if the river hadn't frozen but had simply been interrupted, mid-thought, and the whole world was waiting for it to finish its sentence.

There was a town on the far bank—or the bones of one—and a single light moving along the shore, slow, patient. A lantern. Where there is a lantern on a dead shore, there is a ferryman.

Mara stood at the bluff's edge with the wind pulling her hair sideways and studied the ice with narrowed eyes, the way she studies locks and ledgers and lies.

"There's no crossing," she said. "Look at it. No cracks, no pressure ridges. River ice talks. This ice is holding its breath." She glanced down at me. "You knew that. Didn't you. That's why the maps."

I pointed at my jagged little mark, miles downstream, where the old ford slept under the flood-ice.

"Not the ford either?" she said. "You want the lantern."

I nodded.

She exhaled a long flag of breath and rubbed her face with both hands, and when she lowered them she had put on the face she wears before doing something expensive.

"Ferrymen charge," she said. "And everything costs more in winter. Come on, little ghost. Let's go find out what we've got left to sell."

We started down. Behind us the frost-blinded country stretched away, dotted with the ash-smudges of burning villages, and ahead the stopped river waited with its lantern and its price.

And in my pocket, my fist closed around the old woman's bone button, and I realized I had been holding it so hard the spiral had printed itself into my palm—

—and that the spiral was not just a spiral.

Turned the way my fingers had turned it, it was the cant-sign for *remember*, and beneath it, smaller, older, scratched by a different hand entirely, a second sign I had never seen awake but knew from the falling dark:

*Come home.*

The lantern on the far shore paused. As if it had seen us.

As if it had been told to wait for exactly this.
