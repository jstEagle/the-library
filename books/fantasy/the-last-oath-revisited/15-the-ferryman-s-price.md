## 15. The Ferryman's Price

The river Sere announced itself a full day before we reached it—not by sound, for it had no sound, but by the way the world went quiet around it. The birds stopped. The wind stopped. Even our footsteps on the frozen road seemed to fall into some deeper silence, as though the land itself were holding its breath.

"It's not really a river anymore," I said to the child, because saying nothing had become harder than saying something. "It's a scar."

She looked up at me with that expression she'd been wearing more and more lately—the one that wasn't quite a child's blankness and wasn't quite an adult's knowing, but some unsettling third thing. She didn't speak. She almost never spoke outside of necessity, and when she did, it was in that dead tongue that made my teeth ache to hear it, or in fragments of Common she'd begun assembling like a magpie building a nest from broken glass.

*Cold*, she signed once, hands quick in the gray air. *Deeper cold.*

"I know," I said. "I know."

We crested the last hill at dusk, and there it was: the Sere, half a mile of ice stretching flat and white to the far shore, and on the near bank, a hut built entirely of things other people had abandoned. Oars for rafters. A door made from a wagon bed. Windows of stretched hide glowing amber against the dark. Smoke rose from a chimney that was, if I looked too long, either a tree trunk or a spear—and I decided not to look too long.

Above the door hung a sign so old its letters had been carved twice, the newer carving cutting through the older one. It read:

**PASSAGE COSTS WHAT YOU CANNOT SPARE.**

The child's hand found mine. Her fingers were freezing, as they always were, but her grip was strong.

"We don't have money anyway," I told her, which was true and also beside the point, and we both knew it, and I hated that a six-year-old—or whatever she actually was—could out-stare me in moral matters. "Come on. Cold doesn't get better by standing still."

* * *

The ferryman was nothing like I'd imagined, which is to say he was exactly what the stories promised and I hadn't let myself imagine it. Old the way rivers are old—not wrinkled, not stooped, just *weathered*, his face carrying the same quality as the ice behind him, depth without warmth. He sat by his fire mending a net that had no business existing on a frozen river, and when we pushed open his mismatched door, he didn't look up right away.

"Two travelers." His voice was gravel dragged over wood. "One small. One carrying more than she knows how to set down."

"You can see that?"

"I can see everything that crosses my water." Now he looked up, and his eyes were pale as the underside of ice. "Or will cross it. The Sere remembers what it used to carry. Boats. Barges. Weddings going to the far villages, funerals coming back. It remembers being a river instead of a knife. And it's bitter about it."

"The sign says passage costs what you cannot spare."

"It does."

"Coin?"

"Coin is what you *can* spare. That's why it's worthless here." He set down his net and threaded his fingers together over one knobby knee. "A crossing of the Sere costs a memory of joy. True joy, freely given. Not relief—that's joy's poor cousin, all exhale and no light. Not satisfaction, not comfort. Joy. The kind you'd trade anything to keep, which is precisely why the river wants it."

"Why?" The question came out sharper than I intended. Behind me, the child shifted closer to my leg. "What does a river do with memories?"

"What do you do with them?" He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has heard every objection a thousand times and found none of them interesting. "It keeps them. Feeds them into the water under the ice. Somewhere below the frost, girl, there are fish swimming through other people's wedding days. There are springs that have never happened yet warming themselves on borrowed happiness. When this covenant of yours—" and at that word both his eyebrows rose slightly, telling me he knew far more about us than a stranger should, "—when it's reforged, if it's reforged, maybe the river spends what it's saved. Maybe all that joy comes back up through the ice like bubbles. That's the rumor among my kind, anyway. We're a hopeful trade, ferrymen. You have to be, sitting on a frozen river waiting for spring that doesn't come."

I stood in his doorway with the wind worrying the hem of my coat, and I thought about turning back, and I thought about the heralds who would eventually pick up our trail again—they always did—and I thought about what memory I owned that qualified.

There was only one.

I'd known there was only one since before the hilltop. Probably since the moment I saw the sign, or the smoke, or the amber windows. Some part of me had been counting my joys the whole road south, the way a miser counts coin, and had arrived at a number I didn't want to say aloud.

"There's a room in back?" I asked.

"For the paying. Yes." He rose, joints cracking like river ice in thaw that never came. "You don't have to decide tonight. Sleep first. Joy keeps."

But joy doesn't keep. That's the whole trouble with it.

* * *

The room in back was small and warm and held nothing but a chair and a basin of water so clear it might have been cut from the river's heart. The child refused to stay in the main room with the ferryman—I felt her terror through the wall before I understood its shape, a flat animal dread of being alone near deep water—and so she sat on the floor at my feet while I sat in the chair, and we watched each other in the lamplight.

"You could sleep," I said.

She shook her head.

"You should sleep."

She shook her head again, then climbed onto the chair beside me—it barely fit both of us—and leaned her weight against my arm, and pointed at the basin.

*Show,* she signed. Then, after a struggle, in her careful hoarded Common: *Before.*

"Before what?"

Her hand flattened against her own chest. *Me.* Then against mine. *You. Before you. Show you.*

And I understood, finally, what she was asking, and my throat closed.

Because here was the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do: tomorrow I would sit across from that ancient man, or perhaps stand on the ice itself, and I would reach into my own head and pull out the single warm thing I possessed, and hand it over, and walk away lighter and poorer and no longer quite the woman who had started the crossing. And after that I would be someone who knew she had once been loved by a singing voice but couldn't remember the song. I would be a woman with a hole shaped exactly like her mother.

Unless I looked at it first. Unless I spent tonight doing what I had spent my whole life doing to other people—entering a memory, dwelling in it, learning its texture—except this time the thief and the stolen-from were the same person, and the taking was only a borrowing against morning.

"All right," I whispered. "All right. Watch closely. This is the only copy."

I pressed my palms to the basin's rim and closed my eyes and went down into myself the way I'd gone down into soldiers' heads and merchants' heads and one herald's terrible white mind, except there was no lock to pick here, no resistance, just a door I'd kept painted shut for twenty-five years, opening now onto a kitchen.

* * *

It was summer in the memory, which meant it was the last summer before the Hollow took us in, though I hadn't known that at the time; I'd been four, and four-year-olds don't know they're living in the last page of a chapter.

My mother stood at the table rolling dough for flatbread, flour to her elbows, and she was singing.

That's all. That's everything. She was singing.

It wasn't a song I've ever found since—not in Vellum's archives, not in a hedge-witch's repertoire, not hummed by a single traveler on forty miles of bad road. It may have been hers alone. An Oathkeeper's private music, in a language older than kings, the melody rising and falling like something that had been sung to lull children in three realms at once, back when there were three realms worth lulling. Her voice was low and a little rough, untrained, the voice of a woman who sang while she worked because silence made her think about the things she'd fled, and she turned the dough, and flour dusted the air in the slanting light, and I sat on the floor by her feet fitting puzzle-pieces of stale bread into shapes only I could see, and I remember—

I remember thinking, with the enormous unearned certainty of a small child: *this is what safe sounds like. Wherever this sound is, that's home.*

She looked down at me mid-verse. She smiled. Flour on her cheekbone. A smear of dough on her wrist. And she changed the words of the next line—I'll never know what they had been—to something that made her laugh at herself, and the laugh tangled up inside the song, and the song caught the laugh and carried it, and for one whole breath the kitchen was the warmest place in any world, and I was in it, and I was loved in it, and I did not know yet that love could be a country you could be exiled from.

Then the memory, having shown me all it had, let go.

I opened my eyes in the ferryman's back room with tears running freely down my face and the child watching me with an intensity that bordered on grief. Her small hand rested on my knee.

"Now you," I managed. "Now you've seen her too. If I forget tomorrow—you keep it. You witnessed. That has to count for something."

*Count,* she agreed softly, and touched her chest, then mine, then folded her hands together as if locking something in. *Kept.*

I slept that night without dreaming, which felt like theft, and woke before dawn knowing I wouldn't eat, knowing my hands were steady, knowing that steadiness was its own kind of horror.

* * *

The ferryman met me on the ice itself. Of course he did. The river wanted to be present for the transaction.

Dawn came up gray-gold along the far shore, and the ice beneath our boots groaned once, deeply, like a sleeper turning over. The child waited at the near bank—I had insisted, and for once she'd obeyed without argument, though her eyes had begged me not to make her wait alone with a stranger, even a harmless one.

"How does it work?" I asked.

"You give. I take. The river carries you across while it digests." He held out both hands, palms up, an oddly ceremonial gesture for such a battered man. "One instruction, and heed it well: do not fight me when I draw it out. Folk who clutch their joy to the last second lose pieces of other things with it—the edges tear, and they go away missing more than they paid. Open your hand, girl. All the way open. Grief is easier to carry than regret."

"And if I change my mind?"

"Then you walk two hundred miles north to the bridge at Kell's Ford, and the heralds find you somewhere around mile fifty." His pale eyes were not unkind. They were simply honest, which is rarer and often worse. "Everyone who stands on my ice has already decided. They just haven't told themselves yet."

He was right. I had decided in a kitchen that existed only inside me.

I put my hands in his. His grip was dry and cool, and I felt the pull begin—gentle, patient, utterly irresistible, like the tide taking back what it had lent. I thought of his advice and I opened everything. I stopped guarding the door. I stepped aside from my own heart and let the river in.

It found the kitchen fast. It knew what it had come for; the Sere has been fishing these waters a long time.

Flour to the elbows. Dough turning under her hands. The low rough voice climbing into its verse. *This is what safe sounds like.* The look down. The smile. Flour on the cheekbone. The changed line. The laugh caught inside the song and carried—

and carried,

and carried away.

The ice groaned. Somewhere far beneath us, I swear I heard splashing, as though something enormous had rolled over in its sleep, delighted.

I was still holding the ferryman's hands. My face was wet. I could remember that I had cried, but the reason sat in my chest like a room with the furniture removed—I knew its dimensions perfectly, and there was nothing in it.

"Well?" My voice came out strange and level. "Did it take?"

"It took." He released me and studied my face with something close to reverence. "Clean payment. Whole. You gave it open-handed, like I said." He paused. "You want to know what it sounded like, from where I stand? The river heard your mother sing, girl. First new music under this ice in sixty years. The fish will be talking about it for a generation."

"Don't." The word cracked out of me. "Don't describe it to me. Don't you dare give it back secondhand."

He inclined his head, accepting the rebuke. "Walk east," he said instead. "Straight east until noon, then follow the cairns. There's a fire on the far side most nights—travelers, strays, the kind of folk a winter like this gathers up. You'll want company where you're going." He was already turning away, gathering his impossible net, and over his shoulder he added, almost too quietly to catch: "For what it's worth, I've taken ten thousand joys across this river, and yours was the first one anyone ever handed over smiling."

Had I been smiling? I didn't know. I walked toward the child waiting small and rigid on the bank, and she searched my face with growing alarm, reading the absence there the way she read everything—instinctively, completely.

*Mama-song,* she signed urgently. *Where?*

Gone. Fed to a frozen river to buy half a mile of ice. But I knelt in the snow in front of her and tapped her folded hands, the ones she'd locked shut in the lamplight last night.

"You kept it," I said. "You witnessed. So it's not gone. It's just... not mine anymore. It's ours. Held in trust." I smiled at her, and discovered my face was still wet, and let it be. "When this is over—when the Gate turns and winter breaks—I'm going to find that song again. Somewhere. In some archive, in some old woman's kitchen, in the river itself when the ice melts. I'm going to learn it, and I'm going to teach it to you, and neither of us will ever know if it's the right one. And that will have to be enough."

She considered this with the grave thoroughness she brought to everything, then threw her arms around my neck, and her hug was freezing and fierce and the closest thing to a memory of joy I had left.

We crossed the Sere at sunrise, walking east along the cairns, and the ice sang under our boots in frequencies I chose not to name.

The far shore, when we reached it, was not empty. Smoke rose from a copse of black pines, and around the fire I could make out figures—a big man in a soldier's coat with the insignia torn off, hunched against the cold; a thin woman wrapped in layers of patched wool, a staff across her knees that hummed faintly, wrong, like a bell with a crack in it; and a boy, younger than the rest, who sat apart from the warmth and stared at the flames as though he'd never seen fire before and wasn't sure he approved.

They all looked up as we approached. And every one of them—soldier, witch, boy—turned toward the shard's faint glow beneath my coat the way flowers turn toward a sun that hasn't risen yet.

The ferryman's voice followed us across the ice, soft as snowfall:

*Told you. Strays.*
