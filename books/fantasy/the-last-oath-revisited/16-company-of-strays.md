## 16. Company of Strays

The road south of the Sere's headwaters was the kind of road that existed only because two warlords hadn't yet decided whose it was. No one maintained it, no one patrolled it, and so everyone used it—the desperate, the fleeing, the ones with nothing left to tax.

We'd been walking it for four days when we found the first of them.

He was sitting in the ditch like he'd grown there, a big man in a soldier's coat with the insignia ripped off at the shoulders, leaving only threads and a paler rectangle of cloth that had never seen sun. He had a sword across his knees, unsharpened, and he watched us come with the flat patience of a man who had decided some time ago that whatever happened next was fine by him.

The child stopped walking before I did. She always knew things first now—had known the ferryman's grief before I'd felt it, had stood at the riverbank pointing at the far shore a full minute before the ice groaned and split. She looked at the man in the ditch and then up at me and made a sound. Not a word. A sound, low and insistent, the way she'd once sounded at the shard itself.

"We're not collecting people," I told her. "We're carrying a rock through a frozen country. There's a difference."

She didn't blink. Six years old, maybe seven, with eyes that made lying feel like a waste of both our time.

I walked past him. The child didn't.

That was how it went, those days. I'd learned to read her stillnesses the way sailors read weather, and this stillness said *this one*. So I stopped, turned around, and squatted down at the edge of the ditch, keeping my hands visible, because a deserter with an unsharpened sword was either harmless or very dangerous, and you couldn't tell which until it was too late to matter.

"Which army?"

"Doesn't exist anymore." His voice was rough, unused. "Third Marches Regiment. We were paid in promises and then the promises froze." He looked at my pack, at the child standing slightly behind me, at nothing in particular. "You're not robbers. Robbers walk faster."

"What gave us away?"

"The woman looks like she's deciding whether I'm worth stealing from, and the girl looks like she already knows."

I should have kept walking. Everything in me that had survived twenty-odd years in the Hollow said keep walking, say nothing, leave no thread connecting your life to anyone else's. Corvess had taught me that lesson before I could write my own name, and even knowing what I now knew about him—even carrying his betrayal in my chest like a swallowed stone—I couldn't shake the wisdom of it.

But the child put her small hand on my arm, and the shard in my pack warmed against my spine like a coal wrapped in wool, and I heard myself say:

"Can you fight?"

"Badly."

"Good. Badly is honest. Men who fight well always think they'll win." I stood. "There's food if you carry things. There's no pay. The job is walking until we can't, and then walking more."

He looked at me for a long moment. "What are we walking toward?"

"A gate."

"To where?"

"Nowhere. It doesn't work. That's rather the point."

And here was the strange thing, the thing I would turn over for years afterward: he nodded. He picked up his useless sword, climbed out of the ditch, and fell into step behind us as if he'd been waiting all winter for someone to give him a direction that wasn't a battlefield.

"I'm Tam," he said, after half a mile.

"I know," I said. "You told the child."

He glanced down at her. "She doesn't talk."

"She listens better than most people talk."

* * *

The hedge-witch came to us three nights later, or perhaps we came to her; the distinction blurred out there, where the frost had taken the farms but not yet the willows, and every abandoned homestead looked like every other one.

We were sheltering in a root cellar beneath a collapsed house—Tam had insisted on checking for bodies first, and finding none, had seemed almost disappointed—and I woke near midnight because the shard was humming. Not warm. Humming, a thin vibration through the leather of my pack, like a struck string trying to remember its note.

Someone was outside, kneeling in the snow, holding their bare hands up to our hiding place as though warming them at a fire they couldn't see.

I came up through the hatch with a knife and found a woman older than me by a decade, gray-streaked hair in a practical braid, wearing three coats layered like armor against a cold that her clothes clearly weren't winning. Her fingers were blue. Her face was wet—not with tears, though tears were close behind, but with something more confused than grief.

"It sings," she said, when she saw me. "It's been singing since yesterday. I followed it. I couldn't not follow it." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "My magic's been dying for two years. Little cures, little charms, and each season less. Like a fire going down to coals. And last night I felt—" she pressed her hand flat against the air above the cellar door, feeling the hum through the ground—"I felt like a hearth in a house where everyone's gone. Just radiating. Just *warm*."

"You followed a feeling through a killing frost," I said.

"I followed the only warm thing left in the world." She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the calculation under the desperation: she was mad, but not stupid. "You're carrying it. You and the girl. It answers you."

"It doesn't answer anyone. It's a rock."

"Mara." She said my name and my whole body went still. "The rock says your name when you sleep. Did you know that? It says it over and over, like it's practicing."

Behind me, in the dark of the cellar, the child made her low insistent sound. *This one.*

Her name was Ilsa. She had a staff she leaned on more than seemed strictly necessary for her age, and a satchel of dried herbs she admitted were mostly useless now, kept more from habit than hope. She was, she said, the last hedge-witch of the Aldenmark valleys, which had stopped being valleys in any meaningful sense about the time the frost moved past them.

"Why do you want to come with us?" I asked her. "If your magic is dying anyway, why chase another kind of it?"

She considered that longer than most people consider anything. Then: "Because when it finally goes out, I'd like to be looking at something worth the dark."

I didn't have an answer for that. I let her in.

* * *

The boy was different. The boy frightened me.

We found him—or he found us, again the distinction failing—in the ruins of a waystation two weeks out, a stone building with a good roof that Tam declared defensible and I declared too good, because places that invited shelter usually wanted something for it.

He was maybe ten. He sat in the corner of the main room, perfectly clean, perfectly calm, eating dried apples from a sack that must have been full when he arrived and was empty when we found him. He watched us enter with polite interest, the way you'd watch travelers come into an inn.

"Hello," he said. "Are you lost?"

"No," I said. "Are you?"

"I don't know." He said it without any self-pity whatsoever, which was the frightening part. "I don't remember arriving. I don't remember before. I know things—I can read, I can count, I know what a king is and what winter does—but I don't remember *happening* to me. Any of it." He offered the empty sack apologetically. "I would share, but I finished."

Tam checked the building twice. No one else. No tracks leading in except the boy's own small boots, and those only from the treeline, and beyond the treeline the snow lay unbroken in every direction, as though he'd simply begun.

The child walked straight to him and sat down beside him and took his hand, and the boy looked at their joined hands with an expression of mild scholarly interest, and the shard in my pack went warm—truly warm, hearth-warm—for the first time since the ferryman's crossing.

"He's like me," the child said.

It was the longest sentence she'd managed in weeks, and it landed in the room like a dropped plate.

"Like you how?" I asked carefully.

She frowned, hunting for words that the dead tongue had plenty of and ours apparently lacked. Finally she touched her own chest, then the boy's, then held up her hands and turned them over, empty.

"Hollow," she said. "Good hollow. Safe hollow."

Ilsa, by the doorway, had gone pale. "Child," she said gently, "where did you learn that word?"

The girl just looked at her, and Ilsa looked at me, and nobody said anything else, because there was nothing to say that wasn't enormous.

His name—he'd kept the name, clung to it like a plank—was Wren. He couldn't say where he'd gotten it. When Tam asked him what he *did* remember, he counted on his fingers, serious as a clerk: "How to read. How to whistle. That someone taught me to whistle, though not who. That porridge is better with salt. That being alone is a thing you can survive but shouldn't have to." He paused. "That's most of it. I'm hoping to add to the list."

"And if we say no?" I asked. "If we tell you to stay here?"

He thought about it honestly, which children do and adults pretend to. "Then I'd wait until someone came who said yes. Someone always comes eventually. That's one of the things I know without remembering learning it." He smiled then, quick and unguarded. "Besides. The warm rock likes you, and I like the warm rock. It's very simple."

* * *

So we became five.

I want to say I resisted it harder than I did. The truth is that somewhere between Ilsa's second night of watch (she wouldn't sleep unless she could see the shard, and I pretended not to notice) and Tam quietly reorganizing our packs by weight without being asked, and Wren teaching the child a whistling song in exchange for words in her dead tongue—somewhere in there, without my permission, we became a company.

I hated it. I want that on the record, wherever records go. Every person added was a throat the heralds could cut, a weakness with legs, a debt I hadn't chosen. I had spent my whole life owing no one and being owed by no one, and it had kept me alive in the Hollow the way a locked door keeps a house safe: completely, and at the cost of ever having anyone inside it.

But the frost was taking everything else. Villages burned their furniture and then their floors and then their churches, feeding warmth to a winter that never paid it back. On the roads we passed people walking nowhere with the particular blankness of the truly out of options, and every one of them, sooner or later, lifted their heads as we passed—because they felt it. The shard's warmth carried, faint but unmistakable, like woodsmoke from a neighbor's chimney. They smelled it the way the starving smell bread.

Most of them we passed. Some of them we fed. A few of them, the child decided, we collected.

"Why them?" I asked her one night, watching Tam teach Wren to hold a knife properly and Ilsa mend her third coat with thread she didn't have to spare. "Why these? There are hundreds on this road. Thousands. Why does the rock pick a broken soldier and a dying witch and a boy with no inside?"

The child considered my question with the seriousness she brought to everything, chewing a strip of dried meat like it might contain the answer.

"Not broken," she said finally. "Empty-shaped." She pressed her hands together, curved, making a bowl. "Rock fits."

I thought about the Hollow, where magic couldn't go, where memories were currency because feelings were dangerous, where I had built a whole self out of other people's borrowed pasts precisely because I trusted none of my own. Empty-shaped. The rock fits.

Maybe that was all any of us were to it. Vessels. Containers waiting for whatever the covenant meant to pour back into the world.

Or maybe—and this thought I kept in a locked drawer, and only opened late at night when the shard hummed my name against my spine—maybe it wasn't choosing vessels at all. Maybe it was choosing family. Maybe the Oathstone had spent a thousand years shattered, and what it wanted, what it had always wanted, was exactly the thing I'd spent a lifetime refusing to build: a circle of people bound to each other by choice instead of law.

I watched Ilsa tuck the blanket higher around Wren's shoulders without waking him. Watched Tam take the last watch he didn't need to take, so that I could sleep, because he'd noticed I hadn't been sleeping, because that was the kind of man the army had ruined and the road was slowly repairing.

"Go to bed," I told him.

"In a minute."

"That's an order, deserter."

"Yes, ma'am." He grinned at the fire. "Strange, isn't it. Four days ago I was waiting to die politely in a ditch."

"And now?"

"Now I'm waiting to see where you're all going." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sincerity. "Turns out that's enough reason to get up in the morning."

I didn't answer. But I slept better that night than I had since the ferryman's price, and if the shard sang my name in the dark, softer now, almost fond—

Well. I was starting to get used to being known.

It was two days later, at the burned waystone where the roads forked toward the old duchies, that Ilsa went white mid-sentence, gripped my arm hard enough to bruise, and pointed north along the road we'd come by.

Three figures in white, distant but unmistakable, moving with the unhurried certainty of things that had never once needed to hurry.

They'd found us after all.

And they weren't alone.
