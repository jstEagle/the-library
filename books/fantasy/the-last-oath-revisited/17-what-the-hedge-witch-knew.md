## 17. What the Hedge-Witch Knew

The fire Ilsa built was a hedge-fire, not a campfire—built wrong on purpose, the way her grandmother had taught her. Three stones for the hearth, one for the guest, and a pinch of salt in the kindling so that anything listening from the dark would taste it and turn away. Old craft. Dying craft, now. She could feel it going out of her hands the way warmth goes out of a house with a broken window: slowly, then all at once.

She'd known something was wrong with her magic since midwinter. A hedge-witch's power was small by design—a charm against chilblains, a blessing on the beehive, a word to make milk keep an extra day. It came up through the soles of her feet like water through roots, slow and steady and shallow. Lately the water was lower. Her charms took twice the effort and held half as long. The fever-charm she'd laid on the boy in the last village had lasted four hours instead of four days, and she'd told no one, because what would she say? *My gift is leaving me. My gift is dying.*

She knew why. That was the worst part. She'd known since the frost came early and stayed, since the hedges stopped budding and the wells started tasting of iron. Magic was a conversation between worlds, and one of the parties to that conversation had gone silent—or worse, gone hostile. You couldn't charm a field when the thing you charmed it *against* had stopped answering.

So when the woman called Mara asked her, third night out from the frozen river, whether she could look at the shard—the glowing fragment the child carried like a doll—Ilsa said yes before her caution could catch up with her mouth.

* *

They sat apart from the others. The deserter's absence still hung over the company; they'd buried him that morning under a cairn of frost-split stones, because the ground wouldn't take him, and Mara had stood over the cairn for a long time without saying anything at all. The hollowed heralds sat at the fire's edge where they always sat, empty-eyed and obedient, eating when fed and staring when not, and Ilsa did not look at them longer than she had to. Whatever those things were now, they had been men once, and looking at them too long felt like reading someone else's letters.

The child sat close to Mara, as always, with the shard wrapped in cloth against her chest. When Mara nodded to her, the girl unwrapped it, and the light that came off it was unlike any light Ilsa had ever worked with. Firelight leaned toward things. Lantern-light pushed things back. This light just *was*, the way moonlight is, the way memory is—present everywhere and touching nothing.

"May I?" Ilsa said.

Mara glanced at the child. The child looked at Ilsa with eyes too old for her face, and after a moment, nodded once.

Ilsa reached out—not with her hands but with the thin rootlet of her craft, the sense that let her feel whether bread had spoiled or a cradle had bad air—and touched the shard.

What came back nearly knocked her over.

It wasn't a vision. She was too weak, and it was too big, for visions. It was more like pressing your ear to a door and hearing an ocean on the other side. Depth. Age. A voice so far down and so vast that her little gift registered it less as sound than as *weight*. And threaded through it, familiar as her own name, the signature of Oathkeeper work—the binding-craft, the covenant-grammar, the deep old magic that hedge-wives' charms were the dried-out crumbs of. Every blessing she'd ever spoken was a folk-song version of whatever this was.

Ilsa pulled her hand back and sat very still.

"You know what it is," Mara said. It wasn't a question. "You've been looking at that shard like you know it since the river."

"I know its *family*," Ilsa said. "Not it." She looked into the fire. Her whole life she'd been careful, quiet, small—a woman who kept her head down and her craft humbler than it was. But her gift was dying anyway, and the boy with no memories was watching her from across the fire, and there are moments when a person understands that silence has stopped being a virtue and become just another way of wasting time.

"I'll tell you a story," she said. "But I want you to understand first that it isn't a story. Not really. It's the shape my grandmother gave the truth because the truth itself is dangerous to say plain. She learned it from her mother, who learned it from hers—all the way back to women who were alive when it happened. We kept it wrong on purpose. Kept it as a tale about a stone and three sisters and a broken promise, so that if anyone ever asked us directly, we could honestly say we only knew a fireside fable." She smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile. "That's how you keep a secret through ten reigns and two purges. You make it boring."

The child made a small sound. When Ilsa glanced over, the girl was leaning forward, the firelight sliding across her face, and her expression—

Her expression was the expression of someone hearing their own name spoken in a dark room.

"Three realms," Ilsa began. "That's how it starts. Not heaven and hell and earth—that's the churchmen's version, and it's wrong. Three *realms*. Equal. Side by side, the way three houses stand around a shared well. One was ours—flesh and season, birth and rot, everything that grows and everything that grieves. One was the Court of Winter—ice and stillness, yes, but not evil. Remember that, because everyone gets it wrong. Winter isn't wicked. Winter is *necessary*. Things sleep. Things rest. Things end. Without that realm, nothing in ours would ever finish, and unfinished things go mad."

"And the third?" Mara asked quietly.

"The Summer Court. Flame and growth and endless making." Ilsa shook her head. "And there's the trouble, right there, in that word. *Endless.* Because summer doesn't believe in endings. Summer wants everything to grow forever—more light, more heat, more life, more *summer*—and it cannot abide the idea that some things must stop. So a long time ago—my grandmother said nine hundred years, but numbers that old are just decoration—Summer moved on the well. Tried to claim all three houses' share of it. There was a war, the kind of war that leaves scars in geography, and when it ended, the wise ones of all three realms met at a place between places and made a covenant. An oath. Bound together, each realm promising to keep its season and honor the others'. And the oath needed a keystone. Something to hold all the promises at once, in all three worlds, forever."

She nodded at the shard.

"They made the Oathstone."

The child's hand had crept to the cloth at her chest, though the shard lay bare between them, and Ilsa saw her fingers trembling.

"It stood at the Sundered Gate—the door between the realms, so the seasons could pass back and forth, so winter could come to our world and go home again, so the flow never stopped. And for a long age it worked. That's the part nobody believes anymore, that there was ever a time it *worked*. But here's what my grandmother's line remembered that the chronicles forgot—" Ilsa leaned forward. "The Oathkeepers weren't kings. They weren't priests. They were *keepers*. Sworn servants of the covenant itself, above any crown, above any court. Their job was the balance. Nothing more. And they held it for generations, until the year the Summer Court tried again."

* *

The fire had burned down to coals. Nobody had added wood. Ilsa realized, distantly, that the hollowed heralds had turned their heads—both of them—to face her, as though even emptied vessels recognized when something true was being poured nearby. She chose not to think about that.

"Summer didn't march this time," she said. "Summer *infiltrated*. Bought keepers, turned them, got its people inside the covenant's keeping. And the plan, when it finally showed itself, was elegant: don't break the Stone. *Turn* it. Take the keystone and rotate it, so the flow favored Summer eternally. Eternal growth. Eternal day. Every harvest doubled, every child born strong, every winter abolished." She spread her hands. "Paradise, they called it. And half our ancestors' neighbors knelt to it gladly, because paradise sounds lovely right up until you notice it requires someone else's realm to burn as fuel."

"But the Oathkeepers stopped them," the boy said. He'd drifted closer, drawn the way he was always drawn to the shard's light. "That's the story. The good keepers stopped the bad ones."

"No," Ilsa said gently. "That's the part we changed. Listen, because this is the heart of it, and it's ugly." She looked at Mara. "When the loyal keepers understood how deep the rot went—when they counted the names on Summer's side and found friends there, teachers, lovers, their own sworn brothers—they saw there was no winning. Any keeper left alive could be turned. Any Stone left whole could be turned. So they made the choice my grandmother could never tell without crying, and she was telling it fourth-hand."

Ilsa let the silence sit a moment, because some truths deserve a moment.

"They shattered it themselves. The loyal keepers broke their own Stone. Broke the Gate. Broke the covenant that had held three worlds in balance since before their grandmothers' grandmothers—rather than let it be turned into a weapon of domination. They chose a wounded world over an enslaved one. And the winter that's killed your fields and my craft for these past years? That's not Winter attacking. That's the wound. That's three realms bleeding out through a broken door, and the cold coming through the crack because cold is simply what pours through a hole in the world."

Mara hadn't moved. But Ilsa saw the woman's hand open and close in her lap, once, like someone testing a grip.

"The keepers scattered," Ilsa went on. "Some died. Some were hunted. And some fled into the one place magic couldn't follow—the valleys, the hollows, the dead corners of the world where the covenant's reach had always been thinnest. Hid their bloodlines there. Married into villages. Kept nothing, swore nothing, became farmers and thieves and ferry-men, and the great secret passed down to daughters as lullabies and to sons as warnings, thinner each generation, until it was just a story about a stone and three sisters and a broken promise." She shrugged, old and tired. "Until tonight, when a thief walks into my camp carrying the future in a mute child's arms, and asks me to look at it."

"I'm not a keeper," Mara said. Too fast. Too flat.

"No," Ilsa agreed. "Probably not." She looked at the child—at the way the girl's whole body was angled toward Mara like a compass needle, at the shard's light resting on both their faces equally, as though it could not quite tell them apart. "But blood remembers what names forget. And that Stone didn't shatter clean. Shards went everywhere, and every shard has been trying to get home for nine hundred years." She reached out and, with the last of her failing craft, touched the fragment once more—lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "This one isn't trying to get home. Look at it. It's already *found* something."

Nobody spoke. Beyond the ring of stones, the frost ticked and settled against the earth, patient as a debt.

* *

Later, when the others slept, Ilsa stayed up and fed the fire, because feeding fires was the one act of her craft that had never once failed her. The child came and sat beside her, uninvited, and for a while neither of them said anything.

Then the girl spoke—in her own strange tongue first, softly, tasting the words. Then, haltingly, in words Ilsa knew:

"My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother kept bees."

Isla blinked. "Did she, now."

"Hedge-craft." The child's accent was strange, the vowels bent, but the grammar was perfect, the way a memory is perfect. "Salt in the kindling. Three stones for the hearth. One for the guest." She looked at Ilsa with those old eyes. "You kept it. All of it. Down all the years."

"Somebody had to," Ilsa said. "Mostly we kept it wrong."

"You kept it *alive*." The girl said it with a fierceness that didn't belong on a child's face, and then, abruptly, she seemed to shrink, to remember herself, and looked down at the shard in her lap. "Wrong stories survive," she added, quieter. "Perfect ones die. Everyone knows that where I'm from."

"Where's that, then?"

The child didn't answer. But her thumb moved over the shard, and the light beneath her skin pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding whether to beat.

Across the dead grass, Ilsa saw Mara sitting awake at the edge of the firelight, watching them both with an expression Ilsa had seen before on the faces of people standing outside a house where something important was happening without them. And behind Mara, further out in the dark, the two hollowed heralds stood facing north—both of them, perfectly still, perfectly aligned, like needles finding a magnet.

Toward the Sundered Gate, Ilsa thought. Even empty, they knew the way home.

And so, she understood suddenly, did the shard. Which meant the story she'd told was not finished—it was only the part that came before, and the part that came next would be written by a thief, a child, and whatever was left of a hedge-witch's dying craft.

She put another branch on the fire and watched it catch, and thought: *nine hundred years of women keeping a story wrong so it would live, and it ends with me, in a frost-killed field, telling it true at last.*

Grandmother would have laughed herself sick.
