## 18. Ambush at the Waystone

Dren Hale had run from one army already in his life, and he'd learned the lesson the way most men learn it: slowly, expensively, and with other people's blood on his boots.

The lesson was this: running doesn't save you. Running just decides where you die.

He thought about that a lot these days. He thought about it as he walked at the back of a strange little company through a country turning to frost, carrying a borrowed spear he didn't trust and a debt he trusted less. He thought about it when the boy with no memory laughed at nothing. He thought about it when Ilsa's fire sputtered green instead of gold and she covered it with a cough. And he thought about it hardest of all whenever Mara Nystrom looked at him — because she had pulled a memory out of a soldier's head once, clean as drawing a knife, and Dren knew what that felt like from the wrong end of it, and he knew what she could do, and he followed her anyway.

That was the part he couldn't explain to himself. Not love. Not gratitude. Something closer to recognition: two people who had both done a thing they couldn't take back, walking the same frozen road because stopping meant admitting it.

The waystone came into view an hour past dusk.

It stood alone on a bald hill where the road forked — one branch dropping toward the river towns, the other climbing into pine country gone white and silent. The stone itself was old, older than any of the kingdoms whose borders wandered around it like drunk men. It stood twice a man's height, carved over every surface with script nobody living could read, and it hummed.

Not loudly. Not constantly. But if you put your palm against it, you felt something under the rock like a second heartbeat, slow and stubborn, keeping time with nothing.

"The old roads all ran by stones like this," Ilsa said, breath smoking. "Before the covenant broke, they were lit. You could walk from the Sundered Gate to the sea and never lose your way."

"And now?" the boy asked. Tam, they called him, though none of them were sure it was his name. He wore it the way you wear a coat someone hands you in the cold.

Ilsa looked up at the dark carvings and didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Mara set the child down and let her stand against the stone. The girl pressed both hands flat to its face and closed her eyes, and for three heartbeats the humming deepened, dropped half a tone, like a struck bell settling. Then it stopped entirely, and the silence it left behind was worse than the sound.

"It's listening," the child said. She said very little, and when she spoke it came out flat and certain, a coin spent rather than offered. "Something is coming up the river road. It walks without footprints."

"Heralds," Mara said.

"Or worse." The girl turned. Her eyes caught the last grey light and held it. "We should not sleep here."

"We're sleeping here," Dren said, before he'd decided to say it. Four heads turned toward him. He shrugged, feeling his old uniform ghost across his shoulders even though he'd burned it two winters ago. "Because we've been sleeping nowhere for six days, and tired people die stupid. If they're coming, they're coming whether we're on this hill or hiding in a ditch, and I'd rather meet them standing on ground we chose than lying face-down somewhere we didn't."

Nobody argued, which surprised him. Mara studied him a moment longer than the others did — studying him the way she studied everything now, with that new hunger behind her eyes, the look of a woman who has discovered she can open locks and can't stop checking doors — and then she nodded once.

They made camp in the hollow beneath the stone's north face, out of the wind. Ilsa got a fire going on the third try; her magic had been guttering for days, and everyone pretended not to notice the way she coaxed the flame with actual twigs and actual flint, like some ordinary woman, like anyone. Corvess wasn't with them yet — that was still ahead of them, though Dren wouldn't have known it then — and so their circle held five: a thief, a witch, an empty boy, a silent child, and a deserter.

Dren took first watch because taking first watch was the only thing left of the soldier he'd been that he was willing to keep.

* * *

He sat with his back to the waystone and watched the dark come down off the pines.

The cold was wrong. He'd grown up north of here, in a country where winter was a season, a thing with a beginning and an end, a thing you complained about and survived. This cold had no shape. It didn't come from anywhere. It lay on the land like a hand pressing down, patient, testing how much weight the world would bear before something cracked.

He heard the others settle behind him. Ilsa's low murmuring — prayers, maybe, or inventory. Tam breathing slow and even, asleep between one thought and the next, the way only the truly empty can sleep. Mara awake. He could always tell when Mara was awake; the whole camp changed pressure, like a room before lightning.

And the child. The child never seemed to sleep at all. Sometimes he woke in the night and saw her sitting upright, hands in her lap, watching the darkness with the patience of something that had already seen how it ended.

An hour passed. Maybe two. The moon came up thin and white behind cloud-scum, and by its light Dren noticed the frost.

It was climbing the waystone.

Not forming — climbing. He watched a rime of white crawl upward along the carvings, filling the dead script letter by letter, line by line, like ink following a pen. Where the frost touched, the stone went silent. He realized the humming had stopped some time ago without his noticing, and that the absence had a direction, and the direction was down.

He stood up slowly. His mouth had gone dry in the particular way it always did before an ambush, back when ambushes were his trade — the body knowing before the mind agreed to know.

"Mara," he said quietly.

She was already awake. Of course she was.

"Frost on the stone," he said. "Moving uphill."

She came to stand beside him, and he watched her see it, watched her face do the thing it did — go still, go simple, all the noise stripped away until what was left was just a woman doing arithmetic with terrible numbers.

"Wake them," she said. "Quietly."

He woke Ilsa with a hand on her shoulder and a finger to his lips. He woke Tam the same way, and the boy opened his eyes instantly and completely, no fog of sleep at all, and that had always unsettled Dren more than anything else about him. The child needed no waking. She was standing, and she had taken the shard from wherever she kept it, and it threw a light the color of deep ice — not warm, not friendly, but steady, a lamp rather than a candle.

"They're here," the child said.

"Where?" Ilsa whispered.

"All around."

Dren drew the borrowed spear from the canvas wrap and hated its balance immediately, the way you hate a stranger's handwriting. Below the hill, the river road ran pale through the dark, and along it — he saw them now, and wished he didn't — figures in white were walking. Not marching. Walking, unhurried, spread across the road in a loose crescent, and more of them angling up through the trees on either flank. They moved the way snow falls. No wasted motion. No hurry at all, because everything hurried eventually froze, and they had never once been in a hurry in their long white lives.

"How many?" Ilsa breathed.

Dren counted out of habit and stopped counting out of mercy. "Enough."

The heralds reached the base of the hill and stopped. All of them at once, the whole crescent freezing mid-stride, and the silence that followed had texture, weight, intent. Then one of them stepped forward — taller than the rest, its mask of white porcelain catching the shard's light — and spoke. Its voice was beautiful, which was somehow the worst thing about it. A voice like a cello played in an empty church.

"MARA NYSTROM. DAUGHTER OF THE OATHKEEPERS. YOU ARE ASKED TO COME DOWN. THE COURT OFFERS WARMTH. THE COURT OFFERS REST. THE COURT FORGETS NOTHING AND FORGIVES EVERYTHING."

"I've heard about the Court's forgiveness," Mara called back, and her voice didn't shake, and Dren marveled at that even as his own knees wanted to. "It forgives like a river forgives. Everything it takes stays taken."

"THE CHILD WAS NOT MADE FOR YOUR HANDS."

"No," the child said, stepping up beside Mara, holding the shard out before her like a lantern in a storm. "I was made for hers."

The tall herald tilted its head, and Dren would have sworn the whole crescent of white figures tilted with it, one motion, one will.

"THEN WE ARE SORRY," it said, and they came up the hill.

* * *

Later — much later, in whatever place dead men keep their thoughts — Dren would try to order those minutes and fail. Memory broke apart under that kind of pressure the way ice breaks underfoot: all at once, everywhere, leaving you standing on water and calling it ground.

He remembered the light. The heralds didn't glow but the frost did, every crystal on the hillside catching the shard's blue-white and throwing it back doubled, so that the night itself seemed armed. He remembered Ilsa screaming words in a language that hurt to hear, throwing up a wall of force that flickered like a dying lantern and held for exactly as long as her failing magic allowed — four seconds, five, enough time to matter, not nearly enough time to win.

He remembered Tam, who owned nothing and feared nothing because fear requires a self to be afraid for, stepping calmly in front of the child with a camp-knife in his fist.

And he remembered himself. That was the part that kept. He remembered choosing.

Because there was a moment — clear, wide-open, almost peaceful — when he understood exactly how this ended. The heralds would take the child. They would take Mara, or worse. They would kill the rest as an afterthought. And the only thing standing between that ending and any other ending was time, and he was the largest object in the way.

Running doesn't save you. Running just decides where you die.

He set his feet on the hilltop beside the waystone, gripped the borrowed spear in both hands, and picked the gap between two oncoming shapes of white, and he charged downhill into the middle of the Winter Court.

He killed one. He was almost sure he killed one — drove the spear's butt-spike up under the porcelain jaw of a herald that flowed around Ilsa's collapsing wall like water finding a crack, and felt something give that wasn't bone, and the thing staggered, and its mask split, and what was underneath wasn't flesh or emptiness but weather, a face full of moving snow, and then Ilsa's last burst of force took it sideways off the hill and it came apart into the night like a handful of thrown flour.

Then a white hand closed on the spear shaft, and another on his shoulder, and they were so cold that cold stopped meaning anything, became simply a fact of the universe, and a voice like the cello again said, close to where his ear would have been:

"LITTLE SOLDIER. YOU COULD LIE DOWN NOW. IT IS SO MUCH EASIER LYING DOWN."

"Yeah," Dren said, and grinned, because he'd always grinned at the worst moments, his sergeant had hated it, they'd nearly flogged him for it once. "But I'm rubbish at easy."

He twisted, and the spear tore free of the grip that held it, and he used the last of what he was to do the one useful thing left: he turned his back to the camp and his face to the enemy and he roared — wordless, animal, enormous, the war-cry of a man who had deserted one army and refused, absolutely refused, to desert this one — and every white head on the hill turned toward the sound.

Every head. Even the ones already reaching for the child.

He bought them eleven seconds. He counted them. Old habit, counting — counting paces, counting arrows, counting the drums before a charge. Eleven seconds in which Ilsa dragged Tam clear, in which the child pressed herself and the shard against the waystone's humming face, in which Mara Nystrom turned around and saw a deserter she'd known for nineteen days standing alone in a ring of winter with his stolen spear broken in his hands.

Eleven seconds. Then they took him.

There was no pain, was his last surprise. There should have been pain, and instead there was warmth, sudden and total and obscene, spreading from their hands into his chest like brandy, like summer, like every kind thing that had ever been promised to him and never delivered. The frost bloomed up his arms in feather-patterns. His knees folded. The hill tipped gently sideways, and the last thing Dren Hale saw with his own eyes was Mara's face above the ring of white — not afraid anymore, past afraid, somewhere beyond it where faces stop being faces —

— and then he was lying on his back looking up at the thin moon through the branches of a tree that wasn't there, and he was fourteen years old again and his mother was calling him in for supper, and it was warm, oh, it was finally, finally warm, and he went toward the light of the kitchen window the way water goes downhill, gladly, gratefully, home.

* * *

What happened next, Dren couldn't have witnessed, being elsewhere. But stories travel strangely after death, and pieces of it reached him, the way pieces of a wreck wash ashore.

He learned that Mara screamed his name — his real name, the one he'd given her on the second night with a joke attached, and hearing her use it without the joke was somehow the truest eulogy he ever got.

He learned that Ilsa went to her knees and emptied herself, spending the last functioning ember of her magic in one raw shout that knocked three heralds sprawling and bought nothing, saved no one, changed nothing except to prove she'd tried.

He learned that Tam stood in front of the child and did not move, could not be moved, a boy with no memories and therefore no instinct for self-preservation, offering an empty body to filled ones.

And he learned — this part came to him last, and stayed longest, and he carried it into whatever came next like a coin carried into exile — he learned what Mara did.

She had pulled a memory from a soldier once, outside the Hollow, accidentally, drowning in a stranger's whole life. Since then she had practiced. Small things. Careful things. Edges of minds, shallow waters.

There was nothing small or careful available to her on that hill.

Two heralds reached her at once — the tall one with the split-mask voice and another, younger-seeming, its porcelain still uncracked. They took her arms. The cold poured in. And somewhere inside Mara Nystrom, in the place where the Hollow had trained her to steal and the road had trained her to survive and the shard had trained her to hope, something gave way that had been waiting all along to give way.

She reached into them.

Not for a memory. For *all* of them.

Dren hoped she'd hesitated. He liked to imagine she hesitated. But the truth, when the truth finally surfaced through the wreck, was that she didn't — or that the hesitation happened somewhere too deep to count, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and what came out of her came out the way a scream comes out, without permission, without craft, without any possibility of taking it back.

She drank them.

The tall herald stiffened. Its mask did not fall, but its *presence* fell, the vast patient pressure of it collapsing inward like a snowbank sliding off a roof. The second herald lasted longer, clawing, its cold flooding up her arms and turning her lips grey while she held on with both hands and her teeth and twenty-six years of taking things that belonged to other people, and then it too went quiet, all at once, like a house when the last candle dies.

Two heralds stood on the hillside of the waystone, swaying slightly, wearing their white like clothes nobody lived in anymore. Their masks were intact. Their eyes behind the masks were not empty the way Tam's were empty — Tam's emptiness was a room swept clean, awaiting furniture. These were rooms with the walls torn out. There was no awaiting anything. There was no *anything.*

The remaining heralds stopped.

All of them, mid-motion, the whole white crescent going still as statuary, and for a long moment the only sounds on the hill were Ilsa's ragged breathing and the child's voice saying, softly, steadily, over and over: *Mara. Mara. Mara.* Calling her back the way you call someone out of deep water.

Mara let go of the two empty ones and stepped back and looked at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. Frost was melting off her fingers in the shard's light, dripping, each drop loud as a hammerfall.

"I didn't mean—" she started, and stopped, and started again. "They were going to— he was—"

"I know," the child said.

"I can *feel* them." Mara's voice cracked down the middle. "They're still *there*. They're standing there and there's no one standing in them, and I *did* that, I know exactly what I did, I felt every piece of them go down my throat like—"

"Mara." The child touched her wrist. The touch of the shard-bearer, Dren learned later, was the one warmth in all the world that night. "Look at me. You did it because he died for us, and you will carry it, and carrying it is the proof you are not them. The Court empties people and calls it mercy. You did it in grief, in one terrible second, and you will grieve it every day after. That difference is the whole covenant. That difference is everything."

Mara looked at the empty heralds. They stood patiently among the living, neither enemy nor ally, neither dead nor alive, waiting for instructions from a woman who had unmade them and now owed them a debt that could never be named, let alone paid.

One of them turned its head — slowly, mechanically, following the movement of the shard — and Dren, watching from whatever shore he'd washed up on, felt the horror of it and the pity of it tangle together past undoing.

"What do we do with them?" Ilsa asked hoarsely. She was looking at Dren. At what had been Dren. Someone had closed his eyes and straightened his legs and laid the broken spear on his chest, and it was a fine gesture and it changed nothing and it was everything.

Mara wiped her face with the back of her hand. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and level, the voice of a woman standing at the edge of something she couldn't see the bottom of.

"We don't leave them," she said. "Whatever they were, whatever I made them — they're ours now. They come with us."

And the two empty heralds, hearing her — or hearing the shape of command that lived in her blood, the Oathkeeper inheritance rising in her whether she willed it or not — took their first steps as hollow men, and followed her down off the hill, into the dark, toward the river, toward the Gate, toward whatever waited at the end of the winter for all of them.

Above the waystone, the old carvings filled with frost one final time, glowed briefly blue-white, and went dark forever.

And far to the north, in a court of endless ice, something ancient opened its eyes and said, with the first curiosity it had felt in a thousand years:

"*She took.*"
