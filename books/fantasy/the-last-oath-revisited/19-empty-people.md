## 19. Empty People

They followed us for two days before anyone said it out loud.

I'd noticed them the first evening, at the edge of my awareness the way you notice weather—a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the light. Two figures at a distance of maybe four hundred paces, keeping pace with our column on the ridgeline, never closing, never falling back. They didn't hide. That was the part that made my skin crawl. Heralds in white were supposed to hunt, to circle, to strike from frost and shadow. These two walked in plain sight like pilgrims following a shrine road.

"They're still there," Tam said at dusk, not turning around. He had sharp eyes for a boy with no past. "The tall one and the one who limps."

"I know."

"Are they waiting for reinforcements?"

"No." I watched them through Ilsa's spyglass, though I already knew what I'd see. The tall one stood with his hands loose at his sides. The limping one had stopped to look at a dead bush with an attention that lasted too long, as if he was trying to remember what bushes were for. "They're following."

"Why?"

Because I emptied them. Because I reached into their heads at the Waystone with Deserter's blood still cooling under me and tore out everything that made them heralds—the Queen's whisper, the mission, the oaths, the names—and when my hand came away they were standing in the snow with nothing behind their eyes but sky.

I said none of this. "Get some sleep, Tam."

* * *

By the second morning they were close enough that the children of our little company could see them without squinting, and the company had opinions.

Ilsa wanted to kill them. She said it quietly, over the fire, stirring tea she couldn't warm anymore—her magic had gone thin as winter water, and we all pretended not to watch her fail at small things. "They're Winter Court," she said. "Empty or not. Leave them sleeping in the snow and be gone by dawn."

"They haven't raised a hand against us," I said.

"They haven't done anything. That's worse. A weapon that's dropped is still a weapon, Mara. Someone will pick them up again."

She wasn't wrong. That was the problem with Ilsa—she was almost never wrong, and she wore her correctness like her staff: something to lean on when everything else gave way.

"They're not weapons," I heard myself say. And then, because lying to Ilsa was like lying to a stone wall, "I don't know what they are."

The child knew. The child had known since the first hour. She sat beside me with the shard wrapped in wool against her chest, and when I looked at her she looked back with those too-old eyes and signed, slow and deliberate, in the gesture-language we'd built between Vellum and the river:

*You made them. You keep them.*

Not an accusation. The child didn't accuse; she observed, the way ice observes a lake. But observation from her landed harder than accusation from anyone else, because she was usually right.

*What do I do?* I signed back.

*Ask them.*

*They can't speak. I took their words.*

The child tilted her head. *You took what was put in them,* she signed. *Maybe not what was underneath.*

* * *

I went out to them at midday, alone, which every sensible person in the company told me not to do. Ilsa called me seven kinds of fool. Tam offered to come and was refused so firmly he didn't ask twice. Even Wren, the deserter's ghost of a man who hadn't spoken since his friend died at the Waystone, caught my sleeve as I passed—two fingers, brief, then let go. Permission and farewell in one touch.

The hollowed heralds waited for me at the bottom of a fold in the land where the snow gathered blue in the shadows. Up close they looked worse than they did through glass. Heralds wore beauty like armor—faces cut from winter light, skin with the faint pearl sheen of ice over deep water. These two had lost whatever held the beauty up. Their faces had begun to sag toward ordinary. The tall one had stubble coming in along his jaw, dark against the white, and I realized with a lurch that heralds weren't supposed to grow. Something in them was running again. Some clock I'd started when I broke the mechanism.

"You know me," I said.

They looked at me. It was not the look of men regarding an enemy, or a stranger, or even a woman. It was the look of men regarding the only fixed point in a world of drifting snow. I hated it immediately. I had seen that look before, on the faces of people whose memories I'd stolen for coin—the moment after, when the thief was the only thing left they recognized.

"The tall one," I said. "Do you have a name?"

Nothing. A blink, slow, like a man surfacing from deep water.

I crouched, put us eye to eye. Under the fading pearl, his face was young. Twenty-five, maybe. Somebody's son. Somebody's friend, once, before the Court found him—before the Court did to him what courts do, which is make soldiers out of people by removing the parts that object.

"I'm going to do something," I told him, "and you can stop me if you want. Nod if you understand."

He nodded. Slow. Obedient. My stomach turned over. Obedience was how this had started, somewhere, in some white hall—I'd seen enough of the heralds' minds at the Waystone to know the shape of their making, the kneeling, the vows, the cold poured into the spaces where doubt used to live. I had unmade that. And what I'd left behind was another kind of obedience, because emptiness doesn't choose; it just leans toward warmth.

I reached out with the gift, careful as a thief picking a lock she'd broken once before, and touched the surface of his mind.

There was nothing where the Court had been. No Queen's whisper, no mission, no oath. Good. But beneath the emptiness—not far beneath, like a coin under new snow—there was a residue. Not memory exactly. Memory has texture, weight, a place where it attaches to a self. This had no attachment. It floated. A woman's hands kneading dough. A hillside of yellow flowers. The smell of tar and rope. A name spoken with love, and the name was—

I pulled back like I'd touched a hot stove.

"What is it?" the limping one asked.

I nearly fell over. His voice was rough, rusted, the voice of a man who hadn't used it in years—but it was a voice. "You can speak."

"Some." He worked his jaw like the words were heavy. "Words come back before the rest. He's slower than me." A nod at the tall one. "Or deeper down. I don't know the word for it. You'd know better than us, wouldn't you. What you took."

"Yes," I said, because there was no honest alternative. "I took it. All of it. You came to kill me and I—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "I reached into your heads and pulled out everything that made you *you*, and I left you standing in the snow."

"Not everything," the limper said. "There's things floating. A kitchen. Flowers. A woman singing off-key." His mouth did something that might have been a smile in a living face. "We hold onto those. We walk behind you because your fire is warm and your people don't run from us, and because the little one glows like a hearth. We don't know if that's wanting or just... falling downhill. Toward the low warm place."

"That's not an answer to what you are," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

I sat between them in the snow for most of an afternoon, asking questions and getting back the shape of a horror I hadn't let myself look at directly.

Here is what thieving teaches you, if you do it long enough: every mind is a house. Most people's houses have someone home. The Court's heralds had been houses with the owner evicted and a garrison installed. When I pulled the garrison out, I hadn't restored the owner. There was no owner to restore. The Court had taken him years ago, carefully, in ceremonies with names like *the Quiet Vow*, and what I'd done at the Waystone was burn the garrison out along with the walls.

So now there were two men-shaped structures walking behind us, holding on to a kitchen and some flowers and a woman singing off-key, and the question Ilsa had asked kept circling back around with its teeth out.

A weapon that's dropped is still a weapon.

But a person that's emptied—is he a person? Was he ever?

"You came to the Waystone to take the child," I said. "To bring her to your Queen. Do you remember wanting that?"

The limper considered for a long time. Watching him think was like watching ice form—you could see the process but not follow it.

"I remember the shape of it," he said finally. "Wanting leaves a dent. Like snow after something's been carried across. I know the Queen mattered. I know she was—" he searched, found nothing, tried again— "*everything*. The way your mother is everything, or your god. I know I would have walked into fire for her. And I know that now, when I try to feel that, there's just..." He opened one hand, palm up, catching falling flakes. "Cold. Ordinary cold. Weather-cold. Isn't that strange? I used to think the Queen *was* winter. Now winter is just winter."

Something in my chest cracked along a fault line I hadn't known was there.

Because here was the truth underneath, the one I'd been carrying since the Waystone like a stone in my boot: when I emptied them, I hadn't just disabled enemies. I'd freed them—from the Queen, from the vow, from the whole architecture of compulsion. And freedom, delivered that way, felt exactly like damage. They were the only two beings in all the realms who owed the Winter Court nothing, and they hadn't chosen it, and they couldn't enjoy it, and they followed me because warmth is gravity and they had nothing else to orbit.

I hadn't wronged them, maybe. Or I had. Both things were true, stacked like cards, and neither cancelled the other.

Accountability without absolution. Ilsa's god would have had a word for it. Mine had only ever had a price list.

* * *

That night I brought it to the company, all of it, because a leader who decides alone in the dark is just a tyrant with a smaller kingdom. We sat in the lee of a fallen waymarker, the shard glowing soft under its wool, and I laid out what I'd learned.

"So they're harmless," Wren said. First words in days, and they came out flat as a blade laid on a table.

"Harmless today."

"Then leave them."

"And next month?" Ilsa said. "Next season? The Court knows where its hounds went missing. If they can puppeteer one of them—" she glanced toward the tree line, toward the distant shapes of our followers bedded down in the snow, "—they can puppeteer all of them. Right into our camp. Right into the girl."

"The Court can't reach them," the child said.

Everyone turned. She spoke rarely enough now that her actual voice—low, clear, older than her size—still startled people. "The Queen whispers through the vow. The vow is gone. To re-make them she would have to start from the beginning, and the beginning requires their consent." Her eyes came to mine, and there was something in them I'd learned to dread: the certainty of someone who has already lived through what's coming. "Consent is the one door winter cannot open. Your new heralds taught us that, Mara, whether you meant to teach it or not."

"New heralds," I repeated. "Is that what they are?"

"They're yours," the child said simply. "Everything you break, you own. That's the law of thieves. You told me that yourself, on the Sere. So decide what you own."

Silence around the fire. Wren staring into the flames. Ilsa's knuckles white on her staff. Tam looking at me with the terrible open trust of a boy who has nothing and therefore believes everything.

And I thought about my rule—the one rule I'd kept for fifteen years in the Hollow, the line I'd drawn in the sand of my own trade: *never steal from children.* Everyone thought it was mercy. It wasn't, or not only. It was recognition. Children's memories are load-bearing. Take them and the whole house comes down, and you can't give back what you've dissolved into the space between your fingers. I'd known that about children. I'd somehow convinced myself adults were built differently—that the soldier grown to kill me was fair game, that the merchant and the magistrate and the lovesick lordling had spare rooms, redundant walls. That taking from them cost less.

Standing in the snow at the Waystone with two men collapsing behind my eyes, I had learned otherwise. There are no spare rooms. There is only the house, and the fire, and what you carry out of it.

"We keep them," I said. "Fed, armed if they want it, free to go whenever they want to go. And when this is over—if anything of them is left to recover—we help them recover it. Whatever it costs."

"It'll cost," Ilsa said.

"Most true things do."

Wren laughed, short and bitter. "The memory thief takes responsibility for her thefts. Write it down. No one will believe it later."

But he said it while looking at the fire, and his shoulders had come down half an inch from wherever grief had locked them, and I thought: that's the other thing about accountability. It's contagious. Admit what you broke out loud and everyone else starts counting what *they're* carrying, and suddenly the load is shared.

* * *

I went out once more before sleep, to check on them, telling myself it was caution and knowing it was penance.

They had made camp in the proper soldier fashion, backs to a rock, fire small and disciplined—habits persisting in bodies whose reasons for the habits were gone. The tall one was asleep. The limper sat awake, watching the sparks climb.

"You should have names," I said. "Can't keep calling you the tall one and the limping one."

"Don't remember ours."

"Then pick new ones. Names are the one thing that grows back fast."

He was quiet a long time. Sparks rose. Somewhere behind me the child's shard pulsed gently, a heartbeat in wool.

"When I was in the Court," he said, "we had ranks instead of names. I was Second of the White Road." He tested the words like old joints. "Second of the White Road. That's not a name, that's a leash with a title."

"Then drop it."

"Give me one of yours, then." And for the first time his face moved into something fully alive—not a smile, not yet, but the ground a smile would build on. "You stole plenty. Share some back."

So I sat down in the snow next to the man I had unmade, and I told him about names—about the Hollow, about Corvess calling me *little magpie* because I hoarded shiny things, about the ferryman who'd taken my mother's song and how I meant to steal it back someday. And he listened the way empty people listen, completely, holding each word like a coal carried in cupped hands.

I didn't notice the light until he went still.

Over the eastern hills, where the sky should have been black, a glow was building—white shot through with green, auroral, wrong. Not weather. Not any winter I'd known. It rose like a curtain being lifted at the edge of the world, and under its light the snow began to fall upward.

The child appeared at my shoulder, the shard bare in her hands now, blazing.

"He's found us," she said. "No—" She corrected herself, and her voice went very quiet. "*They've* found us. That's not the Queen. That glow is old, Mara. That's the Gate."

Two hundred miles ahead of us, the Sundered Gate had lit the horizon like a wound reopening—and every face around me, filled and hollow alike, turned toward it as if pulled on strings.

The tall one woke, and without being told, began packing.
