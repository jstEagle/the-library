## 20. The Sundered Gate Revealed

Ilsa had felt the Gate before she saw it, the way you feel a storm behind a mountain—a pressure in the teeth, an ache in the old places where her magic still lived. For three days the sensation had been growing, and for three days the frost had been growing with it, until the ash road gave way to ground that crunched like broken crockery underfoot and the trees stood bare and silvered, every branch sheathed in ice so perfect it looked poured rather than grown.

"Tell me again what your grandmother said," Mara said. She was walking ahead with the child on her hip, which she did more often now than she used to. The girl was seven or eight by Ilsa's guess, light as a bundle of kindling, and she'd stopped fighting to walk on her own frostbitten feet somewhere outside the second dead village.

"She said the Gate stood at the roof of the world," Ilsa said. "She said when the Oath broke, the sky over it cracked like an egg." She paused at the crest of the ridge they'd been climbing all morning. "She also said the hedge-witch who tells you a story is telling you what she needs to believe. So weigh it accordingly."

Mara looked back at her. Wind pulled at the woman's dark hair; there was frost in her eyelashes, and she didn't brush it away. "And what do you need to believe, Ilsa?"

Ilsa considered the question honestly, because lying to this woman had become impossible somewhere around the ferry crossing, when Mara had paid a memory of singing for their passage and hadn't let her face change once. "That the stories are true. That there's a door, and it opens, and winter ends."

"And if the door doesn't open?"

"Then I'll have walked my failing magic across half the world to die in sight of the answer anyway," Ilsa said. "Which is more than most hedges get."

The boy—Tam, though he'd only answered to it because Mara gave him the name—tugged at Ilsa's sleeve and pointed. He didn't speak much. He couldn't remember anything before the road, and he treated speech as something borrowed that might be reclaimed by its owner at any moment. But he pointed now, arm steady, finger aimed past the ridge and up, up into a sky that had gone the wrong color.

The horizon above the next valley wasn't white and it wasn't blue. It was a bruised, deep violet, like the skin over a wound that's been pressed too hard, and from its center rose a column of pale light—not sunlight, nothing warm could be that color—that stood perfectly still while clouds streamed around it.

"That's not weather," Ilsa said quietly.

"No," said the child.

They all turned. It was the first time any of them had heard her voice in four days, since the ambush at the Waystone, since the deserter Bren had died with his sword out and his eyes open, since the heralds had come apart under Mara's hands like wet paper. Her voice was small and clear and old, an accent none of them could place, words shaped with a precision no child learned.

"That's the seam," she said. "Where it was cut."

* * *

They descended into the valley through what remained of a forest. The trees here had died standing, and the frost had preserved them exactly—every trunk white, every fallen leaf crystallized mid-curl, a forest turned to glass and held a breath from shattering. Nothing moved in it. No bird, no beast, no wind. Their footsteps sounded obscene, cracking and grinding, and Ilsa caught herself holding her breath between steps without meaning to.

It was Tam who found the first stone.

He'd wandered a few paces off the path, the way he did, drifting toward things the rest of them didn't see. He came back leading them by the hand to a pillar of gray rock thrust up out of the frost like a broken tooth, taller than two men, its surface carved edge to edge with writing. The script ran in vertical columns, dense and regular, and around its base the snow had melted in a perfect circle, exposing black soil steaming faintly in the cold.

Ilsa's staff grew heavy in her grip. Not metaphorically—the wood actually leaned toward the stone, tugging at her hands like a dog on a rope.

"It's pulling," she said. "The whole thing. Like a drain."

"The wards," the child said, touching the carved columns with one mittened finger. Her voice had that strange doubled quality again, the girl's pitch carrying sentences too old for it. "They're drinking what's left. Holding the shape of the door after the door is gone." She traced one column down, down, and stopped where the writing ended in a gouge, as if something had taken a bite out of the history. "This says: *here the realms were sewn.* This part—" her finger moved to the gouge, "—is missing."

"What happened to it?" Tam asked. It was perhaps his tenth word in two days.

"Somebody needed it more than the stone did," the child said.

Mara had gone still beside the pillar. Ilsa watched her take the shard from the pouch at her belt—the shard that had led them across kingdoms, burning cold in the woman's hand since the night it arrived on her doorstep—and watched Mara hold it up against the carved columns without touching them.

The shard lit.

Not glowed—lit, the way a lamp lights, sudden and total, throwing white light across the frost-forest in a radius of thirty feet. And every column of writing on the stone lit with it, the grooves filling with radiance until the whole pillar stood blazing like a torch, and for one heartbeat Ilsa saw the forest as it must have been: green, alive, loud with birdsong, a road of packed earth running past the pillar toward—

Toward the light in the sky.

Then the vision folded shut like a fan, and they were standing in the frozen corpse of the world again, and the child was saying, "It remembers being useful. Come on."

* * *

The ruins announced themselves gradually, the way great things do. First a line of foundation stones emerging from the frost, running straighter than anything nature built. Then archways, freestanding, their walls long collapsed but their curves intact, each one big enough to drive a cart and ox through. Then staircases climbing to nowhere, terraces of pale stone furred with ice, and everywhere the same vertical script, covering everything, the way ivy covers a wall.

"This was a city," Ilsa said.

"This was a hinge," the child corrected. "People lived here to tend the door. Thousands of them. Priests, masons, oath-singers." She pointed with her chin at a row of low buildings near the central terrace. "Barracks. Those are dormitories. There—" a squat round structure, "—a cistern. They had a whole life here, and none of it mattered except the hour the door opened."

"How often did it open?" Mara asked.

The child was quiet for a moment. "Every year. At the turning. All three realms sent their people through, and they traded, and they swore the year's oaths, and then it closed." Her mitten tightened on Mara's shoulder. "It wasn't a wall. It was a handshake."

Isla looked up at the violet stain in the sky, closer now, huge, wrong. "And someone shook too hard."

"Someone wanted to keep the hand," the child said.

The hollowed heralds trailed behind them as always, five figures in scorched white, moving when the company moved, stopping when the company stopped, their empty eyes fixed on the middle distance where Mara happened to be. They passed through the outer archway without hesitation, and Ilsa noticed—they noticed all of them notice—that the frost thinned where the heralds walked. Just slightly. Just enough that the stones showed through.

"They remember the way here," the child said softly, following Ilsa's gaze. "Whatever she took out of them, the roads are still in their feet."

The central terrace opened before them like a cupped palm, and there Ilsa finally understood what her grandmother's story had failed to convey, because there was nothing in the story about scale, nothing about silence, nothing about the way a wound in the world looks less like destruction and more like absence.

The Sundered Gate had been, judging by its socket, a ring. A ring of white stone perhaps two hundred feet across, set upright in the earth like a coin balanced on its edge, and of that ring perhaps a third remained—a sweeping arc rising from the frozen terrace, carved with script, its inner surface polished smooth as still water. The rest was simply gone. Not toppled, not shattered. Absent, along with whatever had filled the ring, along with the air where it should have been. In its place hung the violet light, a vertical seam in the world maybe forty feet tall, ragged at the edges, and from it poured cold—not wind, not weather, just cold itself, flowing outward like water from a spring, feeding the winter that had eaten three kingdoms.

Standing before it, Ilsa felt her own magic lean toward the seam the way the pillar-stone had leaned, hungry and terrified at once, a dog at the edge of a cliff.

"It's exhaling," she said. "Winter isn't falling on us from somewhere. It's pouring out of *there*."

"The other side is worse," the child said. "Or deeper. Or both." She slid off Mara's hip and stood looking up at the arc of the ring, at the seam inside it, and Ilsa saw her expression do something complicated—grief and recognition braided together, the look of a person coming home to a house that has burned down.

Mara crouched beside her. "Is it what you remember?"

"No," the child said. "In what I remember, it's already broken. I've never seen it whole. Nobody alive has." She reached up and took the shard from Mara's hand. "But the stone remembers. And the stone is patient."

She walked to the base of the surviving arc.

There, at the point where the ring met the earth, Ilsa saw it: a socket. A fist-sized depression in the white stone, hexagonal, its edges worn smooth, its interior dark—and around it, faint but unmistakable, the same vertical script, arranged in a circle like a lock awaiting a key.

The child pressed the shard into the socket.

It fit. Of course it fit—the sound it made was the sound of a bone returning to its joint, a deep, satisfied *click* that Ilsa felt in her sternum rather than heard. Light ran up the arc of the ring, racing through the carved script like fire through a fuse, and the whole surviving third of the Sundered Gate blazed white, and the seam of violet light flickered—

—and steadied.

The cold kept pouring. The winter kept breathing out of the wound in the world. The light died back down the ring until only the socket glowed, a single coal in a dead hearth.

"Turn it," Mara said. "It's a key. Turn it."

The child put both hands on the shard and pushed.

Nothing. Not resistance—nothing, the absolute immobility of a thing that has never been movable. She pushed until her small arms shook. She braced a foot against the ring's base and threw her whole weight sideways, and the shard sat in its socket like a nail in granite, and the seam breathed its endless winter, indifferent.

"Help me," the child said, and her voice cracked, and for the first time since the Waystone she sounded exactly her age. "It's supposed to turn. It's *supposed* to—"

Mara took hold of it with her. Tam added his small hands. Even Ilsa, staff abandoned, laid her chapped palms against the shard, and the five hollowed heralds drifted close and stood watching with their vacant eyes, and the shard did not turn, did not shift, did not so much as warm.

It was Mara who stopped first. Ilsa saw her go still, saw her tilt her head the way she did when she was reading a memory, and then saw her pull her hands away slowly, as if the shard had bitten her.

"It's not locked," Mara said.

"It won't move—"

"It's not *locked*, Ilsa." Mara's voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman handling something explosive. "Locks resist. This is—" she searched for the word, and Ilsa watched her find it and flinch from it, "—this is empty. There's nothing in the mechanism. It's like a lock with no bolt, a lamp with no oil. Turning it would be turning nothing." She looked up the blazing-faded arc of the ring, at the seam, at the winter pouring through. "The key fits. The key is right. But there's nothing behind it to catch."

The child stared at her. And Ilsa, watching the girl's face, saw the exact moment hope and impossibility met—the same meeting she'd been dreading since Vellum, since the ferry, since the first night a mute child had appeared on a doorstep in a valley where magic couldn't follow. The girl's mouth worked. Her eyes went bright and furious.

"Then we fix the mechanism," she said.

"With what?"

"I don't know yet!" It burst out of her, high and cracked, a child's tantrum wearing an old woman's vocabulary. "But I came *back*—I crossed—I *paid*—" She stopped. Pressed her mittens against her mouth. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "There has to be a way. I'm standing proof there's a way."

The wind, if it could be called wind, moved through the ruins, carrying cold out of the seam and across the terrace, and the frost on the stones glittered like a field of tiny knives, and nobody said anything for a long time.

It was Tam who broke the silence. He'd been crouched at the ring's base, tracing the circular script around the empty socket with one finger, following it all the way around, twice, three times. Now he looked up.

"There's words here," he said. "Under where the key goes. Little words. Nobody's read them."

Mara crouched beside him. Ilsa crowded in. And there, worn nearly to nothing, in a script smaller and older than the columns, circling the socket like a whispered footnote beneath a proclamation, the letters caught the last of the shard's glow:

*I am not turned. I am fed.*

The child read it aloud, translating as she went, her voice slowing word by word, and by the end of it she had gone very pale under the wind-chap.

"Mara," she said. "What does an Oathkeeper feed a door?"

And Mara Nystrom, memory-thief of the Hollow, last heir of the covenant-breakers, looked at the socket, and at the seam exhaling winter into a dying world, and at the small girl who had crossed time itself to stand here—and Ilsa watched her arrive at the answer one terrible step ahead of everyone else, her face going carefully, deliberately blank, the way it went whenever she was about to pay for something.

"I think," Mara said lightly, far too lightly, "we're going to need to talk to Corvess."
