## 21. Why It Won't Turn

The shard fit the socket perfectly.

That was the first wrong thing. Mara had spent three weeks carrying it—wrapped in wool against her skin because it burned cold, then unwrapped entirely when Ilsa warned that insulation was feeding something in it—and she had learned its moods the way you learn a horse's. It balked near running water. It hummed before dawn. It grew heavy as a stone of grief whenever the heralds came near.

It did not balk now. She set it into the black socket at the Gate's heart and it slid home like a key into a lock built for it, and the sound it made was not metal but breath—the long exhale of a door held shut for centuries finally being asked, politely, to open.

Nothing happened.

* * *

The Gate itself was worse than Ilsa's stories had promised. Not a gate at all, really—that had been the first disappointment. It was a wound. The Sundered Gate stood where three worlds had once pressed against each other like hands clasped in greeting, and when the Oath broke, the clasp tore free and left this: a seam in the air forty feet tall, edges ragged with frozen light, exhaling winter in slow tides. Behind the frost you could see *through*—glimpses of a green country, a white one, a dark one, all of them dimmed, all of them starving. The frost on our side crept a handspan closer every hour. Tam measured it twice a day with a knotted cord and reported the numbers in a flat voice that fooled no one.

Mara circled the socket a third time. The shard sat in its slot like a coin in a dead man's mouth. Around its rim, faint script had surfaced—Oathkeeper cant, the child's language, the one no kingdom remembered. She couldn't read it. She looked to the girl.

The child stood at her elbow the way she always did, close enough to touch, watching Mara's face instead of the Gate. That was another thing Mara had stopped pretending not to notice. The girl watched her the way you watch someone you are afraid of losing.

"What does it say?" Mara asked.

The girl's lips moved. Her voice, when she used it—which was more often now, syllable by syllable dragged up from wherever she'd buried it—still sounded like someone reading in a foreign accent, each word correct and strange. "Turn and be bound. Bind and be turned." A pause. "There is more. Underneath."

"Read the underneath."

The child went still. Then she said, very quietly, "It says what it costs."

* * *

They made camp in the antechamber—Ilsa's word for the shattered hall that had once, presumably, been a place of ceremony and was now mostly roofless archways and snowdrifts shaped like kneeling figures. Desh rigged a fire in the lee of a fallen pillar, burning the last of their trade-tobacco because there was nothing else left to burn out here on the ash road, and the smoke smelled like someone else's better years.

Mara didn't sit. She walked the perimeter of the Gate, touching the frozen light at its edges only once—it bit her fingertips with cold so old it felt personal—and tried to think like a thief instead of an heir.

Because here was what she knew about locks: every lock is a question, and every mechanism is an answer written in brass. If the key fits and the door won't turn, either the key is wrong or the mechanism wants something the key doesn't carry.

The shard fit. So the mechanism wanted something else.

She'd seen locks like that before, back in the Hollow, in the houses of the rich. Doors that opened only for blood. Boxes sealed to a voice. One merchant in the upper terraces kept his ledger-chest warded so it would only yield to his own hand *and* his own memory of the day he bought it—a two-part truth, body and mind. She'd stolen around it by stealing the memory first, holding it whole in her head while her fingers worked, feeling the man's wedding day play behind her eyes like a play she was starring in against her will.

Two-part truth. Body and mind.

"Oh," she said, aloud, to no one.

Desh looked up. Ilsa looked up. The child did not look up; the child had already gone pale, which meant the child had known longer.

"It's not a key," Mara said. "Or it's not only a key. It's a *pledge*. You don't just put the stone in the socket. The stone has to—" She groped for it. Words were tools too, and hers were slipping. "The covenant isn't stored in the rock. The rock is just the signature line. The oath itself lives in the people who swore it. In what they remember. In who they decided to be."

Ilsa rose slowly, one hand pressed to the small of her back, staff in the other. "Say more."

"The Gate won't turn because the shard is empty," Mara said. "Or—no. Because the shard was never supposed to hold anything. It's a socket inside a socket. The first socket is the stone. The second is a person. An Oathkeeper puts themselves into the stone the way you'd put a letter into an envelope, and the Gate reads the person, not the rock." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "We've been hauling a blank piece of paper across the world."

* * *

They argued until the fire burned low. Desh argued logistics—if it needed a person, could it take any person? Could they fetch anyone, buy anyone? He'd been a soldier; he thought in requisitions. Ilsa argued theology, or whatever you called the study of things that had killed your faith twenty years ago. The hollowed heralds stood at the camp's edge through all of it, silent, patient, their emptied eyes reflecting the firelight like coins at the bottom of a well, and Mara caught herself looking at them too often, because they were the closest thing to an answer standing right there.

People whose memories had been taken. People who still breathed, still walked, still followed her like dogs hoping to be fed.

Not dead. Just—subtracted.

"No," she said, when Desh raised the hollowed ones as an option, testing her. "No. Whatever this takes, we're not making more of those." And she found she meant it with her whole chest, which surprised her, because three months ago she'd have priced them without blinking.

It was the child who settled it, the way the child settled most things now: not by arguing but by knowing.

"The old words say," she began, halting, translating from a tongue that lived in her bones rather than her schooling, "*the Oath is not spoken with the mouth nor signed with the hand. It is given with the whole of the remembering. What you have been, you lay down. What you will be, the Gate holds.*" She stopped. Looked at Mara. Looked away. "Something like that."

"Something like that," Mara repeated. "Meaning?"

"Meaning it isn't death," the girl said. "Death would be easier. Death leaves the body and takes everything anyway. This is—" Her hands moved, shaping something invisible, careful, like handling a bird. "This is choosing it. Every memory, laid down willingly, one by one. Everything you stole. Everything you lived. Everything you are. Into the stone. And then the Gate turns."

Silence, except the wind coming through the wound in the world, which had a note in it tonight, high and thin, almost like singing.

"That's not sacrifice," Desh said flatly. "That's erasure with paperwork."

"It's both," Mara said.

And here was the thing she understood then, standing in the ruin with frost creeping toward her boots and four people watching her and one of them—she was nearly sure—one of them watching her with grief that hadn't happened yet: she understood why the Oathkeepers had broken the stone instead of using it again. Why her parents, whoever they had been, had run to the one valley in the world where magic couldn't follow and let their daughter grow up thinking she was nobody.

Because this inheritance wasn't land. Wasn't title, wasn't power, wasn't even the thieving gift in her blood. The inheritance was a bill. Generations back, someone had accepted payment terms: *you may bind three realms together, and the price is a self.* And every Oathkeeper since had inherited the debt along with the name, and some of them had paid, and some of them—her parents, maybe, or their parents—had looked at the bill and chosen flight instead, and hidden the family silver in a mute child's hands and sent it forward in time like a letter with no return address.

The covenant was paid in identity. It had always been paid in identity. The winter outside, the dying lands, the Queen's hunger—all of it was interest accruing on a debt no one alive had agreed to, and the principal came due in full, in person, in memories.

Mara sat down by the fire at last, because her legs had opinions.

* * *

Later, when the others slept—Desh on watch, Ilsa breathing in the rattling rhythm of a woman spending magic she no longer had, the boy curled nearest the coals with his usual dreamless stillness—Mara took inventory.

It was what thieves did before a job. She'd taught herself that at fourteen: count what you carry, count what you can lose, know the difference. So she counted.

She counted the Hollow first. The smell of wet slate after rain. Old Sef's tobacco-stained grin when she brought him a good harvest. The rule she'd built her life around—never steal from children—sitting in her chest like a keel. The taste of the bread she baked on rest days, the one skill she had that required patience instead of nerve. The exact gray of Corvess's eyes when he lied to her, which she had catalogued without knowing why, because some part of her had always known he would lie eventually.

She counted the road. Ilsa's stories. Desh humming marching songs with the words changed so they weren't marching songs anymore. The boy's hand on her sleeve the night after the ambush, saying nothing, saying everything.

She counted the stolen ones, the hoard she still carried from Vellum and before—the ferryman's first love, the soldier's whole life, a hundred small joys harvested honestly and dishonestly both. Those would go first. That was easy. That was almost relief.

Then she counted the rest, the lived ones, and the counting slowed.

Her mother singing. Gone already—paid to the Ferryman of the Sere, and she still reached for it some mornings like a tongue probing a missing tooth. What remained of her mother was a shape: warmth, a low room, the feeling of being sung *about*. She could surrender that. Probably. Probably she could.

But then: the first time she'd slipped into a stranger's head and come out shaking and *hungry*, and understood what she was. The night she'd fled the Hollow with a burning child in her arms and magic flooding back into her blood like pain and like home. Standing at the boundary with frost on her lashes, choosing, actually choosing—

If she gave all of it, what was left to do the giving?

A person, the philosophers said, was a continuity of memory. Take the thread and the cloth fell apart. She would walk into that stone and the woman walking in would simply stop, and something else—something with her face, her hands, her blood, and none of her reasons—would walk out and turn the Gate and call it victory.

"They'll write songs about you," Desh had said earlier, trying to make it bearable. "The heir who paid everything."

"The heir won't hear them," she'd answered. "The heir won't remember paying. Some other woman will stand there in my skin and get all the credit, and she won't even know what it cost, because knowing what it cost was the thing I gave up." She'd smiled at him. "That's not martyrdom, Desh. That's a con I'm running on my own corpse."

He hadn't had an answer. Nobody had an answer. Even Ilsa, who had answers the way other women had moles—everywhere, mostly harmless, occasionally ominous—even Ilsa had gone quiet and touched her staff and said nothing.

* * *

Near midnight, the frost at the Gate's edge crossed the knotted cord's fourth mark, and Tam called the number in his flat voice, and no one commented, because commenting had stopped helping days ago.

Mara stood alone before the socket. The shard glowed faintly, patient as a creditor. The script around its rim caught the light of the frozen seam and threw thin shadows across her wrists, and she read them now without the child's help—or half-read them, the way you half-hear a language you're beginning to dream in. *Turn and be bound. Bind and be turned.*

She put her hand flat on the stone.

Cold, yes, but underneath the cold, something else. Attention. The Gate was not a machine, she realized. It was a witness. It had been waiting all this time not for a key but for a debtor, and it knew her blood the moment she touched it, and somewhere in its ancient attention there was something that might, generously, be called pity.

"I know what you want," she whispered. "I just haven't decided if I'm going to give it."

The shard warmed under her palm—one degree, no more. Acknowledgment. Or hunger. With gods and locks, she thought, it was hard to tell the difference.

Behind her, footsteps in the snow. Small ones. The child came to stand beside her, not touching, never quite touching, looking up at the wound in the world with an expression far too old for her face.

"You understand it now," the girl said. Not a question.

"I understand it."

"And you know what it means. For you."

Mara looked down at her. "It means the woman who decides doesn't survive the decision. It means I pay with everything, and what's left isn't me." Her throat tightened. "It means I'd rather the winter win than be unwritten, and I hate myself a little for that, but there it is."

The child flinched—as though the words had struck somewhere specific, somewhere prepared for, somewhere long-bruised. She stared at the shard. Her jaw worked. And for a moment Mara saw her wrestling with something, some sentence balanced on the tip of her tongue, some truth with weight enough to crack the ice under all of them.

"Mara," the child said—her own name, the name she should not have known the day she arrived at the door, the name she had never once explained. "Before you decide. There's something you need to know. About who sent me. About *who I am.*"

The frost exhaled. Somewhere behind the Gate, faintly, a bell that had not rung in three hundred years began, very softly, to toll.

The girl drew a breath, and Mara braced herself the way you brace for a lock to give.
