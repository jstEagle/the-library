## 22. The Child's Secret

The words had been building in me for a long time. Longer than Mara knew—longer than the road, longer than the frost, longer even than the Gate with its wound-mouth and its socket of black stone that would not turn.

I had learned her language the way ice learns to be water: slowly, then all at once. By the fire at the ruined waystation, with Ilsa's staff propped against the wall and the empty boy snoring softly beside the coals, I could say almost anything I wanted.

I had not yet said the one thing that mattered.

She sat across from me, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, because sharpening was what Mara did when her hands needed something to do while her mind worked. The shard lay between us on the flat stone, giving off its cold light, patient as a held breath.

"Mara," I said.

She looked up. She always looked up when I said her own name, as if hearing it from my mouth were a small strangeness she had decided to allow. "What is it?"

"I can speak now. Properly. You know this."

"I know it." Her knife paused. "It's been good, hasn't it? Having you talk. Ilsa nearly wept when you told her which herbs were wrong."

"There's something I haven't said."

"Then say it." She set down the knife. She was very still in the way she was still before danger—a thief's stillness, a survivor's stillness—but underneath it I saw something else, something older, and my chest hurt with how well I knew it, because it was mine.

I picked up the shard. It did not burn me. It had never burned me. That should have been the first clue for all of them, and maybe it was; maybe they had all known and said nothing, the way people do around a truth too large for the room.

"When you touched it, the first night," I said, "it answered you. You told me that. Cold fire in your hand, and it knew your name."

"It knows lots of things."

"No," I said gently. "It knows *you*. Because it chose you. And it chose you—" I stopped. This was the part. This was the cliff edge I had been standing on since the snowfall, since the door, since before any of it, standing on this edge inside my own small body with my own small voice, waiting for the courage to fall.

"Mara. I am you."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark the wind moved through the dead trees like a long exhale.

"That's not funny," she said quietly.

"I'm not joking." My hands were steady. I had practiced this moment ten thousand times in the silence of being mute, and now that it came, the practice held. "You asked me once where I came from, and I couldn't answer, and you let it go because you are kind under all the thieving. So let me answer now. I come from after. From a winter that never ended. From a world where the Gate stayed sealed and the realms starved and everyone I loved died slowly or badly or both. I come from a future where *we failed*."

"We?" Her voice cracked on the word.

"You. Me. The same person, stretched across years that shouldn't exist anymore." I turned the shard in my fingers and its light moved over her face, over the face I would grow into, the lines I would earn, the eyes that were already tired the same way mine would be tired. "There was a covenant once. Three realms bound by one oath. Your parents helped break it rather than let one realm rule. But breaking it didn't free anyone—it just made everything worse. Winter ate everything. And there was a woman named Mara Nystrom who spent her whole life trying to fix what her bloodline broke, and at the end, at the very last, at the Sundered Gate with the frost coming in—"

I faltered. Not because I didn't remember. Because I remembered too well.

"—at the end, she failed. The stone wouldn't turn for her. She hadn't understood the price until it was too late, and by the time she understood, there was nothing left of her to pay it with. So the covenant—the reforged covenant, the one that finally worked, the one I walked through—did the only thing left. It sent someone back. Someone who already belonged to the stone. Someone who *was* the heir, so the shard would know her, would warm to her, would lead the company here. It sent me."

Silence. The boy slept on. Ilsa's breathing was slow and even in the next chamber, though I suspected—Ilsa missed nothing—that she was awake behind her closed eyes.

Mara's face had gone white in the shard-light. "That's impossible."

"Yes," I agreed. "Almost everything true is."

"The child at the door. The snow. All of it—you were sent to find *me*?"

"I was sent to be found *by* you. There's a difference." I leaned forward. "Listen to me carefully, because this is the part that matters. The shard chose you before I ever arrived. It chose the heir. But it could not reach you alone—it needed hands that could carry it, and it needed a reason for you to keep carrying it when every reasonable instinct told you to throw it in a river. So it took a future that no longer exists and folded it into a child, and put the child on your doorstep in the first unnatural snowfall, and waited."

"Why a child?" Her voice was barely above a whisper. "Why not send a warrior, a scholar, an army—"

"Because armies fail. Scholars get bought. Warriors die. And because—" I reached across the space between us and took her hand, and her fingers were cold, and mine were colder, "—because you have exactly one rule you have never broken. Never steal from children. Never turn away a child at your door. It's the only uncorrupted thing in you, Mara. The covenant built its whole plan on it."

She pulled her hand back slowly, like someone touching a stove. "How do you know that about me?"

"Because I *am* that about you." I smiled, and it hurt. "I know why you flinch at lullabies. I know why you won't cross running water without counting to seven first. I know you keep a button in your pocket from a coat you wore when you were six, and you don't remember why anymore, but you've never lost it. I know all of it, because it's all going to happen to me. Happened to me. Both. The tenses break here—don't fight them."

"The button," she said faintly, and her hand went to her pocket without her permission, and I watched her close her fingers around it, and I watched her understand.

* * *

She stood up. Walked to the doorway. Stood looking out at the frost-lit ruins, at the half-sealed Gate exhaling its slow breath of winter beyond the ridge, and I let her stand there, because I remembered being her in that exact posture, needing the dark and the distance to hold something enormous.

When she spoke again, she didn't turn around.

"If you're me. Then you know what the Gate wants. What it actually wants."

"Yes."

"And you're going to tell me anyway. Even knowing what it costs you to say it."

"I've had a long time to learn that knowing a cost doesn't stop the thing from being necessary." I joined her at the doorway. We stood shoulder to shoulder, two heights of the same person, breath fogging in twin plumes.

"The Gate needs an Oathkeeper's willing sacrifice," I said. "Not death. Worse than death, if you ask me. It needs memory. All of it. Every stolen thing in your hoard and every lived thing in your head, surrendered into the stone, so the new covenant has material to bind with. A person made entirely of memories cannot swear a new oath unless the oath is paid in memories. That's why it wouldn't turn for Corvess. That's why it won't turn for Ilsa or the boy or any of them. Only for us."

"And if I pay," she said, each word placed down carefully like a coin on a counter, "then Mara Nystrom stops existing. Everything she stole, everyone she saved, every mistake, every morning—gone into the stone. And what walks away from the Gate is..."

"A woman with no past," I said. "Who will need raising. Who will need someone to teach her who she chose to be, even if she can't remember choosing it."

The wind gusted. Far off, the Gate moaned against its seals.

"That's why they sent a child," she said at last, and there was something terrible and wondering in her voice. "Not just to guide me here. To *raise me after*. To make sure the woman who comes out the other side isn't a husk. They didn't send me a guide. They sent me—"

"Family," I said. "They sent you family. The only kind either of us ever had that didn't lie."

She laughed then—a short, broken sound, quickly swallowed. "I don't accept this."

"I know. I refused it too, the first time. From the other direction."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that in the world I come from, when I finally understood the price, I refused too. I stood at that Gate and I said *no*, I said the winter could take the whole world before it took me, I meant it with everything I had." I looked up at her. "And I watched what that refusal cost. I had thirty more years to watch it. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly how much courage it takes to refuse, and I'm asking you for something harder."

"Don't ask me tonight."

"I'm not asking anything tonight." I took the shard and pressed it back into her hands, folding her fingers over it the way she had once folded a blanket around me on the first frozen road, and I felt her startle at the echo of it. "Tonight I'm only telling you the truth. Tomorrow you'll be furious. The day after, you'll try to find a loophole—there isn't one, but look anyway, it's important that you look. And someday soon, when you're ready, you'll walk out onto the ice and treat with the Queen yourself, because you'll realize she's starving too, and you'll build something better than what was broken. I know you will. I've *seen* you do it."

"How can you stand it?" she whispered. "Carrying all that. Knowing how it ends. Watching me not know."

"Because I'm not watching you not-know." My voice finally cracked, all the practice in the world failing me at once, and I let it. "I'm watching you *become*. Do you know what it's like, Mara? To sit across a fire from your own beginning? To see the person who taught you everything you know, before she knows it herself? I have spent this whole journey loving you from the outside of my own life. I got to meet you. Don't you understand what a gift that is? Most people never get to meet themselves. Most people never get to say thank you in advance."

"For what?"

"For the door." I stepped closer. "For opening the door in the snow. For the rule you never broke. For every hard, stubborn, thieving, unforgivable, magnificent choice you're going to make. I'm here because of you. Whatever happens at that Gate—whatever it costs—I was *here*, I was *warm*, I was *known*, because of you. Nothing the winter does can take that back. It already happened. It's the one thing in all the worlds that's safe."

She knelt then, down to my height, and took my face in her hands, and her thumbs wiped wetness off my cheeks that I hadn't known was falling.

"You poor, brave, impossible girl," she said. "You carried this the whole way. Mute, all those miles, holding a secret that heavy."

"I had help." I put my hand over hers. "You talked enough for both of us."

She laughed and cried at the same time, which I recognized, because I had done it, in another life, at another gate.

Then, over her shoulder, past the doorway, I saw the frost on the far ridge catch the moonlight—and saw, moving along the ridgeline, pale shapes walking in formation, unhurried, inevitable.

Heralds. More than we had ever seen together. And they were not coming for the child.

They were coming for the Gate.

Mara followed my gaze and went still, and her hand found the shard, and the shard flared bright as a struck match against the dark.

"They know," she breathed. "Somehow—they know the stone is here, and what it needs."

"Then we're out of time," I said, and the old urgency rose in me wordless as ever, except now I had words for it, and I used every one. "Wake Ilsa. Wake the boy. Whatever you're going to decide, Mara—decide it fast."
