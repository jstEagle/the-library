## 25. The Herald's Offer

I have worn ten thousand dreams like stolen coats.

This is what the mortals do not understand, huddled in their burning villages with their frostbitten prayers: I do not send nightmares. Nightmares are crude instruments, blunt as axes. Anyone can frighten a sleeper. But to enter a dream and *build* — to find the shape of a soul's deepest want and pour it into snow and starlight until she cannot tell where her longing ends and my architecture begins — that is the old craft. That is the work I was made for, back when there was still work worth making.

The thief sleeps badly. I have watched her for three nights through the frost on the antechamber stones, through the thin walls of her exhausted body. She sleeps like a woman standing guard even inside her own skull, one hand always curled toward the child, one toward the shard wrapped in wool against her breastbone. Even unconscious, she hoards. Even dreaming, she counts her treasures.

Tonight I go in myself.

Not a herald. Not a messenger wearing borrowed flesh. The heralds are useful — they are my hands, my eyes, my knives when knives are needed — but an offer of this weight must be carried by the voice that owns it. And besides. I am curious about her. It has been so long since anything has made me curious.

* * *

I build the dream from the seams outward.

Her memories are not mine to take — that is her gift, not mine, though the distinction has grown academic over the centuries — but dreams leak. Dreams are the hour when a mind stops gripping its treasures and lets them drift to the surface like coins in a thawing stream. All I must do is float among them and learn.

Here: a kitchen. A woman singing, off-key, flour on her hands. Gone before the song ends — the dream cannot hold it, because Mara Nystrom paid it away to a ferryman, and even the ghost of it slips through her sleeping fingers like water. Interesting. She has already begun spending herself. She simply doesn't know yet how much of herself the Gate will demand.

Here: a room in the Hollow. A man with ink-stained fingers teaching a girl to read, his patience fraying at the edges, his love fraying nowhere at all. Corvess. The traitor who loved her enough to sell her and hated himself enough to come back. I know his scent; my heralds tracked him across two hundred miles of ash road. He is camped half a mile below them now, keeping a fire small, keeping his penance close. Mortals and their penance. It is the only currency they mint that never devalues.

And here — ah. Here is the thing I came looking for.

A hearth. Not any specific hearth; a composite, assembled from every warm room she has ever stolen a memory of and every one she was ever allowed to keep. Firelight. Bread. A child laughing in another room. Rain on a roof instead of snow, and the particular luxury of having nowhere to be. She does not dream of glory. She does not dream of the Gate or the covenant or the realms rebalanced like scales in a merchant's hands. She dreams of *enough*. Of warmth that asks nothing. Of being, for one unwatched hour, a woman in a room rather than an heir with an apocalypse in her pocket.

Every mortal I have ever tempted has dreamed of something. Most of them dream of power, and those are easy — power is a door that opens onto a longer corridor of doors, and they walk it willingly all the way down. Some dream of revenge, which is merely power wearing mourning clothes. The dangerous ones dream of rest.

She dreams of rest.

So I build her a house.

* * *

She wakes inside the dream the way I knew she would — fast, knife-first, hand closing on a weapon that isn't there because in dreams no one carries weapons except by agreement. She stands in a doorway. Behind her, the house extends: stone floors swept clean, a fire ticking in its grate, bread cooling under cloth on the table. Snow falls past the window, but gently, the way mortals imagine snow fell before I taught it otherwise. Through the inner door, a child's voice sings nonsense syllables to a doll.

"Sit," I say.

She does not sit. She turns slowly, taking the measure of the room the way she would take the measure of a mark's house — exits, valuables, the lie beneath the upholstery. Good. I had hoped she would look for the lie. I want her to find it late.

"You're the Queen." Her voice is rougher than her waking voice. Dreams sand everything down. "The actual Queen. In person."

"Insofar as I am anywhere in person." I let my form settle into something she can look at without flinching — a woman of indeterminate years, silvered, robed in grey-white, hands folded. Grandeur frightens the clever ones into flattery, and flattery ruins a negotiation before it starts. "You may call me Marenveil. That was my name before it was my office."

"Mara."

"I know. I have known since the shard woke in your palm at your own threshold, which — you should understand — was not chance. The Oathstone has always chosen its heirs by proximity to their own blood. You were the nearest piece of itself it could reach." I gesture to the table. "The bread is real. As real as anything is, in here. Your body will wake hungry, but comforted. Consider it a courtesy."

"What do you want?"

Direct. No pleasantries, no fear dressed as manners. My heralds reported this about her and I had wondered if they were flattering themselves — hunters inflate their quarry, it makes the chase mean more. They were not inflating. She looks at me the way she looked, according to the hollowed one's残 memory, at the first herald she ever emptied: as if assessing whether I could be picked up and carried somewhere and set down lighter.

What an extraordinary creature we have made, the realms and I, between us.

"I want," I say, "to tell you a story first. Indulge me. You trade in stories; you know their price better than most. Hear mine, then name yours."

She doesn't sit. But she stops moving toward the window, which in her language is a concession. I take it.

"Before the Oathstone, there were three realms and no law between them. Yours — the mortal one — burned bright and short. Mine was winter, and winter was patient. And between us lay the realm of the Weave, the place where magic is grown rather than spent." I pause. "Your hedge-witch has told you some of this around her fires. Ilsa, yes? She tells it as a nursery rhyme with the bones removed. Let me put the bones back.

"The covenant bound the three realms to mutual obligation. Each would remember the others. Each would feed the others. In spring and summer your world poured life into the Weave, and the Weave poured order into mine, and my winter came to your world on schedule and left on schedule, and no realm grew fat while another starved. It was not kind. Kindness was not its function. It was *fair*, which is rarer and harder, and after four thousand years of fairness, do you know what your ancestors decided?"

"That it wasn't fair."

"They decided *they* were losing." I smile, and let her see it. "The mortal realms remember it as the Great Treachery — brave Oathkeepers shattering a tyrant's chain. What they forget is whose harvests had failed that decade. Whose kings chafed at giving the Weave its tithe. Your parents' generation did not break the covenant out of principle, Mara. They broke it out of appetite, and then they wrote the principle afterward, the way victors always do. And the winter that followed — the long winter, eighty years now and deepening — that is not my cruelty. That is three realms starving because someone cut the rope between them and called it freedom."

"Your heralds burn villages."

"My heralds *forage*." The cold comes into my voice before I can stop it, and I let her see that too — let her watch the ancient grievance surface like a fish breaking ice. "Do you know what it is to rule a realm of hunger, thief? Every year the long winter eats deeper into my country. My people freeze at the edges and I cannot spare them warmth because warmth is *substance* here, it is currency, it is the very meat of my world, and the Weave is dying and sends nothing, and your world's magic pools and rots behind the Hollow's walls while my children eat the cold. Yes. I send heralds. Yes. They take what keeps my realm breathing. If your world had kept its oath, I would never have needed to reach across the border at all."

The fire ticks. Somewhere in the other room, the dream-child laughs again, and I watch the sound land on Mara like an arrow finding a gap in armor. She glances toward the door. I pretend not to notice that I noticed.

"So," she says. "You're here to explain that it's all our fault. That's the pitch."

"No. The explanation is the courtesy. Here is the offer."

I unfold it slowly, the way one unfolds a map — because an offer is a map, it shows you the road and lets you imagine walking it, and the imagining is where the selling happens.

"The Gate requires an Oathkeeper's willing surrender of all held memory. You have learned this. Your boy — the empty one, the vessel — believes he can soften the cost. He is partly right, and the part he's wrong about will kill the parts of you he cannot hold. You know this too. You have been practicing giving yourself away in pieces for six nights, and each time you take back a little more than you surrendered, because that is what thieves do even with their own souls." Her jaw tightens. Good. Let her feel seen. Being truly seen is the most violent gift anyone can give, and I am about to make her a present of it twice over.

"But oaths are written by their makers. The old covenant was rewritten once, at the Sundering — badly, in haste, by frightened people. It can be rewritten again. By me. Tonight, if I choose." I step closer, and I let the dream warm around us as I move, because the warmth is the argument. "Swear the child to my Court. Not her memory. Not her self. Her *oath* — her service, freely given, binding her to the balance as the old Keepers were bound. In exchange: the Gate opens. Winter ends. And you walk out of this whole. Every memory. The ferryman's song returned to you, if you wish it — I hold debts in strange places, and old Ferris owes me a favor older than your valley. Your Corvess lives out his penance at your side. The Hollow opens, the elders grow old, and you grow *old with them*, Mara. Warm. Whole. Safe."

"And the child spends her life serving your Court."

"The child spends her life *keeping the covenant alive*, which is what she was sent back to do. I am offering you the same ending, with the bill paid by someone else." I let a beat pass. "You have noticed, of course. That is the shape of every bargain you've ever struck as a thief. Someone pays. The only question you have ever asked is whether the payer deserved it."

* * *

She is quiet for a long time. In dreams, silence has texture; hers is the silence of a woman doing arithmetic she hates.

Then she says: "Show me the catch."

"There isn't one."

"There's always one. I'm a thief. There's always one, and the good marks are the ones who've convinced themselves there isn't."

"Then show me yours," I say, "because I have been watching you for months, thief, and I cannot find yours either, and it troubles me. You want to say yes. Look at the room." I spread my hands at the hearth, the bread, the muffled laughter behind the door. "I built this from your own leaking dreams. This is not my fantasy. This is *yours*, rendered faithfully. Every mortal who ever stood where you stand wanted power or safety or love. You want a kitchen and a day with nothing in it. So say yes. Say yes and I will give it to you, and unlike every lord who ever made this offer, I will actually deliver, because I need the covenant sealed more than I need your pride broken."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you will go to the Gate, and you will surrender yourself piece by piece, and the woman who walks out of it — if a woman walks out of it — will be a stranger raising a child who used to be her, and you will have bought a spring with everything that made you worth the buying." I keep my voice level. Cruelty here would be redundant; the truth is crueler than anything I could add. "I am not threatening you, Mara. Threats are for the weak positions. I am telling you what happens. You have read enough minds to know I don't bluff."

She walks to the window.

I let her. This is the moment I built the whole house for, though she doesn't know it yet. She looks out at the gentle impossible snow, and beyond it — because I permit it, because I am generous in the way that wins wars — she sees the real world as it is tonight: the antechamber, the banked fire, Ilsa asleep sitting up with her dead staff across her knees, the empty boy curled around his vessel-silence, the child herself asleep with her hand open near the shard, and beyond them all, the black line of the Sundered Gate exhaling winter into a sky with no stars left.

"They're going to hate me," she says quietly, "if I take this."

"They'll never know."

"I'd know."

"Yes," I say softly. "You would. That is the tax on being what you are. It collects regardless of which road you take."

Behind the inner door, the child's voice falters mid-song. Stops. And then — and I confess I did not plan this, did not script it, the dream producing something true on its own the way dreams do — the door opens a crack, and a small face peers out at us both. The child's eyes find me first. They are not afraid. They are *old*, and they are sorry for me, and that sorry is worse than any hatred the mother could have aimed.

Then the child looks at Mara and says, in a voice like a small bell cracking: "Don't sign anything in a dream, Mommy. Nothing signed in a dream stays yours."

The house flickers.

Only for a heartbeat — the fire guttering, the bread going grey, the walls showing their seams like stage flats — but I feel it, the whole architecture shudder, because the child is right in a way I did not teach her and cannot undo: this place is woven from Mara's longing, and the child is Mara, and the child's grief at watching herself be sold is unraveling the thread the offer hangs from.

Mara turns from the window. And she smiles at me — tired, crooked, terrible — and says:

"You almost had me. Do you want to know the funny part? It wasn't the kitchen. It wasn't even the song. It's that you were *kind*, and I haven't been offered kindness by anything bigger than a campfire since my parents died." She holds up one finger. "But you made one mistake, Your Majesty. You said the child serves your Court. You didn't ask her."

"She's eight years old—"

"She's *me*," Mara says, "and I have never once in my life let anyone else decide what I'm worth. Ask her. Right now. In front of me. If she says yes — freely, knowing everything — I'll swear beside her, and you can have us both."

The dream goes very cold and very still.

Because here is what I did not tell her, what I will not tell her, what I fold away beneath the ice where even I rarely look: I cannot ask her. The child was sent back by a covenant not yet forged. Her consent belongs to a future that only exists if the Gate opens on *our* terms — and if I bind her now, outside the ritual, outside the turning stone, I do not gain an heir. I break the loop that brought her, and the child unravels from the timeline like a thread pulled from tapestry, and the shard goes dark, and the Gate stays shut, and every realm including mine dies in the long dark together.

Mara doesn't know that. She is waiting, arms folded, wearing the expression of a thief who has just found the flaw in a lock.

The child steps fully into the room, takes her mother's hand, and looks up at me.

"Well?" the child says. "Ask me."
