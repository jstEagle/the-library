## 26. Justice Outside the Law

The heralds sat where Ilsa's bindings held them, two men made of empty rooms.

That was the phrase that kept coming back to me. Empty rooms. I'd been inside their heads when I tore them open—had felt the architecture of them collapse—and now, three days later, I still couldn't shake the image. Not minds wiped clean like slate. Rooms with the furniture gone but the walls standing, the windows intact, dust settling in shafts of light that fell on nothing at all.

They didn't struggle. They ate what we gave them, slept when we told them to sleep, and watched us with the mild attention of people waiting for a cart that had been delayed. When I spoke to them they listened as if my words were weather. When I asked their names they tilted their heads, and one of them—the taller, the one whose memories I'd pulled first—said, "I think I had one."

"You think," I said.

"It seems like something a person would have." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse than if he'd wept. He said it the way you'd report a missing button. *It seems like something a person would have.*

Ilsa wouldn't look at me while we fed them. The boy did nothing else. He sat across from them for hours, silent as ever, studying them the way he studied maps—as if trying to learn a country he might someday have to cross himself. Once I caught him holding out his own bowl of barley to the shorter herald, and when I started to object, he looked up at me with those old-young eyes and I closed my mouth.

They were weapons I'd made. But the boy kept insisting, without words, that they were also people I'd wronged. And there was only one way I knew to find out which was true.

* * *

I waited until the camp was asleep. Then I took the shard out of its wrappings, sat down between the two hollowed men, and put my hands against their temples the way I'd done it before—only this time slowly, carefully, the way Corvess had taught me to take a single leaf off a branch instead of shaking the whole tree.

The tall one's mind opened like a door into fog.

What remained wasn't nothing. That was my first discovery, and it undid me a little. I'd assumed I'd left them blank. But memory isn't just the things you remember—it's the shape the remembering leaves behind, the grooves worn into a person by ten thousand small repetitions. And the grooves were still there.

He had been a soldier once—I could feel it in how his body held itself, in the discipline coiled in his spine even now, unowned by anyone. He had served the Winter Court for a long time. Decades. Maybe longer; the Court's servants didn't measure time the way the rest of us did. And beneath the emptiness, worn deep as a river cutting stone, there were habits of thought. Duties. A catechism.

I followed the grooves down.

And the Winter Court poured into me.

Not gently. The memories I'd left him were few, but they were load-bearing—the ones the Court made sure its heralds could never forget, the ones repeated at every dawn and carved into the ice of their barracks walls. They came into me all at once, cold and total, and for a long moment I was kneeling in a hall of blue ice reciting words I'd never learned:

*Three realms, one covenant. The covenant is order. Order is mercy. What was broken must be restored. What was restored must be obeyed.*

The catechism of the heralds. I'd heard fragments of it from Ilsa's stories, from the ferryman, from a hundred frightened mouths along the road. But hearing it secondhand was like hearing about drowning. Now I was under the water.

I saw the covenant itself—or the Court's version of it. Saw it rendered in the heralds' training as holy writ: the Oathstone raised above the world, three realms bound to it like hounds to a leash. Summer bound to yield at winter's word. Mortal realms bound to give up their magic, tithed away year by year, so that the Court would never again face a rival. Bound *permanently*. Bound so that no realm could ever rise or fall or change without permission.

Change. That was the sin. That was what the covenant existed to prevent. I felt it in the catechism's bones: the Court's great terror was not rebellion but alteration. A world that could change was a world where winter might one day end. So they built an oath to freeze everything—not just the seasons, but history itself. The covenant wasn't peace.

It was taxidermy.

I came out of the tall herald's mind gasping, my hands shaking, frost on my own breath though the night wasn't cold enough for it. The man watched me with his mild, ruined patience.

"Did you find what you wanted?" he asked.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly.

"Good," he said. "Neither do I."

* * *

I went into the short one next, because the tall one's grooves told me the catechism but not the man, and I needed both. If I'd made these rooms empty, I owed it to them to know what the rooms had held.

This one had been newer to the service. His training was shallower—but shallower meant less polished, and less polished meant I could see the seams. I could see the places where the catechism hadn't quite fit, where some part of him had chafed.

And in one of those seams I found a memory the Court had tried to scour out and failed. It was buried deep, wrapped in shame, hidden even from himself—but shame preserves things. Shame is the best preservative there is.

He remembered the archives at the Court's heart. He'd been low-ranked, a runner of messages, and he'd carried a sealed folio from one frozen lord to another, and because he was young and careless he'd read it.

It was a record. An account of the original forging of the covenant, written by the Court's own hand, meant for internal eyes. And it said—plainly, bureaucratically, in the flat language of ledgers—that the Oathkeepers had not shattered the covenant out of ambition or madness or treachery.

It said the Oathkeepers had refused to sign.

The covenant as first drafted had required the mortal realms to surrender their magic entirely. Not tithe it. *Surrender* it—a permanent transfer, the wells of the world handed over to the Court in perpetuity, in exchange for protection the mortal realms had never asked for. And the Oathkeepers—the nine who stood at the Sundered Gate with the shard in their hands—had read those terms and said no.

Said no, and been told that refusal meant war, and known they could not win a war, and so done the only thing left to people being forced to sign away everything they were.

They broke the Stone rather than bind themselves with it.

Ilsa's campfire story had it differently. In her telling, the Oathkeepers were traitors who shattered a sacred covenant out of pride, and the long winter was the wound their treachery left in the world. Every version I'd ever heard agreed on that much. Traitors. Breakers. The ones who doomed us all.

But here was the Court's own paper saying otherwise, filed away where no one would read it, because even the victors' histories need somewhere to bury the parts that don't rhyme.

The Oathkeepers weren't traitors.

They were rebels. Rebels who lost—and the punishment for losing wasn't death. It was this: a thousand years of winter, a dying world, a story rewritten so thoroughly that the victims raised their children to curse their own ancestors.

My parents among them. My blood among theirs. The heir of people who looked at an unjust oath and chose brokenness over bondage—and were then made into monsters so that no one would ever follow their example again.

I sat back on my heels in the snow, and the boy was suddenly beside me, awake when he should have been sleeping, watching my face. He couldn't ask. He never could. But he took my hand, and pressed it flat against the shard, and the shard warmed under my palm like something answering.

*I know,* I told it silently. *I finally know.*

* * *

I called the company together at dawn, which Ilsa said was a bad omen, councils at dawn meaning decisions too urgent to sleep on. She was right. I told them all of it—the catechism, the archive record, the truth underneath a thousand years of lies. I talked until my voice scraped, and then I stopped, and let the silence do its work.

The deserter was dead; the hedge-witch wrapped her knuckles around her staff; the boy with no memory frowned at the fire like it had lied to him.

"So the Gate," Ilsa said slowly, "doesn't want restoring. It wants—"

"Rewriting," I said. "If we reforge the old covenant, we forge the old injustice with new iron. We hand the world back in chains and call it salvation." I looked around the circle of faces. "The Oathkeepers broke the Stone because they wouldn't sign. The least I can do, carrying their blood and their shard, is not sign either."

The empty-eyed heralds sat at the edge of our fire, as they always did now, neither prisoner nor companion. The tall one spoke without raising his hand, in that flat, patient voice.

"The Court will not accept new terms."

"No," I said. "It won't."

"Then there will be war."

"There's already war," I said. "There's been a war for a thousand years. We've just been losing it politely."

The tall herald considered this with the same mild attention he gave everything. Then he said, "When I had a name, I believe I was proud of my service. I believe I thought the covenant was mercy." He looked into the fire. "I would like to know if I was right. You took the knowing. Perhaps you can tell me, someday, what I thought."

And I understood then what Ilsa had been circling for days without saying: justice outside the law doesn't mean vengeance, and it doesn't mean erasure. It means putting back what you took—even when what you took was taken in anger, even when what you took deserved taking. The heralds were evidence. They were witnesses. They were men whose whole selves I had confiscated in one terrible night, and if there was going to be a new covenant built on memory freely given, then it had to start with memories freely returned.

Even theirs.

Even here.

I looked at the shard, and the shard looked—there's no other word for it—back. Warm against my palm. Patient as a judge.

Beyond the firelight, past the ruins, the Sundered Gate exhaled its slow breath of winter, and I knew what I had to draft, and whom I'd have to face to sign it, and what it would cost me to hold the pen.

The Queen would come for us soon. This time, I intended to be ready with terms.

Terms she would hate.

Terms the world needed anyway.
