## 27. The Company Divided

The boy had no name of his own, so he borrowed other people's moods and wore them like coats.

He'd been doing it since before the Waystone, since before anything he could point to. There was a hole where a life should have been, and into the hole came whatever was nearest: Ilsa's stubbornness when she cooked, the hollowed heralds' terrible patience, the child's wordless certainty. He didn't mind the hole anymore. That was the thing none of them understood. You couldn't miss what you'd never had. You could only notice how much lighter you walked than everyone else.

Tonight everyone walked heavy.

They'd made camp in the lee of a fallen waymarker—a stone pillar older than any kingdom, its carved oaths worn to suggestions—and the argument started the way arguments did now, quietly, over food.

"We're nine days from the Gate," Tam said.

Tam wasn't one of theirs originally. She'd joined at the ash village three days back, a charcoal-burner's daughter with frostbitten fingertips and a brother who'd gone to fight the winter and never come home. She was practical in the way people were practical after they'd already lost the thing they'd been practical for.

"Nine days," she said again, "through country that's getting worse by the hour, to reach a door that won't open unless—" she stopped herself, but everyone finished the sentence anyway.

Unless Mara gave up everything she was.

The fire cracked. One of the hollowed heralds fed it a stick with the careful, empty tenderness they brought to every small task. The boy liked watching them. They were what he might have been, if he'd ever been anyone: vessels with nothing inside, still moving, still choosing to sit near warmth.

"Nobody's saying we abandon her," said Doss.

Doss was the fur-trapper who'd come in at the frozen river crossing, all beard and silence. He'd carried the boy on his shoulders through a whiteout once, and the boy hadn't forgotten that even though there was nothing underneath the not-forgetting. Doss had a wife in a valley two weeks east. Everyone knew it. Nobody mentioned it until now.

"But we're saying it out loud eventually," Doss said, "so here it is. I've got someone waiting on me. This mission is a coffin with extra steps. I'm leaving at first light."

* * *

Mara said nothing.

That was the worst part, the boy thought. She sat across the fire turning the shard's socket-empty hand—no, the shard itself, wrapped in wool against her chest—and she let them talk about leaving as if their leaving were weather. As if she'd already priced it in.

The child sat pressed against her side. The girl spoke plainly now, most days, in that old tongue and in the trade-speech both, but tonight she said nothing either. She just watched the fire the way she watched everything: like she'd seen it before and knew how it ended.

"Ilsa," Tam tried. "You tell her. Your magic's gone. You said it yourself—the staff is just wood now."

"It was just wood before, too," Ilsa said. "Magic was always mostly nerve." But her hands shook around the staff, and everyone saw it, and everyone pretended not to, because that was what company meant.

"You know what I think?" Doss leaned forward. Firelight got into the creases of his face. "I think this whole business is a story we've been telling ourselves so the walking has a shape. A gate. An oath. A woman who can pay the toll. It's a good story. Stories are how you don't freeze." He jabbed a thumb toward the dark beyond the firelight, where the frost stood in the trees like glass antlers. "But stories don't stop winter. Walls stop winter. Valleys stop winter. I'm going back to a valley."

"The Hollow," the boy said.

Everyone looked at him. He almost never spoke first. Words cost him more than they cost others—he had to borrow each one from somebody—but sometimes a sentence assembled itself in him whole, like it had been waiting behind his eyes for years he didn't have.

"The Hollow," he said again. "That's the plan, isn't it? Everybody goes back to a valley and waits for the ice to arrive politely. The Hollow kept magic out. Nothing keeps cold out. Cold isn't magic." He looked at Tam. "Your brother went to fight it. Where's your wall, then?"

Tam flinched like he'd struck her. He hadn't meant to. Or maybe he had. That was another thing about having no past—you couldn't tell whether you were kind or cruel. You just found out afterward, from faces.

"That's enough," Ilsa said softly.

"It's not," the boy said. "It's exactly enough. It's the first honest thing anyone's said all night except Doss."

* * *

Later, when the others slept or pretended to, the boy sat up with the shard.

Mara had left it unwrapped beside the fire while she took her turn walking the perimeter—one of her habits, checking the edges of things. The hollowed heralds dozed sitting up, which they always did, like statues that had agreed to dream. The child slept against Ilsa's side, breathing small and even.

The boy picked up the shard.

It didn't burn him. It never burned him. That was the secret he kept, the one thing he owned: the shard was warm in his hands the way nothing else had ever been warm. Not fire-warm. Remembered-warm. When he held it, the hole in him felt less like a wound and more like a doorway.

And sometimes—only sometimes—it showed him something.

Not visions like the child got, all gates and fire and falling winters. His were smaller. A kitchen. Hands kneading bread. Someone humming off-key. He didn't know whose memory it was. It might not be anybody's. It might be the shard dreaming, or it might be the closest thing to himself he'd ever find: a fragment with no owner, floating, waiting for a vessel.

He held it until the vision faded, then set it down gently where Mara had left it, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal.

"You feel it too."

Ilsa. She'd woken without waking, the way old campers did. She lowered herself onto the log beside him, staff across her knees.

"Feel what?"

"Don't do that." Her voice was kind. "Don't play empty. Empty is what you are; it's not what you do." She nodded at the shard. "It warms you. I've watched. It doesn't warm me anymore. My magic's gone, and the shard knows it. But you—" she tilted her head, studying him with those bright hedge-witch eyes, "—you it likes. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because you've got room." She tapped her own temple. "I'm full of a life. Cluttered attic. But you—there's nothing in there arguing with it. You're the quietest soul I've ever shared a fire with, and I once traveled with a Truce-mother who'd taken vows of silence." She smiled. "You could hold things, boy. Important things. Fragile things."

He looked at his hands. They seemed the same as always. Ordinary. Unclaimed.

"If I'm so useful," he said, "why does everyone want to leave?"

"Because usefulness isn't the same as survival, and they've got survival left to do. Doss has a wife. Tam has grief that needs somewhere to go. That's not cowardice. That's just being people." Ilsa poked the coals. "The question isn't why they want to leave. The question is why *you* want to stay. You've got nobody waiting anywhere. By rights you should be first out the door."

The boy thought about it honestly, because lying to Ilsa felt impossible, like lying to weather.

"Because I've got nothing to lose," he said. "And I've figured out that's not sad. It's a kind of freedom. Doss walks backward, always looking over his shoulder at what he's carrying. Tam walks sideways, dodging her grief. Mara walks forward but she's dragging a whole life behind her on a rope. Me—" he spread his hands, "—I walk light. And the Gate is forward. Everything forward. So I'll walk there."

* * *

At first light, Doss packed.

He did it quietly, competently, the way he did everything, and the packing was worse than shouting would have been. Each buckle was a sentence. Each strap cinched tight was an ending. Tam watched him from the dead fire, arms crossed, and the boy could see her deciding—her weight shifting from foot to foot like a scale.

"I'll take you as far as the crossroads," Doss said gruffly. "Both of you, if you're coming. Crossroads has a garrison. Garrison means walls, walls mean soup. Soup's not nothing."

"Soup," Tam repeated, tasting the word. Her eyes went to Mara, who stood at the edge of camp with the child, looking east toward a horizon the color of an old scar.

Then Mara turned around and came back into camp, and everyone stopped moving, because when Mara Nystrom decided to speak, the air rearranged itself around it.

"You should go," she said.

The boy blinked. So did everyone else. Of all the speeches they'd braced for—pleading, shaming, the arithmetic of sacrifice—that wasn't one of them.

"Doss, go to your wife. Tam, take your grief wherever it needs burying. I mean it. No oath binds you. No debt does. I've stolen enough memories to know the difference between loyalty and habit, and I won't spend yours." Her voice was steady, but the boy, connoisseur of borrowed feelings, caught the tremor under it. She meant it, and it cost her, and she paid anyway. "The Gate may not open. I may stand in front of it and fail. Anyone who stays signs on for watching that."

Silence. Somewhere in the frosted trees, something shifted—wind, or one of the Court's far patrols, or just the world holding its breath.

"And if it opens?" Tam asked quietly.

"If it opens, the winter breaks. Everywhere. Every valley. Including the one with soup in it." Mara almost smiled. "So go. And if you hear spring come early, you'll know why."

Doss stood there with his pack half-buckled, and the boy watched a man fight himself and lose slowly. "Damn you," Doss said finally, without heat, the way you'd curse a river for being wet. "Damn you, Mara. I spent all night talking myself into leaving."

"I know. I listened."

"So did I," said the child.

Everyone turned. She was standing at Mara's elbow, and her voice—clear, plain, that strange old accent worn thin with use—landed in the camp like a dropped stone. She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. She'd counted them listening, and now they were counted.

Tam laughed, a wet, surprised sound. "Well. Can't argue with the ghost girl."

Doss re-buckled the pack he'd just unbuckled, muttering. Ilsa hid a smile in her collar. The two hollowed heralds rose smoothly to their feet, because they always rose when the company moved, faithful as furniture.

Only the boy noticed Mara look down at the child with an expression he filed away carefully—wonder braided with sorrow, like someone greeting a guest they'd been expecting all their life and dreading.

* * *

They walked east, all seven of them, into the deepening white.

By midday the land had changed character entirely. This close to the Sundered Gate, winter wasn't weather anymore; it was architecture. Frost grew in deliberate shapes—arches, ribs, the suggestion of buttresses—as if the cold were building a cathedral around them as they approached. The boy walked at the front, because he was lightest and the crust of the snow held him best, and behind him he heard the company arguing again, but differently now. Logistically. Rations. Rope. Who slept first.

Loyalty, he'd learned, sounded like logistics when it made up its mind.

Near dusk they crested a ridge, and the boy stopped, and the company bunched up behind him, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Far below, in a bowl of shattered land, stood the ruins. Even at this distance he could see the Gate—or the wound where the Gate had been—a seam of wrong-colored light splitting the earth, exhaling plumes of glittering cold. And around it, between it and them, the snow lay flat and unbroken for a mile in every direction.

Unbroken, except for tracks.

Dozens of them. Fresh ones, cutting straight toward the ruins. White cloaks would hardly show at this distance, but the tracks told the story plainly enough: heralds, many, arriving ahead of them.

"They beat us here," Tam whispered.

"No," said the child, in her old tongue, and then in trade-speech, so no one could pretend they hadn't understood: "*We're expected.*"

Beside the boy, Ilsa's grip tightened on her useless wooden staff. And the boy put his hand in his pocket, closed it around the smooth river-stone he carried instead of a memory, and started down the slope, because forward was the only direction he had ever known how to walk.
