## 28. Stealing From Herself

The antechamber of the Sundered Gate had become a kind of home, which Mara found obscene. Frost ferned across the walls in patterns too regular to be natural—geometric, patient, like the frost was thinking—and the company had arranged their bedrolls around the shard's faint glow the way people once arranged themselves around fires. Ilsa's staff stood upright in a corner, its dead wood already growing a skin of ice. The boy slept nearest the shard, as he always did, curled around it like a dog around an ember.

Mara sat apart, cross-legged on bare stone, holding her own hand.

That was what it had come to. She had spent three nights turning the problem over and over: the Gate wanted everything. Not her life—her *memories*. All of them. And she had promised herself, in the stubborn dark of chapter twenty-three's refusal, that she would not pay.

But refusal, she was learning, was not the same thing as surrendering to winter. There was a third option hiding between those two, and she meant to find it or die trying.

She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, where a thief's trick lived—the same trick she used to pull memories from other minds, turned inward for the first time in her life. It felt obscene. It felt like reaching into your own chest to check whether your heart was still beating.

*One memory,* she told herself. *Just one. See if it can be lifted.*

She chose something small. The taste of the Hollow's black bread, dense and sour, the way the crust cracked under her teeth on cold mornings when Corvess set it before her without a word because words were never his currency.

She reached in. She gripped. She pulled—

—and stopped.

Not because it hurt. Because the moment her will closed around that memory, she understood something with terrible clarity: the bread was not just bread. It was Corvess's silence, which was love. It was the morning routine that made the Hollow feel like a place instead of a hiding spot. It was every cold morning she had ever woken up knowing someone expected her at the table. Lift the bread out, and what remained? A woman who woke up hungry and unloved and didn't know why.

She let go. The memory settled back into her like water finding its level.

*So,* she thought. *It's not a purse you can pick coin from. It's a wall. Pull one stone wrong and the whole thing shifts.*

But the Gate did not care about walls. And the winter outside did not care about walls either. Through the antechamber's broken archway she could see the storm that had swallowed the world—the full winter, total and early, the sky so thick with falling white that day and night had become rumors. Somewhere beyond it, the Queen waited. Somewhere inside it, hollowed heralds drifted like ghosts who had forgotten they were ghosts.

She tried again. This time she went fishing deliberately, moving through her own mind the way she moved through other people's, cataloguing. A thief's inventory of a soul.

*Corvess teaching me to pick a lock, age nine.* Heavy. Load-bearing. That one held up half the house of who she was.

*A market day in the Hollow when she was six, watching a juggler drop his pins in the snow while everyone laughed.* Light. Pretty. Weightless. She probed it, tested it, and found nothing underneath it. No Corvess. No mother. No fear. Just a juggler and laughter, a single bright bubble in the deep water of her.

She pulled it out.

It came free with almost no resistance—a soft internal click, like a lock yielding—and then it was simply *gone*. Not dimmed. Not blurred. Absent. She reached for it and found a smooth blank space where it had been, the mind's equivalent of a healed wound, and standing in that blankness she felt something she had never felt in all her years of thieving:

Vertigo.

This was what she did to other people. Every stolen memory she'd ever traded, every hollow-eyed client who wandered back to her stall with gaps in their lives they couldn't name—she had done this. Made this exact silence. She had known it intellectually the way you know fire is hot. Now she knew it in her teeth.

"All right," she said aloud, to no one. "Now you know."

"Mara?"

The child sat up in her blankets, eyes catching the shard's glow. She was nine years old now, or looked it—the timeline healing, the future fading off her like mist off a lake. But her eyes were still old. They had seen the world fail once from the inside.

"I'm practicing," Mara said. "Go back to sleep."

"Practicing what?"

"How to give away pieces of myself without giving away myself."

The child considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to everything, then crawled out of her blankets and came to sit across from Mara, knees drawn up, chin on them. For a long moment neither spoke. The frost on the walls ticked and grew.

"You found the difference," the child said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The difference?"

"Between what makes you *you*—" the child touched her own chest, mirroring Mara's gesture, "—and what you're only carrying."

Mara stared at her. Of course. The child had done this. In whatever failed future she'd come from, she had stood at this Gate and paid, or watched herself pay, or failed to pay. She knew the terrain.

"Tell me," Mara said quietly.

"It's not about big or small." The child's voice was thin and clear in the frozen air. "The juggler doesn't matter. You proved that. But the bread matters, and the bread is small too. So it isn't size." She frowned, working at it, a scholar of her own erasure. "It's... threads. Some memories are knots. Everything else is tied to them. Take a knot out and everything it holds starts coming loose. Other memories are just... loose thread. Cut them and nothing unravels."

"A knot and loose thread."

"Yes." The child nodded slowly. "In the future, I tried to keep everything. I fought the Gate memory by memory, refusing, hoarding, and the Gate wouldn't turn until I had nothing left to fight *with*. It took my knots last, and by then there wasn't enough of me left to be anyone. Don't do what I did." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "*Don't do what I did.*"

Mara reached across and took the child's hands. They were small and cold and they trembled.

"What I'm doing is different," Mara said. "I'm not fighting. I'm choosing. If I have to stand at that Gate, I want to walk there having already decided what I can live without. So the Gate gets weight, and I keep my knots."

"But if the Gate takes the knots anyway—"

"Then it takes them from a woman who chose how to arrive." She squeezed the small hands. "That's not the same death. Trust me. I've watched people die both ways."

The child searched her face for a long moment, and whatever she found there—or remembered finding there, in another life, in another body—made her nod once and crawl back to her blankets.

Mara did not sleep. She worked.

* * *

Dawn, if it could be called that, came gray through the storm-haze. The company stirred. Ilsa built a fire from the last of their wood and heated water for the barley tea that had become their entire diet. The deserter's absence still shaped their camp—a gap where a man should be, honored by nobody speaking of it. The two hollowed heralds sat against the far wall where they always sat, docile as furniture, occasionally tracking movement with eyes like frosted glass.

Mara worked through the morning while the others pretended not to watch.

She laid out her memories the way she'd once laid out stolen goods on a trader's cloth—inventory, appraisal, sorting. She did it with her eyes closed, hands resting on her knees, walking the corridors of herself with a thief's professional calm.

*Knots first. Name them so you know what's not for sale.*

Her mother singing. Knot. Untouchable—even though she'd already given one version of it to the ferryman, she'd taken it back, and she would not pay it twice.

Corvess setting down bread without a word. Knot.

Corvess's betrayal, his blood on the shard, the bargain glowing red behind her eyes. Knot—though a rotten one. It had made her. She kept it the way you keep a scar: proof of what survived.

The child at her door in the first snow, clutching light. Knot. The largest one she owned.

The moment at the Waystone when she'd emptied two heralds and understood what she was capable of. Knot. Horrible, load-bearing. The woman who forgot that might forget why the new covenant mattered.

*Now the rest.*

She went through decades. The Hollow's seasons. Faces of clients whose names she'd never learned. Every theft she'd ever committed, catalogued by victim and price. Here she slowed, because here was the strange territory: her crimes were heavy, but were they knots?

She tested one—a merchant whose joy she'd stripped to pay a debt, a man who'd smiled at her afterward with empty warmth. She pulled at it. It came away clean. Nothing unravelled. It was horrible, but it was *hers*-horrible, not *her*. She set it aside in the pile marked *Gate*, and felt lighter, and hated feeling lighter.

Other things surprised her. The memory of learning to read—she'd assumed knot—but pulling it changed nothing fundamental; the reading remained, only the learning gone, like a bridge dissolved after the crossing. Loose thread. To the Gate-pile. The taste of honey mead at some festival whose name she couldn't recall. Gone. The long gray years of small transactions in the Hollow, hundreds upon hundreds of them, each individually meaningless—those went in a great heap. Together they weighed more than any knot. Together they were most of her mass. And yet, probing them, she found she could surrender the heap and remain. A thinner woman. A stranger woman. But herself.

By midday she had three piles in her mind, clear as goods on a cloth:

*What I keep:* perhaps thirty memories. The knots. The bread, the song, the door, the snow, the child's hands in hers.

*What goes to the Gate:* the vast gray tonnage of an ordinary hard life. Years of weather and trade and hunger and small kindnesses from strangers she'd never see again.

*And a third pile she hadn't expected:* things she'd been carrying that were never hers at all. Stolen memories she'd absorbed so deeply she'd mistaken them for her own—a fisherman's terror of open water. A bride's wedding morning. A soldier's lullaby. These she could surrender with relief, like setting down bags she'd forgotten she was holding. She'd been a hoarder of selves, and half her weight had been other people.

When she opened her eyes, the whole company was watching her.

"You were somewhere else," the boy said. He had no memories of his own, and he studied hers the way a starving man studies bread. "Your face kept changing."

"That," Mara said, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

Ilsa lowered herself onto the stone beside her, joints cracking, and offered a cup of tea. "You mean to do it, then. Selectively."

"I mean to try."

"The Gate may not honor your curation." Ilsa's voice was gentle, which from Ilsa was worse than harsh. "Gates are not merchants. You don't haggle at the threshold of a god's wound."

"No," Mara agreed. "But the covenant is being *rewritten*. I read it in the herald-minds. The old law took everything because the old law didn't trust the payer. The new law can be precise. We're drafting it, Ilsa—you and me and a nine-year-old with a dead language in her mouth. Precision is the whole point."

"And if precision fails?"

Mara drank her tea. It was bitter and lukewarm and the best thing she'd tasted in days, because she had decided, deliberately, to make it a knot. *This cup. This cold morning. This friend.* She tied it fast.

"Then it fails," she said, "with my fingerprints all over it."

* * *

She practiced for three days.

Small surrenders, carefully chosen, each one an experiment. She gave the Gate-pile its first true installment—a decade of gray Hollow winters, weather and waiting, surrendered in a single act of will that left her gasping and weeping without knowing why, because she'd also surrendered, she realized too late, the memory of her father teaching her to skate, which had been tangled in those winters like a root in soil. A knot she hadn't mapped. She sat on the cold stone afterward, grieving a man whose face she could no longer picture, and added a new rule to her craft: *test every thread for roots before cutting.*

The company began, without discussion, to bring her their memories. Not to take—to *give*. The hedge-witch described her daughter's face so vividly that Mara dreamed it. The boy narrated his one possession, a fragment of song in a language he didn't speak, over and over until Mara had it by heart. Even the hollowed heralds contributed—one of them, unprompted, began reciting patrol routes and Court protocols in a dead monotone, intelligence Mara banked against the negotiation to come.

On the third evening, the child brought her something else.

A map. Drawn in charcoal on the back of Ilsa's spare cloak, showing the antechamber, the socket, the half-sealed Gate—and beyond it, sketched from instinct, the interior. The mechanism. Channels carved into the stone like veins, all converging on the socket where the shard would sit.

"The channels aren't empty," the child said, tapping them. "Look. Look closely."

Mara leaned in. And saw it: within each carved channel, faint as breath on glass, ran lines of old script. Oathkeeper cant. The Gate was inscribed with terms.

"It's the contract," Mara breathed. "The original terms. Written into the stone itself."

"We can read them," the child said. Her old eyes were shining. "We can *amend* them."

Mara looked from the map to the child to the storm screaming beyond the archway, and felt something she had not felt since before the Waystone: hope with an edge on it. Not the passive hope of survival. The active hope of a thief studying a lock she finally, finally understood.

Tomorrow they would draft the amendment. Tomorrow she would walk out onto the ice and put her terms to a queen.

Tonight, she took out her three piles one last time, and counted her knots like a miser counting gold, and kept watch over the only fortune she had ever refused to steal.

Thirty memories. One soul. Terms pending.

Outside, the winter waited—and beneath the frost-ferns on the eastern wall, unseen by any of them, a hairline crack had begun to spread, and through it, faint as a dying pulse, something on the other side was listening.
