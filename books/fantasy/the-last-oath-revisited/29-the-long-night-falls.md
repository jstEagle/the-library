## 29. The Long Night Falls

The winter came all at once, the way a debt comes when you've ignored it too long.

Ilsa felt it arrive before she saw it. She was on watch at the antechamber's mouth, wrapped in every scrap of wool she owned, when her magic — that guttering candle she'd been nursing for three hundred miles — went out.

Not dimmed. Not faltered. *Out.* The way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick between wet fingers.

She stood there in the sudden silence of her own skull, in the cold that rushed in where the warmth had always been, and she understood what it meant. Every hedge-witch knew the signs, though none of them had lived to describe them: when the land's magic died entirely, the borrowed kind died with it. What was left was what you'd been born with, and Ilsa had been born with nothing but stubbornness and a talent for herbs.

"Isa?" The boy's voice, from behind her. Tam. He never slept much anymore. None of them did, really, but he least of all — an empty vessel keeps strange hours, like a jar that knows it's meant for holding something.

"Winter's here," she said. "All of it."

He came to stand beside her, small shoulders against hers, and together they watched it come down the valley toward the Gate.

It didn't fall like snow falls. It *advanced*. A white wall moving up the frozen riverbed, swallowing the black pines one by one, each tree going from dark to pale with a soft rushing sound like a held breath released. Behind the wall, the sky had gone the color of old milk. Ahead of it, the last of the dusk was dying red, as if the day were bleeding out.

"The frost line crossed the Sere this morning," Ilsa said. "Ferryman told me it took two days to cross in his grandfather's time. It took two hours today."

Tam said nothing. He rarely did about things that mattered; he saved his words like coins, and spent them only when the purchase was worth it.

"We should get inside," Ilsa said. "All of us. This isn't weather anymore. It's an army."

* * *

They made their camp in the Gate's antechamber because there was nowhere else and because the shard wanted them there.

Mara had explained it once, in her clipped way, pacing the length of the great half-sealed arch while the child sat cross-legged beneath it with her eyes closed and her palms flat against the stone. The Gate exhaled winter through its seams, yes — but around its threshold, the air itself was *thicker*, dense with the old covenant's residue, and the frost came slower there. Slower. Not stopped. Nothing stopped it now.

So they'd built their fire in the antechamber's heart, ringed with stones pried from a fallen pillar, and hung their tarps across the archway's broken flanks, and settled into the long wait the way soldiers settle into a siege: badly, and then, out of necessity, well.

There were seven of them now. Mara. The child. Tam. The deserter's ghost, which is to say the memory of him, which is to say the hole he'd left that no one talked about but everyone stepped around. The two hollowed heralds, who sat by the far wall like furniture that occasionally breathed, and who had begun, in the past week, to mimic small human behaviors — tucking blankets, feeding the fire — in a way that Ilsa found more heartbreaking than any grief.

And Ilsa herself, who had followed a memory-thief and a mute child across half the dying world because her own magic was failing and she'd wanted, desperately, to die somewhere that mattered.

She hadn't told anyone that last part. But standing in the firelight, watching her hands shake not from cold but from the absence that lived in her now where her art had been, she thought: *soon I'll have to tell them.*

* * *

The frost came inward through the night.

Ilsa tracked it the way she'd once tracked weather, by feel rather than sight, marking its progress along the floor in her mind. By midnight it had taken the outer pillars, sheathing them in white so thick they looked carved from ice instead of stone. By the second watch it had reached the ring of hearth-stones, and the fire fought back, roaring up tall and desperate, throwing shadows that danced like things alive.

Mara didn't sleep at all. She sat beside the child, who slept fitfully against the Gate's base, and turned the shard over in her fingers, and sometimes pressed it briefly to her lips like a woman kissing a letter she couldn't read.

"You should rest," Ilsa told her, during the third watch.

"I'll rest after."

"After what?"

Mara looked at her then, and Ilsa saw something in her face she'd never seen there before — not fear exactly, and not resolve either, but some third thing that lived in the territory between them, a country whose name she didn't know yet.

"I've been practicing," Mara said quietly. "Letting go."

Ilsa waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of what hedge-craft actually was.

"There are memories I can give up easy," Mara went on. "Faces of people I cheated. The taste of bad ale. Three days in a ditch outside Vellum that I wouldn't wish on anyone." A ghost of a smile. "But then there are others. Corvess teaching me letters. My mother singing. The first time the child laughed — really laughed, not just that silent thing she does." Her hand closed around the shard until the knuckles went white. "How do I know which ones are me?"

"You don't," Ilsa said. "That's the point. Nobody gets to keep themselves whole. We're all just... edited. Life edits us. Grief edits us. You're just getting to do the editing yourself, ahead of schedule." She paused. "Doesn't make it less terrifying. Makes it more honest, maybe."

Mara huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "You sound like Corvess."

"He sounds like me. I'm older."

Outside, the wind rose to a scream and fell again, and the frost crept another foot up the inner wall, and neither of them moved.

* * *

Near dawn — if it could be called dawn, under a sky gone featureless white — Ilsa's body finally betrayed her.

She'd known it was coming for weeks, the way you know a roof beam is cracked before it falls. Her craft had been leaving her by increments: first the weather-sense, then the finding-charms, then the little warmth-spells that had kept the company's toes intact through the worst of Ash Road. What remained was a trickle. And now, kneeling by the fire to feed it, she reached for even that trickle and found nothing. Stone walls where the water had been. A dry well.

She knelt there a long moment, hands in the coals' warmth, feeling the shape of the absence.

Three hundred miles. Through warlord country and ambush and ambush's aftermath. Past the Sere with its ferryman's terrible price. All of it powered by a candle she'd known was burning down, because she'd wanted to spend her last light on something that mattered, and here, at the end of it, at the foot of the Sundered Gate itself, she'd arrived. She'd *arrived*. Whatever happened next — whatever Mara chose, whatever the Queen demanded, whatever the covenant asked — Ilsa Voss, hedge-witch of the Marches, would be present for it. Would be *part* of it.

Not many failing women got to choose their ending. She intended to choose well.

"Mara," she said.

Mara looked up.

"Come here. Both of you." She nodded at the child, who was already awake, already watching with those old, old eyes. "Both of you. Now, please."

They came. And Ilsa rose, joints cracking, knees protesting, and walked to the corner where her pack lay, and drew out the staff.

It was ash wood, older than she was, older than her teacher, older maybe than the Marches themselves. Its head was crowned with a forked prong of antler, worn smooth by a century of gripping hands. It had been her teacher's before it was hers, and her teacher's teacher's before that, passed down the thin and unbroken line of hedge-witches stretching back to the first women who'd learned to bargain with the land instead of commanding it.

It was not a weapon. It was not a relic of power, not in the way bards meant. It was a *focus* — a thing that gave shape to strength, that turned a trickle into a stream, a stream into a river. In the hands of someone with power.

In her hands, for these past months, it had been mostly a walking stick.

"I can't hold it anymore," Ilsa said simply. "What's left of my craft is gone. Went out tonight, on watch, like a snuffed lamp. What I'm carrying now is habit and arthritis." She held the staff out across both palms, the way you offered tribute, the way you offered surrender. "Take it."

Mara didn't move. "Ilsa—"

"It's not charity. It's logistics." Ilsa kept her voice level, practical, the voice she used for setting bones and delivering babies and telling families hard truths. "The ritual will need a focus. Your hands will be full — shard, oath, sacrifice, God knows what else. The Gate won't care about your good intentions; it cares about *conduct*, about the shape of the working. A conductor needs a baton." She shook the staff gently. "This is a very good baton. Two hundred years of good witches poured their habits into this wood. It knows how to carry a working. It doesn't care whose working."

"I can't take your—"

"*Listen to me.*" The words cracked out of her harder than she'd meant, and the child flinched, and Ilsa softened immediately, dropping to one knee so she was eye-level with the girl, keeping the staff extended toward Mara all the while. "Child. You know what I am. Tell her."

The child studied Ilsa for a long moment. Then she spoke — that dead tongue first, a phrase low and resonant, and then, haltingly, in trade-common: "*The vessel matters less than the pouring.*"

"There," Ilsa said. "See? The kid gets it." She pushed the staff into Mara's reluctant grip and closed the younger woman's fingers around it with her own gnarled ones, sealing the gift. "Two hundred years of hedge-witches, love. We were never mighty. We were never court wizards or realm-shakers. We were the ones who showed up. Flood, fever, famine, birth, death — we showed up, we did what we could with what we had, and we passed the stick along when our arms got tired." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she let it. "My arms are tired. Take the stick."

Mara's eyes were bright and furious. "You're not dying. Don't you dare talk like you're dying."

"Oh, eventually I am, same as everyone. That's not what this is." Ilsa patted her cheek, once, briskly. "This is arithmetic. You need every advantage at that Gate. I need—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I need to know the showing-up counted for something. Let it count, Mara. Let me count."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind clawing at the tarps and the frost crackling up the walls, another inch, another inch, patient as taxes.

Then Mara bowed her head over the staff, the way you'd bow over a fallen friend, and said, in the formal cadence of a vow she must have stolen from someone's mind somewhere: "I'll carry it well."

"I know," Ilsa said. "You steal everything else well enough. Might as well carry things properly too."

And the child — the child did something Ilsa hadn't seen her do since the road began. She leaned forward and pressed her small forehead against Ilsa's knuckles, and held it there, and murmured something in the old tongue that Ilsa's dead craft couldn't translate but her living heart understood anyway.

*Thank you,* probably. Or *I remember you.* Either way, Ilsa wept a little, quietly, without shame, and let the frost creep closer, and did not look away from it.

* * *

By full morning the antechamber had shrunk to a circle thirty feet across.

The frost had taken everything else — the pillars, the arch-flanks, the tarps' outer edges stiffened to boards. Their world was firelight and breath-steam and the slow crystalline whisper of ice advancing across stone, and beyond that, through the antechamber's open mouth, a vista of total white: the valley filled to the brim with winter, no trees, no track, no horizon, just the pale void pressing in like a tide against a seawall.

The hollowed heralds had drawn nearer to the fire without anyone asking them to. They sat now among the living rather than apart, and one of them — the taller one, the one Mara sometimes called Rell, though whether that was its true name no one knew — was carefully, clumsily mending the tear in Tam's coat with thread and needle held in fingers that moved like a puppet learning tenderness.

Ilsa watched it work and felt her chest ache. *Accountability without absolution,* Mara had said of them once. But watching Rell sew, Ilsa thought it was simpler than that, and sadder: even the emptied things remembered how to care for something. It was the last thing to go.

Tam came and sat beside her as the light failed — failed early, the way all light failed now, the sun a rumor behind the milk-white ceiling of the sky.

"Your staff," he said.

"Gave it away. Best use it ever got put to."

He considered this. The boy-with-no-memory, weighing the concept of legacy with the seriousness he brought to everything. "If I had a stick," he said at last, "I'd want it given to someone doing something important."

"That's the whole trick of it, love. Nobody knows if a thing's important until afterward. You just show up, and you pass the stick, and you hope."

He nodded slowly, filing it away in whatever vast empty rooms he kept behind his eyes. Then, unexpectedly, he leaned his head against her shoulder — a gesture no one had taught him, a gesture he must have reconstructed from scratch out of observation and want — and stayed there.

Ilsa put her arm around him and looked out at the coming dark.

The frost had reached the ring of hearthstones now. The fire was fighting harder, burning through fuel faster, and their woodpile — dragged in over days from the dead forest — would last, by her estimate, four days. Five if they burned cold and huddled close. After that, the circle would shrink. Ten feet. Five. One.

Unless Mara went to the Gate first. Unless the ritual came sooner than the cold did.

Which, Ilsa understood suddenly, sitting there in the shrinking warm circle of the world with a motherless boy asleep against her side, was precisely the calculation the Winter Court had made. They weren't besieging the Gate. They were besieging *time*. Every hour of frost was a knife at Mara's throat: act now, act imperfectly, act afraid. Or freeze with your grand covenant unspoken.

"Clever," Ilsa murmured to the white void beyond the firelight. "Cruel and clever. Whoever taught the Queen statecraft taught her well."

"She's frightened."

Ilsa startled. The child stood beside her — when had she risen? — small and grave in the firelight, looking out at the void with eyes that had seen the future fail once already.

"What?"

"The Queen." The girl's trade-common was better every week, but she still spoke it like someone translating from a deeper language, word by word, meaning by meaning. "Winter is starving. The frost isn't cruelty. It's hunger. It eats because it is empty." She turned those ancient eyes on Ilsa. "Like the heralds. Like Tam was. Empty things take. Full things can give."

"And which are we?"

The child smiled — the rare, real smile, the one that transformed her face into something heartbreakingly ordinary, a little girl's smile, a daughter's smile.

"We are the fullest things left," she said. "That's why we came."

* * *

That night — the second night of the total winter, the fourth of their siege — the wind changed.

Ilsa heard it first, because hearing was the one sense her lost craft had left sharpened beyond ordinary. Under the scream of the storm there was a new note, low and rhythmic, pulsing from beyond the valley's mouth like a heartbeat made of pressure. The fire flattened. The frost on the walls glittered, all at once, as if a million tiny lamps had been lit inside it.

Mara was on her feet with the staff before Ilsa could speak, shard in her other hand, the child pressed close behind her. The hollowed heralds rose too, heads tilting in unison toward the sound, and Rell made a noise — the first noise Ilsa had ever heard one of them make voluntarily — a thin, keening note of recognition and dread.

"What is it?" Tam demanded, awake now, on his feet. "What's out there?"

The pulsing grew. Closer. The white void at the valley's mouth began to glow, faintly, from within — a cold blue radiance spreading through the storm like ink through water, and in the heart of it, a shape. Tall. Crowned. Moving with the unhurried grace of something that has never once needed to hurry.

Ilsa's dead craft stirred — one last reflexive shudder, a goose rising on the skin of the world — and her mouth went dry.

"That's no herald," she whispered.

"No," Mara said. Her knuckles were white on the ash staff, two hundred years of hedge-witch legacy gripped in a thief's fist, and her voice, when it came, was steady as a level. "Heralds knock."

The blue light swelled. The frost at the antechamber's edge bloomed outward into patterns — intricate, deliberate, *written* — spiraling glyphs of ice crawling across the stones toward the fire, toward the shard, toward the child.

"This," Mara said, lifting her chin as the Winter Queen stepped out of the storm, "is a declaration."

The temperature plummeted. The fire crouched low. And the Queen of Winter looked upon the little circle of the living — the witch, the boy, the thief, the child — and smiled like a glacier calving.

"I have come," she said, in a voice like snowfall on a coffin lid, "to discuss terms."

*Ilsa gripped Tam's shoulder and thought, wildly, absurdly:* Showed up. At least we showed up.
