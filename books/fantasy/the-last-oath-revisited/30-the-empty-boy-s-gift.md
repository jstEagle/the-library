## 30. The Empty Boy's Gift

I don't know how old I am.

That's not a complaint. It's just a fact about me, like the fact that I'm left-handed, like the fact that I like the crust of bread more than the middle. Somewhere behind my eyes there used to be a boy with a name and a mother and a birthday and a favorite color, and whatever happened to him happened so completely that not even dust remains. I checked once, lying awake in the dark of some roadside barn, going through myself the way you go through pockets. Name: nothing. Face before the road: nothing. First word: nothing.

What I have instead is this: I remember everything that's happened *since* I woke up empty. Every mile, every meal, every fire Ilsa built and every argument the deserter — Tam, his name was Tam, and I carry his name now because nobody else does — ever picked with the hedge-witch about the right way to salt venison. I hold all of it. All of it fits.

That's the thing about being empty. Things fit.

I figured it out three nights ago, though I didn't understand what I'd figured out until tonight.

* * *

We're camped in the Gate's antechamber now, which is a fancy word for a throat. The Sundered Gate stands at the far end of a hall carved by nobody human — the walls curve wrong, the way the inside of a shell is wrong, and the stone has a faint blue pulse to it like something breathing very slowly under a blanket of snow. Frost has come through the wound in the world and claimed everything. Outside, the winter is total now. No dawn worth the name. The villages we passed on the ash road are burning their doors and their pews and finally their floors, and the smoke rises straight up into a sky the color of an unwashed window.

Inside the antechamber it's cold but not killing. The shard sits in its socket in the Gate and glows like a held breath, and around it we have made the smallest possible version of home: Ilsa's bedroll, the cook pot, the heap of gear that used to belong to more people than are still here.

Fewer people than started. That's the arithmetic of this journey. We began as two, then five, then four.

Mara sits by the shard most nights with her eyes closed, doing her practice. That's what she calls it. *Practice.* She's trying to learn how to give pieces of herself away without giving all of herself away, because the Gate wants everything and she's determined to pay in installments if the installments can work. She takes a memory — she says them out loud sometimes, so we know what she's spending — and she presses it toward the stone, and usually the stone refuses it, the way a locked door refuses a shoulder. Or worse, it takes it and gives back nothing, and Mara comes out of the trance looking like someone has read one page out of her and torn it out.

Last night it was the memory of a dog. A brown dog, from her childhood in the Hollow, that used to sleep against her back for warmth. She gave it to the Gate and the Gate took it and she sat afterward for a long time with her hand flat on the floor, and when I asked her if she was all right she said, "I can't remember why I put my hand there," and then she laughed in a way that wasn't laughing.

The child — the other Mara, the small one, the one who talks in that old tongue when she forgets herself — put her arms around the big one and didn't let go for an hour.

So that was the situation, as of three nights ago. The Gate only accepts whole surrender. Installments fail. And Mara Nystrom was preparing, with the terrible tidiness of someone who has already decided, to walk up to that door and give it every single thing she is.

I couldn't stop thinking about the dog.

* * *

Here's how I found out what I am.

Three nights ago I couldn't sleep. The frost sings in these walls at night — a high thin note like a wet finger on glass — and it gets into the gap where my past should be and rattles around. So I got up to sit near the coals, and Mara was awake too, sitting with the practice-memory in her hands. Not the dog. Something smaller. She does that now, keeps a few memories loose in her grip the way a gambler keeps a coin, ready to spend.

"Can't sleep either?" she said.

"No."

She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with her body. "Ask your question, boy. You've been circling it for days."

I hadn't known I was circling anything, but she's usually right about what's inside other people, so I trusted her over myself. "When you take a memory out of someone," I said, "where does it go?"

Her head came up. Just slightly. Like a bird hearing a worm under snow.

"It goes wherever I put it," she said slowly. "Into me, usually. Into the shard, sometimes. Why?"

"Could you put it somewhere else?"

"Where? A rock? Rocks don't want anything."

"I do," I said. "Or — I don't *want*, exactly. But there's room."

And I swear the temperature of the conversation changed. Ilsa woke up — she sleeps lightly now, since her magic died; she says silence is louder than noise ever was — and the child woke, and even the two hollowed heralds standing their silent post by the door turned their empty heads a degree, like weather vanes.

"You're suggesting," Mara said, "that I could put a memory in *you*."

"You said it yourself, weeks ago. On the road, when I asked why you never steal from me." I remembered it perfectly, because remembering things is the one skill I have honed to a blade. "You said, 'There's nothing in you to push against. Taking from you would be like pouring water into a well.' I've been thinking about wells."

"That was a figure of speech."

"So test it."

She stared at me for a long time. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do since Tam died: she smiled, quick and crooked, the smile of a thief who has just spotted a lock she likes.

"All right," she said. "Hold still. If this hurts, tell me."

It didn't hurt. It felt like — you know the moment at dusk when you can't tell whether a light has been turned on or whether your eyes adjusted? It was like that, but inside. Warmth arrived in a place that had never been warm because it had never been anywhere at all.

"There," Mara whispered. Her hand hovered an inch from my forehead. "Oh. Oh, it's *sitting* there. It's not dissolving, it's not bleeding into you — it's just — stored. Like a jar on a shelf." She laughed, unsteady. "Boy, do you understand what you just did?"

"No," I said honestly.

"The Gate won't accept partial payment because it has nowhere to *put* a memory except itself, and itself only takes everything. But you—" She grabbed my shoulders. Her eyes were wet and fierce. "You're not a well. You're a *vault*. I can hand you the things I can't afford to lose, walk into that door, and walk back out and take them up again."

The child made a sound. Small, sharp, half a word in the dead tongue, and everyone looked at her, and she pressed both hands over her mouth, and I thought she was going to cry, and I didn't yet understand why that would be.

Because here is what the rest of them understood and I didn't: Mara had been planning to die. Not with knives, not with frost — worse. She had been planning to be *unwritten*, to feed the Gate the whole loaf of herself, and the woman who came out the other side, if any woman came out at all, would be a stranger wearing her face. They had all watched her arrive at that decision one quiet night at a time, and none of them had found the words to stop her, because the Gate's price was real and somebody had to pay it.

And then the boy with no past had walked over and said, *there's room in me.*

* * *

Tonight we tested it properly.

Ilsa insisted on ceremony, because Ilsa believes that anything done twice should be done the same way both times — "that's half of what magic *is*, dear, the other half is stubbornness" — so we arranged ourselves in a ring around the coals: Mara, the child, Ilsa with her dead staff across her knees like a sleeping animal, the hollowed heralds at the edges, motionless and patient as furniture.

Mara chose carefully. She held out three memories, described them aloud the way she always does now, so we could witness the ledger:

"One: the smell of bread baking in the Hollow on festival mornings. Two: the way Corvess's voice sounded when he told me bedtime stories and pretended he wasn't soft. Three: the taste of the river Sere's water, the day we crossed, when I was happy despite everything."

"Why those?" Ilsa asked.

"Because they're load-bearing," Mara said. "They hold up other rooms. Lose the bread and I lose festivals. Lose Corvess's voice and I lose the only good years I had with him. Lose the river and I lose the proof that I was capable of happiness on this road. These aren't decorations. If the Gate takes everything, these are the beams."

"And if they live in the boy instead," Ilsa said, "the beams survive."

"If they live in the boy," Mara said, "then when I walk through that door and give it everything else — the hoard of stolen memories, the life I lived, the name, the guilt, the whole architecture — there'll be a core left over. A seed. Enough that the woman who wakes up might still be able to become *me* again, instead of starting from zero." She looked at me across the fire. "It depends entirely on whether he can hold them. And whether he can give them back."

"How do we test the giving back?" I asked.

Mara reached over and took my hand, palm up, and I felt the three warm jars settle into me — bread-smell, a low rough storytelling voice, cold clean water — and then she closed her eyes and *pulled*, gently, the way you draw thread through cloth.

They came back. All three. I felt the shelves empty and felt her take the jars into herself, and she gasped like a woman surfacing, and tears ran down her face and she was laughing.

"Bread," she said wonderingly. "Corvess's stupid beautiful voice. Cold river water. They're mine. They came *back*." She gripped my hand hard. "Do you know what this means? It means I don't have to choose between saving the world and ceasing to exist. It means there's a third door."

The child spoke then — plain speech, not the old tongue, which she saves for important things. Her voice was thin.

"He has to know what it costs him."

Everyone went quiet.

"What do you mean?" I asked.

She knelt in front of me. She has her mother's — has *Mara's* — exact grey eyes, and when she looks at you straight on it's like being read by a book. "Memories change what holds them," she said. "Even stored ones. Even sealed ones. While they're in you, they'll seep. You'll dream their dreams. You'll grieve their griefs. You'll wake up some morning missing a dog you never owned." She swallowed. "And when you give them back, you won't return to being empty. Empty was a kind of peace. You'll remember what it was like to be full, and then you'll be empty *knowing what you lost*, and that's a different country entirely."

The fire popped. One of the hollowed heralds shifted its weight, which they almost never do, and I wondered — not for the first time — whether there was still someone in there watching us, and what it thought of all this.

"I've been empty since before I met any of you," I said. "I don't actually know that empty was peaceful. I think maybe it was just quiet, and quiet isn't the same thing." I shrugged. "Besides. Everyone here has been carrying too much since the day I found you. Tam carried his desertion. Ilsa carries her dying craft. Mara carries the entire world's bill. Somebody around here ought to get to travel light." I looked at my own hands, ordinary hands. "Turns out traveling light is a skill. Turns out I'm the best in the company at it. Let me be useful at the one thing I'm actually good at."

Mara made a sound I'd never heard from her — something between a sob and a laugh, strangled at birth — and pulled me into a hug that cracked a rib I'm fairly sure was already cracked.

"You're not 'the least of us,'" she said into my hair. "Stop saying that. You're the keystone. Do you know what a keystone is? It's the stone at the top of an arch that looks the smallest and does the most work. Take it out and everything falls."

"I don't know what an arch is," I said into her shoulder, and she laughed for real, wet and helpless, and Ilsa laughed too, and even the child smiled, and for about ten seconds the antechamber at the end of the world was the warmest place in any realm.

* * *

Later — deep in the singing-frost hours, when the others slept — I lay on my bedroll taking inventory.

Bread-smell. Corvess's voice. River water. Three jars on shelves in the dark warehouse of me, glowing faintly, patient. I turned each one over the way you'd turn a lamp over in your hands, careful not to spill.

And I understood something the others hadn't said out loud, because they were being kind: this plan has a hole in it. The Gate demands *everything*. Willing, total, unconditional. What if it counts the seed? What if it reaches past my shelves and takes the jars anyway, or takes Mara harder for trying to smuggle? What if holding her selfhood makes *me* the target — the Court's faction that moves the hollowed heralds like gloves must have felt the shard's warmth pooling in an empty vessel, the same way a drowning man feels a plank?

Maybe. Probably. None of which changes the answer.

I've been trying to remember, lately, whether I was brave before. Whether the boy I used to be would have volunteered for this. I can't check the records; the records burned. But I know this much: whoever I was, he's beyond help now, and whoever I am next gets built out of what happens tomorrow and after. If I'm going to be somebody, I'd rather be somebody who held the door open with his own two hands.

Toward dawn, the frost-song in the walls changed key. Lower. The child sat bolt upright out of a dead sleep, and the shard in the socket flared white, and far off across the ice — out where the world ends and the Queen's country begins — a light answered. Not hostile. Not friendly. *Formal.* The way torches look along a road when royalty is coming and you'd best decide quickly whose side of the road to stand on.

Mara was on her feet with her knife drawn before her eyes finished opening.

"They know," the child breathed. "She knows what you're planning. Mother — *Mara* — the Queen is coming to make you an offer."

Mara looked down at me, and at the jars sleeping in me, and something settled in her face like a blade sliding home.

"Then she'd better bring good terms," she said, and started wrapping her feet in wool for the ice.

I put my hand flat on my own chest, over the shelves, and kept watch over somebody's whole life until the sky got less dark.
