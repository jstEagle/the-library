## 31. Negotiation With Winter

The ice held her weight the way a held breath does—patient, and only for so long.

Mara walked out alone. That had been the whole of the argument with Ilsa, who had wanted to come, and with the boy, who hadn't argued at all but had simply stood at the edge of the frost-line holding the shard against his chest like a second heart until she pried it from his hands.

"You can't give them this," she'd told him.

"They gave you theirs," he said. He meant the herald-minds. The two hollowed men who followed their company like dogs that had forgotten they were wolves—they still carried everything she'd pulled out of them, packed away in Mara's own skull now, but the *shape* of it lived in them too, the way an empty house still remembers its furniture. "They know how the Court thinks. You're the only one who can speak it back."

She'd kissed his forehead, which surprised them both, and left him with Ilsa at the antechamber door.

Now there was nothing under her boots but winter's own country, three miles of black glass stretching toward a horizon where the aurora came down and touched the ground like something drinking. The cold here wasn't weather. It was attention. It pressed on her from every direction at once, the way a crowd presses when it wants to see what you'll do.

She stopped walking at what felt like the middle of nowhere and began, quietly, to steal.

Not memories. Not yet. She reached instead into the bundle she already carried—the two minds she'd emptied at the Waystone—and laid them open before her like a merchant laying out cloth. Heralds' memories were strange goods. Centuries compressed into seasons, service rendered as instinct rather than thought. She sorted through them with fingers made of will:

The Queen's court: a hall of standing ice where petitioners froze mid-sentence if they spoke too long. Her heralds addressed her by no name at all, because names implied equals. The Queen had not been *asked* for anything in four hundred years.

The Court's law: absolute, simple, and starving. Take warmth from the other realms or die. There was no third clause. No mercy clause. The covenant's collapse hadn't freed Winter; it had locked Winter in a room with its own hunger and thrown away the key.

And one more thing, small and buried deep, wrapped in so much protocol that even the herald who carried it barely understood it was there:

The Queen remembered the old oath being sworn. Not as victor. As *victim*.

Mara closed the memories. Breathed. Somewhere behind her, small and warm, the Gate exhaled its endless winter-breath, and somewhere ahead of her—

The aurora moved.

It didn't brighten. It *condensed*, folding down out of the sky the way water finds a drain, gathering itself over a quarter mile of empty ice into a figure of white and green fire. It did not walk toward her. Distance simply stopped mattering, and then the Queen stood ten paces away, and the cold between them became a wall with a door in it, and the door was open, and entering it would cost exactly as much as the Queen decided.

"Mara Nystrom," the Queen said. Her voice was the sound a glacier makes at night, far off, deciding to move. "Heir of traitors. Thief of minds. Mother of yourself." A pause that might have been amusement. "You are the strangest thing my realm has produced in an age."

"I'm not yours."

"Everything that endures my winter is mine. Everything that flees it is mine. Everything that begs me to stop is mine most of all." The Queen tilted her head, and beneath the fire of her Mara glimpsed a face—or the memory of a face, worn smooth as river stone by centuries of being looked at. "You have come to beg. Good. I have waited four hundred years for someone worth begging to me."

"I've come to trade."

Silence. Even the wind, which had been moaning across the ice since she stepped onto it, went quiet, as though Winter itself leaned closer to hear.

"A thief," the Queen said slowly, "walks onto my ice, carrying stolen knowledge of my own court, and proposes to *trade* with me."

"You're the one who taught us all how it works," Mara said. "Everything has a price. Warmth. Memory. Oaths. Your heralds live by it. So do I." She lifted her chin. "I know what you want. You want the heir's willing oath. Mine. Sworn to the Winter Court, binding me and the child and whatever comes after us to your service. In exchange you let the world thaw."

"In exchange I let your people *survive*. Do not mistake my restraint for generosity. I could take the Gate by force. I could take you by force. I am speaking with you because—" the hesitation was microscopic, a crack in glacier-face, "—because the last time I took something by force, I lost everything."

"The covenant."

"The covenant," the Queen agreed, and something in her voice shifted, dropped a register, became almost—almost—a voice instead of a weapon. "Your parents' order shattered it rather than let any realm rule, and they were right to shatter it, and I have spent four centuries cursing them for it. Because the oath bound all three realms, and when it broke, the binding broke *both ways*. My realm cannot feed without the others' warmth. Yours cannot remember without ours' stillness. And the third—" she smiled, and it was the saddest expression Mara had ever seen on anything that powerful, "—the third realm has been dying so long that even I no longer remember its name."

Mara kept her breathing steady, but her mind was racing ahead, laying the trap she'd come to lay.

"Then we want the same thing."

"No. You want spring. I want *life*. They are not the same bargain."

"They could be." Mara took a step forward. The cold-wall rippled but held. "Here's my offer. Not surrender. Not service. A new covenant—one written fresh, by both sides, with terms each realm can live inside. Mutual obligation. You give warmth back into circulation; the world thaws; your realm gets fed the way it was always meant to be fed, through *exchange*, not extraction. In return—"

"In return?"

"In return, the other realms remember you. Honestly. The Hollow forgets nothing—that's its curse and its nature. Let it hold the true history of the Winter Court. Not the monster story. The *whole* story. What was done to you. What you lost. What you were before the hunger."

The Queen went very still.

For a long moment there was no wind at all, no aurora, no sound except the creak of ancient ice settling beneath their feet, and Mara understood she had touched something rawer than any threat she could have made.

"To be remembered," the Queen said finally, and her voice had gone quiet in a way that frightened Mara more than shouting would have, "is to be *known*. To be known is to be *bound*. Your kind learned that lesson at my expense once already, thief. Why would I hand you the knife again?"

"Because the alternative is another four hundred years alone in the dark, eating scraps, growing crueler, waiting for the last warm thing in the world to freeze so you can finally rest." Mara held her ground. "I'm not offering to bind you. I'm offering to *witness* you. There's a difference. Ask your heralds—they used to know it, before your court forgot the words."

Something moved behind the Queen's eyes. Something old, and tired, and terribly hungry—not for warmth this time, but for the thing warmth was always standing in for.

"And if I refuse?" the Queen asked softly.

"Then I walk back to the Gate, and I turn the shard anyway, and we find out together whether the covenant can be reforged without you." Mara met her gaze. "Maybe it can't. Maybe we all freeze. But maybe—*maybe*—the world learns to live without a queen, and your realm starves in the dark while everyone else figures out how to share. You'd be trading certainty for a gamble either way, Majesty. The only question is whether you gamble alone or with us."

The silence stretched.

Somewhere far behind her, Mara heard Ilsa's voice faintly on the wind—calling her back? Warning her? She couldn't tell, and she didn't turn around.

"You speak," the Queen said at last, "like someone who has already lost everything and decided to make it mean something."

"I learned from watching my friends."

Another long pause. Then, slowly, the Queen extended one hand—not clawed, not grasping, just *open*, palm up, the way you offer something fragile to something else that might also be fragile.

"Not an oath tonight," the Queen said. "Not yet. But terms. Drafted properly. Witnessed. In the old tongue, so neither of us can hide behind ambiguity." Her fingers curled slightly, inviting. "Come. Walk with me to the edge of my hall, heir of traitors, and let us see whether a thief and a god can write a law together."

Mara looked at the offered hand. Looked past it, at the dark horizon where the third realm's name had been forgotten. Looked back at the tiny light of the antechamber fire, where a boy with no memory and a woman with a failing staff and a child who was herself were keeping watch over a shard of everything she was about to risk.

She took the hand.

The cold closed around her like a closing book—but gently, the way a page turns when the reader is careful, and the Queen drew her forward into the green-dark heart of winter, where the first terms of the new world were about to be drafted in a language older than kings, and where, Mara realized with a lurch she couldn't quite swallow,

she had left the shard behind her in a boy's arms,

and the boy was already walking out onto the ice to follow her.
