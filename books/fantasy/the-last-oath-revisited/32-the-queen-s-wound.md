## 32. The Queen's Wound

I have not been asked a question in four hundred years.

The heralds ask nothing. The frost asks nothing. Even my courtiers, when they still had tongues worth listening to, learned early that questions were a form of theft—each one taking something from the answerer that could never be returned. So I made asking illegal in my realm, and then I made it unnecessary, and then I forgot what it felt like to be on the other side of one.

And now this woman stands on my ice with her hands open and her eyes wet and asks me: *What does winter want?*

Not *what do you want.* She is careful. She has stolen enough minds to know the difference between a queen and her crown.

*What does winter want.*

I should kill her. I have killed people for less. A boy once skated too far onto my lake and his laughter cracked the silence of my courtyard, and I let the ice take him rather than hear it again—not out of cruelty, but because laughter was a currency my realm no longer possessed, and I could not afford to be reminded. That is the arithmetic I have lived by for four centuries. Everything costs. Everything is scarce. And scarcity makes monsters of us all, or so I told myself until the telling wore smooth as river stone and I believed it without remembering I had ever chosen to believe it at all.

She stands there waiting. Mara Nystrom. Thief of memories. Last heir of the Oathkeepers. The child beside her breathes white plumes into air cold enough to crack teeth, and behind them both, at the edge of the ice, stand the hollowed ones—my own heralds, emptied by this woman's desperate hands, following her now like dogs who have forgotten whose pack they belonged to.

They are an accusation. She brought them deliberately. I can see that. She wears my failures like jewelry.

"What does winter want," she asks again, and this time it is not quite a question. It is an opening. A door held ajar.

I want to say: winter wants nothing. Winter is want itself, want given form and season and teeth. Winter wants everything and therefore cannot be said to want any particular thing, which is the same as saying it wants nothing, which is a lie I have maintained since before her grandmother's grandmother learned to walk.

Instead I say—and I am surprised to hear my own voice, which sounds like wind through a dead forest, which sounds like nothing I remember choosing—"Winter wants spring."

The words hang between us. Somewhere beneath our feet, three hundred feet down through blue ice older than kingdoms, something shifts. Not an earthquake. A settling. As if the world itself has been holding its breath and, hearing those words spoken aloud after four hundred years, finally lets a little of it go.

"Then why do you make more of it?" Mara says. Her voice shakes. Good. Fear is honest. "Why do you send your heralds hunting? Why did you freeze the Sere? Why are you killing villages?"

"Because I am dying." I say it flatly, the way one reports weather. "Because my realm starves, thief, and starving things eat."

* * *

Let me tell you what the covenant was, from the side that history does not record.

There were three realms once. You know this—Ilsa with her hedge-craft has told the campfire version, and campfire versions always make someone the villain, and in every version she has ever heard, I am the villain. Fine. I have had four hundred years to become comfortable in the role. It fits like an old boot.

Three realms: the Green, where things grow; the Grey, where things are made and thought; and the White, where things rest. Sleep. Endure. My realm. We were not death—we were the pause between notes, the fallow field, the long dark that lets a seed believe in morning. And the covenant bound us in balance: each realm giving what it had in surplus, receiving what it lacked. The Green gave growth. The Grey gave craft and memory and law. And the White—

The White gave rest. Stillness. The sacred pause.

Do you know what we received in return?

Nothing.

Oh, the old tongue is precise about these things, and I have read the original text more times than any living being—the Oathkeepers carved their copies and hid them and preened over them, but I *was there*. I signed it. My name is the first name on the stone, and I can tell you exactly what it promised me: obligation. Perpetual obligation. The Green would grow and grow and grow, season upon season without end, and the White would receive the overflow of its exhaustion—dead leaves, spent soil, tired beasts—to hold and quiet and keep. Forever.

It was framed as honor. It was structured as servitude.

And the realms called it peace, because from the outside, peace looks identical to order, and order looks identical to justice, if you squint hard enough and live far enough away from the machinery.

For an age, I kept the terms. I held the world's weariness and gave back only stillness, and the Green grew fat and endless summer stretched across its face, and the other realms flourished, and no one asked what the White received, because the White receiving anything would have required the Green to stop growing, and stopping was unthinkable, and so the question went unasked for a thousand years.

Until the Oathkeepers asked it.

You think they shattered the Stone out of principle. Out of rebellion against coercion. Your hedge-witch tells it that way around fires, and there is truth in it—the covenant WAS coercion; I will grant the dead their vindication. But here is what your histories omit: they did not shatter it to free all three realms equally.

They shattered it to free TWO of them.

They rewrote the terms in secret—they who were sworn custodians of the balance—and the new covenant, the one that held for an age before it broke, gave the Green and the Grey their autonomy and left the White bound to the old terms forever. Rest without end. Service without reciprocity. They kept me frozen—forgive the word—in perpetual obligation while cutting themselves loose from theirs.

I was the price of their freedom.

And when I refused—when I finally, after an age of service, reached across the boundary and took what I was owed, reaching into the Green to claim some fraction of the warmth and motion that had been extracted from my realm for a thousand years—they called it invasion. They called ME the tyrant. The Queen who would not stop, the Winter that devours.

So yes. The Oathstone broke. Yes, winter spilled. Yes, the long cold has killed and killed and killed, and every corpse is mine to carry, and I do not pretend otherwise. But understand what I am, thief: I am not a conqueror. I am a creditor. Four centuries of defaulted debt, and interest compounding, and the whole world calling me monster for attempting collection.

That is what winter wants. What winter has ALWAYS wanted. To be paid.

* * *

Mara listens. That is the thing that undoes me—not her defiance, not her shard, not the child at her side with my great-grandmother's eyes (yes, I know what the girl is; the covenant's reforging echoes backward through time like a bell rung in a well; I have felt her arrival in my bones for years, a second heartbeat in the architecture of the oath)—but her listening.

Heralds do not listen. Courts do not listen. Kings and queens and councils and elders—none of them listen, because listening obligates response, and response obligates change, and change is the one currency every power hoards against spending.

But this woman, this memory-thief who has taken more minds than my winter has taken villages, stands on my ice in the howling wind and LISTENS, and when I finish speaking she is silent for a long moment, and then she says:

"The ferryman."

I blink. Frost crackles along my jaw. "What?"

"The ferryman on the Sere. He charged me a memory of joy to cross. I paid it. I've been carrying that loss like a wound ever since—and you're telling me you've been carrying four hundred years of it." Her breath comes ragged. "You're not asking for conquest. You're asking for the crossing fee. Someone to pay the toll that lets winter end."

"No one has ever offered to pay it," I say, and I am horrified to discover my voice has changed—it has gone thin, reedy, almost young. When did I last sound young? "No one has EVER offered. The Green sends heroes with swords. The Grey sends scholars with treaties. Both come to take, to bind, to fix me in place again under new words that mean the old words. You come asking what I WANT."

"And what do you want?" she presses. "Not winter. You."

The wind stops.

I did not stop it. I would know—I have commanded this wind for four hundred years and it obeys like a limb obeys; I feel it as extension of self. But just now it stopped on its own, the way a room goes quiet when someone speaks an unexpected truth, and in the sudden stillness I hear—for the first time in centuries—the sound of my own realm breathing.

Because it IS breathing. Did you know that? Beneath the ice, beneath the snow, beneath the permafrost that has crept year by year into lands that were never mine—my realm inhales and exhales, slow as geology, and with every exhale it grows weaker. The covenant broken means no inflow. No warmth flowing in from the Green to be rested and returned. No dreams from the Grey to be quieted and sent home. Just expenditure without income. A kingdom spending down its principal.

I am not conquering the world, Mara Nystrom. I am liquidating it. Every village frozen, every river sealed, every harvest killed—that is me, selling off the furniture of reality to keep my own realm's heart beating one more decade. Cruelty as bookkeeping. Monstrosity as budget management. I have done monstrous arithmetic so long I forgot it was arithmetic at all and started believing it was simply my nature.

"You want it to end," Mara says quietly. "Winter. You want it to be over."

"I want to REST." The word tears out of me like something impacted in a wound for centuries finally lanced. "Truly rest—not the rest of obligation, not holding the world's weariness in perpetuity, but actual cessation, renewal, the pause that ENDS and gives way to the next thing. I have not ended a single thing in four hundred years. Nothing in my realm concludes. Snow falls and falls and falls and none of it finishes falling. Do you understand what that IS, thief? It is hell. It is hell built out of my own nature, turned against me, and I have been its prisoner longer than your bloodline has existed."

The child steps forward.

Small. Silent. But she walks to me across the ice with the absolute certainty of someone approaching a mirror, and she reaches up—she is so small she must reach UP—and touches my frozen hand.

Warmth.

A thread of it, tiny, ridiculous, a candle-flame against my glacier—but real, and MINE, freely given, not extracted, not owed, not coerced. The first unforced gift my realm has received since the Stone shattered.

I look down at her, and she looks up at me, and in her eyes I see it: the future she came from. The one where I won. Where I ground the last of the Green into powder and the last of the Grey into silence and stood triumphant over a universe of ice with nothing left to rule, because triumph and emptiness are the same destination when you travel far enough.

In her future, I got everything I demanded and discovered I had never wanted any of it.

"You've seen it," I whisper. "You've seen what victory looks like."

"She doesn't have to say it," Mara says. "I can see it in your face. You already knew."

"Knew?" The laugh that comes out of me cracks a floe half a mile away. "Thief, I have KNOWN for two centuries. Why do you think the heralds' orders grow more hesitant each decade? Why do you think I negotiate at all, when I could simply bury you? Because some part of me has been praying for someone to defeat me. Properly. On terms. With a treaty instead of a tomb."

I kneel. A queen does not kneel. I kneel.

On the ice, in front of a memory-thief and a child and the hollowed remnants of my own honor guard, the Queen of Winter lowers herself until my eyes are level with Mara Nystrom's, and I speak the words I have not spoken since the Stone first cracked:

"I am willing to treat. Not to surrender—winter does not surrender, and you do not actually want it to, because a world without winter is a world without rest, and a world without rest becomes the Green's endless grinding summer, and THAT tyranny was the original sin. But to treat. Realm to realm. Creditor to debtor. Wound to wound."

"Wound to wound," she repeats.

"You heard me. You think you are the only one who has lost things? I have lost EVERYTHING, continuously, for four hundred years. The difference between us, Mara Nystrom, is that you steal memories to survive, and I devour futures to survive, and we are both so very tired."

Her eyes fill. She does not let the tears fall—I suspect she has not permitted herself that luxury in years—but they gather, and in them I see my own reflection, small and cracked and suddenly, horribly visible.

"The Gate," she manages. "The Sundered Gate. It needs a willing sacrifice—an Oathkeeper's whole remembered life. That's the toll. That's the crossing fee."

"Then the terms are simple." I rise. The wind returns, but gentler now, as if even it has been listening. "Reforge the covenant with three signatures, not two. Write winter INTO the law instead of leaving it outside the walls. Give my realm inflow and outflow, give and return, and I will give you what no Oathkeeper ever obtained."

"What?"

"My name." I extend my hand, palm up, the gesture of supplication inverted into offering. "My true name, from before I was Queen. It is the last piece of the original Stone still unbroken—it has been hidden inside me since the shattering, which is why the Gate will not turn for the shard alone. It needs the name of the one who was wronged. The Gate requires the signature of ALL THREE realms, thief, and mine was never in the shard, because the Oathkeepers never dared carve it. They feared me too much to bind me honestly."

Mara stares at my open hand. Behind her, the hedge-witch has gone pale as my realm. The empty-eyed boy clutches his staff. The child—my historian, my echo, my witness from a future that must now never happen—smiles.

"It was never a lock," Mara breathes. "The Gate. It was never a lock we had to force. It was a door waiting for the third key."

"Four hundred years," I say, "and everyone kept trying to break it down."

* * *

We walk back toward the Gate together.

Not as allies—do not mistake me, trust between a creditor and a debtor is a fragile architecture and neither of us is naive. But as signatories. As parties to a negotiation that should have happened a thousand years ago and did not because everyone involved preferred comfortable injustice to uncomfortable honesty.

The hollowed heralds fall in behind us. My heralds. Following the thief who emptied them. I should be furious. Instead I find myself studying them, walking, breathing, docile—and wondering, for the first time, whether emptiness might not also be a kind of rest. Whether I might learn something from the ones my court failed.

At the edge of the ice, where my domain meets the ash-road and the ruined world beyond, Mara stops and turns to me.

"The others won't believe you," she says. "Ilsa will draw steel. The deserter's ghost—his memory—still rides with us. They have lost too much to your winter to kneel to its queen."

"They don't need to kneel," I tell her. "They need to witness. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire point of the new covenant. No more kneeling. No more thrones above the law. Signatures, all of us, or the Gate stays shut and we all freeze together in our separate hells."

She nods slowly. Then, hesitating:

"When the Gate turns... the ritual takes everything. All my memories. All of me, in a sense. The child says the boy can hold some of it, carry it back to me after—but imperfectly. Gaps. I'll wake up a stranger to myself."

"Yes."

"And you'll be there. At the sealing. Watching me pay the toll your freedom requires."

"I will."

"Then do one thing for me." Her voice drops, roughens. "When I forget—if I forget everything—remember FOR me. Hold the memory of this conversation. Of what you told me on the ice. Someone should know what was true, even if I never do again."

I consider the request. A queen does not hold another's memories; that is theft by another name.

But I am learning, in my final century, that the old laws were written by the frightened, and that the frightened were often wrong.

"I will remember it," I promise. "All of it. Until you can reclaim it—or until the world ends, whichever comes first. That is my first payment on the new covenant: witness. Freely given."

She exhales—a long shaking breath, the sound of someone setting down a weight carried alone for miles—and together, queen and thief and child, we turn toward the Sundered Gate and the antechamber where the company waits, unaware that everything they fear and everything they hope for has just changed shape on the ice.

Behind us, for the first time in four hundred years, snow begins to melt.

Just a little. Just at the edges.

But the heralds see it. And word runs ahead of us through my dying realm faster than any horse: the Queen has met the thief, the Queen has knelt, the Queen is coming HOME—

—and somewhere in the deep places of the White, something ancient stirs in response, something older than the covenant, older than the realms, something that has waited since before the first winter fell.

It is not pleased that the door may soon open.

It is not pleased at all.
