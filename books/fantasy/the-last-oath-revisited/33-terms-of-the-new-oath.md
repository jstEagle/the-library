## 33. Terms of the New Oath

The antechamber of the Sundered Gate had become a war room, though no one called it that. We'd pushed Ilsa's old table—dragged from some ruined farmhouse two valleys back—to the center of the space, and across its scarred surface we spread everything we had: the shard on a square of grey cloth, my stolen herald-memories laid out like ledgers, and scraps of vellum covered in the child's careful, looping script.

She wrote the way she did everything now—deliberately, with the patience of someone who had already watched a world end once and refused to be hurried by this one.

"Again," I said. "From the top."

She looked up at me. Her face was mine—I still hadn't gotten used to that, the way her jaw set when she was concentrating, the small crease between her brows—and for a moment neither of us spoke. Then she dipped her reed into the inkpot and began to translate.

*"Veth-kara sul. The first term."* Her voice was clear and strange, the dead tongue moving through it like water through a channel cut long ago. *"Each realm shall hold in its keeping the true memory of the others. Not stories. Not histories written by victors. Memory. What the frost does to the fields. What the fire does to the forest. What the drowning does to the coast. Each realm will know what its survival costs the others, and it will know it always, and it will not be permitted to forget."*

I listened with my eyes closed, the way I listened to memories when I stole them. It was a trick of the trade—you didn't take a thing until you'd felt its whole weight.

"And if they refuse?" I asked. "If the Queen signs and then simply... stops remembering? Lets it fade, the way everyone lets things fade?"

The child set down her reed. "That's the second term," she said—in my language now, plain and flat. "Forgetting has to *cost*. That's what the old covenant got wrong. It bound them with fear. Fear fades. We bind them with memory, and memory has to hurt to hold."

"Or people will find ways not to hold it."

"Then they break the oath," she said simply. "And the Gate opens again, and winter comes back, and everyone remembers why the terms were written this way." She almost smiled. "You taught me that. Rule one of the trade: never steal from children. Rule two: a debt nobody feels isn't a debt."

"I never taught you rule two."

"You taught me rule two," she said, "the day you paid the ferryman."

I looked away first. Outside, through the antechamber's broken arch, the ice plains ran white to the horizon, and somewhere out there the Winter Queen was waiting for my answer—or hers, or both of ours; at this point I honestly couldn't have told you whose answer mattered more.

* * *

We worked through the night. Not because we had to—the Queen had given us until the next full dark—but because every hour the terms sat unfinished was an hour the cold crept further up the walls, and because writing a covenant turns out to feel exactly like stealing a memory in reverse. You hold the whole shape of it in your mind, all its weight and warmth, and then you give it away and hope whoever receives it carries it faithfully.

The third term was mine.

I wrote it myself, in my own hand, stumbling through the old tongue with the child whispering corrections. *Veth-sul-aran.* The clause of witness. Every realm would send keepers—not wardens, not soldiers, *keepers*—who would live among the other realms and carry their memories home. Oathkeepers, but new ones. Ones who understood what the first order had learned too late: that a covenant is only as honest as the people who can see both sides of it.

"They'll hate it," Ilsa said from her corner, where she sat wrapped in every blanket we owned, her staff across her knees like a sleeping animal. "Realms don't like being watched."

"It's not watching," I said. "It's remembering. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"The first Oathkeepers were rebels before they were traitors," the boy said quietly. He was sitting closest to the shard—it liked him, or he liked it; hard to say which way the current ran. "Maybe these ones get to just be keepers."

"That's the idea," I said.

"And if it fails?" Ilsa again. She always asked the question nobody wanted asked. "If in three hundred years some keeper decides the terms are unjust, and shatters it again?"

I thought about Corvess. About the Gate taking him gently, about his last memory given freely—his hand on my hair, the smell of ink and pipe smoke, *there's my girl*—and how even that hadn't been enough to turn the stone, because love without the blood-right is only love.

"If it fails," I said, "then someone will stand where I'm standing and write better terms than mine. That's not failure. That's inheritance working properly."

The child looked at me for a long moment across the candlelight. Then she picked up her reed and added something to the margin of the vellum, small, in the old script. I didn't ask what it said. Some things you're not meant to read until later.

* * *

Near dawn—the grey, lightless dawn that full winter gives you—we took the draft to the Queen.

Not all of us. The hollowed heralds stood at the edge of the ice like sentries who'd forgotten whose side they were on, which was more or less what they were. The boy stayed behind with Ilsa. The child came with me, because the Queen had asked for her by name, and because I was done leaving her anywhere out of my sight.

The ice field outside the Gate was vast and blue-white and utterly silent. At its center, on a throne of frozen spray, sat the Winter Queen.

Up close she was less terrible than I'd expected and more tired. Her crown was ice, yes, but ice gone thin as old glass, shot through with cracks she'd stopped pretending not to notice. When she saw the child, something moved behind her face—recognition, maybe, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a ruler who has just realized her enemy has brought a better lawyer.

"You've written terms," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way cold carries.

"We've written terms." I held out the vellum. "In your tongue and ours. Read them before you refuse them."

She read. I watched her face while she did, and I'll tell you what I saw there, because I spent twenty years reading faces for a living and I have never seen anything plainer: I saw a starving woman being offered bread and trying to work out whether it was poisoned.

"Memory as law," she said at last. "You want my realm to *feel* the burning of forests. Forever."

"I want your realm to feel what its survival costs. Yes. And mine to feel what the frost costs yours. That's the whole trick of it. Nobody starves quietly ever again."

"And if we forget anyway?" A flicker of the old cruelty. "Memory rots, thief. Even bound memory."

"Then forgetting breaks the oath," the child said, stepping forward. "The Gate opens. Winter returns. And every realm remembers, all at once, exactly what they lost." She paused. "You told my— you told Mara that your cruelty was scarcity. Fine. These terms make scarcity loud. Loud things get answered."

The Queen was quiet for a long time. Wind moved across the ice and made no sound at all.

"There is a clause missing," she said finally.

My stomach dropped. "Name it."

"The realms remember each other. Good. But who remembers the covenant itself?" She rose from the throne, and the cracks in her crown caught the dead light. "Your first Oathkeepers bound the worlds and then hid in a valley and let the words rot. I will not sign a law whose keeping depends on the virtue of the descendants of cowards."

The child turned to me. And I understood, in that moment, what the margin note had been—what she'd written in the old tongue while I slept, the clause she'd been carrying since before either of us knew we'd need it.

"She says," the child translated, and her voice shook only a little, "*the keeper of the covenant shall be the one who paid for it.*"

I stared at her. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning," said the Winter Queen, and here her voice went soft, almost gentle, which was somehow worse, "that the terms are sound, thief. But laws need a living heart. Someone bound to the stone itself. Someone who gave everything and therefore cannot abandon it." She smiled thinly. "Someone, in short, exactly like you."

The wind came down off the glacier then, and it sounded—for the first time in all the long winter—almost like laughter.

The child took my hand. Her grip was small and fierce and shaking.

"She means," she whispered, "they want you to stay."
