## 37. What Remains of Mara

I watched her do it. That is the strangest sentence I will ever be able to say, and I am not sure I will be able to say it for long.

The Gate's antechamber was quiet in the way that a held breath is quiet. Frost had climbed the walls in ferns and feathers, and beyond the half-sealed arch the winter exhaled its long white breath into the world, but inside—inside there was only us, and the shard, and her hands.

Mara Nystrom knelt before the socket where the Oathstone had lived when the world was whole. The boy knelt beside her with his hands cupped like a bowl, because that was what he had offered to be: a vessel. Ilsa stood behind them both with her dead staff planted on the stone like a flag on conquered ground. The hollowed heralds stood at the edges of the chamber, empty-eyed and swaying slightly, like reeds, like people waiting for someone to tell them what they were.

And I stood where I always stood, one step behind her, close enough to catch her if she fell.

She began with the stolen memories. Of course she did. She had told me once—I remember this; I am not sure how much longer I will—that a thief pays her debts first. She had carried other people's lives inside her since she was younger than I am now, and every one of them had been taken from someone who grieved its absence. She opened herself the way she opened locks, gently, professionally, without flinching, and the memories left her in a slow bright stream that the boy caught and held.

I could see them leaving. That is the part no one warns you about. When a memory leaves a person, it does not vanish; it passes through the air between giver and keeper like heat off a stone, and you can feel its shape. A soldier's mother's kitchen. A ferryman's daughter laughing on a rope swing. A herald's whole childhood in a court of ice, before the Court hollowed him out the first time. They went out of her one by one, and each one made her lighter, and lighter is not the same as happier. She grew translucent somehow—not her body, but something behind her body, the thing that made her *her*. With every memory surrendered, more light came through.

She did not cry. I kept waiting for her to cry. She had cried at the ferryman's crossing, giving up the song. She cried small and hard and privately then, one tear, quickly mastered. But now, at the end, with everything going, she was calm. Calmer than I have ever been. I think—I thought, then—that it was because she had already done the crying. In the nights before, walking the ice alone to treat with the Queen, standing in the dark drafting terms in a language older than kings, she had wept for all of it in advance. She had paid her grief early so that at the moment of payment itself she would have steady hands.

That sounds like her. That sounded like her. I am writing these words down because I already know they are the kind of words that go.

* * *

Then came her own.

She started at the beginning and worked forward, which meant she ended at the beginning too, because time runs backward when you surrender it. First the road: Ash Road, the frozen river Sere, Vellum's archive towers, the Waystone ambush where she emptied two heralds' minds and then had to live with what she'd made of them. I felt those leave her and I felt the guilt leave with them, and I wanted to shout at her to keep the guilt, keep that one, guilt is the proof you knew better—but the Gate does not negotiate. It takes what is given. She had chosen to give everything, and everything does not mean almost everything.

Then the Hollow. Her room above the shop with the crooked shutter. Her rule—never steal from children—the rule that had saved me, though neither of us understood that yet, the night I arrived on her threshold holding a shard of the covenant and speaking a language older than any kingdom. The first snowfall. The elders demanding I be given to the snow. Her refusal, born of an instinct she couldn't name, an instinct that was me, calling to her across years that hadn't happened yet.

Corvess went next. His betrayal, his bargain at the valley's rim, the child traded for her safety—and then his return, his penance, his last lesson, the way the Gate had unmade him gently and he had given his final memory freely: raising her. Teaching her letters. Feeding her when she was small and furious and fatherless. She gave up his love and his shame together, and I saw her hesitate over that one, just for a breath, just long enough for the frost on the walls to creep a finger's width closer. Then she let it go anyway. Because keeping it would have been theft, and she was done stealing.

Her mother's singing went near the end. The single recollection of joy she had paid to the ferryman, bought back years later from his estate—one theft she permitted herself, one warm thing hoarded against the cold. She had sworn she would keep exactly that one forever. She broke her own promise at the socket of the Sundered Gate, and she did it without apology, because a promise made by the woman she had been was not binding on the woman the world needed her to become. I heard the melody pass through the air between her and the boy. I heard it whole. I hold it now. I don't know the words—I never learned them; she sang them to me only twice, and both times I was too busy watching her face to listen—but I know the shape of it, the rise and fall, the way it ends on a note that hangs instead of landing. If I ever find my voice again after this, that is the first thing I will learn to carry.

And then the last things. The last things were me.

She gave up the night I arrived. She gave up the shard burning cold in her hand and answering her. She gave up the visions—the broken gate, the falling winter, the woman with her own face standing in fire. She gave up learning who I was. She gave up the revelation, the devastating tenderness of it, the moment I finally spoke plainly and told her I was her, sent back through a reforged covenant from a future where she failed. She gave up her refusal, the honest selfishness of it, the three days she spent refusing to be unwritten, examining her own fear in the light and finding it human and finding it insufficient. She gave up the negotiation on the ice, the Queen's wound, the terms of the new oath written in her own hand. She gave up choosing this.

That was the last one. Do you understand? The very last memory she surrendered was the memory of deciding to surrender everything. She walked up to the line where Mara Nystrom ended and stepped over it carrying nothing, not even the knowledge of why she was walking. The Gate required it. You cannot pay with your whole self and keep the receipt.

The shard turned.

It turned slowly, grudgingly, the way an old lock turns after decades of rust, and the sound it made was not a sound but a permission—a deep click that ran through the stone floor and out into the roots of the mountains, and the winter's exhale from beyond the arch stopped mid-breath. For one moment the chamber was absolutely still. Then the Gate opened.

I will not describe what came through it. Not because I can't, but because describing it would take the wrong shapes. It was not light. It was not warmth. It was *agreement*—three realms reaching toward each other through a wound in the world and touching, finally, after a hundred years of frost, and the touch ran outward faster than any herald ever rode, and everywhere it went, minds that had never met felt each other for one heartbeat. The Queen knelt. I am told the Queen knelt. I felt it happen the way you feel a door close in another room: by the change in the air.

And Mara Nystrom fell.

Not dramatically. She simply tipped sideways, like a coat slipping off a hook, and I caught her, because catching her was the entire reason I existed, and I did not know yet that my existing was finished.

* * *

She woke in the boy's arms.

Or rather—she woke, and the boy was holding her, and there was no reason left in the world why he should be, except that he was the vessel, and a vessel holds what it is given until it finds somewhere to pour it.

He poured it back into her. Imperfectly. That word matters more than any other word in this chapter, so I will say it plainly: imperfectly. The boy had held her core memories safe during the ritual—the ones we had judged, in the long nights of curation, to be load-bearing. The ones that made her able to stand. But a boy with no memory of his own turns out to be a strange kind of container. He held them faithfully the way a cup holds water, faithfully and without understanding, and when he poured them back some of them came out diluted, some came out in the wrong order, some came out with their edges rounded off, smoothed like river stones. What returned to her was not her past. It was her past's silhouette.

She woke knowing one thing with total clarity: that she had chosen this. The boy had saved that memory deliberately—had begged her, in the last hour, to let him keep that one above all others—and she had agreed, because a sacrifice you cannot remember having made is not a sacrifice; it is just damage. So she woke inside the knowledge of her own choice like waking inside a warm coat in a cold room. She knew she had paid. She knew it had been worth it. She knew the winter was ending because of her, and she knew she had done it on purpose, freely, with open eyes.

Everything else was gaps.

She looked at me and did not know who I was. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of her: she did not look at me with confusion, or fear, or the flat blankness people show strangers. She looked at me the way you look at a word on the tip of your tongue. She knew there was something. She could feel the shape of the knowing pressing against the inside of the gap. *You're mine,* she said—or she didn't say it; she reached for it and it wouldn't come, and what came out instead was, *Why do I feel like I've been waiting for you?*

Because you have, I wanted to tell her. Because I am you. Because you sent me back through the covenant you just reforged, and the timeline has healed around us like bone, and the girl standing in front of you is the proof that your choice worked.

But I couldn't tell her. That was the cost beneath the cost. The ritual had taken my future along with her past. The timeline was healing, and healing means closing, and a closed wound does not remember being open. Even as I knelt there holding her, watching her wake, my knowledge of what was coming was draining out of me like water through sand. I could feel myself becoming simpler. Smaller. A child. Just a child. The memories of the dying lands, the maps I drew from instinct, the fluent dead tongue rising out of me word by word—all of it softening, blurring, sinking. By morning I would speak like a child again. By spring I might not remember the Gate at all.

This is what rebirth with gaps looks like from the inside of the gap. One of us lost her past. One of us lost her future. And what remained, between us, was a present tense so narrow you could hold it in two hands.

* * *

The chamber filled slowly with the company.

Ilsa came first, leaning on the staff she could no longer use, and stood over Mara the way you stand over a sleeper you have guarded all night. The boy sat cross-legged with his hands still cupped, empty now, looking at them as if surprised to find them ordinary. The hollowed heralds drifted in from the edges, drawn by something—they did not know what; they did not know anything—and arranged themselves at the walls like furniture that had learned to stand. One of them wept. I never learned which memory the tears belonged to. Perhaps none. Perhaps emptiness weeps too, sometimes, just from the wind moving through it.

Outside, the frost was retreating. We could hear it—not melting, exactly; winters do not melt politely—but loosening, the great white weight of it sighing off the ruined towers of the Gate, and underneath the sigh, faint and impossible, water. Running water. The first in a hundred years. Ilsa heard it and put her ear against the stone floor and listened like a girl, and said, quietly, to no one, *There it is.* Her magic had guttered out entirely days before; she had given Mara her staff as a dead soldier gives up his sword. But she heard the water, and something in her face said the hearing was enough.

Mara sat up.

She moved like someone relearning a language she used to speak fluently. Her hands worked. Her eyes focused. But behind her eyes there was weather where there should have been landscape—clouds passing over gaps, gaps shaped like a mother singing, gaps shaped like a mentor's face, gaps shaped like a mute child on a threshold during the first unnatural snowfall. She touched her own sternum, pressing, as if checking whether anything was still in there.

*I chose this,* she said. It wasn't a question, but she said it like one, offering it up to the room for confirmation, and Ilsa took her hand and said, *You did. Every piece of it. I watched you,* and Mara nodded, and nodded again, slower, filing it away, building herself out of secondhand testimony because she had no firsthand material left.

Then she looked at me again. Longer this time.

*I know you,* she said. *I don't know how. But I know you the way you know a room you've slept in. My body knows you.*

And I—here is the thing I need to set down before it goes, here is the last entry in the ledger of who I was—I smiled at her. I said, in a voice that was already forgetting its own fluency, *You'll figure it out. You're good at figuring things out.*

She laughed. It startled her, the laugh, coming up out of her like water from that new-running stone. She laughed at a joke she couldn't remember making, and I laughed with her, and for a moment the antechamber of the Sundered Gate held two people laughing at a past that belonged to both of them and to neither, while the winter broke outside in sheets and rivers and the world, remade, went on being possible.

* * *

We stayed three days.

Someone had to teach her how to be Mara Nystrom, and the teachers were a hedge-witch with no magic, a boy with no memory, and a child with no future—which is to say, the least qualified faculty ever assembled, and also, it turned out, exactly the right one. Ilsa told her stories about herself. *You bargained with the Winter Queen on open ice. You stole a memory from a herald and found the Court's true aim. You refused to erase Corvess, even when erasing him would have freed you—you took only his betrayal and left him his love and his shame.* Mara listened to these stories about a stranger with her own face the way you listen to tales of a famous ancestor: proud, disbelieving, hungry. Once she asked, *Was she good?* and Ilsa said, *She was just,* and Mara thought about that for a long time, and I could see her deciding, silently, to try to deserve the description.

The boy taught her smaller things. How she took her tea—he remembered that, oddly, faithfully, one of the few details that survived the pouring back intact. Bitter, no honey. How she walked, fast, heels down, like the ground owed her money. How she hummed when she thought no one was listening, tunelessly, a habit she must have had before the Hollow, before everything, because no one had taught it to her and no one could have stolen it. She caught herself humming on the second day and stopped, bewildered, and the boy said, *You always did that,* and she looked at him with such tenderness that I understood, finally, why he had volunteered to be a vessel. Emptiness, it turns out, is not the absence of love. It is room for it.

And me. I taught her the most important thing, the only thing I had left to give, and I taught it badly, in fragments, in a voice that was losing its words day by day. I taught her the melody. I hummed it to her on the third night, the shape of it, the rise and fall, the hanging final note, and she went very still, and then she hummed it back, and it fit her throat like it had always lived there, because it had. *Where did that come from?* she asked, and I said, *Your mother,* and she said, *I don't have a mother,* and then, quieter, looking into one of her gaps: *Did I? Did I have one?*

Yes, I almost said. Yes, and she sang, and you paid a ferryman everything you had to keep one verse of her voice, and you got it back from his estate years later, and it was the only theft you ever allowed yourself, and tomorrow you will give it to the Gate—

But the words were gone. I opened my mouth and the dead tongue rose in me and died on my tongue, untranslated, and what came out instead was a child's sentence, simple and true and useless:

*You had a mother. She sang.*

Mara held that sentence the way the boy had held her memories: carefully, without understanding, certain only of its weight. She hummed the melody again. She decided—it was visible, the deciding, like watching someone set a stone in a ring—that this was hers now. Whatever it was. Whoever it came from. Some things you keep before you know why, and trust the why to surface later, the way bodies float.

On the third morning, the water outside had become a stream, and the stream had become a river running bright over stones that hadn't seen daylight in a century, and Ilsa said it was time, and Mara said, *Time for what?* and Ilsa said, *Home. Whatever home is now. You built the world where we get to have one.*

We packed almost nothing. The shard was gone—turned, spent, become part of the Gate, part of the covenant, part of the law that bound three realms to remember each other. Its warmth lingered in Mara's palm for hours afterward, a phantom heat, and she kept flexing her hand around it like a memory of a memory, which is what it was, and what she was, and what I was becoming.

We walked out of the Sundered Gate under a sky that was doing something unprecedented. It was raining. Warm rain. The company walked in it without hurrying, faces up, letting it land, and the hollowed heralds walked with us, directionless, docile, following the sound of the water the way they had once followed orders. Mara looked back at them and I saw her jaw set, and I knew—even knowing almost nothing, even stripped to a single certainty and a handful of silhouettes—I knew what that set jaw meant, because some things survive the pouring back. She was not leaving them behind. Whatever she had made of them, she intended to answer for it.

The road home ran east, toward the Hollow, toward the valley that had forbidden magic and gotten exactly what it forbade. Toward the elders who had demanded a mute child be given to the snow. Toward a shop with a crooked shutter above which a woman with no past would have to build a life out of testimony and humming and gaps.

Mara walked at the front. She walked fast, heels down, like the ground owed her money.

And I walked beside her, getting smaller with every mile, and on the second evening she crouched down to my height—she had begun doing that, some instinct telling her children require level eyes—and she said, *When we get wherever we're going, you'll tell me who you are. Won't you? All of it?*

And I said yes, because it was true, and because it was the last lie-shaped truth I had left: yes, I would tell her all of it. As much of it as I still was.

The valley's rim rose ahead of us on the fourth day, black pines against a sky the color of thaw, and at the edge of the trees a figure stood waiting—small, hunched, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the warming air, watching the road. Watching *her*.

Mara stopped. Stared. Reached for a name and found the gap where it should have been, and frowned, and said, quietly, to me, to the rain, to whoever was listening inside her:

*I know that face. Why do I know that face?*
