## 38. Homecoming to a Strange Valley

The woman standing at the tree line was Old Wren.

I knew that only because the girl told me. She leaned close and said the name against my ear, the way you'd hand someone a cup in the dark, and the name settled somewhere in me like water finding a crack. *Old Wren.* I turned it over. It fit nothing. But the girl had not steered me wrong yet—not since I'd woken on the ice with no past and a stranger's face full of grief for me—and so I trusted the shape of it the way I trusted the ache in my knees after three days of walking: as evidence of something real.

"You know her," I said.

"I knew her." The girl's mouth did something complicated. "You will."

The gap where my memory should have been wasn't empty, exactly. That was the cruelest discovery of the road home. The boy—the boy whose name lived in a hole in my chest where his story should be, though the girl swore he'd given it gladly, twice—had carried my core back to me across the Gate's threshold, and what came through was not memory but the *sediment* of memory. I knew Corvess had raised me, and could not remember his voice. I knew I had loved someone's singing, and the melody was gone but the longing stayed, sitting behind my ribs like a stone worn smooth by a river that no longer existed. I knew the Hollow the way a bird knows a nest it was hatched in but never saw built.

So when Old Wren stepped out from beneath the pines and said, "Mara Nystrom," in a voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring, I felt recognition move through me without arriving anywhere. Like hearing footsteps in a house you've just inherited.

"That's me," I said. "Apparently."

* * *

The Hollow was green.

I want to write that plainly, because nothing about it was plain. For as long as anyone living could remember, the valley had been a place of gray endurance—frost-stunted orchards, fields the color of old dishwater, a sky pressed down low like a lid. Magic could not follow you in; that was the bargain, or the curse, or whatever the elders called it when they wanted to make poverty sound like wisdom. What grew there grew stubbornly, joylessly, the way weeds grow in a prison yard.

Now the pines at the rim were black-green instead of gray-green. Now there were flowers along the boundary stones—actual flowers, blue and shameless, pushing up through soil that had spent two centuries pretending to be stone. Now, when we crossed the line where the valley began, I felt magic pour into me like warm water into a cold jar, and I understood that this was new, that the valley itself was drinking for the first time in living memory, and that every soul inside it was about to learn what thirst quenched feels like and be undone by it.

"It's different," I said, stupidly.

"It's everything," the girl said.

She walked half a step ahead of me, which she had done since the Gate. Protective, I thought at first. Then I watched her longer and decided it was something else: she was orienting herself. Checking each landmark against a map she carried in her head from a life she was slowly losing. Every day some piece of her future went soft and slid away—I saw it happen, saw her pause mid-stride and frown at a fence post as if it had moved—and every day she seemed lighter afterward, the way a wound looks better as it closes. She was becoming simply a girl. Someone was becoming simply a girl. The grammar of us still tangled sometimes.

Old Wren fell into step beside us without ceremony, which told me everything the elders' faces would tell me later. You don't walk uninvited beside dangerous people unless you've decided they aren't dangerous, or unless you're the one sent to find out.

"You look well," she said to me. Then, correcting herself with the honesty of the very old: "You look *new*. Like a coin fresh from the mint. Same face, no wear."

"There was wear," I said. "It got spent."

Her eyes flicked to the girl, then away. Guilt has a smell. I didn't need stolen memories anymore to read it; the Gate had left me with something better than theft—a kind of clean attention, an ear tuned to what people actually meant underneath what they said. It was, I suspected, what the Oathkeepers had been before they became traitors, before they became myth. Listeners.

"The council sent me," Wren said. "There was argument."

"About whether to let me in?"

"About whether you'd *fit*." Her mouth twisted. "Some of them have been praying for the winter to end their whole lives. Now it's ending, and the prayers are coming true, and they're furious about it. Anger is easier than gratitude. Gratitude makes you owe somebody." She glanced sideways at the girl, and her voice dropped. "And some of them remember wanting to give this child to the snow. They're afraid you remember it too."

I stopped walking.

Wren stopped with me. Ahead, the girl kept going three steps before she noticed, and turned, silhouetted against the flowering boundary stones, waiting with the patience of someone who has waited for much worse things.

"Do I?" I asked Wren. "Remember it?"

"I don't know," she said. "Do you?"

I reached inward, into the sediment, feeling for the night of the first snowfall, the elders in their wool and their candlelight, the word *snow* spoken over a mute child like a sentence being passed. Nothing came. No faces. No voices. Just a residue of something I can only call *refusal*—a hard bright knot under my sternum that I recognized as mine because it still pulled the same way now, when I thought of the girl walking ahead of me.

"No," I said. "But it doesn't matter. She remembers. And the ones who said it will have to live with her looking at them."

Wren exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. "They'll hate that."

"They should have thought of that before they tried to feed a child to a season."

* * *

The Hollow's heart village sat in the valley floor like a thing that had been holding its breath for two hundred years and hadn't yet figured out it could stop. Roofs sagged the way roofs do when they expect snow. Gardens were laid out small and defensive. People stood in doorways watching us come down the road, and I watched them watch, and I saw the exact moment the wind changed direction and brought them the smell of the world outside the valley—green things, far things, the faint iron tang of working magic—and I saw their faces try to reject it and fail.

A child ran out into the road ahead of us. Maybe six. Barefoot, which told me the thaw had reached even the mud. She planted herself in front of the girl and stared up at her with the frank rudeness of the young.

"You're the one from the snow," the child said.

"Yes," the girl said.

"My mother says you brought the warm back."

The girl crouched. I watched her do it—watched her choose it, watched her weight settle—and I thought: *that is a gesture I would make.* Not because I remembered making it. Because it was in the sediment. We are made of gestures before we are made of memories, and whoever holds the memories, the gestures remain.

"I didn't bring it alone," she told the child.

"Who helped?"

"A lot of people. Some of them are dead. One of them was very annoying and died anyway." A pause. "One of them gave up everything so that everyone else could keep what they had."

The child considered this with terrible six-year-old gravity. "Was that you?"

The girl looked up then, past the child, at me. And I looked back, and between us passed the whole unspeakable architecture of it—that she was me, that I was what remained of her sacrifice, that one of us was a woman with no past and the other was a past with no woman, and we were walking into a village together to be its founding miracle, and neither of us had asked for any part of it.

"No," she said softly. "That was someone else."

The child frowned, dissatisfied with the answer, the way children are dissatisfied by true things. Then a woman's voice called from a doorway—sharp, frightened, loving—and the child ran to it, glancing back once at us both with an expression I felt in my teeth.

"She'll remember us," the girl said, standing. "That one. Children keep what matters."

"Good," I said. "Someone should."

* * *

The elders received us in the meeting hall, which was the largest building in the village and still too small for what was coming. Eleven of them, seated in a crescent on benches worn pale by generations of backsides, wearing their authority like coats cut for men long dead. I stood in the middle of the floor with the girl at my shoulder, and I let the silence go on just long enough to become theirs.

"Mara Nystrom," said the eldest, a man named Hobb whose hands were mason's hands, cracked and square. He'd been first among those who'd wanted the child given to the snow. I didn't know how I knew that. Sediment again, or the girl's silence beside me, which had a particular quality when she was near someone who had wronged her—a stillness like deep water. "You left this valley in the night. Against counsel. With—" and here his eyes went to the girl and could not stay, "—with that. And you return with spring following you like a dog."

"Not following," I said. "Carried. There's a difference."

"You claim responsibility for the thaw."

"I don't claim anything. It happened. I was there." I let that sit. "Mostly."

Hobb's jaw worked. Around the crescent, the other elders performed the small theater of their discomfort—shifting, coughing, studying their hands. Outside the hall's open doors, villagers crowded, more of them than the hall had ever held interest in accommodating, drawn by the same pull that had gathered our strays on the road: the shard's warmth, or what remained of it now in the world's rebalanced veins. Magic in the Hollow. Two hundred years of exile ended by a woman who couldn't remember why she'd bothered.

"We require an accounting," Hobb said.

"Of course you do." I nodded, and found—to my own surprise—that I wasn't angry. That was the strangest inheritance of all. Whatever fury the old Mara had carried, most of it had gone into the Gate with the rest of her, and what was left of me could look at eleven frightened old people and feel mainly a kind of distant tenderness, the way you feel toward a wall that was built during a war nobody remembers. "Here it is, then. The winter was dying because the covenant that held the realms together was broken, and breaking it starved all three worlds, including yours. Your isolation wasn't protection. It was a symptom. The child carried the key to fixing it. I took her to the lock. The lock required payment." I spread my hands. "I paid. Here we are."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a bee—actual, impossible, a bee in the Hollow—droned past the doorway, and I watched two elders track its sound with naked wonder and horror.

"And your payment," Hobb said carefully, "was—"

"Everything I remembered. Which means I can't testify to much of it, and neither can she—" I tipped my head toward the girl, "—because her testimony is a paradox with feet. Ask your questions anyway. I'll answer what I can."

"Why should we trust you?" This from a thin woman at the crescent's end, her voice pitched high with fear dressed as principle. "You traffic in stolen minds. You always have. And now you come back with power none of us can see the bottom of, and a child no one can account for, and we're meant to simply—"

"To simply what?" the girl said.

She hadn't spoken since entering the hall. Her voice was small and even and it stopped the room like a hand flat on a table.

"Give me to the snow?" she said. "You considered it once. I was here for that. I remember it better than she does." A nod toward me, and something in my chest clenched at being referred to in the third person by myself. "I'm not angry. I understand. You were cold and afraid and I was strange, and strange things die in winter—it's an old arithmetic, older than this valley. But the arithmetic changed. The world changed while you sat in here deciding whether I was weather or a person." She looked around the crescent, unhurried, meeting each face. "I'm a person. And the weather is leaving. And the question isn't whether you trust me. The question is what you're going to do with the spring now that refusing it isn't an option."

Nobody answered. The bee droned. Far off, someone laughed—a villager, outside, laughing at something, freely, the sound carrying into the hall like an emissary from a country these people had forgotten bordered their own.

Then Old Wren rose from her place at the crescent's edge—the twelfth seat, the one I hadn't counted, the one set slightly apart—and said, "I'll say it, then, since none of you will." She looked around at her colleagues with magnificent contempt. "This girl came to our door with a burning stone and a tongue older than our oldest grave, and we told her mother-figure to drown her in a snowbank. And instead of drowning anyone, they walked out into the dying world and *fixed it*. So I propose we stop asking whether to trust them and start asking whether they'll teach us. Because the magic is in the valley now, neighbors. It's in the water and the wheat and your grandchildren's blood, and it is not leaving, and someone in this room had better make peace with that before the children find it first."

She sat. The silence afterward had a different texture—not resistance but surrender, the particular quiet of people setting down something heavy.

Hobb looked at me for a long time. His mason's hands lay flat on his knees, and I watched him decide something the way you watch a man decide to put down a tool he's held his whole life.

"What would you ask of us, Mara Nystrom?" he said. Not *what do you demand*. *Ask.* The smallest possible capitulation, offered like the last rung of a ladder.

I had walked four days to reach this moment and rehearsed nothing, because the woman who planned things had gone into the Gate, and what was left of me mostly improvised. So I said the true thing, which arrived unbidden:

"Nothing. I'm not here to rule you. I'm barely here." I touched my own sternum, over the smooth stone where the missing years sat. "Half of me is a hole shaped like a life. But the Hole is going to fill up—with magic, with children, with everything the thaw brings—and holes fill badly if no one tends them. So here's what I ask. Teach me the valley I don't remember. Let me relearn my own home alongside everyone else learning theirs. And when the first Oathkeepers of the new age are ready to be trained—and they will come, they're already coming, the shard saw to that—let me be the one who teaches them what we learned on the road: that a covenant is only as good as what it asks of everyone, and memory is the only binding that doesn't rot."

Hobb blinked. "Oathkeepers. You mean to found an order. Here."

"I mean to found a school," I said. "The order can take care of itself."

Something happened to the eldest's face then—something that on a younger man might have been a smile fighting free of a lifetime of governance. "And where would you hold this school?"

I opened my mouth to say *here*, and the girl's hand closed on my sleeve.

Not urgent. Just present. I looked down at her and she looked up at me, and I saw in her face the thing she'd been carrying since the tree line, since before the tree line, maybe since the ferry and the singing and the fire—since a future she was daily forgetting, dissolving into ordinary childhood one soft piece at a time.

"Somewhere with a garden," she said quietly, to me alone. "You always wanted one. You just never remembered to want it until now."

I looked at her for a long moment.

"How would I know that?" I asked.

"You don't," she said. "But I do. And in a year or two, I won't either. So we'd better get started." She smiled then—small, crooked, entirely a child's smile and entirely mine—and tugged my sleeve toward the door, toward the light, toward the green and impossible valley. "Come on. Show me where we're going to live. I've never gotten to choose a home before. Neither have you, really."

And because she was right, and because she was me, and because there was nothing left in the world to argue about that mattered—

I let her lead me out into the spring.
