## 39. Raising Myself

**Mara**

The first fight was about porridge.

Not the big things—the Gate, the covenant, the winter that had broken like a fever across three realms. Porridge. The girl wanted honey in it, and I said no, because I had a rule about children and sweetness I couldn't actually remember making.

"You always say that," she said, arms crossed, standing on the chair so she could glare at me levelly. "You *always* say no first."

"I don't remember saying it before."

"That's not my problem." She held out her hand for the honey jar with the imperious calm of someone who had once negotiated with a queen. Which she had. Which she was me. The thought still landed wrong every time, like a stair that wasn't there.

I gave her the honey.

That was how it went, those first months in the Hollow. She knew me better than I knew myself—which sounds like poetry until you live it. She knew I hummed when I lied. She knew I checked the door twice at night, then a third time when I thought she wasn't watching. She knew I hated being told what I felt, which she used ruthlessly, the way I would have.

"You're afraid," she announced one evening, watching me mend a coat by lamplight.

"I'm sewing."

"You sew faster when you're afraid."

I put down the needle. "What am I afraid of?"

She considered this with terrible seriousness. A child of eight or nine now—I'd stopped counting her age by years she'd already lived and started counting by the ones she was losing. "That you'll do it wrong," she said finally. "Raising me. That some day I'll look at you and see everything you got wrong, because I'll be you again, and I'll know."

The lamp hissed. Outside, spring was doing its slow work on the Hollow's terraces, green climbing the slopes where frost had lived for generations.

"And if I do get it wrong?" I asked.

"Then I'll forgive you," she said, "because I already have." And she went to bed, leaving me with the mending and the strange vertigo of being absolved by yourself.

**the girl**

I am forgetting things.

I don't mind, mostly. I don't miss the ice fields of the far north or the smell of the antechamber or the sound Corvess made when the Gate unmade him—actually I do miss that last one, or rather I miss *having* it, because he deserves to be remembered by someone who was there, and soon there will be no one.

But small things go too. I used to know forty ways to start a fire without flint. Last week I tried to show Mara the one with birch bark and pine resin and I could only remember eleven steps of it, and the twelfth was gone like a word on the tip of the tongue, and by the time I found the thirteenth the fire had gone out twice.

Mara noticed. She notices everything; she taught herself to notice everything back when noticing kept you alive, and even without her memories the habit stayed in her hands.

"What else is gone?" she asked me by the fire, after I'd gotten it lit on the third try.

"Numbers," I said. "How many heralds came to Vellum. What the Queen's voice sounded like. The words of the oath—I wrote them down but reading them isn't the same as knowing them." I turned my hands over, palms up, empty. "It's like snow melting off a roof. It doesn't fall all at once. It just gets thinner."

"Does it hurt?"

I thought honestly about it. "No. It's more like—" I looked for the word and it took a while, another sign, "—like putting down something heavy you forgot you were carrying."

She nodded slowly. Then she did the thing she does that makes my chest ache: she reached across and took my hands, the thief holding her own future, and said, "Good. You should be light. That was the whole point."

But sometimes I wake at night with a name dissolving in my mouth—a deserter's name, a hedge-witch's name—and I lie in the dark trying to hold on, and I can't tell anymore whether I'm grieving them or just missing a story someone once told me. Those are the nights I go stand outside Mara's door, not going in, just listening to her breathe. As long as one of us remembers, I figure, we're not lost.

Except she barely remembers anything either. Except soon it will only be the boy's old vessel-memory and whatever she keeps new.

Except maybe that's enough. Maybe that was always the whole point too.

**Mara**

The village elders came in autumn, when the orchards we'd planted along the old boundary were dropping their first real fruit.

Elder Sanna stood in my doorway with the particular stiffness of a woman preparing an apology she'd rehearsed the whole walk over. Behind her, the others. These were the people who had voted to give a mute child to the snow.

"We've come about the school," Sanna said.

"The school."

"The one you're building. For teaching the old tongue, and the... keeping." She stumbled on the word. Oathkeeping still sat strangely in their mouths, a year after winter broke. "The council wishes to contribute timber. And to ask—if it's not presuming—whether you'd consider letting our children attend."

I let the silence stretch, because I'm told I enjoy that, and because I genuinely didn't know what I wanted to say. The girl appeared at my elbow, honey-sweet and razor-eyed, and looked up at Sanna with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"My mother says yes," the girl said.

"She's not—" I started.

"I meant you," the girl said serenely, and walked off toward the orchard, having won again.

Sanna's shoulders came down half an inch. "We were afraid," she offered. "That winter. That everything."

"I know," I said. "I've read your memories."

Her face went white, then, slowly, understanding. Not stolen—given. When the covenant sealed, every mind touched by it had felt every other for one heartbeat, and what the Hollow had felt from me was a life of taking, and what I'd felt from them was ordinary terror doing ordinary harm. We had all been seen. There was no unseeing it.

"We will send the timber tomorrow," Sanna said, and left.

The girl was waiting in the orchard, sitting under the crooked apple tree with her knees drawn up.

"You called me mother," I said, sitting down beside her.

"It gets easier if you practice."

"I didn't say yes yet."

"You were going to." She picked up a fallen apple, turned it in her small hands. "You always build schools. In—" she stopped. Frowned. Her brow furrowed in that way I'd learned to dread and cherish equally. "In somewhere. I think you always build schools."

The future slipping loose, right in front of me. I put my arm around her and she leaned in, apple and all, and we watched the light go gold across the valley that magic could finally enter, and neither of us said the thing we both were thinking: that someday she wouldn't remember why any of this mattered, and that would be the victory.

**the girl**

There is a boy in the village who steals apples the way Mara used to steal memories—cleanly, without malice, because it's there. His name is Tam. He has a gap in his teeth and a laugh like a cart going downhill.

He walked me home yesterday past the boundary stones, where the grass grows different on each side now, and he asked me what it was like, living with Mara Nystrom.

"She's strict," I said. "She checks the door three times at night. She gives me honey even though she says no first. She's teaching me the old tongue but she pretends she learned it from a book."

"Your ma sounds strange."

"She's not my ma," I said, and then heard how it sounded, and then thought about it, really thought, the way Mara taught me to think, and said, "Actually, I don't know what she is."

Tam shrugged with the vast indifference of ten-year-olds. "Could be both."

Both. Mentor and mother and mirror. I rolled the idea around like a marble in my pocket. That night at supper I watched Mara salt her porridge—she salts it, which I never remembered her doing, which means it's new, which means it's *hers*, grown fresh in a life with no past in it—and something in my chest went quiet and warm.

She is becoming someone I never was. Someone the winter never touched. Every day she does small things the woman at the Gate couldn't have done, and every day I lose a little more of the woman at the Gate, and the arithmetic of it is simple and merciless: she is filling up as I empty out. The covenant trading one of us for the other across a kitchen table.

"Mara," I said.

"Hm?"

"When I forget everything—will you tell me? About the ferryman, and the company, and all of it?"

She was quiet a long moment, spoon resting in the bowl.

"All of it?" she said. "Even the parts where I was selfish?"

"Especially those."

"Then yes." She reached over and tucked a strand of hair behind my ear, clumsy at it, learning. "I'll tell you everything. Even the parts where I got it wrong."

"You'll get some things right," I said. "You already have. I keep a list."

"A list," she said. "Of course you keep a list. I raised you, I know exactly how you are."

And we laughed, both of us, at the impossible truth of it, while outside the first hard frost of the season silvered the orchard grass—ordinary frost, seasonal frost, frost that would melt by noon—and somewhere beyond the hills, spring waited its turn like a patient thing.

**Mara**

She woke screaming once, near midwinter's end. I ran in and found her sitting bolt upright, hands pressed flat against the blanket, eyes wide and wet.

"I dreamed—" she gasped. "There was ice, and a gate, and a woman walking onto the lake alone, and I couldn't—I couldn't see her face, I couldn't—"

"Shh." I sat on the edge of the bed and gathered her in, and she shook against me like a small storm. "It was just a dream."

"No." She pulled back and looked at me, and for one moment her eyes were ancient, were *mine*, were the eyes of someone who had crossed a dying world carrying a shard and a debt. "It was important. It was the most important thing and I can't—" Her face crumpled. "It's going. It's going and I can't hold it."

"Do you want to hold it?" I asked softly.

She stared at me. Somewhere behind her eyes, a war between two lifetimes.

And then, slowly, her hands unclenched. Her breathing slowed. The ancient look drained away and left a child, exhausted, tear-streaked, nine years old and nothing else.

"No," she said at last, wonderingly. "No, I don't."

She lay back down. I tucked the blanket to her chin, and she caught my wrist before I could rise.

"Stay till I sleep?"

"Always," I said, meaning it in every tense.

She was asleep within minutes, breathing easy, her fist closed loosely around the hem of my sleeve. I sat in the dark listening to the winter wind move through a world that no longer feared it, and I understood, finally, what the covenant had bought with everything I was:

Not a memory. A morning. This one, and the next, and the next after that—each one arriving clean, each one hers to grow into, until the girl who saved three realms was simply a girl, and the woman raising her was simply a woman, and neither of them owed the past anything at all.

Outside, the frost was already thinning. I could feel it turning, the long slow wheel coming round, and I thought of a river called Sere and a song I had paid away and might one day steal back, and I smiled in the dark, and stayed until morning.
