## 40. The First Stolen Spring

The bell over the schoolhouse door had been cast from a shard of the old Gate, and it rang wrong in the best way—a sound like a word being remembered.

Mara Nystrom stood at the window and watched the road below the hill. Six students were coming up through the orchard, late as always, their boots muddy with the first thaw. Behind them the valley opened green and ridiculous, all of it new grass and running meltwater, the Hollow's famous fog burned off by a sun that arrived when it was supposed to. Spring on schedule. Eleven years now, and she still caught herself bracing for frost that never came, the way a man who has been struck once flinches at raised hands forever after.

"You're doing it again," said the girl at the front desk without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Watching the sky like it owes you money."

Mara turned. Sefa was fourteen, sharp-tongued, hollow-eyed in the way of children who had survived something before they could name it—the famine winter, in her case, the last gasp of the old cold that had come down the mountains the year Mara sealed the Gate. Half her class carried some version of that look. That was why they were here. The new Oathkeepers were not chosen for power. They were chosen for damage.

"Sit down," Mara said. "All of you. We're starting."

They filed in shaking rain off their shoulders—no, not rain; she checked the window again; no, it *was* rain, warm rain, ordinary rain—and took their seats on benches carved from the antechamber timber of the Sundered Gate itself. Ilsa's staff hung on the wall beside the door, its wood gone silver with age, its magic long since returned to whatever soil magic returns to. Below it, in a frame of black iron, hung a single page: the terms of the New Covenant, written in the old tongue in Mara's own hand, with corrections in a child's careful script. She had refused to let them copy it fair. The crossings-out were the lesson.

"Recite," she said.

Six voices, ragged but together: *"Each realm shall remember the others. Memory is the law. To forget is to breach. To hoard is to breach. What is given freely binds; what is taken by force dissolves."*

"And what does that make you?"

There was a pause, because there was always a pause, because the answer never stopped being strange.

"Witnesses," said Sefa.

"Thieves," said Tam, the ferryman's grandson, who said it every time just to watch her face.

"Both," Mara said. "Both is correct. Sit down, Tam."

* * *

The lesson that morning was extraction—not the taking, but the giving back. This was the part of the curriculum Mara taught personally, the part no text covered, because the texts had all been written by people who thought memory was property.

She brought out the bowl. Plain clay, fired in the village kiln, glazed the color of a winter sky. Inside it, held by nothing anyone could see, swam a memory: a woman standing at a hearth, singing a song in a language older than any kingdom, her voice low and cracked at the edges where grief had gotten into it. Firelight on her cheekbones. A child asleep in the next room.

It was the only theft Mara Nystrom had kept.

Eleven years ago she had walked onto the ice of the river Sere and surrendered everything—every stolen memory in her hoard, every moment of her own life, poured into the turning stone until there was nothing left of her but the choosing. And when she woke in the snow with a girl's hand in hers and a boy's vessel full of her scattered self pressed back into her imperfectly, like a book rebound with the pages slightly shuffled, she had found that one thing missing. One thing gone entirely, spent at the ferryman's crossing years before, paid for passage across a frozen river: her mother singing.

She had grieved it the way you grieve an amputated limb—an ache shaped exactly like something that should be there.

And then, three years ago, old Tam the ferryman had died, and his estate had passed to his family, and his family had found among his effects a small clay bowl that no one could open and no one could throw away, and his grandson—clever boy, too clever for the river—had carried it up the hill to the schoolhouse and set it on her desk and said, "My grandfather wrote in his ledger that he bought this off a woman with a child, winter of the long cold. He wrote that he knew it wasn't rightly his. He wrote that if the thief ever came back, give it home."

She had put her hands around the bowl and wept for an hour while six students sat in terrified silence, and then she had sung the song back into herself, note by cracked note, and it had fit. It had fit like it had never left.

"Now," she said to the class, setting the bowl in the center of the room. "Who can tell me why I keep this? Why this one, when the whole covenant says hoarding is breach?"

Sefa raised her hand. "Because it's yours. You didn't steal it. You took back what was taken from you."

"Close. But the ferryman paid fairly, in his mind. He gave passage for goods. By the letter of the old law—" she tapped the framed page "—I stole it twice. Once from my mother's house, and once from his ledger." She let that sit. "Anyone else?"

Tam, slowly: "Because you're going to give it away. Right now. You're going to show us how."

"No." Mara smiled, and it still surprised her sometimes, how easily the smile came now, how little it cost. "Because a law you can't break isn't a law. It's a wall. The covenant asks us to hold nothing, to take nothing, to keep nothing—but it doesn't ask us to be empty. It asks us to know the difference between what we carry and what carries us." She touched the bowl. "This memory doesn't make me a thief. It makes me a daughter. There's a difference, and the difference is consent, and consent is the entire art. Everything else—the pulling, the holding, the giving back—is technique. Technique can be taught to anyone. The difference cannot. Either you feel it here—" she pressed her fist to her sternum "—or you have no business touching another mind, and my job is to find out which kind of you showed up this morning."

She looked around the room, at each of them in turn. The damaged ones. The ones the world had already reached into and rummaged through. Somewhere in the crowd of faces she saw, always, briefly, a smaller face—a girl of about seven, mute, clutching light—and she let the sight pass through her like weather.

"Take out your bowls," she said. "We begin."

* * *

After lessons, she walked down to the village.

The Hollow had changed so gradually that she had never once been able to point at the moment of it. Magic came back the way water comes back to a dried well—not all at once but seeping, rising, until one day you dropped a bucket in and heard it splash. Hedges bloomed out of season. The elders who had voted to give a child to the snow were mostly dead now, and the ones who remained had learned to bow their heads when she passed, which she neither wanted nor discouraged, because some lessons are taught by time alone. The valley that had been a hole in the world was now simply a valley, green and ordinary, except that its school produced Oathkeepers and its orchard trees hummed faintly if you listened hard.

The child was waiting at the bridge. Of course she was. She was always waiting at the bridge on thaw days; she said the sound of the meltwater reminded her of something she couldn't name, and Mara never told her what it actually reminded her of, because some knowledge is a weight and the girl had carried enough.

She was seventeen now. Tall where Mara had been wiry, quiet where Mara had been sharp, with the same gray eyes and a habit of tilting her head when she was thinking that Mara had been told—by Corvess, once, in a memory given freely at the end—she'd had since infancy.

"The students say you sang today," the girl said.

"I demonstrated a retrieval. Singing was incidental."

"They say you cried."

"They say a lot of things." Mara leaned on the bridge rail. Below, the stream ran loud and bright, carrying the last of the winter down toward the lowlands. "How was Vellum?"

The girl had spent the winter at the archive-city, translating. The old tongue lived again now—there were four hundred speakers under thirty, and scholars came from all three realms to study at the source, which was a girl who had learned it before she was born, in a sense, or after she died, depending on how you counted, and Mara had stopped counting years ago.

"The Queen sent a letter." The girl produced it from her coat, folded small. "For you. She sends one every spring."

"I know. I burn them."

"You read them first."

"I read them first," Mara conceded.

This year's letter was short. The Queen's letters were always short; brevity was the only courtly manner she had ever adopted. *The third harvest of the borderlands failed again,* it read. *The eastern realm petitions for aid. My heralds report your students handled the Sere dispute adequately. Adequately is not excellence. Send me better, thief.* And then, in a different ink, added later, smaller: *The ice on my southern lakes broke up nine days early. I am told this is called a blessing. I am learning the word.*

Mara read it twice and handed it back.

"Well?" the girl asked.

"She wants more students. She thinks adequacy is an insult." Mara looked out at the water. "She's right. It is."

"You'll send her Sefa."

"Sefa would eat her alive."

"That's why you'll send her."

Mara laughed. Downstream, a kingfisher hit the water and came up flashing. On schedule. Everything on schedule.

* * *

That evening she climbed the hill behind the schoolhouse, alone, as she did every year on this day.

The date didn't matter anymore—it was an arbitrary anniversary, picked from a calendar that had been rewritten twice since—but the ritual had stuck, and Mara had learned not to argue with rituals that worked. At the top of the hill stood a cairn of river stones, one added every year, eleven stones now, twelve after tonight. It marked nothing. No one was buried there. It was simply the place where she had woken, empty-handed and brand-new, with a child's mittened hand gripping hers and a voice in her head that was her own voice saying, *You chose this. Remember that much. I made sure you'd remember that much.*

She sat down on the cold grass and let the evening come.

Below, the schoolhouse windows glowed. Somewhere in there, six damaged kids were practicing the hardest art in the three realms: holding other people's lives gently. Somewhere beyond the hills, a queen was learning the word *blessing*. Somewhere in a drawer in the schoolhouse, in a bowl that no longer needed to be locked, a mother went on singing at her hearth, firelight on her cheekbones, and every morning Mara woke and the song was still there, and every morning it was still the only thing she had ever stolen, and every morning she decided, again, to keep it.

Corvess would have called that a flaw. He had spent a lifetime teaching her that a clean soul was the only safe soul, that anything kept was a door left unlocked. He had died wrong about almost everything and right about love, and she had buried him under an apple tree that fruited out of season now, every year, as if apologizing.

The girl climbed the hill as the stars came out. She didn't ask permission anymore; she just sat down in the grass beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way they had crossed a dying world together, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"Do you remember her?" the girl asked finally. "Your mother. Really remember?"

Mara considered it honestly, because the girl deserved honesty; she had bought it at a price no one else would ever fully understand.

"I remember the song. I remember the firelight. I remember that she was tired and sang anyway." She turned her palm up toward the sky, an old habit, checking for snow that never fell. "Is that really remembering? Or is it just what's left when remembering is done?"

The girl was quiet a moment. Then: "When I was—" she stopped, restarted, the way she always did near the edges of the thing they never discussed directly. "Before. In the place I came from. I used to think the worst part would be forgetting the future. Losing what I knew." She pulled her knees up. "It wasn't. The future's mostly gone now—I can't tell you what happens next any better than you can. But the song. I still have the song. I don't know if it's mine or yours or hers. I think it might be all three."

"It's ours," Mara said. "That's the whole covenant, if you want it in one line. Not mine. Not thine. Ours."

The girl laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, seventeen years old and nothing else—and knocked her shoulder against Mara's, and they sat there while the first true warm night of the year settled over the valley like a hand smoothing a wrinkled sheet.

Down in the dark, the schoolhouse bell rang once, unprompted, the Gate-shard inside it humming. Mara felt it in her teeth, faint and familiar, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

The girl heard it too. Her head came up sharply.

"That's new," she said.

"Yes," Mara said, standing, brushing grass from her coat, her heart doing something it hadn't done in eleven years—which was to say, quickening. "That's very new."

Far to the north, past the hills, past the borders, past the whole remembered world, something vast and patient had just turned over in its sleep—and the shard in the bell, cut from the heart of the covenant itself, was singing its name.

Mara smiled, and reached down, and pulled the girl to her feet.

"Get your coat," she said. "Fetch Sefa. Tell the class we're going north."

Spring had come on schedule. But something else, apparently, had come on schedule too—and it had waited long enough.
