## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market in Vharrow began the way all good things did in that city: quietly, and against the law.

Ulyana Kavanagh set out her wares on a strip of oilcloth between a fishwife's brazier and a man who sold clockwork sparrows that had long ago forgotten how to fly. The lantern above her stall was hooded with green glass, so that everything it touched looked drowned, underwater, half-remembered. That was deliberate. In Vharrow, you did not want to be remembered.

Her charms lay in rows like small sleeping animals. Knotted cords braided from three colors of thread—red for blood remembered, black for blood let go, gold for whatever luck remained. Tin moons stamped with her thumb. Little sewn hands, palm outward, to turn away envy. Nothing that *worked*, not officially. Nothing that would interest an inspector. She had spent eleven years learning to make magic look like handicraft: knots that were only knots, unless you knew which finger to press them with; moons that were only tin, unless you whispered to them at dusk.

In Vharrow, where magic had been outlawed since the Godfall, the trick was not power. The trick was being overlooked.

"Charm-seller." The fishwife, Marta, didn't look at her directly. Nobody at the market did; that was the arrangement. "Inspector's walking the row tonight. Crown gray, new boots."

"Thank you." Ulyana kept her voice as flat as the coins she charged. "Which direction?"

"From the fountain. He'll be at the sparrows by now."

Ulyana glanced down the lane of stalls. The clockwork man was already folding his table with the frantic efficiency of someone who had seen inspectors before. Beyond him, at the mouth of the street, stood a figure in the gray livery of the Crown Office of Purity, lamplight sliding off his polished buttons. He moved slowly, reading faces the way other men read notices.

She pulled her left sleeve down over her wrist.

It was habit older than thought, that motion. Beneath the wool, wrapped twice around her forearm and bound with waxed linen, lay her oathmark—the guild-sign of the Order of Small Mercies, inked into her skin when she was six days old in a guildhall most of Vharrow believed had burned down decades ago. A knot with no beginning and no end, drawn in ink mixed with ash from a fire that never went out. If the inspector saw it, she would not be fined. She would be *processed*. There was a word the crown used for people who bore guild-marks now. It was not a word with a trial attached to it.

So she became invisible. It was, she sometimes thought, her truest art—not the knots, not the moons, but this: the practiced slump of the shoulders, the dulling of the eyes, the way she could make her face into something a man's gaze slid off like rain off oiled canvas. She arranged herself behind her table, hunched slightly, and let her hands hang loose and stupid, a woman with nothing to hide because there was nothing in her worth finding.

The inspector came down the row like a slow tide.

He stopped at the fishwife first. Marta held up a smoked eel and said something about the harbor tithes, and he waved her off with the boredom of a man doing arithmetic in his head. He paused at the clockwork sparrows, turned one over in his gloved hand, found it broken, and put it down. Then he came to Ulyana.

"Charms," he said. Not a question.

"Luck-knots, m'lord. Tin moons. Sewn hands." She lifted each item as she named it, letting him see they were only thread and metal and cloth. "For wearing. For giving."

"For working?" His eyes were pale, the color of watered milk, and they went over her the way a butcher goes over a carcass—assessing cuts.

"They're pretty, m'lord. That's all I know about them."

Something flickered across his face—disappointment or suspicion, hard to tell. Inspectors wanted to find something. Finding was how they rose. But there was nothing here, no heat under her skin that his medallion could taste, no mark above her collar, no telltale tremor in her fingers. Eleven years of practice made sure of that. She let her breath come shallow and even. She thought, as she always did during inspections, of absolutely nothing—a gray room, a gray sea, a gray sky. Emptiness as armor.

"Young women who sell charms," the inspector said at last, "often sell other things. Prayers. Blessings. Words said over sick children." He leaned in. The green light made his teeth look like something dredged up. "That's still death, you understand. Godfall law. No exceptions for sentiment."

"I sell knots, m'lord."

He stared at her a moment longer, hunting for the crack in her, the seam. There wasn't one. There never was. Then he straightened, adjusted the medallion at his throat—a disc of cold iron that was supposed to hum in the presence of unlicensed magic—and moved on down the row, leaving the smell of iron and lamp-oil behind him.

Ulyana let out her breath slowly. Under her sleeve, against her skin, the oathmark felt warm. It always did after inspections, as if it were offended at being hidden. As if it wanted to be seen.

*Not yet*, she told it silently, the way she had told it a thousand times. *Not in this city. Not in this life.*

* * *

The widow came near midnight, when the market had thinned to embers and the fishwife was banking her coals.

Ulyana noticed her the way she noticed everyone—sidelong, catalogued, dismissed. A woman perhaps fifty, dressed in mourning grays gone shiny at the elbows, carrying a basket as if she'd forgotten what she'd put in it. She drifted past the stall once, then again, then stopped, looking at the luck-knots without touching them, the way people look at bread when they cannot quite afford it.

"Those are for luck," Ulyana said. "But I'll tell you honestly—they're mostly for hoping. Hope does half the work itself."

The woman's eyes came up. They were red-rimmed, scrubbed raw. "My son," she said, as if continuing a conversation they'd been having for days. "Tam. He was a fisherman. Off the Teeth, in the autumn storms." Her hands twisted in her apron. "They never found him. Three months now. And every night I pray to—" She stopped. Swallowed. "One isn't supposed to say. Godfall law."

"One isn't supposed to do a lot of things," Ulyana said quietly. She found herself reaching for the finest knot on the cloth—red and black and gold, braided tight, finished with a bead of sea-glass she'd traded from a smuggler two winters past. She hadn't meant to pick it up. Her hands had decided before she had. "This one's for grief that won't set, like a bone. You wear it here." She touched her own wrist, just below where the sleeve covered the oathmark. "And when you speak to him—and you may speak to him, grieving isn't illegal yet—you hold it, and you think of him whole. Not in the water. Whole. On dry land. Do you understand me?"

"Whole," the widow repeated. "On dry land."

"That's all a charm is. A thought with a handle on it."

The woman fumbled in her purse and produced coins—too many, worn thin, counted and recounted so often their edges had gone soft. Ulyana took only three and pushed the rest back across the oilcloth.

"The rest buys your supper," she said. "I don't sell to empty purses. Bad luck sticks to a cheated seller."

The widow laughed—a cracked, surprised sound, like ice shifting on a pond—and took the knot with both hands, cradling it. "You're kind," she said. "Under all that gray, you're kind."

"I'm practical," Ulyana said. "Kindness is just practicality stretched over time." But the words sat wrong in her mouth even as she said them, because Sorrel used to say almost exactly that, back when the guildhall had more than ghosts in it, and hearing her own voice borrow his made her chest ache. "Go home. Sleep. Hold the knot and think of him whole."

The widow pressed the knot to her lips—an old gesture, older than the Godfall, older than the laws that had followed it—and walked away into the dark between the lamps.

Ulyana watched her go and felt the familiar hollow open in her chest. This was the trade, the whole rotten bargain of her survival: she sold people handles for their hope, and then she went home alone to a rented room above a dye-shop, and she did not let herself wonder whether any of it meant anything. Wondering was dangerous. Wondering led to testing. Testing led to answers. Answers, in Vharrow, led to pyres.

She was gathering her oilcloth corners when the fishwife spoke.

"Marta," Ulyana said, not turning around, "go home."

"You hear that?"

"Hear what?"

But even as she said it, she heard it. Down the street, past the dead sparrows and the guttering braziers, from the direction of the widow's tenement—a voice. High, cracked with weeping, but unmistakably human, unmistakably *alive*, calling out a name into the night:

*"Mother! Mother, it's me—it's Tam—it's me!"*

Ulyana's hands went still on the oilcloth.

Down the lane, doors banged open. Lamplight bloomed in windows. Somewhere a dog began to bark, and then another, and the street filled with the sound of a woman screaming—not in grief, not anymore, but in a joy so violent it sounded like pain—and above it all, growing louder, footsteps running, wet footprints slapping cobblestone, a young man's voice sobbing *"the sea gave me back, Mother, the sea gave me back—"*

Marta's hand had found Ulyana's arm, gripping hard. "Gods' teeth," the fishwife whispered. "Gods' teeth, girl. Did you—"

"No," Ulyana said. Her voice came out strange and far away. "No. I sold her a knot. Knots don't do that."

Knots don't do that.

But under her sleeve, warm as a coal fresh from the hearth, her oathmark was burning—and somewhere far beneath the city, beneath the mountain, in a dark she had never let herself imagine, something ancient had heard a prayer answered, and Ulyana Kavanagh understood, with a certainty that felt like falling, that nothing in Vharrow would ever be overlooked again.
