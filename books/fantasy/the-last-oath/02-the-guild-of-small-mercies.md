## 2. The Guild of Small Mercies

The guildhall had once been a temple, back when temples were permitted to have names. You could still see it in the bones of the place—the ribs of black oak that held up the roof, the way the windows arched like cupped hands, the faint ghost of gilt in the mortar joints where some long-dead mason had pressed gold leaf into the stone and dared the world to forget what it had been.

The world had not forgotten. The world had simply looked away, which was worse.

Ulyana climbed the hill through the last grey light of evening, her satchel heavy with unsold charms, her boots dark with the mud of Vharrow's lower streets. The rain had stopped an hour ago but the air still wore it, wet wool and coal smoke, and the cobblestones gave back the lamplight in broken pieces. Behind her, the city spread out in its tiers: the harbor at the bottom, all rigging and rust; the middle streets where she sold her luck-knots and fever-wards to people who could not afford to believe in them; and above it all, on its crag, the Crownspire, lit yellow and proud, where men who had outlawed magic sat by fires they did not know how to bless.

She paused at the gate. She always paused at the gate.

It was iron, older than the Godfall, worked into a pattern she'd never been able to fully trace—interlacing lines that resolved, if you stared too long, into something almost like letters, almost like faces. Her sleeve had ridden up as she reached for the latch. She tugged it down without thinking, the way other people scratched an itch or checked for their purse. Beneath the wool, the oathmark lay against her wrist like a second pulse: a small ring interlocked with a small flame, inked into her skin the day she turned seven, warm to the touch and utterly inert.

Or so she had always believed.

Tonight, for no reason she could name, she pressed her thumb against it before opening the gate, and thought she felt—faintly, absurdly—an answering warmth from beyond the wall. From the direction of the hearth.

*Coincidence,* she told herself. *You're tired. Tired people feel things.*

She went in.

* * *

The courtyard smelled of rosemary and ash, which was the smell of the guildhall entire, distilled: the herb beds Sorrel refused to let die, and the fire that refused to die either. Three figures moved in the dusk among the raised beds, bent and slow. Old Tam was cutting rosemary with a knife so worn its blade was more crescent than edge. Beside him, Marta Kessel knelt at a bed of sage, her hands wrapped in rags because her joints wept in cold weather. And near the kitchen door stood Berrin, who had been the guild's bell-ringer before bells were illegal, and who now spent his days carving wooden birds he never gave to anyone.

They saw her come in. They always saw her first; she'd never learned how they did it.

"Little Ulla," Tam said, not looking up from his cutting. "You're late."

"I'm not late. I didn't say I was coming."

"You're always late to things you don't say you're coming to." He held up the rosemary sprig, considered it, and tucked it into her hood as she passed, an old ritual neither of them commented on anymore. "Your hair's wet. Your boots are wet. There's soup on if you want it, and there's Sorrel if you don't."

"Is she in a mood?"

"When is she not?" Marta said from her knees, smiling up with a face like crumpled paper. "The wind shifted this morning and she's been growling at the east wall ever since, as though the wall chose the wind personally."

Berrin said nothing, as usual, but he touched two fingers to his brow when she passed—a bell-ringer's greeting, kept alive now only here—and Ulyana returned it, and felt the small ache she always felt, the ache of loving something that was visibly, patiently dying. The guildhall roof sagged in the middle like a tired back. Moss grew in the gutters. Half the rooms were closed off and full of dust sheets that had themselves begun to rot. There were perhaps forty years left in the building, and fewer than that in its three oldest keepers, and Ulyana tried not to do the arithmetic, and did it anyway, every time.

The Guild of Small Mercies had been, according to Sorrel, many things over the centuries: healers, hedge-witches, charm-sellers, midwives, mourners-for-hire, the people you called when the priest wouldn't come and the problem wasn't big enough for a temple anyway. Small mercies. That was the whole creed, if it could be called one. Not the grand miracles. Not storms turned or plagues lifted. Just the knot tied right, the fever watched through the night, the grave dug deep and the words said kindly. Magic so small the law pretended not to see it, practiced by people too old and too careful to be worth arresting.

And beneath it all—one floor down, behind a door Ulyana had never been permitted to open—a fire that had been burning since before any of them were born.

* * *

Sorrel was in the common room, sitting in the great chair by the hearth with a blanket over her knees and a ledger open on the armrest, and the sight of her hit Ulyana the way it always did: love and fear in the same breath.

Guildmaster Sorrel Ashgrave had once, by all accounts, been formidable. Now she was a sparrow of a woman, eighty-some years folded into a chair, her white hair braided tight against her skull, her eyes still quick and dark as a wren's. The firelight moved on her face and made her look, for a moment, younger than Ulyana had ever known her—and then she glanced up, and the moment passed, and she was simply old again, and watching.

"You're limping," Sorrel said.

"My boot's rubbing."

"Mm." She marked her place in the ledger with a ribbon. "Sit. Warm yourself. You look half-drowned and wholly broke."

"One out of two isn't bad."

"It isn't good, either." But there was something underneath the dryness, the way there always was—Sorrel's affection came disguised as complaint, wrapped in brown paper, and you had to unwrap it carefully or miss it entirely. Ulyana dragged the second chair close to the hearth and sat, and the heat rolled over her, and she sighed despite herself.

Because the fire was *wrong*, in the best possible way. Everyone who visited remarked on it, eventually, if Sorrel let them stay long enough to notice. It burned without wood. Oh, there was a stack of birch logs beside the hearth, cut and seasoned, and occasionally Sorrel would have Tam feed one in—for appearances, for the smell, for the comfort of the ritual—but the flame itself needed none of it. It burned low and steady and golden, and it had burned that way, they said, for longer than the guildhall had stood around it. Longer than Vharrow. Longer, some whispered, than the law that forbade it.

It gave no smoke. It left no ash. When Ulyana held her hands to it, the warmth did not fade with distance quite the way fire should—it clung, gently, like a hand on the shoulder.

And tonight, for the first time in her life, she noticed something else. As she settled into the chair, the flame leaned toward her. A small thing—a tilt, a sway, the way flowers lean after the sun. She froze.

"Sorrel."

"Hm?"

"The fire just—" She gestured, feeling suddenly foolish. "It moved. Toward me."

Sorrel followed her gesture to the hearth. The flame had already straightened, burning innocent and vertical as a candle. For a long moment the old woman said nothing. Then she pulled the blanket higher over her knees.

"Fires flicker," she said.

"That wasn't flickering."

"Then it's the draft. There's a gap in the east window; I've asked Tam to mend it since before you were born." She reopened her ledger. "Eat your soup, Ulyana."

There was no soup yet. There was only the ledger, and the fire, and the silence that Sorrel could build faster than any mason in Vharrow. Ulyana knew every variety of that silence: the bored one, the disappointed one, the one that meant *I am letting you talk yourself out of whatever foolishness you've brought home.* This was none of those. This was the rarest kind—the locked door.

"Why won't you tell me?" Ulyana asked quietly. "About the fire."

"Because you haven't earned the answer."

"I've been coming here since I was six years old. You put the mark on my wrist myself. I've sold your charms in the night market for eleven years, I've hidden from inspectors, I've lied to magistrates, I've—"

"And you've done it well," Sorrel said, and for just a moment her voice softened into something almost tender, almost sorry. "Better than well. That's why, child. Some answers are doors, and once they're open they can't be shut again, and everything on the other side comes through." She looked into the fire, and the firelight made her eyes look wet, though Ulyana knew better than to say so. "Let me keep my one locked room. Every old woman is entitled to one."

"The law says otherwise," Ulyana muttered. "The law says old women aren't entitled to anything."

"The law," said Sorrel, "is a very young thing having a tantrum. It will tire eventually. Things like us learn to wait." She turned a page of the ledger, and Ulyana caught, upside down, a glimpse of columns—names? sums? dates?—before Sorrel angled it away. Another locked room. "Tell me about the market instead. Tell me something small. I've had a day of large silences."

So Ulyana told her. About the widow with the copper-grey braid and the dead son, and the luck-knot, and the prayer that had slipped out of her like blood from a wound. She meant it as a small story, an offering, the sort of thing Sorrel liked—human-sized, market-sized. But somewhere in the telling, Sorrel went very still.

"*Answered,*" the guildmaster said. "You heard it answered."

"I heard—I don't know what I heard. A sound. Like something agreeing."

"Describe it."

"I can't. It wasn't a voice. More like... a bell rung underwater. Or a door opening somewhere below you." Ulyana laughed, uncertain. "That sounds mad."

But Sorrel wasn't laughing. Sorrel had gone the color of tallow, and her hand had found the arm of the chair and gripped it, and the fire—the fire had gone strange again, leaning, straining toward the old woman this time, flames bending horizontal as if drawn by some current Ulyana couldn't feel.

"No," Sorrel said softly. To whom, Ulyana couldn't tell. Perhaps to herself. Perhaps to the fire. "*No.* Not yet. It's too soon."

"Sorrel—"

"Nothing." The old woman straightened, and the mask came down over her face, smooth and terrible in its composure. "Old women startle easily. We mistake our own hearts for omens." She closed the ledger at last and set it aside, and her hand, Ulyana noticed, was trembling—not much. Only a little. Only exactly enough to be seen by someone who had been reading Sorrel Ashgrave's hands since childhood. "It's late, and you're soaked, and there's a bed upstairs that hasn't been aired since the last time you stayed. Go up. Sleep. In the morning—"

"In the morning?"

A pause. The fire cracked, though there was no wood in it to crack.

"In the morning," Sorrel said, "we'll talk about your future."

* * *

The guest room was cold and smelled of lavender and mouse, and Ulyana lay awake in it far longer than she meant to, listening to the house settle.

She had grown up in this building, in a manner of speaking. Sent here at six, an orphan of the river-fever year, dropped at the gate by a cousin who couldn't feed her. She remembered Tam teaching her knots, Marta teaching her herbs, Berrin teaching her—without a single word—that silence could be a gift and not a wound. And Sorrel teaching her everything else: how to read a customer, how to hide a mark, how to make a charm that was mostly kindness and only slightly magic, because *slightly* was survivable and *mostly* would get you hanged.

And always, always, the locked doors. The cellar stair with its rope across it. The ledger she'd never been allowed to touch. The fire that no one would explain, tended by three aged keepers of a guild that had maybe a decade left in it, guarding something with the devotion of priests and the secrecy of criminals.

She had assumed, all these years, that the secrets were griefs. That the guild had lost so much—its name, its rights, its purpose, its members, one by one by one—that hoarding its mysteries was the only dignity left to it. She had found that sad, and forgivable, and hers to inherit someday along with the sagging roof and the rosemary beds.

Tonight, lying in the dark, she turned over the sound the widow's prayer had made. *A door opening somewhere below you.*

Below the city. Below everything.

And she thought of the fire leaning toward her like a flower toward the sun, and the oathmark warm on her wrist for the first time in nineteen years, and Sorrel's face going white as bone as she whispered *not yet, it's too soon*—

—as if something had been expected. As if something had been dreaded. As if the guildmaster had been counting down to this, quietly, for years, the way you count the breaths of someone dying.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, Ulyana heard voices. Tam and Marta, low and urgent, and beneath them Sorrel's voice, thin and hard as wire. She couldn't make out the words. Only the tone—which was not the tone of an old woman scolding the wind.

It was the tone of someone arguing with fate, and losing.

Ulyana got up, dressed in the dark, and started down the stairs toward the cellar door, where the rope had been taken down.
