## 4. Sorrel's Last Confession

The fire in the guildhall had burned for two hundred years without ever being fed, and Sorrel Kavanagh had spent forty of those years keeping its secret the way a miser keeps coins: close, counted, and never spoken of in company.

Now he lay dying on a cot dragged too close to the hearth, and the fire leaned toward him like a listener.

He could feel it leaning. That was the cruelty of the thing—after four decades of tending, the ember knew him better than his own daughter did. It warmed the left side of his face where the right had gone cold hours ago, when the shaking started and would not stop. He had sent the others away. Old Maren with her arthritic hands, young Tam with his stammering piety. *Go home,* he'd told them. *I'll ring if I need anything.* They had gone, because they were guild to the bone and a guildmaster's word was still law even when it was a lie.

Ulyana had not gone.

She sat where she always sat when she visited—on the three-legged stool by the window, mending something that didn't need mending, because her hands required occupation the way other people's lungs required air. He had watched her thread the needle twice and miss twice, and he understood that she was watching him back through the pretense, the way she had watched everything since she was small enough to hide under this same table during inspections.

*Tell her or don't tell her.* The thought turned over and over like a stone in a streambed.

He was dying. He could feel the shape of it now, the way you feel weather coming in an old wound. There would not be another chance after this one, and there had never been a good one.

"Ulyana."

"Mm?" She didn't look up. Good. He needed her not looking at him for the first part, at least.

"Fetch me the ledger. Third shelf, behind the jar of salt."

Her needle stopped. "The debt-book? Why do you want that now?"

*Because I am out of time and cowardice is a luxury the dead can't afford.*

"Please, girl."

Something in his voice must have reached her, because she set down the mending—actually set it down, folded around the needle—and crossed the room. She was twenty-three years old and moved like someone perpetually expecting to be grabbed from behind; she hugged the wall as she walked, kept herself between the window and the door. He had taught her that without meaning to, all those years of flinching at hoofbeats, of dousing lamps, of pressing her small fingers against her oathmark until the ink went pale. He had taught her fear instead of craft, and now there was no time left to teach her anything but truth.

The ledger came off the shelf trailing dust and salt-grain. It was a plain book, calfskin gone soft as cloth, bound in iron corners that had rusted to the color of dried blood. She held it without opening it, waiting.

"Sit," he said. "Here. Where I can see you."

She sat on the edge of the cot. The firelight caught the edge of her jaw, the stubborn line of her mouth—her mother's mouth, gods rest Edda, who had died not knowing either, and thank every cold star in the sky for that mercy at least.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Sorrel said. "And then you're going to hate me, and you're going to be right to."

"Sorrel." His name in her mouth—not *father*, never *father*, not in forty years, though the whole city believed she was only his ward and he had let them believe it because names were dangerous things to give a girl already carrying a mark on her wrist. "Whatever it is—"

"The Godfall wasn't an ending," he said. "It was a bargain."

The words came easier than he feared. Perhaps because he had rehearsed them in the dark for decades, lying in this same hall, listening to the fire breathe.

"You know the official version. The gods grew proud, the heavens cracked, and magic fell out of the world like ash. The crown outlawed it to protect us from ourselves. Every child learns it before they learn their letters." He coughed, and the cough went on longer than he liked, wet and deep. Ulyana's hand hovered near his shoulder, not quite touching. "It's a story we helped write. The guild wrote it, actually. We paid the chroniclers ourselves."

"Why?"

"Because the truth was worse than shame. The truth was guilt." He closed his eyes. The fire pressed warmth against his eyelids like a palm. "The old gods weren't dying, girl. They were starving. Magic isn't a substance—it's a circulation. Prayer flows up, grace flows down, everything moves. And the gods above had grown greedy in their age, drinking deeper every century, demanding more blood, more burning, more of everything. You've read what the old rites looked like. Don't make that face—you have, I've seen Ilse's smuggled texts in your bag. Firstborn sons. Cities given over to ecstasy until they tore themselves apart. That was the price of grace, once."

"And the guild stopped paying it."

"The guild *renegotiated*. Two hundred years ago, when the last dragons were dying and the gods' hunger was about to eat the world whole, seven of us—the founding masters—went beneath Greyhollow Mountain, into the Undercourt, where bargains are binding and lies hurt your tongue like biting iron. And we made a deal with the regent there." He opened his eyes and found her watching him now, needle-still. "We gave them souls. Five of them, pledged across generations. In exchange, the Undercourt took up the burden of feeding the god-fragments—starved them slowly, kept them sleeping, siphoned just enough worship through the mountain to keep them dreaming instead of waking ravenous. And the dragon-fire—" he nodded at the hearth, at the flame that had never once guttered, never once been fed, "—the last living ember in the world. We brought it here. It's the lock on the whole arrangement. While it burns, the hunger stays below. When it dies—"

He stopped. Not for breath. For courage.

"When it dies, everything wakes up hungry."

The silence that followed had texture. He could feel Ulyana in it, feel her mind moving fast behind her still face, sorting, cataloguing, refusing.

"That's why the fire matters," she said quietly. "That's why you never let anyone else tend it alone. That's why it never goes out."

"It goes out when the last soul-debt is paid or forgiven. Those are the only two doors."

"Paid how?"

Sorrel laughed, and it cost him, and he paid anyway. "You always did ask the question under the question."

"Answer it."

*There she is. There's the spine Edda gave her.*

"The ledger," he said. "Open it."

She did. Her hands were steady—he noticed that with a strange, distant pride—as she turned the soft pages past the inventory lists, the charm-recipes, the cipher keys, to the section at the back written in the founding hand. He watched her lips move slightly as she read the heading: *Obligations Outstanding.*

Five entries. Four struck through in the old red ink, each cancellation dated, each initialed. One entry clean and unmarked, its date blank except for a single word in the space where a due-date should sit:

*When the fire gutters.*

"Read the name," Sorrel said.

She read it silently first. He saw the exact moment it landed—her breath caught on nothing, a small mechanical hitch, and the book trembled once in her grip before she mastered it. Then she read it aloud, because she was her mother's daughter and she would not look away from a thing just because it was terrible.

"Kavanagh. Ulyana Kavanagh. Pledged at birth." Her voice stayed level. Only her knuckles betrayed her, going white around the ledger's spine. "Pledged by whom?"

"By me."

* * *

The fire had been leaning toward her for some minutes before Ulyana consciously registered it—that slow, attentive tilt of the flames, as if the hearth itself had turned its head.

She barely felt it. She was looking at the entry in the ledger, and then at Sorrel, and then at the ledger again, as if repetition might rearrange the words into something survivable.

"Pledged at birth," she said. "Mine was pledged at my *birth*. Which means—" and the arithmetic assembled itself with horrible neatness, each number falling into place like bones into a grave, "—which means whoever pledged me knew me before I existed. Before I could consent to anything. Before I was anything at all except possible."

"Yes."

"Only family pledges family. That's the oldest rule of binding. Even Ilse's forbidden texts agree on that much. Blood answers for blood." She looked up. The firelight made his face into something carved and ancient, all hollows and shadow, and underneath it she could see the death already working, the gray creeping in from the edges of him. "So which is it, Sorrel? Am I your daughter, or am I your collateral?"

His eyes closed. A tear slid from the corner of one and vanished into the pillow's seam, and she hated herself for noticing it, hated the twist of pity that came with it, because pity would soften her and she could not afford to be softened. Not now. Not with her own name sitting in a debt-book like a line item.

"Both," he whispered. "Edda was my daughter. My true-born daughter, before the guild went underground, before the purges. We hid her inside the ward-story, called her my ward's wife, let the neighbors think what neighbors think. And when Edda told us she was carrying you—" his breath hitched, rattled, steadied, "—the court came to collect. The fourth soul had just been reclaimed; the Undercourt wanted its fifth named within the year, and the regent's heralds had begun walking in Vharrow's dreams. If no name was given freely, they would take one. Take a child from anywhere. Any crib in the city. That was the threat. That was the *terms*."

"So you gave them me."

"We gave them a promise that was never meant to be kept." His voice found strength from somewhere—shame, maybe, or the last hot coal of a father's desperation. "The pledge was structured to fail, Ulyana. Do you understand? The debt falls due when the fire gutters, and the fire was never supposed to gutter. It was supposed to burn past all our lifetimes, past yours, past your children's. We bought time. That's all the guild has ever done—buy time with promises we prayed we'd never have to keep. Your mother agreed. Your mother *chose* it. She said—" and here his voice broke, truly broke, into the register of a man quoting scripture, "—she said, *better one name owed than one child devoured.*"

Ulyana stood. She didn't decide to stand; her body simply removed itself from the cot, put distance between her skin and his voice, carried her to the window where the night lay black over the rooftops of Vharrow. Somewhere below, the night market was packing up. Somewhere a widow she had sold a luck-knot to was praying, and something was answering.

"My soul," she said. She heard her own voice as if from outside—a stranger's voice, thin and precise. "My actual soul. Not my service, not my name. The thing that makes me *me*. It's been owned since before I drew breath, and none of you—" she turned, and the word came out with edges, "—none of you *told* me. Forty years, Sorrel. Twenty-three years of my life. I slept under this roof. I learned my letters at this table. I bled on these floors when I cut my palms learning knotwork, and you watched me bleed and said nothing, knowing every drop belonged to something under a mountain."

"We were protecting you—"

"From *knowing yourself*?" The word cracked out of her louder than she intended, and the fire flared—all along the hearth the flames leapt a handspan higher, snapping, and both of them froze. Slowly, the fire settled. But it did not stop leaning toward her. It leaned toward her the way a dog leans toward its master's hand.

Sorrel saw it too. She watched him see it, watched the understanding move across his ruined face.

"It knows you," he said softly. "Gods forgive us. The ember knows its owner."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't dress it in reverence. It's a *debt*. I'm a debt. Twenty-three years I've hidden a mark on my wrist from men who'd burn me for less than what I actually am—I'm not even a mage, I'm *property*, and I found out from a ledger while the man who signed me away dies comfortably by the fire he never let me tend alone."

"Ulyana—"

"How much time?" she asked abruptly. "How long does the ember have? How long do *I* have?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then, with the care of a man laying down a heavy load for the last time:

"A season. Perhaps two. The fire has been drawing down faster since the prayers started answering again—the balance is slipping, the court is pulling harder on everything it holds. Maren felt it in the flame a month ago. So did I. That's when the shaking started, girl. The guildmaster doesn't die of a fever. He dies when his fire starts dying with him." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I'm collateral too. Just an older installment."

Ulyana pressed her back against the cool stone of the window frame. Outside, far off toward the river, a bell tolled—some temple, some watch-hour, meaningless. Inside, the ledger lay open on the cot, her name naked in the firelight.

Grief and betrayal were not separate feelings, she discovered. They occupied the same body. Grief said: *he is dying, he raised you, he taught you knots and ciphers and which inspectors take bribes, he loves you in the only language he was ever permitted.* Betrayal said: *and he owned you the entire time, and love was the leash.* Both spoke at once. Neither yielded. She understood suddenly why the guild's oldest texts called such moments *the weighing*—because that was exactly what it was, her heart swinging between pans, and neither side would come down.

"I need to see the terms," she said at last. Her voice came out flat and workmanlike, and she was grateful to it. "All of them. Every clause, every codicil, whatever Ilse copied before she ran. If a debt exists, it exists in a contract, and contracts have seams. Loopholes. Conditions." She pushed off from the wall and crossed back to the cot, and she picked up the ledger, and she looked down at the old man who was her grandfather and her creditor and the only father she had ever known, all three at once, irreconcilable.

"There's a way out of this," she said. "There's always a seam."

Sorrel's breath came ragged now, audible in the quiet hall, and when he spoke it was in fragments, the sentences coming apart in his mouth like wet paper.

"No seam... in an Undercourt bond. That's what the founders learned. What's given below stays below... unless returned willingly." His hand crept out from the blanket, trembling, and found hers, and gripped it with shocking strength. "Listen to me. Listen. Whatever the heralds offer you—and they will find you now, the fire knows you, they'll smell their claim on you like smoke—whatever they offer, there will be a trade in it. Another soul. A shortcut. A friend's name. That's how they collect. They don't take, girl. They get you to *hand it over*, and handing is consent, and consent makes it forever."

"Then I won't hand anything over."

"You'll want to." His eyes were bright and terrible and clear. "Someone you love, bleeding, and a voice saying *one name for another*—you'll want to. Promise me. Promise me on the knot I taught you first, the luck-knot, the one that holds."

She promised. She said it aloud, the old words, her fingers finding the pattern in the air between them—loop, cross, hold fast—and the fire settled low and warm as if satisfied by the oath, and that satisfaction frightened her more than anything else that night.

Sorrel smiled then, really smiled, and his grip loosened, and he began to speak more slowly, giving her the rest in pieces: the descent road beneath Greyhollow, the sign of the half-mad priest who tended the deep shrine, the word that opened Ilse's old cipher-box in the archive. She memorized all of it with the cold, bright efficiency of a woman building a bridge as the flood comes.

Near dawn, he slept. Near dawn, the fire burned low but did not gutter, and she sat beside it with the ledger open on her knees, reading her own name again and again until the letters lost their meaning and became simply marks—ink, contract, weight.

*Unless returned willingly.*

They wanted her soul handed over like a purse. Very well. Then she would walk down to the Undercourt herself, and she would take it back with her own hands, and she would leave nothing willingly behind—not her name, not her blood, not so much as a hair.

The fire flickered. In its light, the unstruck entry seemed almost to glow.

Outside, the first bells of morning rang across Vharrow—and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, rising from somewhere south toward the river district, came the sound of screaming.
