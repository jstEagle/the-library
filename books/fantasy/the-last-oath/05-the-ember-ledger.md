## 5. The Ember Ledger

Sorrel had been dead three days, and the fire in the guildhall still refused to go out.

Ulyana noticed it the way you notice a stranger's breathing in an empty room. She had come back to wash the body and dress it for burning—there were no priests left who would take the work, and the crown's inspectors would ask fewer questions about a private cremation than about a burial—but every time she passed the great hearth, the flame seemed to lean toward her. Not flickering. *Leaning.* Like a dog that knows its master's step on the stair.

"Stop that," she told it, and felt foolish, and did it anyway twice more before she finished with Sorrel.

The old woman lay on the trestle where they'd laid her, her hands folded over the small iron key she'd pressed into Ulyana's palm with her last lucid breath. *The ledger, girl. Under the third stone from the hearth. Count what's owed. Count what's yours.* Her voice had been a thread drawn through smoke. *Then decide if you're brave or only stubborn.*

Ulyana was fairly sure she knew which one she was. Stubbornness had kept her fed through nineteen years of selling luck-knots and fever-charms in Vharrow's night markets, keeping her sleeve pulled low, keeping her name out of ledgers. Bravery was something that happened to other people—people like Wren Calloway, who wore the crown's silver badge and called it friendship.

She washed Sorrel's face with warm water and vinegar. The oathmark on her own forearm itched beneath its wrapping, the way it always did when she thought too hard about the guild. Five interlocking rings, inked into her skin at birth by hands she couldn't remember, in a hall she'd been carried into as an infant and carried out of at six when the last of the raids drove them underground. She had spent her whole life pretending the mark was a scar. Scars didn't hum.

*Count what's owed.*

The guildhall's back room had been Sorrel's study for forty years, and it smelled of all of them: tallow, dried yarrow, the ghost of pipe-smoke, dust that had learned to be furniture. Books lined the walls in languages Ulyana half-recognized from childhood lessons she'd resented. A cot in the corner still held the shape of a sleeping woman. On the desk, someone—Sorrel, surely, in her final week—had left a cup of tea gone to cold oil on its surface, as though she'd expected to come back.

Under the third flagstone from the hearth—she counted twice, knuckles brushing soot—Ulyana found a cavity lined with waxed linen, and inside it a book bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its corners worn round as river stones.

It was warm. That was the first wrong thing. The room was cold enough to see her breath, and the book sat in her hands like a sleeping bird, faintly blood-warm, and she nearly put it down.

*Count.*

She cleared the desk instead. Moved the cold tea. Sat in Sorrel's chair because standing made her knees untrustworthy, and opened the cover.

The first page was not paper. It was vellum stretched thin enough to see light through, and the writing on it was not ink. It moved. Letters of dark gold crawled and resettled like insects disturbed under bark, and Ulyana's stomach turned over slowly, the way it does on a boat, and she understood that she was holding something older than the Godfall, something the inspectors would burn the whole street to possess, and she could not make herself let go.

At the top of the page, in a hand she recognized—Sorrel's, but younger, steadier—was written:

**THE EMBER LEDGER. BEING THE TRUE ACCOUNTS OF THE GUILD OF SMALL MERCIES, KEPT IN OBEDIENCE TO THE FIRST OATH.**

Below it, five lines. Five entries. Each one written in that crawling gold script, each one bearing a name, a date, and a word that made her breath go shallow: *Pledged.*

She read the first four quickly, hungry and afraid.

*Maren Ostell, pledged in the year of the Falling Stars. Reclaimed.* And beside *reclaimed*, in different ink—a brown, ordinary ink, human ink—was written *paid in full, 40 years' service below*, and a date, and nothing else. No note of what service. No note of where.

*Tomas Wrenfield, pledged the same year. Reclaimed. Paid in full, 61 years.*

*Ilse Marrow, pledged. Reclaimed. Terms ongoing.* Ilse. Ilse, who had left for the convent at Greyhollow nine years ago and sent exactly two letters, both polite, both empty. *Terms ongoing.* Ulyana read that phrase five times. It did not become less terrible. Somewhere under a nun's habit, Ilse Marrow was still paying.

*Bram Halloway, pledged. Reclaimed.* And next to this entry, in ink so fresh it still shone, in handwriting that shook: *Collected early. Forgive us. — S.*

Three days ago. The man they'd found burned from the inside, ash in the shape of a person, the door sealed with inspector's wax by morning. *Forgive us,* Sorrel had written, as though Bram had been a debt being called in rather than a friend being taken, and perhaps—to whatever held the ledger open—both things were true.

Four entries. Four souls sold to something, four reclaimed or collected or ground away across decades of *service below*. Service below what? Below the mountain? Below the world?

Ulyana turned the page.

The fifth entry was longer than the others. The gold letters here were larger, denser, arranged in columns of contract-language that made her eyes water if she tried to follow their logic too far. But the opening lines were plain enough, plain as a knife:

*Kavanagh, Ulyana. Pledged at birth, by consent of the guild, as surety against the Ember Debt. To be surrendered upon the guttering of the Last Fire, or upon demand of the Court Beneath Greyhollow, whichever comes sooner.*

Her name. Her whole name, written in fire-script that shifted when she looked directly at it, and beneath it, where the other entries said *reclaimed* or *paid*, hers said only:

***Outstanding.***

The word pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Ulyana set the book down on the desk very carefully, stood up, walked to the window, opened it, and was quietly sick into the gutter.

---

The night air helped. Vharrow's smells helped—cabbage water and lamp oil and the river's iron tang, the ordinary stink of the living city. She hung in the window frame until her arms stopped shaking, watching a drunk argue affectionately with a lamplighter three streets down, and made herself think in lists, because lists were how she survived everything.

List one: Sorrel had known. Sorrel had always known. Every kindness—the charms lessons disguised as knotwork games, the warnings to keep her magic small and secret, the way the old woman's eyes had followed her around the hall for nineteen years like a woman watching a candle burn down—all of it had been a keeper minding collateral. Ulyana had loved her. She examined the love now the way you probe a tooth, looking for the rot, and found to her own fury that the love was still there, whole, tangled up with the betrayal past any hope of separating them. Sorrel had signed her away before she could walk. Sorrel had also taught her every trick she knew, fed her, hidden her, died holding her hand. Both true. Both true forever.

List two: *whichever comes sooner.* The Last Fire. The flame in the hearth that never went out, that leaned toward her like a dog. The flame Sorrel had refused to explain, that three aged guild members had kept fed for decades with wood and prayer and something else besides. It wasn't a hearth-fire. It had never been a hearth-fire. It was a countdown.

List three: the widow. Marta Ellery, who'd bought a luck-knot from Ulyana eight nights ago and prayed over it, and whose dead son had walked home whole yesterday—whole, and hollow-eyed, answering questions a half-beat late, smiling without moving his face. Prayers were being answered again all over Vharrow; Wren's arrival proved the crown thought so too. And every answered prayer, Uyana would have bet her last coin, was drawing down the same account. Something below was spending. Something below was getting ready to collect.

She closed the window. She went back to the desk. She picked up the ledger again, and this time she forced herself to read the fifth entry all the way through—not the contract-columns, she wasn't ready, maybe she'd never be ready—but the marginal notes, Sorrel's handwriting in human brown ink, added over years, the record of a woman arguing with a document.

*The fire holds while the debt stands,* one note read. *They cannot take her while we pay. Keep the flame. Keep the flame. KEEP THE FLAME.*

And lower, smaller, dated—Ulyana checked the style of the numerals—only a few years ago:

*She has grown kind. This is worse. Kind ones bargain. I pray she never reads this book, and I pray she reads it soon. Forgive me, child. We meant to die before you came due.*

*We meant to die before you came due.* And then Sorrel had died anyway, and the flame leaned toward Ulyana through the doorway like something hungry, and somewhere beneath the mountain a court of something ancient held a promissory note with her soul written on it.

Ulyana sat in the dead woman's chair, in the dead woman's study, holding the dead woman's confession, and did not cry. Crying was for people with time.

* * *

She burned Sorrel at dawn, alone, on the guildhall's stone dock where the barges used to unload charm-goods back when charm-goods had been legal. There was no rite she properly remembered, so she invented one: salt in the river-water, a knot tied and cut, words spoken in the half-language of her childhood that she'd pretended for nineteen years to have forgotten. The fire took Sorrel swiftly, hungrily, and Ulyana chose—with a clarity that frightened her—not to look too hard at why the flames burned green at the edges, or why, far off across the rooftops, the mountain above Greyhollow seemed to lean closer to watch.

When it was done she went back inside and stood in front of the great hearth.

The Last Fire was low. She hadn't noticed how low. In her childhood it had roared; the guild members had fed it constantly, singing to it, complaining about it, treating it like a bad-tempered grandparent. Now it burned no taller than a candle-flame, crouched in its own coals, and when she knelt before it, it bent toward her face like a flower toward sun.

"You're dying," she said aloud. "That's what Sorrel wouldn't say. You're the payment clock, and you're running out."

The fire did not answer. Fires don't. But the heat of it touched her cheek with something almost like recognition, and beneath her wrappings the oathmark flared hot enough to sting, and in the ledger behind her—she heard it, distinctly, impossibly—a gold letter scratched itself deeper into vellum, the sound of a quill made of nail and need.

Due when the last dragon-fire gutters out. Due *soon*. Due, perhaps, already due, and only the habit of an old woman's feeding keeping the hour at bay.

Ulyana sat back on her heels.

Every instinct she'd honed in nineteen years of survival said the same thing: run. Take the ledger, take the key, take whatever coin the guildhall hid, and run south past the tax-houses and the inspection stations until Vharrow was a story she told. Someone else's debt. Someone else's war. She'd been invisible her whole life; invisibility worked.

But the widow's son had smiled without moving his face. And Bram had burned from the inside. And Ilse's entry said *terms ongoing*, and there were only so many guild members left, and every single one of them was a door the court could knock on when it grew impatient—and it was growing impatient, she could feel it the way you feel weather changing, pressure behind the ears, the mountain leaning in.

If she ran, the court would collect her eventually. Or it would collect the others first, one by one, *forgive us, forgive us*, until the ledger was clean except for her.

Or—

She looked at the fifth entry. ***Outstanding.*** A debt outstanding was a debt that could be *settled*. The other four hadn't waited to be taken. They'd been reclaimed. Paid. Traded. Which meant there were terms, and terms meant loopholes, and loopholes meant—

"I'm going to steal it back," Ulyana said to the fire.

Her voice sounded small in the great cold hall, and absolutely certain, and those two facts surprised her equally.

"Not wait. Not hide. Not hope some clerk misfiles my soul. I'm going to go under that mountain, into your court, into whatever hole keeps the accounts—" the oathmark burned, and she pressed her wrapped wrist flat against the hearthstone, and the fire licked up gladly around her knuckles without harming her, warm as a held hand, "*—and I'm going to take what's mine."

The flame rose. For one moment it stood tall in the hearth as it hadn't stood in nineteen years, roaring, gold-edged, glad—and in its light Ulyana saw, cast huge and wavering on the far wall, the shadow of wings.

Then it settled, low and patient again. Waiting. Counting.

Behind her, in the silent study, the ledger turned its own page.

Ulyana stood, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, and began, methodically, to pack.
