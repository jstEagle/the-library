## 6. A Hunter Named Wren

The coach from Vharrow smelled of leather polish and the inspector's fear, and Wren Calloway rode in it the way she rode in everything—upright, gloved, and listening.

Three days out of the capital, past the salt flats where the wind combed the grass flat as a dead man's hair, the prayers had started. That was the official phrasing, anyway, stamped in red wax on her warrant: *investigate reports of answered intercession in the districts of Greyhollow Road and the Vharrow night market.* The clerks who wrote it had never heard a prayer get answered. Wren had. Once. When she was nine years old, kneeling beside Ulyana Kavanagh in the reeds behind the tannery, both of them whispering to a river that hadn't been a god in sixty years—and the river had lifted its head.

She had spent twenty years since learning not to think about that afternoon. The crown paid her well to unthink things.

"Inspector." The driver's voice through the little hatch, rough with nerves. "Greyhollow crossroads ahead. There's—there's people at the shrine."

Wren looked out the window. The roadside shrine stood where it had always stood, a squat stone hooded figure worn faceless by rain, its offering bowl dry for longer than anyone living could remember. She'd passed it a hundred times as a girl. Now there were eleven people kneeling around it in the mud, and the bowl was running over—not with coins but with water, clear water rising out of bare stone, and an old man was cupping his hands in it and weeping like his heart would shake loose.

The driver pulled up. "Should I wait, Inspector?"

"Drive on," Wren said. "I'll walk back."

She stepped down into the cold and watched the coach rattle away before she approached, because a mage-hunter arriving in a crown coach turned a miracle into an interrogation, and she wanted—just for a moment—to look at it first with the eyes she'd had at nine.

The kneeling folk didn't notice her at first. They were praying. Not the careful, sanctioned petitions of the Reformed Liturgy, which asked nothing of anyone because asking was how you got taken. These were raw, greedy, desperate prayers: *heal my knee, bring him home, let the baby sleep.* And the stone figure—faceless, dead, a chunk of rock older than the Godfall—was *listening*. Wren felt it the way you feel a draft under a door: a faint cool pressure against her trained senses, something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the earth.

One woman had a child in her arms, a small boy gray-faced with fever, and she was rocking him and begging in a voice scraped down to the grain. As Wren watched, the fever broke. It broke visibly—the flush draining, the boy's breathing easing, his mother's sob cracking into laughter. The woman pressed her lips to his hair and said, half-delirious, "*Thank you, thank you,*" and somewhere below their feet, something took a slow, satisfied breath.

Wren's hand went to the warrant tube at her belt without her deciding anything.

*Something is taking payment*, she thought. Every hunter knew the oldest rule of the craft, taught before swordwork or spellwork: *magic is a trade, and the trader always keeps the ledger.* The crown's law wasn't superstition. Magic answered, and then magic collected, and the Godfall had been what happened when gods stopped trading and started devouring. Sixty years of ash and screaming, ended by the Edict and the hunters who enforced it.

But this didn't feel like devouring. This felt like a shop opening its shutters after decades dark. Patient. Polite. *Come in, come in, we've missed you.*

It was worse, somehow. Devouring you could fight.

"Inspector Calloway?" A village constable had come puffing up the road, red-faced, relieved. "Thank the saints. We sent word three days ago—people are saying the shrines are waking, and there's talk in the taverns about—" He lowered his voice, glancing at the kneeling crowd. "About the old days. About whether the Edict still holds."

"It holds," Wren said.

"Yes, Inspector." He didn't sound convinced. Neither, entirely, did she.

* * *

She worked the crossroads village for two days, and it was like walking through a house where someone had just left.

The signs were everywhere if you knew how to read them. Candle stubs burned to the socket in front of sealed hearth-gods. Fresh chalk marks on door lintels, old warding signs redrawn by hands that remembered the strokes. In the tavern, an ancient fiddler played a tune no one had dared play since the Godfall—a calling-song, meant to invite a hearth-spirit to sit and warm itself—and nobody stopped him, and halfway through, every candle in the room leaned toward him like flowers finding the sun.

And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, a name spoken in whispers: *the night market in Vharrow. The charm-woman. She knew before any of us. She must have known.*

Wren heard the name on the second evening, and it went through her like a fishhook.

*Kavanagh.*

There were a thousand Kavanaghs in the world. There was exactly one Ulyana Kavanagh, and Wren had carried the memory of her for twenty years like a splinter too deep to dig out: a thin, fierce girl with ink-stained fingers and an oathmark she thought nobody knew about, who could make a luck-knot hold true when every other charm on the street was paste and string. Who had once sworn—at fourteen, furious, crying—that she would never take the guild's oath, never be branded, never give the crown a reason.

Who had taken it anyway. Wren had seen the mark herself, years later, glimpsed beneath a sleeve at a funeral neither of them had wanted to attend. Guild of Small Mercies. The smallest, poorest, most stubborn order of hedge-mages in Vharrow, licensed to sell charms of negligible power and forbidden everything else. Ulyana had chosen the mark over the freedom, and Wren had never understood why, and had never asked, because some questions you don't ask the person you love most in the world when you're wearing the other side's uniform.

Now her warrant pointed straight at the night market, and through it, straight at her.

*You can route this,* she told herself, sitting alone in the constable's back office with the warrant unrolled before her. *You can write it up as mass hysteria, weather-sense, contaminated wells. Hunters do it. Half the craft survives on paperwork that says nothing.* She picked up the pen. She held it over the page for a long time.

Then she thought of the fevered boy, and the satisfied breath beneath the earth, and the ledger being kept somewhere in the dark by something patient and polite, and she wrote: *Charm-seller operating in Vharrow night market may have prior knowledge. Recommend interview.*

Not arrest. Interview. The word sat on the page looking exactly like what it was: a door held open.

* * *

Vharrow at dusk was a city pretending it had never been afraid. Lanterns along the canal bridges, fish-smoke and frying oil, the night market unfolding in Tanner's Square like a flower made of noise. Wren came into it plain-clothes, coat collar up, hunter's badge left in her lodgings—for now—and let the crowd carry her past the charm stalls.

She found the stall before she found the woman. She'd know that handiwork anywhere: the knots tied in a pattern too neat to be luck and too subtle to be showy, the little tin tokens stamped with a wren in flight. *A wren in flight.* Ulyana had made her one of those when they were twelve, swearing it would keep her from ever getting lost. Wren still had it. It was in the box with her mother's ring and the river-pebble, and she had never told a soul.

Ulyana stood at the stall's edge selling a folded paper charm to a young widow—Wren clocked the black dress, the smudged eyes, the way grief makes buyers of everyone—and she was thinner than Wren remembered, sharper, her dark hair pinned back carelessly, her left sleeve buttoned at the wrist though the evening was mild.

Hiding the mark even here. Even now. Twenty years, and she was still hiding it.

Wren bought nothing. She simply stood at the edge of the lamplight until Ulyana glanced up out of habit, the way sellers do, checking the next purse in the crowd—

—and froze.

For three full seconds neither of them moved. The market roared on around them. Somewhere a fiddle struck up, badly. Ulyana's hand, still holding the widow's change, slowly closed around the coins, and Wren saw her do the arithmetic every mage did when a stranger's gaze lingered too long: *run, bluff, or fight.* Then recognition arrived, and Wren watched the arithmetic collapse.

"Wren," Ulyana said. Just the name, barely above the noise. "They told me you'd made inspector."

"They told me you'd made guildsworn." Wren nodded at the sleeve. Her voice came out gentler than she intended. "Full marks hidden under wool, Ulya. You used to say you'd rather be hanged."

"I said a lot of things at fourteen." Ulyana handed the widow her change and touched her elbow—a small kindness, *go home, love*—and the widow drifted off into the crowd. Ulyana didn't come around the stall. She stayed behind it, the way a person stays behind a wall they've built themselves. "Are you here to buy a charm, Inspector? Or are you here because of the prayers?"

Straight to it. No warmth first. That hurt more than it should have.

"You heard I was coming?"

"I hear everything in this square. It's how I've stayed alive." Ulyana's eyes flicked past Wren's shoulder, cataloguing the crowd, the exits, the two men loitering by the fountain who might have been nothing. "So. Which is it?"

Wren stepped closer, close enough to speak under the fiddle. "I filed it as an interview."

"A what?"

"That's hunter's grammar, Ulya. It means I told the crown I needed to *talk* to a person of interest before deciding what they were." She kept her hands loose and visible at her sides, an old courtesy between them, from the days when Ulyana's startle-flare had once scorched a fence rail and both their eyebrows. "It means you have a few days. Maybe less, if the district captain gets ambitious."

Ulyana stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes—gratitude, suspicion, and under both of them, the old fierce tenderness that had survived twenty years of silence on both sides. "You're burning yourself doing this."

"I'm filing accurate reports."

"You're lying to your superiors for a charm-seller you haven't spoken to since Sorrel's wife's funeral."

"Six years," Wren said. "And you didn't answer my letters after."

"Neither did you."

"I signed mine *Wren*. Yours would have gone in a file." It came out harder than she meant. The hurt underneath it, older than the argument, showed its teeth for a moment between them, and both of them flinched from it.

Around the stall, the market flowed on. A child shrieked happily over fried dough. The lanterns swayed. And beneath the cobbles, beneath the city, beneath everything, Wren felt again that faint cool pressure, that vast thing turning over in its sleep, closer here than it had been at the crossroads. Closer, perhaps, to Ulyana than to anyone.

"The prayers aren't hysteria," Wren said quietly. "I've seen three answered myself. Fevers broken. Water from dry stone. An old man's knees made new." She hesitated. "And every time, I felt something... take note. Like a clerk marking a line in a book. The magic isn't loose, Ulya. It's *selling*. And I need to know why a charm-woman in Tanner's Square knew it was coming before the crown did."

For a moment—half a heartbeat—fear crossed Ulyana's face. Real fear, the kind Wren had only seen twice before: once at the river when the god lifted its head, and once at the funeral, when the guildmaster Sorrel had gripped Ulyana's arm too hard and whispered something Wren hadn't caught.

"I sell knots and tokens," Ulyana said. "Paper saints and traveler's blessings. If the shrines are waking, that's above my pay, Inspector, and above yours too."

"You're lying to me."

"I'm *surviving* near you. There's a difference, and you'd see it faster if you weren't wearing that coat."

Wren looked down at herself—the grey traveling coat, plain but cut in the hunter style, the high collar, the badge-shaped absence at her belt. She had dressed to be invisible to the market and had forgotten she would never be invisible to Ulyana Kavanagh, who had read her since before either of them had secrets worth keeping.

"I'm not here to take you in," Wren said. "I need you to believe that."

"I want to." Ulyana's voice cracked slightly on the word, and it undid Wren more than any accusation could have. "That's the worst part, Wren. I want to so badly I could weep. But you're the law, and the law is coming for my guild—I don't know how I know, I just *know*, the same way I knew the prayers were starting—and when it does, you'll have to choose. And I have watched you choose the law for twenty years. It's what makes you *you*. I love you for it and I cannot trust you because of it, and I am so tired of carrying both."

The words landed one after another like stones dropped down a well, each one striking deeper than the last.

"I arrested a hedger in Duskfield last month," Wren heard herself say. "He'd healed his neighbor's lung-rot. Saved her life. The neighbors hid him for a week because they loved him, and he gave himself up anyway, because he didn't want them punished for sheltering him. He thanked me at the wagon. Do you know what that does to a person, Ulya? Do you know what it's done to me, every single time?"

"No," Ulyana said softly. "Because you've never once told me."

"I'm telling you now."

"Twenty years late."

"Yes." Wren's throat was tight. "Twenty years late. I'm told that's how I do everything."

Something almost like a laugh escaped Ulyana—wet, brief, real. She looked at Wren across the stall across all those years, and for a moment Wren saw the girl in the reeds again, ink-fingered and fearless, whispering to a dead god just to see if it would answer.

"Come by the hall," Ulyana said at last. "The Guild of Small Mercies, off Cooper's Row. Not tonight—come in two days, when the inspectors' rotation shifts and the street-watch changes. Come as my friend, not my interviewer, and I'll tell you what I can." Her jaw tightened. "Some of it you won't want to hear. Some of it you may wish you'd never learned. But you'll hear it from me before you hear it from a warrant, and that much I swear on Sorrel's name."

"Why two days?"

"Because in two days," Ulyana said quietly, "either everything will have changed, or nothing will have, and either way you'll want to have brought your own bread." A ghost of the old crooked smile. "Guild cooking hasn't improved."

Wren laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound—and stepped back from the stall, and the market swallowed the space between them again. She walked toward the canal bridges without looking back, because looking back would have been surrender, and she was already carrying more surrendered ground than her commission could survive.

At the third bridge she stopped, hands on the cold stone rail, watching lantern-light shiver on the black water. Beneath her, faint as a pulse through bedrock, she felt it again: the great patient attention, the ledger open, the pen moving. *Selling.*

Two days. She'd given herself two days, and given Ulyana two days, and given the crown two days, and every one of those debts was already accruing interest somewhere she couldn't see.

She reached into her coat and found, without meaning to, the small tin token at the bottom of her pocket—the one she'd started carrying again three weeks ago, when the first prayer was answered and her hunter's instincts had whispered *old things are stirring; carry your oldest protections.* A wren in flight, stamped by a fourteen-year-old's careful thumb, promising she'd never be lost.

She'd been lost for twenty years. Standing on the bridge, feeling the mountain breathe beneath the city, Wren understood—with the cold certainty of a woman reading her own warrant—that whatever waited in the dark below had been keeping count of far more than souls.

And that the first entry in its ledger, the oldest debt of all, might very well bear her friend's name.
