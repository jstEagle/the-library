## 8. The Smuggler's Toll

The *Kindly Gull* sat low in the water at Saltquay Stairs, and Dagny Holt sat lower still in the taproom of the Broken Compass, counting what she had and pretending it was more than it was.

Three casks of Vharrow blue-salt, legal enough. Two of something else, packed under tarps that had been declared as dye-wool, which was a lie so old between her and the harbor wardens that it had practically become a formality. A hull that needed caulking by midsummer or she'd be bailing with her hat. And a ledger — always the ledger, its pages soft as cloth from handling — in which every favor owed to her was written in ink and every debt she owed was written in memory, because debts you wrote down could be seized.

She was on her third cup of small beer she hadn't wanted when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a grey coat.

Dagny didn't look up right away. That was the first rule of Saltquay: never look up when someone interesting arrives. Let them come to you, let them think you hadn't noticed, let them stand in the doorway a heartbeat too long deciding whether they were brave enough for this part of the city. She kept her eyes on the ledger and listened instead. Soft boots, worn at the heel. The particular hush of someone who had learned to move quietly not from training but from years of needing not to be noticed. Cloth smelling faintly of smoke and river-mud and, underneath it, something herbal — feverfew, maybe, or rue. Charm-seller's smell.

"Passage," said a woman's voice. Low, roughened. "West, downcoast. Tonight if you have it."

Dagny turned a page she wasn't reading. "Saltquay's got ferry-masters for honest folk."

"I'm not asking for the ferry."

"No," Dagny agreed, and now she looked up, because there was no more pretending either of them hadn't noticed the other. "You're not."

The woman by the door had the look of someone who had run further than she'd meant to. Grey coat travel-stained to the elbows, hood back, dark hair escaping its braid in wet ropes — the fog off the quay got into everything tonight. Younger than Dagny had expected. Pretty in the way that a knife is pretty, all clean lines and an edge doing quiet work. But it was the hands that told the story: they were charm-seller's hands, nimble and quick, and one sleeve was pulled deliberately down over the left wrist even though the taproom was warm enough that everyone else had pushed theirs up.

Dagny had been running contraband out of Vharrow since before this woman was weaned. She knew a fugitive the way a butcher knows meat. What she didn't know — yet — was the flavor of the trouble. Crown trouble was the worst kind: it followed like a dog that had your scent and a writ besides. Guild trouble was better; guilds died too fast these days to chase anyone far.

"You're the one from the night market," Dagny said. "Luck-knots. Little charms against rheumatism and heartbreak." She let a grin show, yellowed but genuine. "Half my crew wears your work. Old Piet swears his sea-charm kept him from drowning twice."

"It kept him from drowning once," the woman said. "The second time he did it himself, on purpose, to get out of paying his bar tab."

Dagny laughed before she could stop herself, and hated how easily it came. There was a trick some people had, of being funny while frightened, and it always made Dagny trust them more than was wise.

"So you know my work," the woman went on, "which means you know I pay in goods, not coin. Coin can be traced."

"Coin can be traced, goods can be weighed, and everything can be taken off you at a checkpoint if the wrong person's feeling ambitious." Dagny closed the ledger and laid her palm flat on it, a gesture that was half habit and half threat. "What I need to know is which wrong people are behind you."

Something moved through the woman's face — quick, controlled, like weather under glass. Fear, mostly. Under it, grief, still fresh, still bleeding.

"The crown sealed the guildhall on Copperlane this evening," she said. "There's an inspector with a warrant, and a mage-hunter with a face I grew up next door to. I need to be gone before morning. West. Anywhere west."

*Guildhall.* Dagny turned the word over. There were forty guilds in Vharrow that mattered and a hundred that didn't, and the one on Copperlane was neither — it was one of those little orders that had survived the Godfall by becoming too small to bother with. Charmsellers. Blessers of hearths and hinges. The Guild of Small Mercies, the old men called it, though whether that was its proper name or just affection Dagny had never troubled to learn.

And now inspectors had sealed it, and the last of it was standing in Dagny's taproom trying not to shake.

"Sit down before you fall down," Dagny said, and kicked a stool out from under the table with her boot.

The woman sat. Her name, when she gave it, was Ulyana Kavanagh, and she said it the way people say names they've decided to stop using soon.

* * *

They talked terms over the third pot of tea, because Dagny Holt negotiated over tea the way other women prayed over beads — ritually, continuously, and with occasional moments of genuine feeling.

"Downcoast passage runs forty crowns for a berth that smells of salt-fish and eighty for one that doesn't. Neither of which you have."

"No."

"And I don't run passengers for promises. Passengers are the worst cargo. They talk, they panic, they get seasick on the manifest."

"I know what I am," Ulyana said. There was no pleading in it, which Dagny appreciated. Pleading made her itch. "I'm asking what you'll take instead."

Dagny leaned back until the chair complained. Outside the window the fog had thickened until the lanterns of Saltquay Stairs hung in it like drowned moons. Somewhere out there, she knew, the harbor wardens were making their slow rounds, and somewhere further out, if rumor traveled true — and it always did, faster than ships — there were crown men asking questions with warrants folded in their pockets.

Here was the thing about Dagny Holt that almost nobody ever learned, because she took pains that nobody should: she owed a debt of her own. Not to a guild, not to a court, nothing so grand. To a girl named Maren Osgood, fifteen years dead now, who had hidden Dagny in a root cellar for three days while crown inspectors tore apart the *Kindly Gull* looking for a mage-tattoo Dagny's brother had paid good money to have inked on her shoulder as a joke, or a test, or both. Maren had lied to the inspectors with a straight face and then asked for nothing except that Dagny remember her when it counted.

Dagny had spent fifteen years remembering, and the interest had compounded into a general policy: she did not hand people to the crown. Ever. For any price. It wasn't kindness. It was bookkeeping.

"Show me your goods," she said. "Whatever you're carrying. I want to see it all before I name a price."

Ulyana hesitated — and there it was again, that flicker toward the left wrist, that instinctive shielding of something under the sleeve — and then she unslung her satchel and set it on the table with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments.

Charms. Dozens of them, wrapped in waxed cloth and twine. Luck-knots braided from three colors of cord. A tin of dried rowan berries strung on red thread. Small clay beads fired with ash worked into the clay, each one stamped with a mark Dagny didn't recognize — a circle with a flame inside it, worn nearly smooth. And one thing that was none of these: a flat stone the size of a coin, river-smooth, with a hairline crack across it that glowed, very faintly, the color of coals at dawn.

Dagny reached for the stone and stopped, her fingers an inch away, because her skin had begun to prickle. Not fear exactly. Recognition. Like hearing your own name called from another room.

"What is that?"

"A coal from the guildhall fire," Ulyana said. "It's supposed to be dead. It isn't."

"Charm-craft's illegal," Dagny said, mostly to be saying something. Her mouth had gone dry. "Hasn't stopped anyone in thirty years, but it's the law, and I'm a law-abiding woman in my own fashion."

"Your own fashion being the operative words." Ulyana pushed the satchel an inch closer. "Take the lot. Knots, beads, rowan, all of it. It's a year of my living. Or—" and here she paused, and Dagny watched her decide something, watched the decision cost her, "—or take one piece. One charm, whatever you choose, and consider us square."

"One charm. Against a sea crossing. Past two checkpoints and whatever's coming behind you." Dagny snorted. "You're a terrible negotiator."

"I'm a desperate one. Desperate and terrible look alike from the outside."

Despite herself — despite the hour and the danger and the fifteen years of careful neutrality — Dagny grinned. Then she looked down at the open satchel again, and the grin faded on its own, without her permission, because the cracked stone was still glowing its faint coal-color and still prickling against her skin like a word she almost remembered.

"Fine," she said. "One charm. My choice." She hovered her hand over the satchel, letting it drift the way she let it drift over card decks and dice cups and the scales at customs houses, reading weight and lie together. It passed over the knots. Over the rowan. Over the beads.

It stopped over the stone.

"That one," she heard herself say, and didn't know why, and hated not knowing why, and said it anyway.

Ulyana went very still. "That's not for sale."

"You just said—"

"I know what I said." The charm-seller's hands had curled closed, knuckles white. "Everything else. Take everything else. Not that."

Now Dagny was interested, and being interested was dangerous, and she had never once in her life let that stop her. "Why? What does it do?"

"Nothing. It's a keepsake. It's — " Ulyana stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a step on ice. "It came out of a fire that should have gone out sixty years ago and hasn't. It's the only piece of it anyone's ever carried outside those walls. And I think — I *think* — that things which answer prayers have started noticing pieces of it. Taking them. I found ash where it used to sit in my master's workshop. Just ash, and a shape in the ash like something had picked the coal up and carried it off."

The taproom felt colder. Dagny told herself it was the fog off the door seams.

"And you're carrying it," she said, "in a bag. Through the streets. At night. While the crown seals your guildhall."

"I'm carrying it because Sorrel told me never to leave it, and then he died, and I haven't yet thought of a good enough reason to disobey him a second time."

*Second time.* Dagny filed that away — there was history in it, deep water — and reached out and closed the satchel flap gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid.

"All right," she said. "New terms. Everything in the bag for the passage, and the stone rides with you, and if it gets us killed, you owe me a ghost-apology."

"That's generous."

"That's terrified," Dagny said honestly. "Generous looks similar from the outside."

* * *

They boarded at the change of the tide, in that black hour when the fog lies thickest on the water and even the gulls give up gossiping. Dagny walked Ulyana down the Saltquay Stairs herself, past the wardens' post where old Cobb was snoring over his lamp with the beautiful professional deafness she paid him forty crowns a year to maintain, and along the weed-slick boards to where the *Kindly Gull* knocked gently against her pilings.

Old Piet met them at the gangway, took one look at Ulyana's face, and said, "Ah. One of those fares," in the tone of a man who has buried too many secrets at sea to be surprised by another.

"She pays her way," Dagny said. "See she stays fed and unseen. And Piet — if anyone asks, she's my cousin from the inland farms, and she's simple, and she cries a lot."

"I've played simpler cousins," Piet said. "Once for a bishop."

Below decks, in a berth that smelled of salt-fish exactly as advertised, Ulyana stowed her satchel and stood for a moment with her hand pressed flat against the ship's ribbing, as if checking a pulse. Dagny lingered in the companionway, telling herself she was supervising and knowing perfectly well she wasn't.

"Kavanagh." The name felt odd in her mouth already, like a coin going out of circulation. "Who's coming after you? Truly. Not the shape of it — the truth of it."

Ulyana was quiet long enough that the ship spoke for her, timber ticking against rope. "A friend," she said finally. "Her name's Wren. Wren Calloway. She hunts mages for the crown, and she's good at it — better than good, she's the best they have — and she grew up four doors down from me, and when we were nine she broke a boy's arm for calling me witch-blood." A breath. "She'll find me, Dagny. Not because she wants to. Because she can't help it. She's never once failed to find me, and I've never once managed to hide."

"And when she finds you?"

"Then one of us learns what the other one is worth." Ulyana said it steadily, but her thumb had gone to her left wrist, pressing down on the hidden place beneath the sleeve, and Dagny understood suddenly that the oathmark — because that's what it had to be, what else do you hide like that — was not just a secret. It was a wound. Some marks were. Some oaths cut both ways, binding the sworn to something that could bleed them.

Dagny had seen that look before. In mirrors. In Maren Osgood's root cellar, on her own face, fifteen years gone.

"Get some sleep," she said gruffly. "We clear the headland before dawn. After that it's three days of gray water and worse cooking, and you'll want your strength, because my cook believes suffering improves fish."

She turned to go, and had one foot on the ladder, when Ulyana said, "Dagny."

"Hm?"

"Why did you say yes? Really. You didn't have to. Forty crowns is forty crowns, and I was offering you a bag of trinkets and a story about a fire."

Dagny gripped the ladder rail. Above them, feet crossed the deck; the foghorn of some distant honest ship mourned once and quit. She could have lied. Lying was her native language; she was fluent in six dialects of it. But there was something about the dark below decks, and the coal-colored glow leaking faintly from the satchel's seams, and the girl's tired, knife-clean face, that made lying feel like a waste of good material.

"Fifteen years ago," she said, "a woman hid me from the crown for three days and asked nothing for it except that I remember her when it counted. I've been waiting fifteen years for it to count." She climbed two rungs, paused. "Turns out it counted tonight. Sleep well, cousin."

She was halfway up when the light changed.

Not the lamps. Something warmer, redder, breathing from below — from the berth, from the satchel, from the cracked stone that was supposed to be a dead coal out of a dying fire. Dagny turned on the ladder and looked down, and saw Ulyana kneeling beside her bag with her hands hovering over it, not touching, and the glow rising through the canvas seams like sunrise under a door.

"It's never done that," Ulyana whispered. Her face in the ember-light looked younger and older all at once. "It's never — Dagny, it's never done *that*."

And then, faint but unmistakable, from inside the satchel, from the stone, from the coal that had burned unquenched for sixty years: a sound. Small. Rhythmic.

Like knocking.

Like something on the far side of a door, asking — politely, patiently, the way creditors knock — to be let out. Or let in.

Above them, on deck, Piet's voice called down, uneasy, saying the fog ahead of the bows had gone strange, that the water past the headland was glowing faint gold in lines and circles, patterns no current ever drew — as if the whole sea beyond Vharrow's harbor had become a page, and something beneath it was writing.

Dagny Holt stood on the ladder between the glowing hold and the glowing sea and did the fastest arithmetic of her life, and the sum came out the same every way she ran it:

She hadn't smuggled a passenger out of Vharrow tonight.

She'd smuggled a spark — and the whole dark coast was already reaching up its hands to warm itself by it.
