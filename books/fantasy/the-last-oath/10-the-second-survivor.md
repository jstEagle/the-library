## 10. The Second Survivor

The convent of Saint Vess had been built to be forgotten.

Ilse Marrow had chosen it for exactly that reason, forty years ago, when she had still counted years instead of candles. It sat in a fold of the Greymarsh hills where the road forgot itself twice before reaching the gate—a road that flooded in spring and froze in autumn, so that visitors arrived only by accident or by desperation. The sisters who lived there grew leeks and kept bees and prayed to a saint whose bones were rumored to lie beneath the altar, though no one had ever dug to confirm it. Their prayers went up like smoke from a doused fire: sincere, brief, and expecting nothing back.

Perfect ground for an archivist of the Guild of Small Mercies. If you wanted to hide something, you hid it among people who had given up asking questions of heaven.

Ilse was seventy-one years old now, or near enough that the difference had stopped mattering. Her hands had gone knuckled and brown as old roots, and her hair—what remained after the customary veil—was white as chalk scraped off a slate. She spent her days in the scriptorium copying psalters in a hand that trembled only slightly, and her nights in the cell she had warded so thoroughly that even the mice forgot which way her door faced. Under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, lay eleven ledgers, four bundles of correspondence, one sealed box that must never be opened while she lived, and the accumulated memory of a guild that the world believed dead.

She was copying the ninety-first psalm when the dog began to bark.

This was remarkable on two counts. First, because the convent kept no dog; the creature belonged to the tanner's family down the valley and appeared at intervals to steal bread and be forgiven. Second, because the dog was barking at the guest gate, and no one had knocked at the guest gate in six years except a tax collector, whom the sisters had successfully bored into leaving.

Ilse set down her pen. She listened the way she had taught herself to listen decades ago—not to sounds but to their shapes. A knock would come soon, she judged. One knock, hesitant, then another. A person unused to asking sanctuary. A person standing too long in the wind.

The knock came exactly so.

* * *

She went down herself, because Mother Abbess's knees were bad and because Ilse had long ago arranged to be the one who answered gates. Through the peephole worn smooth by generations of wary thumbs, she saw a figure huddled in a hooded traveling cloak, mud to the knee, a satchel slung across the chest in the manner of someone who did not intend to put it down.

"Who knocks?" Ilse asked, in the flat ceremonial voice the office required.

"A pilgrim," said the figure. Then, lower, with the accent of Vharrow's night market bleeding through the piety: "Please. I was told—I was told there was a sister here who used to keep records. Before."

There it was. Forty years of careful silence, and the world came knocking anyway.

Ilse opened the gate just wide enough to see the face beneath the hood. Younger than she'd expected. Sharp-boned, exhausted, watchful—the particular watchfulness of someone who had learned to read a street before crossing it. And on the inner wrist, half-hidden by a pushed-up sleeve as the girl steadied herself against the gatepost, the ghost of a mark: faded, deliberately blurred with ink or ash, but unmistakable to eyes that had catalogued a thousand of them.

An oathmark. A living one.

"You'd better come in before the dog wakes the whole marsh," Ilse said.

* * *

She installed the girl—it was Ulyana Kavanagh, of course it was; Sorrel had sent word of her birth forty years ago in a letter Ilse still kept under the floorboards—in the warming room by the kitchen fire, fed her barley soup and dark bread, and sat down across from her with two cups of nettle tea and the expression of a woman prepared to wait out a siege.

"You knew I'd come," Ulyana said.

"I knew someone would come eventually. Prayers are being answered again." Ilse turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "You can feel it from here, if you know how to listen. The shrines are waking up all along the Greymarsh road. Little ones. Roadside altars, well-shrines, the kind that have been cold since the Godfall. They're not cold anymore. And things are being given." Her eyes lifted. "And taken. You've seen it."

"The widow's son," Ulyana said quietly. "He came home. He isn't—he isn't right. He stands in his mother's kitchen and looks at her like he's trying to remember why faces matter."

Ilse closed her eyes briefly. "Yes. That is what it looks like when a god-fragment pays its debts."

"Then you know what's happening. You know more than Sorrel ever told me. He died without telling me, and then—" Ulyana's hands tightened around her cup. "Then Ashen Brannock burned from the inside out on the guildhall doorstep, and the inspectors came, and I ran. I've got the Ember Ledger in my bag. Five souls sold to the court beneath the mountain. Four reclaimed. Mine outstanding." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and very young. "So yes, Sister. I think you know some things. I've walked three days through freezing rain to find you. Please stop making me ask."

Ilse studied her for a long moment. In the girl's face she could see Sorrel plainly—the same stubborn jaw, the same refusal to be managed—and underneath that, fainter, someone else entirely. Someone whose name was written in a ledger in a box under the floor of this very room.

"Terms first," Ilse said. "What you say in this room stays in this room until I release it. What I show you, you carry in your memory, not your satchel. And you do not write anything down while you sit under this roof. Paper travels. Memory at least has the decency to die with us."

Ulyana nodded.

"Then let me tell you what I am," Ilse said, "because you deserve to weigh it before you trust me. I was the archivist of the Guild of Small Mercies for nineteen years. I copied every contract, every oath, every debt the guild ever incurred. When the purges came, when the crown burned the halls and hung the mages from the Weeping Bridge, I was the one they chose to carry the records out. Not because I was brave. Because I was the most forgettable." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I have been forgetting myself on purpose ever since. A nun named Sister Anselm, who tends bees and copies psalms. Do you understand? I did not survive the Godfall. I was *filed away*, like everything else."

"And the others?" Ulyana asked. "Sorrel said five survivors. I've found none but you."

"Brannock is dead—you saw how. Old Maren took fever two winters past. Tam Voss drowned himself in the canal in Vharrow eight years ago, though the coroner wrote 'accident,' and I have never decided whether to correct him." Ilse said it evenly, the way she recited inventories, because grief that has been carried forty years learns to walk quietly. "That leaves Sorrel, who is dead, and me. You're looking at the last survivor of the Guild of Small Mercies, child, apart from yourself. Which means you're looking at the last person alive who knows what was actually agreed beneath Greyhollow mountain."

"Then tell me."

"Not yet." Ilse rose, joints cracking like green kindling. "First you'll help me bring up a box. Some things should be read in the original hand."

* * *

They went up to the scriptorium, where the single window admitted a grey light that made every shadow look archival. Ilse moved the psalter case aside, pried up a floorboard that came free without protest—she had oiled its edges for decades—and drew out a box of black oak bound in iron. Inside, wrapped in linen gone soft as skin, lay a ledger unlike the Ember Ledger Ulyana carried.

"This," Ilse said, laying it open on the reading table, "is the Covenant Ledger. The first book. Everything in your Ember Ledger is a copy—a fair copy, made later, for convenience. This is the original. This is the hand that sat in the dark under Greyhollow and wrote down the words as they were spoken between the first guildmaster and the thing the court below sends up to do its talking."

Ulyana bent over the pages. The ink had gone brown, the vellum scarred with age, but the script was beautiful—each letter placed with the devotion of a jeweler. "Whose writing?"

"Mine," said Ilse. "I was twenty-six. I had the steadiest hand in the guild, and I was considered expendable, which is the chief qualification for witnessing bargains with the Undercourt." She turned a page carefully, and Ulyana caught a glimpse of columns of names, and flinched.

"That's—"

"Every soul. Yes." Ilse's finger stopped at the fifth entry from the top, and there, in the brown ink: *Kavanagh, Ulyana. Pledged in infancy by Sorrel Kavanagh, guildmaster, against the debt of the Ember. Due upon the guttering of the last flame.* "I wrote your name into this book, child. I want you to know that. I held the pen. Sorrel held my wrist steady. Whatever else you decide about her tonight, you should decide it knowing that."

Ulyana stared at the entry for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was strangely level. "Why her? Why me?"

"Because the debt needed collateral that would ripen," Ilse said. "A soul pledged at birth binds longer than one sold outright. The court likes long instruments. And because Sorrel—" Here the archivist paused, and for the first time her composure showed a seam. "Because Sorrel believed, rightly or wrongly, that a soul raised inside the guild, knowing its own debt, would choose freely when the day came. She thought she was giving you a choice. She was giving you a cage and calling the door a choice. I told her so at the time. I was overruled. I wrote it down anyway."

"But the terms." Ulyana's finger hovered over the page without touching it, as if the ink might still be wet after forty years. "The Ember Ledger says due upon the guttering. It doesn't say—there's nothing in there about *how*. Can't I just take it back? Break in, steal the lantern, run?"

"No." Ilse said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "And here is what Sorrel never told you, and what the Ember Ledger does not record, and what I have kept behind my teeth for forty years while good people burned. Listen carefully, because this is the whole of it."

She turned to the final page of the covenant, where the clauses stood in formal rows, and read aloud in a voice gone soft and precise:

"'And the pledged soul shall be returned to its owner upon tender of payment, save only this: that it be returned *willingly*, by the act and consent of the court below, freely given and without coercion. For a soul seized by force is a flame snuffed, and the ember that warms the world shall die with it.'"

Silence filled the scriptorium like water filling a hull.

"Willingly," Ulyana repeated. "The court has to give it back *willingly*."

"They must consent to the return. Freely. Without coercion, without trickery, without a blade at any throat. That was the clause the first guildmaster fought hardest for—or so the record claims—and it is the clause that makes the whole arrangement a lock with the key thrown into the sea." Ilse closed the ledger with exaggerated care, as though it might bruise. "You cannot steal your soul back, child. You cannot trick it out of them, buy it with another's coin, or pry it from their hands. If you take it by force, the last dragon-fire dies at the moment of taking, and everything the guild bought with five souls—all these decades of quiet, all the sleeping gods left sleeping—dies with it."

"So I have to make them *want* to give it back." Ulyana laughed, a short, disbelieving sound. "The court beneath the mountain. The ones who burn people on doorsteps. I have to negotiate with them. Politely."

"Now you begin to see why Sorrel wept when she told you, and why she waited until she was dying to do it." Ilse folded her hands. "But hear the rest, because there is a rest, and it is the reason I sent for no one and waited forty years like a spider in a psalm."

"There's more?"

"When the covenant was struck, the court insisted on a keeper. A witness who would hold the true terms, remember them exactly, and remain alive to interpret them should dispute arise. That is the office I accepted that night under Greyhollow. Archivist of the covenant. Interpreter of terms." Ilse's eyes, in the grey light, looked suddenly very old and very tired. "It sounded honorable. It was not. It was a leash. The court did not want a witness, child. It wanted an instrument. Someone bound to preserve the agreement precisely as written—which means someone bound to prevent anyone from ever finding a loophole. For forty years I have kept these terms inviolate. I told myself it was duty. Lately I have begun to wonder whether it was simply the longest-running betrayal in the history of a guild that specialized in small mercies."

Ulyana had gone very still. "You're bound to them."

"My signature is in that book beside yours." Ilse touched the margin of the page, where a smaller line of script ran like a stitch. "Witness and keeper: I. M. Every year on the winter solstice, I light a candle at the hour of midnight and speak the terms aloud, alone, in this room. That is the observance. That is all they require of me. So far." She met Ulyana's eyes. "You came here hoping to find an ally. I am telling you plainly: you have found one, but she comes with the enemy's stitching in her hem, and you should sew accordingly."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Outside, the marsh wind worried the eaves, and somewhere the tanner's dog barked once at nothing, and gave up.

"Then help me anyway," Ulyana said finally. "Not despite the leash. With it. If you know the terms better than anyone alive, then you know them better than the court wants anyone to know them. There has to be a seam. There's always a seam—that's what you taught the guild, isn't it? Every contract is a garment, and garments are made by sewing, and sewing leaves openings."

Ilse looked at her for a long time—at the sharp-boned face, the exhausted eyes, the stubborn jaw that was Sorrel's jaw to the millimeter—and felt something shift in her chest that had not shifted in forty years. Hope, perhaps. Or the memory of hope, worn smooth as a river stone.

"There may be," she said slowly. "One clause. One. And it will cost you more than you are imagining, and I will not tell you what it is tonight, because you need sleep and bread and an honest night's terror before you hear it." She began wrapping the ledger in its linen. "Tomorrow I will teach you what the covenant actually says, all of it, every clause. And then, child, we are going to have to move quickly—"

She stopped.

Her head tilted, the way it had tilted at the gate, listening to shapes rather than sounds. Down in the valley, thin with distance, a bell was ringing—but not the convent's bell. The bell of the shrine at the crossroads, two miles east, which had stood silent and cold for sixty years.

It was ringing for a visitor.

"Sister Anselm keeps bees and copies psalms," Ilse murmured, almost to herself. "Sister Anselm has never had enemies, because Sister Anselm has never mattered." She turned back to Ulyana, and her face had gone pale and hard. "But someone is coming up this road, and they are coming at a walk, unhurried, the way people walk when they already know what they will find. Get your satchel. Not the front door—the bee-yard gate, and follow the drainage ditch south until you reach the alder stand. Wait there until full dark. If I am not with you by then, take the shepherd's path west and do not come back for me, whatever you hear."

"What about you?"

"I," said Ilse Marrow, archivist of the Guild of Small Mercies, keeper of the covenant, drawing her veil straight with hands that did not quite tremble, "am going to answer the door. It's what I'm for."
