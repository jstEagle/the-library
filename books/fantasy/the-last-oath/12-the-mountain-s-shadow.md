## 12. The Mountain's Shadow

The road stopped pretending to be a road two miles from Greyhollow.

It had been a decent track all morning — rutted, yes, and stitched through with the roots of pines that had no business growing that far north, but a track nonetheless, the kind of road made by people who expected to come back the way they went. Then, quite suddenly, it wasn't. The ruts filled with gravel. The gravel filled with slate. And the slate began to slope downward, gently at first, so that Ulyana only noticed the descent when her knees did, aching in a way that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with walking into the earth.

"Feel that?" she said.

Ilse walked half a step behind her, as she had for three days now — the habit of a woman who had spent twenty years making herself furniture in other people's lives. Her nun's grey was dusty at the hem, and her hands were folded in front of her like a woman in prayer, though Ulyana had learned that when Ilse's hands were folded like that, she was counting something. Coins. Exits. Sins.

"The grade," Ilse said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an answer either.

"Not the grade."

They walked on another hundred paces before Ilse sighed, a small sound, almost fond. "You always did hate being told things slowly."

"I grew up in a guild full of archivists who thought foreshadowing was a moral virtue. Sorrel used to answer direct questions with parables."

"And look how much you loved her for it." Ilse's voice went quiet in the middle of the sentence, the way it always did when Sorrel's name came between them, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the mountain spoke instead.

That was the thing Ulyana couldn't stop noticing: the mountain *spoke*. Not in words. In pressure. The air down here had weight to it, a low constant hum beneath hearing, like the moment before a bell is struck stretched out into forever. Her back teeth ached with it. When she pressed her tongue to the roof of her mouth she could feel the hum answering there, faint and patient, as if the stone were tasting her.

Greyhollow announced itself first by smell — woodsmoke and wet iron and, underneath both, something older and sweeter, like honey left too long in a dark room. Then by sound: a bell, tolling slow, from somewhere ahead. Then by light, which was the strangest part, because there shouldn't have been any. But around the shoulder of the mountain the valley opened up, and there it lay in the early dusk: a town built in layers, houses stacked on houses on the backs of older houses, roofs of black slate going down into the ground instead of up into the sky, terraces sinking toward a central square where lamps already burned with a flame that burned the wrong color — gold shot through with green, like sunlight seen through bottle glass.

Below all of it, below even the lowest terrace, a great door stood open in the mountainside. From this distance it looked like a wound of pure darkness, and people were walking into it. Steadily. Unhurried. The way water walks into a drain.

"They don't close it," Ulyana said.

"There's nothing to close it against," Ilse said. "Down here, everything that goes in has already been paid for."

* * *

Dagny Holt was waiting where they'd agreed she'd wait, at the last honest inn before the shadow line, a low stone place called the Tollhouse whose sign depicted a hand dropping coins into a mouth. She sat in the corner with her boots up, a mug of something dark in front of her, and she watched them come in the way she watched everything — sideways, over the rim of the mug, like a woman keeping score.

"Well," she said. "You both look terrible."

"You should see the other pilgrims," Ulyana said, and dropped into the chair across from her.

"Don't joke here." Dagny said it flatly, without drama, which was somehow worse than if she'd shuddered. She turned her mug a quarter-turn on the table, a small ritual, and Ulyana realized she'd been doing it since they sat down at every table on the whole journey north — turning her luck clockwise, feeding it. "I mean it, charm-seller. I've run cargo past this valley eleven times, and I've got one rule about Greyhollow, and the rule is you don't joke inside it. You don't say anything inside it that you don't absolutely mean."

"That's superstition."

"That's *arithmetic*." Dagny leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the burn-scar along her jaw, old and pale. "Everything costs double here and settles instantly. You'll see. Order the stew and watch what happens."

Ulyana ordered the stew.

What happened was this: the innkeeper's wife brought it out steaming, set it down without a word, and then stood there looking at Ulyana with an expression of profound, weary courtesy, until Ulyana understood — not with her mind, but with her skin, with the fine hairs on her arms lifting — that the woman was waiting for payment. Not after. Now. Ulyana fumbled out two coppers and laid them on the tray, and the woman nodded once, satisfied, and left, and the whole transaction had taken less time than breathing.

"All right," Ulyana said slowly. "Point taken."

"It's not just trade." Ilse had taken the seat beside the wall, back to the stone, eyes on the room. She hadn't touched the bread the innkeeper's wife had put down for her — because Ilse hadn't asked for it, and here, unasked-for gifts apparently didn't exist. "In Vharrow you can tell a lie and walk away from it. Words are cheap there. They're paper. Here—" Ilse turned her wrist, and in the lamplight Ulyana saw, for the first time in three days of traveling together, the faint silver tracery on the inside of Ilse's forearm. Not an oathmark. Older than an oathmark. A lattice of fine lines like frost on a window, and each line ended in a knot. "Every word is a contract. Say a lie, and you've signed a debt you didn't know you were taking on. The mountain keeps the books."

"How do you know that?"

Ilse smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ulyana had ever seen on her, and she had seen Ilse smile over Sorrel's deathbed. "Because I spent forty years as this court's correspondent, child. I know their house rules better than my own order's."

Ulyana looked at the frost-lines on Ilse's arm for a long moment, and decided — deliberately, the way you step over a tripwire knowing it's there — not to ask yet. There would be time. There would have to be time. Instead she pulled her sleeve down over her own mark and ate her stew, which tasted of lamb and rosemary and, faintly, of iron.

* * *

They went down into Greyhollow proper at dusk, and the dusk came early, because the mountain took the sun two hours before the sky gave it up.

The streets sloped. That was the first thing, always the first thing, the body's quiet insistence: we are going *down*. The second thing was the doors. Every door in Greyhollow stood open — shop doors, house doors, chapel doors — and through each one Ulyana glimpsed the same tableau: people sitting at tables, facing the street, hands empty, waiting. Not dead. Not trapped. Just... available. Like shops that never closed because closing would be a refusal, and refusal was the one word this place had forgotten how to pronounce.

"The Undercourt doesn't rule here," Ilse murmured, matching her steps to Ulyana's. "That's what travelers never understand. It doesn't need to rule. It just needs everyone to keep agreeing. Consent, freely given, forever renewed. A town where every handshake is binding and every promise is load-bearing." She gestured with her chin at a pair of merchants sealing a deal across a barrel of nails. They shook hands, and Ulyana saw it — actually saw it, a shimmer passing between their palms like heat off summer stone, sinking down into the ground. "Watch long enough and you can see the debts moving. Down. Always down. Toward the door."

"And the lies?"

"Ah." Ilse stopped walking.

They were in the central square now, under the green-gold lamps, near the well. The well was dry — had been dry for years, Ulyana would learn — but people still came to it and dropped coins in, and whispered things into the dark, and walked away lighter.

"A demonstration," Ilse said, "costs less than an explanation. Tell me something untrue. Anything. Small. Tell me the weather is warm."

The evening air had teeth in it. Frost was forming on the slate roofs overhead. Ulyana opened her mouth to say *it's warm*, to prove some point about her own self-possession —

— and couldn't.

Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* The words assembled themselves behind her teeth and then simply refused to pass, and in the space where they refused, pain bloomed: a hot wire drawn tight across her tongue, a taste of copper and ash, her own pulse hammering in her gums. She doubled slightly, hand to her mouth, and breathed through her nose, and the sensation passed in three heartbeats, leaving her shaking and furious and colder than the night warranted.

She looked up. Ilse was watching her with an expression that held no satisfaction at all — only a tired, ancient grief, the face of a teacher watching a student learn a lesson the hard way because there was no longer time for the soft one.

"Now you know," Ilse said quietly. "And now you understand why I haven't lied to you once on this entire journey, Ulyana Kavanagh. Not about Sorrel. Not about the ledger. Not about myself. Ask me why sometime, when we're above ground, where lying is survivable. For now, hold your tongue like it's the last coin you own. Because down here, it is."

Around them, the square went on with its evening. A woman dropped a coin in the dry well. A child laughed — genuinely laughed, bright and awful in its innocence. The great door at the bottom of the town drank its steady procession of silhouettes. And the hum beneath the world deepened by a fraction, the way a sleeping animal's breath deepens when it dreams of hunting.

Ulyana flexed her right hand inside her sleeve, where the oathmark sat like a brand, like a signature, like a name written on her by other people's bargains.

*Four souls reclaimed,* the ledger had said. *One outstanding.*

She had come down the mountain believing she was coming to take something back.

Standing in the square of Greyhollow, feeling the whole town lean toward her like a creditor recognizing a debtor across a crowded room — heads beginning, one by one, to turn — she understood, with the cold clarity of a woman reading fine print by lightning, that the mountain had been expecting her much longer than she had known she was coming.

At the edge of the square, where the lamplight failed and the shadow of the great door began, something stepped out of the darkness to meet her. It wore the shape of a man and the posture of a servant, and it bowed — precisely, courteously, without a single wasted degree — and when it spoke, its voice was the voice of the hum itself, given lips:

"Welcomed, daughter of the Guild of Small Mercies. Your account has been open a long while. The Court requests the pleasure of settling it."
