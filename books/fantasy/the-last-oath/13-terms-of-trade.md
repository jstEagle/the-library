## 13. Terms of Trade

Greyhollow had a smell Ulyana would carry to her grave: wet stone, old iron, and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. The town clung to the mountain's root the way lichen clings to a tombstone, its crooked lanes descending in terraces toward a mine-mouth that breathed cold air upward, slow and rhythmic, as though the mountain itself were sleeping with its mouth open.

They had been in Greyhollow three days. Dagny called it "acclimating." Ulyana called it what it was—stalling.

Every step deeper into the town made words feel strange in her mouth. She'd noticed it first at an inn on the second terrace, when she'd told the innkeeper the stew was excellent and her tongue had caught fire, a brief scalding bloom behind her teeth that lasted until she washed the lie down with watered wine. The stew had been terrible. Greyhollow did not permit small dishonesties. She could not imagine what a large one would do.

"It's the proximity," Ilse had said that night, in the careful voice she used for archival facts. She kept her nun's habit now even here, though the wimple was off and her grey hair escaped around her face like smoke. "Bargains bind more tightly near the Undercourt's threshold. Words become weight. Lies become pain. It is, I suppose, honest country."

Dagny had snorted into her cup. "Honest country. I've smuggled through six borders, and every one of them honest right up until they searched my hold."

But Dagny had gone quiet too, these three days. Her luck-charms—the little knotted cords she wore braided into her hair—had begun to fray on their own, threads unraveling without being touched, as if something below were patiently picking them apart. Each morning there were fewer knots. Each morning Dagny counted them and said nothing.

Now it was evening of the third day, and Ulyana sat alone in the shrine alcove off the inn's common room—a niche carved into living rock, older than the town, older than Vharrow, holding nothing but a shallow basin and a candle stub that no one remembered lighting. The shrines along the road had been warming for weeks; this one was warm already, the basin faintly slick with condensation, as though something beneath the stone were sweating.

She had come here to think about Sorrel.

*The debt is yours,* he'd whispered, his hand gripping hers with a strength that dying should have spent. *Yours alone. They will offer you ways around it. There are none.*

She turned her wrist over in the candlelight and pushed back her sleeve. The oathmark sat on her forearm like a brand of dark ink shaped from interlocking rings—an old guild sigil, the knot of small mercies—and beneath it, since Greyhollow, a new line had appeared. Thin as a hair. Red as a scratch. A tally mark, counting down or counting up, she didn't know which.

"Beautiful work," said a voice like water finding a crack in a wall. "Though I confess we preferred the original."

Ulyana did not jump. Three days in Greyhollow had taught her body new manners. She looked up slowly.

The figure stood at the mouth of the alcove where a moment before there had been only shadow. It was roughly the shape of a person and roughly the height of one, but it wore its form the way a poor actor wears a borrowed coat—the shoulders slightly wrong, the hands folded too many times over. Its face was smooth as an egg except where features had been suggested, a nose indicated by a crease, a mouth by a fold. It dressed as a herald might dress, if heraldry had continued past the Godfall: a tabard of grey silk worked in tarnished silver thread, patterns that hurt slightly to follow with the eye.

"You're the mountain's voice," Ulyana said. "A herald of the Undercourt."

"I am the court's courtesy." The herald inclined its suggested head. "And you are the outstanding entry."

"I have a name."

"Yes," the herald said, and something in its tone made the word sound like a delicacy. "So we are told."

It glided forward—or seemed to glide; the alcove was small enough that walking would have been absurd—and settled itself against the wall beside the basin, folding its over-folded hands. Up close, Ulyana could hear it breathing, though she could see no chest rise. The breath smelled of that same sweetness, fruit rotting slowly underground.

"I will be direct," the herald said, "as befits honest country. You have come to reclaim a pledged soul. Yours. Collateral on a debt contracted four-and-twenty years ago by your guild's master, may her service be long. The court is prepared to discuss terms ahead of schedule. We find punctuality charming in debtors."

"My guildmaster died last month."

"Her term continues regardless. Death is a change of address, not a cancellation." The herald produced, from somewhere within its grey silk, a document. It unfolded with a sound like frost cracking. "You wish terms. Here are terms."

Ulyana looked at the paper. The writing moved—not legibly, just a slow crawl of ink like insects settling. She recognized the hand, though. It was the same hand as the Ember Ledger, the same cramped, patient script that had recorded five souls sold and four reclaimed.

"The court offers a substitution clause," the herald said. "An ancient provision. A soul of equal substance may stand surety in place of the original pledge. You walk free. Your name comes off our book tonight. Someone else's goes on."

The alcove was very quiet. The candle flame leaned toward the herald, as if listening.

"Someone else's," Ulyana repeated.

"There are candidates." The herald's creased mouth suggested something that was not quite a smile. "We have taken the liberty of preparing a list. The court does so admire preparation."

It extended the document. Ulyana took it because not taking it felt more dangerous than taking it, and read.

The names were written in that patient crawling script, each one followed by a small annotation in the margin:

*Dagny Holt — smuggler. Debt-ridden already; the court holds three of her luck-years. Minimal resistance expected.*

*Ilse Verren — archivist. Long service rendered. Sentimental attachment to the debtor noted. Would consent given adequate framing.*

*Wren Calloway — hunter, crown-sworn. Soul of unusual density. Premium value. Acquisition would require... effort.*

Ulyana's vision went white at the edges. When she could see again, her own hands were shaking, and the paper had crumpled in her grip, and the candle had gone out, though the alcove was somehow still lit by a light that came from nowhere and warmed nothing.

"No," she said.

The herald waited, courteous, terrible.

"No," she said again, louder, and the word came out whole and unburned, because it was true all the way down. "No substitution. No list. Not Dagny, who's burned half her luck keeping my secrets. Not Ilse, who—" she stopped, because she wasn't yet sure what Ilse had done, only that the archivist flinched whenever the Undercourt was mentioned. "Not Wren. Especially not Wren. She's sworn to hunt me, and I won't pay my debts out of her hide."

"A pity," the herald said mildly. "The Calloway woman's soul would have covered the principal and most of the interest. We rarely get souls of that density."

"You don't get *her* soul at all."

"So you say. But then"—the herald tilted its egg-smooth head—"you said the stew was excellent, and your tongue paid for it. Words have weight in Greyhollow, Ulyana Kavanagh. Refusal spoken aloud is also a word. It binds *you* now. The court accepts your refusal of substitution as a formal term. You have just closed a door, and doors, once closed on our threshold, are expensive to reopen."

Cold walked up Ulyana's spine. She had refused a trap and been caught by refusing it. That was how this place worked. That was how it had always worked—that was why Sorrel had whispered warnings instead of instructions, because any instruction could be twisted into a term.

"Then let me say something else," Ulyana said, and made herself stand straight, and made her voice level. "I'll say it carefully, so listen well. I will pay my own debt with my own soul, if payment must be made. I will bargain for better terms, and I will walk away if the terms are rotten, and I will not trade anything that isn't mine. Write that in your book."

For a moment the herald was perfectly still. Then it unfolded from the wall, and its borrowed face shifted, and for the first time Ulyana saw something move beneath the smooth surface—something looking out, briefly, the way a fish looks up through ice.

"Interesting," the herald said softly. "Most debtors ask *how much*. You asked *whose*. Do you know how long it has been since anyone asked whose?"

"Then answer me a question in return," Ulyana said. "Why me? The ledger shows five souls sold. Four reclaimed. Why does the court want mine specifically? What's different about it?"

The sweetness in the air thickened. The candle relit itself, flame standing unnaturally tall.

"That," the herald said, "is above my station. But I am permitted one observation, as a courtesy between negotiators." It leaned close, close enough that she could see that its eyes, when they finally opened, were the grey of deep mine-shafts going down forever. "The court does not want your soul, Ulyana Kavanagh. The court wants what your soul is *for*."

It straightened, folded the refusal into its sleeve, and bowed—a gesture of genuine, horrible elegance.

"Terms received. Terms logged. The court will send for you when the fire has guttered low enough to make negotiation... brisk." At the alcove's mouth it paused, half-shadow already. "A final courtesy. The Calloway woman has entered Greyhollow. She travels alone, without warrant, which her superiors will call desertion. She carries a hunter's kit and a child's wooden bird, and she is asking after you at every door. The court finds this touching. The court also notes that hunters who enter honest country carrying warrants they've abandoned make excellent collateral themselves."

And it was gone, folded back into the wall's shadow like a letter slipped under a door.

Ulyana stood in the empty alcove a long time, the candle burning its tall wrong flame, the tally mark on her arm faintly warm.

Wren. Here. Alone, off-warrant—which meant she'd disobeyed orders, which meant everything Wren had built her life on, she'd set down to come looking. Carrying a child's wooden bird. Their bird, from the summer they were nine, whittled from fence-wood, lost for twenty years.

Ulyana pressed her palm flat against the oathmark until the warmth faded.

*Sorrel,* she thought, *you told me there were no ways around it. You didn't tell me the ways around it would all lead through everyone I love.*

She blew out the candle, climbed the stairs to the rented rooms, and found Dagny awake, sitting fully dressed on her bed, counting knots by lamplight. Two left.

"Pack your things," Ulyana said. "Tonight. And Dagny—when Wren comes asking, and she's coming—we were never in Greyhollow at all."

Dagny looked up, and for once didn't argue, and began untying her first knot.

* * *

Ilse was harder to wake—or rather, Ulyana found her already awake, sitting upright in the dark with her archives spread across her knees, pages she should not have been able to read by that little light. She was pale as paper herself.

"You spoke with it," Ilse said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"Your arm." Ilse's eyes went to the tally mark, and something in her face folded shut like a ledger closing. "There's ink on it that wasn't yours this morning. The court marks what it touches." She rose, began rolling her scrolls with quick, precise hands. "We leave now. Tonight. Before the fire drops another measure."

"Ilse." Ulyana caught her wrist. "The herald offered me substitutions. It had a list. Your name was on it—with a note that you'd consent, given adequate framing."

The lamplight stuttered. For one heartbeat Ilse's expression was an open door, and Ulyana saw through it into forty years of quiet, careful rooms—and then the archivist's face became a wall again, gentle and unreadable.

"Framing," Ilse repeated softly. "Yes. That is what the court calls persuasion." She pulled her wrist free, not roughly, and finished packing in silence. Only at the door, with her pack on and her habit hooded, did she speak again.

"Ask me in the morning, Ulyana. Whatever you need to ask. Ask it in daylight, on the road, away from this rock—because here, whatever I answer, the mountain hears, and the mountain keeps accounts." A pause. "And some answers, once given honestly, cannot be unsaid."

They went down through the sleeping terraces, three shadows among shadows, toward the mine-mouth and the dark beneath it—and Ulyana did not look back at the shrine alcove, where the candle had relit itself one final time, burning steady and patient, like something that knew exactly when she would return.
