## 14. Ilse's Bargain

The herald came to Ilse in the hour before dawn, as it always did, when the candles burned lowest and the mind was softest.

She had not slept. Sleep had grown difficult in Greyhollow, where the walls listened and lies sat on the tongue like river stones. She sat in the narrow cell the shrine-priests had lent them—three paces by four, a cot, a basin, a window of thick glass that showed nothing but the mountain's black flank—and she watched the candle gutter and waited for what she knew would come.

It came through the floor first. A smell of wet iron and old coins. Then the shadows at the base of the wall gathered themselves the way water gathers before it freezes, and the herald stood there, tall and thin as a crack in ice, wearing a face like a half-remembered acquaintance.

"Sister Anselm," it said, using the name she had worn for thirty-one years. Its voice was dry paper, patient. "Or shall we say Archivist Vey?"

"Ilse," she said quietly. "You may say Ilse. You always have."

"Because you gave it to us." The herald folded itself onto the stool across from her, though she never saw it move. "Everything given to us, we keep. That is our whole law, sister. Our whole love."

Outside the window, the mountain breathed. Somewhere below them, deep in the roots of the world, the Undercourt kept its ledgers, its lanterns, its long dark rooms full of dreaming souls. And somewhere down that same corridor of stone, a girl named Ulyana Kavanagh slept the bruised sleep of someone who had just learned her own life belonged to strangers.

Ilse had known for forty years. She had written it down herself.

* * *

She had been nineteen when the court first found her—young for an archivist, proud of her memory, vain about it in the way that careful people are vain, hiding the pride under humility like a coin under a tongue. The guild had sent her to copy the debt-book's margins, the fine print no one read anymore because no one wanted to. She had copied every clause. She had memorized every name.

And one night, copying by candlelight in the guildhall cellar, she had spoken aloud a single sentence of the contract—not a lie, not a betrayal, merely reading aloud, as archivists do—and something beneath the mountain had heard her, and answered.

*We have been waiting for a reader,* it said.

That was all it took. Not a signature. Not a soul, not then. Just attention. Just the terrible courtesy of being *noticed*. The Undercourt did not seize people; it befriended them, slowly, the way rot befriends timber. It asked small questions. What did Sorrel eat for breakfast. Which members doubted. Who among the five whose names were inked in the ledger had family still living above ground. And Ilse, flattered and frightened in equal measure, answered, because silence felt more dangerous than speech, and because—she admitted this only to herself, in the deepest drawer of her heart—a part of her loved being needed by something vast.

For forty years she had reported. Twice a year at first, then monthly, then whenever the herald wished. In return the court left the guild alone. Left the fire burning. Left little Ilse Vey, keeper of records, unharmed and unnoticed by anyone else in the world, which was the only safety she had ever truly wanted.

She told herself it was protection. She told herself it was stewardship. She had even, in her most honest hours, told herself it was penance—for she knew what the guild's bargain had cost the world. She had read the original terms. She knew the gods hadn't died.

She knew they were starving.

* * *

"You have been thinking," the herald said now, "of running."

Ilse folded her hands in her lap. Her knuckles ached; she was sixty-one years old, and the mountain's cold lived in her joints like a tenant who paid no rent. "I think many things. Thinking isn't a crime."

"No. Only breach is a crime." The herald tilted its borrowed face. "You brought them here, sister. The girl. The smuggler. You opened the road with your old words and your old keys. We are grateful. Gratitude, in our country, is also a ledger."

"I know."

"The girl is due to us. Her soul has hung in our keeping since the night of her birth, and the fire gutters, and the terms ripen. But—" and here the herald paused, and Ilse felt the pause the way one feels a hand hovering over one's shoulder—"you wish otherwise."

Ilse looked at the candle. Its flame leaned toward the herald, as flames did here, hungry for anything that wasn't stone.

"I wish her spared," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I taught her her letters." The truth came out of her simply, worn smooth by decades of handling. "Because she was eight years old and she copied the alphabet in the margins of the debt-book while I worked, and she drew a little dragon beside the letter D, and she asked me if dragons had ever really been warm. Because she is the last of them, the last member of a guild that meant to be kind, and everything cruel about that guild happened before she could walk." Ilse's voice caught, and she let it. There was no use performing composure before the court; it read flesh like vellum. "Because someone must end this without adding one more name to your books. And because I am tired, herald. Forty years is enough."

The herald studied her. When the Undercourt looked at you, it looked at everything—the pulse in your throat, the ledger of your breath, the debts stitched invisibly through your marrow.

"There is a way," it said.

Ilse's heart did not leap. Hearts hers age had learned not to leap. "There is always a way. Your ways are all stairs going down."

"This one goes down only once." The herald produced, from the shadow where its hands should be, a key.

Not metal. Bone, or something like bone, pale and faintly warm, carved along its length with script so small it seemed less written than grown. Ilse recognized the script. She had spent a lifetime recognizing scripts. This was oath-script, the first covenant-tongue, the language in which the guild had once bound itself to give magic away—and in which the Undercourt bound everything it touched.

"The vault of embers," the herald said, "is sealed against theft. It is not sealed against payment. If one stands at its door holding a debt already owed to the court, the door opens, for the door cannot tell the payer from the purchase." It set the key on the table between them, gently, the way one sets down an infant. "Carry this. Stand at the door. Speak your name in the old tongue. The vault will open, and the girl will take back her soul, and the ember will pass into her keeping, and the terms of the guild's ancient bargain will be discharged."

"And the price."

"Ah." The herald smiled with its borrowed mouth. It was not a cruel smile. That was the horror of it—it was the smile of a clerk confirming arithmetic. "You know our prices, archivist. They are never coin. They are always self."

* * *

Ilse had spent forty years cataloguing what the court took. Souls, yes—but also names, faces, first loves, griefs, languages, the taste of bread, the memory of mothers. The court was not greedy; greed wanted everything indiscriminately. The court was *precise*. It took exactly what a bargain required, no more, no less, and what it took it kept forever, polished and shelved and dreaming.

"What will you take from me?" she asked.

"That depends on what you ask us to make the payment out of." The herald laced its too-long fingers. "If you offer us nothing specific, we choose, and we choose deeply. Most who deal with us lose their selves entire. But you—" the borrowed face inclined, almost tenderly— "you have been our friend for forty years. We will let you specify."

Specify. Ilse turned the word over. There was mercy in it, and mercy from the Undercourt was a hook dressed as a gift.

"If I specify," she said carefully, "then whatever remains of me stays intact. My mind. My hands. My ability to walk out of that vault and live some remainder of a life."

"Yes. Whatever falls outside the specification remains yours. Ours is a lawful house."

Ilse closed her eyes. Behind her lids, forty years arranged themselves into columns, the way she organized everything: the reports she'd sent, the secrets she'd sold, the small betrayals stacked like coins until they made a fortune of ruin. She thought of Sorrel dying in the guildhall with the firelight on his face, whispering a debt he'd sworn to carry alone. She thought of the other three—old Marta, old Fenwick, poor Ashen whose ashes had been scraped off the doorstep two weeks ago, collected early because the fire was guttering faster than any of them had guessed.

She thought of Ulyana, asleep down the hall, dreaming whatever dreams a person dreams when she doesn't yet know how much of her is missing.

"I'll pay with my memories of the guild," Ilse said. "All of them. Every year, every member, every kindness and every sin. Take the whole archive out of me. Leave me my mind, my hands, my life—but leave me nothing of them."

The herald was quiet for a moment. Even the mountain seemed to lean closer.

"You understand what you are asking to lose," it said. "Your memories are your only remaining wealth, archivist. You are a woman built entirely of what she remembers."

"I am a woman built entirely of what she *betrayed*," Ilse said. "Let the court have the collection. It's yours anyway, in a sense. I only ever borrowed it from you."

"We do not want your guilt. Guilt is heavy and keeps poorly."

"Then take the rest. The good years too. The letters. The little dragon drawn beside the letter D." Her voice cracked clean through. "Take it all. That is the specification. Everything of the Guild of Small Mercies, out of my head, into your keeping, forever. In exchange: the key works, the door opens, the girl walks free, and Ilse Vey's account with your court is closed. Closed, herald. No further service. No further reporting. No calling-in of old favors when it suits you. Am I understood?"

The herald rose. It unfolded upward longer than the room should have allowed, its shadow climbing the wall past the ceiling, and for one naked moment Ilse saw it as it was beneath the borrowed face—something old and patient and terribly neat, a creature that had been balancing books since before books.

"You are understood," it said. "One amendment."

Her stomach dropped. "No amendments."

"One." It held up a single finger. "Memories cannot be taken cleanly while their subjects live nearby and press upon them daily. The extraction must occur after you have used the key—at the vault door, at the moment of opening. Until then, you will remember everything, and you will tell no one, and you will act as though nothing has been promised." The finger lowered. "This is not cruelty, sister. This is chemistry. And it is also, we confess, insurance."

Insurance. Meaning: if Ilse lost her nerve, or confessed, or tried to warn Ulyana off the descent entirely, the court could refuse the payment and reclaim the key, and everything would fall back into the old arrangement—Ulyana's soul forfeit, the ember dying, the gods waking ravenous under a sky that had forgotten them.

Meaning: Ilse would carry the secret to the very threshold of the vault, and only there would she be permitted to put it down.

"When?" she whispered.

"Soon. Days. The fire decides, not we. Watch its color." The herald began to sink into the floor's shadow, dissolving with the unhurried grace of ink in water. At the last it paused, and its borrowed eyes—she had never asked whose face it wore, and she suspected she had known once, long ago, and sold that knowledge too—regarded her with something that in any other creature she might have called respect.

"For what it is worth, archivist," it said, "in forty years, you were the best informant we ever had. And this"—the bone key gleamed once on the table between them, then was gone, transferred somehow to her pocket, cold as a knuckle against her hip— "is the finest report you will ever file."

Then it was gone, and the candle stood up straight again, and Ilse sat alone in the narrow cell with the mountain pressing down and the shape of her sacrifice already settling into her bones like weather.

* * *

She did not sleep. She took out her writing kit—ink, pen, the thin grey paper she had used for forty years of double lives—and she wrote nothing. What was there to write? The record of this night existed in only one place, and that place was scheduled for demolition.

Instead she walked.

Greyhollow at dawn was a town of stooped roofs and shuttered windows, built against the mountain's foot like a child pressed against a sleeping bear. Bargain-country. Everyone here spoke softly and meant exactly what they said, because lies hurt here—physically, visibly, a bloom of blood at the nose, a tremor in the hand—and honesty was therefore not a virtue but a survival skill. Ilse had always found it restful. Above ground, in Vharrow, in the guildhall, she had spent her whole life lying with her posture, her silences, her gentle deflections. Here, everyone bled honestly, and no one was ashamed of it.

She passed the shrine where the half-mad priest kept his vigil. He was awake, of course; he was always awake, muttering to the fire he claimed lived behind the mountain's skin. He saw her and stopped muttering, and his milky eyes followed her with the terrible clarity of the mad.

"You smell of the deep court," he said. "Old woman. Old woman. You've signed something."

"I sign things all the time. I'm an archivist."

"Not anymore." He grinned, gap-toothed, delighted. "After tomorrow, you're a blank page. I can see it standing behind you. Big white thing. Very patient." He cackled and went back to his fire-prayers, and Ilse walked on with her heart hammering and her face perfectly composed, because in Greyhollow composition was easy—only words betrayed you here, and she had said nothing.

Nothing was her oldest craft.

* * *

She found Ulyana in the shrine's common room, hunched over the smuggler's maps, arguing distances with Dagny Holt in a low fierce voice. Dagny looked up when Ilse entered, and Ilse watched the smuggler's eyes flick between them with a trader's instinct—reading weights, measuring trust, noting the archivist's sleepless night and filing it somewhere behind those shrewd grey eyes.

"Well?" Ulyana said. Her face was thinner than it had been a month ago. The road had carved it, and the knowing—knowing what she was owed, knowing what hunted her—had hollowed her further. But her eyes were still the eyes of the eight-year-old with the ink-stained fingers, still lit with that stubborn, unreasonable hope that Ilse had loved her for and, in the end, had sold her for, decade after decade, one small report at a time.

"The priest will guide you," Ilse said. "He knows the way to the fire. He's been talking to it for thirty years; it talks back. He's mad, but he's mad in the right direction."

"And below that?" Ulyana leaned forward. "The Undercourt. You've read the old accounts, Ilse. You know more than you're saying. You always know more than you're saying."

The words landed like a needle finding an old scar. Ilse smiled the smile she had practiced for forty years—warm, wry, self-deprecating, the smile of a harmless old woman.

"I'm an archivist," she said. "Knowing more than I say is the whole profession. When there's something you need to know, Ulyana, you'll find I've left it where you can reach it. That's all I've ever done. Left things where they could be reached."

It was true. It was even, in its rotten way, loyal. And because it was true, she could say it in Greyhollow without bleeding, and she did, and the words came out clean, and only she knew they were also a confession, a farewell, and an apology delivered thirty years in advance to a girl who couldn't hear it yet.

Dagny was still watching her. The smuggler said nothing—but her hand moved briefly to the charm-bag at her belt, the luck-charms Ulyana had made her, and Ilse understood that Dagny Holt, at least, suspected. Suspected and said nothing, because smugglers knew better than anyone that everyone aboard a sinking ship carried their own private cargo.

"Rest," Ilse told them both. "Eat. The descent will want everything you have."

She went back down the corridor toward her cell, and her hip brushed the pocket where the bone key lay, cold and patient, and behind her she heard Ulyana and Dagny resume their low argument over maps, planning their way into the dark, trusting her.

In her cell, Ilse took out the key and set it on the table and looked at it for a long time. Then she took her pen and, on the last sheet of grey paper she owned, wrote a single line—not a report, not a confession, only a marker, a breadcrumb for a mind that might soon be empty:

*Whatever I have forgotten, I forgot it for you.*

She folded it into the lining of her coat, over her heart, and blew out the candle, and sat in the dark listening to the mountain breathe, waiting for the fire to change color, and counting—in the old archivist's habit, the habit that would soon be taken from her—the days she had left of knowing who she was.
