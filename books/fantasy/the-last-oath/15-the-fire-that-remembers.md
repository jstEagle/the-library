## 15. The Fire That Remembers

The priest's name was Brother Ambrose, or so he claimed, though he answered to at least four other names depending on the hour and the weather. He lived in a hollowed-out bell tower at the edge of Greyhollow, in a nest of blankets and candle stubs and bird bones arranged in patterns that might have been devotion or might have been madness, and on the third morning after their arrival he looked at Ulyana across a breakfast of boiled barley and said, without preamble:

"You want to touch the fire."

It was not a question. Nothing Ambrose said was ever a question. Statements poured out of him like water from a cracked jug, some of them clean and some of them foul, and you drank what you could and wiped the rest from your chin.

"I do," Ulyana said.

"You shouldn't." He smiled then, and his smile was the worst thing about him, because it was gentle. "But you will. You're one of Sorrel's. I can smell the oathmark under your sleeve. It smells like candle smoke and promises." He ladled more barley into his bowl with hands that trembled until they touched the food, and then went still as stone. "The fire remembers everyone who ever fed it. It has been waiting a long time for someone from the Guild of Small Mercies to come back. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Good," said Ambrose. "Neither do I. That's why we're going down together. A madman and a debtor. The mountain loves an audience."

* * *

They left at dusk, because Ambrose refused to travel by daylight—"the fire sleeps shallow at noon, but at night it dreams loud, and dreaming is when it listens"—and took the old pilgrim stairs behind the shrine of Saint Verity, the steps worn to shallow spoons by centuries of knees that no longer bent in prayer. Dagny wanted to come. Ulyana told her to stay with Ilse at the wayhouse, watch the road, count the wagons. If Wren came—and Wren would come, sooner or later; the crown did not send its hunters on holidays—someone had to be watching who could lie with a straight face and run with a full pack.

Dagny had not liked it. Dagny did not like anything lately except strong drink and weak arguments, but she had gripped Ulyana's forearm hard enough to leave marks and said, "Come back up. That's the toll. Come back up, or everything I've spent on this road was wasted," and Ulyana had promised, because lying to Dagny Holt felt like kicking a dog that had already been kicked.

Now she followed the mad priest down into the dark, and the dark received her like an old debt coming due.

The stairs gave way to galleries, and the galleries to tunnels where the walls glittered faintly—not ore, not crystal, but something organic, veined, like the inside of a shell. The air grew warm and thick and sweet, sweet the way overripe fruit is sweet, sweetness with rot underneath it. Somewhere far below, water dripped with the patience of geological time. And somewhere else, closer, something hummed. Not sound exactly. A pressure. A note held too long by a throat that never needed to breathe.

"That's it," Ambrose said conversationally, as if remarking on birdsong. "That's the ember singing. It sings the same song it sang before the Godfall. Nobody taught it a new one." He walked with a lantern made of tin and glass, and moths—or things like moths, pale and soft-winged and eyeless—drifted around it in slow spirals. "Do you hear the words?"

"There are no words."

"There are always words. You're just not fluent yet." He stopped at a junction where three tunnels met, and pressed his palm flat against the veined wall, closing his eyes. His lips moved. When he opened his eyes again they were wet. "It says: *they promised to feed us.*" He wiped his face with his sleeve, embarrassed, matter-of-fact. "It's been saying that for two hundred years. Every day. *They promised to feed us.* I used to think it was talking about me. I used to bring it bread." A small, terrible laugh. "Bread. To a dragon's heart. Can you imagine."

Ulyana could not answer. Her mouth had gone dry as ash.

"The vault is below the flooded gallery," Ambrose said, moving on. "Mind the third step. It lies."

* * *

The flooded gallery was ankle-deep in black water so still it reflected the darkness overhead perfectly, so that Ulyana seemed to walk between two abysses with only a thin crust of stone to hold her. In the water's mirror she caught glimpses of herself—her face, her borrowed traveling coat, the sleeve pinned down over her wrist—and once, when she looked too long, a second figure walking beside her, wearing her outline but not quite her posture, its head turned toward her at an angle no neck should allow.

She did not look again.

The third step lied, whatever that meant; Ambrose simply stepped over a gap in the stair where there was plainly solid footing, and Ulyana, following his example, heard behind her—in the empty air where her heel would have landed—a soft wet sound, like a mouth closing.

After that she stopped counting the strange things. There were too many. Roots hung from ceilings that had no trees above them. Warm winds breathed out of side passages carrying smells that did not belong underground: woodsmoke, cut grass, her mother's kitchen, incense, blood. Once, distantly, she heard a choir. Once, much closer, she heard a child crying, and Ambrose took her arm and said, "Not real. Or real, but not here. Down here the difference stops mattering. Keep walking."

And all the while the humming deepened, rose, wrapped around her like wool, until she realized she was humming too, the same note, unconsciously, her chest vibrating with it, and that her eyes were streaming tears she had no memory of starting.

"It knows your bloodline," Ambrose said softly. "It's been waiting for a Kavanagh. Or whatever name your family wore before Vharrow burned the records. Names change. Debts don't."

* * *

The vault door was not a door. It was a wound.

A seam in the living rock, taller than three men, its edges fused shut with a glassy black material that gleamed like cooled lightning. Around it, carved deep into the walls, ran words in Old Vharrowan—the liturgical script, the language of the prayers that had once risen daily from every shrine in the city. Ulyana could read fragments of it; every guild-trained charm-seller learned enough to fake authenticity.

*WE GAVE SO THAT YOU MIGHT SLEEP.*
*WE SEALED IT WITH FIRE.*
*WE WILL FEED IT FOREVER.*

"Pretty words," Ambrose murmured, running his fingers along the letters. "Guild words. The founding generation cut these. Before my time. Before Sorrel's time, even." He produced a key from around his neck—an ugly little iron thing shaped like a tooth—and pressed it against the seam. "Every fifty years, the guild sends someone down to check the seal. To make sure the fire still burns. They call it the Long Maintenance. Very boring. Very holy. Nobody ever asks what the fire is *for*." He glanced back at her, and in the lantern light his madness looked almost like clarity. "You asked. That's why you get to go in. Curiosity is a kind of payment down here. The court accepts it gladly."

The seam split with a sound like a gasp, and heat rolled out—not burning heat, but the heat of a body, of breath, of a sleeping animal the size of a cathedral—and beyond it was a cavern, and in the cavern was the fire.

* * *

It was small.

That was the first thing. After the humming, after the pressure, after two hundred years of weight, Ulyana expected something vast, and instead the last dragon-fire sat in a basin of black glass no bigger than a wash-tub, a knot of flame the size of a fist, burning without fuel, casting light that moved wrong—falling upward sometimes, pooling in corners, bending toward Ulyana like a plant toward sun.

The second thing was that it was beautiful in a way that hurt. Not the beauty of gold or gems but the beauty of the moment before weeping, of music in a minor key, of a face you loved seen across a crowded room for the last time. Looking at it, Ulyana felt grief for things she had not lost yet, and for things lost before she was born, and for a city she had never visited, and she understood suddenly why people had once built shrines instead of armies.

"Go on," Ambrose whispered. "Touch it. That's what it wants. That's what it's been waiting to show someone." His voice shook. "I've touched it eleven times. Each time I forget a little more. But each time I understand a little more too. Fair trade. Everything down here is fair trade."

Ulyana knelt at the basin's edge. The heat kissed her face. The humming resolved, at last, into words—or into the memory of words, or into something older than either:

*Feed me.*

She reached out and laid her hand in the fire.

* * *

It did not burn. It poured.

Light went up her arm like water finding a channel, and the world folded, and Ulyana Kavanagh stood inside a memory that was not hers.

She stood on the slopes of the mountain above Greyhollow, but the mountain wore a different age: the forests were uncut, the sky was heavy and low and *full*, and the air itself was thick with presence. Gods. That was the only word. They hung in the sky like weather systems, vast and slow and radiant, and smaller ones moved through the valleys like deer through grass—gods of hearth-smoke and hedgerows and the first frost, small gods, domestic gods, gods that asked for milk on the doorstep and nothing more. The world was not ruled by them. It was *inhabited* by them, the way a house is inhabited by cats.

And she—she was seeing through another's eyes, someone standing on the slope, someone in guild-gray robes with an oathmark fresh and red on their forearm—watched the sky begin to darken.

Not with storm. With *hunger*. The great gods had begun to turn on one another. She felt it through the body she wore: a vibration in the teeth, a wrongness in the light. War above. The Godfall beginning. And on the mountainside, a circle of robed figures—the guild, the whole guild, dozens of them, kneeling in a ring around a dying dragon.

The dragon was enormous and old and its scales were the color of embers cooling, and it was not fighting them. It was *listening* to them. Its eye, huge as a cart wheel, rolled toward the lead speaker—a woman with gray in her braid and a voice like a blade being drawn slowly—and Ulyana knew her, knew her the way you know a face from a portrait, and did not yet know why.

*"The war will burn the world,"* the woman said. *"You are dying anyway. Give us your fire. We will use it to seal the hungry ones beneath sleep. We will keep the flame alive forever, and we will feed it, and the world will be spared."*

And the dragon—oh, the dragon laughed, a sound like rockslides and like lullabies, and it said, in a voice that Ulyana felt in her sternum rather than heard:

*"Forever is a long word for such short creatures. What will you feed it?"*

*"Our devotion,"* the woman said. *"Our prayers. Our names."*

The dragon's laughter died. Its eye dimmed, considering.

*"Devotion is wind. Prayers are ash. Names—" * the great head lowered, and the nostril-wind stirred the guildsmen's robes— *"names are meat. Very well. I accept. But hear my price, daughter-of-the-small-mercies: a fire must eat. If you feed it wind and ash, it will eat the only meat you've given it. It will eat the *names*. And a god with no name cannot be prayed to. And a god no one can pray to starves. And a starving god does not die."*

The woman hesitated. Ulyana felt her hesitation from inside, tasted it, sour as copper.

*"What does a starving god do?"*

*"It eats what comes near,"* said the dragon. *"It forgets it was ever loved. Remember this: the bargain buys peace, not safety. Peace is only hunger, postponed. Feed the fire true, every generation, or your descendants will stand where you stand and learn what you refused to learn tonight."*

And the guild swore. All of them, voices rising together in the Old Vharrowan liturgy, and Ulyana read the vow off their moving lips even as the memory blurred and churned: *we will feed it forever, our devotion, our prayers, our names, we will give magic away freely as the river gives water—*

And then the vision tore sideways, and she was falling through years like stones through a well.

* * *

She saw the sealing: the dragon's fire drawn out in long bright threads and woven over the mouths of the hungry gods' prison, the great ones sinking beneath the mountain into sleep like whales sinking into fathoms. She saw the small gods flee or fade, homeless, their shrines cold. She saw the first feeding ceremony: the guild gathered in the vault, offering their devotion, their prayers—and their *names*, spoken aloud and given into the flame, and the flame eating them, and the givers walking out of the vault slightly lighter, slightly vaguer, slightly less themselves, smiling.

She saw generations pass. She saw the feeding grow thin. The guild shrank—persecution, the crown's purges, magic outlawed, the Godfall rewritten by victors' pens as the gods' own death rather than their imprisonment. Fewer members. Fewer names to feed. The flame dimming by fractions so small that no single generation noticed, the way a man doesn't notice himself going blind.

She saw the year the feeding failed entirely. Saw the vault empty for a decade, then two. Saw the flame begin, very slowly, to eat the only meat left sealed within its reach: the sleeping gods' own dreams. Their memories. Their *names*, given willingly two centuries ago by people who trusted the guild's forever.

She saw the first god stir. A small one, a hearth-god, starving, mad with hunger, waking just enough to reach through the crack between worlds toward any voice that called it—and a widow in Vharrow praying over a dead son's grave, and the hearth-god seizing the prayer like a drowning sailor seizes driftwood, dragging the boy's shade back into his body, hollow, because the god had nothing to fill him with but hunger—

*The answered prayers,* Ulyana thought, sick with understanding. *It's not miracles. It's leakage. The seal is failing and they're reaching through it and every prayer that gets answered is a hand coming through the wall.*

And then the vision showed her the last thing, the thing she would carry up out of the mountain like a splinter in the eye.

She saw the ledger. The Ember Ledger, Sorrel's debt-book, but young—new-inked, in the founding guildmaster's own hand. Five souls pledged as collateral against the guild's failure to feed the fire: five children of guild blood, born after the sealing, their souls named as surety. Payment owed if the flame ever guttered. And beside the fifth entry, in the founding master's writing, a note in the margin, small and careful:

*The fifth line is the Kavanagh girl. Born under the right star. The court insisted—it wanted one soul of the founding blood, kept close, kept warm, a seed-corn of the original vow. Forgive us, little one. We chose you because you were strongest. We chose you because the fire likes you best.*

The vision ended.

* * *

Ulyana came back to herself on the floor of the vault, on her hands and knees, her face wet, her throat raw. How long she had been under she didn't know. Ambrose sat beside her, holding the lantern, saying nothing, which from him was a kind of mercy.

"The founding master," she rasped. "In the vision. The woman with the braid. Who was she?"

Ambrose looked at her for a long moment.

"You saw her?"

"I saw everything."

"Then you know," he said gently. "Everyone who touches the fire meets her eventually. First guildmaster of the Guild of Small Mercies. Mother of the bargain." He paused. "The Undercourt's regent wears her face these days. Has for a hundred years. Whether it *is* her, or merely keeps her, no one living can say. Perhaps you'll find out. Perhaps that's why the court wanted your soul kept close, little debtor. Seed-corn of the original vow." He smiled his terrible gentle smile. "The fire likes you best."

Ulyana pushed herself upright. Her legs shook. The flame in its basin burned on, patient, small, ravenous, and now that she had seen what she'd seen she could not stop seeing it: not a guardian light but a mouth. A mouth the guild had opened and promised to feed forever, and then forgotten how to feed.

The gods hadn't fallen. They'd been starved. Starved by her own guild, her own blood, her own inheritance—and the debt Sorrel had whispered about wasn't just her soul. It was all of it. The whole broken covenant, come due at last.

"We have to move," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which frightened her more than the trembling would have. "Tonight. Before the regent learns what I've seen. Because if the fire dies—if we let it die—everything above us wakes up hungry."

Ambrose began snuffing candles, unhurried. "And if you feed it?"

"Then I pay with my soul and the hunger waits for my grandchildren."

"Mm," said Ambrose. "There's usually a third option. There's always a third option. It's just expensive." He lifted the lantern and started for the seam. "Come along, little debtor. Someone's been knocking at my door since sundown, and mad as I am, I don't think it's a pilgrim."

Far above them, muffled through two hundred feet of living rock, came a sound Ulyana knew from her childhood—from inspections, from sealed doors, from the night the guildhall's gates closed on Sorrel for the last time.

Boots. Crown boots. Many of them, on the stairs behind them, descending.
