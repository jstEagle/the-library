## 16. Wren's Warrant

The warrant arrived at dawn, carried by a courier bird that had flown hard and arrived ragged, its feathers crusted with frost along one wing. Wren Calloway took the message tube from its leg in the grey light of the Greyhollow waystation yard, and even before she cracked the seal—the crown's black wax, stamped with the eye-and-scales of the Office of Inquiry—she knew what it would say.

She knew because she had written half of it herself.

*Evidence of unlicensed thaumaturgy confirmed. Answered prayers traced to Guild remnant. Detain all persons of interest. Report to Vharrow by fastest post. The Crown thanks you for your diligence.*

*The Crown thanks you.* She almost laughed. Fourteen years in the Office and she had never once been thanked without being asked to do something that would cost her.

The yard was still dark except for the lamp burning over the stable door, and the smell of straw and horse sweat and cold iron rose around her like a familiar coat. Somewhere inside the inn, Dagny Holt was sleeping—or pretending to sleep—in the room two doors down from Ulyana's empty one.

Wren stood in the cold a long moment, the warrant unrolled in her hands, watching her breath hang in the air.

Then she went inside to do her duty.

* * *

Dagny Holt was awake after all. Wren found her in the taproom below, sitting alone at a table near the dead hearth with a cup of something steaming and a set of dice laid out before her in a careful line, as though she were teaching them patience.

"You're up early," Dagny said, not looking up. "Or up late. Hard to tell which, in this town. The sun doesn't so much rise here as give up."

"Where is she?"

"Now that's an interesting question." Dagny turned one die over with a fingertip. "Because there's two answers to it, aren't there? There's where Ulyana Kavanagh is—which I don't rightly know, on account of she left before first light and didn't tell me—and then there's where you want me to *think* she is. And I suspect those are different places."

Wren pulled out the chair across from her and sat. The wood groaned. Everything in Greyhollow groaned; the whole town sat under the mountain's shoulder like a man hunched against rain, and even the furniture seemed to flinch.

"Dagny."

"Wren."

"I have a warrant."

"I know." Dagny finally looked up. Her eyes were the flat grey of river ice, and they did not widen or narrow or do any of the things eyes did when people were surprised. "You've had it since yesterday. You've been carrying it around in your breast pocket like a stone. You keep touching it when you think no one's watching. You've done that since you were nine years old, only back then it was your mother's locket and you'd stolen it to sell."

Wren's hand dropped away from her breast pocket.

"That was one time," she said. "I was going to give it back."

"You were going to buy sweets with it and then feel terrible forever. Which is more or less what you're doing right now, only the stakes are worse and the sweets are further away." Dagny lifted her cup, blew on it, drank. "So. Read it to me, Inspector. Let's hear the law in all its glory."

Wren took out the warrant. Unrolled it again, though she had it memorized by now, every clause, every seal. "*Detain all persons aiding or abetting the fugitive mage known as Ulyana Kavanagh. All such persons to be conveyed to Vharrow under guard for examination by the Office of Inquiry.*"

"*Examination,*" Dagny repeated. She smiled, but it was a thin thing, stretched over something else. "That's a nice word. In my trade we call it the same thing by a different name. When the harbor master wants his cut, he calls it *tolls*. When the guild bosses want a man broken, they call it *questioning*. Everyone dresses the wolf in different clothes."

"The Office doesn't torture people."

"No," Dagny agreed pleasantly. "It just sends them somewhere the cells are cold and the questions are long, and lets the winter do the work. I've heard about the Examination Halls. Everyone in my line of work has. It's practically a recruitment poster for my trade—half the smugglers in Vharrow started running goods because someone they loved spent a season under *examination* and came out wrong."

"Dagny." Wren leaned forward. The lamp between them guttered and steadied. "Listen to me. If you come with me quietly—if you don't make me put the irons on—I can write in my report that you cooperated fully. Cooperating detainees get better quarters. They get interviewed instead of examined. There's a difference. It's not a small difference."

"And if I run?"

"Then I chase you. And I catch you. And it goes worse."

"You always could run," Dagny said, almost fondly. "Even as a girl. You ran like the ground owed you money." She set down her cup and spread her hands, palms up, an old smuggler's gesture—*nothing hidden, nothing held*. "But I'm not going to run, Wren. I'll tell you why. Running proves their story. A woman who runs from the law has something to hide, and everything she touches afterward turns to evidence. Whereas a woman who sits very still and lets them take her—" she tapped the table with one knuckle, twice, "—she keeps her hands clean, and clean hands are worth more than fast feet. That's the first lesson of my trade. You should learn it too. It applies to yours."

Wren felt something twist behind her ribs. "You're not making sense."

"I'm making perfect sense. You just don't like the tune." Dagny stood, unhurried, and held out her wrists. They were scarred at the edges of the cuffs' reach, old rope marks gone silver with years. "Go on then, Inspector. Do your duty. But do me one courtesy first."

"What?"

"When you send your report to Vharrow—and you will send it tonight, because waiting would be another kind of hiding—write down exactly what I tell you to write. Word for word. Will you do that?"

Wren hesitated. Every instinct she had, every year of training, said: *never let a detainee dictate correspondence.* And beneath that, older and quieter, a girl's voice saying: *you promised me once you'd never lie for anyone, so don't start lying now.*

"Say it," Wren said.

Dagny's grin came back, brief and real. "Write: *the smuggler Dagny Holt states that Ulyana Kavanagh departed Greyhollow before dawn on the mountain road, heading for the Undercourt passes, and that the smuggler made no attempt to stop her, because the smuggler owes the Kavanagh family a debt older than the crown's law and intends to pay it in full.*"

"That last part will hang you."

"It might." Dagny shrugged. "But it's true, and true things weigh less than lies when they're hung around your neck. Trust me. I've worn both."

* * *

Wren wrote the report that night by lamplight, in the waystation's little office, with the frost climbing the window in fern-patterns and the ink freezing in the well until she warmed it against the lamp glass.

She wrote it three times.

The first draft was honest in the way reports were supposed to be honest—dates, movements, corroborations. She described how she had traced the answered prayers north from Vharrow, shrine by warming shrine, each one a small impossible miracle blooming along the road like crocuses through snow. She described the guild connection: the mark she'd found seared into the doorpost of the burned man's room, the oath-cipher of a guild that had officially ceased to exist forty years ago. She described Greyhollow, the waystation, the smuggler.

And then she reached the part about Ulyana, and her pen stopped.

Because what was she supposed to write? *Subject identified as Ulyana Kavanagh, formerly of Candle Lane, who taught me to swim in the Tallowmere canal when we were eight and held my head when I threw up my first tankard at fourteen and stood beside me at my mother's funeral because my father couldn't bear to.* The Office wanted a description: height, hair, distinguishing marks. They wanted a threat assessment. They wanted her to reduce fifteen years of friendship to a wanted poster.

In the end she wrote: *Subject is a charm-seller of minor ability. Subject is not violent. Subject is believed to be seeking the source of the answered prayers, motive unknown.*

*Motive unknown.* It was the kindest lie available to her, and it wasn't even a lie—it was the truth wearing a blindfold. She did know the motive. She simply couldn't prove it, and couldn't say it, because the truth was that Ulyana was chasing something the crown had spent forty years insisting was dead, and if Wren wrote that down in official ink, the Office would stop sending warrants and start sending soldiers.

She sealed the third draft. Held the wax a moment too long, until it burned her thumb—a small bright pain, a penance, though she couldn't have named what for.

Then she gave it to the courier bird's handler, and watched the bird go up into the dark, south toward Vharrow, toward the towers and the Examination Halls and the Minister whose signature she'd forged onto nothing yet but whose attention she had now invited down upon this whole frozen valley.

*There,* she thought. *Done. Duty done.*

It tasted like copper coins and canal water.

* * *

She went back down to the cells.

Greyhollow's jail was a single cell carved into the mountain's root-stone, which meant it stayed at the same temperature summer and winter, which meant cold. Dagny sat on the bench with her back to the wall and her dice out again, rolling them one at a time against the far wall, catching them without looking.

"You didn't have to stay," she said, as the key turned. "Watching me sit in a hole isn't in your job description."

"My job description says convey detainees under guard. Guard means present." Wren sat down across from her, on the floor, because there was no second bench. The stone bit through her trousers. She didn't care. "Besides. I have questions."

"Ask, then. I'm a captive audience. Best kind."

"The debt you mentioned. To the Kavanagh family." Wren kept her voice level, professional, though the question underneath it was anything but. "What is it?"

Dagny rolled a die. A five. She caught it without looking, which should have been impossible, and tucked it into her sleeve.

"You know the story everyone tells about the Godfall?" she said. "How the gods died, and magic broke, and the crown outlawed it to keep people safe?"

"Everyone knows that story."

"Mm. Well. My mother told a different one. She was a riverwoman on the Tallowmere before the purges, back when charmsellers worked the banks openly." Dagny's voice had changed—flattened into the cadence of recitation, a story told so many times it had worn smooth as a stair-stone. "She said the gods didn't die all at once. They starved. Slowly. And there were people who fed them—small magics, small offerings, keeping the little gods alive through the worst of it. And one family in Vharrow fed more than most. Fed them past all sense, past all safety. Spent themselves hollow doing it."

Wren went very still. "The Kavanaghs."

"The Kavanaghs," Dagny agreed. "My mother bought a charm from Sorrel Kavanagh's daughter the winter my brother was dying of lung-rot. Cost her everything she had. And it worked—not a miracle, nothing the crown would burn anyone for, just enough warmth in his chest to see him through till spring. And when my mother tried to pay more, because it had worked, because it was worth more than she'd given—the charm-woman wouldn't take it. Said the guild didn't charge for mercy, only materials." Dagny looked at her then, directly, and her river-ice eyes were not flat at all. "My mother swore a debt on the water that day. Debts sworn on water don't lapse. They don't forgive. They wait."

"So you've been protecting her because of your mother."

"I've been protecting her because some things are worth more than the law, and I learned which ones young." Dagny rolled another die. Three. Caught. Sleeve. "Your turn for honesty, Inspector. Why are *you* down here? Your report's sent. Your duty's done. You could be asleep, or drinking, or writing yourself a pretty justification for all this. Instead you're sitting on cold rock talking to a smuggler. What does the law call that?"

The law called it nothing, because the law had no name for it. Wren stared at the dice, at the frost-light coming faintly through the high slit of a window, and told the truth because she was too tired to do otherwise.

"When I got the warrant," she said, "my first thought wasn't *how do I serve the crown.* It was *how do I keep her alive through whatever comes next.* I've been telling myself for two days that those are the same goal. That bringing her in gently is protecting her. That the Office examines and releases, mostly, usually, if the case is minor." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "But she's not minor anymore, is she? Answered prayers. A guild cipher. A dead man burned from the inside. This stopped being minor the moment I reported it. And I reported it anyway. Because that's what I *do.* I report. I've never once not reported."

"No," Dagny said softly. "You haven't. That's the tragedy of you, Wren Calloway. You're honest in a city that runs on lies, and so they use you like a blade—clean edge, no questions. And you let them, because being used by something true-feeling is easier than choosing for yourself."

"I chose to come down here."

"Did you?" Dagny smiled. "Or did you just follow the same pull that's been dragging you north since Vharrow? Be honest. When did you last sleep properly? When did you last eat a meal you tasted?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. The truth was she hadn't slept properly since the widow's son had walked home with dead eyes; hadn't tasted food since the burned man. Something had been pulling her north for weeks, some current under the ice of her own discipline, and she had dressed it up as investigation because dressing things up was what she did best.

"The prayers are wrong," she said instead. "That's why I'm really here. Not the warrant. The prayers. In Vharrow, when the first ones came, people wept with joy. By the time I left, they were afraid to pray. The miracles started asking for things back. And nobody at the Office wants to hear it—they just want the mage responsible, one arrest, problem closed." She pressed her palms against the cold stone. "But it's not closing, Dagny. It's opening. Whatever's answering those prayers, it's getting stronger. And it's getting *hungrier.*"

"Yes," Dagny said simply. "It is. And your friend has gone to meet it."

The cell went quiet. Somewhere above them, in the inn, a floorboard creaked; the wind worried at the eaves; the mountain itself seemed to breathe, slow and vast and indifferent.

Wren got up. Her knees ached. She was thirty-one years old and her knees ached on cold stone, and tomorrow she would have to decide whether to requisition horses and follow the mountain road, deserting her post, or stay in Greyhollow guarding a prisoner she no longer believed needed guarding, and either choice would be the wrong one, and she would live inside whichever wrongness she picked for the rest of her life.

At the door she paused.

"Dagny. If I ride north after her—if I leave you here under the waystation keeper's watch—will you behave?"

Dagny considered the ceiling. "Define *behave.*"

"Don't escape."

"Oh, I won't escape." Dagny's grin flashed white in the lamplight. "Escaping's for people with something to hide. I told you. Clean hands. Besides—" she drew the five-die from her sleeve and set it on the floor between them, deliberate as a chess move, "—wherever you're riding, Inspector, I have a feeling you'll need someone who knows these passes. And I have a feeling you'll realize that around the second fork in the road, and come back for me. Dice don't lie. They just wait."

Wren looked at the die for a long moment. Then, because she had no better answer and because some stubborn girlhood reflex made her, she picked it up and pocketed it.

"That's theft," Dagny observed.

"So report me."

* * *

Two hours before dawn, Wren saddled her horse in the freezing yard and rode north out of Greyhollow without a requisition, without orders, without a plan beyond a road and a name.

She left the warrant behind, weighted under a stone on the waystation desk where the keeper would find it, along with a note in her precise inspector's hand:

*Gone in pursuit of subject. Detainee Holt remains secured. Send reinforcements to the Undercourt passes. Tell no one else.*

The last line was a lie, or close enough to one. She was already composing, in her head, the report she would never send: the one that began *I followed her because the prayers are wrong and someone has to witness what's actually happening,* and ended, somewhere in the dark of the mountain passes, with a confession she couldn't yet shape into words.

Behind her, Greyhollow slept under the mountain's shadow. Ahead, the road climbed into passes where shrines that had been cold for forty years were glowing like coals breathed upon, and where her friend was walking toward the oldest debt in the world.

Wren rode into the dark after both of them—the law and the woman—and understood, with the clarity that comes only from doing the irreversible, that she could not serve them both much longer.

The frost thickened on her cloak. The shrines brightened ahead, one by one, like eyes opening.

She did not look back.
