## 17. Descent

The key Ilse had traded away was small and ugly—a sliver of black iron shaped like a tooth, cold enough to burn. Ulyana wore it on a cord against her sternum, and all the way down the mountain road she felt it ticking against her heartbeat like something alive that had not yet decided whether to wake.

Dagny walked beside her with her hands still cuffed behind her, because Wren's men had lost the key to the manacles somewhere between the interrogation tent and the moment Ilse had appeared out of the rain with a writ that should not have existed. The writ had cost Ilse everything—forty years of archives, every ledger, every letter, every scrap of the guild's written memory, surrendered to the crown's clerks in exchange for one smuggler's freedom. Ulyana had watched her do it. She had watched Ilse carry the boxes out into the courtyard one by one, unhurried, precise, her face as smooth as a locked door, and when the last box was gone Ilse had turned to Ulyana and said only: *Go down before they change their minds.*

She hadn't said goodbye. That was the part Ulyana kept returning to, turning it over like a coin worn past reading. Forty years of a life given away, and no goodbye.

"Stop brooding," Dagny said. "You're doing it out loud. Your whole face is doing it."

"I'm not brooding."

"You're brooding at a volume I can hear from inside your head." Dagny rattled the manacles for emphasis. They were iron, plain iron, but Wren's office stamped its work with quiet wards, and every few hundred paces the cuffs gave off a faint hum, like a struck tuning fork remembering its note. "Besides, if anyone gets to brood, it's me. I'm the one walking to my death in jewelry."

Ulyana stopped on the road long enough to look at the cuffs properly. She had been avoiding it—avoiding the shape of what Wren had done, what Wren's office made—but there was no more room on this road for avoidance. The mountain was close now. Greyhollow's slag-heaps rose black against a sky the color of old pewter, and the air coming down off the slopes tasted of rust and deep water.

"Hold still."

"Oh, I *love* this," Dagny said. "Every time you say *hold still*, something terrible happens to me."

Ulyana took the cuffs in both hands. The oathmark under her sleeve warmed—not painfully, not yet, but with the attention of something that had noticed her noticing. She thought about what Sorrel had taught her, in the last weeks of his life, when he'd thought she wasn't listening: *A charm is just a promise small enough to keep.* She whispered one now. Not a spell—the old words for spells were gone, burned out of the world or bought out of it—but a promise, said the way promises are said over cradles and deathbeds, which is to say completely.

*Let go of her. She's paid enough for other people's debts.*

The cuffs shivered. The hum changed pitch, climbed, and then the iron went soft as candle wax and slid off Dagny's wrists like something ashamed of itself.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

"That," Dagny said finally, flexing her fingers, working the blood back into them, "shouldn't have worked."

"No."

"You haven't done anything like that since—" Dagny stopped herself. She was good at stopping herself; it was half of what smuggling was. But her eyes finished the sentence anyway. *Since Vharrow. Since you buried it. Since we were girls and you swore you never would again.*

"It answered too fast," Ulyana said quietly. "I barely asked."

They stood on the road and looked at each other, and the mountain looked at both of them.

"Well," said Dagny, rubbing her wrists, "at least we know the Undercourt will be pleased to see you."

* * *

Greyhollow had been dying for sixty years and refused to admit it. The mine-mouths gaped along the valley wall like a row of pulled teeth; the town above them was built entirely of things waiting to be sold or abandoned—sheds, hoists, carts with one wheel missing. The people who remained had the particular silence of those who live above something vast and have learned not to listen to it.

But they listened tonight. Ulyana could see it in the way the lamplighters worked, hurrying, doubling back, lighting lamps that didn't need lighting. In the way an old woman in a doorway pressed her thumb to her lips and then to the doorframe, twice, three times—an old gesture, pre-Godfall, a warding nobody had needed in two generations and nobody had quite forgotten either.

"The shrines are warm," Ulyana murmured. On the walk up from the valley floor they had passed three of them: little stone niches in walls, hollowed for offerings, empty for decades. Each one had been freshly swept. One held bread. One held a copper ring. The third held nothing at all, but the stone around it was warm to the touch, warm as a sleeping animal, and when Ulyana had laid her palm against it something far below had shifted its weight, slow and enormous, like a body turning over in bed.

"They've been warm for a month," said the innkeep where they bought their last hot meal, a woman with forearms like ship cable and eyes that had seen everything worth seeing twice. She said it the way people report weather. "Used to be you couldn't get a prayer to stick anywhere in this valley. Now they stick too well. My sister prayed for rain in the dry summer and got a flood that took her roof." She set down their bowls. Her voice dropped. "Whatever's down there, it's awake, and it's *listening*, and it doesn't know how to be careful anymore. You going into the mines?"

"Yes," Ulyana said.

The innkeep looked at them both—at Dagny's smuggler's coat, at the place where Ulyana's sleeve hid her mark—and then did something Ulyana would remember for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be. She reached across the table and took Ulyana's hand and pressed it flat against the wood, the way you'd press a seed into soil.

"Then go down kindly," she said. "That's all. Whatever you meet down there—it was a person once. Go down kindly."

* * *

The mine they wanted was called the Old Wife, and it had been closed for fifty years, sealed with a timber gate and a crown placard that read CONDEMNED — SOUL HAZARD in letters that had faded almost to apology.

"Soul hazard," Dagny read aloud. "Charming. Very bureaucratic. You'd think they'd have put something useful, like *abandon hope* or *mind the gap*."

"They weren't wrong, though." Ulyana put her shoulder to the gate. The timber was soft with rot; it gave on the second push with a sound like a rib cracking. Cold air rolled out of the dark beyond—not dead air, not stale mine-air, but *moving* air, breathing air, air with intention in it. "Sorrel told me once. After the Godfall, when the guild started selling, this was where they brought them. The souls. There were shafts here that already went deeper than any map. The court beneath liked them. Said they came *pre-hollowed*."

"Poetic," Dagny said, and lit her lantern, and her hands were not quite steady, and she did not pretend otherwise, which Ulyana loved her for.

They went in.

The first hundred fathoms were ordinary darkness—the honest kind, the kind rock makes. Their boots rang on old rails. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of centuries. Ulyana counted her breaths the way Sorrel had taught her, four in, six out, and let the mountain's closeness press against her instead of frightening her.

At the second hundred fathoms, the dreams began.

It started as light. A glow ahead, sourceless, the color of the moment just before waking. Then sound—or almost-sound, voices heard through a wall, rising and falling in patterns that were almost speech. Then, rounding a bend where the tunnel widened into an old staging chamber, they saw them.

Souls.

There was no better word, though Ulyana reached for several—*echoes, shades, remnants*—and none fit. They hung in the chamber like lanterns hung for a festival that had been forgotten mid-celebration. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Each one a soft gold-white radiance roughly the size of a heart, and each one wearing, faintly, at its center, a face. And each one dreaming. Ulyana could see it—the flicker of them, the way their light swelled and dimmed in rhythms like breath, like sleep, like someone talking in their sleep. A man's face, young, mouth moving: *the horses, mind the horses, the ice is bad past the ford.* An old woman, serene: *stir it clockwise, love, always clockwise.* A child, and Ulyana had to look away from the child, and then made herself look back, because the innkeep's voice was in her ear: *go down kindly,* and kindness meant not flinching.

"They're not in pain," Dagny whispered. It wasn't a question, but it wanted answering.

"No." Ulyana's throat was tight. "That's the horror of it, isn't it? They're *comfortable*. Whoever sold them sold them whole—they kept the shape of the lives they came from. They just... dream them, forever, down here, in the dark, and no one ever comes."

"Someone came," Dagny said. "Us."

"We're not rescue. We're a debt collection." Ulyana reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a bird—and let her fingertips come within a hair's breadth of the nearest soul-light. The dreamer was the young man with the horses. His face turned toward her warmth the way flowers turn toward sun. *Mind the horses,* he murmured, to her, to the dark, to no one. *The ice is bad past the ford.*

Her chest ached so badly she nearly couldn't speak. "Ilse's books said five souls sold by our guild. Four reclaimed." She drew her hand back. "Which means one of these isn't guild stock. Which means some of these belong to other bargains, other sellers, other debts. We can't free them. Not tonight."

"Tonight," Dagny repeated. She looked at the chamber full of dreaming faces and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was about to do something reckless and call it commerce. "But you're writing down where this chamber is. Chalk on the wall. Big. Because when this is over—if anything of us is left over—we are coming back with rope and lanterns and every holy word Ilse ever archived, and we are carrying these people up into the daylight. Every single dreaming bastard of them."

Ulyana looked at her—this sharp-tongued, soft-hearted woman who had spent her life moving contraband through cracks in the law and had somehow never once moved her conscience out of the light—and felt something settle in her chest. Something that had been loose and rattling since Sorrel died. Something like a keel finding water.

"Yes," she said. She took out her chalk. "Yes, we are."

She wrote it large: *SOULS HERE. WE WILL RETURN.* And below it, because the mountain was listening and she wanted it to hear her say it plainly: *This is a promise.*

Down in the deeps, something very old turned over in its sleep—and smiled.

* * *

Below the soul-chamber, the mine stopped being a mine.

There was no seam in the rock to mark the change, but Ulyana felt it the way you feel a threshold in a house—the difference between the stairwell and the cellar, between owned ground and ground that merely tolerates you. The rails ended. The timbers ended. Even the rot ended. The tunnel continued cut into living stone, smooth-walled, and the cuts were wrong: too clean, too curved, made by no tool meant for human hands. And the walls had begun to sweat a faint luminescence, veins of pale blue-green light running through the stone like marrow through bone, enough to see by, enough to make their shadows swim.

Here the dreams changed too.

Above, the souls dreamed their old lives, small and human and heartbreaking. Down here, the sold souls dreamed *forward*. Ulyana passed alcoves where single lights hovered, and the faces in them were not remembering—they were imagining. A woman whose lips shaped the same phrase over and over: *green, I want to stand in green, grass, real grass, rain.* A man who dreamed in numbers, endless patient sums, as though arithmetic were a country he was building one brick at a time. And one light—older than the others, dimmer, worn like a river stone—who simply sang. No words. A lullaby in a language that had died before Vharrow had a name. Ulyana stood and listened until Dagny touched her elbow, and even then the melody followed her down the tunnel for a hundred paces before it let her go, the way music follows you out of a church.

"How far down does it go?" Dagny asked. She'd stopped making jokes an hour ago. Her voice had gone quiet and level, the voice she used in storm-weather.

"Ilse's notes said the Undercourt lies beneath the roots of the mountain. Nine descents. This is the third." Ulyana touched the black-iron key at her breastbone. It was warmer now. It ticked faster, like a small heart quickening as it neared home. "The fourth gate is the first one that will actually be guarded."

"Guarded by what?"

"By the terms," Ulyana said. "In Greyhollow, bargains bind. Lies hurt. That's the whole architecture of the place—it's built out of enforced honesty. So the gates don't need soldiers. They need questions."

Dagny considered this. "I hate it already."

They descended. The fourth gate, when it came, was exactly that: a doorway of seamless stone in the tunnel wall, framed in carved script that predated every alphabet Ulyana knew, and standing before it—though *standing* was generous, though *before* was generous—was a figure made of the same blue-green light as the veins in the rock, shaped roughly like a person the way a knot is roughly like a tree.

It spoke without sound. The words arrived directly behind Ulyana's eyes, tasting faintly of copper:

***WHAT DO YOU CARRY?***

And here was the thing about Greyhollow, about the deep places where the old bargain still held: you could not lie. Ulyana opened her mouth to say *supplies*, and the lie sat on her tongue like a coal, and she understood that speaking it would scald her throat shut. So she stood in the swimming shadows and told the truth, all of it, to a door, in the dark, while her oldest friend watched.

"A key I don't fully understand. A friend I don't deserve. A promise chalked on a wall above us. A name I may not bring home. And an ember I intend to steal from a court that has kept my soul in a lantern for twenty-three years." Her voice shook on the last part and she let it shake. "I carry a debt. I've come to pay it differently."

Silence. The figure's light pulsed, once, twice—considering.

***THE KEY OPENS WHAT THE KEY WAS MADE FOR,*** it said at last. ***NOTHING ELSE. GO DOWN KINDLY.***

And the stone swung open, and the cold breath of the true Undercourt rose up to meet them—air that smelled of ink and honey and old, old grief—and Ulyana Kavanagh squared her shoulders, and stepped through.

Behind her, far above, chalked in a dead miner's staging chamber, a promise glowed faintly warmer.

Ahead, down nine descents and one impossible bargain, in a market that dealt in selves, something wearing her face lifted its head—and, for the first time in twenty-three years, began to hope.
