## 18. The Soul Market

The mines ended where the dreaming began.

Ulyana felt the boundary before she saw it—a change in the air like stepping from cold water into a warm bath that had something drowned in it. The tunnels of Greyhollow's undercity had been worked stone and rot and rusted rail; beyond them, the rock itself grew strange, veined with seams of pale light that pulsed in slow rhythm, as if the mountain were breathing through its wounds.

Dagny stopped at the mouth of the last gallery and held up her lantern. "There it is," she said quietly. "The Soul Market."

Ulyana had imagined a market all her life without knowing it—stalls, shouting, haggling, the ordinary music of trade. What lay before her was none of that. It was a cavern so vast the far wall existed only as a suggestion of darkness, and filling it were lanterns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They hung from iron chains that descended into the dark from heights no eye could follow, clustered on shelves carved into the living rock, floated in still black pools where no ripple ever came, and each one burned with a light that was not firelight.

It was the light she had seen in mirrors all her life and never trusted. The light behind her own eyes when she woke at three in the morning and could not say why she was grieving.

"Oh," Dagny said softly. "Oh, that's not right."

"They're souls." Ulyana's voice came out flat, stripped of everything but fact. She had known this was coming since Sorrel died with her hand clutching his and his mouth shaping words like *debt* and *yours* and *forgive*. Knowing had been a map. This was the country itself. "Every one of them. Someone sold. Someone traded. Someone pledged."

The nearest shelf held perhaps forty lanterns, each of smoked glass and tarnished brass, each burning its private color. One glowed the deep green of moss after rain. Another flickered amber, restless, like a dog circling to lie down. A third burned nearly white, steady and small, and when Ulyana looked directly at it she felt an old woman's patience settle over her shoulders like a shawl—the sensation so specific, so *occupied*, that she stepped back before she understood what she was doing.

"Don't look too long at any of them," said a voice from the shadows between the shelves.

* * *

The keeper of that row was difficult to describe afterward, and Ulyana suspected that was by design. It had the shape of a person the way a glove has the shape of a hand—correct in outline, hollow in implication. Its face was pleasant. That was the worst of it. It wore courtesy like a uniform.

"Visitors," it said. "How rare. The market receives pilgrims, sometimes. Debtors, more often. Which are you?"

"Neither," Ulyana said. "I'm here for what's mine."

Something moved behind the keeper's pleasant face—not a smile, exactly, but the memory of one. "Everyone here is here for what's theirs. That is rather the point of a market. What did you bring to trade?"

"Nothing. I brought nothing. I'm not selling."

"Then I'm afraid you've come a very long way to be disappointed." The keeper gestured with a hand that had one finger too many, gracefully arranged. "Nothing leaves the market unpaid. That isn't cruelty. That's grammar. You may as well ask a sentence to mean two things."

Dagny shifted beside her, and Ulyana heard the smuggler's breathing go shallow and controlled—the sound of a woman calculating exits. There were no exits. There hadn't been since the second gallery, when the tunnels behind them had sealed themselves with a sound like a book closing.

"I'm looking for a soul pledged by the Guild of Small Mercies," Ulyana said. "Five generations ago. Pledged at my birth. The name attached to the debt is Kavanagh."

For the first time, the keeper went still. Truly still—insects still, weather still, the stillness of something that has just recalculated everything.

"You," it said. "You're the outstanding balance."

"I'm the person you took it out on."

"The same thing, below the mountain." The keeper turned and began walking down the aisle of lanterns, and though it had not invited them, its back said *follow*, and their feet obeyed before their pride could object. "Come. You should see how we keep things. Most debtors never do. They pay from above, through agents, through grief, through time—and they sleep beautifully, never once imagining a room like this."

The aisles went on. Ulyana lost count of the shelves, then of the colors, then of the small distinct griefs each light carried. Some lanterns hummed. Some wept condensation that ran down the glass like tears and pooled on the brass, and the pools never dried. In one vast alcove, hundreds of lights burned in perfect unison, brightening and dimming together, and Ulyana realized they must have belonged to people who had made their bargain together—a congregation, maybe, or a crew, or a family that had sold itself whole to survive a winter.

"How many?" Dagny asked. Her voice was rough. Smugglers, Ulyana thought, spent their lives moving goods across lines drawn by men who never asked what else crossed those lines at night.

"Here? Forty thousand. Below this floor? We don't count anymore. Counting became cruel." The keeper paused at a junction where six aisles met beneath an archway of fused lantern-glass, generations of vessels melted into one another until the arch looked like frozen candle-flame. "Your guild's holdings are in the older quarter. We keep long accounts separately. Sentimentally, some would say. I would say: accurately. Old debts are load-bearing."

* * *

The older quarter was colder.

Not temperature-cold—Ulyana's breath didn't fog—but a cold of attention, as if the dark itself had turned to look at them. The lanterns here hung lower, nearer the ground, and there were fewer of them, and each was housed in finer work: silver cages, filigreed iron, one vessel of clear crystal that held its soul-light like water holds the moon.

And then she saw it.

She knew it before she understood it, the way you know your own handwriting on an envelope before you read the address. It hung alone at the end of the oldest aisle, on a chain of black iron, in a lantern of greenish glass—Vharrow glass, cheap glass, the kind night-market charms came wrapped in—and it burned the color of her own eyes in a certain slant of morning light, a grey-green the color of the river in thaw.

Inside the light, a face formed. And the face was hers.

Not a reflection. Reflections wait for you. This face moved on its own—blinked, pressed close against the inside of the glass, left smears of light where its palms flattened against the curve. It had her mother's jaw and the scar on her chin from the fall off the tannery roof when she was nine. It looked tired in a way Ulyana had never allowed herself to look, tired the way a thing gets tired when it has been awake for twenty-six years in a bottle.

"No," the face said. Its voice reached her thin and distant, as if shouted up from a well. "No, no—you can't be. Go away. Please. Please go away."

"Dagny," Ulyana said. Her voice sounded wrong to her. Far away. "Is that—"

"That's got your face, love." Dagny's hand found her arm, gripped hard enough to bruise. "That's got your face, and I'd cut the chain myself if cutting chains meant anything down here, which it doesn't."

The keeper stood aside, hands folded, radiating the satisfaction of a merchant whose finest piece has finally drawn a buyer. "This," it announced gently, "is the pledge of the Guild of Small Mercies. Fifth generation. Outstanding. Do you wish to inspect it? Inspection is permitted. Possession is not."

Ulyana stepped forward.

Up close, the lantern was warm. That undid her more than anything—the warmth of it, ordinary as a hearthstone, as a sleeping child, as Sorrel's kitchen on the mornings when he let her sit silent with tea while the fire that never went out ticked and settled. Her soul was warm. Of course it was. Everything she had ever loved had been warm, and she had spent her whole adult life selling small warmths to strangers—luck-knots, grief-charms, blessings priced to move—and had told herself it was commerce, survival, nothing, when every knot she tied was a letter sent to someone who couldn't answer.

"Please," the face begged again, and now she could see that it had been begging for a long time, that the glass was worn faintly cloudy where its palms had pressed, years and years of pressing. "I don't know who you are. But everyone who comes here wants something, and when they take me out I go somewhere terrible, and I'm so tired. Leave me in the lantern. Please. The lantern is quiet. The lantern is almost like sleep."

"I know who you are," Ulyana whispered. The words came out before she chose them, and they were true, and truth below Greyhollow had a weight to it—she felt the sentence land in her chest like a stone dropped down a shaft. "I know exactly who you are. You're me. You're the part I left behind."

The face stilled. Its eyes—her eyes—searched hers, and Ulyana watched recognition arrive in them slowly, the way dawn arrives in a valley, touching the high places first.

"When I was born," Ulyana said, and the telling steadied her, gave her something to hold besides the horror, "the guild owed the Undercourt. Sorrel's master's master made a bargain to end the Godfall, and the price passed down the ledger until it landed on me. They took—" her throat closed, opened— "they took the part of me that would have known what I was. That's why I hid for twenty-six years. That's why the oathmark felt like a wound instead of a gift. They kept the girl who would have been proud, and left me the girl who was afraid."

"Yes," the soul said. And then, softer, with wonder: "Yes. I remember being proud. I remember being *her*. Before the lantern I remember standing somewhere high and green, and someone holding me up to the sky, and everyone singing. Was that real?"

"It was real. It was our naming day."

"I've been trying to hold onto that," the soul said. "All this time. It goes blurry. The court comes and asks me questions and I try to keep the green place, but it's going blurry, Ulyana. It's going so blurry."

Hearing her own name in her own voice, from her own face, broke something open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close again.

Behind her, the keeper made a small sound—almost a cough, almost a chime. "Touching," it said. "Genuinely. The market appreciates pathos; it moves inventory. But I must remind you both of grammar. Nothing leaves unpaid."

"I'm not leaving without her."

"Then you will need an account settlement, a writ of release, and an authority empowered to sign it. I am merely a keeper." The extra finger tapped the lantern's chain, and the soul flinched back from the glass, and rage rose in Ulyana so fast and so clean that for one vertiginous moment the whole cavern seemed to tilt toward her, every lantern on every shelf leaning like grass in wind. The keeper noticed. The keeper's pleasantness acquired, briefly, a careful quality. "Ah. Yes. That's the other reason the old debts are stored apart. You do carry quite a lot of heat for someone who claims to have no magic."

"She doesn't claim anything," Dagny said, stepping half in front of Ulyana, hand drifting toward the knife she must have known was useless. "She claims passage. She claims her property. Take us to whoever signs the writs, because I have watched this woman talk her way past crown inspectors, smuggler captains, and one extremely unfriendly river godling, and I promise you, keeper, whatever you think you're protecting down here, she's about to make it your problem."

The soul pressed its hands to the glass again. "Don't fight them," it begged. "They'll put you in a lantern too. They'll put you next to me, and we'll watch each other go blurry forever. Ulyana. *Listen to me.* If you can't buy me back—leave. Being owned is survivable. Being owned together is not."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You said that like it was noble." Her own face looked at her with an exhaustion beyond age. "It's just stubborn. I would know. I'm your stubbornness. It's the only part of me they couldn't take out, because it's the part that refused to be taken. Ask me why the lantern hasn't dimmed in twenty-six years. Every light in this cavern fades, Ulyana. Ask me why mine doesn't."

"Why?"

"Because I'm waiting for you," the soul said simply. "I've been waiting for you since the day they hung me up. And if you walk out of here without me, I will wait another twenty-six, and another fifty after that, because that's what I *am*. I'm the part of you that keeps the flame. So stop wasting time arguing with furniture"—a flicker of her old market-stall sharpness crossed the borrowed face, and despite everything Ulyana nearly laughed—"and go find the thing that owns us. Make it an offer it has to hear. You're good at offers. You've been making small ones your whole life."

The keeper cleared its throat. "The regent grants audiences," it said, "to debtors of the old ledger. Rarely. On the mountain's sufferance. If you truly intend to negotiate the Kavanagh account, the way lies deeper. Past the vault door. Past everything."

It extended the too-many-fingered hand toward the darkness at the aisle's end, where the rock opened onto a stair descending farther than any lantern's light could reach—and carved into the stone above the first step, in letters older than Vharrow, older than the Godfall, older than any law that had outlawed magic:

**THE COURT BENEATH RECEIVES THE OUTSTANDING.**

Ulyana looked at her soul one last time. The face in the glass pressed close, and mouthed—no sound now, just the shapes of the words, patient, certain, hers:

*I'll keep the flame.*

She turned toward the stairs. Behind her, the un-dimming light burned steady at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, and ahead the dark went down and down, toward whatever wore a regent's crown beneath the weight of a mountain.

Dagny exhaled. "Right," she said. "After you."
