## 19. The Court Beneath

The regent had watched ten thousand souls come down the mountain road, and she knew before the herald spoke that this one would be different.

She felt it the way she felt everything now—not through her skin, which had long ago stopped belonging entirely to her, but through the deep stone of Greyhollow itself. The Undercourt's halls were grown, not built: ribs of black granite arching over avenues of pale root-flesh, ceilings that breathed in slow centuries. Every footstep on those roads rang through her like a bell through a struck body. Most visitors rang small. Merchants rang like coins. The sold and dreaming souls in their lanterns along the Processional rang like rain on a tin roof—endless, arrhythmic, maddening if she let herself listen.

But the girl walking up from the mines rang like a door being opened.

*Ulyana Kavanagh.* The name arrived in the regent's mind before the herald's runner did, carried up through the roots the way a bruise arrives through flesh. She stood at the window of the audience chamber—a window that showed nothing, that had been cut as a courtesy to surface-born expectations, framing a wall of living rock veined with slow amber light—and she pressed her palm flat against the cold.

She remembered the day that name was written into the ledger. Not read about. Remembered. Her own hand had held the pen. Her own voice had spoken the terms while a midwife's fire guttered and a newborn screamed in the next room, screaming the way children scream when they are born already owed.

One hundred and sixty-two years ago.

The regent closed her eyes and counted, as she always did, the reasons.

* * *

They came for her at the turning of the third hour, when the Undercourt's light ran amber-to-iron and the whole court seemed to hold its breath between beats of its slow heart.

She received them in the Chamber of Terms, because the room mattered. Everything below the mountain mattered—the angle of a chair, the warmth of a lamp, whether water was offered before or after speaking. Bargain-craft was not a matter of clauses. It was a matter of making the other party feel, bone-deep, that they had walked into something older than themselves and would be wise to behave accordingly.

So: the chamber was vast and low, hewn so that even tall men stooped. The lamps burned dragon-amber, borrowed light, the last light of the last fire, and it made every shadow lean toward whoever stood beneath the lamps. The regent sat on a plain stone seat—never a throne; thrones invited defiance—wearing the grey robes of an under-priestess, unadorned, her face a thing that visitors always struggled afterward to describe. Surface folk expected monsters below. She gave them instead a face like a question they'd forgotten asking.

The herald led them in. Behind him: the girl, Ulyana Kavanagh, travel-worn, one sleeve pinned where a wound had been hastily bound, her chin set at exactly the angle Sorrel used to set his own chin when he lied about the guild's accounts. And behind her—

The regent's attention snagged, briefly, painfully, on the second figure. Broad-shouldered, salt-scoured, walking with the loose swagger of someone who had decided fear was a luxury. Dagny Holt. Smuggler. Debtor. A woman whose ledger the regent kept a copy of, as she kept copies of all debts that touched her court.

And a third presence, fainter, arriving late and low along the floor like smoke: a crown hunter's badge, a hunter's trained silence, Wren Calloway keeping to the shadows of the doorway. The regent did not look at her. Looking would tell the girl she'd been seen.

*All three of them,* she thought, and something in her chest that might once have been relief tightened instead. *Sorrel's little congregation, gathered at last.*

"You are Ulyana Kavanagh," the herald said, "last-pledged of the Guild of Small Mercies, daughter of the debt. Kneel, and be heard."

"I'll stand," the girl said.

The regent almost smiled. Of course she would stand. Sorrel had taught her that too—or perhaps no one had, and it was simply what the guild bred into its bloodline: the stubborn refusal to kneel that had gotten four generations of them killed, saved, ruined, redeemed.

"Then stand," said the regent, and spoke for the first time, and heard—as she always heard—her own voice come out wrong. Too level. Too old. A voice like a ledger being opened. "You've come a long way to argue with a debt, child. Sit anyway. Standing arguments tire faster than sitting ones."

Chairs were brought. The girl took hers sideways, knees ready to rise. Good. The smuggler sprawled like a man in a tavern, which was its own kind of armor. And in the doorway, the hunter's breathing stayed even, and even, and even—the breathing of someone memorizing exits.

The regent let the silence do its work. Below the mountain, silence was never empty. It was full of the Processional's ten thousand dreaming lanterns, full of the roots' slow respiration, full of every promise ever made in this room still hanging in the air like dust. Surface folk lasted ninety seconds in that silence before they began filling it.

Ulyana Kavanagh lasted two hundred and forty.

"You know why I'm here," the girl finally said.

"I know why you *think* you're here." The regent folded her hands. "You think you've come to steal back your soul. You've read the ledger—I know you have; Ilse Varn was always going to crack eventually, poor woman, carrying my correspondence in her head for thirty years. You believe there is a lantern with your face in it, somewhere in my market, and that if you take it, the account closes and you walk out of the mountain whole." She tilted her head. "I want you to understand, before we speak of terms, that I do not blame you for believing that. It is what I would have believed, at your age. Before I understood what the ledger actually weighs."

"The ledger weighs five souls," Ulyana said. "Four returned. One outstanding. Mine."

"Five souls," the regent agreed softly. "*Paid.* Do you know what was bought with them?"

"Peace. That's what Sorrel claimed. He died claiming it."

"Sorrel," the regent said, and for a moment—carefully, deliberately, the way a surgeon opens a wound—she allowed herself grief. It crossed her face like weather. "Sorrel Ashgrave, ninth master of the Guild of Small Mercies, who spent forty years keeping a fire he hated and a secret he never forgave me for. Yes. Peace. But you are young enough to think peace is a small word. So let me show you what your guild bought, child. Let me show you what sleeps above your head right now, closer than the skin of the mountain."

She rose. The lamps dimmed without being touched—they always did, when she stood; the fire remembered her—and the amber veins in the walls pulsed once, twice, like a great slow pulse deciding whether to continue.

"When you descended," the regent said, "you passed through the mines. Did you see the dreaming ones? The sold souls in their lanterns, still wearing their old lives like nightclothes?"

"I saw them." The girl's jaw tightened. "They begged."

"They dream," the regent corrected. "It is not the same. A soul in a lantern does not suffer, whatever your smuggler's imagination has told you. It *waits*, the way a seed waits. And the reason it must wait—do you know why? Because a soul is the only vessel that can hold a god-fragment without breaking." She began to walk, slowly, along the line of the wall, and the veins of amber followed her like dogs. "When the gods fell—in truth they did not fall, child, they were *unmade*, unraveled by their own starving faithful during the Godfall—the pieces did not die. Pieces cannot die. They drifted. They sank. And the largest of them sank into this mountain, into the roots, into the dark under the dark, and it began to wake, and to hunger, and to reach."

She stopped before the far wall, where the amber ran thickest.

"There was no spell that could kill it. There was no army. There was only trade. The first masters of your guild—the guild you inherited without knowing it—came down here with a single offer: our souls, freely given, to line its cradle and lull it. Five souls woven together make a net fine enough to hold a sleeping god. Four have been reclaimed over the generations as the net frayed—each return weakening the weave, each return bought at terrible cost. Your Sorrel spent his last strength re-weaving his own thread the week he died. What remains—" she turned, and looked at the girl directly, and let the whole weight of one hundred and sixty-two years sit in her gaze, "—what remains is your soul, and the ember, and the thin gray space between waking and sleep. Cancel the debt. Take your lantern home. And the net tears, and the hunger above us wakes ravenous, and it will not be gentle, and it will not be slow, and Vharrow will be the first city it eats because Vharrow is directly above."

The silence afterward had texture. The smuggler had gone still—truly still, the stillness of a woman recalculating every plan she'd boarded with. In the doorway, the hunter's breathing had changed for the first time: shorter, sharper.

"That's a story," Ulyana said at last, but her voice had lost some of its iron. "A well-made one. You're a court of bargainers. Stories are your stock-in-trade."

"Yes," the regent said simply. "It is a story. It may even be true—that is for you to weigh, and I will not pretend the weighing is easy. But consider what I gain from lying. If you leave without your soul, the debt stands, the net holds, and I remain below forever. If you take it and the world ends, I die in the dark with everyone else. I have no heir, no ambition, no throne worth the name. Child, I have been the regent of this court since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to write. Do you imagine I lie *for pleasure*?"

"Then give me proof."

"The proof walks past you every day in the Processional." The regent gestured, and the amber light rose, and for a moment—only a moment—she opened her senses wide and let the girl feel what the regent felt always: the vast slow weight overhead, coiled in the mountain's roots like a serpent around a heart, dreaming of teeth. Ulyana Kavanagh went white. The smuggler swore under her breath and gripped the arms of her chair. Even the hunter in the doorway swayed.

"You felt it," the regent said quietly, withdrawing. "That is what your prayer-hearth feels like from underneath, every time a candle is lit to a dead god. Every answered prayer on the surface is a finger poking through the net. Your widow's son walking home hollow-eyed—that was not the court's doing. That was the fragment above, tasting the hole your prayers tore, pushing a puppet through it. We are not collecting early, whatever your guild's survivors whisper. We are *holding*. There is a difference, and the difference is measured in cities."

* * *

Afterward, alone, the regent permitted herself the weakness of the window that showed nothing.

She pressed her forehead to the stone and thought about the girl's face when the weight had brushed her mind. Not terror. Recognition. That was the worst part—the part the regent had not anticipated, across all her long calculations. The Kavanagh line ran thick with guild blood, and guild blood knew the taste of a hungry god the way a sailor's blood knew storm. The girl had felt that coiled dark and had not flinched from it so much as *toward* it, the way her ancestors had, the way Sorrel had, the way the regent herself had once, in another life, in another name, standing in a birthing-room holding a pen over a ledger while a newborn screamed her inheritance into the world.

*They will offer something,* the regent thought. *They always do. That is the sickness of the guild—it cannot meet a debt without trying to renegotiate it. Sorrel tried. His mother tried. The girl will try.*

Let her.

The regent had prepared for every offer: service, substitution, sacrifice, surrender. She had drafted counter-clauses in four languages. She had rehearsed refusals until they wore smooth as river stones. What she had not prepared for—what no amount of rehearsal could armor against—was hope.

Because if the girl found a flaw in the terms, a seam in the contract, a way to pay without paying—

Then the regent could stop counting the reasons.

She straightened. Smoothed her grey robes. Let the lamps remember their brightness.

"Send her back in," she told the herald. "Terms of trade."

* * *

The girl came back in with her spine straight and her eyes bright with something dangerous, and the regent thought: *there it is, there's the guild in her, there's the whole mad lineage of mercy about to open its mouth.*

"I won't take the soul and run," Ulyana Kavanagh said. "But I'm not leaving this mountain owned by a ledger either. So let's talk terms—regent to debtor. I have a counteroffer."

And the regent, who had waited one hundred and sixty-two years for someone to say those words, folded her hands and prayed—for the first time in a century, and silently, and to no god at all—that this one would not break her heart.
