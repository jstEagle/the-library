## 20. Counteroffer

They gave Ulyana a night to decide.

The herald had said *night*, though there was no night beneath Greyhollow—only a slow dimming of the phosphor-veins that ran through the cavern walls, a tide of pale green light withdrawing into the stone like breath drawn in and held. The Undercourt kept its own hours. Ulyana lay on a cot in a guest cell carved from the mountain's root, listening to the dark settle around her like silt in still water, and tried to count what she had left to lose.

Her soul, hanging in a lantern somewhere in the vaults below. Her name, which she had spoken aloud at the threshold and felt the mountain take from her mouth like a coin dropped down a well. Wren, who was somewhere above her now with a warrant and a conscience at war. Dagny, arrested or fled or dead. Ilse, who had traded forty years of friendship for whatever the court had given her, and whose face when Ulyana last saw it had been smooth and pleasant and utterly empty of recognition.

*You have until the light comes back,* the herald had said, and smiled with too many teeth arranged too neatly, the way courtiers smiled here, where every expression was a negotiation.

Ulyana did not sleep. She lay with her palm pressed flat against the cold floor and felt the mountain dreaming—the vast, slow attention of it, the sense of being held inside something that remembered the world before names. In Vharrow she had spent her whole life making herself small. A charm-seller in the night market, hood up, sleeve down over the oathmark, one more face in the crowd of the godless. She had thought invisibility was safety. Now she understood it had only been a long apprenticeship for this: learning how to stand before power and not be seen through.

But Sorrel hadn't taught her to hide. That was the thing grief kept returning to, turning over like a river stone. Sorrel had taught her knots and wards and the hundred small mercies of the guild—and then he had died whispering about a debt, leaving her to learn the rest alone, the way every guildmaster before him apparently had. The ledger in her memory: five souls sold, four reclaimed, one outstanding. *Due when the fire gutters out.* Hers. Pledged at her birth by people she had loved, to buy an ending they could not otherwise afford.

She should have hated them. Some part of her did—a hot, bright coal of it that flared whenever she let herself think of Sorrel's hands folding hers closed around a luck-knot while all along his own guild had sold her before she could speak. But another part, quieter and older, remembered the fire that never went out in the guildhall hearth, and the way Sorrel had looked at her across it some nights—not with guilt, exactly. With something closer to *reverence*. As if she were not a debt but a promise.

The light came back.

It seeped up through the floor first, veins kindling vein, until the cell glowed like the inside of a lantern and the door unsealed itself with a sound like a sigh. The herald stood beyond it, robed in gray so fine it seemed woven from fog, holding a lantern of black iron that cast no light at all.

"The Court will hear you," it said.

* * *

The throne room—or whatever the Undercourt called the place where bargains were weighed—was not a room so much as a hollowing. The cavern opened upward into dark Ulyana's eyes refused to measure, and downward likewise, and everywhere the walls were hung with chains that did not hold anything. They simply hung, links thick as her wrist, trailing into shadow, and she understood after a moment that they were not ornaments. They were *terms*. Every chain a contract, every link a clause, forged in metal instead of ink because down here promises had weight and weight needed something to hang from.

The regent sat at the center, upon a chair of fused glass that might once have been sand on some distant shore. Ulyana had seen her before, at the audience three days past—but seeing again did nothing to soften the strangeness. The regent wore the shape of an old woman, tall and spare, with white hair bound in a knot like a closed fist and eyes the color of banked coals. Not burning. Not dead. *Waiting.*

Around the dais stood the Court itself: figures in the half-light that resolved differently each time Ulyana blinked. Once they were robed dignitaries. Once they were standing stones with moss-grown faces. Once, horribly, they were people she almost knew—the set of a jaw like her mother's, the slope of shoulders like Sorrel's—and she wrenched her gaze away and fixed it on the floor, on the seam of her own boots, and breathed until the almost passed.

"Ulyana Kavanagh." The regent's voice filled the hollow without echoing. It simply arrived, complete, as though it had always been in the air and Ulyana had only now tuned herself to hear it. "Or rather—" the coal-bright eyes glittered "—the woman who was called that, before my mountain took the name for safekeeping. You asked for another audience. The Court grants few such. Speak."

Ulyana had rehearsed this in the dark for hours. Rehearsed it the way Sorrel had taught her to tie a ward-knot: not thinking about the whole, but about each crossing, each turn, each place where the pattern either held or failed. She made herself lift her chin. Made her voice come out level.

"I've come to renegotiate."

A sound moved through the Court—not laughter, but the place where laughter would live, if the things wearing those shapes had ever needed such a mechanism. A stirring of robes. A shifting of stone.

"You are in no position to negotiate," the regent said. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it; there was no cruelty in her at all, only the immense patience of someone playing a game whose end had been settled generations ago. "Your soul is ours. Your guild is dust and debt. The fire above gutters, and when it does, the terms come due, and we collect what was promised to us before you drew your first breath. What would you offer us that we do not already hold?"

"Myself," Ulyana said. "Differently."

She stepped forward—one step, onto ground that hummed faintly under her soles, the way a plucked string hums—and forced herself to keep speaking while the Court's attention gathered on her like weather.

"The contract takes my soul. I've read the ledger. I know what collection means—I've seen what came back for the widow's son, and I saw Ash on Bramwen's doorstep, burned from the inside out. You don't want souls the way a thief wants coin. You want *service*. Souls are just the currency because currency is what the old bargain made of us." She swallowed. Her mouth tasted of iron and cave-dust. "So pay me in service instead. Take my labor, my oath, my life's work. Bind me to the Court as its agent—as the guild's last living oath, sworn to *you*—and let the soul go free. Let me carry the ember back up the mountain myself. Isn't that what you wanted all along? Someone willing?"

The word rang strangely in the hollow. *Willing.* Ilse's voice in her memory, steady as a ledger line: *the soul must be returned willingly or the fire dies.* And the reverse of that coin, which Ilse had never quite said aloud: the soul must be *taken* willingly, too, or the whole architecture of the bargain trembled.

The Court went very still.

Even the chains stopped their faint, perpetual swaying. Ulyana stood in the silence feeling like a diver who had gone too deep and run out of air, watching the surface recede. She had gambled everything on one intuition—that a court built on contracts would respect the form of an offer even from a debtor, that pride lived in these ancient things the way hunger lived in the god-fragments sleeping above—and now she waited to learn whether the intuition had been wisdom or just another kind of drowning.

The regent rose.

It took a long time, the way dawn takes a long time—not slowness but ceremony, unfolding. When she stood she was taller than she had any right to be, or perhaps the throne room adjusted itself around her, and the coal-light of her eyes brightened by a degree that Ulyana felt against her skin like warmth off a forge.

"Willing service," the regent said, tasting the words. "Freely offered. Sworn in this hall, under these chains, binding as blood and longer than bone." She descended one glass step. "Do you understand what you propose, little oath? Service to this Court has no stated term. The last who swore it served until the mountain itself forgot her face."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I've met her work. I grew up in it."

Something crossed the regent's face then—fast, quickly buried, but Ulyana caught it, the way she'd learned to catch the flicker in a customer's eye that meant a charm had struck true. Not amusement. Something older and wearier. Recognition, maybe. Or grief.

"You have Sorrel's stubbornness," the regent said quietly, and before Ulyana could seize on that—*Sorrel? You knew him? knew him how?*—the old woman lifted one hand and the chains began to move.

They did not rattle. Chains of contract do not rattle; they *unroll*. Links poured down from the darkness overhead like water finding its level, and the air filled with the smell of hot iron and cold stone and something underneath both, older: the smell of the first promise anyone ever made and regretted. The herald drifted forward bearing a slab of slate, and upon the slate words began to write themselves, dragging into being letter by letter in lines of silver fire, and the regent read them aloud as they formed, because down here a contract unwitnessed was no contract at all.

"*The debtor offers service in place of self. The Court accepts the exchange of essence for oath. The debtor shall serve the Court and its purposes—*"

Her voice continued. Ulyana heard it, heard every word clearly, and yet at the same time something else happened, something she would spend the rest of her life trying to remember precisely: the cavern *breathed*. A single vast inhalation, the phosphor-veins dimming all at once, and in that half-dark the regent's voice slid sideways, split, became two voices speaking in perfect overlap—and the second voice said words that Ulyana's ears received and her mind somehow did not hold, the way water holds a stone's shape only while the stone is in it. A clause, long, winding, spoken in the register of the mountain itself. She strained toward it. She felt it slide through her grasp like a wet rope.

Then the light surged back, and the regent's voice was one voice again, saying only: "*—in perpetuity, or until the fire dies. So sworn, so witnessed, so sealed.*"

The slate blazed white and went blank.

"Well?" The regent regarded her, hands folded, patient as geology. "The Court does not repeat its terms twice. Do you accept?"

And here was the flaw in the foundation, though Ulyana could not see it—could not see anything but the lantern waiting for her in the vault below, and the ember beside it, and the long climb home with the world's last dragon-fire cupped against her heart. Hope is a loud thing. It fills the ear the way a bell fills a tower, and everything quieter than hope goes unheard.

"I accept," Ulyana said.

She spoke the words the herald instructed—her true name, surrendered and returned in the same breath like a coin flipped and caught; her oathmark bared at last, sleeve pushed to the elbow, the old guild sigil flaring violet in the cavern's gloom; her hand extended while a chain-link cooled around her wrist into a bracelet thin as a thread, invisible above, binding below. The mountain drank the vow. She felt it go out of her, the way warmth leaves a hand plunged in snow, and felt something else arrive in its place: weight. Purpose with hooks in it. A belonging so absolute it was indistinguishable from captivity, and she told herself they were the same thing, that this was what Sorrel had carried all those years behind his kind eyes, that oaths were simply love that had learned to bind.

The regent watched her with those banked-coal eyes, and if there was pity in them, Ulyana did not see it, and perhaps that was mercy of a kind.

"It is done," the regent said. "Your soul returns to you. The ember is yours to bear. Go up, little oath, and serve." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "We will call for you when we require."

* * *

They led her down afterward—the herald silent at her shoulder, the new weight at her wrist humming like a struck tuning fork—to the vaults.

The door was older than the city above. Older, Ulyana thought, than most gods. It was made of a wood no tree had grown, banded in iron, and carved across its face in script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts after years of night-market shadows. The herald spoke a syllable that was less a word than a permission, and the door swung inward on air that smelled of cinders and honey and something like rain on hot stone.

Two things rested within, on a shelf of black basalt, side by side.

The lantern first. Black iron, unremarkable, the kind Dagny Holt might hang on a smuggler's skiff. But inside it, where a flame should be, there was *her*—a soft golden shimmer that shifted and stilled and shifted again, and when Ulyana leaned close it shaped itself, briefly, unmistakably, into a face. Her face. Younger than she was now, unlined, unafraid, and it looked back at her through the iron lattice with an expression she recognized from mirrors on bad nights: the look of someone waiting to be left alone, and knowing she wouldn't be.

"Hello," Ulyana whispered.

The shimmer pressed itself against the glass, the way a cat presses against a window, wanting out or wanting in, impossible to say. Warmth bloomed through the iron into her palm. Her own warmth. Her own laugh, maybe, locked in there; her own grief for Sorrel, her love of rain, the taste of plum wine at midwinter—all of it, everything the ledger had counted as collateral, breathing behind glass.

Beside it, the ember.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was her first thought, absurd and profane: *all this, for something I could hold in one hand.* It sat in a dish of fused glass like a coal that had decided never to die, and its color was not red or orange but the color of the first morning after a long illness—gold shot through with living rose—and its heat reached into her chest and found the hollow where her fear lived and lay down in it like a dog coming home. Dragon-fire. Last of its kind. The ember that starved the gods and saved the city, bought with five souls and kept alive by four centuries of quiet sacrifice, and it pulsed gently, steadily, in rhythm—she realized with a lurch—with her own heartbeat.

As if it had known her all along. As if it had been waiting.

"Take it," the herald said. "Both are yours to carry. The Court honors its contracts."

Ulyana lifted the lantern. It weighed nothing, or everything; her arms could not agree. Then, with a reverence she usually saved for the dead, she lifted the ember, and the vault's darkness leaned back from it, and for one suspended moment she stood between the two halves of herself—the self that was taken and the self that remained—and felt them strain toward each other through the air like magnets denied.

*One vessel,* said a thought that was not hers, or was hers, or was the mountain's. *One.*

She did not hear it. Hope had filled the tower; the bell was ringing; she was already turning toward the door, already climbing, already ascending through the mines where sold souls dreamed in the walls—already free, she believed, already redeemed, carrying her ransom home in a lantern of black iron while the clause she had not heard curled patiently around her future like a root around a coffin, waiting for the season it was written for.

Behind her, far below, in the hall of chains, the regent sat back upon her glass throne and watched the light of the ember dwindle up the shafts, and said, to no one, in the oldest language there is:

"So it begins again."

Above Greyhollow, the first starving god stirred in its sleep—and opened its mouth.
