## 21. What Wren Found Below

Wren had been a hunter for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned one rule above all others: the warrant tells you where to look, but never what you'll find.

She stood at the mouth of the old Vetch mine at Greyhollow's edge with Ulyana Kavanagh's file in one hand and a lamp in the other, and she understood that she was about to break every rule she'd ever kept.

The order from Vharrow had been explicit. *Secure the perimeter. Await reinforcement. Do not descend.* Her superiors believed the answered prayers were a cult phenomenon — desperate people, cold shrines warming, some hedge-witch stirring up the old superstitions below the mountain. They wanted names, confessions, a tidy trial. They did not want whatever lived beneath Greyhollow, because the crown's official histories said nothing lived there. The Undercourt was a story mothers used to keep children from the mines. The Godfall had ended everything. The gods were dead. Magic was dead. The law said so.

But Wren had interrogated Dagny Holt in a cell that smelled of tar and tallow, and the smuggler had looked at her with eyes like banked coals and said, *You don't even know what your own warrant is for, do you?* And then she'd burned her luck — Wren had watched it happen, watched the small bright aura of a charmed life gutter out around the woman like a candle pinched between fingers — rather than say one word more. People didn't burn their luck over cults. People didn't bleed their futures away to protect a rumor.

And Ulyana. Ulyana, who had been a skinny, laughing girl who once taught Wren to whistle through a blade of grass behind the tannery row; who had grown into a woman selling luck-knots to widows because it was the only crime small enough to survive. Wren had read her file until the words blurred. *Charm-seller. Unregistered practitioner. Nonviolent. Flight risk.* It read like a description of a stray cat, not a fugitive.

*Do not descend.*

Wren looked at the mine mouth. Cold air breathed out of it, slow and regular, like something sleeping with its back against the door. The timber frame was black with age and carved — she crouched, held her lamp close — with markings she recognized from her training manuals only as *pre-Fall guild signs*, the kind the Inquisition had spent two centuries chiseling off every lintel in Vharrow. Here they remained, deep enough that no inspector's chisel had ever bothered. A knotwork circle. A hand open, palm up.

*The guild exists to give magic away.* She didn't know where the phrase came from. It arrived in her mind whole, like a coin dropped down a well, and she stood very still while the ripples spread. She had never heard those words. She was almost certain she had never heard those words.

"Damn you, Ulyana," she said softly, to no one, to the dark, to the girl with the grass-whistle. "Damn you for making me curious."

She went down.

* * *

The mine swallowed sound first. Three hundred paces in, Wren noticed her own footsteps had gone strange — not muffled exactly, but *deferred*, each boot-fall arriving a half-beat late, as though the mountain were considering whether to permit the noise before allowing it. Her lamp behaved worse. The flame leaned constantly toward the depths, not flickering but straining, a dog on a leash.

She passed the first of the dreaming ones an hour later.

She nearly walked into him. He sat against the tunnel wall in the posture of a man resting between shifts, pick across his knees, helmet still strapped, and if not for the dust lying undisturbed on his shoulders she would have taken him for living. His chest rose and fell. His eyes were closed. And from him came a light — faint, pearl-colored, rising off his skin the way heat rises off summer stone.

Wren knew dead men. Eleven years of hunting had made her fluent in death's many dialects: the stiff, the rotting, the burned, the drowned. This man was none of them. He was neither alive nor dead. He was *elsewhere*, and his body had been left here the way travelers leave coats in an inn they mean to return to.

His lips were moving.

She knelt, hating herself, and leaned close. He was counting. "...forty-one, forty-two... Elsie, I'll be home by forty-six, I told her forty-six..." A miner. Dead — or sold — generations ago, still walking home in his sleep, still telling his wife when he'd arrive.

Wren sat back on her heels and found that her hands were shaking. Not from cold. From the shape of the thing assembling itself in her understanding. The crown taught that magic died at the Godfall, that the gods starved or fled or never were, that the world since had been clean of wonder and needed only to stay clean. But this. This was not absence. This was *storage*. These tunnels were full — she felt it now, a pressure against her skin like the moment before lightning — full of people who had been put somewhere, carefully, the way grain is put in a silo.

Someone had been paid. Someone had been paid in souls, and the crown had built its law on top of the transaction and called the silence peace.

She walked on. There was nothing else to do. Behind her, faintly, the miner counted on toward home.

* * *

The second gallery opened without warning, the narrow shaft spitting her out into a vaulted dark so large her lamp gave up before it reached the far wall. Wren stopped at the lip of a stair cut in a spiral into the living rock, and below her —

Below her was a market.

She had expected horror. She wantonly admits, in the privacy of her own skull where no confession could ever be extracted, that she had armed herself with the expectation: gibs and chains and screaming, the Inquisition's woodcut vision of hell. What she saw instead was worse, because it was *beautiful*, and beauty is the only thing that has ever truly broken her.

Lanterns. Thousands of them. They hung in tiers down the cavern walls and floated in slow constellations overhead, and each one glowed with that same pearl light she'd seen rising from the miner, and each one held a face.

Not etched faces. Not shadows cast by flame. Faces *in* the light, moving, breathing, speaking to themselves or to no one — a young woman braiding hair that wasn't there, an old man mouthing a blessing over a table set for guests long gone, a child chasing something luminous just beyond the glass. Every lantern was a soul, kept. Displayed. *Shopped for*. Broad avenues wound between stalls where figures moved — tall, wrong-jointed, courteous in the way of things that have practiced courtesy for a thousand years — and the figures examined the lanterns, held them up to a lightless light, turned them, weighed them.

Browsing. They were browsing.

Wren pressed herself into the shadow of the stairwell, and her training — eleven years of it, the crown's finest — dissolved like sugar in rain. Because she knew what she was looking at. She had spent her career confiscating charms, burning grimoires, dragging weeping hedge-witches before tribunals, and she had believed — she had needed to believe — that it was all protection, that magic was a knife and the law a scabbard, that somewhere above her the city slept safe because she did the ugly necessary work.

There was no safety here. There was only inventory. And the inventory was *people*.

A herald-thing drifted past her hiding place close enough that she could smell it — cold stone and beeswax, incense and grave-dust, the smell of every funeral she'd ever attended folded into one — carrying a lantern delicately in claws too fine for their size. Inside the lantern, a soul pressed its palms flat against the glass from within, and its mouth formed a word, over and over. Wren read the shape of it. Anyone would have.

*Mama.*

Her faith did not shatter dramatically. That was the terrible part, the part she would carry afterward like a stone in her boot. It simply went out, quietly, the way the miner's life had gone out, and what remained was colder and clearer than anything the Inquisition had ever given her. The Godfall. The law. The purges. Two hundred years of ash, and underneath it all a ledger, a debt, a *transaction* — and the crown either knew and profited, or didn't know and had built its righteousness on a lie sold to it wholesale. Either way, every warrant she'd ever served had been a receipt.

She thought of Dagny Holt burning her luck in a cell rather than speak. She had thought it fanaticism. Now she understood it was arithmetic. Some knowledge costs more than a future.

She thought of Ulyana, and the thought arrived with a new and dreadful weight: *her file says nonviolent, flight risk — but why would the court beneath the mountain care about a charm-seller? Unless she isn't a charm-seller. Unless she's merchandise.*

Wren gripped her lamp and made the decision the way she did everything: quickly, and then forever.

She went down the stairs.

* * *

The market's edge had rules; she felt them like a change in water temperature. Where the stalls ended, a causeway began, running along an underground river whose surface reflected no light at all — a river of pure dark, and things moved in it occasionally, vast and patient, the way whales move under ships. Along the causeway, alcoves had been cut, and in the alcoves more lanterns hung, older ones, their lights dimmed to embers. Retired stock. Forgotten stock.

In the last alcove before the dark took over entirely, one lantern burned brighter than the rest, and Wren stopped, because the face inside it was one she knew.

Not Ulyana's — she checked twice, heart slamming, and it was not, and she couldn't decide whether the relief or the disappointment frightened her more. It was a woman perhaps sixty, sharp-boned, with ink-stained fingers and the particular stubborn set of the jaw that Wren had last seen bent over tribunal paperwork in Vharrow. Archivist Ilse Brandt. Missing thirty years. Declared dead. Her name was in three of Wren's own case files as a cautionary tale about mages who fled too deep.

Ilse Brandt's soul looked up through the glass, and for one vertiginous moment Wren was sure the woman saw her. The soul's mouth moved. No sound crossed the lantern, but the shapes were deliberate, patient, repeated until Wren understood:

*Count the lanterns. Count them and go back and tell them. Tell them how many.*

Then the light in the lantern turned away, and it was only a light again, and Wren stood in the dark of the world's basement holding a lamp that suddenly seemed like a child's toy.

She counted. It was what she was good at — evidence, enumeration, the dull holy work of making lists that could not be argued with. She counted four hundred and six lanterns in the outer galleries alone before the numbers stopped meaning anything and became only a weight. Four hundred and six souls in the rooms she could see. The crown's records listed, in two centuries of Godfall-era tribunals, fewer than ninety confirmed deaths by magical sentence.

The arithmetic did not balance. It had never balanced. The missing had been coming down here — or being brought — since long before Wren's grandmother was born, and the law she'd sworn her life to had been standing guard over the door.

Wren sank down against the alcove wall, out of sight of the avenue, and let herself shake. It lasted less than a minute; she rationed it. Then she took out her field journal, and by the covered light of her lamp she wrote, in the tight cipher her instructors had called the best they'd seen in a decade: date, depth, count, descriptions, the miner, the heralds, Ilse Brandt. Evidence. If she died here, the journal might not. If she lived, the journal was a weapon the Inquisition had no defense against, because it was made of the only thing they feared: the truth, written plainly, by one of their own.

She was closing the clasp when she heard it.

Voices, far up the avenue — the courteous wrong-jointed kind, and beneath them, threaded thin through the cavern's acoustics like wire through wax: a human voice arguing, tired and furious and utterly unbowed, and Wren knew that voice the way you know a scar. She had heard it last across a night-market stall, saying *you always did follow the letter of things, Wren. I hope it keeps you warm.*

Ulyana. Alive. Deeper in. Walking willingly toward whatever audience waited at the bottom of this place.

Every rule Wren had ever kept said: retreat, report, return with a company and a writ. Every one of those rules had just died in the dark behind her, four hundred and six lanterns deep.

Wren Calloway snuffed her lamp, shouldered her pack, and followed her oldest friend down into the dark — not as a hunter anymore.

She no longer knew what she was. But she knew which direction home lay, and for the first time in eleven years, it was down.
