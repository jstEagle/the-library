## 22. Reunion in the Dark

*Wren*

The dark below Greyhollow was not like other darkness. Wren had known the black of sealed cellars and the black of moonless forests, and both of those were simply absences—light withheld. This was a presence. It pressed against her eyes like wool, breathed against her skin, and when she lifted her lantern it did not scatter so much as negotiate, holding a small sphere of gold around her while the rest of the tunnel watched from beyond its edge.

She had been walking for hours since the mine shaft collapsed behind her. That had been deliberate—she'd wedged her hunter's pry-bar into the support timber and leaned her weight against it until the wood groaned and gave—but she hadn't expected the fall to be quite so thorough. The way back was gone. Whatever she found down here, she would have to find a different way out, or find nothing at all.

Her superiors would call this desertion. Worse: contamination. A crown hunter who went into the Undercourt came back either dead or compromised; that was doctrine, written in the margins of three centuries of reports. Wren had signed off on enough mage executions to know what happened to hunters who started sympathizing.

And yet here she was, descending with a stolen warrant in her coat pocket and Dagny Holt's name sitting on her conscience like a stone swallowed whole.

The lantern guttered. She stopped, cupped her hand around the glass, and felt it—a warmth in the stone underfoot that had nothing to do with geology. The tunnels down here remembered people. She could feel them faintly through her boot soles, old dreams seeping up out of the rock, and every few hundred paces she passed them: figures standing or sitting in niches, translucent as breath on glass, their faces slack with something between sleep and contentment. Sold souls. Ilse's archives had described them, but archives didn't prepare you for the way one of them turned its head as you passed, tracking you without waking, wearing a face that someone above still mourned.

Wren kept her eyes forward and her hand on her knife, though she knew neither would help her if the dark decided to take an interest.

It was the singing that told her she wasn't alone anymore. Thin, tuneless, half-swallowed by the tunnels—the kind of sound a person made when they thought no one could hear and had stopped caring whether they were right. She knew the voice before she knew the song. She'd heard Ulyana Kavanagh hum that same broken little market-lullaby over a boiling kettle twenty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and burnt sugar, before the Godfall's laws had grown teeth and friendship had grown dangerous.

Wren rounded a bend and saw the light first: a charm-glow, soft and greenish, hovering at shoulder height. Then the woman beneath it, small and sharp-faced, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps. She stood with her back to the tunnel wall, one hand pressed flat against the stone as though listening to it, and she looked exhausted in the particular way of someone who had been frightened for days and had learned to carry the fear like a second spine.

Ulyana turned. Her eyes found the lantern first, then the hunter's coat, then Wren's face—and Wren watched recognition move through her like weather, watchfulness giving way to disbelief giving way to something older and more dangerous than either.

"Wren." Her voice cracked on the name. "You're supposed to be in Vharrow."

"You're supposed to be dead," Wren said. "I filed the paperwork myself. Well. I'm about to."

For one heartbeat neither of them moved. Then Ulyana laughed—it came out of her sideways, startled, half sob—and crossed the distance between them, and Wren let herself be held, arms wrapped around a friend she'd spent six years pretending she'd stop looking for in every crowd. Ulyana smelled of charcoal and road dust and the cheap rosemary oil she'd used since they were girls. Some knot behind Wren's sternum, wound tight since the day she'd taken the hunter's oath, loosened by a single grudging degree.

"You're crying," Ulyana said into her shoulder.

"Hunters don't cry. We leak. It's in the regulations."

They pulled apart. Ulyana wiped her own eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and Wren saw the moment the wariness reassembled itself behind her face—not gone, never gone between them, but gentler now, a fence rather than a wall.

"How did you get past the collapse?" Ulyana asked.

"I made the collapse."

"Of course you did." Ulyana studied her, and Wren watched her arrive at the obvious conclusion, watched it land. "Dagny's not with you."

* * *

*Ulyana*

The tunnel seemed to lean in around the answer before Wren gave it.

"She's alive," Wren said quickly. Too quickly. "There was a raid on Greyhollow two nights ago. My unit. I tried to warn—" She stopped. Started again, slower, the way she did when the truth needed to be handled carefully, like a blade by the flat. "I couldn't warn her. I was being watched. They took her at the tollhouse. She gave them nothing, Ulyana. Not your name, not your direction, not the route. She sat in an interrogation cell for nine hours and lied to a Truth-reader until the reader bled from the nose and quit."

"And then?"

"They're holding her in the garrison at Greyhollow. Pending transfer to Vharrow." Wren's jaw tightened. "Pending my report, actually. Which is why I'm here instead of there. I wrote two versions. One says she resisted and escaped. One says she's a cooperative prisoner who should be treated gently because she may lead us to you."

"Which one did you send?"

"I sent the escape," Wren said. "And then I came down here to find you, which means I've burned the gentle version, which means when they realize Holt hasn't fled anywhere, they'll go back to her with questions I can't un-ask." She exhaled. "I bought her time. Days, maybe. I didn't buy her freedom. I need you to understand that. I want you to know exactly what I've done and haven't done, because I'm not going to stand in front of you and let you think I'm a hero. I arrested your friend, Ulyana. I put the irons on her myself, because if I hadn't, Rennick would have, and Rennick breaks things."

The words settled over Ulyana like ash. She had imagined this reunion a hundred ways in the dark stretches of the descent—in the warm ones, Wren arrived with an apology already made and forgiveness came easy as breathing; in the cold ones, Wren arrived with drawn steel. She had not imagined arriving here: the truth laid open, ugly and honest, offered without varnish.

Dagny, in irons. Dagny, silent for nine hours. Dagny, whose luck-charm Ulyana still carried in her pocket, paid out like a coin tossed into deep water, buying passage and loyalty both.

"Why are you telling me this?" Ulyana asked quietly.

"Because you deserve to be angry at the true version of me, not the flattering one." Wren met her eyes. In the charm-light her face was stripped of everything official—all the crown's polish, all the hunter's armor worn like a second skin. What was left underneath was just a girl from Tallow Street who had once split a stolen pear three ways and sworn, laughing, that she'd never take sides against Ulyana Kavanagh as long as she lived. "And because I need you to decide, right now, whether you can work beside me. Because I can't do what comes next alone, and neither can you."

"What comes next?"

"The vault." Wren pulled a folded paper from her coat—the warrant, Ulyana realized, the actual warrant with the Undercourt's sigil pressed in black wax, stolen from her own superiors' strongbox. "Your archivist friend traded for a key. I read the intercepts before I burned them. The court keeps the dragon-fire and your soul in the same place, Ulyana. Together. They've always meant them as one payment. And the regent down there—" her voice dropped, and the tunnel seemed to drop with it, "—the regent has been hastening the fire's death. Every answered prayer on the surface, every starving god stirring—that's the fire guttering. If we don't reach the vault soon, there won't be a choice left to make."

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. Six years of silence. Two arrests she knew of, probably more she didn't. A lifetime of friendship buried under law and fear—and now dug up again, dirt still on it, standing in front of her in a tunnel full of dreaming ghosts.

"You could still walk away," Ulyana said. "Go back up. File a clean report. Say you never found me."

"I could." Wren almost smiled. "I've been telling myself that for six years. It doesn't work. Trust me—I've done the research."

* * *

*Wren*

They walked. The tunnel sloped downward in long, patient spirals, and the sold souls grew denser as they descended, drifting in the walls like fish under ice. Wren found she couldn't look at them directly for more than a few seconds; something about their contentment was worse than agony would have been. These were people who had been purchased, and somewhere along the way they had decided to enjoy the arrangement.

"Do you feel that?" Ulyana asked suddenly.

Wren did. A pull, low in the chest, gentle and enormous, like the tide deciding to take an interest in a grain of sand. The deeper they went, the stronger it grew, and threaded through it was heat—not the dry heat of the lanterns but a living warmth, ancient and slow-breathing.

"The ember," Ulyana said. "We're close enough to feel it."

They came out of the spiral into a gallery where the ceiling opened upward into darkness and the floor was veined with lines of dim red light, like the cooling blood of some vast buried animal. And there—sitting cross-legged in the center of the gallery, exactly where the veins converged—was a figure Wren recognized from a hundred wanted circulars, aged and softened: Ilse Varn, guild archivist, traitor, nun.

She looked up at them with an expression of perfect, courteous blankness.

"Hello," Ilse said pleasantly. "I'm sorry—you'll have to tell me your names. I appear to have traded mine away."

* * *

*Ulyana*

The word hit Ulyana like a blow to the ribs. *Traded.* She had known—Ilse had told her, or the shape of it, before going down alone into the court's halls—but knowing and seeing were different countries, and Ilse sat in front of her now as a stranger wearing a beloved face, hands folded, patient as a stone saint.

"Ilse. It's me. Ulyana."

Something flickered across the old woman's features—there and gone, a fish under ice, like the souls in the walls. "That's a lovely name," Ilse said. "Did I give it to you? You seem like someone I would have named."

Wren's hand had gone to her knife. Ulyana shook her head, minutely, and stepped forward, and knelt in the red-veined dust so that she and Ilse were eye to eye.

"You kept the guild's ledgers for forty years," Ulyana said softly. "You hid as a sister of the Quiet Order for thirty-one of them. You make terrible nettle tea and lie about how much honey goes in it. And you went down into that court and gave up everything you remembered about us so that I would have a key." She reached out and took Ilse's folded hands. They were cold. "I'm not asking you to remember me. I'm asking you to come with me anyway."

Ilse regarded her for a long, unreadable moment. Then, slowly, she unfolded herself and rose, joints cracking like green wood.

"The key is in my habit's lining," she said. "Whoever I was, she planned well. I suppose we should honor that." She paused, head tilting toward the tunnel ahead, where the pull of the ember beat against them all like a second heart. "There's a door at the bottom of these mines that only opens for someone the debt recognizes. I don't know what that means. But I think it means you."

Behind them, far up the spiral, a sound drifted down through the dark: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, thin and carrying, the flat official voice of a man reading names off a list—Rennick's voice, Wren's face going white as she heard it.

"He brought a full squad down," Wren whispered. "He found my report trail. Ulyana—he's not coming to arrest anyone."

They ran.

* * *

*Wren*

They ran through galleries of sleeping souls, past shrines carved into living rock, down stairs that had not been stairs for miners in four hundred years. The ember-pull grew until it was less a feeling than a direction, hauling them forward like a hook set gently in the breastbone. Ilse ran better than a woman her age should have, steady and tireless, humming something that might have been a hymn or might have been a ledger recited aloud.

And behind them, gaining, the boots.

At the last turning, where the corridor narrowed to a cleft in the rock, Wren stopped and turned around. Ulyana grabbed her sleeve.

"No."

"Somebody holds that gap," Wren said. She drew her knife, and then, after a moment, drew also the thin wand of hunter's iron from her belt—the tool of her trade, the instrument of every execution she'd ever carried out. It hummed in her grip, eager. She had spent her whole career pointing it at people like Ulyana. Now she braced it against the stone and pointed it up the tunnel at her own comrades, and found that her hand did not shake at all. That was the part that frightened her most. "Go. Both of you. Find the door."

"Sixty seconds," Ulyana said fiercely, gripping her wrist. "Then you run after us. Swear it."

"Sworn." Wren allowed herself one grin, sharp and bright in the ember-light, the grin of a girl splitting stolen pears. "Now go, before I start feeling sentimental. Hunters don't cry. We leak."

Ulyana went, pulling Ilse with her into the cleft, and their footsteps faded into the deep places of the world. Wren turned to face the tunnel, alone, the red veins pulsing under her feet like the heartbeat of something enormous beginning to wake.

The boots grew louder.

And from the darkness ahead of them, out of the walls themselves, the sold souls began—one by one, in the hundreds, in the thousands—to open their eyes.
