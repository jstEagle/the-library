## 24. Ilse's Price

The road back from the Undercourt was longer than the road down.

Ilse knew this in the way she knew the weight of her own hands—without knowing why she knew it. The tunnels beneath Greyhollow did not run straight; they coiled like the gut of some buried animal, and the herald who had led her to the vault door had walked them without hesitation, his lantern swinging a light that cast no shadows. She had followed. She had signed. And now she climbed alone through the dark with a key that hummed against her breastbone and a hole where sixty years of her life had been.

She stopped often. That was strange too. At the third turning, where a vein of pale crystal ran through the rock like frozen lightning, she found herself standing still for a long time, her hand pressed flat against the stone, weeping without any grief she could name. The tears came and went like weather. She let them. There was nothing in her head to argue with them about.

*You will not remember what you trade,* the herald had said, and his voice had been kind, which was the cruelest part. Kindness from the Undercourt was a knife presented handle-first. *The guild gave you to us in pieces over decades, archivist. We are only returning the shape of the bargain.*

*Take it all,* she had answered. *The archives. The years. Her name out of my mouth if you have to carve it.*

And they had. Oh, they had.

Ilse Voss stood in the dark with wet cheeks and dry eyes and tried to remember what she had bought. She held up the thought like a merchant holding a lamp to a coin, looking for the stamp, the proof. *A key.* Yes. A key to a fire-vault, deep under Greyhollow, where something burned that the world above needed kept burning. Someone needed her to open a door. Someone—

Her hand went to her collar, where a small cloth-wrapped bundle hung on a cord. Inside it, wrapped in three layers against damp and dark, lay the key: a bar of black iron longer than her forearm, worked along its length with script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. It was warm. Not metal-warm. *Alive*-warm, the way a sleeping bird is warm, and when she pressed her palm to it she felt—not a voice, nothing so clean as a voice—a pressure, a leaning, like a dog pressing its skull into your leg because it knows you before you know yourself.

"All right," she said aloud, to the key, to the tunnel, to whoever she had been. "All right. I'm going."

Her voice sounded old in the stone. She supposed she was old. Her hands were an old woman's hands, knuckled and spotted, though they moved with a sureness that surprised her—fingers that found purchase on rock without being told, feet that chose the stable stones out of habit so deep it survived even this. Whatever else they had taken, they had left her the walking. They had left her the *going*.

They had not left her the reason.

* * *

Above ground, the world was ending politely.

That was how Ilse thought of it, later—the only way she could think of it, having no history to measure it against. The sky over the mountains was the wrong color, a bruised violet that belonged to no hour she recognized, and the wind carried a smell she had no word for until she made one herself: *hunger*. Old hunger, vast and patient, like a cellar full of starving things finally allowed to sniff the air.

Greyhollow crouched at the mountain's foot, a town built of mine-spoil and stubbornness, its houses leaning together like conspirators. Smoke rose from chimneys. Lamplight showed in windows. People moved in the streets—but wrongly, all of them, drifting rather than walking, their faces turned up toward the bruised sky with expressions of such naked longing that Ilse's skin crawled. A woman stood in a doorway kneading bread and staring upward, dough forgotten between her flour-white fingers. Two children sat on a step, perfectly silent, watching a cloud that was shaped like nothing at all.

"Excuse me," Ilse said to the woman with the bread. "Is there—an inn? I've come a long way."

The woman looked at her. Looked *through* her, almost, then back. "You're not listening yet," the woman said. Her voice was gentle and terrible. "It's better if you listen. It stops hurting."

"I haven't heard anything."

"You will." The woman returned to her bread, kneading, gazing up. "Everyone does, now. It's starting to call us home."

Ilse walked on. Behind her she heard the woman begin, very softly, to hum—not a song she recognized, but a tune that seemed to recognize *her*, that plucked at threads inside her chest she hadn't known were there. She quickened her pace. The key warmed against her sternum as if in answer, or warning, or both.

An inn stood near the town's heart, its sign a painted pickaxe gone gray with weather. Inside, the common room was full and quiet in the way a room is quiet when everyone in it is listening to something else. Ilse counted nine patrons, four of them motionless with their heads tilted like birds. A fifth—a broad man with smuggler's hands and a fresh scar across his brow—was arguing with the innkeep about rooms, loudly, gratefully, as if loudness were a rope he was hauling himself up hand over hand.

"—don't care if it's the broom cupboard, Holt's expecting me back by tomorrow's dark and I'll sleep standing up before I wait—"

"Dagny Holt," said a voice from the corner table, "can go hang till morning, Bren, sit *down*—"

Ilse knew the name. That was the first thing that had come back to her whole since the vault door closed behind her: *Dagny Holt*, arriving in her mind with a weight of association she couldn't unpack. Smuggler. Debt. Something owed, someone saved, a ledger somewhere with her own handwriting in it—no. No, that wasn't hers. That was—

She gripped the edge of a table until the wood bit her palms.

"You all right, mother?" The loud man—Bren—was looking at her with concern. "You've gone white as the ceiling."

"I'm looking for someone," Ilse said, and heard herself say the next words as if reading them off a page she'd hidden from herself. "A young woman. Dark hair. She carries charms. Luck-knots, ward-strings. She may be traveling under—" Under what? The blank yawned. She reached across it and found, astonishingly, a single word, worn smooth as a riverstone from handling: "*Kavanagh.*"

The room changed.

It was subtle—a settling, a stillness, the particular silence of people deciding whether you are dangerous—but Ilse had spent a lifetime, apparently, reading rooms, and she read this one easily. The innkeep's rag stopped moving. The man called Bren glanced toward a stairwell. And at the corner table, a lean figure in a crown-gray coat set down a cup with great care, as if it might otherwise become evidence.

"That name's worth trouble here," the coat-figure said. A woman. Younger than her voice suggested. Sharp-faced, watchful, with the restless hands of someone accustomed to warrants. "Who's asking?"

"No one," Ilse said honestly. "I don't know who's asking. I need to find her, and I don't know why, and that is the entire truth of me."

Something flickered across the hunter's face—recognition, perhaps, of a fellow creature hollowed out by duty—and then suspicion again, deeper. "Wren Calloway," the woman said, rising. "Crown service. You'll forgive me if 'no one' gets searched."

"Ilse," said Ilse, offering the name like a beggar offering an empty bowl. "Just Ilse. Apparently."

*Apparently.* Even as she said it, the word rang false and true at once. She had a scholar's cadence. She had ink stains in the creases of her right hand, decades deep, that no amount of scrubbing would lift. She had been someone who lived among books and records and careful truths, and someone had taken that away and left her the posture of it, the ghost of it, like a house stripped to its beams.

Wren Calloway circled her slowly. "Where did you come down from?" she asked. "The mountain road's closed. Has been for two days. Nothing comes down from the high passes except—" She paused. Her eyes went to Ilse's collar, to the cord showing there. "What have you got around your neck?"

Ilse stepped back. It was pure reflex, older than memory, and Wren read it instantly.

"Hold still."

"I'd rather not."

"Hold *still*—" Wren's hand shot out; Ilse twisted; and then two things happened at once, so fast that afterward Ilse could never say which came first. Wren's fingers closed on the cord—and the key flared.

Not brightly. That was the horror of it. It didn't blaze; it *darkened*, drinking the lamplight of the room into itself until every candle flame bent toward Ilse's chest like grass in wind, and in that borrowed darkness everyone in the common room saw, briefly, the shape of what slept in the black iron: a coil, a wing, a slow red pulse like a heart deciding whether to beat.

Wren snatched her hand back as if burned. The light snapped back. Candles guttered upright. And in the ringing silence, the humming woman outside began to laugh—a low, delighted, dreadful sound, like a child finding a door left open.

"It woke," the woman sang through the window glass. "It woke, it woke, it's waking *up*—"

"Out," said the innkeep hoarsely. "Both of you. Out of my house. Now."

* * *

They ended up in the smithy's ruined shed at the edge of town, because Wren insisted on questioning her somewhere private and Ilse insisted on existing somewhere at all, and the shed was the only compromise the night offered. Rain had begun—real rain, mercifully ordinary rain—and drummed on the slumped roof while Wren lit a stub of candle and studied her prisoner with a hunter's patience.

"The key doesn't leave your neck," Wren said. "I've decided that. If it drinks light when I touch it, I don't want to think about what happens if it drinks *me*. So you'll tell me instead. Where did you get it?"

"I don't remember."

"Try harder."

"There is no harder." Ilse heard her own voice crack with frustration, and underneath the frustration, something worse: grief with no object. "There is a hole in me, Crownswoman, running from roughly my twentieth year to yesterday. I know I can read six languages because my mouth knows them. I know I was a scholar because my hands are stained like a scribe's. I know I traded *something*—the most valuable something I owned—for that iron, and the something was everything I was. Do you understand? I am asking you questions about my own life because the answers were the *price*."

Wren's jaw worked. "Traded it to whom?"

"Who do you think?" Ilse touched the bundle at her throat, gently now, the way one touches a sleeping animal that has already bitten you once. "The court beneath the mountain. The Undercourt. I walked down their stairs and sat at their table and they fed me wine that tasted like remembering, and I signed, and I walked out lighter than I've ever been and I don't know if I mean that as a blessing or a wound."

"What did you buy with it?"

"A key." Ilse unwound the first layer of cloth. "To a vault. Where a fire is kept. The last of—" The word rose in her unbidden, huge and ancient: "*dragon-fire.*" Saying it hurt, the way a missing tooth hurts, the tongue returning again and again to the absence. "Someone has to carry it out of there. Before the ones above wake all the way. Before—" She gestured vaguely at the sky, the humming, the woman kneading bread with her face turned up like a flower after a sun that wanted to eat her. "Before *this* finishes happening."

"And the person who sent you. The one whose name you're carrying around like contraband." Wren leaned forward. Candlelight moved in her eyes. "This Kavanagh. What is she to you?"

And there it was. The question Ilse had been circling since the tunnels, prodding the blank space like a tongue probing a wound, and the blank space answered the only way it could: not with facts, but with *weight*. Somewhere in the stripped rooms of her, a chair remained. Not remembered—*felt*. A shape of obligation and affection so dense that it survived the demolition around it, the way a hearthstone survives a house fire.

"I don't know," Ilse said, and her voice broke clean in half. "That's the truth. I don't know who she is. I know—" She pressed her fist against her mouth, steadied. "I know I would burn down the world for her. I know I already paid everything I had for her sake, and I know I'd do it again, and I don't know *why*, and that is the loneliest thing that has ever happened to me, and I once—" She faltered. "I was about to say *I once lost a library*. I don't remember losing a library. But I was about to mourn it just now, out of pure habit."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The rain filled the pause.

"My orders," she said at last, "are to arrest anyone carrying Undercourt iron and hold them for the magistrates in Vharrow." She rubbed her face. "My orders also used to make sense to me. That was this morning." She stood, doused the candle, and relit it, as if the gesture could restart the day. "There's a girl in the taproom upstairs pretending very hard to be a smuggler's cousin. Dark hair. Sells luck-knots. Been asking after a nun who was supposed to meet her on the mountain road." Wren looked at Ilse steadily. "Were you a nun?"

Ilse laughed—a startled, cracked sound. "I don't know. Possibly. It sounds like excellent cover for an archivist."

"It's what she called you." Wren pulled open the shed door. Rain and violet light poured in. "Sister. She's been calling you Sister Ilse for two days, and she's been wrong about nearly everything else, so I'd hurry."

* * *

The taproom was empty when they came down—all the listeners had drifted out into the streets an hour past, following their humming—which meant there was no one to see Ulyana Kavanagh come down the last three steps at a speed that abandoned all pretense of caution.

Ilse saw her and felt the hearthstone in her chest swing like a bell struck.

It was absurd. It was unbearable. The young woman was travel-stained, sharp-eyed, one hand tucked inside her coat where Ilse would have bet anything a blade or a charm lived, and she was a *stranger*, a complete stranger, a face Ilse had never—

"Ulyana," said Ilse's mouth, all on its own.

The girl stopped dead on the bottom step. "Sister." Hope and fear fought openly across her face. "Sister, do you—did it work, do you have—"

"I have the key." Ilse's hand was already at her collar, working the knot. "I don't have anything else. They took the rest. I want you to understand that before you look at me like that, because I can see you're about to look at me like that, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do with it. You know me. I can see that you know me. And I know—" her breath shuddered— "*nothing.* I stood in front of the regent's table and I told them take the archives, take the sisters, take the chapel bells and the winter I was nine and the name of my first teacher, take all of it, and they did, and the only thing I made them leave was—" 

She had the cloth unwound now. The black key lay bare against her breastbone, its moving script glinting, and it was warm, warmer than it had been all the long climb, warm as a living thing pressed close.

"—was the knowing," Ilse finished quietly. "Not the memory. Just the *knowing*. That you mattered. That whatever I was buying, it was for you. They couldn't take that out without taking the whole trade back, I think. I think it was the load-bearing wall."

Ulyana Kavanagh crossed the room and took Ilse's hands, and her grip was shaking, and her eyes were wet, and Ilse stood inside the stranger's grief like a woman standing in a doorway of a burning house she couldn't remember building.

"Sister Ilse," Ulyana said. "Archivist of the Guild of Small Mercies. Keeper of the Ember Ledger. You taught me to read contract-script when I was eleven and you pretended to be cross about it for four years. You are the reason I can do half of what I do." Her voice frayed. "You were the last person in the world who remembered Sorrel's real name. And now—"

"Now the Undercourt remembers it instead," Ilse said. "Let them keep it. Consider it a deposit." She was astonished to find she could smile. Grief with no memory attached turned out to be strangely portable—you could carry it, even joke over it, because it weighed nothing but itself. "Tell me the rest later. All of it. I'll learn myself back the hard way, one story at a time, like a student. I was apparently good at students."

"You were the best of us," Ulyana whispered.

"Then I'll manage." Ilse lifted the cord over her head and held the key out on her flat palm, and the moment it left her skin the whole room dimmed, candles bowing, shadows leaning in like a crowd. "Now. Someone tell me where this vault is, and someone tell me why the sky is the color of a bruise, and someone tell me quickly—because I spent sixty years of my life on this iron, and I intend to see it opened before whatever is calling those people out into the streets finishes calling."

Outside, the humming had changed. It had stopped being scattered voices and become one voice, hundreds of throats finding the same note, and the note was rising, and beneath it—far beneath, under the town, under the mountain, under the roots of the world—something answered.

The key pulsed on Ilse's palm. Once. Twice. Like a heart remembering its work.

Toward the vault, it seemed to say. *Hurry.*
