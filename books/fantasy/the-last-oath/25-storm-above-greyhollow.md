## 25. Storm Above Greyhollow

The ember guttered at the hour when no honest person was awake, and the world felt it before anyone could name it.

Far below Greyhollow, in a vault older than the city that had grown over it like scar tissue, the last dragon-fire drew itself thin—a thread of gold pulled taut across a darkness that had waited generations to drink. The flame did not go out. It *leaned*. And every prayer that had been answered cheaply in the past season shivered on its hinge, like a door about to swing the wrong way.

Above, the storm came down off the mountain with no wind behind it, which was the first wrong thing. It fell straight, in fat silent drops that hissed where they struck stone, and the shepherds of Greyhollow stood in their doorways watching rain that made no sound against the roof and all its sound inside their chests.

* * *

In Vharrow, Maren the widow woke to her son standing at the foot of her bed.

He had walked home whole three months ago—whole and hollow, with eyes like wells you dropped a coin into and never heard it land—and she had learned to love him the way one loves a chair someone died in: carefully, around the shape of what was missing. But tonight he was standing, and he had not stood unbidden since his return, and he was looking at her with an expression that was almost hunger and almost grief and almost nothing at all.

"Mother," he said. "I'm so hungry."

"You've eaten, love. I fed you at supper."

"Not for what's missing." He raised one hand, slow, like a man wading through deep water, and touched his own chest. "They took it out when they put me back. It's been gone this whole time. I only now remembered to miss it."

The lamp guttered. Outside the shuttered window, the night market's charms—luck-knots, fever-wards, the small harmless weavings Ulyana Kavanagh had sold by the hundred—began to glow all at once, a streetful of pale green eyes opening.

Maren reached beneath her pillow for the charm she had never taken off. It was warm. Then hot. Then it was *pulling*, drawing warmth out of her palm the way a leech draws blood, and she heard—faintly, sweetly, terribly—a voice that was not her son's say, *You prayed. We answered. Now feed us.*

She snatched her hand back. The charm went dark. Her son turned his head toward the sound of her fear with the smooth attention of a weathervane finding wind.

"They're waking up," he said, almost gently. "All of them. Everything that ever listened."

* * *

Wren Calloway was three days' ride south of Greyhollow when her warrant-case began to whisper.

It was a plain thing of oiled leather, crown-stamped, holding the papers that made her what she was: inspector, mage-hunter, keeper of the Godfall's long cold peace. She had slept with it under her head for eleven years and it had never said a word, because paper doesn't. Paper is dead law, and dead law was all the crown had left of the gods.

Tonight it whispered. Tonight the ink moved.

She reined in at the edge of a shrine-town called Candlemere, where the road forked toward the coast, and found the town square lit though the hour was past midnight. Every house had hung something out—ribbons, buttons, a baby's shoe, a strip of wedding lace—old offerings, offerings from the time before the outlawry, dug out of attics and grandmothers' boxes because the shrines were *warm*. Wren dismounted and pressed her bare hand to the stone basin of the central shrine, expecting cold.

It was blood-warm. And beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse through a wall, something knocked.

Three times. A pause. Three times again.

Asking, in the old grammar every child once knew and no one had taught her, to be let in.

"Warrant-officer." An old man had come out of the crowd, hat in his hands, and there were tears freezing on his cheeks. "My wife's hands. The arthritis, forty years. Last night she prayed at this stone, just talking to it like her mother used to, just *talking*—" He held them up: knuckles smooth as river stones. "And today my neighbor's boy, the one with the crooked spine. Straight as a lance. Everyone's being *answered*, officer. Isn't that a good thing? Tell me why it doesn't feel like a good thing."

Because it isn't, Wren thought. Because nothing gives without taking, and these things have been starving for two hundred years, and starving things do not give gifts—they make *loans*.

But she looked at the old man's shining face and the crowd pressing close behind him, hopeful and holy and utterly unafraid, and she said: "Go home. Bar your doors. Don't pray tonight."

They stared at her as if she had told them not to breathe. Behind her, unhurried, the knocking in the stone continued.

That night she wrote in her report, by lamplight, the first true sentence of her career: *The Godfall did not end. It was postponed.*

* * *

Dagny Holt felt her luck burn out all at once, like a fuse reaching powder.

She was in the hold of her own boat, riding at anchor in the estuary below Greyhollow, counting crates of salt-fish that were not salt-fish, when the dice in her pocket—the loaded ones, the ones that had come up seven for her on demand since she was nine years old, a smuggler's charm bought off a woman in Vharrow who sold harmless things that worked—went cold in her hand.

Then colder. Then they cracked, both dice, clean through, sitting untouched in her palm.

"Holt," said her mate, Perrin, from the hatch above, and his voice was wrong. Flat. "Come see the water."

The water was glowing. Not albedo, not moonlight—a green-gold shimmer rising from beneath, from far beneath, from the direction of the mountain, as if the sea were a lampshade and something below had struck a match. Fish were surfacing belly-up in rings, thousands of them, but they weren't dead; their mouths opened and closed in unison, and when Perrin leaned over the rail to look, Dagny grabbed his collar and hauled him back because their mouths were saying something. All of them. The same word, over and over, in a voice like gravel poured slowly:

*Mine. Mine. Mine.*

"Debts," Dagny said aloud, to no one, because she'd spent enough years around Ulyana Kavanagh to know how the guild folk talked. "Everything down there thinks it's owed."

Her luck had been a debt too, then. Bought cheap, spent easy, called in now with interest. She looked at her empty, cracking palms and thought of the girl she'd ferried out of Vharrow, the one paying passage with charms that shouldn't work, walking toward the mountain with a soul in hock, and Dagny Holt—who had never in her life done anything for free—discovered that her hands were already weighing anchor.

Not for free, she told herself firmly. For later. For a favor owed. That was all.

The fish said *mine* until dawn, and then, all together, they sank.

* * *

Ilse did not dream, because Ilse had sold the material dreams are made of. But in the grey hour she lay in the guest cell of the convent at Marrowgate and felt, through the walls, through the floor, through the earth itself, the Undercourt turning over in its sleep like a sleeper whose blanket has been pulled away.

The abbess hammered on her door at first light. "Sister Anna," she said—Ilse's false name, worn so long it fit better than her real one, which she could no longer remember—"the chapel."

The chapel's relic, a fingerbone of some saint whose story Ilse had dutifully memorized and forgotten, was floating. Two inches above its velvet cushion, rotating slowly, trailing light like smoke. Around it the novices knelt in ecstatic prayer, and Ilse saw with an archivist's cold clarity what none of them could: the bone wasn't ascending. It was being *reeled in*. Something above or below or between wanted its property back, and had begun collecting.

Every consecrated object in Marrowgate was doing the same, to greater or lesser degree. Chalices trembled. Bells hummed their own names. And in the crypt below, the dead were restless—not rising, not yet, but shifting in their graves the way sleepers shift when a voice calls them from another room.

Ilse stood in the doorway with her sold-out heart hammering and understood, with terrible precision, exactly which contract was failing. The terms had lived in her memory once. She had traded the memory, but the shape of the loss remained, the way an amputee feels weather in the absent limb, and the shape of this loss was *fire*.

The dragon-fire was guttering. Which meant the vault was open. Which meant—

Which meant the girl. The one whose name Ilse had given away and whose face she still, stubbornly, treasured without knowing why.

"Sister Anna?" the abbess said. "Are you ill?"

"I have to go," Ilse said, and was already packing.

* * *

On the high moor road, three days from Greyhollow, Ulyana Kavanagh stopped mid-stride because the ground exhaled.

There was no other word for it. The turf beneath her boots swelled and sighed, a long breath drawn through a hundred miles of buried dark, and the frost on the grass stood up—all of it, every crystal at once, like a field of tiny glass soldiers presenting arms—and then lay back down.

Beside her, the half-mad priest, Brother Cael, began to laugh. It was not a happy laugh. "There it is," he said. "There's the waking. I told you. I told you what starves does not die, girl. It only gets patient, and then it stops being patient."

Ahead of them, the mountain wore its storm like a hood. Below the storm, Greyhollow's lights burned in their valley, and between here and there the moor was dotted with the old wayside shrines, hundreds of them, cold stone fingers pointing at nothing for two centuries.

They were lighting themselves. One by one, west to east, a slow procession of blue-white flames kindling in basins no human hand tended, and each flame cast a shadow that did not match the shrine that made it—shadows tall and jointed wrong, shadows with too many elbows, shadows leaning east toward Greyhollow like iron filings finding their lodestone.

Ulyana's oathmark woke on her forearm. It had been quiet for days, a sleeping brand under her sleeve, but now it burned and *pulled*, and when she shoved the sleeve back she saw the guild-sigil glowing gold, and gold threads running out from it—visible, actual threads of light, streaming from her arm toward the mountain, toward the vault, toward the lantern where the rest of her waited.

"It knows you're coming," Cael said, delighted and horrified. "The fire knows you. Or the debt does. Same thing, down there."

The nearest shrine-flame flared as she watched. And in the flare she heard it—not words, not yet, but intention, vast and childish and starving, the way a newborn's cry is language without grammar:

*Feed. Feed. Feed.*

Two hundred years ago her guild had ended the Godfall by locking the gods' last scraps behind a wall of dragon-fire and calling it peace. She had learned that in the visions, in the fire-that-remembers: the gods hadn't died. They'd been *starved*, deliberately, patiently, by people who loved their city more than heaven. Her people. Her inheritance.

Now the wall was failing, and everything it had imprisoned was waking with two centuries of appetite.

"We can't outrun it," she said. "Cael. Can we outrun it?"

"Nothing outruns a famine." The priest shouldered his bundle of mad relics and started downhill, toward the lights of Greyhollow, toward the mines, toward the descent. "But maybe it can be fed. That's always been your guild's trade, isn't it? Small mercies. Come along, youngest member. Let's go see what mercy costs at wholesale."

Ulyana looked back once, at the line of burning shrines marching toward her across the moor, at the storm crowding down the mountain like a fist closing, at the gold threads streaming from her own flesh into the dark.

Then she ran, and the shadows of the shrines ran too, parallel to her, pacing her, all the way to the valley's edge.

* * *

And in Vharrow, as the sun rose on a city that had outlawed wonder and was about to drown in it, the Crown's great silence-bell rang the alarm hour—and would not stop. The rope pulled itself. The bell tolled and tolled and tolled, and with every stroke the wards laid into the city walls flared brighter, and with every flare the crowds in the streets grew larger, kneeling, praying, begging the walls to answer them, while the inspectors' patrols broke like sticks on the gathering tide.

In the Bastion, a clerk finished transcribing Wren Calloway's latest dispatch—*the Godfall did not end; it was postponed*—read it twice, went white, and carried it up three flights to the Lord Inspector with hands that shook.

By noon the order would be drafted. By dusk it would be sealed and riding hard toward Greyhollow with a single line of text that would set friend against friend on the doorstep of the Undercourt:

*Subject Kavanagh is the cause. Kill her on sight.*
