## 26. Riot of the Faithful

Wren Calloway had spent nine years learning to read a crowd the way hunters read spoor—where it thickened, where it turned, where it went quiet in the wrong way. The crowd filling Cathedral Square on the morning after the sky cracked was unlike any spoor she had ever tracked. It did not move like fear. It moved like hunger.

She stood on the steps of the Assize Hall with her warrant-badge cold against her breastbone and watched three thousand people press toward the sealed doors of the old cathedral, and she understood, with the flat certainty of a trap snapping shut, that the city had already decided something without asking anyone's permission.

The night before, above Greyhollow, the dragon-fire had guttered. She knew this the way everyone in Vharrow knew it—not by report but by body. Every lamp in the city had burned blue for eleven heartbeats. Every mirror had shown its owner a second face, older, hungrier, looking out from behind the eyes. And every prayer spoken aloud in the dark had been *answered*—not gently, not the way prayers were supposed to be answered, but the way a starving thing answers a hand offered through bars.

In the Tanneries, a woman had prayed for her husband's cough to end, and his lungs had filled with light until he drowned standing up. In the Weavers' Quarter, a man had prayed for rain, and it had rained fish, silver and gasping, for an hour, and then it had rained blood-warm water that smelled of iron and old temples. In the Lamplighters' Row, a child had prayed for her dead cat to come home, and it had come home—with too many joints in its legs and an expression of profound, patient hunger—and the child's mother had beaten it to death with a broom while the neighbors cheered, and then everyone had gone very quiet, because they had all understood at once what cheering for that meant about them.

And still they came to the square. Still they prayed.

Because the world had broken, and broken things make believers of everyone.

* * *

"Inspector Calloway."

Commander Aldous Vey stood beside her at the balustrade, and Wren noted, in the professional part of her mind that never stopped cataloguing, that his hands were shaking. Commander Vey did not shake. He had once held a mage's severed hand steady while it tried to cast with its dying fingers. He had shaken prisoners awake at all hours for thirty years and never flinched at what woke up. His hands were shaking now.

"You've read the orders," he said.

"I've read the orders." She had. They sat in her breast pocket like a coal: **All guild-affiliated persons are to be considered agents of the ongoing magical emergency. Lethal force authorized on contact. Priority designation: Kavanagh, Ulyana. Kill on sight. Do not take prisoner. Do not attempt interrogation. The subject is presumed compromised.**

Presumed compromised. As if Ulyana were a well gone bad, a herd with rot in it. As if the girl who had once stolen honeycakes with her behind the chandler's shop, who had laughed so hard at Wren's terrible impression of the assize-crier that milk came out of her nose—that person were simply a vector now, a thing to be put down before it spread.

"She went down into the Undercourt," Wren said. "Three weeks ago. If the reports from Greyhollow are accurate, the whole mountain came down behind her. There's a reasonable chance she's dead."

"There's a reasonable chance the moon is made of cheese," Vey said. "You taught me that phrase, did you know? Back when you were young enough to have jokes." He rubbed his trembling hands together. "The Kavanagh woman is alive. Everything that's happening—the fire going out, the god-fragments waking—it started when she went down there. The Magistry has drawn its conclusions."

"The Magistry draws conclusions the way drunkards draw portraits," Wren said. "Loudly, and nothing like the original."

"Wren." He said her name quietly, and that quiet frightened her more than the orders had. "I'm not your enemy in this. I requested you for the Greyhollow detail because you know her. Because if anyone can find her quickly and end this cleanly, it's you."

"*Cleanly.*"

"Would you prefer the Second Cohort? They burn houses with families inside when they suspect a charm under the floorboards. You know they do. I've watched you file complaints about it for six years." He looked out over the square. "There are two versions of how the next month goes, Inspector. In one of them, you find your friend, and whatever's left of the law does the finding. In the other, the mob finds her first. Look at them. Tell me which version is kinder."

Wren looked.

The square seethed below, and it was true, it was worse than she'd admitted even to herself. Someone had dragged the statue of Saint Verity off its plinth and was trying to pry open its stone hands, shouting that the saint had held miracles back long enough. A wall of chalk graffiti climbed the cathedral's flank, layer over layer: *GIVE US BACK OUR GODS. GIVE US OUR DEAD. WE PRAYED FIRST.* Someone had painted a crude lantern on the Assize doors—a soul-lantern, Wren realized, with a lurch, the mark of the soul market, though no one down there could possibly know what it meant. Unless they dreamed it. Unless the waking gods were leaking into the city's sleep, teaching it its own iconography.

A priest of the Reformed Rite stood at the barricade the inspectors had thrown up, and he was not preaching against the crowd. He was preaching *to* it, arms wide, voice cracking: "Brothers! Sisters! The law told us magic was death, and we believed, and our dead stayed dead, and now look—look at the Tannery widow, look at the fish-rain—God is not dead, God was only *starving*, and we starved Him with our obedience—" The crowd roared. The barricade bowed.

"They're going to breach," Wren said.

"They're going to do worse than breach." Vey's jaw worked. "At dawn, the Magistry votes on suspending the Statute entirely. Not enforcing it—suspending it. Opening the vaults. Letting every hedge-charmer and bone-reader in Vharrow practice openly, on the theory that licensed magic we can watch is safer than this." He gestured at the square, at the chalk, at the saint with its pried fingers. "Nine years of the law, undone in nine days. And the ones who built the law—you and me and everyone wearing this badge—we're the ones they'll remember did the starving."

* * *

They breached the barricade at noon.

Wren was three streets away when it happened, walking fast toward the Tanneries on Vey's errand—to confirm the identity of the woman whose prayer had killed her husband, to determine whether she was guild-connected, whether the Kavanagh thread led anywhere—but the sound reached her anyway: the crack of timber, the rising animal note of three thousand voices getting what they wanted, and beneath it, faintly, unmistakably, the sound of singing. Not hymns. Older than hymns. The kind of wordless rising melody that crowds find in their throats only when something else is putting it there.

She stopped in the mouth of Cooper's Lane and closed her eyes and listened, because a hunter listens before anything else, and what she heard in that singing was the exact cadence of the prayers she'd heard answered wrong. The melody rose and asked. It did not ask nicely.

When she opened her eyes, the lamplighter on the corner across from her was watching her badge with an expression she had never seen on a civilian's face directed at hers. It was not fear. It was *accounting*. He was weighing what her badge would fetch, or what it would cost him to leave her be, or what the badge was worth now that the law it represented had begun, visibly, audibly, to collapse.

"Move along," Wren said, and hated how thin it sounded.

The lamplighter smiled. "You move along," he said, pleasantly, and turned back to his work—he was lighting his lamps at noon, one after another, though the day was bright, and each flame as it caught burned blue for a heartbeat before settling, and he hummed the crowd's song as he went, and Wren understood that the city had already crossed some line that existed on no map, and that everything she served was now on the far side of it.

* * *

The Tannery widow's name was Maren Ostrow, and she was not guild-connected, and she did not want to talk to an inspector, and she told Wren so through a door held open precisely the width of a chain.

"My husband died of my prayer," Maren said. Her eyes were swollen but dry; she had passed through weeping into some harder country. "Your law said I couldn't pray properly for nine years. So when the sky broke and praying worked again, I prayed the natural way a wife prays. And the light took him. So no, Inspector, I don't know your Kavanagh woman. But if she's the one who went to fix whatever your lot broke—if she's the one who went down the mountain—I'll tell you what I told the Second Cohort before they smashed my neighbor's door in looking for charms." She leaned closer, and the chain creaked. "Tell her to hurry."

Wren stared. "Hurry?"

"The answered prayers are getting worse. Faster. Hungrier. Yesterday the fish-rain; today my sister-in-law prayed for her son to stop drinking, and it stopped his heart, and when they laid him out he sat up at the wake and thanked everyone for coming and drank the toast with them and none of us could leave the room because the door wouldn't open until he finished." Maren's voice did not rise at all. That was the worst part. It stayed level as a ledger line. "Whatever your Kavanagh woman went down to get—tell her the surface can't wait for her to be careful. Things are answering prayers here that nobody's making. My dead husband spoke to me last night from the chimney. He wasn't my husband. It was wearing him like a coat, learning the buttons. Tell her to hurry."

The door shut. The chain rattled home like a small, final verdict.

Wren stood in the lane among the tanning-stink and the fish-bones from yesterday's impossible rain, and she took out the orders, and she read them again, and for the first time she noticed the line she must have skimmed past in her fury and grief: *the subject is presumed compromised.*

Not *dangerous*. Compromised. The Magistry's own phrasing assumed that whatever came up out of the Undercourt would not entirely be Ulyana Kavanagh. And standing in the tanner's lane, hearing the crowd's wordless song drifting from the direction of the cathedral, feeling the air itself go taut as a snare-drum skin, Wren had to admit the possibility lived in her too: that the woman she loved like a sister might climb back into the light carrying something that wore her like a coat, learning the buttons.

And the orders said kill on sight regardless.

And Maren Ostrow said tell her to hurry.

Both things were true. Both things pulled. Wren folded the orders away and walked on, and noticed she was walking faster than investigation required, and did not examine why.

* * *

By evening, Vharrow was burning.

Not all of it. Not yet. But the Chantry of the Reformed Rite on Candlemaker Street was aflame—torched, Wren learned later, not by the mob but by its own congregation, who had carried out the pews and built a bonfire in the street and were feeding the church to it piece by piece while they sang, because the Reformed Rite had preached the Godfall for a century and its priests had known, some of them, about the bargain, about the starvation, and the crowd had found one such priest's letters in the vestry and read them aloud by firelight, and that had been the end of patience.

The Second Cohort rode into the Candlemakers with fire-pikes and made it worse in the way the Second Cohort made everything worse: they saw a crowd, they saw a fire, they saw their orders, and they did arithmetic that ended in corpses. Wren arrived in time to see the first volley, to see a boy of perhaps fifteen go down with a firepike bolt in his shoulder, to see the crowd's song change key—from asking to *answering*—and to feel, with her hunter's senses, the moment the street's ambient dread condensed into something with attention.

Every lamp on Candlemaker Street went out at once.

Then every lamp relit, burning blue, and the flames bent—all of them, uniformly, like grass before wind—toward the Second Cohort's line.

"Fall back," Wren said. She didn't shout it. She said it the way you say a fact, and the sergeant nearest her heard it under the noise anyway, because fear carries. "Sergeant. Fall *back*. Now."

The squad commander did not fall back. He raised his pike and shouted the Statute's enforcement litany—*in the name of the lawful silence*—and the flames on the street stood straight up, all together, like soldiers presenting arms, and Wren felt the hairs on her neck lift, because flames do not present arms, and then the fire left the lamps.

It did not spread. It *left*—lifted out of the glass housings in a dozen blue ribbons and hung in the air above the street, coiling, considering, and every person on Candlemaker Street stopped moving at the same instant, mob and cohort alike, three hundred people holding one collective breath, and the ribbons of fire turned, slowly, like compass needles, and pointed at the squad commander.

He ran. To his credit, he ran. The fire let him reach the end of the street before it caught him, and Wren never learned whether that was mercy or sport, and she hauled two screaming inspectors backward around a corner and pressed them into a doorway and held them there while the light of the burning went strange and holy and terrible against the walls, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*This is what the law was for. This is why Sorrel's generation took the bargain. This exact street. This exact fire.*

And the bargain had failed, or been outlived, or had always been a loan rather than a cure, and now the interest was coming due on the whole city at once, and somewhere beneath a collapsed mountain her friend was carrying the only coin that mattered, and Wren's official instructions regarding that friend were four words long.

*Kill on sight.*

She touched the folded orders in her pocket and felt them differently now: not as a coal but as a key. Whoever wrote those orders had imagined a compromised Ulyana crawling up out of the dark with a god in her teeth. Nobody in the Magistry had imagined *this*—had imagined that by the time Ulyana surfaced, if she surfaced, the question would not be whether she was dangerous, but whether she was the last sane arrangement left standing between Vharrow and its own answered prayers.

* * *

Near midnight, in the smoking quiet of Candlemaker Street, Wren did the thing she had been circling all day the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

She sat on a fallen lintel, took out her field notebook—the hunter's book, the book where evidence went to become reports—and she did not write a report. She wrote two lists.

On the left page, everything the law said: *Kavanagh alive, location unknown, presumed compromised, kill on sight. Statute failing. Cohorts losing control. Magistry voting to suspend the very law we enforce. City burning.*

On the right page, everything else: *Maren Ostrow says hurry. Fire presented arms. Crowd sings a song it didn't learn. The prayers aren't being granted—they're being collected. Something is eating faith in this city, and the law's answer is to shoot the person trying to feed it.*

She looked at the two pages for a long time. A cat with too many joints watched her from a rooftop with an expression of patient hunger, and she looked back at it, and neither of them blinked, and eventually it left.

Here was the truth she had been refusing since the orders arrived: she was not going to kill Ulyana Kavanagh. She had known it in the Assize Hall, known it in the tanner's lane, and now, sitting in the ashes of a church with the law collapsing around her like a burning roof, she finally let herself know it fully. Whatever came up that mountain—even if it wore Ulyana's face and got the buttons wrong—Wren would not loose a bolt at it on the strength of a magistrate's presumption. She would look first. She would listen. She would use every craft they had trained into her, the tracking and the reading and the patience, and she would aim all of it at the one target the Magistry had forbidden her to treat as human.

Which made her, by the letter of her own commission, a criminal as of the moment she set down the pen.

Wren tore the left page out—the law's page—held it to a dying ember from the church-fire, and let it go. It rose on the heat, blackened, unfolded midair like a small dark flower, and was gone over the rooftops toward the river.

The right page she folded away next to her badge, where the orders had been. Then she took the orders out, and looked at them one last time—*kill on sight, do not take prisoner*—and did not burn them. Burning them was romance. Instead she copied them, word for word, into her notebook, so that someday, if it mattered, if there were ever a tribunal or a history or even just Dagny Holt's smirking face wanting proof, she could show exactly what she had disobeyed and exactly why.

Then she went to find a horse that hadn't been stolen, a road that wasn't watched, and the fastest route to Greyhollow.

Behind her, the cathedral bells began to ring—unsilenced for the first time in nine years, rung not by ringers but by the crowd, wildly, joyfully, arrhythmically, a sound like a heart remembering how to beat. Ahead of her, the mountains stood black against a sky where the stars had begun, very faintly, to drift—slowly, majestically, in patterns no astronomer had charted—as if the heavens themselves were turning over in sleep and beginning, at last, to dream.

Halfway to the city gate, a child stepped out of an alley, no older than eight, barefoot in the ash, and looked up at Wren with eyes that reflected light that wasn't there.

"You're going to her," the child said. It was not a question. The voice was the child's, but the cadence underneath it was the crowd's song, the answered prayers, the hungry patience of the thing that had worn Maren Ostrow's husband like a coat. "Good. Tell her the table is set. Tell her we have waited so long to be fed that we have forgotten whether we are guests or the meal. Tell her—" and here the child's face flickered, one instant, into something vast and starving and terribly sad— "*tell her to bring enough for everyone.*"

Then the child blinked, and shivered, and looked around at the alley as if waking, and said, in an ordinary child's ordinary voice, "Mum? Where's my mum?"—and ran off crying into the smoke.

Wren stood alone in the ruined street, heart hammering, and understood that the message had not been meant for her at all.

It had been meant to travel. And she was the fastest road to Ulyana Kavanagh that the waking things had.
