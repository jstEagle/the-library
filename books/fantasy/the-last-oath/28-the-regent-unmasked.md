## 28. The Regent Unmasked

The vault did not open so much as *admit* them.

The door of black basalt—older than the city above it, older than the Godfall itself—swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, and the silence of that soundlessness was worse than any groan or shriek could have been. Stone that had waited three hundred years to move should have screamed. Instead it parted like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand.

I stood at the far end of the chamber and watched the girl walk in.

She carried my fire in a lantern she had not yet learned to hold correctly. That was the first thing I noticed—the way her grip was too tight, fingers white around the handle, as though the ember were a thing that might be wrested from her by force rather than a guest she had agreed to host. She had not yet understood. None of them ever did, not at first. The fire does not need guarding. It needs *feeding*, and what you feed it is the only currency it has ever accepted.

Behind her came the smuggler—Dagny Holt, bloodied at the shoulder, one hand pressed to a wound the Undercourt's salt air had already begun to sour—and the hunter, Wren Calloway, whose warrant I had felt arrive in my halls like a fishhook dragged through water. And somewhere behind all of them, faint as smoke, the archivist Ilse moved through corridors she remembered with her bones if not her mind, carrying the key she had purchased with everything she was.

They thought they had fought their way here.

They thought the door yielding was victory.

*Oh, children,* I thought, and did not let the weariness reach my face. *Every door in this mountain opens for those who owe it something.*

---

Let me tell you how a woman becomes a regent.

It is not ambition. Ambition is a young person's sin, loud and warm and forgivable. What makes a regent is smaller than that and colder: it is the moment when you realize that no one else will do the arithmetic, and so you must, and so you will, every day, forever, because the alternative is everyone dying.

Three hundred years ago there were nine gods walking Vharrow's streets like fat merchants, taking worship the way merchants take coin—casually, constantly, entitled. They called it love. It was consumption. A prayer answered was a soul grazed; ten thousand prayers a day, and the city grew thin as winter grass under their beautiful attention. We called it grace. We sang to it. We starved ourselves smiling.

Then came the Godfall—or rather, then came the bargain that made the Godfall necessary, which no chronicle records because I burned the only copy myself, with these same hands, younger then and still soft.

The gods were starving us. The court beneath the mountain offered terms: seal the gods away—not kill them, nothing so clean—and the city lives. But the seal required fuel. Not gold. Not blood spilled once. Fuel that *lasted*. Souls, given willingly, held in trust, burning slow as peat in a banked hearth. Five souls from the Guild of Small Mercies—the guild I founded, the guild I built from nothing but a conviction that magic belonged to the poor before it belonged to anyone—that was the price. Five souls to buy a city's freedom from its own devotions.

Four of the five we reclaimed within two generations, each return negotiated down the years like a mortgage paid in grief. The fifth was pledged unborn. Kavanagh blood. Ulyana Kavanagh's soul, promised before she drew breath, held in a lantern at the heart of this mountain beside the last dragon-fire, the two of them bound together as payment and furnace both.

And I have kept the accounts ever since.

Do you understand what that means? Do you understand what three hundred years of bookkeeping looks like on a human frame? I was forty when I signed the first page. I am older than the stone this chamber is cut from now, older by choice and by cost, sustained by the same arrangement I administer—a salary drawn from the till I guard. Every year I extend my service is a year the seal holds. Every year the seal holds is a year Vharrow's children grow up godless and *alive*, ignorant and breathing, never knowing why the sky stays where it is.

They would call me monster. Let them. Monsters are permitted appetites. I have none left. Only the ledger.

---

"You," Ulyana said.

Her voice cracked across the vault like ice settling on a pond. She had stopped six paces inside the door, the lantern lifted slightly, as if the ember's light might show her something her eyes refused.

I let the regency fall from me the way one lets a cloak drop—deliberately, folding the office off my shoulders even as the shape beneath it remained unchanged. The crown of woven iron. The robes stitched with contracts in dead scripts. All of it theater, and always had been; the Undercourt loves ceremony the way a leech loves warmth, and I had learned to dress my duty in their costume so that they would leave the substance alone.

What remained when the trappings settled was an old woman in a plain grey dress, hands knotted with age, standing beside a table where five lanterns had sat for three centuries. Four stood dark and cold, long since emptied, their glass fogged from within like breath on a window. The fifth—

The fifth wore her face.

Ulyana saw it. I watched her see it: her own soul in its prison of glass, pressing palms against the inside, mouth moving soundlessly. Begging. It always begs. That is not cruelty; that is what a soul does when it remembers being whole. Mine had begged too, once, before I taught it better manners.

"Say it plainly," I said. "You've earned plain speech. Everyone in this room has bled for plain speech."

"You're her." Ulyana's voice shook. "The first guildmaster. Sorrel's—Sorrel had your portrait in the hall, half burnt, he never said—" She stopped. Started again. "You founded us. You wrote our oath."

"I wrote your oath," I agreed. "*Give freely, take lightly, hold nothing above the smallest mercy.* Yes." I touched the table's edge; dust came away on my fingertips, three hundred years of patient accumulation. "And then I wrote the other document. The one without poetry in it. The one with numbers."

Wren Calloway had gone very still near the doorway, hunter's stillness, the stillness of someone recalculating everything. Good. Let her recalculate. The crown's version of the Godfall—that mad gods fell, that righteous law buried them—was a story written by men who needed the past to justify their fear. The truth was less flattering. The truth was a tired woman at a table, signing.

"The debt," Dagny Holt rasped from beside the door, one hand still on her wound. "You're the creditor."

"I'm the *debtor*, smuggler." I smiled, and felt how terrible a smile must look after three centuries of disuse. "That is what none of you understand. That is what the court wants you never to understand. I do not collect this debt. I *pay* it. Continuously. With service, with vigilance, with—" I gestured at myself, the ancient ruin of me, "—this. The contract named a guarantor who would tend the seal until all obligations were discharged. Four souls returned, yes. The fifth outstanding. So I remain. And remain. And remain."

"And if she takes her soul back?" Wren asked. Her voice was flat, controlled—the voice of a woman asking a question she already dreaded the answer to. "The clause. The counteroffer Ulyana struck with you. If the debt clears—"

"Then the guarantor is released." I turned to look at the girl directly, and I made myself do it kindly, because kindness was the last honest thing I owned. "Yes. That is true. And here is the rest of the truth, since plain speech was promised: the seal does not run on the contract. The contract runs on the seal. The words are the lock's *description*. The fire is the lock. Take back the soul, discharge the debt, void the clause—all of it, every legal fiction this mountain has produced in three hundred years—and the fire gutters just the same, because the fire was never fed by paper. It was fed by *held things*. Souls held in trust. Devotion held in check. Hunger held at bay. Release what is held and the starving begins again, and this time there is no guild left strong enough to strike another bargain, and no one willing to pay what I paid."

The silence that followed had weight. I felt it settle on all four of them, different burdens on different shoulders: on the smuggler, the arithmetic of survival; on the hunter, the collapse of the law she'd served; on the archivist—somewhere out in the dark, key in hand—nothing at all, which was its own kind of tragedy.

On Ulyana, I felt the burden I had placed there deliberately, the way you place a stone on a grave marker: recognition.

Because she had seen it. I watched her see it, there in the ember-light, watching me stand among my lanterns in my plain grey dress with my ruined hands. She saw the mirror. She saw the shape of the road that began with *one small compromise made for love of many people* and ended here, at a table, alone, forever, keeping books for the dark.

"That's what Sorrel meant," she whispered. "When he said the guild dies slowly. He didn't mean members dying. He meant—" Her throat worked. "He meant it becomes *you*."

"Sorrel was a good man," I said, and grief moved through me, unexpected, an old scar remembering rain. "He found the ledger thirty years ago and read every page, and he came down here—I never told any of you this, but you deserve it now—he came down the mines alone, old and half-blind, and stood where you're standing, and he looked at me the way you're looking at me. And do you know what he said?"

No one answered. The ember in her lantern pulsed once, slow, attentive. It knows its history. Fire remembers everything; that is its curse and its nobility.

"He said, *Then we'll find another way to feed it.*" I laughed, softly, helplessly. "Thirty years he searched. Charms and knots and prayers sold in night markets—yes, child, I knew about you, I have known every member of my guild since the day I swore them in—he was looking for a currency the fire would accept that wasn't a self. Generosity instead of collateral. Love instead of lien." My voice roughened. "He died still believing it was possible. And here you stand holding his answer in your hands, and you don't even know yet whether it's an answer or a eulogy."

---

Ulyana set the lantern on the table.

Not slammed—placed, carefully, beside the four cold lamps, so that five vessels stood in a row again for the first time in a century. The light from the ember reached across the dark glass of the empty ones and made them glow faintly, ghosts of fires past, and I confess my breath caught. Three hundred years. Three hundred years of watching that row diminish, one lamp going dark per generation, each reclamation a victory that was also a subtraction. To see it lit again, even partially—

"Do you want it?" she asked me.

The question went through me like a blade finding the one gap in armor I had forgotten to close. *Do you want it.* No one had asked me anything in two centuries except terms, deadlines, conditions. The Undercourt negotiates; it does not inquire.

"What I want," I said, and heard the rust in my own voice, "stopped mattering before your grandmother's grandmother was born."

"That's not what I asked."

The girl had steel in her. Of course she did; Sorrel had trained her, and Sorrel had been trained by Ilse, and Ilse had been trained by me, and whatever else three hundred years of my stewardship had corroded, it had preserved that—one line of stubbornness passed hand to hand like a coal, down through the generations, until it arrived here in this vault glowing hotter than the fire itself.

I looked at the lantern. At the face pressed against the glass—my own face, once, when my lamp was full. At the ember beyond it, the last living fragment of dragon-fire in the world, the heart that beat so the gods would not.

"I wanted," I said finally, "to put it down. That is all I have wanted since the ink dried. To finish the work and put it down and be *finished*. And I have come to understand, over the centuries, that this wanting is precisely why I can never be trusted to decide when the work is done. The exhausted guard opens the gate, child. It is not malice. It is only that she is so *tired*."

I straightened. Something in the chamber responded—the basalt walls, the waiting dark, the court's attention turning toward us through a thousand unseen ears, because of course they were listening. They had always been listening. Why do you think I spoke in riddles to every visitor? Because plain speech was for moments worth spending it on, and this was one.

"So hear my last accounting," I said, loudly enough for the walls, for the listeners, for the ledger. "The girl Ulyana Kavanagh has satisfied every condition of the counteroffer. Her service is rendered. Her courage is documented. By the letter of the original compact, the fifth soul may be released, the debt discharged, and my office ended." I paused. Let it hang. "And by the spirit of the same compact—the spirit I wrote into it when I was young enough to believe spirits mattered—the seal must not fail while a single starving god still hungers above us. These two truths cannot both stand. One of them must break."

"Which one?" Wren demanded.

I smiled again, and this time I let the smile be what it was: an old woman's apology, delivered three hundred years late.

"The one that keeps me comfortable," I said. "Naturally."

I reached for the fifth lantern—her lantern, her soul, her face—and lifted it, and felt the court below me go taut as a bowstring, because they had waited three centuries for exactly this moment, and I had waited longer, and neither of us intended to waste it.

"But understand," I said to Ulyana, and my ruined hands trembled around the glass, "that the moment I set this down, everything I have held back comes due at once. Every hunger. Every god-fragment circling this mountain like gulls round a fishing boat. They will come for the fire. They will come for *you*. And the fight will begin before I finish speaking—"

The lantern's glass split along a seam no eye could see.

Above us, through a mile of stone, something vast exhaled.
