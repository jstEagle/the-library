## 29. Fight for the Lantern

### DAGNY

The vault door was a slab of black iron older than any kingdom Dagny Holt had ever smuggled through, and it was trying to close.

She had her shoulder against it, boots braced on stone worn slick by centuries of other people's bargains, and the iron was winning—not because it was heavy but because it was *hungry*. The metal flexed under her hands like the flank of a living thing. Somewhere inside its blackness she could hear breathing. Slow. Patient. The breathing of something that had been paid to wait and was very good at its work.

"No," Dagny told it, through her teeth.

She was not a mage. She had never wanted to be a mage; magic was cargo like anything else, and cargo you didn't ask questions of. But her whole life had been spent learning how doors worked—how they were bribed, flattered, forced, picked—and this one did not want to be opened so much as it wanted to be *persuaded*, and Dagny Holt had talked her way past customs officers in three kingdoms and a warlord's gatekeeper twice.

"You've held this door for five hundred years," she rasped, hauling her weight against the closing edge. Her palms were bleeding. She could feel her own pulse in them, hot and quick as a bird's. "Longest job anyone ever gave you. I know what that's like."

The iron shuddered. Something behind her face—the thing she'd burned half her luck to keep from Wren's interrogators in that cellar in Vharrow, the small bright coin of fortune her grandmother's line had carried—shifted in its socket beneath her skin, and Dagny made a decision she would never have made sober or sane.

She spent the rest of it.

Not all at once. Luck didn't work that way; you fed it out like rope off a cliff-edge, hand over hand, and every span you gave up was gone forever. She felt each one leave—a warm thread pulling out through her bleeding palms and into the door. *There. There. There.* The memory of her mother's laugh. The certainty that the tide would come in tomorrow. The name of the cove where she'd hidden her first stolen boat. Small things. Then not-small things. The iron drank them and slowed, groaning, and for a breath—for one long, ringing breath—it stopped moving altogether.

"Hold," Dagny whispered to it, the way you'd speak to a horse in a thunderstorm. "Just hold, love. Just until they're done."

Beyond her, in the heart of the vault, Ulyana Kavanagh stood between two pedestals—one bearing a lantern full of slow gold fire, one bearing a lantern full of a woman who wore Ulyana's face and was weeping without sound—and Dagny understood, with the terrible clarity of a gambler watching the last card turn, that only one of those lanterns was leaving this room.

She put both shoulders to the door and held.

### ILSE

Ilse Voss remembered nothing.

This was the truth of her now, and she had learned to carry it the way one carries an empty bucket down a long well-rope: patiently, without looking down. The court had taken her memories as payment for the key—the archives of forty years, every page she'd copied, every friend she'd catalogued—and what remained was a smooth white space where a life had been. She knew *how* to do things. She did not know *why*. She could read the old script on the vault walls; she could not remember learning it. She could walk; she could not say toward whom.

Except.

Except there was one word left in her, lodged like a fishbone in the throat of her emptiness, and when the regent's servants came through the collapsing corridors—things of smoke and ledger-ink, tall as doorways, their faces blank as unsigned contracts—when they came flowing up the stair toward the vault where the bleeding smuggler fought the door, Ilse heard herself shout a name.

"**Ulyana!**"

It burst out of her like a bird from a burnt house. She didn't know whose it was. She knew only that it mattered more than anything else in the white world of her skull, that it was the last coin in the last purse, and that if these ink-things reached the vault, whatever the name meant would be lost.

So Ilse Voss, archivist, nun, informant, traitor—she didn't know those words anymore either, though they waited for her somewhere ahead like weather—took up an iron pry-bar from the mine rubble and planted herself in the corridor.

The first servant came at her like a filing error given legs. Its arm was a scroll that unrolled and unrolled, and where the paper touched her sleeve it began writing on her skin, lines of fine script crawling up her wrist: *terms, clauses, conditions*. It hurt the way guilt hurts—in the place behind the ribs where a person keeps the account of themselves.

"I don't—" Ilse swung the bar. "—*sign*—"

The blow connected. Ink splashed like blood. The servant recoiled, hissing static, and Ilse understood suddenly, with an archivist's instinct that had survived the burning of everything else, what she was fighting. These were creatures of contract. They existed in the spaces agreements left open. And she—

She pressed her palm flat against the corridor wall, against the vein of dragon-warmth that ran through all the deep rock here, and spoke in the formal cadence the old script demanded, words rising out of her like water finding an old channel:

"*Let the record show that no term of any standing agreement grants right-of-passage to the undersigned's agents during active negotiation.*"

The corridor went still.

The ink-things paused, quivering, and Ilse felt the most extraordinary sensation of her hollow new life: the pleasure of a loophole found. Somewhere far above, in whatever registry governed the Undercourt, a clerk turned a page and frowned.

They came anyway. Of course they came anyway—the regent was older than the registry, and rules bent for those who wrote them—but the pause had bought seconds, and seconds were a currency too. Ilse swung her pry-bar and shouted the name again, again, a bell rung in the dark, feeding the single word she owned into the fight like coal into a furnace:

"Ulyana! *Ulyana!*"

Until she fell, and the ink began writing up her arms, and even then—even pinned beneath a creature of pure contractual obligation, its scroll-arm filling itself with her surrender—Ilse Voss kept saying the name, smaller and smaller, like a candle refusing a wind.

### WREN

Wren Calloway had spent eleven years learning how mages died.

That was the ugly honest core of her craft, the thing the crown dressed in titles and warrants and called *hunting*: she studied endings. She knew the seventeen ways a spell failed under pressure, the six ways a shield-mage's nerve broke, the exact moment in a duel when fear overtook discipline. She had written reports on all of it in a neat clerk's hand. She had believed, once, that knowing how magic ended was the same as understanding it.

Standing in the throat of a mountain while the Undercourt's regent poured her will against three people Wren loved, Wren finally understood it differently.

Magic didn't end. Magic was *spent*. Every working was a purse being emptied, and the regent—who had been spending for five hundred years, who had bought a city's peace with a guild's souls and called it mercy—was the greatest spender alive. Which meant she had habits. Rhythms. A tell.

Wren saw it on the fourth surge.

The regent stood at the vault's threshold, robed in shadow the color of closed ledgers, her power rolling toward Ulyana in waves that smelled of cold ash and old paper. And before each wave, for exactly one heartbeat, the shadows around her *drew in*—gathered, like a fist closing, like lungs filling. Five hundred years of practice had made her perfect, and perfection had made her legible. Even gods, Wren thought, even almost-gods, could be read.

Wren had hunted mages by denying them the future. Now she turned the same craft inside out.

"The third wave comes high," she called across the vault, her voice steady in the way that had once made inspectors twice her age go quiet. "Watch her left shoulder. When the shadow gathers, it pulls from the *left* first—her old wound, whatever it was, she favors it. Duck under and come up inside."

Ulyana moved like someone hearing music through water—half-hearing, trusting—and when the next surge came, roaring high along the ceiling in a river of hungry dark, Ulyana dropped beneath it and rose inside the regent's reach, and the great working broke apart like foam on rocks.

The regent turned her head. For the first time in five hundred years, something like surprise crossed the carved-stone calm of her face.

"A hunter," the regent said. "Here. Below."

"Not hunting," Wren said. She walked forward, unhurried, her warrant-case still slung at her hip like a relic from another country. "Auditing."

And she showed the regent what eleven years of studying failures had taught her: where the shadow-things' joints were weakest, which of the ink-servants faltered when their terms were spoken aloud, the precise rhythm of a power that had grown too old and too vast to change its stride. She called the surges before they came. She named the gaps before they opened. She did it in the flat, clear voice of an official report, because that voice had always been her weapon, and the regent—who had negotiated with kings and guildmasters and gods—found herself, for the first time, facing someone who treated her majesty as merely a *pattern*.

Patterns could be broken.

"Your grace keeps excellent books," Wren said, as another working shattered. "But every book has a misprint. Yours is that you believe everyone down here signed something." She nodded toward Ulyana, toward Dagny's bleeding back, toward Ilse's ink-scrawled arms. "We didn't. We just came."

The regent's shadow drew in—that gathering heartbeat—and this time Wren was already shouting "*Now!*" before it fell, and Ulyana was already moving, and the oldest power beneath the mountain missed for the first time in living memory.

### ULYANA

Between the two lanterns, the world had gone very simple.

That was the strange gift of extremity, Ulyana was learning. All her life she had lived doubled—charm-seller above, oathmarked fugitive beneath; friend to Wren, liar to Wren; debtor without knowing the debt. But here, in the vault where the air tasted of struck iron and the floor thrummed with buried fire, there was no doubling left. There was the ember. There was her soul, wearing her face, weeping in its cage of glass. There was her body, bruised and singing with borrowed courage, standing between them because someone had to stand between them.

Dagny held the door. Ilse, who no longer knew her, screamed her name into the dark like it was the only word left in creation—and perhaps it was. Wren, her Wren, her hunter, read the regent's five-hundred-year-old power like a street peddler reading marks, calling out the seams in the storm.

None of them owed her this. That was the unbearable part. The debt had been hers since birth, signed by Sorrel's hand and sealed in a fire that never went out, and these three—smuggler, traitor, hunter—were paying it for her anyway, in luck and memory and law, spending themselves down to wicks.

The regent surged again. Wren's voice cut the dark: "*Left!*"

Ulyana ducked the river of shadow and came up inside it, close enough now to see the regent's face properly—the face that was, some treacherous corner of her mind insisted, familiar. A jawline like Sorrel's. Eyes the grey of old guild-limestone. She had no time to wonder. Later. Everything later.

The two lanterns sat on their pedestals an arm's length apart, and the vessel problem was the simplest and cruelest thing in the room: one vessel, shaped to hold one treasure. The ember that kept the starving gods asleep. Or the soul that had been hers before she could walk. Fire or self. Warmth or name.

Her soul saw her looking.

Through the glass, the other Ulyana pressed her palm flat to the inside of the lantern—the mirror of every prayer Ulyana had ever taught widows to press against a charm—and mouthed words the din swallowed. But Ulyana read lips the way she read ledgers, slowly and completely, and what her soul said was not *save me*.

What her soul said was: *"Take the fire. Leave me. Be free."*

The regent screamed—a sound like a city's worth of contracts tearing—and threw everything, the last of the vault's gathered dark, and behind Ulyana Dagny roared as the door gave an inch, and Ilse's voice cracked on the name, and Wren shouted "*NOW, ULIE, NOW—*" using the childhood name no one below should have known—

And Ulyana Kavanagh reached for the lanterns, one hand for each, and chose.
