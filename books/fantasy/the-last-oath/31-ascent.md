## 31. Ascent

Dagny Holt had been running from things her whole life — tax collectors, harbor guards, a husband in Saltmere with a long memory and a longer knife — but she had never once run *toward* anything until she followed a charm-seller and a broken archivist down into the dark under Greyhollow.

Now she was running toward the light again, and it was trying to kill her.

The Undercourt was coming apart around them like a ship breaking its back on rocks. She could feel it through the soles of her boots before she heard it: a deep, grinding groan that traveled up through stone older than any nation above, the sound of a bargain being torn out by its roots. Then the groan became noise, and the noise became falling.

"Left!" Wren shouted ahead of her, hunter's voice cutting clean through the chaos. "The gallery's giving way on the right!"

Dagny went left. Everyone went left. That was the thing about Wren Calloway — even turned against everything she'd served, she still had a voice people obeyed, and Dagny supposed she always would.

Behind them, the soul market burned.

Not with flame. With *light* — the pale, sourceless glow of a thousand lanterns going out one after another as their contents woke, or fled, or simply ceased to be kept. Dagny had seen a lot of ugly things in thirty years of moving contraband past men who smiled too much, but nothing had prepared her for the sound the lanterns made when they broke. Like a sigh. A thousand small sighs, relieved and grieving at once, rising through the shattered galleries of the market where selves were bought and sold.

Ulyana Kavanagh ran at the center of them all with her arms wrapped around something that glowed like a coal pulled from the heart of the world.

The ember. The last dragon-fire. Dagny could feel its heat on her face from six feet away, could feel it in her teeth, in the old scar on her knuckles, in the luck she'd spent and spent and spent since Vharrow until there was almost nothing left in her ledger but blood and stubbornness. It was the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen and the most terrible, because every god-fragment starving in the world above could smell it now, and they were all coming home to feed.

"Stairs," Ilse said, and her voice was wrong — flat, lost, a woman reading a map written in a language she no longer knew. She had traded every memory of the guild for the key to the fire-vault, and what remained was a nun-shaped hole full of other people's names. But some part of her still knew these tunnels, deeper than memory, in the marrow where the guild had lived before Ilse ever learned to write. "There are stairs. Service stairs. The court never used them. They're proud."

"Proud is good," Dagny panted. "Proud means slow."

They found the stairs behind a curtain of hanging roots that had no business growing this far underground — white roots, thick as hawsers, drinking from some lightless spring. Wren cut through them with a hunter's knife that Dagny only now noticed was etched with wards, the crown's cold script flaring blue-white as it bit. Beyond the roots: steps cut for smaller feet, spiraling up into black.

And below, behind, beneath them all — the collapse came on like weather.

* * *

Dagny counted stairs the way she counted coin: obsessively, because counting kept panic in its box. Two hundred and forty. Three hundred and ten. The spiral narrowed. The air grew warmer, then hot, then unbearable, because Ulyana was carrying summer in her arms and the mountain had never been asked to hold it.

"Put me down a minute," Ulyana said at the four-hundredth stair, and her voice scared Dagny more than the collapsing mountain did, because it was thin. Stretched. The ember was drinking her. Dagny had watched it happen in the vault — watched Ulyana reach for the lantern holding her own soul and choose the fire instead, watched her close her hands around something that should have killed her and walk anyway. Now the cost was coming due in installments: the gray creeping into her lips, the tremor in her forearms, the way she leaned on the wall when she thought no one saw.

"You put yourself down and you won't get up," Dagny said. "I've carried casks of salted eel heavier than you and twice as whiny."

"I'm serious, Dagny—"

"So am I. Eel, Kavanagh. You don't want to know what the brine does." She took Ulyana's elbow and steered her onward, and felt the ember's heat wash over her own hands like standing too near a forge, and did not let go.

Wren climbed above them, knife drawn, eyes constantly upward. Ilse drifted between them all like a ghost attending its own funeral, murmuring directions she couldn't explain knowing. And the mountain screamed.

It wasn't an earthquake, exactly. Earthquakes didn't have *intention*. This was the Undercourt dying, and it did not intend to die alone. Whole galleries sheared away somewhere far below; the sound arrived late and huge, a whale-fall of stone. Dust came down the stairwell in gray rivers. Once, through a crack in the wall, Dagny glimpsed a chamber they'd passed on the way down — the mines, the ones full of sold souls still dreaming their centuries-long dreams — and the dreams were pouring out of it like smoke from a doused fire, shapes of light streaming upward through the rock itself, homeward, or wherever souls went when nobody held their paper anymore.

One of them paused at the crack. Just for a heartbeat. It was small and bright and it had no face, but Dagny would have sworn on her mother's grave — the real grave, not the one she'd bought for the woman whose name she'd borrowed — that it looked straight at her.

Then it was gone, up through forty fathoms of stone, and Dagny stood there with her heart hammering and her eyes stinging and absolutely no intention of telling anyone why.

"Move," Wren said softly, from above. Not urgent. Worse than urgent. Certain. "Something's coming down."

* * *

It came down the stairwell like water finding a drain.

Wren saw it first and shouted, and they pressed themselves into a landing alcove barely wide enough for four bodies, and the thing went past. Dagny got one look and wished she hadn't: a god-fragment, or the ghost of one, all hunger and geometry, a shape like a cathedral that had been folded wrong, trailing cold instead of heat. It flowed down the stairs past them without stopping, without seeing, drawn by something below—

Drawn by the ember's *wake*. By the path of heat Ulyana had left on every step she'd climbed.

"It's following your trail," Wren breathed. Her face, lit faintly blue by her knife's wards, looked carved out of winter. "We need to cool it. Cool you. Somehow."

"Can't," Ulyana said. Simple as arithmetic. "If I shield it, it gutters. If it gutters here, everything above dies screaming, and we die with it. So we climb faster than it climbs. That's the whole plan. That's all I've got."

For a moment nobody spoke. Somewhere below, the fragment found the place where they'd rested and shrieked — a thin, furious sound like a kettle made of grief — and began to come back up.

"Well," said Dagny Holt, who had once talked her way past a Vharrowi customs officer while actively on fire, "I've heard worse plans. I've *had* worse plans. Most of them involved eel."

They climbed.

* * *

The five-hundredth stair. Six hundred. Dagny's legs had stopped reporting in; they simply continued, dumb and faithful as mules. The heat of the ember had soaked through Ulyana's coat, through Dagny's sleeve where she gripped the woman's arm, and Dagny realized distantly that her own skin was blistering where they touched and she did not care. You didn't let go of a person carrying the last fire in the world. That was just seamanship.

At seven hundred and twelve, the stairs ended.

They opened onto a ledge above a vast vertical shaft — the old mine's throat, going up beyond sight into darkness shot through with falling dust. Far, far above, a coin of gray daylight. The surface. Freedom, or at least a different kind of trouble.

Between them and it: a hundred feet of open shaft, and the remnants of a lift platform hanging from rusted chains, swaying gently, like something inviting them to trust it.

"Oh, you're joking," Dagny said.

"The ore lifts," Ilse said. Her hollow voice echoed strangely. "They brought silver up this way, before. Before the court. Before—" She stopped. Frowned at her own hands. "I don't remember what before. There was a before. Someone told me there was a before."

"There was," Ulyana said gently. "You were in it. You'll be in it again." She said it like a promise, and Dagny thought: *that's the difference between us, girl. You make promises you can't possibly keep and then keep them anyway.*

Wren tested the nearest chain and it sang a low, rotten note. "Two will hold. Maybe three. We go one at a time, and the rest of us brace the mechanism. Ulyana goes last—"

"No," said Dagny. "Ulyana goes second. Ember can't wait around on a ledge while we play pulley games — every second it burns, more of those things smell it. I go last."

"Dagny—"

"Somebody has to crank the windlass, and somebody has to be strong enough to do it bleeding, and that's my whole skill set, sweetheart. Smuggling is ninety percent hauling heavy things for ungrateful people." She was already at the winch, hands finding the wheel's spokes the way they found a tiller, muscle memory older than fear. "Ilse first. Then our torchbearer. Then Wren. Then me, and I ride this beautiful rusted coffin up to meet my fortune."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Hunter's eyes. They missed nothing — not the blisters, not the gray lips, not the way Dagny's left hand shook when she thought no one was watching.

"You're hurt worse than you're saying," Wren said quietly.

"I'm a smuggler. Lying about my condition is load-bearing."

But the truth was that Dagny had been bleeding since the vault. One of the regent's honor-guard — those beautiful, terrible things made of contract-language and malice — had opened her side with a claw of pure clause before Wren's warded knife took its head off. Dagny had bound it with her sash and her shirt and a truly heroic quantity of denial, and she'd been climbing on it for seven hundred stairs, and the binding was soaked through, and the world had developed a gentle tendency to tilt.

She cranked the windlass anyway.

Ilse went up first, weightless as ash, staring at the walls of the shaft as though they might tell her who she used to be. Then Ulyana, curled around the ember in the platform's corner, glowing like a saint in a window, looking down at Dagny with an expression Dagny refused to examine. Then Wren, who stepped onto the platform like it was a parade ground and mouthed something at her — *don't you dare die, I owe you an arrest* — as the chains took her weight.

Dagny threw the brake, gripped the wheel, and began to haul them up into the dark.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about heroism, Dagny thought, somewhere around the ninetieth foot of the haul: it's mostly just labor. Cranking. Counting. Keeping the rhythm steady so the chains don't judder and snap. Sweat in your eyes. Blood making your grip slippery. Your shoulders screaming in a language older than words.

She was sixty feet up the shaft's wall, anchored to a bollard some dead miner had trusted his life to, when the mountain decided it was done being polite.

The groan came first — deeper than anything, deeper than the Godfall itself must have sounded — and then the ledge they'd stood on simply ceased. She heard it go rather than saw it: stone becoming avalanche, the service stairs folding down into the dark like a fan closing. The dust wave hit the shaft a heartbeat later, a hot gray blast smelling of iron and old prayers, and the lift platform swung wild on its chains, and Wren shouted something, and Ulyana's light flared like a struck match in a tomb.

And the bollard moved.

Not broke — *moved*, tilted, its ancient mortar giving up its ancient ghost, and Dagny felt the whole physics of her situation rearrange itself in one lurching instant. The windlass skidded. The chains paid out. The platform dropped two feet, three, five, and above her Wren's ward-knife flared as the hunter did something clever and desperate with the guide rail, and none of it mattered if the anchor went, because the anchor was going, tilting, tearing free—

There wasn't time to think, which was fortunate, because Dagny Holt had never been much of a thinker. She was a doer. A hauler. Ninety percent of smuggling was moving heavy things, and the heaviest thing she'd ever moved was the platform's guide cable, which she caught in both bare hands as the bollard tore loose, wrapping it twice around her forearm and throwing her whole body backward across the winch housing, becoming, for exactly as long as she could manage it, the anchor herself.

The cable bit. The weight hit. The world became pain.

She held.

She held the way she'd held everything her whole life: badly, angrily, without any talent for it whatsoever, out of sheer ornamental refusal to let go. The cable ground across her forearm and she felt skin go, then something under skin, and the heat of it was almost funny given everything else she'd endured today. Above her the platform steadied, swung, steadied. Ulyana's face appeared over its edge, ember-light pouring down the shaft like honey, and Dagny had never seen anyone look so much like dawn.

"DAGNY!" Ulyana screamed. "Let go! We'll come down for you!"

"And drop you?" Dagny called back, and heard how wrecked her voice was, and laughed anyway, because what else was there? "Sweetheart, I'm a professional. Cargo comes first. Always. Ask anyone I've ever robbed."

"Crank," Wren's voice cut through, hard as a cleaver. "Dagny. Look at me. Don't hold — *haul*. Short strokes. I've got the rail braced from above. You pull, we take the slack. Together. On my count."

So they did it together, the four of them strung up a mine shaft like beads on a rotten string: Wren levering the rail, Ulyana hauling with ember-bright hands that shouldn't have had strength left in them, Ilse — hollow, memory-less Ilse — throwing her whole slight weight on the winch handle beside Dagny without being asked, without knowing why, her body remembering solidarity her mind had sold away. And Dagny pulled, stroke by stroke, foot by foot, watching her own blood fall past her boots into the dark and thinking, with the strange bookkeeping clarity of the badly wounded, that she had finally found a debt worth paying in full.

The rim of the shaft came up to meet them. Gray sky. Cold wind. Grass — actual grass, bent and wind-flattened, on the shoulder of Greyhollow mountain under a sky bruised purple with storms that weren't weather.

Dagny got the platform hooked on the surface crossbeam, got everyone over the lip, and got exactly one knee onto solid ground before her ledger finally, mercifully, called in its balance.

* * *

She came back to herself in pieces.

Wind, first. Real wind, cold and enormous, combing over the mountainside. Then weight — someone's coat over her, someone's hands pressing her side, the wet-soak warmth that meant the pressing wasn't working well enough. Then voices, layered like tide.

"—still breathing, she's still breathing, hold the light closer—"

"—can't stop it, the edge is ragged, I need a needle, I need—"

"—there's a village, Greyhollow town, half a mile down the track, I saw chimneys—"

"—she can't be moved—"

"She *will* be moved," said Ulyana Kavanagh, and her voice had changed. Dagny knew voices the way other people knew faces, and this one had banked coals in it now, dragon-fire banked under human ribs, and when Ulyana spoke the storm overhead actually quieted to listen. "Help me lift her. Gently. Gently — she's carried enough."

Hands slid under her. Strong ones, careful ones. She was lifted off the mountain she'd bled on, and she managed, through a throat full of rust, to get out the only thing that mattered:

"Ledger's... in my left coat. Page one. Cross out... everything. Start fresh."

"We'll start fresh," Ulyana whispered. Dagny felt a tear land on her cheek, absurdly warm, absurdly welcome. "Everything crossed out. You're not allowed to die until you see it."

"Bossy," Dagny murmured. "No wonder... the court wanted you..."

The sky overhead was doing something wrong. She saw it through half-closed eyes as they carried her down the track toward the chimney-smoke of Greyhollow town: clouds peeling back in vast slow spirals, stars showing through at the wrong hour, and among the stars, shapes. Hungry shapes. Descending. Every starving god-fragment in the world turning its blind attention toward one small figure on one mountainside carrying the last ember of dragon-fire toward the shrine at the summit.

Toward the guttering. Toward the end of scarcity, or the end of everything, depending on what Ulyana Kavanagh chose when she got there.

Dagny tried to say something useful about this. Something tactical. What came out was: "Don't spend it all... in one shop..."

"Hush," said Ulyana. "Save your strength. You'll want it for the argument we're going to have when you're better — about you nearly dying on my behalf."

"Worth it," Dagny said, meaning it, and let the dark take her, trusting — for the first time in her entire crooked, careful, debt-ridden life — trusting completely that someone would carry her the rest of the way.

Behind them, far below, the last galleries of the Undercourt fell silent forever. Ahead, at the summit, the old shrine waited with its empty altar-stone, and the sky came down to meet it, and the ember in Ulyana's arms beat like a second heart, counting down to morning.
