## 32. The Regent's Last Oath

The regent had worn many names across her long service, but the oldest one was Sorrel's mother's name, and the name before that belonged to a girl who had once sold luck-knots in a night market that no longer existed.

Her name, in the beginning, had been Maren Vael.

She lay now on the floor of the vault of embers, where she had lain for what felt like an age and was, in truth, only the space between one heartbeat and the next. The collapse of the Undercourt groaned through the stone above her like a whale singing in deep water. Dust sifted down in golden threads, catching the light of the dragon-fire — *her* fire, *their* fire, the ember she had carried for three hundred years so that the world would not wake up hungry.

She had been the first guildmaster. She had written the debt-book with her own hand, in ink mixed with her own blood, because that was how binding contracts were made in those days, before the Godfall taught everyone to be more careful. She had watched five souls weighed against one city and called it mercy. She had told herself, every morning for three centuries, that it was mercy.

She did not tell herself that anymore. That was what dying was for, she thought. Dying was when you finally stopped lying to yourself, because there was no longer anyone left to lie to.

* * *

The battle, if it could be called that, had lasted less time than a bell's toll.

Dagny Holt had held the door with her back and her blood and a smuggler's talent for making barriers out of nothing — a chain here, a fallen beam there, the whole architecture of a life spent slipping through locked places turned inside out into a life spent keeping them shut. The regent had heard her laughing even as the court-things broke against her, laughing the way sailors laugh in weather, because the alternative was to weep and there was no time.

Wren Calloway had fought like someone unlearning everything she'd ever been taught. The crown trained its hunters to bind, to silence, to erase; Wren had taken those same knots and tied them backward, and the court's heralds had come apart like wet paper in her hands. The regent had watched her do it and thought: *there is the guild's future too, if she wants it. They always come back to us, one way or another. Magic remembers who served it honestly.*

And Ilse — poor Ilse, hollowed down to a single memory, standing in the firelight with her hands open and empty and her face lit with something the regent hadn't seen since before the Godfall: pure, uncomplicated recognition. *I remember one thing,* she had said. *Her name.* And she had said Ulyana Kavanagh's name aloud in the vault where names were currency, and the saying of it had broken two of the court's bindings like ice on a spring pond.

The regent had done the least of any of them. That seemed right. She had done the most damage of any of them, over the longest time, and the ledger was finally coming to collect from *her* account.

The regent — Maren, she corrected herself, *Maren* — had been struck through by the last herald as it died. It had not used a blade. It had simply reached into her chest and taken hold of the oath itself, the old oath, the flawed one, the contract she had signed in blood and hubris, and it had pulled. She felt it go out of her like a hook leaving flesh. And with it went the thing that had kept her alive past all reason: the clause. *Serve until the fire dies.*

The fire was not dead. But the court was falling, and contracts need two parties, and one of them was crumbling into rubble beneath a mountain.

So now she lay on the warm stone, and the stone was warm because the ember was near, and the ember was near because Ulyana Kavanagh had carried it out of the lantern with both hands and had not flinched, even though the vessel could hold only one thing, soul or fire, and she had chosen the fire. She had chosen the world over herself. Maren had known, watching her make the choice, that this was the moment the guild stopped being hers.

* * *

Footsteps came across the vault floor. Not running. The running was over; the others were hauling Dagny toward the ascent stairs, arguing in low urgent voices about pressure wounds and smuggler's stubbornness, and the mountain was coming down around their ears, and still these footsteps came slowly, because they belonged to someone who had decided that some things must not be hurried.

Maren turned her head. It cost her more than it should have. Everything cost more than it should have, now.

Ulyana knelt beside her. The ember sat cradled against the young woman's chest in its iron vessel, and its light moved on her face like water moving on a riverbed — gold, then rose, then a white so clean it looked like the first hour of the world. Her sleeves were scorched. Her oathmark showed plainly on her wrist where the sleeve had burned away: the knotwork sigil of the Guild of Small Mercies, glowing faintly, awake for the first time in generations.

"You're dying," Ulyana said. It was not a question. In the vault, under the ember's light, lies were painful and truth came easily, and besides, Maren thought, this one had always seen straight through to the bone. That was the trouble with charm-sellers. You learned to read people or you starved.

"I have been dying for three hundred years," Maren said. "I've simply been very slow about it." She tried to smile. She wasn't sure the muscles remembered how. "Come closer, child. My voice doesn't travel anymore. I gave it to the mountain, along with most of the rest of me."

Ulyana leaned down. Up close, Maren could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the soot in the lines of her hands, the terrible steadiness of her. She had the look of someone who had set down an enormous weight and not yet discovered that her arms were free.

"Sorrel sent me," Ulyana said quietly. "Before he died. He said — he said the debt was mine, but the guilt was never supposed to be. He said you'd know what that meant."

"Sorrel," Maren said, and the name was an ache. "He was my student. Did he know? Did he ever guess what I was?"

"He guessed you were very old. He said you hummed songs nobody had written yet."

Maren laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough tasted of ash. "That's one way of putting it. Yes. I hummed the songs I wrote, and let the years forget me, and wrote them again under other names, and let the years forget those too. It's remarkable how thoroughly a person can disappear while standing in plain sight. You would know."

"I hid behind a market stall," Ulyana said. "You hid behind a throne."

"A throne is just a stall with better lighting." Maren closed her eyes. Above them the mountain groaned again, deeper, and a crack ran across the vault ceiling and wept dust. There was not much time. There had never been much time; that was the whole lesson of her long life, learned too late to be of use. "Listen to me, Ulyana Kavanagh. Listen, because I will only say this once, and then I am going to stop being the regent, and I would very much like to spend whatever remains being Maren Vael, guildmaster, for a few minutes at the end."

* * *

"When I founded the guild," Maren began, "it was not a guild. It was six frightened people in a cellar, the week the sky tore open. Do you know what the Godfall actually was? Not a war. Not a punishment. A famine. The gods did not die of swords, child. They died of hunger. The world's faith had been dammed up — hoarded — by the temples, by the crowns, by every great house that wanted magic as property. Power pooled where it was hoarded, and everywhere it didn't pool, things starved. The god-fragments that woke ravenous — they are not monsters. They are what devotion looks like after three hundred years of drought."

Ulyana's breath caught. Maren felt it — the young woman's understanding arriving like cold water.

"The court beneath the mountain offered me a bargain in that cellar week. Five souls against the city's survival. I took it. I told myself the souls were a price, but that was a lie I needed; the souls were a *mechanism*. A soul held in trust is a vessel, and a vessel can carry an ember, and an ember can keep the starving things asleep until the world learns to feed them properly. Your soul, Ulyana — pledged at your birth, before you could consent — was never meant to be eaten. It was meant to be a lamp. I let the court convince me that the lamp mattered more than the person. That was my sin. Not the bargain. The arithmetic. I put the fire first and the people second, and everything rotten in the last three centuries grew from that one reversed fraction."

"The true oath," Ulyana said. Her voice was careful. "Sorrel whispered about it. He said the original guild had an oath before the contract, and the contract buried it."

"Yes." Maren gathered herself. This was the part that mattered. This was the only part that mattered, and she found, to her astonishment, that her eyes were burning. When had she last wept? Not in the vault, not in the court, not at any of the funerals she had secretly attended for the four souls she had reclaimed. Perhaps at the fifth. Perhaps at a birth, long ago, when a guild midwife placed a squalling infant in her arms and said *name her*, and Maren had looked at the child and thought: *this one will end what I began.* "Say it after me. Not because I command it — I have no right to command anything, and less than no right to pass you a burden without your hand upon it. Say it because it is true, and because you have already lived it, tonight, in this room, when you chose the fire over your own face in that lantern."

She took Ulyana's wrist — the marked wrist — in both her ruined hands. The oathmark pulsed under her thumbs like a small captive sun.

"Magic is not a treasure," Maren said. "It is bread."

Ulyana's lips moved. "*Magic is not a treasure. It is bread.*"

"It exists to be given away. The moment we hoard it — for safety, for law, for the greater good, for any reason at all — it begins to starve, and starving magic devours. The crown outlawed it and made the famine worse. My contract rationed it and made the famine worse. Every lock we ever built fed the dark. So: the guild's one law, older than the debt, older than the fall —"

"Give it away," Ulyana whispered. "Give it away, give it away, until no one needs to take."

"*Until no one needs to take,*" Maren echoed, and felt the words leave her and enter the girl, and felt them *take root* — she felt it physically, a warmth passing from her hands into the oathmark, the mark drinking the old vow and brightening, and somewhere in the stone beneath them the last threads of the flawed clause dissolved like sugar in rain.

She was released. Three hundred years of service, ended by four spoken words passed hand to hand like a cup of water.

Maren Vael lay back on the warm stone and began, at long last, to be mortal.

* * *

"Will it hold?" Ulyana asked. Her voice wavered now; the steadiness was cracking, and Maren was glad, was fiercely glad, because steadiness was the regent's disease and tears were a healthy woman's luxury. "The new covenant. The mountain is collapsing, the court is breaking, the fire — the fire is in my arms and the starving things are waking above us. What holds it together now?"

"You do," Maren said. "Not your soul. Your choice. Don't you see what you did in this room? The court demanded payment and you refused to pay with anyone but yourself — and then, given the final choice, you refused scarcity itself. One vessel, one treasure, take it or leave it — that was the court's game, and it was *my* game, and you declined to play. You'll find a third option. You always would have. That is why I chose your grandmother, and her mother before her. We Kavanaughs — yes, child, I named your line, I have been choosing guildmasters for nine generations and telling myself I was only an observer — we are not a family of heroes. We are a family of *shopkeepers*. We sell small mercies at fair prices and we have never once believed that anything holy should be locked away."

Above them, the mountain screamed. Not settling-stone, not collapse-rumble: a scream, high and thin, the sound of god-fragments tearing loose from their long sleep, and Maren felt the ember in Ulyana's arms shudder like a living heart hearing its name called by hungry mouths.

"They'll converge on the fire," Maren said. "On you. They can smell it — the last warmth of the world's first morning. You cannot outrun them and you cannot fight them; fighting a famine with fists is how my generation killed our gods." She fumbled at her throat and drew out a cord she had worn for three hundred years under court silk and regent's steel: a plain iron key, unremarkable, the kind that opens a shrine door. "The mountaintop shrine above Greyhollow. The old one, the pre-fall one, where the gods were fed openly, before the hoarding. Take the fire there. And when they come — and they are coming now, child, all of them, I can hear them climbing —"

She stopped. Looked at Ulyana's face, soot-streaked and tear-bright and waiting.

"Don't hoard it," Maren Vael said, and smiled, and this time the smile worked. "Don't spend it. *Share* it."

Ulyana took the key. Their fingers touched around the iron, and the ember flared once — gently, the way a banked fire leans toward a draft — as if it recognized, at the end of its long captivity, the shape of the hands that would finally set it free.

Maren's sight was going gray at the edges. Through the gray she watched Ulyana rise, watched her turn toward the stairs where Wren stood supporting Dagny's weight, watched Ilse reach out a hand to steady the girl who was, by every measure that mattered, the youngest member of the Guild of Small Mercies.

"Maren," Ulyana said — her name, her real name, spoken aloud for the first time in three hundred years by anyone but herself. "Thank you."

"No," Maren said, sinking back into the warm stone, into the dust-gold light, into the long-delayed rest. "Not thanks. *Inheritance.* Go up, guildmaster. Go up, and give it all away."

The mountain shook. Somewhere far above, the first of the starving gods crested the ridge of Greyhollow and opened its mouth.

And Maren Vael closed her eyes, smiling, and began — at last, at last — to sleep.
