## 33. Climax: The Guttering

The mountain had a name once. Ulyana was sure of it—some syllable the old guild songs had carried before they were burned out of every book in Vharrow—but no one living could say it anymore, and so the peak above Greyhollow went nameless, wearing its snow like a shroud it refused to remove.

She climbed toward it through wind that did not feel like weather.

That was the first wrongness she noticed, hours before the shrine came into view: the wind had intention. It pushed against her left shoulder and eased her right, herding her, the way a shepherd's dog moves a flock it has been told not to bite. The air tasted of copper and candle-smoke and something older underneath, something like the smell of a room where someone had wept for a very long time.

Behind her, Dagny leaned on Ilse's arm, her bandaged side seeping rust-brown through the linen with every third step. She had insisted on coming. *You don't get to have all the stupid ideas yourself,* she'd said, and then coughed blood into a rag, and Ulyana had not known how to argue with that.

Wren walked last, her hunter's coat swapped for plain wool, her warrant left somewhere below in the collapsing dark of the Undercourt along with everything else she'd believed about the crown. She carried the vault's iron lantern-case empty against her chest, as if already mourning what it would not hold.

And at Ulyana's own breast, wrapped in three layers of blessed ash-cloth that Sorrel himself had woven decades ago, the ember of dragon-fire breathed.

Not burned. *Breathed.* She could feel it against her sternum like a second heart, slow and vast and patient, expanding when she inhaled and contracting when she exhaled, until she could no longer tell which rhythm belonged to whom. It had been that way since the vault. Since she had reached past her own soul—the soul in its lantern, wearing her face, begging her with her own voice—and chosen the fire instead.

*The vessel only holds one.*

She still heard that begging sometimes, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Her own voice, saying *please* to her. She had made herself not answer it. There were debts you paid by looking away from, and there were debts you paid by walking straight into them, and this mountain held the second kind.

"There." Ilse pointed with her free hand. Her voice came out strange—flat, wondering. She still didn't remember the guild, didn't remember Sorrel or the ledger or the forty years she'd spent feeding the court beneath the world its information grain by grain. But some things lived deeper than memory, and when Ilse looked at the shrine, her hand knew the gesture of pointing before her mind knew why.

Above them, where the treeline surrendered to scree and the scree surrendered to sky, stood the mountaintop shrine: a ring of standing stones older than Vharrow, older than the Godfall, their surfaces worn smooth by ten thousand years of pilgrims' palms. Snow lay in the hollows between them like unspilled milk. And in the center of the ring, on a low altar of black glass, sat a brazier that had been cold for two hundred years.

It was not cold now. It was worse than that. It was *hungry*.

Ulyana felt it before she understood it—a pulling sensation behind her navel, faint but constant, like the first tug of a tide going out. The stones themselves seemed to lean inward. Even the snow crept, grain by grain, toward the empty altar, as though the whole mountaintop were slowly being swallowed.

"They can smell it," Wren said quietly. "The fragments. They can smell the ember through the cloth."

"Then let's not keep them waiting," said Dagny, and grinned, and ruined the grin entirely by wincing.

They climbed the last stretch in silence, because there are silences that are emptiness and silences that are fullness, and this one was so full it pressed on Ulyana's eardrums like deep water. The wind had stopped herding them now. It simply watched.

* * *

The god-fragments came at dusk.

Ulyana saw the first one as a distortion in the snowlight—a place where the evening air bent, the way heat bends over summer road-stone. Then the bending took on edges, and the edges took on suggestion, and the suggestion became almost-shape: something like a shoulder, something like a crown of thorn-light, something like an eye that was less an eye than the *idea* of being seen.

It drifted down the mountainside without touching the ground. Where it passed, the snow did not melt; it *remembered melting*, going briefly glassy and warm, then refreezing in patterns no water had ever made.

More followed. Out of the western clouds, down from the northern crags, up through cracks in the rock itself—they converged on the shrine from every direction, dozens of them, then scores, each one a splinter of something that had once been whole. Starved things. Two hundred years ago, the guild's bargain had dammed the flow of worship and sacrifice and prayer, had starved the old gods down to embers of themselves, and these were the embers' ghosts—or perhaps the gods' ghosts were the embers. Ulyana no longer trusted the difference.

The nearest fragment turned toward her. It had no face, and yet she felt seen—seen the way she had never been seen in her life, seen past the oathmark under her sleeve, past the name she'd fed to the ledger, past the soul she'd left in a lantern below. It saw the ember. Of course it did. To a starving thing, a hearth is visible from the end of the world.

"*Little keeper.*"

The voice did not come through her ears. It arrived fully formed inside her skull, the way a memory arrives, the way Sorrel's dying whisper had arrived. It sounded like a thousand voices speaking in imperfect unison, slightly out of step, a choir that hadn't practiced together since before the Godfall.

"You brought the fire back to us," the choir said. "How thoughtful. How *cruel*. To carry bread past the mouths of the starving."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. She made herself speak anyway, and her voice cracked only a little. "I didn't bring it for you."

"No," agreed the fragments, all around her now, drifting into the ring of stones like smoke pouring into a bottle. The light dimmed. Not cloud-dimmed—*consumed*-dimmed, the dusk itself being drunk. "You brought it to burn. You climbed all this way to feed it to the sky and call it mercy. We know the shape of human generosity, little keeper. It is always a shape with a knife in it."

Dagny shifted beside her. Ulyana felt more than saw Wren's hand drift toward the hunter's knife she still wore out of habit. Ilse stood utterly still, tears freezing on her cheeks—she didn't know why she was weeping, and that somehow made it worse.

"No knife," Ulyana said.

"Then what?"

And here was the moment she had been climbing toward for weeks, through mines full of dreaming sold souls, through a market where selves were traded like spices, past the regent who had been her guildmaster's guildmaster, carrying an ember that wanted to be given and a grief that wanted to be kept.

She unwrapped the ash-cloth.

Light poured out—not the light of flame exactly, but the light of *first mornings*, gold shot through with green, the color the world must have been before anyone thought to name the days. The dragon-fire. The last of it. Enough warmth in that small writhing knot of brightness to wake every sleeping god on the continent, or to end them forever, depending entirely on the hands that held it.

The fragments surged. The choir-voice rose to a frequency that rattled her teeth: "*GIVE IT—*" and every stone in the ring groaned, and the snow leapt off the mountain in a white shriek, and Ulyana Kavanagh, charm-seller, oath-breaker's heir, final living member of the Guild of Small Mercies, held the fire out over the black-glass altar—

—and did not put it down.

"Hear me first," she said, and her voice, small as it was, cut through the roar like a bell through fog. Because she was standing in a ring of standing stones that had been built for exactly this—for oaths, for covenants, for words spoken aloud in front of witnesses older than nations—and the stones *listened*. She felt them listening, felt the mountain lean in. "Hear me, or starve."

The fury of the fragments stuttered. Curiosity, even in starving gods, is stronger than hunger for precisely one instant—and she spent that instant the way Sorrel had taught her to spend everything: carefully, and all at once.

"I know what you want," she said. "You want the fire. All of it. Swallowed whole, gone down your throats in one night of feasting, and by morning you'd be whole again and the world would be yours—" She shook her head. "And I know what happens after. I've seen it. In the vault, in the visions the fire showed me. A god that eats everything in one meal wakes ravenous forever. That's what the Godfall was. Not your death. Your *appetite*, unbound. You fell on the world like a famine falls on a granary, and my guild bought peace by locking the pantry door—with your food inside, and mine."

The nearest fragment had gone very still. Its almost-face tilted, listening. Behind it, the others stilled too, a storm deciding whether to become weather.

"So here is my counteroffer," Ulyana said. "The same one I made below. I'm told it's becoming a habit."

She opened her hands.

"But I'm not selling this time. And I'm not trading. And I'm sure as the snow not *bargaining*—your court taught me what bargains cost, and I decline the tuition. I am going to give you the fire. Freely. No contract, no clause, no debt. But not *to* you." She drew a breath that tasted of gold and green. "Through you. Every fragment that takes from this ember takes a *portion*—no more than its share, and no fragment goes without. Not the strong ones. Not the loud ones. Not even the ones down there in the dark who sold themselves away and forgot they ever had names. Everyone eats, or no one does. That is the covenant. That is the whole of it. Take it or leave me standing here until the sun burns out."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

Then the choir-voice, and it was softer now, and infinitely more terrible for it: "Why?"

Of all the questions. Of all the accusations, the bargains, the threats—it asked *why*, in the voice of a thing that had not been offered anything freely in two hundred years, and Ulyana found her eyes burning.

"Because my guild got it backwards," she said. "For two hundred years we hoarded. We hid magic in sleeves and cellars and ledgers, we sold souls to keep the peace, we treated wonder like contraband. And it starved you, and it hollowed us, and it killed everyone I loved. The regent died down there still believing scarcity was safety. She was wrong. Scarcity is just starvation with good manners." She lifted the ember higher, and it flared, and the light touched every almost-face in the ring at once. "So this is the last act of the old guild, and the first act of whatever comes next. Magic was never meant to be owned. Not by crowns. Not by courts. Not even by gods."

She set the dragon-fire down on the black-glass altar.

And gave it away.

* * *

It did not go out. That was the miracle she would spend the rest of her life explaining, and the part no one would believe: it did not go out.

The ember split—but *split* was too violent a word, too much like breaking. Rather, it *shared*, the way a candle shares itself with a hundred candles without growing shorter, the way a song shared is not a song diminished. A thread of gold-green light unspooled from the heart of the fire and flowed into the nearest fragment, and the fragment—

*sang.*

There was no other word for it. The almost-shape blazed suddenly into almost-presence, and the presence made a sound that was not sound, a resonance in the bones of the mountain, a chord that had been waiting two hundred years to be struck. And it did not devour. It *held* its portion the way cupped hands hold water, careful, astonished, and passed nothing back and took nothing more, because taking more had never once occurred to it now that enough existed.

The second fragment received its portion. The third. The tenth. The fiftieth. The ember poured itself out across the ring of stones and did not shrink, and the shards of dead gods lit up one by one across the mountaintop like a city waking at evening, and the light they shed was not the harsh gold of conquest but the soft mixed light of many different dawns—each fragment burning its own color now, amber and violet and river-green and one that was a color Ulyana had no name for and suspected no language had yet invented, because the world had been saving it.

The wind changed. It had been herding and watching and waiting, and now it simply *moved*, flowing down the mountain in long warm rivers, carrying the scent of the shared fire south toward Vharrow, east across the plains, everywhere at once, a message written in temperature: *the famine is over.*

Ulyana stood at the altar's edge with her palms open and empty, watching the last of the ember's original body dissolve into the distributed blaze, and felt—nothing leave her. That was the strangest part. She had expected loss. Sacrifice was supposed to cost; every story she'd ever been told agreed on that. But the fire had not been taken from her, because it had never been hers. It had only been passing through, the way all gifts pass through, the way Sorrel's kindness had passed through him from people neither of them ever met.

"It's beautiful," Ilse whispered. She was crying openly now, and laughing, and pressing both hands flat over her heart as though checking that it still worked. "I don't remember—I don't remember any of you, I don't remember why I know this—but I know this. I know this *fire*." Her eyes went wide. "Ulyana. I remembered your name. Why do I remember your *name*—"

Later. There would be time for later. Because Dagny was swearing softly and reverently in smuggler's cant, and Wren had dropped to one knee in the snow with her head bowed like a woman at a funeral or a coronation, unable to decide which.

And the fragments—the god-fragments, the starving splinters of the old world—turned, all together, all of them at once, toward the four small mortal figures standing in their light.

Ulyana did not flinch. Whatever came, it would not be teeth. She had seen to that. Nobody at this table went hungry, and so nobody at this table needed to bite.

The choir-voice spoke one final time, and it was no longer a thousand voices out of step. It was in tune now. It said:

"*Little keeper. What shall we call you?*"

And Ulyana—who had sold her name to a ledger, whose family line ended with her, whose soul slept in a shattered lantern far below—smiled through her tears and answered with the only title she had left, the one that fit her like a key fits the lock it was cut for:

"Call me what the guild called me. Call me the youngest member."

The light swelled. Far below them, in Vharrow, in a hundred towns whose names the maps had forgotten, prayers rose like sparks off a bonfire—and for the first time in two centuries, something answered back gently, asking only small things in return.

But on the mountainside below the shrine, half-buried in fresh snow, something else had begun to glow: the iron lantern-case Wren had carried up empty, still holding the shape of the vessel that had held Ulyana's soul.

And inside it, faint as breath on winter glass, a face was forming.
