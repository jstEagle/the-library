## 34. Aftermath of Light

The world did not end the way Wren Calloway had been trained to expect endings.

She had been taught—drilled, sworn, scarred with the doctrine of it—that wild magic was an ending. That the Godfall had been a mercy, that the crown's law was a dam holding back a flood that wanted nothing but to drown the living. Every hunter's oath she'd taken at nineteen had been built on that single stone: *magic is hunger, and hunger must be starved.*

Now she stood on the balcony of the Vharrow garrison-house and watched magic come home like rain.

It came in small ways first. A fishmonger on the Cornmarket had prayed over his dead nets—just muttered it, the way grieving men do—and by dawn the nets were full, so heavy his sons could barely haul them up the stairs. An old woman in Saltrow had asked for one more spring in her knees, and got it, and danced in her doorway until the neighbors came out to watch. A child had wished for snow in the middle of Thawmonth, and gotten exactly one perfect flurry, fat flakes melting on tongues, gone before the hour turned.

Small gods. That was what the people were already calling them, in the markets, in the taverns, in the low-voiced way folk speak when they're afraid to jinx a good thing. Small gods who answered prayers and asked for small things in return: a cup of milk left on a windowsill, a song sung badly and honestly, a lie confessed to a neighbor, a coat given away. Not blood. Never blood. The old gods had wanted cathedrals and obedience and, at the last, everything. These new ones wanted to be *noticed*.

Wren had spent three days writing reports about it, and every report was a lie.

Not by commission—she was too good a hunter for that—but by omission, which was worse. She wrote that the phenomenon remained under observation. She wrote that containment protocols were being assessed. She wrote nothing at all about the mountain, or the light that had poured from its summit for six hours on the night the world changed, visible from the city walls, bright enough that insomniacs swore they could read by it. She wrote nothing about the Undercourt collapsing into silence below Greyhollow. She wrote nothing about Ulyana Kavanagh.

Her quill hovered over that name now, in the margin of her latest dispatch, where she'd written it once and then struck through it so hard the nib had torn the paper.

"Superintendent wants you." Corporal Ashe stood in the doorway of her office, young and earnest and still wearing the soot he hadn't managed to scrub from behind his ears—the riot had been two days ago and half the garrison still smelled of smoke. "About the Kavanagh file."

Wren set down the pen. "Of course he does."

"He's asking whether you can confirm the death."

She looked past Ashe, through the tall window, at the city. Vharrow had torn itself open in the week since the fire guttered and then blazed. The anti-magic law hadn't fallen—it had *evaporated*, like frost in sun. Inspectors who had spent careers breaking charm-sellers' fingers now stood in market squares watching hedge-witches work openly, their warrants fluttering uselessly in their hands like moths. Some had thrown their badges into the river. One had knelt in the mud and asked a passing charm-maker to bless his dying wife, weeping while he begged.

And above it all, the mountain. Still smoking faintly. Still warm. When Wren looked at it too long she felt something in her chest turn over, like a door being opened onto a room she wasn't ready to enter.

"I'll come," she said, and gathered her file.

* * *

Superintendent Hale's office smelled of ink and boiled mutton and fear. He was not a cruel man; Wren had always counted that in his favor. He was simply a man whose entire understanding of the world had been built on a law that no longer existed, and he gripped the arms of his chair the way drowning men grip wreckage.

"The Crown wants certainty," he said. "The Privy Council is drafting new statutes—they're calling it the Covenant, apparently, some arrangement with these... presences. Until it's ratified, the old law stands in theory. Which means the woman who walked into the Undercourt carrying stolen dragon-fire—" he said it carefully, as if the words might bite him, "—is either the greatest criminal in the history of Vharrow or the reason any of us are alive. And you, Hunter Calloway, are the only officer who followed her."

"You have my report," Wren said. "I filed it the morning I came up out of the mines."

"I've read your report." He slid it across the desk. It was thinner than it should have been. "Your report says the subject entered the vault during the collapse. It says you became separated. It says you found no remains." His eyes came up, flat and patient. "No remains, Calloway."

"It was a collapse, sir."

"A collapse that killed hundreds of court-things and buried a mine system older than the city, and yet somehow left no trace of the one woman the Crown most wants accounted for." He folded his hands. "I'm going to ask you plainly, because I think you've earned plainness. Is Ulyana Kavanagh dead?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, in the yard, recruits were drilling, their boots striking cobbles in a rhythm that suddenly sounded like a heartbeat.

Wren thought about lying. She was good at it—hunters had to be; interrogation was mostly theater and patience. She could say yes. She could say *I saw her die*, and the Crown would close the file, and there would be no warrant, no manhunt, no squad of hunters kicking in the door of a rebuilt guildhall on the Cornmarket. Saying yes would be the kindest act available to her.

It would also be a lie told to her oath, and she had spent the whole descent learning what happened to oaths kept carelessly. She had seen the sold souls dreaming in the dark mines. She had seen the soul market, the lanterns, the faces. She had seen what the crown's version of the Godfall cost, generation after generation, paid by girls born into guilds that never knew they were collateral. If her oath meant anything now—if it had ever meant anything—it had to mean she didn't get to choose which truths deserved the law's protection.

"I don't know," Wren said.

Hale's jaw tightened. "You don't know."

"No remains, sir. No witness. The vault burned with fire I don't have words for—I've tried to find them and failed. What I know is that she carried the ember up the mountain and gave it away. What I know is that the starving things stopped waking that night. What I know—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it would be another kind of lie, "—what I know is that if she lived, she isn't hiding. She's working. There's a guildhall reopening on Lantern Row, and anyone with eyes can see the chimney smoke."

Hale stared at her for a long moment. "You're telling me where to find her."

"I'm telling you the truth. It seemed worth trying, given how rarely this office has heard any."

"That insubordination alone ends your career."

"My career ended somewhere around the third mine shaft, sir. I'm just the only one who hasn't filed the paperwork."

Something moved behind Hale's face—not anger. Something wearier. He looked at the file, at the struck-through name, and Wren realized he had read her omissions perfectly well all along. He was not a fool. He was a man hoping someone else would make the decision he couldn't.

"If I send hunters to Lantern Row," he said quietly, "and they bring back a woman who fed the hungry gods instead of hoarding the fire—what do you imagine the city does? The same city that tore the inspection houses apart with their bare hands? You've seen the riots, Calloway. You've seen what happens to anyone in a grey coat now. Do you truly believe I can put her in chains?"

"No," Wren said. "I don't. But I believe you could try, and I believe the trying would kill people. So I'm asking you not to try. I'm asking you to write what I wrote: no remains. Case unresolved. Subject unlocated." She leaned forward. "Or write what you know, Superintendent, and let the Covenant deal with her openly. Either way, stop asking me to do it with a lie. I've carried enough of those down that mountain. I won't carry them back up."

The clock on his mantel ticked. Outside, faintly, someone in the street was singing—one of the new hymns, shapeless and glad, the kind that sprang up overnight now like mushrooms after rain.

Hale closed the file. He pressed both palms flat upon it, as though taking its weight onto himself.

"Unresolved," he said at last. "Unlocated. Pending review under the Covenant." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and human. "Get out of my office, Hunter. And Calloway—if a private citizen should happen to visit a certain guildhall, that is no concern of this department. Provided she stays a private citizen. Provided whatever she's teaching doesn't grow teeth."

"It won't," Wren said. Then, because she owed him honesty even in hope: "But it will grow roots. That's rather the point."

* * *

She didn't go straight there. She made herself walk the long way, through the Cornmarket, because she wanted to see it with a hunter's eyes one last time before she saw it with her own.

The market had never been so full. Charm-sellers worked in the open—no sleeves pulled low, no marks hidden, no sudden silences when grey coats passed. Wren watched a girl of maybe twelve sell luck-knots from a tray, her oathmark bare on her forearm like a birthmark, ordinary as freckles. Nobody flinched. An inspector bought one, for his daughter, and paid double, and the girl thanked him without fear.

At the fountain, a small god had taken up residence. Nobody had summoned it; it had simply arrived, the way cats do. It looked like a shimmer in the water shaped vaguely like a heron, and when people dropped in coins—or crumbs, or buttons, or apologies—it granted tiny things. A lost ring returned. A headache eased. A grudge softened. In exchange it asked each petitioner for one small true thing, spoken aloud: *my mother's soup is better than yours; I envy my brother; I am afraid of the dark.* The fountain rang all day with confessions, and the city, impossibly, seemed lighter for them.

Wren stood at the edge of it for a long time. Her hand drifted to her collar, where beneath her shirt hung the hunter's sigil she had worn since her swearing-in, and she found she was gripping it the way Sorrel's guild had once gripped their secrets—tightly, and to no good purpose.

Then she walked to Lantern Row.

The guildhall looked better than she remembered, which was strange, because memory insisted it had been a ruin. New glass in the windows. The door standing wide—wide, in a city that had locked its doors against magic for eighty years. Above it, freshly painted, letters that made her throat tighten:

**THE GUILD OF SMALL MERCIES — OPEN**

Smoke rose steadily from the chimney. Through the open door she could see the hearth-fire burning, gold and steady, and four figures moving within it—Dagny Holt, arm in a sling, arguing cheerfully with someone about ledgers; Ilse, serene and terrible, instructing two students in the careful grammar of wards; and at the big desk that had clearly once belonged to Sorrel—

Ulyana.

She was thinner. There was a scar along her jaw that Wren didn't remember, and her sleeve was rolled to the elbow, her oathmark bare, and when she laughed at something Dagny said, the sound carried out into the street like the market-bells.

Wren stood in the doorway a moment longer, hunter's habit cataloguing exits, threats, sight-lines—and then, deliberately, one by one, she let all of it go. It felt like setting down a pack she had carried so long she'd forgotten the weight of it.

Ulyana looked up.

For a heartbeat neither of them moved. Then Ulyana set down her pen, and Dagny—without appearing to look at all—began loudly explaining ledger arithmetic to Ilse, steering the students toward the back room with the shameless grace of a smuggler clearing a deck.

"Wren." Ulyana's voice was careful. "They said you filed me dead."

"They said a lot of things." Wren stepped inside. The hearth-warmth hit her face, and beneath it she caught other scents—ink, bread, woodsmoke, the particular clean smell of wards freshly laid. "I reported the case unresolved. Unlocated. Pending review." She paused. "The review may take years. Bureaucracy is the one god that never died."

Something eased in Ulyana's shoulders. "And you? What did you report?"

Wren took off the hunter's sigil from around her neck. She held it a moment—eighty years of law, three generations of Calloways, her own name scratched inside the band—and then she set it down on Sorrel's desk between them, gently, like a charm returned to its maker.

"Nothing," she said. "Today I'm just a private citizen visiting a guildhall. I hear you teach."

"We do." Ulyana's eyes were bright and wet and steady. "First lesson's free."

"What is it?"

Ulyana smiled—the smile from the night market, from before all of it, the one Wren had crossed a city and a hell to find again. "That magic was never the thing to be afraid of. Hoarding was. Come in. Close the door behind you—it keeps the warmth in better that way."

Wren closed the door. Behind her, out in the street, the fountain-god sang its small silver song, and the smoke from the guildhall chimney rose straight and unhidden into the wide morning air.

Inside, Ulyana reached for a fresh sheet of paper, dipped her pen, and wrote at the top of the page, in a clear teacher's hand, the first line of the guild's new ledger:

*Member, fifth: Wren Calloway. Debt: none. Terms: hers to choose.*

"Welcome," she said, "to the Guild of Small Mercies."
