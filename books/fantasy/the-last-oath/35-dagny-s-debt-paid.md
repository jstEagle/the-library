## 35. Dagny's Debt Paid

The fever broke on the ninth day, in a room that smelled of beeswax and river water.

Dagny Holt knew it was broken because she dreamed of nothing at all — no mines, no lanterns wearing faces, no door she had held with her shoulder while something on the other side hammered and hammered like a heart trying to get out of its cage. Just blackness, warm as tar, and when she surfaced from it the ceiling above her was old plaster gone gold in the morning light, crossed by beams where swallows nested every spring.

Her mother's house. Or what remained of it, three miles downriver from Greyhollow, where the mountain's shadow thinned enough to grow barley and keep goats and pretend the world had not nearly ended twice in one lifetime.

She tried to sit up and discovered that her body had opinions about this. The wound ran along her left side from hip to ribs where the Undercourt's collapsing stone had caught her — or rather, where she had caught the falling lintel meant for Ulyana and Ilse and the girl who'd carried the ember up out of the dark. The healers from the village, a pair of sisters who smelled of comfrey and woodsmoke, had packed it with moss and honey and told her she was lucky. She had laughed until the stitches pulled, which taught them both something about luck.

*You're lucky,* they said. *A few inches deeper.*

A few inches deeper and she'd have missed all of this: the swallows, the light, the smell of bread rising somewhere below, the sound of the river going about its business without any regard for gods or guilds or ledgers full of names.

She lay there a while longer anyway, cataloguing what still worked. Fingers — yes, all ten, though the left hand ached deep in the bones the way it always would now, she suspected, the way a barometer aches before weather. Legs — yes, when she tested them under the blanket. The rest of her — present, accounted for, complaining.

Outside, someone was singing. A pilgrim's song, one of the new ones, the kind that hadn't existed a year ago. It went up the river valley thin and clear, and Dagny found herself listening to the words, which were simple almost past bearing:

*Small god of the ford, I bring you bread,*
*I ask you only for a safe road ahead.*

That was the whole verse. That was most of the song. The small gods, it turned out, liked things plain.

She swung her legs over the side of the bed, hissed, and stood anyway.

* * *

The ledger lived in the bottom of her sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, because a smuggler learned early that paper rotted faster than reputations. She'd kept it twenty-three years. Every run, every toll, every name that had paid her to move what shouldn't be moved — fugitive mages before the Godfall's laws loosened, then fugitives of every other kind after; contraband charms, unlicensed relics, once an entire altar stolen piecemeal from a shrine in the Teeth and sold to a collector in Vharrow who'd wanted to own something holy and hadn't cared whose.

Every name. Every debt owed to her, and every debt she owed to others, written in her own cramped hand, in ink she'd mixed herself from soot and tallow so it couldn't be traced.

She carried the chest out to the yard on legs that shook and set it down beside the fire pit, where her mother used to burn the year's stubble, and she sat on the bench with her wound throbbing in time with her heartbeat, and she looked at the chest for a long time.

The river was loud that morning. Snowmelt coming down off Greyhollow's shoulders, brown and muscular, carrying sticks and last autumn's leaves and, if you watched long enough, things less explicable — a wooden bird with gilded wings turning in an eddy, a child's shoe, a ribbon of pale silk that snagged on the willow roots and stayed there, streaming, like something waving goodbye.

Pilgrims walked the towpath in twos and threes. They'd been walking all spring, ever since the Guttering, ever since the sky above the mountaintop had filled with light like the inside of a lamp, and the starving god-fragments had come down from their cold perches and been fed. They walked toward Vharrow mostly, toward the reopened guildhall, toward the shrines that had warmed overnight like stones held in cupped hands. Some of them stopped at the ford below her mother's field and left offerings — bread, buttons, a comb, once a whole roasted goose that the foxes appreciated more than any god did.

Dagny had watched them for nine days through the window, too weak to do anything but watch, and had felt something turn over in her chest each time, slow and heavy, like a boat coming about.

Because here was the thing she knew, the thing the fever had burned down to its simplest shape while she lay sweating and dreaming of doors: she knew these roads. Every crossing, every ford, every smuggler's stair cut into the riverbank, every innkeeper between here and the coast who could be trusted and the seventeen who couldn't. Twenty-three years of moving people quietly through a country built on fear. And now the country wasn't afraid anymore, and the people were walking openly, singing, carrying their bread offerings in their open hands — and they were still getting lost. Still drowning at bad fords. Still robbed on the high passes by men who hadn't heard yet that the world had changed, or who had heard and didn't care.

Nobody knew those roads like she did. Nobody alive.

She opened the chest. Lifted out the ledger. Its oilcloth wrapping came away with a sound like a sigh.

It was a thick book, bound in grey hide — goat, she thought, though she'd never asked — with brass corners gone green. Her name wasn't in it anywhere, because you didn't put your own name in a thing like this. But her hand was on every page. Twenty-three years of her life, weighed and priced.

She read it for the last time, standing there in the yard with the wind pulling at her hair. Names of clients, some dead, some pardoned, some — she paused at one entry, *M. Calloway, passage for two, Vharrow to Greyhollow, paid in silence* — some complicated. Sums outstanding: eleven debts still owed her, totaling less than she'd spent on ale last winter. Sums she owed: none written down, because those she'd kept in her head, and her head had gotten very crowded since Greyhollow.

There was a debt she owed to a woman named Ulyana Kavanagh, who had paid her with a charm that shouldn't have worked and did. There was a debt she owed to a hunter named Wren Calloway, who had arrested her and then, when the mountain came down, had come back for her — had dug through rubble with bleeding hands until the sisters from the village took over. There was a debt she owed to an archivist who no longer remembered why Dagny flinched whenever anyone mentioned the Undercourt's heralds, and a debt to a dead guildmaster she'd never met, whose bargain had bought the world another century and cost everyone everything.

And underneath all of those, older than any of them, a debt to a girl of nineteen who had drowned at the Widow's Ford because Dagny Holt had taken a cargo of salted eels across that river in flood season rather than wait a day, and the wake of her overloaded boat had swept the girl off the stepping stones, and Dagny had stood on the far bank and watched her go under, and had told herself for twenty years that there had been nothing she could have done.

The fever had settled that question. In the dreams, there had always been something she could have done. In the dreams she had waded in. In the dreams she had reached her. The dreams were right. She had known it even then, standing dry-shod on the bank, doing the arithmetic that smugglers do — cargo against stranger, risk against certainty — and choosing wrong, choosing the arithmetic, choosing the part of herself that kept ledgers.

The girl's name had been Petra Voss. It was in the ledger, actually, in a way — under the entry for that crossing, *eels, 40 lb, ford passable, margin thin.* No mention of the girl. Ledgers didn't record what mattered. That was the whole trouble with them.

Dagny crouched, hissing at the pull of the stitches, and laid the book in the fire pit. Then she hesitated, because twenty-three years is twenty-three years, and her hands — clever hands, hands that could tie a bowline in the dark and pick a customs seal in under a minute — hovered over the grey hide and did not want.

The wind came down off the mountain and stirred the pages. Somewhere on the towpath the pilgrims were singing again, the same plain little song, and the words drifted across the barley like seed:

*Small god of the ford, I bring you bread,*
*I ask you only for a safe road ahead.*

"All right," Dagny said aloud, to nobody, to everybody, to the girl under the water. "All right."

She struck the flint three times before it caught — her hands weren't what they were, not yet — and the dry stubble in the pit took the flame hungrily, and the ledger caught with it, and she stood there while twenty-three years curled and blackened and lifted away on the wind in flakes of ash that rose higher than the chimney smoke, higher than the willows, toward the white shoulders of Greyhollow where the light still lingered some mornings, faint as breath on glass.

When it was done she raked the ashes flat and sat back down on the bench and discovered she was crying, which annoyed her considerably, and let it happen anyway.

* * *

The boat was her mother's, technically. A broad-beamed river barge called the *Patience*, thirty feet of grey oak that had hauled barley and wool and, during the worst years, things Dagny's mother had chosen not to ask about. It had been sitting under a tarp behind the house for six years, taking rain, growing a respectable crop of moss along its gunwales.

It took her three weeks to make her seaworthy again — river-worthy, rather, though the *Patience* had been down to the delta once, in her mother's day, and could be again. Dagny worked slowly, because her body insisted, and carefully, because her hands needed something exact to do while the rest of her healed. She scraped the hull and paid it with pine pitch. She recaulked every seam. She replaced four planks with oak bought honestly, at market price, from a timber-man who seemed personally offended by the transaction. She carved the moss off the rails and, on an impulse she didn't examine, left a small offering at the waterline before the relaunch — a heel of bread and a copper coin — because the times had changed and it cost nothing to be polite.

The sisters from the village came down to watch her launch. So did half the hamlet, because a spectacle was a spectacle, and a Holt putting a boat on the river after six years was that.

"Where are you bound, then?" asked the elder sister, Marta, squinting against the light off the water. "Back to your old trade?"

The younger sister elbowed her. Dagny just smiled, and it was strange how easily it came, sitting there at the tiller of a clean boat on a bright morning.

"Ferry," she said. "Passengers. Pilgrims, mainly. Anyone who needs to get somewhere and doesn't know the way."

"Is there money in that?"

"Some." She'd done the sums, of course — she'd always do the sums, that part of her wasn't going anywhere, it would just have better work to do now. "Enough. And there's this." She tapped her temple. "I know every ford on this river and every fool who'll try to cross one in flood. Seems wasteful, letting all that go to waste."

"And the other roads?" Marta pressed, because village women got to press. "The mountain passes? The routes south?"

"In good time. One river first."

They helped her push off, a dozen willing hands, and the *Patience* slid out into the current with barely a shiver, and Dagny stood at the tiller and felt the river take hold of the hull the way it always had — like an old dog recognizing an old friend — and something in her chest that had been clenched since Greyhollow finally, fully, let go.

Her first fare was a widow, naturally. They were everywhere that spring, widows and widowers and the otherwise bereaved, walking the roads with their bundles and their grief worn smooth as river stones. This one was maybe sixty, dressed in good black gone grey at the seams, and she wanted passage down to Vharrow to see the new guildhall with her own eyes before she died, and she counted out her coins one at a time, apologizing for each.

Dagny took exactly half and pushed the rest back across the thwart.

"Half fare for mourners," she said. "House rule."

"I'm not—" the woman began, and then her face did the thing faces did, and she closed her mouth, and nodded, and put the coins away.

They went downriver together through the morning, past the ford where the offerings piled up, past the drowned meadows loud with birdsong, and the widow talked, the way people talked when they'd been alone with a grief too long, and Dagny steered and listened and said little, which was a skill she'd never valued before and valued now beyond price. At the bend below the ruined watchtower, the widow fell quiet, watching the water.

"My son," she said, eventually. "He was taken by the answered prayers. Last winter. Before the mountain." Her hands knotted in her lap. "He came home hollow, and then he went out one night toward the shrines, and we never found him. I prayed, after — I prayed to the small god of lost things, the way they say you can now, safely, the way they teach you at the guildhall. Do you know what it asked me?"

"No."

"A button." The widow laughed, wet and wondering. "One button off my coat. That was all. And three days later a fisherman downstream pulled my boy's body from an eddy, and I buried him properly, and I could grieve. Cleanly. Do you understand? The god gave me back my grieving." She looked at Dagny with eyes red-rimmed and calm. "People say the miracles are small now. Petty. A button, a loaf, a safe road. But I tell you, I've seen the other kind. Small is a mercy."

Dagny thought of a lantern in a vault beneath the mountain, wearing a face she knew. Thought of a market of traded selves, rows and rows of them, dreaming in the dark. Thought of the hammering on the door, and her shoulder braced against it, and how long she'd held.

"Yes," she said. "Small is a mercy."

By midsummer the *Patience* was known on forty miles of river. Dagny kept no ledger. She kept the fares in a tin box and the names nowhere at all, and it astonished her how quickly she stopped missing them — the lists, the tallies, the dense gray comfort of accounts. In their place she accumulated a different kind of record: the widow ferried free in flood season because she recognized the look of her; the young mason traveling to the guildhall to apprentice, who paid with a carved wooden bird for the *Patience*'s prow; the priestess of the ford-god, young and earnest, whom Dagny taught the location of every dangerous eddy between the bridge and the delta in exchange for nothing but company, and who left, at summer's end, a small woven charm on the tiller that was supposed to keep boats true.

It shouldn't have worked. It was made of rushes by an amateur. Dagny hung it there anyway, and the *Patience* never shipped so much as a drop where it wasn't supposed to, and she decided — deliberately, the way you decide to believe a friend — that this was craft, not magic, hers and the river's, and left it at that.

Some evenings, moored in some quiet reach with her supper cooling, she'd take out a scrap of paper and write letters she rarely sent. To Wren, in Vharrow: *Still alive. River's high. Ask me nothing.* To Ulyana at the guildhall: *Ferrying your pilgrims now. Half fare for mourners. You started this.* Once, hesitantly, to Ilse — a note with no news in it, just *the river is green this year, thought you'd want to know* — and no answer ever came, and Dagny understood why without being told, and kept writing anyway, once a season, small notes, because some debts you pay knowing they can't be acknowledged, and those are the cleanest ones.

The scar ached when the weather changed. Her left hand ached in its bones. She woke some nights certain she could hear hammering, and would lie listening to the river instead, wave after patient wave against the hull, until the sound became just water again.

And on the first cold morning of autumn, poling the *Patience* through the mist below the Widow's Ford — she still wouldn't take passengers through there in fog, some crossings deserved respect — Dagny saw a figure waiting on the near bank. A traveler in a hooded cloak, travel-worn, alone, with a bundle at her feet and her hands folded in front of her like a woman who had been standing there a long time, deciding.

Dagny brought the boat alongside, easy against the current, and looked up at the hooded figure, and the mist thinned just enough.

"You're a long way from the sea, sister," Dagny said. "Last I heard, you were keeping a chapel in the hill country."

Ilse pushed back her hood. Her face was thinner than Dagny remembered, and there was something in her eyes that hadn't been there before — or rather, something that had been there for decades and had only now come back: recognition. Recognition, and underneath it, dread.

"I remember," Ilse said. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not. "Dagny. I remember everything."
