## 36. Ilse Remembers Everything

The memories came back the way floodwater comes back to a dry riverbed—not all at once, but in a rising that could not be argued with.

Ilse felt it first in her hands. She was kneeling in the guildhall's east chapel—or what had been a chapel, before the Godfall, before the law, before she had traded away everything she knew—scouring soot from flagstones with a brush of horsehair and lye. The court beneath the mountain had broken three days past; its collapse had rolled through Greyhollow like a bell struck too hard, and every soul it had held in pawn had woken weeping. And with the waking had come the returning. All over the city, people were remembering things they had sold: first kisses, mothers' voices, the taste of a specific apple on a specific afternoon. The Undercourt had kept its inventory well, and now the inventory was spilling out into the streets like grain from a torn sack.

Ilse had told herself she would remember nothing. She had paid her memories gladly—or as gladly as one pays anything to that place—and she had thought the emptiness where they had been was a kind of mercy. For six days since the collapse she had been a stranger among strangers who treated her with a tenderness she did not understand. The tall smuggler with the burned hands brought her tea without being asked. The hunter woman with the crown's brand still scarred into her wrist sat near her at meals and said nothing, watchful, like a dog deciding whether an unfamiliar animal was friend or threat. And Ulyana—the youngest member, the nameless one, marked now with the guild's full oath burning silver along both forearms—Ulyana looked at Ilse the way one looks at a house where something terrible once happened: carefully, kindly, and without entering.

Ilse had not known why. She had not known anything. It had been almost restful, this ignorance, like lying in snow.

Then the flood came.

It began with the smell of the lye. Lye and tallow—the smell of the archive, of the long tables where she had spent forty years copying ledgers by lamplight, keeping accounts no one was meant to read. Her hands stopped moving. Her breath went strange and shallow. And then the river broke its banks and everything she had sold came home at once.

She remembered the guildhall when it was whole. She remembered Sorrel young, with ink on his knuckles and a laugh that made the rafters dust themselves. She remembered the night of the Godfall—the screaming sky, the gods turning ravenous as their worship thinned, the city on its knees—and she remembered the five who had gone down beneath the mountain with the first guildmaster to buy the silence. She remembered writing the terms in her own careful hand. *Five souls pledged against the peace of Vharrow. Four to be reclaimed. One held in perpetuity.*

One held in perpetuity.

She remembered the child. A girl born in the guildhall's north room in a winter of ash-fall, squalling and red and utterly ordinary, and how the first guildmaster had lifted her toward the light and said, *This one will do.* Ilse had written the entry. She had sealed it with wax from the archive candle. She had watched the girl grow up selling luck-knots in the night market and had never once, in all those years, walked across the street to speak to her.

And then the other memories arrived, the ones she had buried deepest because they were the ones she least deserved to keep:

The letters. Forty years of letters to the Undercourt, reporting the guild's numbers, its weaknesses, its dwindling faithful. Reporting Sorrel's failing heart. Reporting which members still believed and which had begun to doubt. She had told herself it was stewardship—that someone had to keep the court informed or the court would come down itself and take what it wanted without terms. She had told herself that right up until the night she had written the words *the fire gutters sooner than they know* and sent the letter by way of a mine-shaft courier, and four days later Ashwin had been found burned from the inside out on the guildhall doorstep.

Ashwin. Gentle, half-deaf old Ashwin, who used to hum while he swept. The mountain had collected him early because of her letter, because the regent had read *sooner than they know* and decided not to wait for the natural order of things.

And Ilse remembered—this last, worst thing arriving with the taste of copper—the day she had gone to the court alone, while Ulyana slept in Greyhollow, and negotiated. Not for the guild. Not even for herself. She had traded her archives—forty years of the guild's collected knowledge, its histories, its charms, its true purpose—to buy Ulyana a clear road down. And she had done it knowing, knowing, that Ulyana would never learn the price, because Ilse had arranged that too. *Let her think I did nothing,* she had asked the herald. *Let her owe me nothing. That is part of the payment.* Vanity dressed up as sacrifice, or sacrifice dressed up as vanity—she still could not tell which, and the not-knowing was its own punishment.

The brush fell from her fingers and clattered on the stone.

She did not remember standing. She remembered only that she was suddenly in the courtyard, on her knees in the cold mud where the guildhall's sign had lain face-down for twenty years, and she was making a sound she did not recognize as coming from a human throat until she understood it was her own.

* * *

They found her there. Of course they did. Dagny Holt came first, moving fast despite her bandaged hands, and stopped a few paces off with the particular stillness of a woman who has talked her way past worse things than grief and knows when talking will not serve. Wren Calloway hung back by the doorway, arms crossed, watching Ilse the way she watched everyone now—with the eyes of a hunter relearning what it was to hunt for something other than people.

But it was Ulyana who knelt.

She knelt in the mud without hesitation, without regard for the grey wool of her new guild robes, and took Ilse's face in both hands, and said, "Ilse. Ilse. Look at me. What came back?"

"Everything." The word tore coming out. "Everything, all of it, oh—oh, you were a baby, I wrote your name in the ledger myself, I wrote it, I—" Her voice cracked apart and she pressed her fist against her own mouth to hold the rest in, and failed. "I reported to them. Forty years. Every weakness, every doubt, every secret Sorrel ever confided in me over supper thinking I was safe. Ashwin died because of a sentence I wrote. He hummed while he swept, do you understand, he *hummed*, and I killed him with ink."

Ulyana's hands did not leave her face. They were steady. Everything about Ulyana was steady these days, in the way that a rope is steady under enormous weight.

"Ilse. Breathe."

"I bought your road down with our archives," Ilse said, because if she was going to drown she would drown entirely, she would hold nothing back, not one scrap of herself, not after forty years of holding everything back. "I made the herald promise you'd never know. I didn't want your gratitude. That's the truth of it—I couldn't bear for you to be grateful to me, because gratitude is a debt, and I have spent my whole life inside debts, and I wanted—Gods forgive me, I wanted to feel clean for once. I wanted to buy my way out with a coin no one would count." She laughed, and the laugh was the ugliest sound she had ever made. "And it worked. You thanked the air. You thanked *fortune*. I stood there and let you thank fortune."

Dagny made a small sound, low in her throat. Wren had not moved from the doorway, but her arms had come uncrossed.

"And now I remember it all," Ilse whispered, "and there is nowhere left to put it. There is no archive deep enough. I know every betrayal the way I know my own alphabet, and I am asking—" and here her voice finally, completely broke, and she bowed her head until her forehead nearly touched the mud between Ulyana's knees— "I am asking for judgment. Judge me. Someone has to. The court is broken and no one will ever judge me again unless one of you does it freely, and I cannot carry this unjudged, I would rather burn like Ashwin burned than carry it unjudged."

The courtyard was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city of Vharrow was rebuilding itself around the wild new magic—she could hear hammers, and laughter, and somewhere a street-preacher proclaiming the names of the small new gods—but here there was only wind in the ruined eaves and four women breathing.

Ulyana let go of her face.

For one black moment Ilse thought: *she is going to stand up and walk away, and that will be the judgment, and it will be just.* She braced for it the way one braces for a blow, with a strange relief underneath the fear. At last. At last, consequence.

Instead, Ulyana reached down and took Ilse's hands—both of them, the archivist's hands, the traitor's hands, ink-stained and shaking—and drew them upward until Ilse was forced to lift her head and meet her eyes.

"Do you know what judgment looks like?" Ulyana said. "I saw it. I stood in front of the regent of the Undercourt and I saw what a lifetime of judgment does to a person. She judged herself every hour of every day for two hundred years, and she called it duty, and it ate her hollow from the inside until there was nothing left but the accounting." Her grip tightened. "I will not build this guild on that. I will not build it on penance. If you want to punish yourself, Ilse, you'll have to do it somewhere else, because here—" her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in the steadiness— "here we are trying something different. Here the whole point, the entire point, the thing Sorrel died whispering and the regent never learned in two hundred years of service, is that magic is given, not hoarded. Mercy too. Especially mercy."

"That's not—" Ilse's breath hitched. "That is not justice."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "Justice would have you burned. Justice had Ashwin burned. I have seen enough justice beneath a mountain to last several lifetimes." She released one of Ilse's hands but kept the other, and turned it over, palm-up, the way one examines a hand for an oathmark. "What I'm offering isn't forgiveness yet. Forgiveness has to be earned, and you haven't earned it, and I won't insult either of us by pretending otherwise. What I'm offering is work."

"Work," Ilse repeated, stupidly.

"Apprenticeship." Ulyana said the word plainly, but Ilse heard the iron underneath it, heard the structure being built even as she spoke. "You spent forty years keeping accounts for a court that starved gods. Now you'll spend whatever years you have left keeping accounts for the opposite. The ledger of the new guild opens tomorrow. Every charm given away, every prayer taught safely, every debt forgiven—it all gets written down. And you're the only person alive who knows how to keep a ledger honest, because you're the only person alive who knows exactly what a dishonest one costs." She paused. "You'll write it all in your own hand. Including the parts about yourself. The Ashwin entry goes in first."

Ilse stared at her. Mud soaked through her skirts. Her chest hurt with something that was not quite grief and not quite hope and occupied, precisely, the territory between them.

"You would put a traitor behind the guild's books."

"I would put the person who knows the price of every lie in charge of recording every truth." Ulyana's mouth curved, and for a moment Ilse saw the girl from the night market, the charm-seller, the widow's comforter—the ordinary extraordinary soul she had helped pledge before she could walk. "Besides. You gave up your archives. You don't get to claim you're useless. You're the most educated novice this hall has ever taken."

From the doorway, Wren snorted—a single, involuntary bark of a laugh, quickly suppressed. Dagny did not bother suppressing hers. "Gods help us," the smuggler said, flexing her bandaged fingers. "An archivist-novice. She'll have the broom closet catalogued by week's end."

"She'll have the *whole hall* catalogued by week's end," Ulyana said, "and I will love her for it." Then her expression sobered, and she turned back to Ilse, and her voice dropped into something quieter and more dangerous. "But hear the terms clearly, because I mean them. You don't get to decide anymore what other people should or shouldn't know. No more merciful omissions. No more arranging outcomes from the shadows. Whatever you learn, whatever you suspect, whatever shame rises in you at three in the morning—you bring it to this courtyard and you say it out loud. That's the oath. Not obedience. *Disclosure.* Can you swear that? Because if you can't, the door stays open and you go find your judgment somewhere else, and I'll grieve you, but I'll let you go."

Ilse looked at the offered hand. She looked at the mud, at the fallen sign, at the ruined hall rising around them with its windows like empty eye sockets that were somehow, today, beginning to look more like open doors.

Forty years she had managed truth like a miser manages coin—doling out the smallest possible amounts, hoarding the rest, telling herself scarcity was safety. She had watched what that scarcity did to a city, to a world, to gods. Scarcity of truth was the same sickness as scarcity of fire. She understood that now, with the terrible clarity of the fully flooded.

"I can swear it," she said. Her voice came out rough but level. "Disclosure. All of it. Starting now, then, since apparently we begin immediately." She wiped her face with her free hand, took a breath that shuddered going in and came out steadier. "There's something you need to know. Something I remembered ten minutes ago, in the middle of the rest of it, and it's the reason I came out here instead of staying on my knees in the chapel."

Ulyana waited. Even Dagny and Wren drifted closer, pulled by the gravity in Ilse's voice.

"The ledger," Ilse said. "Sorrel's debt-book, the one you found in the vault. You read the entries—five souls, four reclaimed, yours outstanding. But you read it in the court's copy. The regent showed you what the regent wanted shown." She gripped Ulyana's hand harder. "I kept the original. My own hand, the true terms, sealed the night of the Godfall. And the true terms contain a clause the regent removed from every copy but mine—a clause about what happens to the guild's oathmark when the final debt is discharged. It doesn't simply fade, Ulyana. It *transfers*. To whoever holds the ledger. The original ledger." She met Ulyana's eyes, and saw the silver oathmarks flicker along the younger woman's arms as if the marks themselves had heard. "Which means the question of who carries the guild's last living oath—who bears it, and what bearing it will cost—is not settled. It was never settled. And I have been sitting on that answer for forty years, and I am telling you now, out loud, in the courtyard, as my first act under the new oath."

The wind moved through the ruined eaves. Somewhere beyond the wall, a hammer rang against stone, patient and rhythmic, the sound of a world rebuilding itself one strike at a time.

Ulyana looked down at her own marked arms for a long moment. When she raised her head, her expression was unreadable—but her hand did not let go of Ilse's.

"Then tomorrow," she said quietly, "we open the ledger together."
