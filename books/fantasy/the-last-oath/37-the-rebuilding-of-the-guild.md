## 37. The Rebuilding of the Guild

The guildhall smelled of ash and new paint, which was a strange combination—grief and hope sharing the same air. Ulyana stood in the doorway on the first morning of spring and let herself feel both.

The fire had taken the east wing before it was stopped. That had been Wren's doing, in a way—the inspectors who sealed the hall after Ash's death had left lamps burning, and the old timbers had caught with an eagerness that felt personal. But the stone bones remained, and Dagny had brought masons from Greyhollow, men and women who owed her favors and worked cheap and asked no questions about why a smuggler wanted a guildhall rebuilt in a city where magic was still, officially, against the law.

That law had three weeks left to live, maybe four. The crown was drafting its surrender in careful language: *the Covenant of Small Gods*, they were calling it, as if naming it politely would make the wildness of it tame. Prayers answered by small gods who asked small things—a copper for the shrine, a kindness to a stranger, a memory offered freely at the well of Saint Vessa. Ulyana had watched a baker pray for rain on his wheat and pay for it by carrying water to his neighbor's widow for a month, whistling the whole time. It worked. It *worked*, and no one burned from the inside, and no one woke hollow-eyed, because the small gods took only what was given gladly.

She had taught the widow Maren that, a year ago now, without knowing what she was teaching. She thought of it sometimes—that luck-knot sold in the night market, the prayer that should have gone unanswered—and wondered if the whole covenant had begun there, in that small transaction between two women who had nothing left to lose.

*No,* she corrected herself, stepping inside. *It began with Sorrel.*

* * *

They had voted on everything, the four of them, because that was how the new covenant worked—nothing hoarded, nothing hidden, every decision made in the open where any member could challenge it. Ilse had insisted on the votes. "I spent decades making decisions alone," she'd said, her hands steady but her eyes not quite meeting anyone's. "Alone is how betrayal starts." So they voted.

The vote to reopen had been unanimous. The vote to teach openly—no more charms sold in shadowed corners of night markets, no more oathmarks buried under sleeves—had been three to one, with Ulyana voting against.

"You're outvoted," Dagny had said, grinning around the stem of her pipe. "Democracy's a cruel mistress."

"I'm not outvoted," Ulyana had said. "I'm terrified. There's a difference."

But she'd lost, and she was glad to have lost, and this morning she walked through the rebuilt hall and touched the new timber and felt the terror settling into something steadier.

Ilse was already there, of course. She lived in the archive now—or rather, the archive lived in her, since half her memories had returned when the Undercourt broke and the other half came back slowly, like tide filling channels. Some days she remembered things Ulyana had never known about the guild's history; some days she forgot where she'd put her spectacles. She had hung a slate by her desk where she wrote down each recovered memory as it surfaced, dated and signed, so that the record could be trusted even when she couldn't trust herself.

"Look at this," she said when Ulyana passed the archive door. She held up the ledger—the old one, Sorrel's debt-book, its pages scorched at the edges. But someone—Ilse herself, three weeks ago, according to the slate—had written across the final page in careful script: **PAID IN FULL.** Below it, in fresher ink: **New accounts opened: four.**

"Four," Ulyana said. "You counted me twice?"

"I counted the guild twice," Ilse said. "Once as it was. Once as it will be." She set the ledger down gently, the way you set down something that might still be alive. "Sorrel used to say a ledger isn't a record of debts. It's a record of *trust*. Every entry means somebody believed we'd honor it."

Ulyana looked at the words a long moment. Then she went to find Dagny, because if she stood in the archive feeling things much longer she'd be late for her own humiliation.

* * *

The humiliation had a schedule. Nine o'clock, main hall, first lesson of the reopened Guild of Small Mercies. Open doors—literally open; Dagny had propped them with iron wedges and refused to close them—"so people can see there's nothing to fear, and leave whenever they want, which is also nothing to fear."

Eleven students had come.

Ulyana had expected six. Maybe eight. She had prepared for six, standing in Sorrel's old study the night before, writing lesson plans on paper she kept crumpling and smoothing. What do you teach people whose grandparents were taught to fear the very thing you're offering? How do you begin?

In the end she began the way Sorrel had begun with her, forty years of guild history compressed into a single sentence spoken to a frightened child hiding a mark beneath her sleeve:

*"Magic isn't power. It's attention. Everything else is technique."*

She stood before the eleven—two widows, a ferryman's son, a girl with burn-scarred hands who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, a former inspector who had resigned rather than enforce the old warrants, and five others whose names she was still learning—and she said it aloud, and watched it land differently on different faces.

"My guildmaster told me that when I was nine years old," she said. "I didn't believe her. I thought magic was power, because power was the only thing I lacked, and I wanted it the way starving people want bread." She held up her wrist, bare now, the oathmark visible in the morning light—an ember-colored ring of interlocking knots that no longer needed hiding. "This mark meant I belonged to something. For most of my life, belonging meant hiding. Today it means this room, these doors being open, and all of you."

The girl with the scarred hands raised her own arm without a word. Beneath the burn tissue ran a birthmark shaped like a river delta.

"It answers," the girl said quietly. "It's been answering since the Godfall broke. I didn't know what to do with it. I was afraid I'd—" She stopped.

"Afraid you'd become one of the hungry ones," Ulyana finished. "The god-fragments that woke ravenous?" A murmur went around the room. Everyone knew about those—the ones the ember hadn't reached, the starved remnants that had turned predatory before Ulyana climbed the mountain and shared the fire instead of spending it. "That's the first lesson, then. Not technique. Hunger management."

Dagny, leaning in the doorway with her arms crossed, snorted softly. Ulyana shot her a look that said *you're enjoying this too much*, and Dagny's grin said *yes, obviously*.

"The old law said magic corrupts," Ulyana went on. "And under the old law, it did—not because magic is corrupt, but because secrecy is. When you hide your hunger, it eats you from the inside. When you hide your gift, it curdles into something desperate. Every mage the crown ever burned started as a person who was alone with something too big for them." She let that sit. "So here's what we do differently. You practice in pairs. Always. You tell your partner what you want before you work, and what you're afraid of. And when the hunger comes—and it will come—we name it out loud, in this room, together, and we feed each other instead."

The ferryman's son frowned. "Feed each other?"

"Attention," Ulyana said. "Remember? Magic is attention. Loneliness is starvation. We don't let anyone in this guild starve." She thought of Sorrel, dying in a chair by a fire that never went out, whispering about a debt, keeping secrets until the secrets killed everyone she loved. *We're done with that.* "My predecessor believed protection meant concealment. I loved her, and she was wrong, and the proof of how wrong is written in a ledger downstairs. Our covenant is the opposite: we exist to give magic away. Freely, openly, and in company."

The former inspector raised a hand. His name was Aldric, and he still wore his hair cropped regulation-short out of habit. "And when the crown changes its mind? Laws reverse. I've seen it happen. What happens to all of you when some future regent decides the Godfall was right?"

It was the question Ulyana had been asking herself since the day the covenant passed. She gave him the only honest answer she had.

"Then we'll have been practicing openness for years while they were practicing enforcement, and we'll know each other, and trust each other, and there will be too many of us to seal in one building." She smiled, thin and real. "Also, we keep the back exit clear. Sorrel wasn't wrong about everything."

Laughter moved through the room like warmth through cold hands, and Ulyana felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the night market, since the widow, since the beginning.

* * *

At midday she went upstairs to Sorrel's old study—which was now her study, though she hadn't been able to make herself move the chair.

The chair sat behind the desk the way it always had, oak and leather, worn smooth at the arms where Sorrel's hands had rested for fifty years. Ulyana had dusted it. Had oiled the leather once, carefully, in winter. Had never sat in it.

There was a superstition in it, and she knew it. Chairs weren't thrones. Dead guildmasters didn't haunt furniture. But sitting in it felt like claiming something she hadn't earned, and everything she *had* earned—the ember shared, the court broken, the covenant written—still felt like it belonged to other people: to Dagny's blood on the stairs of the Undercourt, to Ilse's surrendered memories, to Wren's report filed with a lie in it, to Sorrel's last breath.

Today, though, the vote had settled it. Three to one. *The guildmaster takes the guildmaster's chair,* Dagny had argued, "or else why are we rebuilding this place? So it can be a museum with a haunted seat?"

Ulyana stood in the doorway a long time. Sunlight fell across the desk in a long warm bar. Downstairs, faintly, she could hear the eleven students laughing about something—Aldric's dry voice rising above the rest, the girl with the river-delta birthmark actually giggling—and the sound filled the old hall the way the fire once filled the grate: steadily, without asking permission.

"You'd hate this," she said to the empty room, meaning Sorrel. "Open doors. Public lessons. Voting." She crossed to the desk. "And you'd have been wrong to hate it, and you'd have known it eventually, because you were wrong about the important things and you knew that too, at the end. You tried to tell me. In the chair, at the fire. *We sold a soul.* You tried to confess and I wouldn't hear it, and then you died before I could ask you the questions that mattered."

She put her hand on the chair's arm. Smooth wood, warm from the sun.

"But here's what you got right," she said. "You got *attention*. You paid attention to a frightened child in a night market when no one else did. You fed me when I was starving. Everything broken in our ledger started with love that hid itself, and everything worth saving started with love that didn't." Her throat tightened. "So I'm going to sit in your chair, and I'm going to do it badly for a while, and I'm going to keep the doors open even when it terrifies me. And someday I'll die in this room, probably mid-argument with Dagny, and someone else will stand where I'm standing and be just as afraid. That's the inheritance. Not the fire. Not the name. This."

She sat down.

The chair received her without ceremony, as chairs do, and that was somehow the point. No thunder. No visions. Just oak and leather and sunlight and the sound of students below, and the ember-marked knotwork on her wrist catching the light, and somewhere in the walls the old fire—relit now in the main hearth, fed by the covenant, burning steady—making its small patient noise.

She opened the ledger to the fresh page. Four names. She added hers last, in Sorrel's handwriting-style slant that she'd inherited without meaning to:

*Ulyana Kavanagh, guildmaster. Debts: none. Trusts: many.*

Then, after a moment, she crossed out the surname. Not yet—next week, the ceremony, the formal surrender to the ledger—but she wanted the page to start telling the truth now. Above the struck-through name she wrote simply:

*Ulyana. Youngest member.*

Below it, in smaller script: *The chair is not mine. It is the guild's, and I am borrowing it, and so will you be, whoever you are. Welcome.*

* * *

Wren came at dusk.

Ulyana heard the knock—three measured raps, the cadence of a warrant officer even now, even retired from warrants—and found her friend standing on the step with a wrapped bundle and the particular expression of someone delivering news they've rehearsed and failed to soften.

"They ratified it today," Wren said, stepping in. "Full council. The Covenant of Small Gods is law. Magic instruction requires licensing, and—" she held up the bundle, "—guess who's licensed."

Inside the wrapping: a document, heavy paper, wax seals, and Ulyana Kavanagh's name printed in a government clerk's careful hand. *Licensed Instructor, First Class. Guild of Small Mercies, Vharrow.*

"They spelled it right," Ulyana said, absurdly moved.

"Took three tries. The clerk wanted to write 'Kavanagh' with a second 'n.'" Wren looked around the hall—at the open doors, the students' abandoned teacups, the hearth-fire burning steady and bright—and something in her face eased that Ulyana had waited a year to see ease. "You know I wrote them a letter recommending licensure. Official capacity. They'll have it in the file."

"I know."

"And I know what you're going to say, so don't say it. Yes, the report stands. Ulyana Kavanagh died in the Undercourt collapse, mourned by none, case closed. As far as the crown is concerned, this guildhall is run by an unnamed woman of no recorded lineage." Wren's mouth twitched. "Which, incidentally, makes you the least prosecutable person in Vharrow. Legally, you don't exist. I checked. It's beautiful."

They stood a moment in the doorway, two women who had spent their whole friendship learning how to stand in doorways together—on opposite thresholds, then the same one.

"There's one more thing," Wren said, and her voice changed, and Ulyana's chest went tight with old reflexes. "Not bad news. Strange news. A pilgrim came through the checkpoint at Greyhollow yesterday. Heading south. Alone. Carrying a lantern."

Ulyana went very still.

"Not a soul-lantern," Wren said quickly. "Those are all accounted for—I made sure. This one's ordinary glass, brass fittings. But the pilgrim told the guards she was carrying a flame to a shrine that doesn't exist anymore, on a road where the shrines all went cold years ago. And when they asked her name, she said—" Wren hesitated. "She said, *I'm bringing it back to where it was borrowed.*"

Outside, the evening bell rang from the temple district, and the hearth-fire flickered once, as if listening.

Ulyana looked at the lantern-light dancing on the wall, and thought of the ember she had shared on the mountaintop, and of all the small gods now waking gentle across the land—and of one flame, maybe, that remembered being carried somewhere once, long ago, by a guild that no longer existed.

"Did the guards stop her?" she asked.

"No. She showed them papers signed by the regent's own office." Wren met her eyes. "Sealed the year the Godfall ended. Fifty years ago."

The fire in the hearth leaned toward the door, the way a dog leans toward a familiar footstep, and Ulyana understood that whatever came next was already walking toward the guildhall, patient as flame.

"Get Dagny," she said. "Wake Ilse. And leave the doors open."
