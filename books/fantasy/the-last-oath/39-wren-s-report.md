## 39. Wren's Report

The ink was still wet when Wren Calloway understood what she had done.

She sat in the requisitioned office above the Greyhollow garrison—a room that smelled of cold ash and other people's pipe smoke, its single window looking out on the mountain that had nearly swallowed her—and she read her own handwriting as though it belonged to a stranger.

*Report of Magistrate-Hunter Wren Calloway, Third Circle, concerning the collapse of the Undercourt complex beneath Mount Vharrow. Subject: Ulyana Kavanagh, unlicensed mage, wanted under Crown Decree 114 for sedition, unlawful thaumaturgy, and conspiracy against the realm.*

*Subject is deceased.*

She wrote it plainly, without flourish, the way she'd been taught at the Academy: state the facts, sign your name, let the law do the rest. *Deceased.* Two syllables, four letters of consequence. She had written the word before—dozens of times, in dozens of reports, about smugglers and hedge-witches and one poor mad priest who had walked into the fire-vault singing. It had never cost her anything to write it.

This time the pen had felt like a bone saw.

*Subject perished in the structural failure of the lower galleries on the night of the Guttering,* she continued. *Body not recovered. Given the nature of the collapse—total, and attended by phenomena consistent with the release of concentrated dragon-fire—recovery is deemed impossible. Recommend subject be struck from the active rolls and the warrant retired with honors.*

Honors. That was a kindness she hadn't planned, and it surprised her even as she wrote it. But it mattered, she told herself. If Ulyana Kavanagh died a hunted criminal, then somewhere in the archives her name would sit forever beside words like *sedition* and *conspiracy*, and children studying the law would learn her only as a warning. But if she died honored—if the Crown itself recorded that she had gone down into the dark carrying the ember, that whatever she had done beneath the mountain had ended the waking of the starving gods—then the record would have to bend around her like water around a stone.

Wren knew what the record did not say. The record did not say that the gods had not died in the Godfall; they had been starved, deliberately, by a bargain struck to buy Vharrow's peace. The record did not say that every answered prayer since the Godfall had been a debt called in by things grown ravenous in their long hunger. The record did not say that the Crown's own founding law—the law Wren had sworn her life to—was built over a lie the size of a mountain.

The record said what the record needed to say. And so, for once, did Wren.

She dipped the pen again. Her hand was steady. That steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have.

*Addendum:* she wrote, *the undersigned acknowledges that she entered the Undercourt without authorization, in violation of orders, and that she witnessed events below which cannot be reconciled with the official history of the Godfall. The undersigned requests that this addendum be sealed for fifty years, to be opened only by an officer of the Fourth Circle or higher, or upon the formal repeal of Decree 114.*

There. Not a confession—she wasn't ready to hand the truth to men like High Inquisitor Vael, who would use it as kindling. But a seed, planted in the archive, waiting. Fifty years was nothing to an institution like the Crown. Someday someone would open that seal, read her account of the soul market, of the lanterns full of faces, of the regent who had been the first guildmaster all along—and someone would begin the slow work of telling the truth properly, with documents and precedent, which was the only way truth survived in Vharrow.

Wren signed the report. *Wren Calloway.* Her name looked smaller than usual on the page, as if some part of it had stayed behind in the dark.

She blotted it, folded it, sealed it with the grey wax of the Hunter's Office, and set it in the dispatch tray with the rest of the morning's correspondence. Then she sat very still for a long moment, listening to the mountain.

It had been quiet since the Guttering. Truly quiet—not the held-breath silence of the weeks before, when the whole city seemed to be listening for something hungry—but the ordinary silence of stone that has nothing left to hide. Sometimes, standing on the ramparts, Wren thought she could feel the mountain breathing again, slow and easy, like a sleeper whose nightmare had passed. Whether anything still lived beneath it—any court, any regent, any remnant of the old bargain—she could not say. The mines were sealed now, officially out of respect for the dead. Unofficially, because no one who had been sent down to look had come back up willing to talk about what they'd seen.

A knock at the door. "Magistrate? The courier leaves within the hour."

"Send it," Wren said. "All of it."

Boots receded down the stair. Wren rose, took off her hunter's coat—the grey one, with the silver circle of the Third embroidered at the collar—and hung it on the peg by the door. She would need it again tomorrow, and the day after. The world didn't stop needing hunters just because one war had quietly ended. But tonight, tonight she was going to do something she had not done since she was nine years old, running barefoot through the night market with a girl who sold luck-knots and laughed at the inspectors.

Tonight she was going to visit a friend.

* * *

Vharrow had changed in the year since—or perhaps Wren had changed, and the city had simply come into focus around her new eyes.

The night market was louder now. That was the first thing. Under the old law, the market had made itself small after sundown, vendors hawking only honest goods, everyone keeping one eye on the watch-lanterns. Now the lanterns burned every color, and half the stalls sold things that would have gotten their owners hanged two winters ago: love-salves and storm-charms, little glass bells that rang when someone lied to you, paper prayers folded into birds that actually flew. Nobody prayed safely yet—that art was still being learned, and there had been incidents, ugly ones—but the fear had changed shape. It was no longer the fear of being caught. It was the fear of asking too much.

Wren walked through it in plain clothes, hood down, and no one looked twice at her. That was the strangest mercy of the year: how quickly the hunters had become furniture again, once the mob violence burned out and the Crown, in its infinite institutional wisdom, decided that mages were now a matter of licensing rather than extermination. There were new magistrates in Vharrow who had never worn the silver circle. Some of them were mages themselves. Wren tried to imagine explaining that to the girl she'd been at nineteen, and failed.

She bought a skewer of grilled eel from a stall near the fountain, mostly because she remembered Ulyana buying them, and ate it standing up while the market flowed around her. A child bumped her leg, apologized with enormous formality, and pressed a paper bird into her hand—"for luck, lady, it's free today"—and flew off before Wren could respond. She stood holding the little folded thing, absurdly moved, until an old woman selling candles asked if she was quite alright.

"I'm fine," Wren said. And found, to her surprise, that it was true.

The guildhall sat where it had always sat, on Crooked Saint's Lane, between a cooper's shop and a house that leaned as though listening. From the outside it looked much as it always had—cracked plaster, a roof patched with three different colors of slate—but the windows glowed warm now, all of them, and the front doors stood open.

Open doors. Wren stopped on the threshold and just looked at them. She remembered when those doors were barred, when Sorrel used to peer out through a slit before letting anyone in, when the guild's whole existence depended on being invisible. Now a wooden sign hung beside the entrance, freshly painted, lettered in a careful hand:

**THE GUILD OF SMALL MERCIES — LESSONS FREELY GIVEN. ALL WHO COME IN PEACE ARE WELCOME.**

Beneath it, smaller: *No miracles performed on the premises. Please ask nicely anyway.*

Wren smiled despite herself. That last line had Dagny Holt's fingerprints all over it.

She went in.

The great hall was warmer than any building had a right to be in the last month of autumn. The hearth fire burned at the center of the room—not in a fireplace but in a ring of black iron set into the floor, the old flame, the one that had never gone out, burning now steady and gold and somehow *attentive*, as though it were listening to the conversation happening around it. Tables lined the walls. A dozen strangers sat at them, some taking notes, some simply warming their hands, and at the head of the room a young woman with ink-stained fingers was demonstrating something with threads and knots to an audience of three apprentices.

Ilse. Except Ilse had her memories back now—all of them, every betrayal, every year of informing, everything she had traded away and been given back when the court broke. Wren had heard the story secondhand, from Dagny, and had not known what to make of it: that Ulyana had offered the woman who sold her out not punishment but a place at the guild's own table, teaching, rebuilding, atoning in the only currency that mattered. Forgiveness as structure, Dagny had called it, in one of her rare philosophical moods, usually preceded by ale. Wren wasn't sure she could have done it. She suspected that was exactly why Ulyana could.

Ilse looked up mid-lesson, met Wren's eyes across the hall, and went very slightly pale. Then she nodded—slowly, deliberately, a nod that contained an entire apology and an entire acceptance of judgment deferred—and returned to her thread.

"You're blocking the doorway."

Wren turned. Dagny Holt leaned against the wall beside the door, arms crossed, looking healthier than Wren had seen her since the ascent—since the moment in the collapsing gallery when Dagny had thrown herself between the party and a fall of burning stone and had come out of it with a ruined shoulder and a grin, saying, *that's the toll paid, then.* The shoulder worked now, stiffly. The ledger was gone; Wren had heard she'd burned it page by page in the guild hearth.

"Dagny."

"Hunter." Dagny pushed off the wall, studied her, and something in her weathered face softened by a degree. "You got my letter, then."

"I got your letter."

"'Come see the guildhall, doors are open, bring nothing official.' Plain enough instructions. You're not carrying anything official, are you?" Her eyes flicked, professional and quick, over Wren's plain coat. "Because I'll know. I always know."

"Nothing official," Wren said. "I filed my report this morning. It's on a courier to Vharrow already."

"And?"

"And Ulyana Kavanagh is dead."

The words came out level, and Wren was proud of that. Dagny searched her face for a long moment, then grunted—an acknowledgment, a receipt, maybe something close to approval.

"Dead in the collapse," Wren continued, quieter. "Body unrecoverable. Struck from the rolls with honors. My addendum's sealed for fifty years." She paused. "Fifty years is a long time."

"It's nothing," Dagny said. "To an institution like yours, fifty years is a long weekend. The truth keeps. Ask anyone in this room." She jerked her chin toward the hall, toward the fire, toward the students. "Ask her. Oh—" and here Dagny's grin came back, sharp and bright and utterly delighted, "—but you haven't met her yet. Have you."

"Met who?"

Dagny didn't answer. She just stepped aside and gestured down the corridor beyond the great hall, toward the back rooms, toward the chair that had been Sorrel's, and Wren's heart began doing something complicated against her ribs.

"She doesn't go by Kavanagh anymore," Dagny said. "Gave the name up. Gave the inheritance up. All of it, into the ledger, to void the old contract forever. So don't go shouting surnames at her, eh? She's just—" and Dagny shrugged, spreading her hands, enjoying this far too much, "—the youngest member."

* * *

The back room was small and cluttered and lit by a lamp and by the spill of firelight through the open door. Shelves of ledgers lined one wall—the Ember Ledger itself sat among them, Wren noticed, no longer hidden but displayed, its debts all crossed out except the final line, which had been rewritten in a new hand: *Paid in full. Paid otherwise. Paid well.*

Ulyana stood at the desk with her back to the door, sorting charms.

She was thinner than Wren remembered. The year had carved lines into her face that hadn't been there before—around the eyes, around the mouth—and her hair was bound back plainly, without ornament. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, and on her left forearm, uncovered for the first time in Wren's living memory, the oathmark showed: the interlocked knot of the Guild of Small Mercies, dark as fresh ink, and around it newer marks, finer ones, that Wren did not recognize. Marks earned, not given.

Wren stood in the doorway and found that every speech she had composed on the walk over—all the careful sentences, the explanations, the apologies arranged in order of importance—evaporated at once.

"Your boots squeak," Ulyana said, without turning. "They've squeaked since you were twelve. I heard you come up the lane."

"You couldn't have."

"The third floorboard outside always gives you away. It gave you away when we were children and you were sneaking me honeycakes past the curfew patrols." Ulyana set down the charm she was holding and turned around.

For a moment neither of them moved. The fire murmured in the next room. Outside, faintly, the night market sang its loud new song.

Then Wren crossed the room in four strides and embraced her, hard, awkward, one hand fisted in the back of her shirt like a child afraid of losing her, and Ulyana held on just as tightly, and neither of them said anything at all, because everything either of them might say had already been said in the dark beneath the mountain, in the reunion among the dreaming souls, where forgiveness had been offered and not yet earned and both of them had known it.

When they finally drew apart, Ulyana was laughing a little, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "You're crying."

"Hunters don't cry."

"Hunters absolutely cry. I've seen the reports."

They sat down across the desk from each other, in the two mismatched chairs, and for a while they simply talked—small talk, market talk, the safe terrain. Wren learned that the guild had eleven students now, and that Ilse's lessons on prayer-craft were oversubscribed, and that Dagny had begun ferrying pilgrims to the mountaintop shrine on the weekends and complained constantly about it and would clearly die before giving it up. Wren learned that the widow from the night market—the first widow, the one whose answered prayer had started everything—came by every week now, and had learned to pray safely, and was teaching other widows to do the same. Wren learned that the fire in the hall had burned steadily since the Guttering, neither growing nor guttering, and that Ulyana checked it every morning the way other people check the weather.

And then the talk ran out of safe terrain, as talk between them always eventually did, and Wren sat back and looked at her oldest friend and said the thing she had come to say.

"I reported you dead."

"I know. Dagny told me." Ulyana's voice was gentle. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. It's not absolution, Lyana. I didn't spare you because I believe in what you did. I mean—I do, but that's not—it wasn't—" Wren pressed her palms flat against her knees, the old gesture, grounding herself. "I lied in an official document of the Crown. Me. Do you understand what that is? I built my whole life on the idea that the law was worth serving even when it hurt. Even when it was wrong, I thought, you serve it, and you change it from inside, slowly, honestly, and that's the only loyalty that means anything. And then I went below, and I saw the lanterns, and I understood that the law I served was founded on a lie, and I came back up and I lied right back. First stroke of the pen." She shook her head. "It felt exactly the same. That's the worst part. It felt exactly the same as writing the truth."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"When Sorrel was dying," she said, "she told me something I hated. She said the guild's oath was never about hiding magic. It was about giving it away. Magic hoarded rots—that's why the old gods starved, that's why the regent lasted so long and became what she was. Every secret is a little hoard." She reached across the desk and tapped the wall of shelves, where the Ember Ledger sat in the open air where anyone could read it. "Sorrel hid the truth for sixty years because she thought the truth was dangerous. And the truth grew rotten in the dark and killed her. I'm not going to make her mistake twice. So." She met Wren's eyes. "You want to serve something honestly? Serve this. Not me. Not the guild, even. This." She tapped the ledger again. "The record. The real one. You keep records better than anyone I've ever known, Wren Calloway. You always did. Even your lies are meticulous."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's the highest compliment I know how to give."

Wren laughed—short, surprised, real. Then she took a breath, and reached inside her coat, and set something on the desk between them.

It was a folder, thin, sealed with grey wax—the wax of the Hunter's Office, but broken already, opened and resealed with plain tallow. Ulyana looked at it and did not touch it.

"What is it?"

"A copy of my addendum. The sealed one. The one nobody opens for fifty years." Wren turned the folder so the tallow seal faced up. "Except I broke my own seal this afternoon and had a copyist in Greyhollow duplicate it, and I resealed the original and sent it on, and this copy I'm giving to you. Because you're right. Secrets rot. And because someday—maybe not in fifty years, maybe sooner, maybe when the Fourth Circle finally gets curious about why the mountain went quiet overnight—someone official is going to want the truth about the Undercourt, and when they come looking, they should find it kept by people who lived it. Not locked in my office where it can be lost, or burned, or quietly misplaced by someone who finds it inconvenient." She pushed the folder an inch closer. "Consider it evidence. Consider it testimony. Consider it—" she faltered, and then finished, quieter, "—consider it the last thing I take off the Crown's books and put on yours."

Ulyana picked up the folder. Held it a moment, weighing it. Then she opened the ledger on the desk, chose a blank page, and wrote, in her careful hand, an entry Wren could read upside down:

*Received into the guild's keeping, this day: the testimony of Wren Calloway, hunter, friend, first witness of the truth. To be given freely to any who ask.*

"There," Ulyana said. "Now it's not a secret. It's a record. Those are different things entirely."

"They are," Wren agreed, and found her throat unexpectedly tight.

Outside, the market bells rang the hour—nine o'clock, the hour when they'd been children sneaking through the stalls, the hour when everything forbidden had seemed possible. Somewhere down the lane the third floorboard creaked under Dagny's deliberate weight, announcing her approach to anyone who cared to listen, because in this house nobody snuck anymore.

Ulyana glanced toward the door, then back at Wren, and her expression shifted into something Wren hadn't seen on her face in years—something shy, almost young.

"Stay for supper," she said. "We eat late. Ilse cooks like she's still doing penance and Dagny eats like she's still smuggling, so there's always too much. And after—" she hesitated, and Wren watched her choose honesty, watched her lay down another little hoard of secrecy, "—after, I'm teaching the evening lesson. First-years. Prayer-craft, beginner's forms. Nothing dangerous. Just... how to ask nicely." A pause. "You could stay for that too. If you wanted. No official capacity. Just a chair by the fire."

Wren looked at her friend—at the oathmark bare on her arm, at the ledger open on the desk, at the firelight spilling through the door of a guildhall whose doors would never bar again—and understood, finally, completely, what loyalty had been trying to tell her all along. It had never been a choice between the law and the truth. It had been a choice about which one you were willing to serve when they parted ways. She had spent her whole life believing the answer was obvious.

She pulled her chair around to Ulyana's side of the desk.

"I'll stay," Wren said. "But I'm warning you now—if any of your first-years asks me a question about legal procedure, I will answer it. At length."

"Gods help us all," Ulyana said, laughing, and went to call the others in to supper.

And far above them, past the lamplit streets and the loud new market, the mountain stood silent under the stars—keeping, at last, nothing at all.
