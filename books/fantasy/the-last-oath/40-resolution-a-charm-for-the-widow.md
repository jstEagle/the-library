## 40. Resolution: A Charm for the Widow

The night market in Vharrow had learned a new shape.

A year ago, the stalls had huddled in the shadows of the Tanners' Row like criminals waiting to be caught, lanterns hooded, voices low, every charm sold wrapped in three layers of plausible deniability. *It's just thread. It's just knots. It's just a thing my grandmother taught me, nothing in it at all.* Now the stalls stood in the open where the moon could see them, and the lanterns burned unhooded, and the vendors called their wares aloud—luck-knots and fever-salves and little bells that kept nightmares from the door—and nobody looked over their shoulder when they spoke of magic. The word itself had come back into common use, soft as an old coat taken down from a high shelf, still fitting after all these years.

Ulyana walked through it all unnamed.

She had no stall. She never did. She carried her wares in a basket over one arm: bundles of rowan twigs cut at the proper hour, twists of red thread, wax tablets stamped with the guild's mark—a small flame inside an open hand—and a few of her own making, charms so simple a child could learn them, which was rather the point. The youngest member of the Guild of Small Mercies did not sell power. She sold instructions.

The evening smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions and the particular green scent of the river in early autumn. Somewhere a fiddle was playing, badly and joyfully. Children ran between the stalls playing the new game, the one that had swept the city since spring, where one of them wore a paper crown and pretended to be a small god granting small favors—"I bless your dog," "I bless your boots," "I bless your supper, may it not be burnt"—and the others paid them in chestnuts. A year ago, such a game would have earned the whole lot of them a visit from an inspector. Now their mothers watched from doorways with something that might once have been fear and was now only tenderness.

"Charm-woman." An old man selling roasted apples nodded to her as she passed. He didn't know her name. Nobody did. That was the arrangement, and she had made it gladly, and on most days—even now, even a year on—she woke in the morning and felt the absence of her name the way one feels the absence of a heavy pack set down after a long march: a lightness, an echo, occasionally a phantom ache where the weight used to be.

*Ulyana Kavanagh is dead,* Wren's report said. *Lost in the collapse of the Undercourt, along with the smuggler's records and the court's regent. No remains recovered. Case closed by order of Magistrate's Office.*

Wren had written those lines in her careful hunter's hand, and then she had walked past the reopened guildhall every week or so, always at dusk, never coming in until one evening she simply came in, sat down at the long table, and asked if there was any soup. There was soup. There was always soup now. Ilse made it, humming tunes she'd relearned along with everything else—the memories had flooded back the day the court broke, all of them, the betrayals included, and Ilse had wept for two days and then stood up and said, *Well. I know exactly what I'm capable of. That's more than most people can say. Teach me to be better at something else,* and Ulyana had given her the ledger and the apprenticeship in the same breath.

Dagny ran pilgrims up the river road now, her old smuggling sloop refitted with benches and a painted sign: HOLT'S HONEST PASSAGE, NO QUESTIONS ASKED EXCEPT THE FRIENDLY KIND. She'd burned the ledger in the guildhall's hearth, page by page, and cried only once, when she got to a name near the end. Ulyana had never asked whose. Some debts you pay privately or not at all.

And the fire—the fire in the great hearth of the guildhall burned steady, the way Sorrel had always claimed it must, the way it had guttered and trembled for a hundred years while its true purpose sat locked beneath a mountain wearing a dead woman's face. They fed it oak and ash and sometimes, ceremonially, a single written wish, folded small. It took them all. It gave back heat, and light, and on certain nights—if you sat very close and were very quiet—a warmth that went deeper than skin, a sense of being *known* without being judged, which was, Ilse said, the closest thing to a god most people would ever need, and thank all the scattered heavens for that.

But that was later, and home, and the fire kept itself. Here was the market, and here was the work.

Ulyana stopped at the corner where the fiddler played, because she had seen the widow.

She knew grief when she saw it; she'd spent half her life reading it across market crowds, learning which faces needed luck-knots and which needed only to be left alone. This woman was young—younger than Ulyana, perhaps twenty-four—with the raw, scrubbed look of someone who had been crying in private and performing composure in public for longer than her body agreed to. She wore mourning grey gone thin at the elbows. She stood before a fruit stall holding two pears, and she held them too long, staring at nothing, and Ulyana understood: buying food for one was the hardest arithmetic the newly widowed ever did.

She waited until the woman had bought a single pear and turned away, and then she let herself be found. That was the trick of it—there was no trick. You simply stood where a grieving person was walking, with kindness visible on you like a lamp, and waited.

"You're the charm-woman," the widow said. Her voice was hoarse. "They said you'd be here tonight. Near the fiddler."

"They say right, then."

"I don't have much money."

"I've heard that before too." Ulyana shifted her basket to her hip. "What is it you're wanting? And mind"—she smiled, gentle—"the honest answer, not the polite one. Polite answers make weak charms."

The widow's composure cracked, just at the edges. "I want to pray," she said. "For him. My husband. He died in the spring—the mill collapse—and everyone keeps telling me he's at peace, he's with the small gods, he's *somewhere*, but I don't know how to talk to anywhere. I never prayed before the Godfall ended. Nobody taught us. We were told praying was dangerous, praying was how the old ones got their hooks in you, praying was—" She stopped. Pressed the pear against her sternum like a shield. "And now I hear stories. About prayers answered wrong. About things that take payment. My sister-in-law swears her neighbor prayed her rheumatism away and lost her sight instead. Is that true? Is it true?"

"It's true," Ulyana said, "that it happened. Once. In a season when the world was starving and anything warm-blooded that listened was listening with teeth." She had been there. She had fed the starving ones herself, had poured the last ember out among them like bread among beggars, and had watched them remember, slowly, painfully, what they had been before the famine made monsters of them—not gods of thunder and conquest, mostly, but gods of thresholds and rain-crops and safe childbirth and the particular holiness of a finished chore. Small gods. Hungry no longer, or hungry in the way living things are hungry, which is to say fed by what is freely given. "It isn't true anymore. But the fear is still true, and fear needs handling same as grief does. Come and sit. I'll teach you."

"There's a fee."

"There's a custom." Ulyana led her to the low wall beside the fountain, where the moon fell clean and the fiddle covered their words from any ears that mattered, though no such ears existed anymore either—but customs are the bones of a practice, and bones keep a body upright. "You give me what the lesson's worth to you. A copper. A pear. A story about your husband. Whatever's true."

The widow sat. Held out the pear.

"Keep the pear," Ulyana said. "Eat it while we talk. Grief goes easier with sweetness in the mouth—I don't know why, but my guildmaster swore it, and she was right about nearly everything and wrong about the things she loved most, which is also the human way." She took out a length of red thread and began, without hurry, to tie the first knot. "Now. Your husband. Tell me his name and one small true thing about him."

"Tam." The name came out of her like a breath held since spring. "He—he sang while he worked. Badly. The whole mill complained and he never stopped."

"Sang badly and never stopped." Ulyana tied the second knot. "That's a good foundation. You'd be surprised how many prayers are built on less."

*

Here is what Ulyana taught her, there by the fountain, while the pear was eaten down to its core and the fiddler moved into a slower tune:

You do not pray *at* the dark, because the dark is mostly empty and emptiness doesn't answer. You pray *along* the warm places. A threshold. A hearth. The spot where sunlight pools at four in the afternoon. The small gods live where life is lived, because that is what they are made of—they always were, though the old books liked to pretend otherwise, and the Godfall had been, in the end, a famine of attention. You speak a name, because names are doors. You ask for something small, because small asks fit through small doors, and a god who grants you sunshine on washing day has done more real good than one who promises you kingdoms. And you give something back—bread on the sill, a song sung badly and without stopping, a kindness done in the dead one's name—and this is not payment, this is *conversation*, because no love survives long as a one-way street.

"And if I do all that," the widow whispered, "will he hear me?"

"He'll hear you," Ulyana said, tying the last knot—a luck-knot, four crossings, the same pattern she had sold a grieving widow in this same market a lifetime ago, before the ember and the descent and the choice that had left her carrying fire instead of herself. "Whether he can answer is his business and the gods'. But hear you? Grief travels further than light, my dear. Nothing in creation misses it."

The widow looked down at the knot in her palm. Then she closed her fingers around it, and her face did something complicated and holy—grief and hope wrestling in the same small space, neither winning, both staying.

"How much?" she asked again.

"A story," Ulyana said. "Someday. When it doesn't hurt to tell it. Come to the guildhall on Tanner's Row—the one with the open door and the fire in the window—and tell me about Tam and his terrible singing. That's the whole fee. That's the whole fee for anyone."

The widow laughed, wetly, wonderfully. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead, briefly, to Ulyana's hand—an old gesture, older than the Godfall, one Ulyana had seen in visions in the vault, worshippers greeting the dragon-priests of the deep past—and murmured, "Bless you. What's your name? Who are you?"

And Ulyana felt the question land where her name used to live, in that smooth and sunlit absence, and found—as she did each time—that it did not hurt. It rang. Like a bell struck in an empty room, filling the whole of it.

"No one," she said, smiling. "Just the youngest member. Go home. Pray small. Sing badly and never stop."

*

She walked back through the market slow, letting the night have its way with her.

The lanterns. The fiddle. The children crowning each other with paper gods. Overhead, the sky was clear and enormous, and if you knew where to look—above the mountain, above Greyhollow's ruined mouth—you could see a faint auroral shimmer where the shared ember breathed, all the god-fragments fed from one fire, waking and sleeping and dreaming kindly, a whole constellation of small divinities warmed by what had almost been hoarded. Some nights Ulyana stood in the guildhall yard and talked to it. Not praying, exactly. Reporting. *The widow took the knot. Dagny's boat sprang a leak and she fixed it swearing. Ilse remembers everything and forgives herself a little more each month. Wren brought soup. Your fire is steady.*

The fire was steady. She felt it before she saw the hall, the way you feel a lit hearth from the street on a cold night—a pressure of warmth against the face, an invitation. The guildhall windows glowed gold onto Tanner's Row, and the door stood open as it always stood open now, day and night, because a guild that hid had starved the world once, and this guild would never hide again.

Inside: Ilse at the ledger, pen scratching, humming. Dagny's boots by the door, her owner asleep in the good chair after a long run upriver, snoring like a landslide with opinions. And the hearth-fire burning tall and quiet, oak-fed and ember-hearted, throwing light on the empty master's chair—Sorrel's chair, which Ulyana sat in during meetings but never otherwise, because some seats you inherit and some you earn, and she was still earning hers, one knot, one lesson, one widow at a time.

She set down her basket. She warmed her hands. Outside, faint and off-key and utterly unstoppable, someone was singing.

The fire burned steady. It would burn steady tomorrow, and the day after, and for as long as anyone needed light to pray by.

Ulyana—who had been Kavanagh, who was now no one, who was free—pulled her chair to the hearth, and stayed.
