## 1. The Thirteenth Moon

There were three rules to running a charm-shop in Vellum, where magic had been outlawed for longer than Sable Delacroix had been alive, and she kept all of them the way other people kept saints' days.

One: sell nothing that actually worked.

Two: never let them see your hands shake.

Three: if a Ministry clerk ever came through your door, you were a widow who sold luck-knots and love-charms to gullible sailors, and you had been nothing else for twenty years, and you would be nothing else until they put you in the ground.

Sable was dusting rule three when Ivo Ren knocked.

She knew it was him before he touched the door. She'd spent two decades learning the footsteps of Vellum's streets—the flat slap of dockworkers, the quick patter of children stealing apples from Maren's stall, the heavy synchronized tread of Ministry patrols that made her spine itch like weather coming. This step was new. Measured, but not patrol-measured. A man trying to walk casually and thinking very hard about it.

He knocked anyway, which was considerate of him. Most Ministry men didn't knock; they arrived the way rain did.

"Madame Delacroix?" He had to duck under her doorframe, which gave her an unfair advantage she intended to enjoy. He was tall and narrow as a bookend, with the kind of face that suggested he'd been earnest in the cradle and simply never stopped. His coat was good wool, brushed and patched at one cuff—clerk's wages, archivist's pride. He held his hat in both hands like it might bolt.

"You're early," Sable said, because he was twenty minutes late, and because flustering Ministry clerks was one of the few pleasures left on her menu.

"I—" He checked a pocket watch that was not there, patted the pocket, and went faintly pink. "I appear to have read my own appointment wrong. Which is embarrassing in an archivist. We're meant to be good with dates."

"We're all bad with the ones that matter." She stepped aside. "Come in before the fog gets your hat."

Her shop was small enough that two people in it constituted a crowd. Shelves climbed every wall, crowded with the inventory of her honest dishonesty: knotted red thread for safe travel, dried gull-feather charms against drowning (which offended her personally, though she sold them anyway), little tin moons that were supposed to bring sweet dreams and mostly just brought tin. A kettle muttered on the brazier in the corner, and the whole room smelled of salt and chamomile and the particular dust of old paper, which she had never managed to scrub out of anything, including herself.

Ivo Ren stood in the middle of it looking like a man who had wandered into a church of a faith he didn't practice but respected enormously.

"Your sign says *Charms, Honest Prices*," he said.

"The prices are honest."

"And the charms?"

"Are exactly what they claim to be." She poured tea into two chipped cups. "Which is to say, thread and feathers and hope. Hope does most of the work. It always has."

"That's a very cynical thing for a charm-seller to say."

"It's a very practical thing for a survivor to say." She handed him the cup and watched him drink without testing it first, which told her something either very reassuring or very damning about him. "You're Ministry."

His cup paused halfway down. "I'm an archivist. The Ministry employs the archives. That's not the same thing, whatever the patrols think."

"It's exactly the same thing when the patrols are deciding whose door to kick."

"Yes," he said quietly. "It is." He set the cup down and looked at her with those earnest eyes, and Sable felt the particular horror of a man about to be sincere at her. "I asked you here tonight—or asked to come here—because I wanted you to know what I am before we went any further. I don't lie well. You may have noticed."

"I noticed you can't find a watch in your own coat."

"So you'll understand why I won't pretend the last three weeks weren't the best part of my year." He said it plainly, no flourish, which was somehow worse than flourished. "The readings in the west gallery. The arguments about marginalia. Your terrible opinions about the provenance of the Harrow Psalter."

"My opinions were *correct*."

"They were *heretical*, and I've thought about them every day since." And then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of letting a moment breathe, he reached into his satchel. "Which brings me to the reason I'm early-late and nervous and probably ruining this. There's something I want to show you. And I need you to promise me you won't scream."

Sable had survived the fall of a nation, twenty years of exile, and Odile Fenn's cooking. She did not intend to scream over a book.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

What he drew from the satchel was wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth it was bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, and the moment her fingers were within a hand-span of it, the hairs on her arms stood up like a crew bracing for a squall.

She didn't touch it. Twenty years of discipline said *don't touch it*.

"What is this?" Her voice came out level. She was proud of how level.

"A seizure ledger," Ivo said. "Officially. It's catalogued as *Confiscated Devotional Text, Salt Anew provenance, restricted*. Officially, nobody has requested it in ninety years." He unwrapped it the way other men opened letters from home. "Unofficially, I've spent eleven years in those stacks, and I've learned that the Ministry doesn't burn dangerous books. It files them. Burning implies the book matters. Filing implies it never mattered at all. They've erased an entire country, Madame Delacroix, and shelved it under 'miscellaneous.'"

Salt Anew.

The name went through her like cold water through cloth. Nobody said it. Nobody *could* say it—the word wasn't banned, precisely, it was worse than that, it was *unwritten*, struck from maps and registries and gravestones until saying it felt like speaking into a wound. Children in Vellum learned that the coast east of the Teeth had always been empty sea. Her grandmother had drowned along with everything else, along with the language and the kings and the storm-calling line that had guarded those waters since before Vellum had a harbor.

"I found it by accident," Ivo was saying, turning pages with a scholar's tenderness. "A misfiled index card. One card, mislabeled, and underneath it—a whole nation." He reached a page near the end and stopped. "And then I found this."

He turned the book toward her.

The page showed a woodcut illustration, faded to brown ink: a coastline, a broken tower, a moon above it drawn with thirteen rays instead of twelve. And standing in the foreground, rendered in crude, confident lines, was a woman. Storm-dark hair. A scar—no. A mark at the left collarbone, curved like a wave about to break.

Sable had that mark. Had been born with it, had hidden it under high collars for twenty years, had stopped looking at mirrors below the throat the way other women avoided their debts.

Beneath the figure, in a hand older than the Ministry itself, ran a single line:

*When the thirteenth moon turns, the storm returns.*

"This is why I came tonight," Ivo said. His voice had gone soft with the excitement that Sable, in three weeks of knowing him, had already learned was both his best and most dangerous quality. "Not the provenance arguments, not even—" he flushed again, "—not only the company, though the company is... The point is, I found a country that was erased, and a prophecy that says it comes back. And I thought—I don't know what I thought. That you'd want to see it. You always get this look when you handle old things, like you're listening to them."

Sable was not listening to the book.

The book was listening to her.

Because the thirteenth ray of that woodcut moon was glowing. Faintly, gently, the way a coal remembers fire—a thin amber light rising off the page that had no business existing, that could not exist, that violated every law Vellum had passed and every rule Sable had lived by, because the moon was responding to her, to the blood in her veins, to the storm-caller's inheritance she had smothered and starved and buried under twenty years of thread knots and honest prices—

"Sable?" Ivo said. "You've gone white. Should I not have—"

"Put it away."

The words came out in a voice that wasn't entirely hers. Lower. Older. Carrying, the way her grandmother's voice used to carry across a harbor, the way a voice carries when the wind decides to help it.

Ivo froze. The glow brightened. Outside the window, far off across the rooftops, thunder rolled—and there was no cloud in the sky, there had been no cloud in Vellum for weeks, and Sable felt the sound move through her chest like a hand closing around her heart.

*I am seen*, she thought, and the thought was terror, pure and total, the terror of the hunted animal whose cover finally breaks. Twenty years of being nobody. Twenty years of being a widow with a shop and a kettle and a talent for knots. Gone. Undone by a misfiled index card and a kind man with an earnest face.

He was staring at the page. Then at her. Then at the mark she could feel burning through her collar like a brand.

"The thirteenth moon turns next month," he whispered. "I checked the almanac. Sable—it turns in nineteen days."

"Put it away," she said again, and this time she heard it clearly, heard what she sounded like, heard the storm under the words, and closed her eyes. When she opened them, she made herself smile—the shopkeeper's smile, the widow's smile, warm and harmless and practiced ten thousand times.

"Ivo. Whatever you think you've found in that book, I'm a charm-seller. I sell thread and feathers and hope." She reached out and took the grey-leather book from his hands, feeling its age hum against her palms like a live wire, and wrapped it back in its oilcloth with steady fingers. Rule two: never let them see your hands shake. "Hope does most of the work. It always has."

But Ivo Ren was still watching her, and the thunder was still rolling somewhere out over a clear and cloudless sea, and neither of them said what they were both thinking:

That the storm had returned already.

That it had been hiding in a charm-shop all along.
