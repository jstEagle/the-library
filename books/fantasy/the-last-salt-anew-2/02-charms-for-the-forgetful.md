## 2. Charms for the Forgetful

The Magpie's Debt was moored at Tanners' Quay, which was not where ships belonged but exactly where smugglers preferred them. Sable found the crew in the hold playing cards by lantern light, the way they always did on rest nights, and she stood at the top of the ladder with her heart going like a trapped moth and nearly climbed back down twice before Pell spotted her.

"Charm-seller!" he crowed. "Down here! We've been robbed of your company a whole week."

"I had shop business," she said, descending.

"Shop business," Odile repeated from the shadows near the mast, one eyebrow climbing. "That what we're calling it now? Because Wren saw you Tuesday. Harbor district. Laughing."

"I do laugh sometimes," Sable said. "Rarely. It's a medical condition."

Wren, the youngest of them, grinned around a mouthful of bread. "You were laughing at an *archivist*. I'd know that coat anywhere. Ministry grey. You were sitting with a *Ministry man*, Sable, and you looked—" she paused, searching for the word, "—*happy*. It was unsettling. Like watching a cat eat soup."

The crew laughed, and Sable sat down among them on a crate that smelled of tar and pepper, and let the laughter wash over her while her hands quietly shook in her lap.

They knew her as the luck-bringer. That was all. Six years ago she had wandered up the gangplank of this rust-bucket with a satchel of charms and no history, and Odile had looked at her for a long moment—too long, Sable had thought since—and said, "Can you cook?" And Sable had said yes, which was true, and that had apparently been qualification enough.

Since then: charms stitched into hems before dangerous runs, little knots of knotted thread that turned customs officers' eyes away, salt packets blessed over in whispers that kept hulls whole through storms that should have cracked them. The crew called it her knack. Sailors were pious about luck the way other people were pious about gods; nobody asked too many questions of a woman who brought it.

But tonight she had to tell them something, and her mouth had gone dry as old paper.

Odile dealt her in without being asked. That was another thing about the captain—she never asked questions, which either meant she didn't care or cared enormously, and after six years Sable still hadn't decided which.

"You're going to lose," Pell warned, shuffling. "She plays like a priestess. Never smiles when she's got a good hand."

"She never smiles at all," Wren said.

"I smile."

"You grimace. There's a difference."

Sable picked up her cards. Three queens and a knave. She set them face-down and didn't pick them up again.

"I need to tell you all something," she said.

The hold went quiet in that particular way ship-people go quiet—the way that says *we have heard this tone before, on decks, before storms*. Even the lantern flame seemed to lean in.

"The man I had dinner with," Sable said. "The archivist. His name is Ivo Ren. He works in the Ministry archives, deep stacks, restricted collections." She swallowed. "He showed me a book he shouldn't have. A prophecy book. Century-old. And in it there's a drawing, and the drawing is of me, and under it are written the words *the storm returns.*"

Silence. Then Pell said, carefully, "That's a coincidence, surely. Lots of people look like old woodcuts. Old woodcuts look like everyone."

"It's my face, Pell. My exact face. Down to the scar over my eyebrow, which I got falling out of a pear tree when I was nine, in a country that doesn't exist anymore."

Nobody moved. Wren had stopped chewing. The lantern hissed.

"My name isn't Sable Delacroix," Sable said. "Well—it is, but it's the second one. The first one I don't say. I was born in Salt Anew, before it drowned, and I'm not just from there—I'm the last storm-caller of its royal line, and if the Ministry ever learns I'm alive they will kill me slowly and put my head on a spike above their gates, because twenty years ago they didn't drown Salt Anew by accident. They drowned it on purpose. And I—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She pressed on. "I've been hiding in this city selling love-charms to teenagers for two decades, and I am so tired, and I didn't know who else to tell."

She had expected many things. Fear. Recoiling. The terrible polite silence of people rearranging their faces around a stranger.

What she got was Odile saying, "Pell, deal her out. She's not playing tonight."

And then Odile got up, crossed the hold, and sat down beside her on the crate, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said:

"Took you long enough."

Sable stared at her. "I'm sorry?"

"Six years." Odile counted on her fingers with theatrical patience. "Six years I've watched you charm a knot against squalls. Watched you mutter over salt. Watched you stand at the bow in weather that sent grown men below decks, with your hair standing up like you'd put your finger in a lightning socket, looking *homesick*. I know what a storm-caller looks like, girl. My grandmother came off the water too."

"You—you knew?"

"From the first night." Odile shrugged, unrepentant. "You think I let just anyone onto my ship? You walked up my gangplank smelling of ozone and grief and you flinched every time thunder rolled, the way people flinch around a wound. I could have asked. Instead I asked if you could cook."

"Why?" It came out almost angry. Sable heard the anger and couldn't stop it. "Why would you keep that secret? You didn't know me. I could have been anything. I could have sunk your ship with a thought."

"You could have," Odile agreed placidly. "Instead you fixed our leaky roof and taught Wren to make stew and cried once, quietly, on the anniversary of something, and I pretended not to see. People show you what they are, Sable. Magic's just weather. Character's the climate." She nudged Sable's shoulder with her own. "Besides. Luck-bringers are worth their weight in gold, and storm-callers, I hear, are worth their weight in trouble. I decided I liked you better than gold and could survive the trouble."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob. When Sable looked at the rest of them—Pell gripping his cards, big Bram the bosun very still, old Marta with her knitting forgotten in her lap—they weren't looking at her like a monster. They were looking at her like a member of the crew who'd finally told them her real birthday.

"So," Bram rumbled. "The prophecy. Says what, exactly?"

"That I'll either raise Salt Anew from the sea," Sable said, "or drown this coast. And that I have until the moon finishes thirteen turns to find some Door out of a country that's been underwater for twenty years."

"Moon's got how many turns left?" Pell asked.

"Twelve."

"Well," said Pell, laying down his hand—he'd clearly been winning, and clearly no longer cared. "Good thing you told us now and not next week. We'll want time to provision."

Sable blinked at him. "Provision? For what?"

"For sailing, obviously." He said it the way he'd say *for breathing*. "You can't find a Door in a drowned country standing on a quay in Vellum. Somebody's got to take you there, and somebody's got to keep the Ministry off your back while you look, and there's exactly one ship in this harbor stupid enough for both jobs."

"We're not stupid," Marta said mildly. "We're sentimental. It's worse."

"There's a price on her head," Sable said, and her voice cracked on it. "There will be. If the Ministry learns what I am—if any of you are caught helping me—they won't fine you. They'll hang you. All of you. You have families. Wren has a sister. Bram has three kids and a wife who already thinks this ship is a floating death wish—"

"Bram's wife is right," Bram said comfortably. "It changes nothing."

"This isn't yours to carry," Sable said. "I've carried it alone for twenty years. I can—"

"No," Odile said, and the word came down flat as a hatch closing. "That's exactly what you can't do, and it's exactly why you're sitting in my hold telling us this instead of running tonight, which is what you'd have done six years ago. Am I wrong?"

Sable opened her mouth. Closed it. Six years ago—no, six months ago—she'd have run. She'd have burned the shop's ledgers and vanished by dawn and been a different name in a different city by winter, and it would have hurt less than this, and that was the whole problem, wasn't it. Hurt less and meant nothing.

"I'm wrong," she admitted.

"You're wrong." Odile took her hand—calloused palm, sailor's grip, warm. "Listen to me, storm-caller. Twenty years alone is enough. Whatever's coming out of that water, you don't meet it by yourself. That's not a prophecy. That's just how crews work."

Wren scooted over and leaned against Sable's other side, bony and warm. Pell declared that anyone who betrayed Sable would answer to him personally, a threat somewhat undermined by the fact that Pell weighed about as much as a wet sail. Marta started knitting again, faster, which was how Marta prayed.

And underneath it all, Sable felt something loosen in her chest—a knot tied twenty years ago, in flood-water and smoke, when she'd sworn never again to be known by anyone. Not undone. Just loosened. Knots like that didn't untie; they only loosened, and you hoped.

Later, when most of the crew had gone up to their hammocks, Odile poured two fingers of plum brandy and handed her one.

"One more thing you should know," the captain said. "That archivist. Ren."

Sable went still. "What about him?"

"He came asking at this quay three weeks ago. Asking after a charm-seller with sea-grey eyes. Official channels—said he was cataloguing trade records." Odile swirled her drink. "Could be nothing. Could be a lonely bureaucrat with a crush. Could also be that your date has been looking for you longer than you've known him."

Outside, far off over the black water, thunder muttered—distant, patient, counting.

* * *

Sable didn't sleep aboard. She walked home through the sleeping city with the brandy warm in her chest and the crew's voices still in her ears, and she told herself the tightness behind her ribs was only fear.

But when she reached her street she stopped, because the lamp outside her shop was lit, and she had left it dark.

A figure stood beneath it—slight, grey-coated, hat in hand, waiting. Ivo Ren looked up as she approached, and his face was pale, and in his arms he carried a leather folder wrapped in oilcloth as though it were a sleeping child.

"Sable," he said quietly. "I stole something. And I think they know."
