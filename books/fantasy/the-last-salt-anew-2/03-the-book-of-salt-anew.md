## 3. The Book of Salt Anew

Ivo Ren had been a clerk of the Ministry of Silence for eleven years, and in that time he had developed three unshakeable professional beliefs: that every document had its proper place, that every place had its proper key, and that no one—no one—had ever actually read the Ministry's founding charter, because if they had, the coffee-stained thing would have been reprinted decades ago.

He was proud of his orderliness. He was proud of his institution. He was, on most days, proud of the small and necessary work of keeping Vellum's memory honest: catalogued, cross-referenced, dusted.

Which was why, at half past nine on a Thursday evening, standing in the Restricted Stacks with a stolen ring of keys in his sweating hand, he felt rather like a man discovering that his house had been built over an old graveyard.

The trouble had started with Sable.

Not with her face—he was not so dramatic as all that, whatever Pell the navigator might say about archivists. It had started with her handwriting. On the charm-shop receipt she'd given him (for a luck-knot he did not need and a jar of "genuine river salt" that was almost certainly table salt with ambitions), she had written *Delacroix* in a looping, old-fashioned script—and Ivo's trained eye had snagged on it the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

Because he had seen that script before. Not the name—the *style*. The flourished capitals, the way the descenders curled like wave-crests breaking leftward. It was the hand of the Salt Anew diaspora registries, the ones from ninety-eight years ago, the ones filed under ERASURE PENDING in a drawer he was not supposed to know existed.

He had gone home. He had not slept. He had told himself it was scholarly curiosity, the same harmless itch that made him alphabetize his spice rack.

Then he'd looked up ERASURE PENDING, and scholarly curiosity had died quietly in the dark.

* * *

The Restricted Stacks smelled of cold iron and older paper. Ministry archives were organized by a principle Ivo had always found elegant: nothing true was kept where anyone could find it, and nothing false was kept anywhere at all. Truth, in Vellum, was a security risk.

Drawer 13-C was labeled SEASONAL LEDGERS, HARVEST YEARS 40–61, which was the archival equivalent of a locked door painted to look like a wall. The key turned with a sound like a swallowed word.

Inside: ledgers, as promised. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a book bound in grey leather that might once have been blue.

Ivo carried it to the reading alcove with both hands, the way you carry something sleeping. He lit the permitted lamp, then, feeling reckless, the forbidden second lamp as well. Two lamps. He was practically a pirate.

The title page read: *THE BOOK OF SALT ANEW, being a true account of the nation, its waters, its works, and its people, compiled by the Royal Archivist Ysolt Delacroix, Last of Her Office.*

Delacroix.

His heart performed a small bureaucratic irregularity.

He began to read.

What he had learned in school—what every schoolchild in Vellum learned, in the single dry paragraph allotted to the subject—was this: *Salt Anew was a coastal nation lost to the sea in a great storm some century past. Its people were resettled. Let us remember them fondly.*

One paragraph. He had always assumed the brevity was grief. Nations do not like to talk about their dead.

But the Book of Salt Anew talked about its dead at length, and the dead, as it turned out, had opinions.

*They tell us now that the sea took us,* wrote Ysolt Delacroix in what Ivo realized, with growing horror, was the final entry. *The sea took nothing. We were taken. I have seen the orders. I have copied the orders into my own hand because the originals will be burned by morning, and someone must keep a copy even if keeping it is treason.*

*The storm-callers refused the Conscription Accord. Our king said: our magic is rain for the fields and safe passage for fishing boats, and we will not make it a weapon. So the Ministry decided that a country which will not serve must not exist. They drowned us with our own weather—turned our callers' gifts against our harbors, and when our people fled inland, they scattered us across forty parishes and renamed us at the border desks. My granddaughter was four years old. They gave her a new name and told her parents that remembering the old one was sedition.*

*If you are reading this: her name was Sabine. Her mother called her Sable, for the color of her eyes before a storm.*

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Ivo sat very still. Somewhere above him, a cart rolled across the archive floor, and he flinched like a guilty man, which, he supposed with detached academic interest, he now was.

He kept reading. He could not have stopped if the building caught fire; indeed, part of him noted that if the building caught fire, the Ministry would probably call it another act of the sea.

There were pages on the storm-caller line—nine generations of it, each portrait rendered in silverpoint, each face surrounded by annotations in Ysolt's careful hand. And there, on the last annotated leaf, a sketch that was less a portrait than a warning: a young woman, sharp-boned, dark-eyed, hair pinned back in the practical manner of someone who expected wind.

Beneath it, in the leftward-breaking script Ivo now knew better than his own signature:

*And should my granddaughter's line endure—if the gift sleeps in her as it slept in me—let this be written plainly, so that no archivist after me may claim ignorance was innocence: the prophecy names her. The storm returns, says the water. The Door opens. Thirteen turns of the moon, and Salt Anew rises or the coast drowns. May she be kinder to herself than history has been to us.*

*May someone love her enough to tell her the truth gently.*

Ivo put his head in his hands.

* * *

Here is what eleven years in the Ministry teaches you, if you are paying attention: the machinery of silence does not grind loudly. There is no villain twirling a moustache in the sub-basements. There is only paperwork. Forms in triplicate. A renaming decree signed by men who went home to their dinners. A drawer marked SEASONAL LEDGERS. An entire nation filed under a harvest year and forgotten on purpose, and everyone who did the filing went home to their dinners too, and slept, presumably, quite well.

Ivo had always believed the Ministry was clumsy but decent—a great slow animal that meant well. He had spent his career apologizing for it, the way you apologize for an enormous well-meaning uncle who breaks chairs.

But a country does not drown itself. A people do not forget their own names by accident. Somebody had built drawer 13-C, and somebody had built the lie in the schoolbooks, and the lie had been maintained, lovingly, for ninety-eight years, by clerks. By men exactly like him. Men who went home to their dinners.

He thought about Sable's face when she'd shown him the luck-knot she'd made—how she'd held it out flat on her palm, watchful, like a woman feeding a wild bird. Twenty years hiding in a city that outlawed what she was, selling charms to people who'd have reported her for the price of a drink, and she had still invited him to dinner.

And the Ministry's file on her—if there was a file, and there was certainly a file—probably had her down as a minor registration anomaly.

He looked at the prophecy page again. *Thirteen turns.* He did some arithmetic against the almanac on his desk upstairs, and the arithmetic came back ugly: the thirteenth turn was weeks away. Days away, if the almanac's marginalia could be trusted, and Ministry almanac marginalia could always be trusted, because nobody bothered to falsify margins.

He thought about what happened to unauthorized persons connected to prophetic anomalies. He had processed those files himself. He knew which forms came next. Form 9-S, Disposition of Irregular Assets. He had never once asked what "disposition" meant in practice. He had initialed the routing slips.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking and, out of habit, wished he had a form for it.

* * *

It took him until nearly midnight to decide, and when he decided, it was not with a thunderclap but with the small, terrible click of a drawer closing.

He could return the book. That was the orderly path. Log the access, note the discrepancy, submit a memorandum to the Committee for Archival Integrity, and wait six to eight years for a reply that would arrive redacted.

Or.

Or he could do the thing that would end his career, his pension, and possibly, per Form 9-S, rather more than that.

Ivo Ren was not a brave man. He wanted that entered clearly in whatever record existed of this night. He was a tidy man, a punctual man, a man whose greatest transgression in eleven years had been under-filing a memo on purpose because the memo was wrong and he lacked the courage to say so aloud.

But he had been raised on his grandmother's stories, and his grandmother had been born two parishes inland of a coast no one could name anymore, and she used to hum a lullaby in a language the Ministry's maps said had never existed. He had thought, his whole life, that this was just a grandmother thing. Grandmothers hummed. It was in the regulations somewhere, probably.

He opened the book to Ysolt's final entries and began, with an archivist's hands—steady now, steadier than they'd been all night—to copy. Every word. Every portrait. The leftward-breaking script. He worked until the candles guttered, and when he finished, he wrapped the original in its oilcloth and put it back in drawer 13-C, because taking the whole book would be noticed, but paper misfiled is paper invisible.

Then he stole four pages.

His own copies. His own hand. Treason in his own neat, defensible cursive, which was somehow worse and somehow more Ivo.

At the door of the stacks he paused, lamp in hand, and looked back at the long dark shelves—all that ordered silence, all those proper places and proper keys—and felt the strange lightness of a man stepping off a ledge who discovers, mid-fall, that he can breathe.

"Forgive me," he said to the shelves, because he was still, at core, a polite man. "The filing was wrong."

Then he walked out through the sleeping Ministry with four folded pages against his chest, past the desk of the night clerk, nodding good evening to her as though he were merely a tired man going home to his dinner.

Which, in fairness, he was. He simply intended, for the first time in eleven years, to eat it honestly.

* * *

Sable's charm-shop was dark when he reached it, the little brass bell silent, the window displays of luck-knots and bottled weather sleeping behind the glass. He stood across the street for a long moment, a bureaucrat with stolen treason in his coat, rehearsing sentences.

*I've found something. About your grandmother. About your name. Please don't hate me for how I'm about to ruin your entire life.*

None of the rehearsals survived contact with reality, as usual.

Because as he watched, the shop door opened—not Sable coming out, but Odile going in, quick and quiet, with Pell at her shoulder, and behind them two more of the Magpie's crew moving like people with urgent business and bad intentions, and Ivo understood with a cold, archival clarity that he was not the only one who had spent tonight reading things he shouldn't.

The lamp in the shop window flickered once—though there was no draft—and in the glass, for half a heartbeat, the reflection showed rain falling upward.

Ivo clutched his stolen pages tighter and crossed the street.

Whatever was about to happen, it was not going to wait for the thirteenth turn.
