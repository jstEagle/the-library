## 4. What the Ministry Keeps

The knock came at half past four in the morning, which is the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are pretending, and Sable Delacroix was doing neither.

She had felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes—the old, salt ache she hadn't known since Vellum, since before Vellum—and then the knock arrived, three raps, polite as a tax collector, and she understood that the ache and the knocking were related.

"Sable." Odile's voice through the floorboards of her own shop, where the captain had apparently been sleeping on a pallet among the charm-stock like a woman guarding a dragon's hoard. "Back door. Now."

Sable didn't ask how she knew. Odile always knew. It was one of the three things that made her a good captain; the other two were a talent for arithmetic and a laugh that could end an argument mid-sentence.

Sable pulled on her coat, and her hand—without asking her permission—closed around the little tin of sea-glass charms on the workbench. Not for selling. For something else. She noticed this and did not like it.

Through the shop's back window she could see the street: grey, drizzling, and wrong. Wrong because it was empty. Vellum's streets at any hour held someone—a drunk, a baker's boy, a cat with opinions—but this street had been *cleared*, the way water clears before a wave.

Three figures stood under the lamp at the corner. They wore no uniform. They didn't need to. The Ministry of Silence dressed its hunters in whatever the neighborhood wore; that was the whole horror of them. You could be talking to your neighbor, and your neighbor could be the Ministry, and you would only find out when the questions started.

One of the three turned his head—not toward the window, toward the *shop*, the way a man turns toward a sound only he can hear—and Sable felt the pressure behind her eyes spike into something bright and furious.

*They know,* said the part of her that had spent twenty years listening for exactly this. *They've known since Ivo put that book on my counter. They let him carry it to me so they could watch what I did.*

"Back door," Odile said again, closer now, already inside, already moving. "We're not using doors anyway."

* * *

"We're using the cellar," Odile corrected herself, hauling up the trapdoor behind the flour shelf, which was not a flour shelf, which had never been a flour shelf, which was the entrance to the flooded undercity that every smuggler in Vellum pretended didn't exist and every smuggler in Vellum used weekly.

"The cellar floods," Sable said.

"The cellar is *supposed* to flood." Odile dropped through the hole without ceremony. "That's what makes it ours. Come on, storm-girl."

Nobody had called her that in twenty years. The name went through her like a hook through a fish's cheek, and she followed it down.

The undercity of Vellum was the old city—the city that had been there before Vellum decided to be respectable and paved over its own childhood. Half-drowned streets, candlelit canals where the tide kept its own schedule, staircases that went up into the foundations of buildings whose ground floors had forgotten them. The crew of the Magpie's Debt moved through it like eels. Sable moved through it like a woman being carried by eels.

Brick was on her left, lantern high. Pell was ahead, testing depth with a pole, muttering about silt. Quill—the youngest, all elbows and loyalty—had appointed himself Sable's personal guard and kept touching her elbow at every corner as if she might dissolve.

"You're shaking," Quill whispered.

"I'm fine."

"You're shaking *and lying*. That's two crimes."

"I'm a criminal, Quill. It's a Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Don't correct your elders."

Odile stopped at a junction where three canals met under a dome of ancient brick, and held up a hand, and everyone stopped, and in the silence Sable heard it: above them, muffled by stone and street, boots. Many boots. Moving in formation across the neighborhood above.

"They're not just searching the shop," Brick said quietly.

"No," Odile said. "They're searching the *street*. The *block*. Which means they think she ran, which means—" she looked at Sable, and her face in the lantern-light was doing something complicated, "—which means somebody told them to expect a runner."

*Ivo,* thought Sable, and the thought had teeth. But even as it bit, she knew it was wrong. Ivo Ren had shown her his own ruin with both hands. Whatever else he was, he wasn't this.

Then who? She looked at Pell, at the navigator's careful face, and made herself look away. Paranoia was a luxury. Everyone here had bled for her tonight.

Everyone here had *bled* for her tonight. That was the problem. That was the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast.

"Odile," she said. "You should leave me here. Give me the boat, point me at the sea gate, and go home. If they catch me alone, they catch a lone exile. If they catch me with you, they catch a conspiracy."

Odile considered her for a long moment. Then she sat down on a wet step, which was a very Odile thing to do—sit down when things got serious, make the world wait.

"Do you know what I keep?" she asked.

"...What?"

"What the Ministry keeps. You've been wondering since the raid started, I can see it chewing on you. Why now, after twenty years quiet. Here's why." She patted the step beside her. "Because they don't keep laws, girl. Laws are for courts. What the Ministry keeps is *ledger books*. And somewhere in those books there's a page with your grandmother's name on it, and pages get read, and readers get ambitious, and ambitious men come looking at half past four in the morning."

Sable sat down beside her. The stone was cold through her trousers.

"So we run," she said. "You take the Debt out past the harbor chain before they seal it—"

"And sail where?"

"Away."

"Away," Odile repeated, tasting it. "I've sailed away from six ports in my life, and every one of them was full of people I owed. There's no away, Sable. There's only *who comes with you*." She stood, and her voice changed—the captain's voice, the one that ended arguments. "So here's what happens. We vote. All hands. Right here, knee-deep in a canal, because I'm not making this decision like it's small."

* * *

They voted in the flooded junction, standing in a rough ring, lantern swinging between them, their breath fogging in the cold air that came off the old water.

Odile spoke first, and she did not soften it. "Sheltering her makes us traitors. Not smugglers—traitors. Smuggling is a fine and a confiscated hold. Treason is the Quiet Yard and your name struck off every registry, and your family never allowed to say it again. That's the price. Anyone can walk. No shame in walking. I'll row you to shore myself."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a clock.

Brick went first, because Brick always went first and thought later. "My gran came from the coast," he said. "Never said from where on the coast. Never said why she flinched at rain. I'm in."

Quill, immediately, loudly: "In. Obviously in. Sable, if you ever sell me out I will be so hurt—"

"Pell?"

A pause. A long one. Pell looked at the black water, at the pole in his hands, and said, "In," and his voice was steady, and Sable was too frightened and too grateful to hear the seam in it.

"Quill, stop hugging people, we're standing in a canal. Sable."

"I vote no," Sable said.

Every head turned.

"I vote no," she said again, louder, because her magic had begun to hum under her skin like a struck bell and she needed her voice to be bigger than it. "You heard the captain. Traitors. Struck names. Families who can't say yours. I have spent twenty years being the reason people whisper, and I will not be the reason you drown. Give me the skiff. Point me anywhere. I'll—"

"Sit down," Odile said, not unkindly.

"—I'll vanish, I'm good at vanishing, I've had practice—"

"*Sit down.*"

And the bell under Sable's skin cracked open.

It happened between one word and the next. The lantern flame stood straight up like a soldier. Every drop of water in the junction—on the walls, in the canals, beaded on five faces—rose at once, a hundred small moons hanging in the dark, trembling. The old brick began to sing, a low harmonic, the sound of a cathedral remembering itself. Somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep.

Sable stood in the middle of it, hands out, terrified, magnificent, twenty years of stillness breaking like ice in spring.

"I didn't mean—" she gasped. "I can't—Odile, I can't always stop it—"

"I know." Odile hadn't stepped back. Of everyone in the ring, she was the only one who hadn't stepped back. She was looking up at the hanging water with an expression Sable had seen once before, years ago, on a night crossing through a squall that should have killed them: wonder wearing a workingman's face. "Look what you're doing."

"I'm not *doing* anything—"

"You're scared," Odile said, "and the water knows your name. Girl, that isn't a curse. That's a *crew*." She reached out and took Sable's wrist, warm and calloused and utterly unafraid, and the water came down all at once, gentle as rain, soaking them all, and Quill laughed like a drain, and the bell under Sable's skin went quiet—not silent, never silent again, but quiet, held, *listened to*.

"The vote stands," Odile said. "Five yes, one no, and the no doesn't count, because nobody votes themselves out of a family. That's not how crews work. Now come on. We've a skiff at the third landing and a long way to go, and you're going to tell us everything on the way. Everything. Starting with why an archivist looks at you like you hung the moon."

"He doesn't look at me like—"

"Sable."

"...He might look at me a little like that."

"That's what I thought." Odile picked up the lantern. "Move out."

* * *

They went deeper instead of up—deeper being safer, Pell said, and nobody argued, though Sable noticed he steered them past one turning and toward another with a navigator's certainty that sat slightly wrong against the dark.

At the third landing, a skiff waited under oilcloth, and they loaded her in it like contraband, which, Sable supposed, she now was.

As they pushed off into the current that would carry them toward the river gate and the Debt and the open, hunted dark, Sable looked back once at the flooded junction, at the place where she had broken and been caught.

Belonging, she thought, watching Odile's silhouette bend to the oar. It had been purchased tonight at a price she had never agreed to pay—five names written into treason because hers was worth chasing. The debt was enormous. The debt was unbearable.

The debt, she realized with a lurch, was also exactly what family was.

Ahead, the river gate loomed, and beyond it the harbor, and beyond the harbor the sea that remembered her grandmother's name.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Sable had dreaded all her life and would dread until she died:

Whistles. Many whistles. Closing fast along the water.

Someone had sold them.
