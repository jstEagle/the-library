## 5. Maps of a Drowned Country

**Sable**

The Magpie's Debt smelled of tar, onions, and Odile's terrible pipe tobacco, and Sable had never loved anything so much in her life.

She stood at the rail as Vellum shrank behind them, its grey towers dissolving into morning haze. Three days since the raid. Three days since her magic had torn through her shop like a startled bird, shattering every charm on the shelves and singing the Ministry hunters' eyebrows clean off. Three days since the crew had voted—Pell abstaining, which she tried not to think about—to make themselves traitors for her sake.

"You're brooding," said Wrenn, the ship's cook, pressing a bowl of fish stew into her hands. "Brooding's bad luck on a crossing. Eat."

"I'm not brooding. I'm contemplating the vastness of the sea."

"The sea's not that vast. My uncle fished all of it." Wrenn settled beside her on a coil of rope. "Ivo's looking for you. He's been in the hold since dawn with his papers, muttering like a kettle. I think he's finally gone round the bend."

Ivo Ren had gone round the bend somewhere between stealing Ministry pages and lying to a harbormaster, and Sable suspected she was to blame. She found him belowdecks, cross-legged amid a nest of charts, his spectacles pushed up into his hair, copying coordinates onto a scrap of sailcloth by lantern light.

"Archivist," she said.

"Charm-seller," he replied, without looking up. "Or storm-caller. Or fugitive. You really do have the most impressive collection of titles." He patted the deck beside him. "Come look at this. It's either brilliant or it's nothing, and I've been wrong about brilliance before."

She sat. He spread three charts across the boards: two modern, one so old its vellum had gone amber and soft as bread crust.

"Every map of this coast made in the last hundred years shows open water here." His finger traced a stretch of sea east of Vellum. "But the Ministry didn't just erase Salt Anew from the histories. They erased it from the geography. Surveyors' logs, shipping records, lighthouse registries—all of it corrected. Except—" he tapped the ancient chart, where faint ink lines showed a harbor, a river delta, streets laid out in the shape of a fan "—except nobody told the old tide-tables. And tides, Sable, are honest. Water remembers what paper was paid to forget."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying there's an anomaly. Twice a day, at low tide, the currents out here move *wrong*. Around something. Something big enough to redirect a current has to be—" He stopped. Looked at her. "You already know, don't you?"

Her hands had gone cold around the stew bowl. Because she did know. Not with her mind—with something older, under her ribs, that had been pulling toward the east since they'd left the harbor, like a compass needle finding north.

"My grandmother used to sing me a song," Sable said quietly. "In a language I thought she'd invented to annoy my grandfather. About a city where the streets filled with water twice a day and everyone called it the Tide's Gift." She looked down at the amber chart, at the fan-shaped streets. "That's it. That's exactly it."

* * *

**Ivo**

Ivo had spent eleven years in the Ministry archives learning that history was a filing system. Events went in folders. Folders went in cabinets. Cabinets went in basements, and basements went unvisited until someone needed proof of something already decided.

He had believed, in a mild bureaucratic way, that this was how truth worked: filed, stamped, retrievable.

Then he had watched Sable Delacroix put her hand flat against the deck of a smuggler's ship and go still as a drowned thing, and he understood that some truths were not filed anywhere. Some truths lived in people's blood, waiting.

It happened at dusk on the third day out. The water changed color—not dramatically, nothing a surveyor would flag, just a deepening, a blue going bruise-dark beneath the hull. And Sable, who had come up on deck complaining of a headache, stopped mid-sentence and walked to the rail like a sleepwalker.

"Sable?" Ivo followed her. "Sable, what—"

"Listen," she whispered.

He listened. Wind, rigging, Wrenn swearing at a pot below. Ordinary sea-noise.

"I can't hear anything."

"No." Her eyes were wide and wet and fixed on the horizon. "*I can.*"

And then the wind came off the water carrying something that was not wind—a pressure, a hum, a note too low for ears and felt instead in the teeth—and every lantern aboard flared green-white, and Sable's hair lifted from her shoulders as if she stood in a rising column of summer air.

What happened next, Ivo would later try to write down and fail, because the words were all archival words and none of them fit. But he saw it: she gripped the rail and her knuckles went white and her mouth moved, shaping syllables in a language he'd catalogued once, in a sealed drawer, labeled *extinct*.

"She's speaking Salt Anewan," he breathed. "That's—that's impossible, there are maybe four people alive who—"

"There were never only four," said Odile at his shoulder. The captain had come up quietly, pipe unlit, watching her luck-bringer with an expression Ivo couldn't read. "There were thousands. There just weren't any left who remembered they were allowed."

Sable turned to face them, and for one terrible moment Ivo saw someone else looking out of her eyes—an older woman, tall and furious and crowned in spray—before she blinked and it was only Sable again, shaking, gripping the rail hard enough to splinter it.

"That wasn't me," she said. "That was—I don't know whose voice that was. It knew the harbor. It knew *where the palace gates were*, Ivo, before we could possibly—" Her breath hitched. "Something down there is talking to me."

"Not to you," Odile said softly. "To what you're made of."

* * *

**Sable**

The memories came at night, and they came like the tide: irresistible, patient, covering everything.

She dreamed in a bed she'd never slept in, in a room with shells set into the walls to sing when the water rose. She walked streets that flooded at noon and drained at dusk, past fishmongers hawking in two languages, past a library built on stilts where children learned letters by writing them in wet sand. She stood in a hall of white stone while a woman with her own dark hair pressed a circlet of storm-glass onto her brow and said, in the language of the drowned city: *We do not command the weather, little one. We keep faith with it.*

She woke each morning with salt on her lips and grief in her chest for people dead a century, and she could not tell anymore which grief was hers.

Wrenn found her on the fourth night, sitting up in the dark with her knees drawn to her chest.

"Bad dreams?"

"Wonderful ones." Sable laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's the problem. I dreamed my grandmother's wedding, Wrenn. A wedding fifty years before I was born, in a country that doesn't exist, and I could smell the flowers. I know the groom's name. I know he was terrified of eels. How do I know that? Why would the sea give me *that*?"

Wrenn considered this with the seriousness she gave all matters, including soup. "Maybe because somebody ought to remember him. Being scared of eels is the sort of thing that gets lost first."

Sable stared at her. Then, helplessly, she laughed again—really laughed—and Wrenn nodded as if a difficult patient had turned the corner.

"It doesn't feel like remembering," Sable admitted. "It feels like being *filled*. Like there's a whole nation sitting in my chest, and it wants its chair back. And the awful part—the truly shameful part—is that part of me likes it. Out there is everything I ever lost, all at once, handed to me like a gift. All I have to do is stop being Sable Delacroix, charm-seller, bad card player, woman who burns toast." She hugged her knees tighter. "All I have to do is disappear into it."

"Sounds lonely," Wrenn said. "Being a whole nation. Who'd burn your toast?"

* * *

**Ivo**

On the sixth morning, Pell came to Ivo at the wheel and asked, very casually, how far the anomaly was from Ministry patrol routes.

Ivo answered without thinking—he'd memorized the coastal charts years ago—but that evening, turning the conversation over, he found the question sitting wrong in his stomach, like a swallowed hook. Patrol routes. What did a navigator need with patrol routes?

He mentioned it to Odile, who smoked her pipe in silence for a long time and then said only: "Watch him. Don't accuse. Accusing makes men honest about the wrong things."

But it was Sable who noticed the real strangeness, two nights later, when the Magpie's Debt crossed over the edge of the drowned capital itself.

They anchored above it in the last violet light. Below the keel—everyone felt it rather than saw it—lay streets. A fan-shaped city holding its breath under forty fathoms, windows dark, gates shut, the Tide's Gift returned a thousandfold.

Sable stood at the rail, and the water beneath the ship began to glow.

Not reflected starlight. Glow—from below, rising, a soft green-gold luminance spreading outward from directly beneath the hull in widening rings, and in the rings Ivo could see shapes: rooftops, spires, the pale suggestion of avenues, lit as though ten thousand lamps had just remembered their duty. The whole drowned city waking up to look at her.

"Oh," Sable whispered. Her hands rose from her sides without her willing it, sparks crawling along her fingers like eager insects, and her voice came out doubled—one voice young and frightened, one older, resonant, coming up through the water. "Oh, hello. Hello, I'm here. I'm *home*—no. No, that's not—" She shook her head hard. "That's not what I said."

The glow brightened. The sea went glass-still. Far below, at the heart of the light, Ivo glimpsed something vast and pale in the sunken palace—a shape, or a doorway, or a door.

And Sable smiled, and it was the most frightening thing he had ever seen, because it wasn't entirely her smile.

"Come down," she said, in two voices. "The thirteenth moon is nearly full. Come down, daughter of storms, and be whole."

Then she swayed, and Ivo caught her as the glow sank away like a lamp being carried off down a corridor, leaving the black water blacker than before.

When she opened her eyes, she looked up at him from the circle of his arms and said, in her own single voice, small and clear:

"Ivo. I don't think the sea wants me to visit."
