## 6. The Turncoat Tide

Odile Vasse had been captain of the *Magpie's Debt* for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to read her crew the way other captains read weather. Pell's silences meant rain. Bram's humming meant money. And when Cook started making the good stew—the one with the saffron smuggled out of Corvane in a hollowed-out cheese—it meant somebody on board was frightened enough that they needed feeding.

She stood at the galley door that evening and watched three bowls of saffron stew go out to the deck. Three. For a crew of seven.

"Who's scared?" she asked Cook, who was scrubbing a pot with the intensity of a woman exorcising something from it.

"Pell," Cook said, not looking up. "And me, a little, since I know what Pell being scared means."

Odile nodded and went topside.

The *Debt* lay at anchor in the Grey Reach, that stretch of coast where Vellum's harbor laws got vague and the Ministry's patrol boats got lazy. The fog came off the water in slow white ribbons. Sable sat at the bow with her back against the mast, teaching Bram a card game that Odile suspected she'd invented five minutes ago for the sole purpose of losing at it cheerfully. That was Sable all over—she had been aboard nine days now, hiding in the hold during daylight, and still she spent her nervousness on making other people laugh.

Nine days since the raid on her shop. Nine days since Odile had watched lightning come out of a charm-seller's fingertips and thought, *well, there goes twenty years of pretending not to notice.*

Because she had noticed. Of course she had. You don't run cargo past the Ministry of Silence for two decades without learning what fear looks like, and Sable wore hers like a coat tailored by someone who hated her: beautifully, and too tight. The way the girl flinched from open water. The way she'd once, drunk on festival wine, hummed a lullaby in a language none of them knew—and stopped mid-note as if the song had bitten her.

Odile had known. She'd said nothing, because saying nothing was how you kept people.

Now she climbed to the helm where Pell stood at the wheel, though there was nowhere to steer but circles.

"Talk," Odile said.

Pell was sixty, built like a bundle of rope, with a face that had been weathered into kindness by sheer accident. He'd navigated the *Debt* through three storms, two blockades, and one incident involving a customs official, a goat, and a forged marriage certificate that Odile still refused to explain to new crew members.

"Captain," he said carefully, "how long do you think this lasts?"

"The sheltering?"

"I mean any of it." He nodded toward the bow, where Sable was laughing—actually laughing—at whatever Bram had just played. "They burned her shop. They'll have her name in every office between here and the border. We're seven people on an old ship, Odie. They're the Ministry."

"They're also, historically speaking, terrible sailors."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Odile admitted. "But humor is what I've got, so I'm spending it while the market holds."

Pell didn't smile. That was the wrong part. Pell always smiled; he'd smiled through the goat incident. His hands were steady on the wheel, which was worse than shaking hands would have been. Shaking hands meant feeling. Steady hands meant decision.

"How much are they offering?" Odile asked quietly.

Pell's knuckles whitened on the wood. "I don't—"

"Pell. I've watched you lie exactly once before, and it was about the goat. Your ears turn red. They're red now."

He looked out at the fog for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and terribly reasonable, the voice of a man who had rehearsed.

"A pardon. Full amnesty for the whole crew. Every debt, every warrant, wiped clean." He turned to face her. "One person in exchange for six lives. Six, Odie. And she's already doomed either way—you know what they do to storm-callers. But us? We could just be smugglers again. Nobody has to die for a war that ended before we were born."

"And you told them where we'd be."

"The Grey Reach, tomorrow night, when we take on water." His jaw worked. "I haven't confirmed it yet. I can still send word calling it off. I wanted—" he stopped. Started again, honest now, which was somehow more painful. "I wanted you to agree first. So it wouldn't be betrayal if we all chose it together."

"That's not how it works."

"I know," he said. "That's why my ears are red."

* * *

The crew vote happened below decks, around the table, with the good stew going cold because nobody ate. Odile laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only gift she had left to give: the offer, the terms, the odds.

"We vote," she finished. "All of us. Including her." She nodded at Sable, who sat very straight at the end of the bench, hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman watching her own funeral arrangements get discussed over dessert.

"You're asking me to vote on whether I get handed to the people who want me dead," Sable said.

"I'm asking you to vote on whether these six people spend their lives on your account. It's not the same question."

"It feels like the same question."

"It isn't," said Bram, who at nineteen had a genius for cutting through things sideways. "One of them is about you. The other's about us. I vote we keep her." He said it fast, the way you jump into cold water.

Cook voted to keep her. Rennick, the mate, voted to hand her over—not cruelly, with his eyes down and his voice thick, saying only, "I've got a daughter," and Odile didn't argue, because a daughter was an argument no captain should try to beat.

Then it was four to one against Pell's plan, with Pell abstaining, staring at his own hands.

Sable stood. "There's another option nobody's said aloud. I leave tonight. Alone, in the skiff. They're hunting a storm-caller traveling with the *Magpie's Debt*. Take that away, and you're just a ship again. I take the problem with me."

"No," Odile said.

"With respect, Captain, it's not your choice."

"It's my ship, and my choice what cargo leaves it. And you—" Odile pointed at her, aware she was being unfair, doing it anyway, "—are cargo I'm fond of."

Something crossed Sable's face then—something young and unguarded, gone almost before it arrived—and Odile understood suddenly that in forty years of sailing she had never once been anybody's reason to stay, and had decided somewhere along the way that this was fine, and was only now discovering it was a lie.

"Nobody leaves alone," she said gruffly. "We sail at dawn. Vote stands."

Pell looked up. "Odie—"

"Dawn," she repeated. "You've got till then to call off your friends in the Ministry. Do it, and we never speak of this again. Don't, and..." She couldn't finish the sentence. She'd threatened crew before—mutineers, thieves—but never Pell, never him, and her voice broke on the empty space where the threat should go.

That night she heard him on deck, past midnight, whispering into the signal-shell. She heard him say, "No. Cancel it. Cancel it," and then, after a pause, in a smaller voice: "It's done. There's nothing I can do. They were already coming."

Some doors, once knocked upon, don't close.

* * *

The Ministry cutter came out of the fog an hour after dawn, black-hulled, silent, running no lights because it needed none. Odile saw it first—captains see things first; it's the whole job—and rang the bell, and the morning went to pieces with remarkable efficiency.

They came aboard at the starboard rail: eight hunters in grey, faces masked, moving like the tide comes in, which is to say without malice and without mercy and without stopping. Rennick put up his hands immediately. Bram reached for the boathook and Odile caught his wrist and shook her head—*no, boy, not this, not against eight*—and he subsided, shaking.

"Sable Delacroix," said the lead hunter, in a voice like a filing cabinet closing. "Come forward. The rest of you will be processed."

"She's not here," Odile said.

The hunter regarded her. Behind him, at the rail, Pell stood with his wrists already bound, and he was crying, silently, and Odile found—to her lasting surprise—that she wasn't angry at him. Not even slightly. He had tried to buy six lives with one, and the arithmetic had broken in his hands, and he would carry that forever. Anger would have been a mercy. She gave him a small nod instead, *it's alright, old man*, and watched it destroy him more thoroughly than any curse.

Then the hatch opened and Sable came up onto the deck.

"Don't," Odile breathed.

But of course she did. Sable Delacroix, who had hidden for twenty years, walked forward into the grey line of hunters with her chin up and her hands loose at her sides, and said, "Let the crew go and I'll come quietly," and Odile thought: *twenty years of hiding and she still walks toward trouble like it owes her money.*

The hunters closed around her. And the air changed.

Odile felt it in her teeth first—a pressure, a gathering, like the moment before the sky splits. Sable's eyes had gone pale, silver-bright, and the fog over the water began to turn, slowly, in a great circle around the ship, and somewhere far off thunder answered a question nobody had asked yet.

"Storm-caller confirming herself," the lead hunter said, and made a gesture, and the hunters moved to bind her—

—and Odile moved too.

Later, she would not be able to explain it as strategy. There was no strategy. There was only the fact that this girl had eaten at her table and lost at cards with her boy and slept safe in her hold for nine days, and that Odile Vasse had spent her whole life keeping people, and was not going to stop now, not for eight hunters, not for the Ministry, not for anything under the sky.

She threw herself between them.

The blade took her under the ribs, angled up, professional and quick. She heard Sable scream—a sound that was half voice and half weather—and then the world turned white and loud, and through it Odile registered, distantly, with a captain's detached satisfaction, that the girl was magnificent: rain falling upward, lightning walking on the water, hunters thrown like dolls. She registered Bram hauling her bodily toward the rail. She registered Sable fighting free and catching her other arm, both of them dragging her down into the skiff, and the sea itself rising smooth beneath them like a hand offered palm-up.

"Odile. Odile, stay with me. Stay with me, you stubborn old woman."

"Language," Odile managed. It came out mostly blood.

The last thing she saw clearly was the *Magpie's Debt* falling astern, Pell at the rail with his bound hands raised in farewell or apology or both, and Sable's face above her, lit by her own lightning, tears cutting tracks through the spray.

"Hold on," Sable was saying. "Hold on. I know where to go. Hold on."

The water carried them south, fast and impossibly calm, toward the drowned country and the Door beneath it, and Odile Vasse, bleeding warm into the bottom of the boat, held on—because keeping people was the whole job, and she had never once quit a job halfway.
