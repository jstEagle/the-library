## 7. The Door Beneath the Water

The water remembered how to be a hallway.

That was Sable's first thought, treading in the dark above what had once been the Grand Processional of Salt Anew — that the sea hadn't simply covered her grandmother's capital, it had *furnished* it, holding up arches that should have fallen, keeping marble columns standing like guests who refused to leave a party long after the hosts died. Magic soaked into stone did not drown. It just waited.

She had swum down from the skiff alone. That had been the whole of the plan after Pell's betrayal, after Odile went down between the crates with red blooming through her coat like ink through paper, after Sable had screamed at Ivo to get them out, *all of them*, and leapt overboard rather than be handed to the Ministry like contraband spice. The Magpie's Debt was gone or captured; she didn't know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed anchor.

So she swam. Storm-callers, it turned out, could breathe water, if the water was willing. Hers was very willing. It parted around her face in a soft bubble, warm as a held breath, and whispered things to her in a language she had never learned and somehow knew.

"Stop that," she told it, and her voice came out strange and small inside the air-pocket. "I'm not talking to you. I'm talking to myself. It's called coping."

The water giggled. Water shouldn't giggle. She decided, firmly, not to think about it.

* * *

The throne room of Salt Anew opened before her like a wound that had learned manners.

It was vast — vaster than any hall in Vellum, where architecture was something the Ministry permitted grudgingly, in shades of gray and apology. Here the ceiling was a mosaic of the night sky done in lapis and shell, and though the stars were furred with twenty years of silt, they still burned faintly, still turned slowly overhead, because someone had enchanted them and enchantments kept their promises longer than people kept anything.

And there, where a throne should have been, stood a door.

It was freestanding. That was the first absurdity — a door with no wall, no room behind it, planted on the dais like a garden trellis. It was made of pale wood gone silver with age, carved all over with waves and gulls and small running figures, and through the gap beneath it poured light the color of lightning held very still.

Sable hung in the water ten feet away and laughed, actually laughed, a broken hiccup of a sound.

"A door," she said. "Twenty years I've been looking for a way out of a country that doesn't exist, and it's been sitting here under the harbor like an old coat on a hook." Her voice cracked. "You could have put up a sign."

The door said nothing, being a door, but the light beneath it pulsed once, gently, the way Odile used to tap the table twice when she wanted Sable to sit down and stop pretending she wasn't worried.

Sable swam closer. Her legs shook — a ridiculous thing, shaking legs, when nothing below her knees was doing any actual work — and she stopped at the threshold's edge, close enough to feel warmth roll off the wood like bread from an oven.

And then it showed her.

* * *

Not with words. Prophecies, she would learn later, rarely bothered with words; they were show-offs that preferred pictures.

She saw Salt Anew whole. Not drowned — *risen*, streets climbing out of the green water tile by tile, towers unfurling like ferns, the harbor bells ringing dry for the first time in a century. She saw the scattered people — the charm-sellers and dockworkers and quiet luck-bringers of Vellum, the ones who'd changed their names three generations back and taught their children not to hum certain songs — turning in the street as memory flooded home into them like tide into a dry channel. She saw them weep. She saw them come home.

And she saw herself at the center of it, and this was the part that frightened her, truly frightened her, more than Ministry hunters or drowning or grief:

She saw herself *happy*. Effortlessly happy. Standing tall in storm-grey robes with lightning braided through her hair like a crown, and her face bore no lines from squinting at ledgers, no scar from the time she'd dropped a soldering iron on her wrist in the shop, no expression at all, really, beyond serene purpose. The woman in the vision was beautiful the way statues are beautiful. She was the last storm-caller of the royal line of Salt Anew, and she had never sold a luck-charm to a nervous bride, never burned a pot of tea while reading, never spent an evening arguing with Odile about whether the crew's share of a smuggling run counted as *wages* or *adventure money* (it was wages; Odile was wrong; the argument was one of Sable's favorite things in the world).

All it would cost was everything she was.

She understood the terms without being told, the way you understand the terms of your own funeral. The door required a storm-caller of the line, freely given, wholly given. Not her body. Her *self*. Sable Delacroix, exiled daughter, bad cook, worse singer, keeper of a cramped little shop on Tallow Lane where the third floorboard squeaked and she'd never fixed it because the squeak meant *home* — that woman would step through and be unmade, politely, completely, and what walked out the other side would be the storm the prophecy ordered. Clean. Heroic. Certain.

"You're offering me a promotion," Sable said to the door, and her voice wobbled horribly. "In exchange for firing me."

The light pulsed. Patient. Kind, even, in the way of large impersonal forces.

"And if I say no?" she asked. "If I just swim back up and let Vellum keep its gray little coast and its gray little Ministry? What happens to all of them? To the shopkeepers who don't know why they flinch when someone says their old family names?"

The vision shifted. She saw Vellum as it might stay — the diaspora still scattered, still nameless, still humming forbidden songs only when the shutters were closed. Still waiting. Forever.

"Oh," Sable said softly. "You're not giving me a choice at all, are you. You're just letting me pick which guilt to carry."

The door, to its credit, didn't pretend otherwise.

She drifted closer. Put one hand flat against the silvered wood. It was warm — blood-warm, skin-warm — and through it she felt the stored centuries of her nation pressing back against the other side: every lullaby, every harvest song, every argument ever had about wages versus adventure money. All of it saved. All of it hers, if she would stop being herself to hold it.

And the terrible thing — the thing she would never tell anyone, if anyone ever asked — was that some tired, drowning corner of her wanted to say yes.

Twenty years of hiding was heavy. Twenty years of flinching at boots on cobblestones, of swallowing magic until it tasted like swallowed thunder, of being *quiet luck-bringer* instead of *last of her line* — a clean identity sounded like rest. Like putting down a sack she'd carried so long she'd forgotten her shoulders were supposed to be straight. Let the storm have the burden. Let the storm have the crown and the certainty and the beautiful effortless face. Sable Delacroix could finally, finally stop.

Her grandmother's memories stirred in her, borrowed and vast, and for one vertiginous moment she almost couldn't find the edges of herself — couldn't remember which griefs were hers and which were inherited, which name fit.

Then, from somewhere impossibly far above, through a hundred feet of darkening water, she heard it: bells. Harbor bells. And beneath them, thin and distorted, the unmistakable drone of Ministry horns sealing the bay.

The thirteenth turn had begun.

* * *

Sable took her hand off the door.

"Here's my problem with you," she said conversationally, because talking to ancient artifacts was apparently a thing she did now, and honestly, alone in a drowned palace, a girl needed hobbies. "You want the best parts of me and none of the rest. The bloodline without the burnt tea. The crown without the squeaky floorboard. But that's not how people work. I'm not a sword somebody forged and left in a lake for a hero to find. I'm a *person*. I'm a badly assembled person with a limp inventory and a crush on an archivist and a captain bleeding somewhere up there because of me, and every single embarrassing piece of that is mine, and I am not—" her voice rose, and the water around her began to tremble, rings of pressure shivering outward from her chest, "—I am not going to trade it for a *costume*."

The light under the door flared, almost offended. Above, the horns sounded again, closer now, and far off across the black harbor she could see lights — many lights — moving toward the sunken palace. Ministry boats. And one boat cutting ahead of the rest, recklessly fast, with someone at its prow waving both arms like a man trying to flag down a coach.

She knew that reckless rowing. She knew those flailing arms.

"Not yet," she told the door, and found, to her enormous surprise, that she meant it in a way she hadn't expected: not *not ever*, but *not like this, not on your terms*. There was a third thing. There was always a third thing; Odile said so constantly, usually right before committing a crime. "Keep your light on. We're not finished, you and I. But nobody walks through you tonight — not the storm, and not me."

The water around her churned. Far above, the moon was rising on its thirteenth turn, and the prophecy's voice was gathering in the deep like a held note, and the Door's light spilled across her face, patient and golden and terribly sure it would win.

Sable Delacroix squared her shoulders, turned her back on salvation, and started swimming upward — toward the horns, the hunters, and the idiot waving his arms.
