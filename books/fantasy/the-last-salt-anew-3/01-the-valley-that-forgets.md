## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ledgers of Vess smelled of salt and tallow, and Theo Iversen had spent eleven years learning to love that smell, because loving it meant she could stay.

She sat in the scriptorium's back corner with her sleeves pushed up and her stylus moving in the small, even strokes the temple prized. Around her, nine other scribes bent over their work. None of them looked at her. That was the arrangement. In Vess, you did not look too closely at anyone, because looking closely invited questions, and questions were how people vanished.

Outside the narrow window, the salt flats stretched white to the horizon, blinding under the late-autumn sun. Magic could not cross them. Everyone said so the way they said the sun rose — as fact, as comfort, as law. The flats kept Vess pure. The flats kept Vess safe.

Theo was the flaw in that sentence.

Her gift was small, and she kept it smaller. A candle flame steadied when a draft threatened it. An inkwell never ran dry mid-word. A fever broke a night early in the child of a neighbor who'd once shared bread with her family. Nothing anyone could name. Nothing anyone could prove. She traded these small mercies for silence, and the silence held, year after year, like a wall she built one brick at a time and never let herself lean against.

*Stay small,* her mother had told her, the night before the hunters took her mother away. *Small things don't cast shadows.*

Theo pressed the stylus harder into the ledger and wrote: *Grain stores, third quarter, 340 sacks.*

* * *

Maren found her at the evening meal, sliding onto the bench beside her with the particular brightness that meant something was wrong. Maren Halvard had been Theo's friend since they were six, since the day Maren had defended her from boys who mocked her patched clothes, and in all those years Maren had never once asked why Theo's soup was always warm or why her headaches always faded when she touched her own temples.

That was the friendship. Not asking.

"You heard," Maren said quietly. "They came through the pass this morning."

"I heard."

Crown hunters. Three of them, riding horses too fine for the valley road, wearing the king's silver stag on their collars. They'd taken rooms at the Wayfarer's Rest and posted a notice on the temple door: *By decree of His Majesty, all persons touched by the old magic must present themselves for assessment. Sheltering such persons is treason.*

"Assessment," Theo repeated. The word tasted like ash.

"They won't find anyone here." Maren's hand found hers on the bench between them, squeezing hard. "There's no one in Vess but honest people. Salt people." She said it like a prayer, like a promise, and Theo understood that Maren needed it to be true more than she needed it to be spoken kindly.

"Maren—"

"My father saw them reading names off a list. Names from the capital. People who fled south years ago." Her voice dropped. "One of the names was Iversen."

The scriptorium noise went distant. Theo's own heartbeat sounded loud in her ears.

"That's not possible. My mother died on the road. They said—"

"They said what they say about everyone who doesn't come back." Maren turned to face her fully, and her eyes were wet and terrified and sorry all at once, and Theo felt the first cold thread of understanding slide down her spine. "Theo. If they're checking names against faces. If someone describes your mother to them—"

"Then I leave tonight. Quietly. Before dawn."

"And go where? Across the flats? Alone?" Maren laughed, a broken sound. "You can't cross the flats alone. Nobody crosses the flats alone."

"People have."

"People have *died*, is what people have done." Maren gripped her hands tighter. "Stay. Hide deeper. You've hidden eleven years — hide eleven more. They'll ride on. Hunters always ride on."

Theo wanted to believe her. She wanted it so badly her chest ached with it. Stay small. Stay quiet. Let the wall hold one more winter.

But she thought of the notice on the temple door, and the list of names, and the way the hunters' horses had stood in the temple yard that afternoon, steam rising off their flanks, patient as death.

*Small things don't cast shadows.* Her mother had been very small indeed, at the end.

"I'll think on it," Theo said, and squeezed Maren's hand back, and lied to her friend for the first time in fifteen years.

* * *

She did not think on it. She packed.

A satchel: two shirts, dried fish, the good knife, her mother's ring worn on a cord under her collar where no one would see it. Coin from eleven years of skimming a copper here, a copper there, from ledger lines only she would ever read. She left the coin pouch half-full on the pallet of the widow Sorel, whose cough she'd been quietly easing all autumn. Payment, maybe. Apology. She didn't examine which.

At midnight she stood in the scriptorium one last time, running her fingers along the ledgers she'd filled. Eleven years of grain counts and birth records and deaths, all in her careful hand. Somewhere in those pages was every small mercy she'd ever worked, invisible, unproven, deniable.

She blew out the candle without magic, the ordinary way, and walked out into the dark.

Maren was waiting at the edge of the temple yard.

Theo stopped. For one absurd moment she thought — hoped — that Maren had come to help, to walk her to the flats' edge, to stand watch while she slipped away. Fifteen years of friendship reached toward that hope like a hand toward a rail.

"Theo." Maren's voice cracked. Behind her, at the far end of the yard, three figures stood by the well, and lantern light caught the silver stag on a collar. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. They came to our house. They knew my brother took grain he didn't declare — they'd have taken him, taken my father, and I thought if I gave them something true, something real, they'd—" Her words collapsed. "They said a name given freely buys mercy. Please. Please understand."

Theo understood. That was the worst part. She understood completely, instantly, the arithmetic of terror, the way fear made people trade other people's lives like coins. She understood, and it shattered her anyway, cleanly, like ice underfoot.

"You didn't even ask me," Theo said. "Fifteen years, and you sold me before you warned me."

"There wasn't time—"

"There's never time. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? Staying small doesn't keep you safe. It just means there's less of you to bury."

One of the hunters started forward. Theo did not wait for the rest.

She ran.

* * *

The flats at night were a country of the moon. White on white, the crust crackling under her boots, the temple lights shrinking behind her until they were a smudge, then a memory. The cold came up through the soles of her feet like the valley itself objecting to her leaving.

She ran until her lungs burned, then walked, then ran again. Behind her, faint and fading, voices shouted across the salt. The hunters had horses. Horses were faster. But horses hated the flats — the crust broke under hooves, and every rider in Vess knew the brine pits that swallowed livestock whole. Theo knew them better than anyone. She'd memorized the paths years ago, tracing them in ink in a private map she'd burned the same night she drew it.

So she ran the thin ways, the safe ways, the secret ways, alone under the stars.

And then the snow began.

Theo stopped dead.

Snow did not fall in Vess. Snow had not fallen in Vess in living memory — the flats drank every cloud dry before a single flake could form. Every child learned it. *The salt refuses the sky.* It was the oldest fact of home, older than the temple, older than the kingdom.

But the snow fell anyway, fat and slow and wrong, catching in her hair, hissing faintly where it struck the salt crust. Each flake melted with a tiny sound, like whispers. Like counting.

She looked north, past the flats, toward the distant dark line of the mountains and whatever lay beyond them, and the snow thickened in that direction, falling in curtains, as though the whole horizon were dissolving.

Something was wrong with the world. Something far larger than crown hunters, far larger than a betrayed friendship, far larger than a scribe who'd spent her life being small.

Behind her, hoofbeats on the crust. Ahead, an impossible winter swallowing the sky.

Theo pulled her mother's ring out from under her collar, kissed it once, and ran north into the snow.
