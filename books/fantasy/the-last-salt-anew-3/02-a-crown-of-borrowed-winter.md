## 2. A Crown of Borrowed Winter

The horseman caught her at dusk on the third day.

Theo heard him before she saw him — the crunch of hooves on crusted snow going wrong, too heavy for a deer, too steady for the wind. She ducked behind a leaning waystone at the edge of the flats' last reach and put her hand against the stone and did the thing she'd done all her life in small, deniable ways: she pushed. Not much. A suggestion of cold, of nothing-here-to-see, folded into the air around her like a sheet drawn over furniture.

The rider drew up ten feet away. A woman, broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair cropped short, wearing a hunt-captain's cloak with the captain's clasp torn off. The empty spot where the clasp had been was the first thing Theo noticed; the sword was the second; the exhaustion in the woman's face was the third, and it was the exhaustion that made her stay hidden instead of running.

"You can stop that," the woman said. "Whatever you're doing to the stone. I'm not a hunter anymore."

Theo didn't move. Snow gathered on her shoulders.

"I said—" The woman sighed and dismounted, deliberately slow, hands wide. "Three days I've tracked you across the flats. Do you know how hard that is? You leave no tracks. You're a smear in the snow, a wrongness. That's why I know what you are." She crouched, forearms on knees, eye-level with the shadow behind the waystone. "My name is Captain — no. Was Captain Signe Vahl. Two weeks ago I refused an order to burn a village because three children in it glowed when they cried. They stripped my rank and named me traitor. So." She spread her hands. "Here we both are, hiding in the same snow."

Theo let the veil thin. Just enough.

"Why track me?" Her voice came out rusted from disuse.

"Because the king is dying," Signe said, "and the winter is killing everything between here and the capital, and I have heard — rumor, drunk talk, physician's gossip — that somewhere out here is a scribe who can do things with cold and quiet that no one should be able to do." She stood. "I don't need a weapon. I need a miracle with legs."

Theo thought of Maren's face in the temple doorway. *Forgive me, Theo. Please.* She thought of staying small, and where it had gotten her.

"Your miracle has legs," she said, and stepped out of the snow.

* * *

They were two days into the hill country when they found the child.

She was sitting in the middle of the road, alone, in the exact center of a circle where no snow fell. Twenty feet of bare brown earth around her, white world beyond, as if winter had drawn back from her like a hand from a stove. She couldn't have been more than eight. She had mud on her chin and terror in her eyes and when Theo approached, the girl flinched and the circle widened.

"Easy," Theo said. She knelt at the edge of the bare ground and, slowly, deliberately, let her own gift show — frost blooming from her fingertips, delicate as fernwork, harmless as breath on glass. The girl stared. Then, tentatively, she raised one small mittened hand, and frost answered her, matching Theo's pattern fern for fern.

The child opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She tried again, frustration crumpling her face, and then pointed north — urgently, twice — and made a shape with her hands: a crown. Then she wrapped her arms around herself and shivered theatrically, and pointed at the crown-shape again.

"She's mute," Signe said quietly. "And she's telling us where the winter comes from."

Theo took off her own cloak and held it out. After a long moment the girl shuffled forward across her own bare ground and let herself be bundled into it. She weighed nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

"What do we call you?" Theo asked.

The girl considered, then pressed two fingers to Theo's ring finger — to her mother's ring — and then to her own chest. Borrowing. *Same as you,* the gesture said.

"Runa," Theo decided. "You look like a Runa."

Runa considered this too, and nodded once, gravely, like a queen accepting terms.

* * *

The capital of Aldis had never seen snow like this.

It lay in the streets in drifts taller than men. It sealed doors. It sat on rooftops in slabs that groaned under their own weight, and the people who still moved through the city moved hunched and quick, eyes down, as if the sky itself had become something not to look at. In the great square before the palace, Theo counted nine gallows. Seven of them were occupied. None of the bodies had been cut down, and none wore the marks of ordinary crime — their hands were frosted white, every one, frost blooming from their fingertips even in death.

"They're not executing criminals," Theo whispered.

"No," said a voice behind them. "They're executing symptoms."

He was a small man, round-faced, dressed in a physician's robes with the court insignia burned away — burned, not torn, a clean deliberate wound in the cloth. He looked at Runa's bare-circle ground, now dusted with new snow, and at Theo's face, and he did not seem surprised by either.

"Doctor Emeric Voss," he said. "Formerly His Majesty's personal physician. Currently a man very much hoping you're who I think you are." He glanced up at the gallows, then at the palace rising black and glittering behind them. "Walk with me. Quickly. And whatever you do, don't stare at the windows."

"Why not?"

"Because the king watches from the windows," Voss said. "And the king sees more than a king should."

* * *

In the physician's buried rooms beneath the old infirmary — rooms heated by no fire Theo could find, though they were warm — Voss laid it out with the terrible calm of a man who had rehearsed his own damnation many times.

"The king is twenty-three years old," he said. "Aldric. He liked dogs and hated fish and laughed too loudly at his own jokes. I tell you those details because none of them remain. What sits on the throne now wears his body the way that crown sits on its head. Loosely."

"The entity," Theo said. "In the crown."

Voss's eyebrows rose. "You know of it?"

"I've met its work." She told him about the flats, the magic that wouldn't cross them, the binding so old the valley had forgotten it was a binding at all. She watched understanding move through him like weather.

"Then you understand better than our ancestors did," he said. "The crown was forged to hold something. Something that eats will. It fed for centuries on the bearer — slowly, politely, a sip at a time. But Aldric was crowned young, and crowned afraid, and fear is a door." He poured three cups of something hot and bitter and pushed them across the table; Runa cupped hers in both hands and breathed the steam. "Now it isn't sipping. Now it drinks. And everything it takes, it spends. The winter isn't the entity's malice. It's the entity's *appetite*, leaking. Every scrap of warmth and green and living summer in the kingdom, drawn north to feed the thing in the crown while it finishes digesting my king."

"And the executions?" Signe asked.

"Anyone whose gift flickers near the palace causes it pain. It has taught the court to call gifted folk traitors. It is, I confess, an efficient piece of statecraft." He set down his cup. "Which brings us to the part you won't like."

"There's a part I'll like?"

"Unbinding." Voss leaned forward. "The binding on the flats was cast by someone willing — that's why it holds. Willing bonds can only be unbound by willing bearers. To crack what's in that crown, someone with the gift must stand before it, open themselves completely, and speak the truth of the binding aloud — all of it, unhidden, in front of the entity and everyone it commands. The gift must be offered. Voluntarily. Fully." He paused. "The entity will offer them anything to stop. Anything. And most who try—"

"Die," Signe said.

"Worse," Voss said gently. "Most who try *accept*. It doesn't kill bearers, Captain. It hires them."

The room went quiet. Runa looked at Theo, and Theo looked at the fireless warmth, and understood suddenly where it came from: the physician's rooms sat directly beneath the throne room, and warmth leaked downward through the stones like blood through bandage.

"When?" she asked.

"The solstice feast," Voss said. "Nine days. Every lord in the kingdom assembled, the court at its most crowded, the entity at the height of its feeding." He met her eyes. "There will never be another moment when so many people see the truth spoken at once. And there will never be a worse one."

Signe swore softly. Runa reached across the table and touched Theo's mother's ring with one fingertip.

Theo turned the ring once around her finger and found she was already answering.

"Nine days," she said. "Teach me the throne room."
