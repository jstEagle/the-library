## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There were four hundred and six impossible things in the vaults of the Guild of Impossible Things, and Marguerite Vasquez had counted every one of them twice.

He counted a third time because the numbers had stopped agreeing with him.

"Again," he said to the ledger, as if it might apologize. It didn't. Ledgers never did. That was the whole point of them — ink did not flatter, ink did not negotiate, and in eleven years as the guild's keeper of accounts he had built his entire reputation on the simple, brutal honesty of a well-kept book.

The book was lying to him now.

Item 212: *one bottled thunderhead, vintage of the Sundering, unopened.* Present. He could see it on its shelf, sweating condensation that smelled like a summer that had died before he was born. The ledger agreed. Good.

Item 213: *seven keys to doors that no longer exist.* Present. Agreed.

Item 214: *the last honest mirror in the country.* Present. It showed him exactly what he expected: a thin man of indeterminate middle age, grey shot through black hair cropped practical, hands ink-stained at the cuticles no matter how he scrubbed. A face like an accountant's. A face nobody remembered at parties, which had always suited him fine.

He made his tally mark and moved down the row, boots quiet on stone, lantern swinging from its hook above. The vaults ran three levels beneath the guildhall, older than the guild itself, older — some said — than the country, though Marguerite had learned long ago not to put much faith in what people said about age. People exaggerated their own histories the way merchants rounded up profits.

Item 215: *one winter, folded small.*

That one always unsettled him. It sat in a cedar chest the size of a coffin, and even through the lid you could feel it — a cold with intention behind it, patient as a creditor. He checked the seals. Intact. He wrote the mark. Agreed.

Everything agreed. Which was, he realized somewhere around item three hundred, precisely the problem.

Because when he'd done this inventory a week ago, there had been four hundred and seven items.

He stood very still in the lamplight and let the arithmetic settle over him like snowfall. One missing thing. No gap on any shelf, no empty crate, no torn label. Just a number that had quietly lost a digit's worth of truth between then and now.

Marguerite did what he always did with a discrepancy. He did not panic. Panic was for people whose lives were not already ruined once; he had used up his lifetime supply of panic in another city, under other stars, in a life he kept in a locked drawer at the back of his mind and did not open. Instead he took out his pen, uncapped it, and wrote in the margin of the ledger, in his own small precise hand:

*Discrepancy of one (1). Nature unknown. Investigation warranted.*

Then the stars went out.

He felt it before he understood it — a pressure change, like the moment before lightning, except inverted, sucked inward, the world inhaling instead of exhaling. The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no draft. And through the high ventilation shafts that let air down into the vaults, where there should have been nothing but night, there came a light — faint, silver, wrong — and then the absence of it, total and immediate, like a lid closing over the sky.

Marguerite climbed the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The guildhall's reading room occupied the top floor, and it was built the way the founders had built everything: ostentatiously, defensively, and with more windows than any sensible building required. Nine great arched windows faced the cardinal points and the points between them, because the founders had believed — with the particular arrogance of people who invent institutions — that impossible things deserved to be watched from every angle.

Through those windows, the night sky was blank.

Not clouded. Not overcast. Blank. Marguerite pressed his palm flat against the cold glass and looked up at a darkness so complete it had texture, like velvet laid across the world, and where the constellations should have been — the Lantern-Bearer, the Weeping Sisters, the Salt Road arcing from horizon to horizon — there was nothing. Not even the memory of them. His eyes searched for familiar patterns and found only smooth dark, the way a mouth is dark after the tooth beneath it is pulled.

Below him, the city of Vell noticed all at once. He heard it happen: doors opening, voices rising, a dog somewhere beginning to bark with the frantic rhythm of an animal that has decided the world is ending and intends to say so.

"Marguerite."

Guildmaster Odile Vasse stood in the doorway of the reading room, and Marguerite took his measure of her the way he took his measure of everything — quickly, quietly, without being seen doing it. She was sixty and carried it like a weapon. Silver hair bound tight, a face that had spent decades deciding things, and eyes that were doing something now they almost never did.

They were afraid.

"You saw," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The stars are gone." He kept his voice level. Practiced calm was a skill like any other, and he had drilled it the way soldiers drill swordwork. "All of them. There's no cloud cover. I checked the western window first — the horizon line is clean."

"Yes." Odile crossed to the central table, where the guild's duty roster lay half-finished in someone else's handwriting. Her fingers spread flat on the wood. "The wards along the border went down ninety seconds after the sky changed. Every one of them. North, south, east, the sea-wards off the Salt Coast. All at once. Simultaneously."

Marguerite felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like a body shifting in shallow water. "Simultaneously isn't failure," he said. "Wards fail locally. Storms take them out in stretches. Even sabotage takes them out in sequence — you have to walk the line or work the anchor-stones one by one."

"I know how wards fail, Keeper."

"All of them at once means one thing took them down. One cause. One—" He stopped himself, but Odile finished it anyway.

"One hand," she said.

Somewhere below them, deep in the vaults, a sound began.

It started small — a high, crystalline note, like a wineglass singing at the edge of shattering — and then it multiplied, caught, spread, became a chorus of that same note in ten thousand registers, rising, and Marguerite understood with a lurch what he was hearing because he had inventoried the components of the border wards himself, years ago, entry by entry: *nine hundred panes of wardglass, each attuned to one degree of the country's boundary, each resonant with the true name of the nation they protected.*

The wards weren't falling silently. They were breaking glass.

The sound swelled until the windows hummed in sympathy, until the inkwell on the duty table shivered rings into its own surface, until Marguerite's teeth ached with it. Then, all together, the notes dropped — a full octave down, a groan, a dying chord — and stopped.

Silence rushed in behind the sound like water filling a hole.

In the dark sky outside, where the stars should have been, Marguerite thought he saw — for just a moment, in the corner of his vision, gone when he turned to look — a shape. Something vast moving against the nothing, unhurried, the way a hand moves across a page.

Odile's voice came out stripped of all its authority, down to the woman underneath. "The country is open."

* * *

By the time Marguerite reached the ground floor, the guild was sealing itself.

He watched the order move through the hall like weather. Enforcers in grey livery barred the great doors — actual oak beams, iron-shod, relics of a founding paranoia everyone had laughed about for a century. Lamplighters snuffed the windows-facing lamps. The archivists came up from their warrens blinking, clutching whatever they'd been holding, and were herded toward the interior rooms with the brisk gentleness of shepherds who have seen wolves before.

And through it all, the members of the inner circle gathered at the base of the great staircase, and Marguerite counted them the way he counted everything, because counting was the only prayer he trusted.

Deputy Corvane, tall and silver-templed, issuing instructions in a voice pitched to carry — calm, competent, everywhere at once, the way he always was. Marguerite had audited Corvane's expense ledgers for nine years and never found so much as a rounding error, which either meant the man was honest or the most dangerous kind of careful.

Master Yeva of the Wards, grey-faced, her hands still smoking faintly from whatever she'd done trying to reach the border anchors before they died.

The twins, Osk and Anselm, who kept the menagerie of impossible animals and looked, for once, like brothers rather than rivals.

And Odile, descending the staircase slowly, having paused upstairs just long enough to rebuild herself into the Guildmaster the country needed. When she reached the bottom step, the fear was gone from her face. Completely gone. Marguerite knew it had been there twenty minutes ago. He also knew he could find no trace of it now, and something about the completeness of that erasure troubled him in a way he couldn't yet name.

"Listen to me," Odile said, and the hall went quiet the way a courtroom goes quiet. "You will hear rumors tonight and worse tomorrow. So you will hear it from me first. At moonrise, every ward on our borders failed at once. The stars are extinguished — not hidden, *extinguished*, and Master Yeva confirms the sky beyond our own wards shows the same. Whatever did this, it did not do it from outside. Nothing can cross our border unless we let it, or unless—"

She stopped. Just for a breath. Just long enough.

"Unless it was already inside," Corvane finished, smoothly, and Marguerite watched a dozen faces in the crowd go pale at once.

"That is speculation," Odile said, with a glance at her deputy that Marguerite would later spend many sleepless hours reinterpreting, "and speculation is not guild business. Guild business is this: the doors are sealed. No one enters, no one leaves, until we understand what we are protecting and from whom. Rations at half-draw. The vaults go to double-watch. And the ledgers—"

Her eyes found Marguerite across the crowded hall.

"—the ledgers will be reconciled tonight. Everything accounted for. If something left this building, if anything was taken, sold, moved, or misplaced, I want to know before dawn. Do I make myself clear?"

"Clear, Guildmaster," Marguerite said.

But as the circle broke apart and the guild dispersed to its tasks, Odile caught his sleeve, drew him aside beneath the staircase, into the shadow where the portraits of dead guildmasters watched with painted indifference.

"A word, Keeper, and only a word." Her voice was low, pitched beneath the noise of the hall. "When you reconcile the books tonight — start with the acquisitions ledger. The old one. The deep one."

"The deep ledger hasn't been touched in years. Nothing's been acquired worth recording since—"

"Since before your time. Yes." Her grip tightened on his sleeve, and for a moment the mask slipped again, just at the edges, the way ice slips at the edge of a river. "I have been Guildmaster for nineteen years, Marguerite, and I am telling you that tonight, when the sky went dark, my first thought — my *first* thought, before the wards, before the city, before any of it — was of a transaction I have spent nineteen years pretending I never read. Reconcile the books. Find me the lie. Because if there is a traitor in this guild, they did not begin tonight."

She released his sleeve and stepped back into the light, and her face was smooth and commanding again, and she was giving orders to the enforcers before she'd taken her third step away.

Marguerite stood beneath the staircase a moment longer.

Outside the high windows, the starless dark pressed against the glass like something reading over the city's shoulder. Somewhere beyond the borders, in the open country, the winter that would not end for anyone was already gathering. He didn't know that yet. What he knew was smaller and sharper: one missing item in his count, one transaction in a ledger he'd never opened, and a Guildmaster who was afraid of her own memories.

Practiced calm. He put it on like a coat, buttoned it to the throat, and went down into the vaults to get the deep ledger — and to learn, before the night was out, that his own handwriting was waiting for him inside.

Four hundred and six impossible things.

He was about to discover that he himself was one of them.
