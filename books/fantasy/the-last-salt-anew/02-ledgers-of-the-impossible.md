## 2. Ledgers of the Impossible

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in the Vault of Small Hours, a room that existed slightly before dawn no matter what time you entered it. Marguerite had always found this convenient. Numbers, like grief, were best faced in the dark.

He carried three lamps and a pot of black tea gone cold twice over, and he had not slept since the stars went out. Sleep was for people whose countries stayed put overnight.

The ledgers lived on shelves cut into stone older than the guild itself—stone that had been part of something else once, though no one could say what. Marguerite pulled the first volume down with hands that only shook a little. *Inventory, Impossible Goods, Year of the Long Light.* He set it on the reading table, squared its spine to the table's edge, and opened it.

Order was a kind of prayer. He had learned that much in exile.

* * *

For six hours he did nothing but read.

This was the work nobody thanked you for: the audit after catastrophe, when every impossible thing in the guild's keeping had to be counted and confirmed against paper, because when the world broke, the first question anyone important asked was *what did we lose, and can we bill someone for it.*

Marguerite Vasquez was very good at questions like that.

He worked through the manifest item by item, ticking each entry against its physical verification slip. The bottled thunderstorm, third shelf, temperamental—present. The left hand of a clockmaker who could stop time, pickled and still twitching—present, still twitching, which was either reassuring or deeply not. The door that only opened for liars. The winter that fit in a teacup. Seven hundred and forty-one entries, each one catalogued in his own neat, slanted hand, each one accounted for.

The stars went out at midnight. By noon he had confirmed that nothing on the guild's books had escaped, been stolen, or died.

Which meant the loss hadn't come from inside the vault.

It had come from inside the ledger.

* * *

Here is what most people never understood about impossible goods: they were not objects. Not really. A bottled thunderstorm was a storm that had agreed, once, under specific terms, to be held. An unlit candle that burned backwards was a bargain between the candle and the concept of time. Every item in the vault ran on contract, and every contract ran on language, and all of that language—the terms, the clauses, the names—was written down.

Names especially. Names above all.

A country, for instance, had a true name. Not the word on maps, not the sound people made with their mouths, but the thing underneath—the name the land answered to, the name the border wards spoke when they sealed themselves shut at dusk. Nobody wrote that name down. That was the whole point. It lived in the collective keeping of everyone who remembered it, passed mouth to mouth down generations, too widely held to steal.

Unless, said a small cold voice in Marguerite's head, someone found a way to make it into an object. A transaction. A line in a book.

He turned pages faster now, tea forgotten, lamplight trembling as his elbow knocked the stand. He was looking for anomalies—ink that sat wrong on the page, entries out of chronological order, a hand that wavered where his hand never wavered.

He found the anomaly at the bottom of page four hundred and twelve.

* * *

The entry read:

**Received into escrow, per contract 9-Vhalcrest, payment rendered: one (1) country's true name. Condition of transfer: voluntary surrender by rightful keepers. Witnessed and recorded.**

There was no date. There was no counter-signature. And there was no memory of writing it.

Marguerite stared at the line for a long time—not because he couldn't read it, but because his mind kept sliding off it the way a boot slides off ice, again and again, refusing purchase. His eyes took in the words. His understanding declined to arrive.

Then he did the thing he had trained himself to do instead of panicking, which was to look at the handwriting.

Handwriting does not lie. Ink has habits; pressure has a signature of its own. He knew his own script the way he knew his own face in bad light—the slight forward lean of the letters, like a man walking into wind. The way his *y*s descended straight and then hooked left, impatient. The way he crossed his *t*s in a single motion without lifting the pen.

The entry on page four hundred and twelve leaned forward into wind. Its *y*s hooked left. Its *t*s were crossed in one motion.

It was his hand. Not similar. Not forged well. *His.*

"No," Marguerite said aloud, and the Small Hours swallowed the word without echo.

He checked the ink against his own stock—umber-black, iron-gall, mixed with a pinch of ash from burned correspondence, a recipe of his own devising that exactly two people alive knew, and one of them was him. He checked the nib-width against the pens in his own kit. He checked the page position: filed in proper sequence, in proper season, dated by implication to a night he could account for—or thought he could.

Everything about it was his. Except the remembering.

* * *

There are vertigos of the body, and then there are vertigos of the self, and the second kind is worse. The body's vertigo passes when you sit down. This one didn't.

Marguerite sat down anyway, because habit is a raft.

He tried to reconstruct the night the entry must have been made. He walked backward through his own days the way he'd walk back through a burning building, touching walls, counting doors. Three weeks ago: inventory of the eastern annex. Four weeks: the quartermaster's dispute over the teacup winter—he'd won, obviously. Five weeks:—

Nothing.

Five weeks ago was a smooth white wall in his memory, featureless as fresh snow. Not painful, not torn, not missing the way things are missing when they're lost. Missing the way things are missing when they were *removed*, cleanly, by someone who knew the craft.

Or by himself.

Because here was the thought he could not put down, the thought that came up through the floorboards of his mind like cold air: he was an exiled man. Exile had a price. Everyone in the trade knew that certain payments were taken in memory—you surrendered the deed along with everything that would let you undo the deed. He had come to the guild five years ago with excellent references, impeccable ledgers, and a past he described, always, in exactly four sentences, no more, as if reciting something rehearsed.

Four sentences. He'd never noticed until now that they never varied. Never noticed that he could not, if pressed, add a fifth.

Had he sold the country's name? Had he stood at this same table, dipped this same pen, and written those words in his own careful hand—and then paid the cost of the writing with the memory of the writing, so that the act vanished even as it was completed?

That was a transaction so clean it was almost beautiful. Sell a thing, forget the sale, keep your conscience spotless. The perfect crime committed by the perfect victim against himself.

"Unless I'm being framed," he said to the empty room, "by someone who knows my hand better than I do."

The Small Hours offered no opinion. The Small Hours never did.

* * *

He spent another hour doing the work, because the work was the only solid ground left. He traced contract number 9-Vhalcrest through the registry index. The index pointed to a drawer. The drawer contained a folder. The folder contained a single sheet of paper, blank on both sides, with a faint rectangular ghost where something had lain against it long enough to leave a shadow of itself.

The contract was gone. Torn out cleanly—no ragged edge, no haste. Whoever removed it had taken their time and known exactly which page.

But the ghost of the rectangle remained, and ghosts leave details. Marguerite held it up to the lamp, angled it, breathed on it the way old clerks do to raise what ink has abandoned. Along one edge, in indentation too shallow for any eye but a ledger-keeper's: the pressure-marks of a signature. Two lines of them. One he recognized with a lurch that felt like falling—

His own. Again. The lean-into-wind, the hooked *y*s.

And beneath it, overlapping, a second signature he could not read—but whose shape was long, angular, ending in a downward slash like a blade coming to rest.

Someone had signed below him. Someone had countersigned the end of the world, and his own hand had gone first.

* * *

Marguerite closed the ledger. Squared its spine. Set it precisely in the center of the table, because order was a prayer, and right now he needed all the praying he could get.

Then he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding. If the name had been transferred—if Vhalcrest, whoever and whatever they were, held the country's true name in escrow—then the border wards hadn't failed. They had been *released*. Sold open. The stars going out wasn't a symptom; it was a receipt. The sky itself had been marked *paid*.

And the winter that was surely coming—the winter he could already feel gathering beyond the guild's sealed doors like breath held before a scream—that wasn't weather. That was the country beginning to forget itself. A land without its name could not remember what it was for. Snow would fall because snow was what fell on places that had stopped being anywhere.

All of it flowing from one line of ink. His ink.

He thought of Odile upstairs, convening the inner circle within the hour, asking her hard bright questions, trusting—as she had trusted for five years—the one member of her guild whose books had never once been wrong. He thought of her face when she learned that the guild's most reliable keeper might be the hole in its heart. He thought of telling her, and could not decide which was worse: that she might believe him guilty, or that she might believe him innocent, when he could no longer swear to it himself.

What do you trust, when your own hand testifies against you and your own memory testifies for you, and both of them might be lying?

You trust the paper. Paper remembers what flesh is made to forget.

Marguerite lifted the ledger again and turned, slowly, deliberately, through every page from cover to cover, hunting for anything else written in the style of himself that he could not recall. Twice he paused. Twice he found entries—small ones, mundane, the receipt of a minor impossibility, the settlement of a petty debt—that rang faintly false, like a note played just shy of pitch. He flagged them with strips of blue paper, eleven flags total, a little white fence of them standing up from the pages.

Eleven moments of his life that belonged to someone else's design.

Above him, muffled through floors of old stone, he heard the bell that called the inner circle to council. The sound came down the stairwell like water finding cracks, and Marguerite understood that the next hour would be full of faces, and that one of the faces around Odile's table would belong to a person whose signature ended in a blade's rest—and that every one of them would be watching him, the exiled storm-caller, the man who kept the books, wondering what he knew.

They should wonder. He wondered it himself.

He banked the lamps, tucked the ledger under his arm like a shield, and started up toward the council chamber—past the shelf, past the drawer with its ghosted rectangle, carrying page four hundred and twelve folded against his chest like a letter from a stranger with his handwriting and no return address.

Whatever he had done or failed to do, whatever had been taken from him or by him, there was one thing the vertigo could not shake loose, one fact that held steady as a keel in rough water:

Somewhere in this guild, tonight, someone had signed away a country.

And they were about to sit down to tea with him.
