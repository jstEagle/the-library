## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

Odile Vasseur had buried three husbands and one guild, and she knew the difference between grief and betrayal. Grief came like weather. Betrayal came like a knife you'd sharpened yourself.

She stood at the head of the long table in the Council Room, watching her inner circle file in through doors that had been sealed with salt-iron since midnight, and she counted them the way a woman counts her own pulse. Nine faces. Nine people who had sworn the Oath of Custody on their own true names. Any one of them could have sold a country.

The stars were still gone. Through the high windows, the sky was a flat black nothing, like ink spilled across silk, and it made her think of blank pages — which made her think of ledgers — which made her want to put her fist through the glass.

Instead, she folded her hands.

"Sit," she said.

They sat. Corvane first, as always, taking the chair at her right hand with the smooth efficiency of a man who had been rehearsing for that seat his whole life. He wore grey today, grey like the sky over Vhalcrest, and Odile noted that too, filed it away in the drawer where she kept things she wasn't ready to look at. Marguerite took the far end, as always, close to the door. She'd never decided whether that was caution or escape instinct or simply a storm-caller's habit of keeping his back against something solid.

"Report," she said.

Corvane spoke first, because Corvane always spoke first. "The border wards fell at moonrise. All of them, simultaneously. Not breached — *fell*. That requires either force we don't possess or—" he paused, delicately, a man setting out fine china, "—the true name."

"Which is held by exactly one institution in this country," Odile said.

"Which is held by us," Corvane agreed. "So either someone stole it from us, or someone gave it away. The first is a catastrophe. The second is treason."

The word hung in the air like smoke. Odile watched their faces, all nine, the way she'd once watched card players in the smuggler dens of Sableport: not for what they said, but for what they failed to say. Hespera the archivist looked down at her notes. Old Tammas, who had run the vaults for forty years, sat perfectly still in the way of a man who had learned that stillness was safety. Young Pell, the ward-smith, couldn't stop glancing at the black window, and his hands shook — but Pell's hands always shook; fear was simply how he metabolized air.

And Marguerite. Marguerite watched *her*.

That was the thing about him. Everyone else in this room was looking at the table, at their hands, at the impossible dark outside. Marguerite was looking at her with those pale eyes, calm as deep water, and Odile had the sudden vertiginous certainty that whatever lie she was about to tell, he would see the seams in it.

Good, she thought. Let him look closely. Maybe he'll find what I can't.

"Here is what I know," she said aloud. "Last night, at approximately the same hour, three things happened. The stars went out. The wards fell. And an entry appeared in our own acquisition ledger recording the sale of an item that should not exist outside these walls." She let the silence stretch. "I have read that entry myself. The handwriting on it is flawless."

"It's a forgery," Tammas said. "It must be. No one could—"

"The handwriting is *flawless*, Tammas. That is precisely what frightens me. A forgery good enough to fool the ledger itself." The guild's books were not paper, not truly. They were bound in oath-leather and warded so that no entry could be written by any hand but its owner's. Whatever had been written in them last night had been written *as* Marguerite Vasquez, down to the pressure of the pen, down to the way his letters leaned.

Or it had been written by Marguerite Vasquez.

She did not say that part. She watched him instead, and he did not flinch, and somehow that was worse than flinching would have been.

"Then the traitor has access to the inner archive," Corvane said. "Which narrows it considerably." His gaze traveled around the table, unhurried, reasonable, landing nowhere — and that, too, Odile noted. A man genuinely hunting a traitor would look harder. Corvane looked the way a man looks when he already knows where the fox went to ground and wants everyone else to keep guessing.

"Does it?" Odile said lightly. "Names are strange goods, Corvane. You don't need to stand next to a name to steal it. You need only know it. And every soul in this room knows more names than they admit." She let her gaze rest, briefly, on each of them. "Including mine."

"Guildmaster," Hespera said carefully, "what do we tell the members? Two hundred guildsmen sealed inside, and the city already asking questions. They'll want to know if we're safe."

"We're safe," Odile lied.

It came out smooth as poured honey. She'd had practice. Twenty years of telling frightened people comforting falsehoods while she did the ugly arithmetic behind her eyes — that was the job, that was the whole rotten heart of the job. You kept the impossible things locked up and the impossible truths locked down, and sometimes those were the same work.

But this morning the lie tasted different. Because she didn't know yet whether she was protecting her guild from a threat, or protecting a traitor from discovery, or protecting herself from something worse: the possibility that the leak had come from a place so deep inside the walls that no audit would ever reach it.

*One of them*, she thought again. One of nine. Possibly me. She had signed things in her life she barely remembered signing; every keeper of secrets eventually forgot which ones were hers.

"The outer districts will freeze within the week," Corvane said. "Vhalcrest will move before midwinter. Our best position is neutrality. We hold the goods; whoever wins the country will need us. Guilds survive wars by being worth more intact than conquered."

"That's not neutrality," Pell burst out. "That's surrender with better manners."

"That's survival," Corvane said mildly, "which I understand is unfashionable among idealists."

"And what does the Guild of Impossible Things become," Marguerite asked quietly, "if it survives everything except its purpose?"

His voice was soft, but it cut the room like weather. Odile saw Corvane's jaw tighten — a hairline crack in the marble — and she saw something else, something that snagged in her chest like a fishhook: when Marguerite spoke, Corvane didn't look surprised. He looked *prepared*, like a duelist whose opponent had finally made the expected move.

She filed that away too. Her drawer of unexamined things was getting full.

"Our purpose," Odile said, rising, "is to keep faith with what cannot keep faith with itself. That includes a country too stubborn to admit it's dying. So here is what we tell the members: the wards are being rebuilt, the guild stands, and no one speaks of last night's ledger entry to any living soul. Hespera, seal the archives — your key and mine, no other. Tammas, inventory the vault. If so much as a teacup is missing, I want to know before the snow stops falling. Corvane—" she paused, and smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, "—you'll draft the statement. Your prose comforts people."

"I live to serve," Corvane said.

"Yes," she said. "Don't you just."

* * *

The meeting broke apart like a fist opening. One by one they left, bowing or nodding or avoiding her eyes, until only two remained: Odile at the head of the table, and Marguerite at the far end, near the door, making no motion to go.

"You have something to say, Ledger-Keeper?"

"You lied to them."

"I'm the Guildmaster. Lying to my people is a core competency."

"Not about safety. About the entry." He rose, walked the length of the table without hurry, and set one sheet of paper in front of her — a copy, she realized, made in her own office, which meant he'd been in her office, which meant she should be angrier than she was. "You told them the handwriting was flawless. You didn't tell them it's mine."

"No," she agreed. "I didn't."

"Why?"

"Because half this guild would hang you from the rafters by lunchtime, and the other half would spend the afternoon deciding whether to help them. Because a confession isn't evidence. And because—" she stopped, hearing her own voice do the honeyed thing, and forced it straighter. "Because I don't believe you did it. Which is either loyalty or stupidity, and at my age I've stopped being able to afford either."

He studied her. Up close his eyes were unsettling in a way she'd never let herself examine: the pale grey of a sky holding its breath.

"Do you suspect me?" he asked.

"Marguerite, I suspect everyone. It's why I'm still alive." She tapped the copy of the entry. "What I *know* is that this ledger doesn't take dictation. Whatever wrote this used your hand because it had your hand, or had *you*. Those aren't the same thing, and until I know which, you stay inside these walls, you touch no ledger but your own, and you trust no one — including me."

"Including you," he repeated. Not a question. A wound, maybe. Or a warning.

"Especially me," she said, and found she meant it in ways she couldn't explain even to herself. There was a thread running through this whole catastrophe that tugged somewhere behind her sternum, some pattern she almost saw — the timing, the handwriting, the way Corvane moved through the crisis like a man following sheet music. "Go. Inventory your impossible goods. Count them twice."

At the door he paused. "Odile. When you lied to them just now — 'we're safe' — your hands didn't move."

"They never do."

"No," he said. "Most liars' hands give them away. Yours don't. That means you've lied enough that the body's given up objecting." He said it without accusation, almost gently, and then he was gone, and Odile sat alone in the Council Room under the starless dark, feeling seen in a manner she found professionally unacceptable.

* * *

She worked until the bells rang terce, and then she did the thing she'd been not-doing all day: she unlocked the bottom drawer of her desk and took out the letter.

Vhalcrest stationery. Cream-colored, expensive, smelling faintly of cold iron. It had arrived six days ago — *before* the stars went out — carried by no courier anyone admitted to seeing. She had read it once and burned it, and here it was anyway, because some letters refuse to burn, and she was the Guildmaster of Impossible Things and ought to have known that.

*Madam Vasseur. Winter is coming to your country regardless of what you decide. Whether it comes as guest or conqueror depends entirely upon the hospitality shown to our envoy. We request a private audience. Bring no witnesses. Bring no ledgers.*

Bring no ledgers. As if a guild could be discussed without its books. As if the whole negotiation weren't *about* the books.

Someone inside her guild had sold the country's name — she was nearly certain of it now, certain in the way you're certain of a shape moving behind a curtain. And Vhalcrest had written to her first. Which meant they believed she was either complicit, corruptible, or convenient. Three options, and none of them flattered her.

There was a knock. Corvane entered without waiting — his one small insolence, tolerated for eleven years because his competence was real and his ambition, she'd told herself, was merely visible rather than dangerous. He carried the draft statement she'd asked for, and he set it down, and he lingered.

"The members are frightened," he said. "Frightened people look for someone to blame. If the inner circle can't produce a culprit soon—"

"Are you offering to be blamed, Corvane? How generous."

"I'm offering strategy." He smiled his thin smile. "You looked tired this morning. I only mean that the guild needs its Guildmaster sharp."

"I'm always sharp. It's the world that keeps going blunt."

He left. She listened to his footsteps fade down the corridor, even and unhurried, a man walking toward an appointment he was confident of keeping.

And there it was again — that fishhook tug behind her sternum. *Before the stars went out.* The letter had come before. Which meant Vhalcrest had known the fall was scheduled. Which meant the schedule existed. Which meant someone had drawn it up, and circulated it, and the cream-colored envelope had been addressed to *her* — not to Corvane, not to Tammas, to her — because Vhalcrest believed the Guildmaster's cooperation was already purchased, or already assumed.

By whom?

Unless—

She put the letter in the drawer. She locked the drawer. She sat for a long time in the dark with her hands flat on the desk, perfectly still, in the old smuggler's way, listening to the guild breathe around her: two hundred souls, nine suspects, one country going cold.

Tomorrow, she would convene them again. Tomorrow she would smile and lie and count their pulses. But tonight, one thought circled her like a wolf circling a fire:

*If Vhalcrest thinks I'm already bought, then either they've spoken to my traitor — or my traitor has spoken as me.*

And somewhere below her feet, in the sealed dark of the archives, a ledger waited with her ledger-keeper's handwriting in it, and a page torn out that she hadn't noticed missing until now.

She hadn't noticed it missing.

Which meant whoever took it had known exactly what she would notice — and what she wouldn't.

Odile blew out the candle, and in the starless dark she began, very quietly, to make a list.
