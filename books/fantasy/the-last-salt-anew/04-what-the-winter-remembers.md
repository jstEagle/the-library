## 4. What the Winter Remembers

The snow began at dawn, and it did not stop.

I noticed it first as a sound—or rather, as the absence of one. The guildhouse sat in the oldest quarter of Vell, where the streets had names older than the country they belonged to, and normally there was always sound: cart wheels, fishmongers arguing about the freshness of things that were not entirely real, the bells of Saint Ovel's ringing hours that no calendar agreed with. That morning there was nothing. Just a soft hiss against the windows, like the world exhaling through its teeth.

I stood at my window on the third floor and watched the snow come down. It fell in perfect silence, which was the first wrong thing. Snow makes noise. Anyone who has lived through a Velli winter knows the particular whisper of it, the creak of it settling on rooftops, the way it muffles the city into something almost kind. This snow made no sound at all. It fell like it was being poured from a great height by someone very careful, very patient, and very angry.

By midday it was knee-deep in the lower streets. By evening, the gutters had vanished, the lamps burned halos around themselves, and still it fell—fat, deliberate flakes, each one identical, which I only noticed later because identical is not what snow does.

* * *

It was three days before the refugees came.

I heard them before I saw them: a shuffling in the lane outside, the wet drag of boots that had been walking too long. From the window I counted maybe forty figures moving up toward the guild doors through the white, bent double under the weight of the sky. They came from the eastern districts, where the border wards had come down and the cold came through like water through a split hull.

Guildmaster Odile gave the order to open the doors herself. She stood on the top step with her grey cloak snapping in the wind, and she said, "We are the Guild of Impossible Things. Impossible times are our native country." Which was a beautiful thing to say, and I believed her completely for approximately six hours.

They filed into the great hall, shaking snow from themselves like dogs, and we did what guilds do: hot broth, dry blankets, a ledger to record who had arrived. I took the ledger myself, because that is my function here—I keep the books of impossible goods, and lately the books have become the closest thing this country has to a spine. Name, district of origin, condition, possessions. Simple columns. I have written ten thousand entries like them.

The fourth refugee to reach my table was an old woman named Brey, a netmaker from the Saltmarsh district, though she would not know it for long.

"Name?" I asked.

"Brey," she said. "Brey Odanva."

"District?"

She frowned at me. The frown of someone reaching for a word on the tip of their tongue, except the tongue was her whole life. "Salt—" she began, and then stopped, and her face did something terrible and small. "There was salt," she said slowly. "Water. My hands know knots. Why do my hands know knots?"

I wrote down *Brey Odanva, Saltmarsh,* because I knew it, and told her it would come back to her. It did not. An hour later she could not tell me her name either, only that her hands knew knots, and she held them up in front of her like strange animals attached to her wrists, flexing fingers that remembered everything while the rest of her drained away.

* * *

By nightfall I understood the pattern, and understanding it was worse than ignorance.

Every refugee who crossed the guild threshold arrived whole and began, within minutes, to fade. Not their bodies—their bodies were fine, frostbitten but fine. Their memories went. Names first. Then places. Then faces. A dockworker named Illem forgot his daughter's face but kept, desperately, the fact that he had loved someone enough to carry her memory across a frozen country; he described the shape of the love over and over, the way you'd describe a room after the furniture has been removed. A pair of brothers from the Tannery Quarter lost their shared childhood within the same hour, simultaneously, so that when one tried to remind the other of the tree they'd climbed, neither of them could remember why the tree mattered.

And the fading stopped precisely at the threshold. Inside the guildhall, whatever was taking them slowed. Outside, it ran like an open vein.

I tested it. I am a ledger-keeper; testing things is what ledgers are for. I took a young woman called Sefa—not the mapmaker Sefa, another, commoner name—and asked her to recite her street address every five minutes, and I wrote each recitation down with timestamps. Her address dissolved syllable by syllable. *Copperlane Nine. Copper— Copperl— There was a street.* But the moment she stepped across the threshold into the cellar stairwell, the erosion paused. Held still, like a wound that stops bleeding under pressure.

The wards. Whatever remained of the old border wards, broken as they were, still pooled faintly inside the guildhouse foundations—the guild had helped build those wards four hundred years ago, had bound a piece of itself into them. We were standing in the last shallow puddle of the country's protection, and even here, people were leaking.

I stood in the great hall at midnight among two hundred people who were forgetting themselves, holding a ledger full of names that were becoming fiction, and I felt something crack in me. Not break—crack. There is a difference. Breaking is what happens to the weak spot. Cracking is what happens to the strong spot, when it takes more than it was built for.

I thought: *someone did this.* The wards did not fall on their own the night the stars went out. Wards are contracts, and contracts have parties, and parties have signatures. Somewhere in my ledgers—somewhere in the neat, cramped handwriting I have trusted for eleven years—was the answer. Possibly in my own hand. Especially possibly in my own hand.

That entry haunted me. *One (1) country's true name, sold.* Written in ink I knew, in a slant I knew, in a pressure of pen I knew better than I knew most people's faces. And no memory of writing it. No memory is never nothing. No memory is a wall, and walls are built by people with something behind them.

* * *

On the fifth day of snow, Brey the netmaker died.

Not in body. In body she was the healthiest person in the hall. But I came to my table in the morning and found her sitting where she always sat, hands idle in her lap, and when I said good morning she looked at me with the bright, untroubled courtesy of a stranger, and asked me where she was, and whether it was warm where I came from, and if there was work for a woman with clever hands.

Her hands still knew knots. She tied them all day, beautifully, endlessly, without knowing what a knot was for or that she had ever touched rope. Watching her was like watching a cathedral sing with no one inside it.

I sat down across from her, and I did something I had not done since my exile began. I reached across the table and took her hands in mine, knots and all, and I said, "Your name is Brey Odanva. You are a netmaker from the Saltmarsh. You have fished these waters for fifty-one years, and your nets hold because you tie them true." I recited it like scripture. Because that is what it was becoming. Scripture. The last copies of a burning library, read aloud by whoever still had pages.

She smiled at me, pleased and vacant, and my chest hurt in a way I recognized with horror, because it was the exact feeling of the hole where my own memories should be. I knew that emptiness from the inside. I had been carrying my own small version of it for years—a gap in me shaped like a city and a storm, smooth-walled, carefully made. I had told myself it was exile's scar tissue.

Sitting there holding a stranger's forgetting in my hands, I understood for the first time that mine might not be a scar at all. It might be a harvest. Someone had cut a field out of me too, once, and sold what grew there.

Grief is a strange fuel. It burns low and long and it does not care whether you consent to be consumed. I felt it change in me that morning—from the wet, formless thing it had been since the stars went out into something drier, harder, with an edge. Grief hardening into purpose has a specific sensation: the world gets quieter, not louder. Your hands stop shaking. You start counting.

So I counted.

Two hundred and seventeen refugees. Average loss of personal memory upon arrival: catastrophic within nine hours. Rate of loss inside the wards' residual pool: slow, measurable, and—here was the number that mattered—*proportional to distance from the guild's foundation-stones.* The closer to the cellar, the slower the bleed. Which meant the wards weren't just broken. They were being *drunk*. Something was siphoning the country's remembering through the breach in its borders, and it was using the snow to do it.

I went back through my records of the night the stars vanished. Cross-referenced the ward-failure timestamps against inventory movement, deliveries, personnel logs. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as boring as a cover story should be.

Except one line. A supply requisition, dated the day before the fall, signed out of the cellar stores: *chalk, ceremonial grade, twelve sticks.* Chalk for ward-work. Drawn by someone with cellar access. Signed for with initials I had seen ten thousand times and never once questioned, because they appeared on a hundred innocent requisitions a year.

*C.V.*

Corvane Vess. Odile's deputy. The man who had held my chair at the inner circle meeting, who had spoken soothingly of patience and procedure while the country bled.

I am an honest bookkeeper. I want to say that plainly, because everything else about me is complicated. I do not leap. I verify. So I did not run to Odile with initials and suspicion. Instead I copied the requisition, sealed it, hid the copy inside the binding of a ledger no one reads—*Catalogue of Miracles Decommissioned Prior to 1180,* which is exactly as thrilling as it sounds—and I went down to the cellar to see with my own eyes what twelve sticks of ceremonial chalk might have drawn.

The stairs were colder than they should have been. The cold in the cellar had a direction, a current, flowing downward past me like a river running the wrong way, and at the bottom, in the far dark beyond the wine racks and the decommissioned miracles, I saw it: a faint tracery on the floor, chalk-ghosts of a sigil half-scrubbed away, arranged in a spiral. A siphon. A tap drilled into the root of the wards, drinking the country upward and outward, flake by flake, memory by memory, into the white and starless sky.

Someone had built a straw into our skull, and the snow was what the drinking looked like from inside.

I stood there a long time in the dark, listening to the silent snow press down on the roof above two hundred forgetting people, and the grief in me finished hardening. It set like mortar. I thought of Brey's hands tying knots for no one. I thought of a ledger entry in my own hand selling a country's name. I thought of Corvane's initials, tidy and unremarkable, on a stick of chalk.

Then I turned back up the stairs to find Guildmaster Odile—because if Corvane was the traitor, she needed to know tonight, and if she wasn't to be trusted either, I needed to learn that in her face when I told her.

Behind me, in the dark, the chalk spiral went on drinking.
