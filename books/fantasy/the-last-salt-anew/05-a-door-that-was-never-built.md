## 5. A Door That Was Never Built

The clue was a rounding error.

That was the thing about impossible goods—they resisted arithmetic. A jar of last year's laughter might gain an ounce in storage. A bottled thunderclap lost volume the longer you ignored it. Every ledger-keeper in the Guild of Impossible Things learned, on their first day, to forgive the books their small sins.

But Marguerite Vasquez had kept ledgers for thirty years, and he did not forgive them anything.

He sat in his narrow office above the counting floor, lamplight pooling on the page, and looked at the discrepancy for the fourth time that hour. Cellar inventory, taken the night the stars went out, against cellar inventory taken three nights later. Everything balanced—except one line. *Storage, miscellaneous, shelf forty-one.* First count: eleven items. Second count: ten.

One object had left the cellar between the fall of the wards and now. No withdrawal slip. No signature. In a guild where even the air was catalogued by weight and provenance, an unlogged removal was not sloppiness.

It was a message. Or a wound.

He pulled the older volumes, working backward through the years, and found shelf forty-one mentioned exactly once more—a transfer entry from nine years ago, in his own handwriting: *Received: one (1) item, origin unknown, description withheld per Guildmaster's order.*

His handwriting. He remembered none of it.

Marguerite set down the pen before his hand could shake visibly. This was becoming a habit, finding himself in the margins of his own books like a stranger's coat hung in his closet. The sale of the country's name. Now this. Either someone was forging him with terrible skill, or there were whole rooms of his life standing dark behind locked doors inside his skull.

*Per Guildmaster's order.* He would have to ask Odile. He would have to watch her face while he asked.

He decided, instead, to go down first.

* * *

The guild's cellar ran deeper than the building had any right to allow. Everyone knew this and no one said it aloud—the way everyone knew the third stair creaked in a language that wasn't quite warning. Marguerite took the spiral steps with a lamp in one hand and the inventory book in the other, past shelves of confiscated weather, past the vat where they kept the sea's apology (still fermenting), past the cold room where unspoken vows hung in rows like hams.

Shelf forty-one was at the very back, in the oldest section, where the stone changed color. The newer cellars were dressed granite, quarried and proud. Back here the walls were rough-hewn black rock that sweated in all seasons and drank lamplight rather than returning it.

Eleven niches cut into the wall. Ten occupied.

He counted twice. Then he held his lamp close and read the small brass plates beneath each niche, because brass plates were the guild's oldest habit and its most honest: *A door knocker from a house that burned before it was built. A key that fits every lock except its own. The sound of your mother's voice, aged nine years. A map of here, drawn before here existed.*

And the empty niche—its plate read only: *RETURNED TO OWNER.*

No date. No signature. Just those five words, stamped deep, the letters filled with old verdigris as if they'd been waiting a long time to be needed.

Marguerite stood in the cold dark with his breath fogging and felt something turn over in his chest, slow as a ship coming about. Returned to owner. Someone had come for it the night the stars went out. The same night the wards fell. The same night, if his forged—or unforged—ledger was true, that the country's name had been sold.

He should have gone up. He knew that. He should have climbed the stairs, knocked on Odile's door, laid out the discrepancy like a card he'd been saving. Instead he put his palm flat against the black stone beside the empty niche, the way you press a hand to a wall to feel a distant train.

The stone was warm.

Everything else in the cellar was cellar-cold, but under his hand the rock held heat like a body recently left. And beneath the warmth—faint, so faint he might have invented it—a rhythm. Not a pulse. A tide. In, and hold, and out. The breathing of something patient.

"Ledger says you're gone," he told the wall. His voice came back flattened, swallowed. "So why are you still here?"

The wall did not answer, but his lamp guttered once, deliberately, the way lamps do when they are being polite about something enormous.

He began, without quite deciding to, to search.

* * *

It took him until the third hour past midnight, and what he found was not a seam or a hinge or any of the hundred clever mechanisms the guild used elsewhere in the building. What he found was worse: nothing. The back wall of the oldest cellar was simply, unmistakably, a wall—until you stood at exactly the right distance, which was too far away to touch it, and looked at it slightly wrong.

At that angle, in that half-focus, the mortar lines stopped looking like mortar lines and started looking like handwriting. Long horizontal strokes. Descenders. A script worn smooth by repetition, as though someone had written the same word across the entire wall, over and over, for years, and then buried the writing in stone.

Marguerite recognized the script because it was his own.

Not similar. His. The distinctive way he hooked his lowercase r's. The slight forward lean of a man always writing faster than he should. He had spent thirty years keeping the guild's books; apparently he had also spent some forgotten season writing a word into the bones of the world until the world agreed to keep it.

He could not read what the word was. That was the cruelest part. His eyes slid off it the way they slid off the blank places in his memory—there was a shape, a weight, a meaning pressing against the inside of his skull like a face against frosted glass, and then nothing.

"Fine," he said quietly. "You want to be a wall. Be a wall."

He set the lamp on the floor. He took out his pen—the good one, goose-quill, iron-gall ink, the one tool of his trade he trusted absolutely—and he did the only professional thing left to do with an unreadable document.

He annotated it.

He wrote directly on the stone, small and neat, beside the lowest line of buried script: *Observed [date]. Wall exhibits text consistent with hand of M. Vasquez. Content illegible. Recommend—*

The pen skipped. Dragged. He looked down and saw that his hand had written something else entirely, in a hurry, as if his fingers had been waiting thirty years for permission:

*Recommend opening it, you coward.*

The lamp flame leaned toward the wall like a listener. And the wall—there was no other way to say it—the wall exhaled.

Mortar dust sifted down. The horizontal strokes of the hidden script shifted, resettled, and Marguerite watched, heart slamming, as a vertical seam appeared where no seam had been, hairline thin, running floor to ceiling. Cold air poured through it, smelling of woodsmoke and rain on hot slate and something green—grass, actual grass, growing things—in a winter that had already killed everything green within a hundred miles.

The seam widened to the width of a hand. Enough to see through. Not enough to pass.

He pressed his eye to the gap.

* * *

On the other side was evening. Real evening, with a sky the color of a bruise healing, and low hills stitched with stone walls, and a road going down toward a town whose name arrived in his mouth before he could stop it:

*Carrowmere.*

He said it aloud and the word hurt, the way weight hurts after a long illness—you'd forgotten you used to carry it everywhere. Carrowmere. He knew the crooked bell tower. He knew the inn with the blue door that was never open on Tuesdays. He knew, with a certainty that bypassed his mind entirely and lived somewhere below his ribs, that the baker on the corner burned her loaves on purpose because her husband had loved the burnt ends, and she had never stopped, even after—

Even after what?

The knowledge stopped there, sheared clean, the way all his knowledge of himself stopped. He could remember the town but not his place in it. He could remember the storm-darkening sky over those hills—he could almost taste the ozone—and not whether he had raised it or fled it or been forgiven for it. It was like reading a novel with every other page torn out and knowing, somehow, that he was the villain on the missing ones.

There were people on the road. Distant figures, walking toward the town through the long grass, and one of them—a woman with a red shawl, laughing at something a child said—turned, briefly, toward the gap in the wall. As if she'd heard him say the name. As if she'd heard him breathe.

For one absurd, lurching moment Marguerite was certain she was going to walk up to the seam, look through it, and know him. Say his name. Give him back the torn pages.

She didn't. She turned back to the child, and the little procession crested the hill, and went down into Carrowmere, and the bell tower caught the last light like a held note.

Marguerite realized he was crying only when a tear hit the back of his own hand, and he wiped it with an anger that surprised him—not grief's anger, but the specific fury of a man who has just discovered the size of the theft. They hadn't stolen his memories the way you steal coins. They'd stolen them the way you steal a country: leaving the borders intact, the towns standing, the roads passable, and removing only the flag. He could visit every room of his past. He simply couldn't live in any of it.

"That's mine," he said to the seam, to the evening beyond it, to whoever had done this. "All of it. Even the parts I'd have paid to forget. Especially those."

The seam pulsed once, gently, like a hand squeezing his. Then, slowly, grudgingly, it began to narrow.

"No—" He jammed the pen into the gap, wedging it, and the quill snapped with a crack that echoed down the cellar corridors. The seam stopped closing. Held, at hand's width, trembling around the broken nib as if annoyed.

"All right," Marguerite breathed. "We negotiate."

He sat down on the cold flags with his back against the warm stone, tore a page from the inventory book, and began, by lamplight, to do what he did best. He made a record. *Item: one doorway, unauthorized, located behind shelf forty-one. Opens onto Carrowmere (unverified). Responds to my handwriting. Currently held open by one (1) broken quill.* And then, after a moment, in smaller letters, because honest ledgers were the one vow he had left: *Note—I appear to have built this. Or paid for it. Or been its price. Further inquiry required.*

Further inquiry required. He almost laughed. Somewhere in the guild above him, a traitor was selling the country's name piece by piece, the Guildmaster was lying beautifully to people who trusted her, refugees were arriving at the doors with holes where their childhoods used to be—and he was sitting in a cellar negotiating terms with a door he didn't remember making.

But that was the thing about ledgers. You didn't fix a corrupted account by burning the book. You worked backward, line by line, until you found the first false entry, and then you followed it home.

He got up, left the lamp burning for the door—it seemed polite, and doors remembered such things—and climbed the stairs toward Odile's study, carrying the one question that mattered more than treachery now:

*Guildmaster. Nine years ago, what did you order me to receive?*

He was two flights up when he heard the voices through her door—one hers, low and controlled, and the other one wrong for this hour, wrong for this building, smooth as oiled leather and speaking with a Vhalcrest accent.

Marguerite stopped on the stairs, in the dark, and did not knock.
