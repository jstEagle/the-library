## 6. The Storm I Used to Be

The dream did not come like a dream. It came like weather.

Marguerite woke in the dark of his cell with salt on his lips — actual salt, grainy and wet, though the guild's storerooms were three floors above and sealed against the endless winter. He sat up. The candle he'd left burning had guttered to a coin of wax, and the cold had a texture now, a pressure, as if the snow outside were leaning its whole weight against the stone.

In the dream, there had been a city.

He could not hold the name of it. He reached for it the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark that isn't where his foot remembers, and the absence was so precise, so *shaped*, that he understood immediately: this was not forgetting. Forgetting is smooth. This had edges. Someone had cut this out of him and left the hole behind.

He lit a fresh candle with hands that would not quite steady, opened his ledger to a blank page, and wrote: *Dreamed of a city. Salt. A woman's voice saying* come inside, *or possibly* come inside before it takes you. *Do not remember why.*

Below that, after a long pause, his pen moving without permission from any part of him he trusted:

*I think I killed someone I loved.*

Then he crossed it out. Then he crossed out the crossing-out, because a ledger-keeper's first law is that nothing entered may be falsely erased, and he had built his entire second life on that law.

He sat until the candle burned down to his knuckles, listening to the snow press against the world, and told himself the sentence meant nothing. He was very good at telling himself things. He had had years of practice.

* * *

The fragments came at odd hours after that, the way water comes through a cracked wall — not all at once but along the path of least resistance, finding the weak places in him and widening them.

A smell of ozone in the cellar corridor and suddenly he was standing on a sea-wall with his arms raised and the sky over him was *wrong*, green-black and swollen, and somewhere below a bell tower was ringing and ringing and no one was running because no one believed yet—

He came back to himself with his palm flat against the cellar door, the hidden one, the one that opened onto a country he half-remembered, and the wood under his hand was warm as skin. He snatched his hand back as if scalded.

Another night: the taste of ink and blood, indistinguishable. Another: a name spoken in a voice gone hoarse from shouting into wind — *Marguerite, Marguerite, put it down* — and the terrible lightness in his chest when he did not.

Put what down? Put *what* down?

He began sleeping in the ledger room, surrounded by the guild's records of impossible things: bottled last breaths, keys to houses that had never been built, a jar of thunder labeled with a date forty years future. If memory had been taken from him, then perhaps it had been taken *into something*. That was how the trade worked; he knew the guild's economics better than anyone alive. Nothing vanished. Everything was sold, stored, or spent.

Somewhere there was a vessel holding the shape of a city and the reason he had drowned it.

He just had to find it before whatever was left of him found it first.

* * *

It was Sefa who broke the dam, though neither of them knew it at the time. She had spread her charts across three tables in the map-room, the ones she'd been inked for, places that no longer existed rendered in careful fading lines, and she said, offhand, tapping the coast of a country that had eaten itself:

"You know the strange thing about erased places? They leave tides."

"Tides," Marguerite said.

"Water remembers level. You pull a coastline off the maps and the sea still swells toward where it used to break." She looked up at him, and her eyes were the flat grey of a chart's empty margin. "People are the same. You take a memory out of a man and everything around the hole still bends toward it. His habits lean. His fears lean." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Yours lean south, Marguerite. Toward the coast. Every single time we pass a south-facing window, you stop talking mid-sentence."

He had no answer for that. So he went to the archives instead, alone, at the hour when even the enforcers slept, and pulled every record bearing the Guild of Impossible Things' oldest seal — the seal from before Odile's time, from before his own, from the era the ledgers called only *the Reckoning Winter*, which he had always assumed was metaphor.

It was not metaphor. It was a season. It had lasted eleven years, and it had ended, according to a single line in a shipping manifest he found wedged inside a false-backed drawer, *when the storm-caller paid what the storm-caller owed.*

The storm-caller.

He read the line eleven times. His own handwriting stared back at him from the marginal note beneath it — his handwriting, unmistakable, the same cramped rightward slant he used today — and it said:

*Paid in full. Never again.*

Never again what? Never again paying, or never again calling?

His hands were shaking so badly he had to set the manifest down. In the silence of the archive, snow ticking against high windows like fingernails, he became aware that he was whispering. He didn't know the word he was whispering until he heard it: *no, no, no,* in the tone of a man arguing with himself across a gap too wide to bridge.

* * *

That night the dream came whole, or nearly whole, and he woke weeping and could not say for whom.

He saw it in pieces even now, assembling itself wrong each time, like a shattered window re-frosting: 

A city on a southern coast, white-walled, named — and here the hole again, the cut edge, the missing stair —

Ships in the harbor flying colors he did know, because he had seen those colors two months ago on an envoy's carriage: the black-and-gold of Vhalcrest.

Himself, younger by fifteen years and arrogant by thirty, standing on the sea-wall with the sky coming down like a judgment he had personally requested. Because he *had* requested it. That was the thing the fragments kept trying to tell him and he kept refusing to hear: the storm had not come for the city. He had *called* it. Called it down on Vhalcrest's invasion fleet, yes — but storms do not distinguish between the ships they drown and the harbors they drown them in, between soldiers and fishermen and a woman on the quay who had run out into the open rather than shelter because —

Because she was looking for someone. Because she was shouting his name into the wind.

*MARGUERITE.*

And he had seen her. He had seen her face turned up to him through the rain, and he had had one heartbeat — he remembered this with horrible clarity, one heartbeat in which he could have closed his fist and let the storm spend itself seaward, spare the city, lose the fleet, lose the war, let Vhalcrest walk in over the bodies of the defenders — and he had chosen the country over the woman, or told himself he chose the country, and brought the whole screaming sky down on all of them together.

He woke with his own hands locked around his throat and pried them loose finger by finger, gasping, and lay in the dark doing arithmetic with the only numbers he trusted: *one fleet destroyed, one invasion broken, one city drowned, one woman —*

One woman saved or killed. That was the wound that would not close, the fragment that refused to resolve. Some nights she died on the quay with her mouth full of sea. Some nights he caught her — he remembered arms, aching arms, hauling her up a rope while the wave-towers fell — some nights he caught her and carried her inland and never saw her again, and both versions felt equally true, and that was the cruelest thing about a stolen memory: whoever took it took the verdict with it, and left him sentenced either way.

"I don't want it back," he said aloud to the dark, in a voice he barely recognized. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it proves. I don't—"

The candleflame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

Outside his window, far off beyond the sealed doors of the guild, thunder rolled across a sky that had held nothing but snow for weeks. Once. Twice. Like something enormous clearing its throat, or knocking.

Marguerite pressed his back to the wall and made himself breathe. *Deny it cleanly,* he told himself, the way he'd tell a junior clerk. *If you're going to lie in the ledgers, lie cleanly.* He took out the book, wrote *no further dreams to report*, signed it, and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

* * *

Morning brought Odile to his door, which was unusual enough that his paranoia — lately less a flaw than a discipline — stood fully upright.

She did not enter. She stood in the corridor with her hands folded and her guildmaster's face on, the smooth one, the one he had watched her lie through in the inner circle, and she looked older than she had a month ago, worn at the seams like a ledger rebound too many times.

"You've been in the old archive," she said.

It was not a question, and he did not insult them both by pretending otherwise.

"There's a record," she went on, before he could speak, "that has been in my keeping since the day I took this office. Left to me by the guildmaster before me, who was left it by the one before her. It concerns a debt the guild has carried a long time, and a payment made once — and, we all prayed, finally." Her eyes moved over his face with something that was not suspicion and was worse than suspicion: recognition. "I had hoped it would never become relevant again. But the stars went out, Marguerite. The wards fell. And yesterday my deputy tells me someone has been pulling the Reckoning-era files."

"Corvane told you that?"

"Corvane tells me many useful things." Something flickered there and was gone, too fast to read. "Tonight I have an appointment I cannot avoid, and afterward there will be a decision made that I may not be able to protect anyone from. So I am telling you now, while I still can: whatever you remember — whatever comes back to you — do not trust the version of events written in any book in this building."

"Including your ledgers?" he asked.

"*Especially*" — and her voice, for one syllable, cracked like ice underfoot — "*especially mine.*"

She turned and walked away down the corridor, unhurried, composed, a woman going to keep an ordinary appointment, and Marguerite stood in his doorway with his heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't heard since the sea-wall: *come inside, come inside, come inside before it takes you.*

He watched her descend the stairs toward the guild's lower doors, toward the snow, toward wherever she was going tonight and whomever she intended to meet.

And because he had spent fifteen years learning to distrust exactly this kind of silence, he took down his coat, checked the wax-sealed ledger against his ribs, and followed her into the white.
