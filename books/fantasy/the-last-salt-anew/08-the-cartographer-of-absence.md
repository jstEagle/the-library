## 8. The Cartographer of Absence

The map shop had no sign, which was the first honest thing about it.

Sefa Ilves noticed the storm-caller before he noticed her — of course she did; noticing was the whole of her trade. He came down Vell Street with his collar up and the snow taking him apart piece by piece, and he stopped at her window the way everyone did. Not because the maps in it were beautiful, though they were. Because the places on them were gone.

*Ashfall Harbor. The Nine Bridges of Luen. The Salt Coast, entire.*

She watched him read the labels. She watched his face do the thing faces always did — the small flinch, the reaching for a memory that wasn't where they'd left it. Most people walked on after that, faster than before, the way you hurry past a mirror in a house you're not sure is empty.

He didn't walk on. He pushed open her door, bringing winter with him, and stood there dripping.

"You're closed," he said, because her lamp was out and her shutters half-drawn.

"I'm never closed," Sefa said. "Closed implies there's something to close. I keep a shop of absences, Master Vasquez. There's very little to steal."

His eyes came up sharply. *So he didn't know he'd been followed.* That was interesting. Guild enforcers had been shadowing him for two days now — she'd charted their routes out of professional habit, three men in gray, rotating at the fountain on Coppersmith Row — and either he'd made them and lost them, or he hadn't made them at all, and if a man who'd once called a storm down on a city couldn't spot a tail, then whatever the guild had done to him had taken more than memories.

It had taken his instincts. And Sefa knew what instincts were worth, because she'd spent eleven years drawing coastlines that no longer existed, and instinct was all an absence left behind.

"Who are you?" Marguerite asked.

"Sefa Ilves. Cartographer." She gestured at the walls. "Of nothing, mostly."

He stepped inside despite himself. They always stepped inside despite themselves. She'd learned early that grief for a place behaved exactly like grief for a person — it wanted somewhere to sit.

"These aren't real," he said, but his voice said otherwise. His voice said *please*.

"They're more real than the ones the Survey Office sells you," Sefa said. "Those maps show you where things are. Mine show you where things were. There's a difference, and the difference has been getting wider every day since the stars went out." She lit the lamp — no reason to perform darkness; he was already caught. Light climbed the walls and set the absent places glowing: Ashfall Harbor in faded blue ink, its lighthouse drawn with a careful hand and a note in the margin, *light visible from nine leagues, or so my mother swore.* The Nine Bridges of Luen, each one named. The Salt Coast, entire, with little ticks along the shore marking villages whose names she'd gathered from refugees over three years of interviewing anyone who still remembered.

Marguerite stood in front of the Salt Coast a long time.

"I kept ledgers," he said finally. "For the guild. Entries for everything. I don't remember—" His jaw worked. "I don't remember the Salt Coast being on any of them."

"No," Sefa said. "You wouldn't."

That got his attention properly. He turned from the wall, and she saw the calculation start in him, the ledger-keeper's mind doing what it did — weighing, auditing, looking for the entry that balanced.

"Say it plainly," he said. "I've had a long week of people saying things around me."

*Have you,* she thought, and almost smiled. *You have no idea how long your week actually is.*

"The country is being erased," she said. "Not conquered. Conquest leaves ruins, and ruins can be mapped, and mapped things can be remembered. This is cleaner. Vhalcrest isn't burning us. They're *unwriting* us. Every border village that goes dark, every road that stops existing on their surveys — they redraw the map without it, and the world, being agreeable, follows the map." She tapped the Salt Coast. "Three years ago this took me four months to chart, because people still argued about it. Last month a woman came through my door who grew up in Tessel-on-the-Salt and could not tell me the color of her own front door. The memory had been harvested out of her like a tooth."

"And you know this how? A cartographer with a lamp and a back room?"

"A cartographer," Sefa said, "with correspondents." She crossed to her desk — scarred oak, ink-burned, the only object in the shop she'd brought from her old life — and unlocked the drawer with the key she wore on a cord under her shirt. From it she took a map.

Not one of hers. The paper was wrong for this country — too smooth, too white, sized in the Vhalcrest fashion with the border marked in their double rule. She'd bought it off a smuggler in the autumn, paid too much, told herself it was research.

She spread it on the desk between them.

Marguerite looked down at it and went very still, the way water goes still just before it freezes.

"That's the Salt Coast," he said.

"That's where the Salt Coast used to be," Sefa said. "Look at the label."

He looked. She watched him read it twice, because she'd read it forty times and still sometimes had to read it twice. Where the coast should have borne its own name, the Vhalcrest engravers had printed: *Vhalcrest Maritime Province, Southern Reach. Settled time immemorial.*

"Time immemorial," Marguerite said quietly.

"It's the phrase they use when they want a lie to sound older than argument," Sefa said. "And here's the part that matters to you, ledger-keeper." She turned the map over. On the reverse, in the corner, was the engraver's mark — and beneath the mark, a jobbing number, the kind printing houses used to track commissions. "This plate wasn't cut in Vhalcrest. The paper stock, the ink binder, the numbering convention — it's guild work. Guild paper, guild ink, cut to Vhalcrest order. Someone inside the Guild of Impossible Things is selling them our name, and they're printing it onto maps so smoothly most people will never notice the surgery."

Marguerite sat down. Not dramatically. Just — sat, the way a man sits when a floor gives way and he decides to pretend he meant to.

"My ledgers," he said. "There's an entry in my hand. A sale. One country's true name." He laughed, short and terrible. "I found it two days before the wards fell and I assumed I'd gone mad, because I had no memory of writing it. Now you're telling me—"

"Now I'm telling you the sale went through," Sefa said. "The maps are proof of delivery."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the snow kept coming down with that patient, total silence it had developed, as if it had all the time in the world, which, lately, it did.

"Why me?" Marguerite said at last. "Why come to me? If you know the leak is in the guild, why bring this to the guild's disgraced bookkeeper?"

"Because you're the only man in this country who audited the impossible for a living and then forgot what he found," Sefa said. "Think about it, Master Vasquez. You wrote that entry. Your hand, your abbreviations — I've seen guild ledgers; the clerk's marks are as individual as fingerprints. Which means the man who sold the name either *is* you, or someone knows your hand well enough to wear it like a coat. Either way, the answer is in your books. In the entries you can't remember making. In the pages someone may already have torn out."

"And if it's me?"

Sefa met his eyes. She had interviewed three hundred refugees and she knew every way a face could refuse to hope, and his face was doing all of them at once.

"Then we'll both know," she said. "I'm offering partnership, not absolution. I chart what's missing. You audit what remains. Between us we might find the shape of the theft — because here's the thing about erasure, storm-caller. It's never perfect. To remove a thing cleanly you have to know it completely, and knowing leaves residue. Ink ghosts. Margin notes. The memory of a front door's color, sitting in some Vhalcrest archive like a pulled tooth in a jar." She began rolling up the enemy map, her hands steady, her heart not. "Absence has a shape. I've built a career on drawing it. Help me draw this one."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of a man picking up a tool he'd sworn off.

"The guild enforcers following me," he said. "Gray coats, rotation at the Coppersmith fountain."

Sefa blinked. "You made them."

"I made them about ten minutes ago," he said. "Which should frighten both of us." He stood, and something moved behind his eyes like weather building past a headland. "If Corvane's people see me walking into a shop of erased maps, your correspondence becomes evidence and you become an accomplice. So we do this carefully. I'll copy what's left of my ledgers — the true entries, hidden inside inventory counts, the way I hid everything worth hiding. You match them against your charts. We meet where nobody watches."

"The Nine Bridges," Sefa said.

"The bridges are gone."

"So am I, officially." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Drowned in the Salt Coast survey, year three. It's why I'm good at my work. Dead women chart absences very thoroughly, Master Vasquez. Nobody tells us to stop."

Something that was almost respect crossed his face — the first unguarded thing she'd seen him do. He pulled his collar up again, and paused at the door with the snow light behind him.

"The woman from Tessel-on-the-Salt," he said. "Her front door. What color did it turn out to be?"

"Blue," Sefa said. "Her sister remembered it, from across the border. Blue, with a brass knocker shaped like a fish." She held his gaze. "That's why I do this. Every door we get back is blue."

He nodded once and went out into the white.

Sefa stood a moment among her absent harbors and drowned bridges, then blew out the lamp and opened her desk drawer again — the false bottom this time, the one no partner knew about yet.

Inside lay the second map she'd bought off the smuggler. Newer. Dated next month.

On it, where the capital should be, the engravers had already begun the new lettering — and beneath the unfinished word, in faint pencil, someone had noted the commission number of the plate still to come:

*Final revision pending receipt of original document. — C.*

* * *

Outside, the snow erased his footsteps behind him, and Marguerite Vasquez walked toward the guild with a dead cartographer's warning folded against his ribs, unaware that somewhere above the white streets, in a window of the embassy that did not appear on any map, someone was watching him go — and making a note.
