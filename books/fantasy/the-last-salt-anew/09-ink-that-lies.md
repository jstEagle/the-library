## 9. Ink That Lies

The ledgers did not lie. That was the first thing they taught you in the Guild of Impossible Things, before wards, before inventory, before anything: *the books do not lie, because we do not let them.* A ledger was a covenant. Ink set down in good faith bound the world to its word and the guild to its debts. Marguerite had kept those covenants for eleven years, through exile and hunger and the slow erosion of everything he'd once been, and he had trusted them the way other men trusted their own heartbeat.

So when he found the torn pages, his hands shook.

It was the fourth hour of the morning shift, or what passed for morning under a sky that no longer remembered how to be anything but white. The counting room smelled of tallow and iron gall ink and the faint ozone tang that clung to him always now, like the storm had left a residue in his blood that soap could not touch. He worked by lamplight at his usual table, third from the window, because the window still showed him the snow falling on the outer districts and he had decided — without admitting it was a decision — that someone should keep watching.

He was reconciling the winter stores against Odile's requisitions when his thumb caught on the binding of Volume Fourteen.

The spine was wrong.

Not visibly. Not to anyone who hadn't rebound forty ledgers with his own hands during the lean years, when the guild couldn't afford a bookbinder and Marguerite had learned to stitch vellum like a surgeon closes a wound. But the thread was off by a hair's width. The tension sat a fraction too loose near the headband. Someone had opened this binding recently, carefully, and sewn it back with excellent technique and slightly different wax on the linen.

Marguerite set down his pen.

He did not open the volume immediately. He sat very still and listened to the counting room: the scratch of junior clerks copying entries two tables away, the tick of the frost-clock Odile kept because she distrusted magic timepieces, the wind worrying the shutters. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a place that believed itself safe.

Then he opened Volume Fourteen to the winter requisitions, and the vertigo hit him like a hand flat against the chest.

The pages were wrong. Not the entries — the entries were flawless. His numbering system, his abbreviations, the little hooked descenders he made on *f* that the scriveners teased him about, even the way his hand grew smaller and tighter toward the bottom of a long page as though the words themselves were trying to economize. Whoever had written these pages knew his handwriting better than he did.

But the paper was new. And the ledger was old.

Volume Fourteen predated the exile. Its paper had a grain to it, a warmth of age, a faint foxing at the margins that Marguerite could have identified blindfolded, by touch alone, the way a mother knows her children's coats in the dark. These pages were smooth. Bleached. Cut to fit, aged at the edges with tea or smoke — done skillfully, but done. And the ink sat differently on them. His iron gall darkened over years until it bit brown into the fiber; this ink was still black and proud on the surface, a stranger sitting comfortably in a borrowed house.

Someone had taken pages out of his ledger and put lies in their place.

Marguerite turned back through the volume with hands that felt like they belonged to another man. There. Entry 4471: *Received into custody, one memory, unpriced, donor unknown* — dated three days after the stars went out. He had no memory of receiving it. He would have remembered; an unpriced entry was a violation of everything the guild stood for, and he would have flagged it, contested it, raised it to Odile in writing.

There. Entry 4489: *Transferred to Vhalcrest embassy, per standing arrangement.* In his hand. Sealed with his personal stamp — the little storm-sigil he'd had cut in his second year, the one he pressed into warm wax without thinking, the way he signed his own name.

There. And there. A whole architecture of transactions leading out of the guild and across the border, each one plausible, each one dated correctly, each one initialed M.V. in the margin where he initialled things.

And threaded among them, so cleverly he almost missed it: entries designed not to look like theft, but to look like *his* theft. A sale of refugee memories to a Vhalcrest factor. An authorization to harvest the fading recollections of the people crossing the threshold — the ones whose pasts dissolved like breath on glass. An entry recording payment received. Payment *to Marguerite Vasquez*, itemized, in sums that matched, with cruel precision, the exact amounts that had appeared in his accounts over the past season, the ones he'd traced to no source and explained to himself as clerical error, as rounding, as the small sloppiness of a large institution.

They weren't errors. They'd been seeded. Placed there months ago, waiting for someone to find them, waiting for exactly this audit, exactly this winter, exactly this moment when a hunted guild needed a traitor more than it needed truth.

His name was being written into guilt one entry at a time, in his own hand.

* * *

He did not remember leaving the counting room. That frightened him most of all, afterward — that his body could simply stand up and walk while his mind stood still inside itself, watching. He came back to himself in the archive stairwell, halfway down, gripping the rail hard enough to whiten his knuckles, breathing the cold stone smell of the lower levels.

Think, he told himself. You are a ledger-keeper. Think like ink.

Who could do this?

Not anyone. That was the horror of it, narrowing down to a point like the last light of a lamp going out. This was not forgery by a hired hand, not some Vhalcrest scribe working from samples. To reproduce his hand this perfectly — the hooks, the tightening script, the marginal initials placed exactly where he placed them, the pressure of the pen matching his fatigue at the end of long days — someone would need years of his writing. Hundreds of pages. They would need to have studied him the way he studied the ledgers: line by line, entry by entry, learning the man through the mark his pen made.

Or they would need to have been close enough to watch him write.

He thought of the counting room. Third table from the window. Eleven years at that table. Junior clerks rotating through on their training terms, senior clerks drifting over with questions, Odile herself standing behind his shoulder sometimes, reading upside-down as she spoke to him, the way busy people do.

Anyone. It could have been anyone. Everyone he had ever written in front of was a suspect, and everyone in the guild had seen him write, and he had never once thought of his handwriting as a door that could be picked.

His own stamp. His own seal. Had it ever left his person? He tried to reconstruct — he who had no memories older than exile to lose, losing even the recent ones now to panic — the location of the storm-sigil stamp. It lived in his coat pocket. Except when it didn't. When he left it in the drawer of his table overnight because carrying it home through the snow seemed paranoid, and paranoia was beneath a man whose books balanced.

Paranoia, Marguerite thought, climbing the rest of the stairs on legs gone cold, is just foresight that arrived early.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing the thing that made him sickest: checking his own work against itself. He pulled the duplicate abstracts — the fair copies every entry required, filed separately, sealed, supposed to be tamper-proof. If the originals were forged, the abstracts would tell him when.

They told him worse than when.

The abstracts were clean. Every corresponding fair copy matched the true history: no memory transfer to Vhalcrest, no sale of refugees' fading names, no payments to M.V. Whatever had happened to Volume Fourteen had happened to Volume Fourteen alone, which meant the forger had access to the original volumes but not the sealed abstracts —

— or had chosen not to touch them. Because a forger smart enough to do all this knew something Marguerite was only now understanding with a cold, sliding dread: when the discrepancy between original and abstract was discovered, the guild would not ask *which is false?* The guild would ask *who benefits from the discrepancy?* And the man whose name sat in the forged entries, whose stamp sealed them, whose hand filled them — that man would say the abstracts were the forgery. That man had motive. That man was already strange to them, already half-foreign, already the exiled storm-caller nobody fully trusted, already the one Odile watched too closely since the night the stars went out.

The evidence was built to survive scrutiny by pointing at its own creator.

Marguerite sat in the archive with the clean abstracts spread before him and understood that he was holding the only proof of his innocence, and that proof was indistinguishable, to any honest examiner, from a frame-up executed by a guilty man.

Unless he moved first. Unless he brought it to Odile before anyone else found the discrepancies — before Corvane's auditors finished their sweep of the winter accounts, before the deputy's tidy mind reached Volume Fourteen and drew the conclusion that had been drawn for him in advance, in ink that lay.

*Corvane.*

The name surfaced and Marguerite flinched from it. Deputy First Class Corvane, keeper of standards, the man who had taught Marguerite the abstract system himself, twelve years ago, patient and precise. The man who checked every volume quarterly. The man whose eye for another clerk's script was legendary — who could spot a falsified entry across a room, who said once, laughing, that handwriting was a face you couldn't wash.

Who else could have stitched the binding of Volume Fourteen so well?

No. Marguerite pushed the thought down. Suspicion was a fire that consumed the innocent fastest, because they had the most to burn. He had no evidence against Corvane. He had a feeling, and feelings were worth less than nothing in a guild that traded in impossible things, where feelings could be bought, planted, harvested out of a sleeping man's head along with everything else he was.

That was the thought that finally broke him open: *they can take memories here.* Someone in this guild had sold the country's name. Someone had built machines of commerce around the harvesting of minds. What was forging a page, next to that? What was forging a decade?

Had he really written those entries? Some of them? Long ago, before the exile took his memory — had his hand done this work once, honestly, and been copied? Was the frame built on truth the way the best lies always were? He looked down at the clean abstracts, the true records, and could not swear — could not *swear*, he who had sworn his life on these books — that somewhere in his erased past there wasn't a version of Marguerite Vasquez who had done every terrible thing written in Volume Fourteen.

You cannot prove innocence, he thought, with a ledger-keeper's terrible clarity, if you have sold your own witness.

* * *

He went looking for Sefa at dusk and found her instead, or she found him — waiting in the shadow of the map alcove with her coat still dusted white, holding a rolled chart like a drawn knife.

"You've seen it," she said. Not a question.

"Seen what?"

"Don't." Her voice was low and fast. "Don't do the thing where you're calm. I charted the Vhalcrest maps today. The new survey sheets coming through the smugglers' channels. Your country's coastline is on them, Marguerite. Our coastline — drawn in their ink, labeled with their transliteration, with the true name written underneath in characters that are *fading as I watch*. They're rewriting it. Page by page. And every sheet carries a provenance note."

She let that hang. Marguerite felt the counting room floor tilt beneath him, though his feet had not moved.

"Provenance," he repeated.

"'Source: authenticated guild records, certified by the Keeper of Ledgers.'" She looked at him, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow the worst part. "Your title, Marguerite. Your certification mark. They're using your seal to launder the theft. The maps cite your books."

The lamp above them guttered. Somewhere below, in the cellar where a door waited that opened onto a country he half-remembered, the frost-clock ticked its indifferent count.

"Then someone wants me to have sold my country twice," Marguerite said quietly. "Once in fact, and once on paper."

"No." Sefa stepped closer, snowmelt dripping from her hem. "Someone wants the guild to believe you sold it once. The question — the only question that matters tonight — is whether they want that because you're innocent, or because you're guilty and they're cleaning up around you."

"And how do I find out?"

"You can't." She said it flatly, a cartographer's honesty. "Not from inside. Not from any document in this building, because whoever did this controls the documents. You follow the ink outward. Forgeries have supply chains, Marguerite. Paper, ink, aging agents — tea, smoke, whatever they used. Somebody bought those materials. Somebody carried them through a gate. Find the purchase, find the buyer, find the hand."

"The guild's stores are audited," he said, then heard himself, and laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Audited by me. Or by whoever has been me lately. Gods."

Sefa tucked the chart back into her coat. "There's a stationer in the Tanners' Row who sells to both sides of the border. Old woman, half-blind, remembers everything she's ever sold because she writes it down three times. If your forger bought locally, she'll know."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight," Sefa said. "Before the guild finds Volume Fourteen before you decide how it was found."

They went down through the archive together, past the sealed abstracts, past the shelves of impossible goods catalogued in impossible detail, and at the bottom of the stairs Marguerite stopped, because the frost-clock had stopped, and in the sudden silence he heard — faint, from the direction of the counting room above — the sound of a drawer opening.

His drawer. Third table from the window.

He looked at Sefa. She had heard it too. Above them, unhurried, someone was helping himself to the storm-sigil stamp, and Marguerite stood in the dark with the only honest copies of his life pressed against his chest, and understood that the frame was not being built anymore.

It was being finished.
