## 10. The Long Winter Falls

The dead did not freeze all at once. That was the thing nobody understood about cold, Sefa thought, as she inked another district into the atlas of the lost. Cold was patient. It took a city apart the way water took a map apart—one edge, then one street, then one name at a time—until what remained was not a ruin but an absence, smooth and white and unmarked.

She had charted forty-one districts since the stars went out. She kept them in a separate book from her living maps: thin paper, cheap ink, because there was no sense wasting good materials on places that no longer existed. The Ashen Quarter. Tallow Row. The ferry villages along the Vess. Each entry got two lines—a name and a number. The number was the people.

Tallow Row had held nine hundred souls when winter began.

This morning the number was zero.

Sefa closed the book and pressed her palm flat against its cover, as if she could hold the names in by pressure alone. Outside her window, the snow came down with the same idiot gentleness it always had, fat flakes drifting past the glass like they had somewhere pleasant to be. Somewhere under that softness were nine hundred people who had stopped being a number at all, because there was no one left to count.

"You should eat," Marguerite said from the doorway.

She hadn't heard him come up the stairs. He moved quietly for a man who had once been a storm; she'd gathered that much in their weeks of uneasy partnership, though he flinched whenever anyone said the word *storm* aloud, which told her he remembered more than he admitted and less than he needed to.

"I'll eat," she said, "when I finish the count."

"The count will still be hungry tomorrow."

"That's rather the point." She turned the book toward him so he could read the entry. He was a ledger-keeper; numbers would reach him where arguments couldn't. "Nine hundred, Marguerite. Last week it was four thousand across six districts. This week—" She tapped the page. "Zero is not a small number. Zero is every number, gone."

He looked at the page for a long time. His face did the thing it always did—the practiced calm settling over him like a lid on a pot—but beneath it she saw the dread move, the way she'd learned to see weather moving behind a clear sky.

"And the guild?" he asked.

"Still counting its own warmth."

* * *

That was the phrase the city had coined in the third week of winter, when it became clear the guild's doors were not going to open again except to let supplies in: *counting its own warmth.* The Guild of Impossible Things sat on stores that could have heated the outer districts until spring—a cellar full of bottled hearthfire, jars of captured summerlight, a salamander in a brass cage whose body heat alone could have warmed a street. Impossible goods, hoarded against an impossible season, while the impossible happened everywhere outside the walls.

Guild law was explicit. In any calamity, the guild preserved its inventory first, its members second, the public last. Odile had recited it to the inner circle like a woman reading her own death sentence, and then she had enforced it anyway, because the alternative was spending the guild's future on a present that might never end.

Sefa understood the arithmetic. She hated that she understood it.

What she did not understand—and what she had begun to suspect Marguerite did not understand either—was why Odile's voice had sounded, that day, less like a leader making a hard choice and more like a woman following someone else's script.

"We go today," Marguerite said now, standing in the doorway of Sefa's archive with his coat already buttoned. Not a question. Not quite a request.

"Go where?"

"Tallow Row."

"There's nothing in Tallow Row but snow and the dead."

"There's the *why* of the dead," he said. "I've been through the ledgers again. The ward-stones that should have protected the outer districts weren't broken. They were *redirected*. Someone drew the heat off those wards and fed it into the guild's reserves. Tallow Row didn't freeze because winter came. It froze because winter was *sent*, and the receipts are sitting in our own books."

Sefa stared at him. Then she reached for her coat.

* * *

They went out through the carters' gate, because the main doors had not opened for anyone below the rank of journeyman in nineteen days, and even the carters' gate required a signed chit that Marguerite forged with the ease of long practice—which troubled Sefa more than she said, because a man who forges his own guild's seals that smoothly has done it before.

The city beyond the walls was a country Sefa half recognized. She had mapped it, years ago, in a spring so vivid it felt invented now: awnings strung between buildings, fishwives shouting prices, the smell of hot oil and river mud. Now the streets were white canyons. Snow stood waist-deep in the lanes. Doors were marked with chalk—the survivors' code, three strokes for food, two for water, none for nothing—and the marks grew sparser with every block they walked toward the river.

At the mouth of Tanner's Lane they passed a house with its door standing open and frost climbing the inside of the windows like ferns. Sefa did not look inside. She had learned, in forty-one entries, exactly how much looking she could afford.

"The refugees," Marguerite said quietly. "When they crossed the guild threshold, their memories faded. You told me that."

"I told you what I saw."

"Do you think the cold does that? Takes the memories?"

"No," Sefa said. "Cold doesn't take anything. It only reveals what was already loose." She pulled her scarf up over her chin. "Memory is being harvested, Marguerite. I've been saying it since the ferry villages. A place stops existing when no one remembers it—that's not poetry, that's cartography. Whatever stole the country's name is feeding on the smaller names too. Districts. Streets. People. Every person who freezes forgets a little faster, and every forgetting makes the winter a little stronger. It's not a season. It's an appetite."

Marguerite walked several steps without answering. When he spoke, his breath came out in a white plume that hung between them like something written and immediately smudged.

"When we get back," he said, "I'm going to open the guild's stores."

Sefa stopped walking. "You can't. Odile sealed them. The inner circle voted."

"The inner circle voted to preserve the guild. The guild *is* the people it was chartered to serve. If the charter and the survival of the guild have come apart, then the charter wins. That's not sentiment. That's bookkeeping." He said it flatly, but his hands were shaking, and Sefa noticed he shoved them into his pockets to hide it. "I'm the ledger-keeper. I know what the guild owns. I know what it owes. And right now the debt column is nine hundred names deep and growing."

"They'll call it theft."

"They'll be right. And I'll do it anyway."

There it was, Sefa thought. The moment she'd been waiting for since the night she'd recruited him—the moment the careful man with the lid on the pot finally let something boil through. She had recruited a ledger-keeper hoping for precision. She was getting, instead, a man deciding what his precision was *for*.

"Then we do it properly," she said. "If you're going to commit an impossible thing, you document it. Every jar, every lamp, every degree of heat—we log where it goes. If the guild survives to audit us, we hand them clean accounts. Let them hang us with accurate numbers."

Something moved across Marguerite's face that might have been the first real smile she'd seen from him. "You want me to keep honest books about my crimes."

"I want the crimes to be legible. There's a difference between a thief and a reformer with good records."

* * *

Tallow Row was worse than the numbers.

Numbers were Sefa's mercy and her armor; they let her hold grief at arm's length in tidy columns. But standing in the actual street, she understood that zero had a texture. It was the texture of chimneys that had gone cold so long ago that the snow on their caps had frozen into gray stalactites. It was doorways drifted shut. It was a child's sled, upright against a fence, buried to the seat. It was the silence—not the absence of sound, but the presence of it, a silence with weight, pressing down like the sky itself had decided this street no longer deserved noise.

Halfway down the row they found the ward-stone.

It stood where the crossroads had been, a pillar of green granite taller than a man, and it should have been warm. Ward-stones drank heat from the country's name and gave it back as protection; every child in the outer districts had leaned against one on winter evenings and felt it hum like a sleeping animal. This one was caked in ice a foot thick, and when Marguerite brushed the snow from its base, Sefa saw the copper conduits bolted to the stone—sleek, professional work, guild-make, running away south toward the walls.

Someone had tapped the stone like a keg.

Marguerite knelt and laid his bare hand against the ice. Sefa watched his expression change—not the practiced calm, not the dread, but something older and colder underneath, something that made her step back without deciding to.

"Heat doesn't vanish," he said softly. "Ledgers taught me that if nothing else did. Everything taken is recorded somewhere. Everything spent has a receipt." He rose, and when he looked at her, his eyes had the flat brightness of a sky before lightning. "I know these conduits. I installed half of them myself, years ago, for flood control. Someone repurposed my own engineering to bleed the outer districts dry."

"So we follow them home."

"We follow them home," he agreed. "And then we open every store in that cellar if I have to write the requisition in blood."

* * *

They were two blocks from the carters' gate when the enforcers found them.

Three of them, in white winter cloaks, guild-sigils dulled with frost. The tall one in front carried the long pole they used for testing ward integrity, though Sefa noted he carried it like a spear, which told her what the guild's enforcers thought the testing season had become.

"Ledger-keeper Vasquez." The tall one's voice was almost apologetic. "Guildmaster's compliments. Your quarters were empty at evening count."

"Evening count," Marguerite repeated. "We're counting corpses in the outer districts now. Shall I add ourselves to the roll?"

"The guildmaster asks that you return within the walls. There are matters regarding the ledgers that require your attention."

"Ah." Something flickered across Marguerite's face—too fast for Sefa to read, but slow enough to frighten her, because whatever he saw in the enforcer's words, it confirmed something he'd been carrying for days. "Matters regarding the ledgers. Would those be the pages that went missing last week? Or the ones that appeared?"

The tall enforcer's jaw tightened. Behind him, his companions shifted, and Sefa's hand crept into her coat pocket where her charting knife lived.

"Ledger-keeper. Within the walls. Tonight."

"Tomorrow," Marguerite said. "Tonight I have an inventory to take."

For a moment Sefa thought the tall one would use the pole. Instead he stepped aside—reluctantly, the way men step aside when orders and instincts disagree—and the three of them watched Marguerite and Sefa walk through the drifts toward the guild wall, two small dark figures in a white country, carrying nothing but a forged chit, a charting knife, and the beginnings of a decision that would burn a great deal warmer than it looked.

At the gate, Marguerite paused with his hand on the iron ring.

"Sefa," he said, not turning around. "When we open those stores—when the warmth goes out into the city—it won't stop at warmth. Once the guild starts giving things back, people will ask what else it's been keeping. The name. The wards. All of it."

"I know."

"And the ones who redirected the ward-stones—they're inside. They'll know tonight, or tomorrow, that I've seen the conduits. After that, every hour I stay in this building, I'm a man walking through a room where someone has arranged the furniture."

"Then why go back in at all?"

He pushed the gate open. Warmth breathed out from the guild's threshold, thick and golden and obscene, smelling of woodsmoke and bottled summer, and beyond it the corridors waited with their lamps and their ledgers and whoever had been rearranging the furniture.

"Because the receipts are inside," Marguerite Vasquez said, and stepped through.
