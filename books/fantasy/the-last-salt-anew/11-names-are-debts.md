## 11. Names Are Debts

The debt-collector found me before I could find him.

That was the first sign of how badly I'd underestimated this. Sefa and I had spent two days tracing the name *Vhalcrest* through the guild's oldest ledgers—following a trail of ink that kept dissolving under my fingers like frost in sunlight—and every road ended at the same dead end: the House of Quittance, a counting-house on Candlemaker's Row that no one entered willingly and no one left without paying.

I had planned to approach it carefully. To case the building. To prepare.

Instead, on the third morning, I woke in my rented room above the dye-works to find an old man sitting in the chair by the window, hands folded on a cane of black wood, watching me with the patience of someone who had watched many people wake up to bad news.

"You snore," he said, "in ledger-lines. Very tidy. Four beats to a breath."

I was across the room with a knife from under my pillow before I finished deciding to move. The old man didn't flinch. He looked at the blade the way a banker looks at counterfeit coin—with mild professional disappointment.

"Marguerite Vasquez," he said. "Former storm-caller. Former keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things' ledgers. Current resident of a city that is forgetting itself one snowfall at a time." He tapped the cane twice against the floorboards, and somewhere below us the dye-works fell silent—the vats, the workers, even the wind against the shutters, all of it pausing as if the whole street had been asked to hold its breath. "My name is Ossian Vetch. I collect debts of names. And you owe more than you know."

"I don't owe anyone anything," I said.

"That," said Ossian Vetch, "is precisely the problem."

* * *

He made tea while I kept the knife out and my back to the wall. He moved slowly, deliberately, like a man who had learned that haste frightened the things he handled. The kettle sang. The dye-works resumed its noise below, tentative at first, then ordinary.

"Do you know what a name-debt is?" he asked, pouring two cups.

"I've audited guild ledgers for eleven years. I've seen contracts for borrowed shadows, leases on unlived lives, a mortgage on somebody's future grief. I know what impossible debts look like."

"Then you know they're all the same debt underneath." He set a cup near me without approaching. "Everything can be priced. A shadow. A memory. A *country*. The only question is who signs, and what the collateral is." He settled back into his chair. "Three nights ago you came to the House of Quittance's outer office. You read the public registry. You were looking for transactions involving Vhalcrest."

Sefa had told me the registry existed. She hadn't told me it would be watched.

"What did you find?"

"Nothing. That's what you found. Every entry concerning Vhalcrest scrubbed clean—not torn out, not burned. *Paid.* Settled in full. Which is why you're here, in this drafty room, instead of home in your guild with your tidy books." He sipped his tea. "Because a paid debt leaves a scar, Master Vasquez, and scars don't lie about where the knife went in."

"Whose debt?"

Vetch smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a man about to do his job, and his job was ruin.

"Yours."

The word landed in the quiet room and sat there.

"That's absurd. I've never—" I stopped. Because that was the problem, wasn't it? Eleven years of ledgers, and I couldn't remember making the entry for the country's name. Couldn't remember the night the stars went out from the inside. There were whole seasons of myself that arrived in my memory already worn smooth, like stones carried a long way underwater. I had always assumed exile had taken them. Grief. Shock. The mind protects itself.

Or the mind has been *collected*.

"I have never signed anything with Vhalcrest," I said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"No," Vetch agreed pleasantly. "You signed with their *creditor*. Vhalcrest didn't buy your memories, Master Vasquez. They hold the lien. Someone else holds your paper." He reached into his coat and produced a slim document wrapped in oilcloth, which he laid on the table between us without unfolding it. "Would you like to see the terms?"

Every instinct I owned said no. I opened it anyway.

* * *

It was a standard instrument of quittance—I knew the form better than almost anyone alive, because I had processed dozens of them for the guild. Debtor: Marguerite Vasquez, storm-caller, second circle. Creditor: the House of Quittance, acting on behalf of an unnamed principal. Collateral: *the debtor's memories of the years 1189 through 1193, inclusive, together with all knowledge contained therein, per schedule attached.*

Per schedule attached.

There was no schedule attached. Of course there wasn't. The schedule was inside my head, and it had been inside Vhalcrest's files for six years, and every night since the stars went out I had been lying awake next to a hole in myself shaped exactly like those five missing years.

"This is forged," I said. My voice was steady. I was proud of that, later. "Anyone can draw up a quittance. The signature—"

"Is yours," Vetch said. "Witnessed by your guildmaster. Notarized under seal. Dated the ninth of Frostmonth, 1193—the night before you were exiled." He let that settle. "You think you were exiled for calling the storm. Everyone thinks that. Storms are crimes of passion; they're forgiven, eventually, the way weather is forgiven. No. Your exile was the *payment*, Marguerite. You sold five years of yourself to satisfy a debt, and the price of defaulting on a name-debt is that the debtor forgets the debt exists. That clause is right there—fourth paragraph, the one you're reading now with your jaw tight enough to crack teeth."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why would I sell my own—what could possibly be worth five years of my life?"

"That," said Ossian Vetch, folding his hands again, "is the interesting part. You see, I'm not just a collector. I'm also, in a manner of speaking, a historian. Names pass through my house the way grain passes through a mill, and sometimes a little flour stays in the cracks." He leaned forward. "Six years ago, a young storm-caller came to the House of Quittance at three in the morning, soaked to the bone, half-mad with something he'd learned. He wanted to make a sale so large we had to wake the senior partners. He was very clear about what he was buying with the proceeds."

"Buying."

"A quittance isn't only payment, Master Vasquez. It's purchase. You pay in memory; you receive consideration. Do you know what you bought?"

I stared at him. The tea had gone cold in my hands without my noticing.

"He bought silence," Vetch said softly. "A binding of silence, absolute and irrevocable, on one piece of information—one name, one fact—which the buyer was legally prohibited from speaking, writing, or remembering aloud, held in escrow until a condition was met." He paused. "And then, because the silence alone wasn't enough to cover the price, he threw in the five years as well. Voluntarily. The partners tried to talk him down. He wouldn't hear it. He said—" and here the old man's voice changed, taking on a cadence I recognized with a sick lurch as my own, the way I speak when I am past arguing and into certainty—"'Take the years. Take all of it. If I remember why I did this, I'll undo it.'"

The cup slipped from my fingers. Vetch caught it without looking—a small, impossible courtesy, the kind of thing that happens around people who traffic in names.

"The condition," I said. My mouth was dry. "Escrow until a condition was met. What condition?"

Vetch looked at me for a long moment, and for the first time the professional pleasantness dropped away, and what was underneath was old and tired and faintly sorry.

"The stars going out," he said. "You bought silence on the true name of this country, Marguerite. You hid it where no one could torture it out of you—not in a vault, not in a book. In the one place you could guarantee no thief would ever reach it. Your own head. And then you cut the head off the hiding place, because you understood something our senior partners took years to accept: a secret kept in a locked room can be stolen. A secret kept in a man who doesn't know he's carrying it cannot be found, because there is nothing to find. You made yourself into a vault and then you melted the key."

* * *

I don't remember standing up. I remember the window, and the snow coming down beyond it, endless, patient, eating the city one street at a time. I remember thinking: *the wards fell the same night the stars went out.* I remember thinking: *someone sold the country's name.* I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*Someone tried. And couldn't.*

Because the name wasn't in the ledgers anymore. It wasn't in the wards. It was in me—or it had been, before I paid it away, before it went into escrow, before—

"Where is it now?" I asked. I still wasn't looking at him. "If I sold it. If it went to the House of Quittance. Where does the House keep—"

"We don't keep it," Vetch said. "We can't. Silence-bonds aren't storage, Master Vasquez; they're *stasis*. The name hasn't moved in six years. It's exactly where you put it." He rose, slowly, leaning on the black cane. "In the escrow of your own erased memory. The five years you sold weren't payment, Marguerite. They were the *lockbox*. Everything you forgot—the storm, the exile, the reason—all of it is packed around that name like clay around a seed. Which means every power in this country that wants its name back, and every power that wants to make sure it never comes back, needs one thing."

I turned. He was already at the door.

"They need you," he said, "to remember."

The snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere far off, out past the border wards that no longer existed, something howled—not wolf, not wind, not anything with a throat.

"One more thing," Vetch said, hand on the latch. "Professional courtesy. Three days ago, an agent of Vhalcrest opened an account with my house. A retrieval order. Priority rating: absolute. The asset to be recovered—" he inclined his head toward me, an oddly formal little bow— "is standing in this room. They will come for you within the week. Possibly sooner. The guild's deputy signed the authorization himself."

"Corvane," I said.

"Corvane," Vetch agreed. "Do try to stay solvent, Master Vasquez. Dead men's debts are such a bother to administer." He opened the door onto the stairwell and the grey morning light, and paused. "Oh—and if you go looking for the original contract, the one with both signatures, don't trust the copy in the guild archive. It's been doctored. The true instrument has been in my strongroom for six years." His eyes glittered, dry and bright as ledger-paper. "Come collect it. Assuming, of course, you can afford the fee."

The door closed behind him. I stood in the empty room with the cold tea and the snow and the enormous, howling shape of what I had done to myself, and I understood at last why the entry in my own handwriting had felt like a stranger's work.

It was mine. All of it was mine. I had built this prison around the country's name brick by brick with my own hands, and then I had walked out of it and thrown away the map, because the alternative was to carry the knowledge of what I knew—and knowing it, I might have given it up. Under torture. Under grief. Under the slow persuasion of someone I loved.

So I had made sure there was nothing left to love it out of me.

Outside, the winter went on falling. And somewhere across the white city, in an embassy full of warm light, men were already signing papers with my name in the margin—and the only way to stop them was to become, once more, the man I had paid six years to never be again.
