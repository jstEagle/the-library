## 13. Fugitive in a White Country

They came for us an hour before dawn, and I only knew because the wards on Sefa's archive sang.

Not rang—sang. A thin, high note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of guild iron being unhooded. Enforcers carried hoods over their instruments the way other men carried sheaths over knives. The hood meant they hadn't decided yet whether to draw.

I was awake anyway. I had been awake most of the night, sitting in the map room with Odile's letter open on my knees, reading it for the ninth time as though the words might rearrange themselves into something that made sense of my life.

*You were never the traitor, Marguerite. You were the price someone else paid.*

Sefa appeared in the doorway with a lamp turned down to a coal. She had slept in her clothes again; there was ink on her jaw from where she'd rested her chin on her hand while working. Behind her, the archive's shelves rose into the dark like cliffs—every one of them holding maps of places that no longer existed, places eaten by wars or winters or worse, drawn by cartographers who refused to let absence be the end of the record.

"They're at the corner," she said. "Six of them. Maybe eight."

"Guild?"

"Guild colors under civilian cloaks." She set the lamp down. "Corvane is thorough. He wants you taken quiet, before the city wakes up and starts asking why the Guild of Impossible Things is hunting its own ledger-keeper through the snow."

"And Odile?"

"With you or dead," Sefa said flatly. "Those are the options Corvane has left her. She'll choose the first if she can. She's too stubborn to die in someone else's story."

I folded the letter and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where my heartbeat could argue with it. Then I took up my ledger—the real one, the true copy, not the forged thing Corvane's people had seeded through the guild stacks—and wrapped it in oilcloth.

"What's in that that's worth dying for?" Sefa asked. Not cruelly. She was the kind of person who needed to know what a thing weighed before she agreed to carry it.

"A country," I said. "Or the memory of one. I'm no longer sure which."

* * *

We went out through the coal cellar.

Sefa's archive sat on a street that had been respectable once, back when the city had seasons and the river froze in patterns people wrote poems about. Now the whole district was buried to the second floor, snow packed hard as salt pork, and the cellar door opened directly onto a white slope where a bakery used to be. You could still see the chimney sticking out of the drift like a periscope.

The cold hit me the way cold always hits a storm-caller: personally. It found the lightning sleeping in my bones and pressed its thumb there, curious. I breathed through it. Twelve years of practice. Exile teaches you to hold your own weather like a full cup, careful not to spill.

Sefa moved ahead of me, quick and low, following a route she'd clearly mapped in advance—which shouldn't have surprised me, because mapping was all she did, but it did anyway. It surprised me that anyone had ever bothered to chart this drowned white country we were fleeing through. She paused at each buried landmark—a lamppost, a hitching ring, the corner of a wall that had been a tavern—and touched them like old friends.

"Here," she said, crouching beside a drift. "Under here. Old storm drain. Runs two streets east before it collapses."

"You've used it before."

"I've *mapped* it before," she said. "There's a difference. Maps are promises. Using them is faith." She pried up the grate with a bar she'd brought for exactly that purpose, and the smell came up out of the dark—old stone, standing water, the mineral breath of a city holding its breath underground.

I went down first. If there was an enforcer waiting at the bottom, better it found a storm-caller than a mapmaker.

There wasn't. There was only the drain, and the dark, and the sound of our breathing, and above us, muffled by three feet of snow, the tread of boots passing over the grate.

We waited until they faded. In that silence I became aware of how loud fear is when you finally stop running from it and listen. It isn't a shout. It's a ledger. It itemizes. *They know your face. They know your handwriting better than you do. They took your name off the guild rolls an hour ago and wrote 'traitor' where it used to say 'Marguerite Vasquez.' Every door in the city will close now, except the ones nobody remembers exist.*

Except, I thought, that was precisely Sefa's profession.

"Sefa." My voice echoed down the drain. "How many doors are there in this city that nobody remembers?"

She smiled in the dark. I couldn't see it, but I heard it.

"More than Corvane could seal in a year," she said. "Come on. We're going to need a map."

* * *

We surfaced near the tanneries, in a district so poor the winter hadn't finished killing it yet. That sounds like mercy. It wasn't. It was just that death moved slower where there was less to burn.

People stood in doorways watching us pass. They knew what we were—I'd been recognized twice within the first hundred yards, faces turning away quickly, the way you look away from something contagious. The guild's broadsheets had gone up overnight on every surviving post:

**BY ORDER OF THE INNER CIRCLE: MARGUERITE VASQUEZ, LEDGER-KEEPER, AND GUILDMASTER ODILE FERRAN, TRAITORS TO THE CHARTER. INFORMATIONS RECEIVED AT ANY GUILDHOUSE. REWARD: WARMTH.**

*Reward: warmth.* Two words that would have meant nothing a season ago. Now they were worth more than gold, more than blood. The guild hoarded heat the way dragons hoarded coin, and Corvane had learned to pay his informants in degrees.

A woman with a child wrapped in every scrap of cloth she owned spat as we passed. Not at me—at the broadsheet, actually, though I flinched as if the spit had landed true.

"Traitors," she said, but without heat. Almost wondering. "That's what they're calling them."

"The guildmaster too?" the child asked.

"The guildmaster *especially*."

I kept walking. But something lodged under my ribs and stayed there. Because I had read those broadsheets, and I knew what was true in them and what was forged, and the truth was this: Corvane hadn't needed to invent much. He'd needed only to take the real crime—the sale of the country's name, written in my stolen hand—and point it at the two people who were closest to finding him. Odile, who had convened the inner circle and started asking questions with her chin lifted like a blade. And me, whose handwriting was the murder weapon.

*Someone knows your hand intimately.* I'd written that in my own notes, weeks ago, like a man diagnosing a disease in someone else. It had never once occurred to me that the disease was mine.

* * *

We stopped at noon in the shell of a counting-house whose roof had collapsed under the snow-load, half-buried, warm-ish in the way that ruins trap what little heat survives them. Sefa spread her charts on a broken desk and weighted the corners with coins.

"Here's the problem," she said. "The city has maybe four hundred ways in and out, and Corvane knows three hundred and ninety of them, because he has the guild's records and the guild recorded everything the founders built. Ward-doors, gate-tunnels, the old ambassadorial passages from before the border fell. What he doesn't know—"

"—is the places that don't exist anymore," I said.

"Is the places that don't exist *on paper he can reach*." She tapped a chart done in brown ink gone almost amber with age. It showed our district as it had been two hundred years ago, when it was called something else and the river ran where the tanneries now stood. "This street was called Vhal Street, incidentally. Before Vhalcrest, even. Names get reused. Names get *stolen*. That's rather the point of everything, isn't it."

I looked at her. She didn't look up from the chart.

"How long have you known about the name?" I asked quietly.

"Known? I suspected. Charted around the edges of it." She did look up then, and her eyes were steady in a way that steadied me. "Marguerite. I recruit people who can walk through absences. You are, functionally, an absence with legs. Half of you is missing and everyone can see the hole where it used to be. I didn't bring you along out of charity."

It should have stung. Instead it felt like being seen for the first time in twelve years.

"So where do we go?" I said. "Odile is hiding somewhere in the Salt Quarter with maybe six loyal people and a warrant on her head. Corvane controls the guildhouses, the warmth, the ward-keys, and the story. And somewhere across that white border, Vhalcrest is preparing to finish eating a country that no longer has a name to defend itself with."

Sefa rolled up the chart and began packing.

"First," she said, "we get to Odile, because an army of six loyal people is still an army, and because you two have unfinished business—you especially. You saw her meeting the envoy. You *misread* it. I've read her file in the guild registry, what survives of it. Odile Ferran has spent thirty years paying bribes she hated paying to keep the guild neutral, and the one time she got caught doing it, she was buying time for the city, not selling it. You owe her an apology."

"I owe her more than that."

"Yes," Sefa said. "You probably do. Second—" she slung her pack over her shoulder, and the coins slid off the corners of the abandoned chart, and the amber street names caught the grey light one last time before the paper curled—"we go and find the one door in this city that appears on none of the guild's records because it was never built. It was *remembered*. There's a difference, and the difference is going to save our lives."

* * *

The Salt Quarter had been a fortress district once, back when countries defended themselves with walls instead of paperwork. Its streets ran narrow and steep, and the snow lay on the rooftops in slabs that groaned sometimes in the wind, like a ship. Somewhere below us, in the buried levels, refugees huddled around guild-distributed heat, trading memories for warmth because they had nothing else left to trade, and every night the harvesters came with their quiet instruments and skimmed another layer off the top of who these people were.

I thought about that as we climbed. About memory as currency. About a debt-collector of names telling me, in a voice like dry paper, *you sold yours first, storm-caller. Everyone in this economy is either buyer or sold.*

And I thought about the counter-ward. The thing I supposedly carried in my erased head—the one piece of knowledge that could lock the country's name back into place, if I could only survive remembering it. Every step toward Odile was a step toward the ritual. Toward the price. Toward the possibility that restoring my memories would kill me before Corvane's enforcers got the chance.

Fear, I discovered somewhere on those stairs, does not go away. It sharpens. It becomes a tool, the way a storm becomes lightning if you know how to hold the handle.

"Stop," Sefa whispered.

We had come out onto a landing overlooking the Salt Quarter's central square—or what had been the square, now a white bowl with a frozen fountain at its heart. And there, crossing the square with the unhurried stride of a man who owns the weather, walked Deputy Corvane.

He was alone. That was the frightening part. Eight enforcers had hunted us through the drains all morning, and here was the architect of it all walking open-eyed through the district he'd branded us traitors in, because he knew no one would touch him. He wore grey, unremarkable, and he stopped at the frozen fountain and stood looking at it for a long moment, and then—he took something from his pocket and set it on the fountain's rim.

Then he walked on, and was gone down the far stair.

Sefa looked at me. I looked at Sefa. And then I went down to the fountain, because some part of me already knew, the way you know the shape of your own handwriting, what would be waiting there.

It was a page. Torn from a ledger. My ledger.

One line, in my hand, in my ink, in my slanted, cramped, unmistakable script:

*The counter-ward dies with the man who holds it. Come home, Marguerite. Bring the page. We can still make a bargain.*

No signature. No signature needed.

I stood there in the white square with the page trembling in my fingers, and the fear that had sharpened all day finally found its edge and pointed itself outward instead of in.

He didn't want me dead. He wanted me *recruited*—the last living key to the country's name, delivered willingly to Vhalcrest's table, signing myself over the way I apparently once had before, in a life I couldn't remember.

"Marguerite," Sefa said softly, behind me. "Whatever it says—we should move. There's a door on Vhal Street that only opens for people who remember it. And I think—" she hesitated, and for once the mapmaker sounded unsure of her map, "—I think Odile may already be there. Waiting. She left me a message an hour ago. Three words."

"What words?"

"*'The house remembers.'*"

I put the page in my coat, next to Odile's letter, where my heartbeat could argue with both of them, and followed Sefa toward a door that had never been built and could not be found by anyone who had forgotten how to believe in it.

Behind us, the snow kept falling on a country that existed, for now, only in its people's remembering.

And I intended to make sure it kept existing.
