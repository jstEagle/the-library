## 17. Corvane's Country

The embassy smelled of cedar and cold iron, and Corvane had learned to love both.

He stood at the tall window of the signing room and watched the snow come down over Vhalcrest Square. It fell the way it had fallen for four months now — steadily, indifferently, like ash from a fire no one remembered starting. Below him, the last lamplighters of the old country made their rounds with torches instead of oil, because the oil was gone, because everything was going, because the country itself was going the way of its stars: out.

Behind him, a servant laid out the documents.

Corvane did not turn around yet. He wanted one more minute of looking at the city he was about to unmake. That was not sentiment. Sentiment was what had ruined everyone else in this affair — Odile clinging to her guild like a mother to a drowning child, Marguerite Vasquez haunting his ledgers like a ghost who hadn't finished dying. Corvane had no such weaknesses. He had arithmetic.

And the arithmetic said: *this* is how you save ten thousand people. Not with storms. Not with wards. With ink.

"Deputy Corvane." The envoy's voice came from the doorway, smooth as poured cream. "The Ambassador will see you now."

"I know," Corvane said. "I've been seeing him all week."

He turned from the window.

* * *

Ambassador Ilvane Vess received him in a room without clocks.

Corvane had noticed that on his first visit and had spent three sleepless nights working out why it unsettled him before admitting the obvious answer: Vhalcrest did not need time. Time belonged to countries that intended to continue. Vhalcrest's business was endings, and endings kept their own schedule.

Vess sat behind a desk of black glass, small and neat, gray-haired, with the mild face of a schoolteacher. That was the trick of them, Corvane thought. Everyone expected conquerors to look like wolves. They looked like clerks. They looked like men doing paperwork.

"You've brought it," Vess said. Not a question.

"The guild seal. The ward-keys. And my signature on the instrument of transfer." Corvane set them down one by one on the black glass, each item landing with a small, precise click. "As agreed."

"And the Guildmaster?"

"Contained."

"Contained," Vess repeated, tasting the word. "She believes she's a prisoner in her own guild, awaiting rescue."

"She believes whatever keeps her quiet." Corvane sat, uninvited, because sitting uninvited was one of the few pleasures left to him. "By the time anyone comes for her, there'll be nothing here worth rescuing her *to*. She'll understand eventually. Odile is many things, but she has never been stupid about survival."

"And Vasquez?"

The name landed in Corvane's chest like a swallowed stone. He let it settle before answering.

"Fugitive. In the white districts, most likely, with that cartographer woman. He knows his ledgers are compromised. What he doesn't know—" Corvane smiled thinly, "—is that I know them better than he does. Better than he knows himself. Literally better. He sold the parts of himself that would have understood what we're doing tonight."

"Yes," Vess said softly. "His memories. A remarkable transaction. You brokered it yourself."

"I did." Nine years ago. A frightened storm-caller in a cellar that smelled of salt, begging for exile instead of execution, and Corvane — then just a junior factor with a talent for contracts — showing him the mercy of forgetting. *You won't even miss it,* he'd told him. And it had been true. That was the beauty of it. Marguerite had signed away the storm, the city, the dead, the whole terrible weight of what he'd done, and walked out lighter than any man had a right to be.

Corvane had watched him go and felt something he refused, even now, to call pride.

"He may still be dangerous," Vess said. "Men who have lost their memories sometimes find their way back to them."

"Not in time."

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain of the schedule," Corvane said. "The signing completes at moonrise. After that, the name transfers permanently, the wards re-key to Vhalcrest authority, and this country becomes a province with a nostalgic accent. Whatever Vasquez remembers after that will be history. And history—" he tapped the black desk, "—is written by whoever holds the ledger."

Vess studied him for a long moment. There was something almost like respect in the gray eyes, or perhaps only appetite wearing respect's coat.

"You understand," Vess said, "that when this is done, your position here is... unusual. You'll be the highest-ranking officer of a dissolved institution. A deputy of nothing."

"I'll be the man who ended a war before it started," Corvane said. "Titles can be negotiated afterward. I've read your annexation protocols, Ambassador. Article Twelve. Provisional stewardship. I did my homework."

"I'm beginning to believe you did."

"Then let's not waste each other's evening." Corvane drew the instrument of transfer across the desk and uncapped his pen. "Where do you want me?"

* * *

But Vess raised a hand, and Corvane — hating himself for the flicker of obedience — stopped.

"A question first," the ambassador said. "A formality. Our protocols require it, and I confess some personal curiosity." He folded his small hands on the black glass. "Why?"

"Why what?"

"Why any of it. You were born here. Educated here. You rose through their guild. By every measure we possess, you should have been loyal to them." Vess tilted his head. "And instead you sold us the true name of your own country. I have bought many things, Corvane. Countries, mostly, in pieces. But I always ask the seller why, because the why tells me whether I can trust the sale to hold."

Corvane set down the pen.

He had prepared an answer for this question years ago, in the same way he prepared every answer — carefully, rehearsed, armored. *Because they were weak. Because they deserved what was coming.* All true, as far as it went. But Vess had a schoolteacher's patience, and schoolteachers could smell a memorized recitation.

So Corvane gave him the true one instead. Or close enough to true that the difference didn't matter.

"Do you know what the guild did during the famine year?" he said. "Before your people ever looked at our border?"

"Tell me."

"They hoarded." He heard his own voice go flat and even, the voice he used for audits. "The winter came early and the crops failed, and the Guild of Impossible Things — keeper of wonders, guardian of the impossible — locked its doors and inventoried its goods. We had warmth enough in the vaults to keep the outer districts alive. We had memory-charms, weather-glass, a hundred miracles on the shelves. And Odile ruled that releasing them would destabilize the market for wonder. Her words. *Destabilize the market for wonder.*" He laughed once, without humor. "Four hundred people died in Ashfall Row that winter. I counted them. Counting is what I do. And the guild wrote a beautiful memorial ledger, and hung a plaque, and went on selling impossibilities to people who could afford them."

"That is a grievance," Vess said. "It is not yet a reason."

"It became one." Corvane leaned forward. "Do you know what I realized, counting those bodies? That country doesn't exist. It never did. Not really. It exists in ledgers, in stories, in the collective agreement of people who agree to remember it. Take away the remembering and there's nothing underneath but cold and hunger and men like Odile charging admission to the dark." His hands were steady on the desk; he made sure of it. "Your power didn't take anything from these people, Ambassador. It offered to stop the lie gently. Erasure is mercy. I've seen the alternative. The alternative is a war in an endless winter, cities burning in the snow, and Marguerite Vasquez calling down another storm because it's the only thing he was ever good at."

"And so you chose the knife that cuts without bleeding."

"I chose the knife that works."

Vess regarded him for a moment longer. Then, softly: "You know that Vasquez once saved this country. With a storm. Did you know that?"

Something cold moved along Corvane's spine, slow as frost climbing a windowpane.

"I know he was exiled for calling one."

"Mm." Vess unfolded his hands. "Our archives say otherwise. Our archives say the storm he called broke our first invasion fleet against the coast nine years ago. Your guild exiled its savior and erased his memory, and called the erasure punishment." The ambassador smiled his schoolteacher smile. "I mention it because you should understand what you're buying tonight, Deputy. You are not ending a war. You are finishing one that a storm-caller interrupted. And storms, in my experience, remember being interrupted."

For one absurd heartbeat Corvane wanted to laugh. Fear dressed as laughter, maybe, or laughter dressed as contempt — with Vess it was hard to tell the difference, and harder to tell which one he was feeling himself.

"Marguerite Vasquez," Corvane said, "doesn't remember how to be a storm. I made sure of that personally. I drew up the contract myself. I held the pen while he signed away every scrap of it. There is nothing left in that man but bookkeeping and guilt." He picked up his pen again. "Now. The schedule."

This time Vess nodded.

Corvane bent over the instrument of transfer.

The document was a masterpiece — he had revised it eleven times — written in contract-ink that bound deeper than law, in language older than either country. *One (1) nation's true name, complete and undivided, together with all wards, claims, borders, and memories appurtenant thereto.* Seller: the Guild of Impossible Things, by its lawful deputy. Witness: the Crown of Vhalcrest, by its ambassador.

Below the seller's line, his signature already waited: *E. Corvane*, in a hand so like Marguerite's that even he sometimes found it unsettling. He had studied that hand for years. Studied it the way a forger studies, yes, but also the way a student studies a teacher whose lessons he intends to surpass.

He signed the final page.

The ink took with a sound like a held breath released. Somewhere far beneath the embassy, Corvane felt — actually felt, in his teeth, in the roots of his hair — the wards shift their grip. The country's name loosening in the world's mouth. Another hour and it would be done. Another hour and the snow could stop falling on a place that no longer needed to exist, because there would be nothing left for it to fall on except a province.

"There," Vess said quietly. "That wasn't so difficult."

"No," Corvane said. "It never is. That's what none of them understand. Mercy is easy. It's the martyrs who make everything hard."

He stood, gathered his seals, and allowed himself the smallest human luxury: he looked at the window one last time. Snow over the rooftops. Torchlight in the lanes. Somewhere out there, a fugitive cartographer and a half-erased storm-caller were hiding in the ruins of the white districts, believing — because belief was free, the last free thing — that a country could be saved by remembering it.

Let them believe. Belief was just another entry waiting to be balanced.

At the door, Vess spoke again, mild as milk.

"One more formality, Deputy. The final instrument requires a second witness. Guild protocol, I'm told — two signatures above the seal, or the transfer won't hold." He slid a fresh page across the black glass. "Yours is there. We need one more."

Corvane frowned. "From whom? Odile won't sign."

"Not her." The ambassador's smile widened by a single degree. "Someone whose hand the wards will recognize as guild authority. Someone whose signature appears on the original contract — the one your fugitive is hunting even now."

He turned the page toward Corvane.

The witness line was already filled in.

And the name written there, in careful, familiar strokes, was not a name Corvane had written, nor Vess, nor anyone in this room —

— and across the embassy, faint but unmistakable, the first thunder of the season rolled through the endless snow.
