## 21. Stars, Returning

The first star came back over the harbor.

I know because I was standing in the embassy's shattered atrium, ledger open against my chest, watching the wards unravel like thread pulled from a hem, when a point of light punched through the smoke above Vhalcrest's broken sigil and hung there, trembling, as if it couldn't believe its own existence.

Then a second. A third.

By the fourth, I was on my knees, and I'm not ashamed to write that down. Ledgers are for truth, even the truths about ourselves that we'd rather burn.

The storm I had called — every storm I had ever called, returned to me at once in the archive beneath Sefa's maps, wearing me like a coat of lightning — went out of the sky all at once. It didn't fade. It *finished*. The way a sentence finishes. The way a debt closes. One moment the clouds above the city were a black ocean churning with my name, and the next they tore open down the middle, and cold clean air poured through the gap, and the stars were there behind them, thousands of them, all of them, the whole impossible census of the night sky accounted for at last.

I counted. Of course I counted. Somewhere between the fifth star and the fiftieth I realized I was laughing, or sobbing, or both, and that Odile was on the floor ten feet away with Corvane's knife-wound in her side and Sefa pressing both hands against it, shouting words I couldn't parse, and none of it mattered as much as the fifty-first star, which arrived late, like a guest who'd stopped to argue at the door.

The winter broke that same hour.

I don't mean it eased. I mean it broke — a sound like the whole country exhaling after holding one breath for a hundred and forty days. The snow stopped mid-fall. I watched flakes hang in the air above the embassy steps, suspended, undecided, and then fall the rest of the way like ordinary snow, landing like ordinary snow, melting on the hot stone where my storm had struck. The temperature rose so fast that frost crawled *backward* off the windows. By dawn the gutters were running. By full light there were people in the streets who weren't digging, weren't burning furniture, weren't carrying the frozen. They were just standing in the slush looking up, doing what everyone in this country does when the sky does something worth remembering: they were memorizing it.

That is, I understand now, how we survived at all. Not the wards. Not the guild. Us. A country exists only in its people's remembering, and for one hundred and forty days we had remembered hard enough to keep breathing.

But I'm ahead of myself. There was blood on the atrium floor, and most of it was Odile's.

* * *

They carried her up three flights to the embassy's infirmary, which Vhalcrest had kept stocked with medicines better than anything our own healers had seen since the borders fell. There is a bitter joke in that which I refuse to make. She was conscious the whole way, giving instructions.

"Sefa — inventory of everything in this building before the enforcers touch it. Marguerite — the ledgers, all of them, mine included, into one room under guard. And someone find out if Corvane signed."

"He didn't," I said. "I was faster."

"Was anyone hurt?"

"Corvane's people surrendered when the stars came back." I hesitated. "Some of them cried."

Odile closed her eyes. "Of course they did," she said. "They thought they were saving us." And then, quieter, mostly to herself: "So did he. That's what makes it unforgivable."

She slept for two days. I sat with her through the worst of the fever, reading ledger entries aloud in a low voice because the healer said familiar voices helped and because I couldn't think of anything else to do with my hands. I read her the guild's founding accounts. I read her the entry from the year she took mastership, the modest line item: *one guild, inherited, condition used.* I read her the page where I had once written, in my old life, before exile, before erasure: *audited by M. Vasquez; discrepancies noted; Guildmaster Odile informed; her response recorded as laughter.*

"She laughed?" Sefa asked from the doorway, on the second evening. They'd been cataloguing the embassy for two days straight and looked like a map left out in rain.

"For an hour," I said. "It's in the margin. Three exclamation points. I remember writing it. That's the strange part — everything else from those years is still fog, but that page came back whole."

"Memory's a map too," Sefa said. "It comes back along the roads you walked most often." They hesitated. "She's going to step down. You know that."

"I know that."

"Do you? Because half the guild thinks you'll take the chair yourself. Storm-caller, restorer of names, the man who wrote a country back into existence. There's already talk. Songs, Marguerite. There are *songs*, and they're terrible, and one of them has a verse about your eyes."

"My eyes?"

"Lightning-colored. Apparently." Sefa's mouth twisted. "The verse is worse than I've made it sound."

* * *

The reckoning began on the third day, in the guild's great hall, which still bore the scorch-marks from the night the wards fell — we had never repaired them, some superstition about not papering over a wound — and which now held nearly every member of the Guild of Impossible Things who could walk there through the melt-water streets.

I stood at the front beside Odile, who insisted on standing though the healer had prescribed a week flat on her back, and I watched four hundred faces try to understand what we told them.

We told them everything. That was Odile's decision, made before she could be argued out of it, and it was the right one, and it cost her more than the knife did. We told them about the sale of the country's true name. About Corvane — who sat in irons in a cell below, refusing food, refusing counsel, asking only for paper and ink, which Odile granted him, because even now she believes a man should be allowed to account for himself, even falsely, even beautifully. We told them about the forged entries in my hand, the torn pages, the years of quiet rewriting that had turned a guild of custodians into a guild of accomplices without any single signature ever saying so aloud.

And then we opened the floor, and the guild did what guilds do.

They argued about procedure for six hours.

I want to record that honestly, because it would be easy to dress it up as something nobler. There were moments of nobility — a junior appraiser named Wren, who had lost her family's farm to the winter, stood and said that the question wasn't who to punish but what to *stop being*, and got a standing ovation — but between the noble moments there were six hours of jurisdictional dispute, factional accusation, a fistfight between two archivists over a filing system, and one elderly certifier of miraculous weights who fell asleep upright and snored through the entire testimony about the harvested memories.

Exhausted relief looks like that. I know the songs won't say so. But the songs weren't there when a woman whose brother had been taken by the white country — his memories stripped, his body walking somewhere in Vhalcrest now, empty — stood up and asked, in a perfectly level voice, whether the guild's charter had ever contained a single sentence protecting the people it served, and the answer, audited honestly, was no. The charter protected things. Impossible things. Rare things. Valuable things. People appeared only as sources, couriers, collateral.

"We kept a country's true name in a vault," Odile said at last, when the hall had gone quiet enough to hear the meltwater running in the walls, "and we never wrote down a single ordinary person's name unless they owed us money. That's the finding. I move it be entered into the record."

"It is so moved," said the hall, four hundred voices, ragged and unwilling and true.

"And the second finding," Odile said, "is that the guildmaster bears responsibility for what the guild became on her watch." She let the murmur rise and fall. "Which brings me to the third matter."

She looked at me. Four hundred faces followed hers.

"No," I said.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say my name."

"I was going to say your name," she agreed. "Marguerite Vasquez. Who erased his own memory to survive exile and then spent a winter auditing his own ghost. Who found the theft in his own handwriting and refused to look away from it, even when looking away would have been kinder to him than to us. Who called down every storm he ever summoned rather than let a clerk sign this country into a vault." Her voice cracked, just once, on the last clause, and she pressed on through it. "I move that the guild offer him the chair."

"Seconded," said half the hall.

"No," I said again, and this time I said it loudly enough to reach the back wall. "No. Thank you. No."

The silence that followed had a different texture. Curiosity. Relief, in some corners, I suspect. Wren was watching me with her chin lifted, waiting to see what I'd actually say, because she understood before I said it that refusal can be a kind of answer.

"I spent a hundred and forty days learning what happens when an institution keeps its books cleaner than its conscience," I said. "The ledgers were immaculate. Every theft was filed correctly. Corvane will tell you the same thing from his cell — he'll show you the paperwork, because there *was* paperwork, because we taught him that paperwork is virtue." I looked down at the ledger in my hands, the old one, scarred and salt-stained, the one that had anchored a country's name back into the world. "I don't want to preside over this guild. I want to help it become something that wouldn't need presiding over. And I want —"

I stopped. Because what I wanted hadn't fully formed until that moment, standing in the scorched hall with the stars visible through the high windows and the sound of running water everywhere, the whole country thawing around us like a held breath finally released.

"I want to see what's outside," I finished. "There's a door in the cellar of this building that opens onto a country I only half-remember. It's still there. Sefa checked yesterday. And there are other doors. If our borders fell because someone sold our name, then other countries' names are sitting in vaults too, and other winters are coming, and somebody has to go out and knock." I shrugged, feeling suddenly absurd, four hundred faces staring at the man with lightning-colored eyes. "Also I am very tired, and I would like to sleep for approximately a season."

The laugh that went through the hall was small and ragged and real, and I'll keep it.

* * *

Odile found me that night in the cellar, by the door.

I'd brought a lamp and the old ledger and I was just standing there, the way I had stood there in the early weeks of winter, when the door was a wound I couldn't stop touching. Beyond it, faintly, the smell of a country I had loved and failed and half-forgotten: cut grass, river water, woodsmoke from a hearth someone else was tending.

"You're leaving," she said.

"In the morning. Sefa's coming. Someone has to chart the roads between here and wherever the doors go, and Sefa claims unmapped territory gives them hives, which I think means they can't wait."

"Mm." She lowered herself carefully onto an overturned crate, one hand pressed to the bandage at her side, and looked at the door the way she looked at difficult entries in a ledger: directly, and for a long time. "The council accepted my resignation tonight. Effective at the spring convocation. Wren's going to run for mastership. She'll win." A pause. "She should win."

"The guild will survive you."

"The guild will *change*, which is different and overdue." She was quiet a moment. "I lied to you, during the winter. In the hall, when I convened the inner circle. I suspected the leak was inside within the first week and I said nothing, because saying it would have meant admitting what I suspected about Corvane, and I loved him like a brother and I gambled that I was wrong. My lie bought him time. People died in the time it bought." She said it flatly, without performance, which is the only way Odile knows how to confess. "You should have that in your ledger too. The guildmaster lied."

I took out the ledger and wrote it. *Audited by M. Vasquez. Discrepancy noted. Confession received and recorded.* I showed her the entry. She read it twice, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, the way a building settles after a storm.

"That's the first honest audit this guild has had in twenty years," she said. "Do you know what Corvane asked me, when I visited his cell? He asked if I thought erasure was mercy. He genuinely believed it. He signed away a country the way you'd sign a dying friend's DNR — convinced he was sparing us pain." She shook her head slowly. "I told him mercy isn't something you do to people in secret. That's the whole of it, isn't it? Everything that went wrong this winter went wrong in secret. And everything that went right—" she gestured upward, toward the hall, the streets, the returning stars, "—went right in the open, in ink, witnessed."

"That's why you're stepping down, isn't it. Not the knife."

"Not the knife." She smiled, tired and crooked. "A guildmaster should be someone the members never have to wonder about. After this winter, I'm a thing they wonder about. Best cure for wondering is a fresh face and open books." She pushed herself up off the crate, wincing. "Walk me up? The stairs and I are renegotiating terms."

We climbed together, slowly, her arm in mine, past the scorched hall and the sleeping archives and the rooms where refugees had wintered and would soon, God willing, go home. At the top of the stairs there was a window, and through it the sky was doing the thing it had done for three nights now that still made people stop in the streets: being ordinary. Dark, starred, indifferent, ours.

"One more thing," Odile said at the top. "Before you go through that door. Whatever's out there — other countries, other vaults, other names in other ledgers — you'll find the same rot. Institutions that love their inventories more than their people. Don't go out there to audit them, Marguerite."

"What should I do instead?"

"Go out there and teach them to laugh in the margins," she said, and squeezed my arm, and went to bed.

* * *

I stayed in the cellar a while longer after she left. The lamp burned low. Through the door, the other country breathed its grass-and-river smell into the dark, patient as a held note.

I opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote the heading I'd been carrying around unspoken for weeks: *Accounts of the Outside.* Under it, the first entry, dated properly, witnessed properly:

*Item: one door, previously unwarranted, opening outward. Condition: used. Provenance: forgotten, now restored. Value: —*

I sat with the blank space after *Value* for a long time. Then I wrote:

*Value: to be determined by what we carry through it.*

Tomorrow morning Sefa and I walk out of the only country that remembers itself, into all the ones that have forgotten how. I have a lamp, a ledger, and a guildmaster's blessing delivered in the form of an insult about margins.

It's more than enough. It's exactly enough.

In the morning, we knock.
