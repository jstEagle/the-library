## 22. Anew

The guild hall had never been this full for a ceremony that involved no one dying.

I stood at the long table where the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things had been kept for three hundred years, and I watched the inner circle arrange themselves in their old seats — Odile's chair empty at the head, Corvane's seat conspicuously vacant, a folded cloth draped over the backrest the way you drape a cloth over a mirror in a house of mourning. Sefa sat where the mapmakers always sat, which was nowhere official, which meant she'd dragged a stool to the end of the table and dared anyone to object. Nobody objected. After what she'd charted through the white country to bring us home, they'd have dragged the stool for her.

The restoration scroll lay in front of me. Odile had written it herself, in her own hand, no deputy's forgery anywhere near it. *Marguerite Vasquez, storm-caller, ledger-keeper first class, restored to rank and standing by unanimous consent of the surviving council.* Unanimous. The word still tasted strange. For most of my life I had assumed that unanimity was something that happened to other people, in better countries, on days when nothing was on fire.

"You don't have to sign it," Odile said quietly. She stood beside my chair, not behind it, not above it. Her arm was still bound from the embassy — Corvane's enforcers had been thorough before the wards came down, before the storm came up, before everything ended and began in the same hour. "The rank is yours whether or not you take the title."

"That's not how ledgers work," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

I picked up the pen. It was the same pen I'd used to write a country's true name back into existence eleven days ago, because apparently I was sentimental now, and the nib still carried a faint scorch mark along one side like a scar. I wrote my signature beneath Odile's. Then, because honest ledgers were the whole point, the entire point, the only thing left that mattered — I crossed it out.

The room went very still.

"Marguerite," someone started, and I held up a hand.

"Hear me out. I'm keeping the rank. First class, ledger-keeper, all of it — the work is mine and I'm not giving it back." I tapped the crossed-out signature. "But the title comes with a seat on the inner circle, and a seat means authority over the guild as it stands. And the guild as it stands is a building full of hoarded warmth in a city that nearly froze while we counted our stores." I looked down the table, meeting eyes. Ferren, who had followed Corvane too far and knew it. Old Marette, who had signed the neutrality papers without reading them because Odile told her to. All of them flinching a little, all of them here anyway. That counted for something. It didn't count for enough.

"I'll serve," I said. "But not inside these walls. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

* * *

They let me go without a fight, which told me more about how much the guild had changed than any resolution could have. Odile walked me to the doors herself, past the great sealed bolts that had locked us in on the night the stars went out, now thrown open for good. Someone had already begun unscrewing them from their housings. There was talk of melting them down into bell-metal. There was talk of a lot of things. A country that exists only in its people's remembering turns out, once remembered properly, to be a country full of arguments about bells.

"The council will vote on restructuring within the month," Odile said. "If you change your mind—"

"I won't."

"If you *change your mind*," she repeated, with the particular patience of a woman who had taken a knife meant for me and refused to make it dramatic, "the door is open. Both of them, actually."

Both of them. I looked down the cellar stair, visible from the entry hall now — we'd removed the false wall entirely rather than reseal it. At the bottom, half-lost in shadow, stood the door that was never built, the one my own forgotten memory had hidden from me, the one that opened onto a country I had half-remembered and then, after the ritual, remembered whole: orchards under a sky with stars in it. My mother's kitchen. The storm I called down on Vhalcrest's first invasion, lightning standing in the streets like trees.

And beyond it, now, the other door. The new one. The one the cartographers were arguing about even as we spoke.

"Odile." I hesitated. There was a thing I had been carrying since the night she volunteered to hold my name while the memories tore back into me, since I woke wreathed in lightning on Sefa's archive floor with her hand clamped around my wrist and my name in her mouth like something rescued from a burning house. "Thank you doesn't—"

"Don't." She smiled, tired and real. "You'll put it in a ledger somewhere and then I'll have to audit it, and neither of us wants that."

"Entry one thousand four hundred and six," I said. "*One debt of witness, incurred willingly, repaid never, because some debts are load-bearing.*"

She laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, the first time I'd heard it since before the winter. "Load-bearing," she said. "You're impossible, Vasquez."

"We're a guild of them. It's in the charter."

* * *

Sefa found me at the border two days later, which surprised me only because I'd expected her a day sooner.

The border wasn't anything anymore. The wards had fallen; the winter had broken; the stars were back, and with them the slow ordinary business of a country being a country again instead of an act of collective will. But the old boundary stones still stood out in the thawing fields, leaning like tired sentries, and the ground between them and the road was soft with mud the color of strong tea. I'd come out to look at it because I couldn't stop coming out to look at it. Free sky. Open edge. A country that could be left, which meant a country people stayed in by choice.

Sefa came across the field carrying her satchel and a rolled sheet of vellum, boots ruining themselves in the mud without complaint. She was mapping the thaw — of course she was. Somewhere in her archive there would soon be a chart titled *The Country That Remembered Itself,* and every stream and fence line would be drawn in ink that told the truth.

"You're really doing it," she said, arriving beside me. Not a question. She nodded at the stones, at the space between them, at the road beyond that ran toward Vhalcrest and everywhere else.

"I'm really doing it."

"They offered you everything back."

"They did."

"And you crossed out your own signature." She grinned. "Best thing I've seen all year, and I've seen the stars come back."

"It wasn't defiance," I said. "Or — it was, a little. But mostly it was bookkeeping." I turned to face her. "Corvane believed erasure was mercy. He sold the country's name because he thought forgetting would hurt less than fighting. And I looked at him at the end — you weren't there, Sefa, he was so *certain*, kneeling in the wreckage of his own signing with the storm coming off my shoulders — and I understood him. That's the part I can't shake. I understood him, because three years ago I sold my own memories to buy my way out of exile, and I told myself the same story. Mercy. Less pain. A clean page."

The wind moved through the field. Somewhere behind us, in the direction of the city, a bell rang — the new one, cast from the door bolts, rung on the hour by volunteers who hadn't asked anyone's permission. Its voice was slightly cracked. Everyone loved it.

"So the ledger has to stay open," I went on. "All of it. What the guild did, what Corvane did, what I did — the sale, the exile, the storm, the people in that city whose faces I finally remember. If I take the rank and sit in the circle and preside, I get to decide what gets recorded and what gets closed. And I don't trust myself with that power. Not yet. Maybe not ever. So I'm going to do the one job in the world I can't corrupt." I patted the satchel at my hip, where the ledgers rode — the old ones, the recovered pages, the forged entries marked FORGERY in red across their faces so no future auditor would be fooled. "Honest books. Nothing erased. Ever again."

Sefa unrolled her vellum against the wind, weighting the corners with stones. It was a map of the border itself, drawn fresh, and where the boundary should have been there was instead a dotted line and a margin note in her small precise hand: *here the country ends, or here it begins — same line.*

"I have something for you," she said. "Two things. Pick whichever hurts less."

"Sefa."

"Thing one." She produced a second document, smaller, folded twice. "Vhalcrest sent envoys last week. Actual envoys, with actual credentials, asking after terms. Trade, borders, restitution for the invasion their first fleet paid for with its entire existence. They want to negotiate with someone who keeps records they can't dispute." Her grin widened. "I may have mentioned I know a ledger-keeper first class with a reputation."

"And thing two?"

She reached into the satchel and drew out a single sheet of paper, and my chest went tight before I even saw what was on it, because I recognized the format. Contract stock. The heavy kind. The kind used for sales of names.

It was blank except for the header, freshly lettered: *Deed of Foundation — the country hereby named anew.* Below it, two lines waited for signatures. Above them, in the space where the buyer's name would go, someone had written, in careful block letters:

*Its people.*

"It needs a seller too," Sefa said softly. "That's how deeds work. Someone has to give it up — the old version, the closed country, the one that existed only by being remembered. Somebody has to formally surrender it so the new one can be entered into the record." She held out the pen she'd been carrying the whole time, and I saw now that it was scorched along the nib, and I almost laughed. She'd gone back to the guild vault and borrowed it. Sentimental, both of us. It was going around.

"You want me to sign away the old country," I said.

"I want you to *witness* it," she corrected. "That's what I've been trying to tell everyone who'll listen. You're not the man who saved us — well, you are, congratulations, terrible burden, moving on. You're the man who keeps the accounts. And a founding is just an account with better handwriting." She pressed the pen into my hand. "Sign as witness. Then help me write the rest, because Marguerite — nobody has ever founded a country honestly before. No one's ever had to write down *everything*. Where the roads go. Who owes whom. What was done in the winter, all of it, the hoarded warmth and the frozen districts and the names of everyone who didn't make it. It's going to be the longest ledger in history and it has to start somewhere and I—" She stopped. Breathed. The wind took her hair across her face and she pushed it back, and for a moment the mapmaker of absence looked simply young, and simply afraid, and simply ready. "I don't want to write it alone."

I looked at the contract. *Its people.* Two signature lines. An open page.

Behind us the cracked bell rang the hour. Ahead of us the road ran out of the country toward everywhere else, and the border stones leaned in the mud like old men watching children leave for school, and the sky overhead was enormous and starred and ours by consent rather than enchantment.

I uncapped the pen.

"*Entry one,*" I said, and bent to write.

* * *

That evening, in a rented room above a bakery that smelled of yeast and woodsmoke, I opened a brand-new ledger to its first leaf and ruled the columns in straight lines, the way I'd been taught, the way I'd teach it in turn if anyone wanted learning. On the cover, in ink that would not fade and would not lie, I lettered the title:

*Accounts of the Country Called Anew.*

Then I sat by the window while the bakery banked its fires below, and I made the first entries. One star returned, verified by observation, no fee charged. One bell, cracked, beloved. One debt of witness, load-bearing, ongoing. One door, opened, remaining open. One country, surrendered by its people to themselves, terms generous.

Outside, the first snow of the new winter began to fall — ordinary snow, ending by morning, falling because winters do that. I watched it come down over the rooftops and I felt the strangest thing, the thing I'd been circling all along without naming it, and so I named it, because that was the job now. I wrote it at the bottom of the page in small letters, the closing line of the day's account, the first honest word of the rest of my life:

*Belonging: balance confirmed.*
