## 1. The Taste of Borrowed Years

Rain in Greyharbor doesn't fall so much as it settles, like a debt collector who knows you're home. It came down that night in fine grey needles, slicking the cobbles to the color of old pewter, and I moved through it the way I move through most things: quietly, apologetically, and with my hands where they don't belong.

The merchant's name was Halveric Senn. Salt-factor, third tier, which meant he owned a warehouse and a lie of respectability but not yet the guards to make either safe. He kept rooms above his counting-house on Brine Street, and every night for the past eleven weeks he had drunk himself toward sleep and then lain awake remembering his daughter.

I knew this because I'd watched him for nine of those weeks. You don't lift a memory the way you lift a purse. A purse is taken; a memory must be *persuaded*, coaxed up out of its moorings like an anchor from deep silt. And grief — grief is the best holding-ground there is. A memory buried in grief grows roots. It also, if you're careful, grows handles.

I let myself in through the counting-house window at the hour when the watch changes and the new men are still arguing about whose round it is. The lock was salt-corroded and surrendered without complaint. Inside, the smell of the place: tallow, wet wool, ledger-ink, and underneath it all that faint mineral bite that hangs over every building in Greyharbor, the smell of a town that has made its peace with salt the way other towns make peace with God.

Senn slept badly in the front room, one arm hanging off the bed like something washed ashore. A candle had guttered out beside a half-finished letter. I didn't read it. Reading is how thieves get sentimental, and sentiment is how they get caught.

I knelt by the bed and did what I do.

There's no drama to it. No incantation, no glowing runes. If you've seen it done properly it looks almost like nothing — a man kneeling, two fingers resting light against the sleeper's temple, breath slowed to match the sleeper's breath until the two rhythms agree on a story. The trick is agreement. You cannot take a memory from someone who is defending it, even asleep; the mind locks down like a fist. So you become, for a little while, the least threatening thing in the world. You become the tide. And the tide, given time, takes everything.

Senn's daughter rose up through him like a bubble through dark water. Her name was Issa. Seven years old. She had a gap where her left eyetooth should have been and she'd refused to smile closed-lipped about it, had laughed instead with her whole head thrown back so everyone could see the hole. She'd drowned in the harbor shallows fourteen months ago, in water not deep enough to drown a dog, and Senn had been twenty feet away haggling over a shipment and had turned around and she simply wasn't there anymore.

I took all of it. Not the fact of her death — he'd keep that, poor devil, some things are too load-bearing to pull — but her. The gap-tooth laugh, the weight of her on his shoulders, the way she pronounced *warehouse* as *warhorse* and he'd never once corrected her because he loved it. Fourteen months of her, lifted clean, and in their place I pressed a smooth grey absence, the shape of a stone worn by water. His sleeping face twitched. Grief needs somewhere to go, and tomorrow he would wake with tears already dried on his cheeks and no idea why, and that is the part of my trade I try very hard not to think about while I'm doing it.

It took four minutes. It always takes four minutes; I've never learned why, and the one time I asked another practitioner about it she looked at me as though I'd asked why the sea is wet.

Then I was out the window and into the rain with a dead girl folded up in the hollow behind my sternum, and I want to tell you it felt like carrying nothing. That's the honest horror of it: carried memories weigh exactly as much as your own. For the length of the walk to Lowquay she was mine. I knew the warhorse joke. I caught myself humming the tune she used to bang out on Senn's counting-beads, and I stopped humming, and then I made myself start again, because stopping felt worse.

* *

The rival's name was Quillam Voss, and I hated him professionally rather than personally, which in Greyharbor is the only durable kind of hatred. He dealt in the same trade from a better address, wore gloves indoors, and had once described my work to a mutual acquaintance as "competent, in the way a knife is competent." I'd taken it as a compliment. Knives get used.

His establishment sat above a chandler's on Lowquay Steps, and he received me in a room full of bottled light — those fat glass orbs the free cities export, each one holding an hour of somebody's remembered noon. Voss liked them because they flattered his complexion. He was pouring wine he had no intention of drinking when I arrived, because Voss performed everything, including hospitality.

"Lockridge." He said my name the way you'd read an invoice. "You look damp."

"It's raining."

"Is it? I hadn't noticed." He smiled at his own wit with genuine warmth, which was the only genuine warmth in him. "What have you brought me?"

"Third-tier factor, Brine Street. Daughter. Age seven, drowned." I recited it flat, catalog-fashion, because that was safer than any other tone. "Full set. Thirteen years of her, minus the last fourteen months — I left him the death. Clean edges."

Voss went still in a way I recognized. It's the stillness of a man doing arithmetic. "A drowned child," he said slowly, "with the death intact for the seller." He looked at me with something that in a better man might have been curiosity. "Why leave him the death?"

"Because without it there's no grief, and without grief he starts asking why he cries at odd hours, and asking questions leads to hiring people, and hired people eventually find whoever's buying." I shrugged. "Also because taking the death felt like a step I'm not interested in stepping."

"A moral position. From you." He said it lightly, but I watched him file it away; Voss filed everything. Then he named a price, and it was a good one, insultingly good, which told me he wanted this particular memory badly, and that worried me more than any lowball offer would have.

"Who's the buyer?" I asked.

"The buyer," said Voss, "is a gentleman of refined tastes who prefers discretion, which is why he pays what he pays." He counted coins onto the table between us, and I confess my eyes followed them the way a dog's follow a sausage, and I hate that about myself too. "Why do you care? You've never cared before."

He had me there. Caring is expensive and I've always priced it out of my budget. But the girl was still sitting behind my ribs, humming on the counting beads, and something in me — some threadbare scrap I keep meaning to mend — wanted to know where she'd end up.

"Refined tastes," I said. "Meaning what? Some widow wants to feel like a mother again?"

"Meaning," Voss said, and now he wasn't smiling, "that there are buyers these days paying for grief specifically. Not joy, not love. Grief. They say forgotten oaths are coming back into fashion, and that sorrow is the coin they're minted in." He pushed the coins across. "Take your money, Lockridge, and take your morals with it, and stop asking questions above your station."

I took the money. Of course I took the money. A man who steals memories for a living can't afford principles; they're the first thing to go, right after the childhood, and we'll come to my childhood someday, or rather we won't, because I sold pieces of it years ago to buy bread during the bad winter and I couldn't tell you now what's missing. That's the trade's dirty secret, the one none of us says aloud: after enough years, you can't audit yourself. Somewhere in my head there are gaps shaped like a boy I used to be, and I fill them with guesses and call it a life.

I handed over the girl.

Voss drew her out of me with a small silver instrument like a tuning fork, and I felt her go — the warhorse joke, the gap tooth, the tune on the beads — sliding out along some channel I don't have a name for, and then the space behind my sternum was just space again, and I stood in Voss's warm dry room feeling lighter and infinitely poorer.

That's the taste of borrowed years. Everyone thinks the theft feels like loss. It doesn't. It feels like relief, and the relief is the worst part, because every time, some small voice in me notes with satisfaction: *see how much better off you are without it,* and some smaller, older voice answers: *that's what the sea says about drowning men.*

"Always a pleasure," said Voss, seeing me out.

"Always a transaction," I said, and went down into the rain.

* *

I walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, because I didn't want to be home yet. Home is a rented room above a rope-walk with three exits and no keepsakes; I call it a safehouse because calling it home invites questions I can't answer.

Greyharbor lay under the rain like something half-dissolved. Out past the breakwater the old salt road ran west toward Thornfell and the free cities, and I could see it even now, faintly, in the dark: a pale seam on the black water, the last of the great roads, the one the covenant built before anyone alive remembered there being a covenant. Children skip stones off it. Sailors swear it hums in storms. The merchants' guild says it's just a reef with good publicity.

But that night, walking the wall, I noticed the road was flickering. Not glowing — *flickering*, like a candle in draft, like something trying to remember whether it existed. I stopped and stared at it longer than a sensible man would, and while I stared, a strange thing happened in my mouth. My tongue, entirely without my permission, shaped a word in no language I know. Two syllables, heavy as wet stone. The rain seemed to lean in to hear it.

And the salt road steadied. Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough.

I stood there with rain running down my collar and my heart going like a smuggler's heels, telling myself it was exhaustion, the wine, the job, the empty place where a dead girl had recently been lodged — anything, anything but the obvious.

Behind me, on the deserted quay, I heard a knock.

Three knocks, patient and small, coming from the direction of my own door — from a safehouse no living soul could find unless I led them there myself. And beneath the sound of the rain, thin as thread, I heard a child's voice speaking that same heavy two-syllable word.

The word I had just spoken.

The road held steady, and waited, and so did whatever was knocking at my door.
