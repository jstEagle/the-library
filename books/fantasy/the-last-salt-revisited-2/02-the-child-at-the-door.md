## 2. The Child at the Door

There are three rules to my trade, and I have broken every one of them at least twice.

Rule one: never steal from a face you know. Rule two: never keep what you lift. Rule three — and this is the one they engrave on your bones if you survive long enough in Greyharbor — never, under any circumstances, answer the door after midnight.

The knock came at half past twelve, three raps soft as a pickpocket's fingers, and I sat there in my chair with a cup of cold chicory and told myself it was the wind. The wind in Greyharbor has manners. It rattles shutters, it whistles through the salt-crusted eaves, but it does not *knock*, and it certainly does not knock again when you ignore it the first time.

I set down the cup. I looked at the door. The door, being a door, offered no opinion.

"Go away," I said, because I am a professional, and professionals establish early that they are not at home.

A third knock. Then a voice, small and hoarse, speaking words I did not understand and understood completely anyway.

That is not a sentence I write lightly. I have spent eleven years handling other people's memories — lifting them, weighing them, wrapping them in salt-paper like a fishmonger parcels herring — and I know what it feels like when something foreign settles into your skull. It slides. It has edges. You can feel where it doesn't belong, the way you can feel a stranger's coat on your shoulders.

This was different. The words went into me like water into dry ground. No edges. No seams. Just a sudden, terrible familiarity, as though some part of me had been waiting all my life for this particular sentence and had only now been told what it was waiting for.

I opened the door. Rule three, broken with prejudice.

* * *

The child standing on my landing was perhaps eight years old, soaked to the ribs, barefoot on boards furred with frost. He had the grey look of someone who has been running longer than his body agreed to, and his eyes were the wrong color — not brown, not blue, but the pale translucent shade of sea ice over deep water. Salt had crystallized along his lashes and in his hair, fine white needles of it, though the rain outside was ordinary rain. I checked. I'm thorough about rain. Rain is often an alibi for worse things.

"You're Lockridge," he said, and this time it was Common Tongue, thin and cracked. "They said — the gulls said Mirabel Lockridge keeps a room above the cooperage and steals the years off grieving men."

"The gulls," I said, "talk too much."

"Please." And then he said something else — that other language, the one that had gone through me like a bell through water — and every grain of salt in my safehouse stood up.

I mean that literally. I deal in salt the way a moneylender deals in coin; I had jars of it lining the walls, coarse grey harbor salt, memory-salt packed in wax-sealed crocks, even a little dish of the white Thornfell stuff I kept for delicate work. When the boy spoke, every crystal in the room turned on its axis with a sound like a whispered *yes*. The dish of white salt rose a finger's width off the shelf, hung there glittering, and settled back down in a perfect cone, point upward, like a tiny mountain deciding to be respectful.

I have seen a lot of strange things in my line of work. I once watched a widow eat her own wedding day and smile for a week afterward. But I had never seen salt *listen*.

"What did you say?" My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only kind of steady I've ever managed.

"I don't know," the boy said, and here his composure cracked, just at the seam. "It comes up out of me when I'm scared. I don't know the words until they're already out." He hugged himself, shivering. "The men with the torches do, though. They heard me say it in Lowmarsh and they started screaming. One of them cut his own hand off rather than—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You're the only one they said might help."

Nobody helps anyone in Greyharbor; we merely extend credit. But the words were still settling in me, that alien sentence finding rooms in my head I hadn't known I'd furnished, and in one of those rooms — one that had been locked since before I could remember, which for me is a professional condition and also a private wound — something turned over in its sleep.

"How did you find me?"

"The salt told me." As if that were a normal thing a child says. "It said: *the thief who keeps nothing, ask him what he keeps.*"

I laughed. It came out ugly. "Ask him what he—" I gestured at my own empty shelves, my own famously vacant eyes. "Kid, I keep nothing. That's the whole trade. People pay me to take the memories they can't carry, and other people pay me to borrow them for a while, and at the end of the night everyone walks away lighter and nobody remembers why. It's honest work, if you don't look at it directly."

He looked at me directly. Eight years old, half-frozen, hunted by men who mutilate themselves at the sound of his voice, and he looked at me with the patience of tide.

"That's not true," he said. "You keep one. I can see it. It's wrapped so tight you can't open it yourself anymore."

And there it was. The thing I never discuss, the flaw in the product, the splinter under the nail: somewhere in the thefts of my childhood — before I was Mirabel Lockridge, or in the earliest hours of being him — I had taken one memory for myself and buried it so deep that even I couldn't reach it. I knew it the way you know a locked door in a house you've lived in forever. You stop seeing it. You arrange your furniture around it. You tell visitors it's a load-bearing wall.

"There are men coming," I said instead. "Torches, you said. Where?"

"Behind me. A street away. Maybe less."

I should have closed the door. Let me be clear about what the smart move was: close the door, bar it, put out the lamp, sit in the dark among my jars of stolen grief until the trouble passed, as it always passes, as it has passed eleven years running. The child was not my problem. Children are never my problem; that is one of the quiet perks of being the man people hire to forget things.

Instead I heard myself say, "Can you run?"

* * *

They came to the cooper's district while we were still crossing the rooftops above it, and I learned what "searching for the child" means when the searcher is Vane Corvess.

Not doors kicked in. Not the usual ugly theater of a raid. They burned the whole quarter — methodically, block by block, starting downwind — and they did not loot, and they did not chase, and they did not scream. That was the worst part. I have watched fires in Greyharbor my whole life and they come with screaming attached, the way thunder comes with lightning. This fire was silent except for the crackle, and the men walking ahead of it with their brands held high walked in step, unhurried, faces empty as unwritten slate.

Oren — he'd given me his name on the rooftop, gasped between two jumps — pressed against a chimney stack beside me and watched the orange light climb the cooperage wall, where Mrs. Halloway lived, who sold me bread, whose husband's laugh I stole once and sold twice and never lost a night's sleep over until exactly now.

"They won't feel it," Oren whispered, and his voice had that doubled quality again, the child's voice with the older thing underneath. "The ones with the torches. They sold their remembering. Fire is just weather to them."

"Selling your memories for strength." I'd heard rumors. Everyone had. Corvess's oath-breakers, the unkept men, tireless and fearless and cheap to feed. Rumors make good stories precisely because they stay stories. "That's not possible. Memory is the whole person. Take it out and there's nothing left to swing the sword."

"Something swings it," Oren said. "Something that used to be a man."

Below us, one of the torch-bearers stopped walking. Tilted his face up toward our rooftop. His eyes caught the firelight and gave none of it back — flat, pale, the exact color of the boy's, and I felt a lurch of recognition I did not want and did not understand.

He looked at us for three heartbeats. Then he turned and kept walking, and I understood that too, a moment later, with a chill that had nothing to do with the frost: he had looked *through* us. We were not hidden. We simply weren't worth keeping.

"He didn't see us," Oren said.

"No," I said. "Worse. He saw us and filed us under *nothing*. Which means somewhere in that burnt-out skull there's a ledger with our names in it and blank pages after." I pulled back from the roofline. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me professionally. "We can't stay in Greyharbor. We can't stay anywhere he's looking, and right now he's looking everywhere."

"Then where?"

I ran the map of the coast behind my eyes. South: the free cities, weeks away by sea, and every harbor mouth watched. East: the lowlands, soft country, Corvess country now. North—

North was Thornfell. The mountain. The salt roads. The old ways through the passes that smugglers used and maps pretended not to know.

"Thornfell," I said, and hated the word as it left my mouth, because everyone hates the word for the place they're most afraid of. "There are roads through the mountain that don't exist officially. If anything of the old world survived, it survived up there."

"What old world?"

I looked at him — at the salt still needling through his hair, at the ice-colored eyes, at the small exhausted creature who had knocked on the one door in Greyharbor that should never have been answered — and I realized I was going to say something true, and that it frightened me more than the burning district below.

"I don't know," I admitted. "But when you spoke on my doorstep, in that language, I understood it. Not translated it. *Understood* it, the way you understand your own name. And there's a hole in me where my childhood ought to be, kid, and your words fit that hole like they were cut for it. So whatever the old world is—" I stood, knees cracking, and took the last look at my safehouse, at the jars and crocks and sealed griefs of eleven careful years. "—it appears I'm owed some back rent."

The mountain waited northward under cloud, black against blacker, its peaks wearing snow like a debt unpaid. Somewhere behind us the cooper's district finished its silent burning. Somewhere ahead of us was whatever it was that made salt stand up and listen.

Oren slipped his small cold hand into mine without asking, the way children do, the way no one had done in longer than I could remember — which, granted, was not a long time.

"All right, Lockridge," he said. "Show me the way."

And I swear to you, on whatever I have left that's mine to swear with: as we dropped from the rooftop into the smoke and ran north, I knew the way. Every turning. Every shadowed stair. Roads I had never walked arranged themselves in my mind like sentences I'd forgotten starting.

The mountain, it seemed, remembered me first.
