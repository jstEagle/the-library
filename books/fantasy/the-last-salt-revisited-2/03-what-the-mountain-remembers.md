## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

I had forgotten what stone tastes like.

That is not a figure of speech, though the man I walk with would take it for one and file it away among my oddities, between *talks to birds* and *eats like a small wolf*. Stone tastes like patience. Old stone tastes like a sentence someone started and never finished. And the stone of Thornfell — the grey ribs of it rising out of the mist as Mirabel Lockridge hauled me up switchback after switchback, swearing at the grade in the cheerful profane dialect of Greyharbor docks — the stone of Thornfell tasted like a promise that had been swallowed whole and was still, after all these centuries, trying to speak around itself.

I know how that sounds. I was wearing a child's body at the time, and children are permitted a certain strangeness before adults stop listening entirely. I used this shamelessly. It is one of the few honest advantages I have ever taken.

"Keep up, sprout," Mirabel said, without turning around. He had a way of talking to the road ahead of him rather than to me, as if the road were more likely to answer. "The pass closes by dusk. Whatever that means out here — you'll notice nothing about this mountain closes, it just gets *worse* — I'd rather not find out what worse looks like in the dark."

I kept up. Children's legs are short and their lungs are ridiculous, so this was less effort than he believed. What cost me was the walking itself, because every step landed on memory.

You have to understand: humans think roads are things. Marks on land. Lines on maps that merchants argue over and armies bleed across. But a road is a *decision*, made over and over until the ground agrees to hold its shape. Every smuggler's track through Thornfell began as somebody's vow — *I will get this cargo through, I will come home, I will meet you at the cairn past the frozen falls* — and vows, unkept or otherwise, do not evaporate. They sink. They fossilize. The mountain is not made of rock with memories scattered through it like raisins in bread. The mountain is made of memories that forgot they were anything else, and the forgetting pressed them flat into something hard enough to walk on.

So when I put my bare hand against a cliff face to steady myself, I heard the oath inside it groan in its sleep.

And when Mirabel chose our path — always choosing, always reading the ground with those quick grey eyes, ducking under an overhang no map could have warned him about, skirting a scree slope because, he said, "that one lies," which is a mad thing to say about gravel and also completely accurate — I watched him do it, and the dread in me got a new address.

He thought he was a thief. He had built an entire life on being a thief, worn it like a coat with the collar turned up. But I had felt his craft from the inside two nights ago, when he'd lifted a dead daughter out of a merchant's skull with fingers that barely trembled. And now, watching him move through the passes, I recognized the shape of what he did, the way you recognize a song played wrong on the right instrument.

Thieves take. Wardens keep. What Mirabel did was neither and both — he moved along the old covenant-roads of the mind, found the promises stored there, and carried them elsewhere. That is warden-work. It is warden-work done with the oaths broken, the offices dissolved, the ledger burned, and the wages stolen instead of paid. A corrupted rite performed by a man who did not know there had ever been a pure version.

Which meant either the universe has a very cruel sense of humor, or somewhere behind his stolen childhood there was a door, and someone had locked it very carefully from the outside.

"You're staring again," Mirabel said. "Children stare. It's one of your worst qualities."

"You said the scree lies."

"I said—" He stopped. Looked back at me properly for the first time in an hour, rain beading in his dark hair, that merchant's shrewdness narrowing his eyes. "How did you know that? I didn't say it aloud."

"You hummed it," I said, which was true in the way most of my truths are true: sideways. His thief-craft leaked. When he read the ground, the reading came off him like heat off a stove, and anyone attuned to oaths could feel the shape of his conclusions before he spoke them. "It's a habit. My mother hated it."

This was a lie. I have no mother. I have a function, a founding date, and a great deal of guilt. But the lie fit the face I wore, and faces are for fitting lies to, and I was getting alarmingly good at this human business.

Mirabel snorted and walked on. "Your mother and every teacher you'll ever have. Come on. There's a waystation ahead — an old salt-post. If the roof holds we'll dry out."

The waystation held a hearth, half a roof, and forty years of other travelers' graffiti scratched into the beams. Mirabel lit a fire with the competence of a man who had slept rough enough times to make peace with it, and while he coaxed wet kindling I put my palms flat against the floorboards and listened to the building.

Every structure is an argument that won. This one had been argued into being by salt-wardens — real ones, the old office, before Vane Corvess's predecessors gutted the covenant and left men like Mirabel picking through the wreckage for loose memories to sell. I could taste the founding vow still in the joints: *let any keeper rest here unmolested.* It had gone thin, the way all the old words had gone thin, but it was not dead. Nothing in Thornfell was dead. That was rather the problem.

"Eat," Mirabel said, pressing dried fish and a heel of black bread into my hands. Then, because he was incapable of silence lasting longer than a candle-flicker: "That language you speak. The one that makes the salt do — whatever it does. Where's it from?"

"A long way down," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's a direction."

He gave me a look that would have curdled milk, then let it go, which was wise of him, because I had decided he was going to live long enough to hate the full answer, and there was no point spoiling the surprise.

I slept — or performed sleep, which for a thing like me amounts to the same posture — and dreamed the way stones dream, which is to say I remembered.

* * *

They came into the mountains behind us on the second day.

I felt them before the wind changed, before the birds went quiet in their rude abrupt way, before Mirabel's instincts finally caught up and he pulled us off the trail into a fold of rock thick with pine. I felt them the way a tooth feels a coming ache: as pressure in the covenant itself, a wrongness moving uphill against the grain of the old vows.

An army does not usually register as an absence. This one did. Corvess's soldiers had sold their memories for strength — I knew this the way I knew the taste of stone, absolutely and uselessly early — and a sold memory leaves a hole, and holes in people drag at the world around them like anchors of nothing. Three hundred hollow men climbing a mountain made of memory is a blasphemy of engineering, and the mountain *felt* it. Under my feet, deep in the fossilized strata, the old promises flinched.

"They're in the pass," I whispered.

Mirabel's head snapped toward the trail below us. "I don't hear—"

"You wouldn't yet." I closed my eyes. Through the rock I could see it the way you see a bruise through skin: a dark column winding up the switchbacks we had climbed yesterday, silent, tireless, faces smooth as riverstones where faces should have had history written on them. At the head of the column, a rider whose hollowness went all the way through, wearing authority the way a scarecrow wears a coat. "Two hundred. Maybe three. They're following the road."

"The road bends east at the frozen falls. We can cut north—"

"No." My mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, since I am mostly a metaphor wearing a boy. "Mirabel. They aren't following the road. They're following *you*."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched a man perform arithmetic he did not want to do. Stolen memories. Hundreds of them, lifted from grieving merchants and forgetful widows and — if Brannoc's ledger was any measure of the family trade — from places far older than Greyharbor. Fragments of the covenant itself, sitting in his skull like coins in a miser's mattress, and every hollow soldier below us a living demonstration of what Corvess did with unkept things.

"If they want what's in my head," Mirabel said slowly, "then running is a strategy with an expiration date."

"Yes."

"And you knew they were coming."

"I knew," I said carefully, "that everything comes."

He exhaled through his teeth and did not press, which I was beginning to understand was his signature mercy: he collected questions the way other men collect debts, and hoarded them, and spent none. Instead he peered north through the pines, where the pass narrowed toward a ridge I could taste from thirty miles — a ridge with a name that had once been a warning.

"There's a crossing ahead," he said. "River gorge. There's a woman there who runs the ferry, and she hates my family with a passion I've never fully gotten explained to me." He grimaced. "She may charge us in ways that aren't coin."

Everything is paid for in memory eventually; I could have told him that the ferrywoman was merely more honest than most about the exchange rate. I said nothing. Above us the clouds tore open long enough to show the peak called the Hollow Crown, and beneath my feet the mountain shifted its weight, and somewhere in its oldest stratum a vow I had helped write stirred like a sleeper hearing its own name.

Behind us, patient as erosion, the empty men climbed.

"Come on, sprout," Mirabel said. "We've a debt to pay."

And I followed the corrupted warden down toward the ferry, carrying the last clause of the covenant in a child's chest, while the mountain remembered everything and told no one — except, perhaps, me.
