## 4. The Ferryman's Ledger

The river at Vell's Crossing did not so much flow as *remember* — a slow brown ribbon of water carrying silt and old promises down out of the Thornfell passes toward the salt flats. I had crossed it a dozen times in my working life, always paying the standard fare: two coppers, or one if you were willing to listen to Brannoc complain about the weather. This time I arrived with a child who spoke in crystallizing syllables, no coppers worth the name, and the growing suspicion that every road in Thornfell was about to demand something worse than coin.

Oren walked beside me with that strange, unchildlike steadiness of his, watching the water as though it were speaking to him. For all I knew, it was.

"She'll want payment," he said, in his ordinary voice — the plain Greyharbor accent he used when the other tongue wasn't leaking out of him. "Not the kind you're thinking."

"Everyone wants payment," I said. "It's the one honest thing about this world."

The ferry itself was less a boat than an argument between a hull and rot, moored to a post wrapped in faded blue cord — warden's cord, though I didn't know that yet, and wouldn't for another hour, an hour that would ruin me more thoroughly than any thief ever had.

Brannoc stood on the far bank like something the river had decided to keep. Broad as a door, grey-haired, forearms roped with muscle from forty years of hauling rope and grief across the current. She saw us coming and did not smile. Brannoc never smiled before she'd weighed you.

"Lockridge," she said, when we were close enough. Not a greeting. A reading.

"You know me?"

"I know the name." She spat into the water, ceremonially, the way some folk bless things. "That name owes me."

I laughed, because laughing is cheaper than thinking. "I've never crossed your river owing anything but fare."

"Fare's not what I'm talking about." She jerked her chin at Oren. "And it's not the boy's debt either, though the boy's the reason it's come due. Get in. We'll settle it on the water. I don't do business on solid ground — ground holds grudges too long."

The crossing was slow and cold and smelled of iron and weed. Halfway out, where the current pulled hardest, Brannoc shipped her oars and let us drift, which is the ferrywoman's way of saying: *now we talk.*

"The Lockridges," she said, "held an office. Before your time. Before mine, near enough. Wardens of the Salt Covenant. Keepers of the crossings." She tapped the blue-wrapped post with one oar as we drifted past its twin on the far bank. "Every ferry, every bridge, every ford between the mountains and the sea was sworn into their keeping. They were meant to guard the memory of the pact. To make sure nothing that should be remembered got forgotten."

"That's not a trade I've ever heard of," I said carefully.

"No," she said. "You wouldn't have. Because they broke it."

The river said nothing, but I felt it listening.

"They sold their office," Brannoc went on, with the relish of a woman who has waited decades to tell this story to the right ears. "Or parts of it. Piece by piece, year by year, to men who wanted oaths forgotten rather than kept. Your family didn't just abandon the covenant, Lockridge. They *liquidated* it. Fed it to the forgetting, one clause at a time, and grew fat and comfortable on the proceeds. And when the last of them died — or fled, or was eaten, accounts differ — the debt passed down the blood. To you."

"That's absurd," I said. "I'm a thief. I steal memories. I don't inherit debts from ancestors I've never—"

I stopped. Because that was the trouble, wasn't it. I had never known my ancestors. I had never known my childhood. There was a hole in me shaped like the first ten years of Mirabel Lockridge, and I had built my whole self on the rim of it, telling myself the hole was natural, the way people tell themselves scars are character.

Brannoc watched me stop talking and smiled, narrowly, the way a cat smiles at a cornered mouse.

"There it is," she said softly. "You feel it now. Good. Makes the rest quicker."

"The rest being?"

"Payment." She let the word sit on the black water between us. "The ledger doesn't take coin. It takes memory. That's the old law of crossings — the covenant priced everything in remembering. You want passage on a warden's ferry, you pay in what you can't afford to lose. That's how you know it's real payment."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you swim, and the river keeps what it catches of you, and the boy drowns, because the current here has opinions about covenant-breakers' companions." She looked at Oren with something almost like reverence, and something very like fear. "Though I suspect the boy would argue. The boy argues with rivers."

Oren said nothing. He stood at the prow with his hands folded, watching the water run beneath us, and once — only once — his lips moved in that other language, and where his breath touched the spray, the droplets hung in the air a half-second longer than droplets should.

I thought about what I carried. My stock. The merchant memories, the dead daughter I'd lifted from Halloran and sold twice over, the griefs and weddings and ordinary golden afternoons I kept in glass vials like a man hoarding other people's lives because he had none of his own. Any of those would have paid Brannoc. Cheaply. Painlessly. She might even have accepted one without knowing it was stolen goods.

But I knew — the way you know a thing about yourself at three in the morning — that paying with stolen memory would be a lie, and that lies paid to ledgers like hers accrue interest.

There was one memory in my keeping that was mine. One I hadn't stolen, hadn't bought, hadn't fabricated to fill the hole in myself. I don't know where it came from. It had simply been there, the way a stone is there under your tongue — a single evening, a kitchen, a woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists, a voice singing low and off-key, a smell of yeast and woodsmoke and rain through a cracked window. I had carried it through every bad year of my life like a talisman. Proof, I told myself, that somewhere, sometime, someone had loved me before whatever took my childhood took it. When the trade hollowed me out — and it did hollow me, every job, every sale, a little scraping at the walls of me — I went back to that kitchen and stood in it and breathed, and remembered what it was to be a person rather than a function.

I had never told anyone it existed. I had certainly never named it aloud.

"Brannoc," I said. "I'll pay. But I choose what I pay with."

"You always could," she said. "That's the other half of the old law. The ledger takes what you offer willingly. It just remembers what you offered." Her eyes narrowed. "So. What'll it be?"

I took a breath that hurt more than any breath I'd taken in the Thornfell passes, with oath-breakers burning districts behind us and an army at our backs. Because I understood, in the way of a man stepping off a ledge, that this was the first true choice I'd been given since a child knocked on my door. Everything since had been flight. This was payment. And payment, unlike flight, tells you who you are.

"Not the stock," I said. "Not the stolen goods. Those aren't mine to spend."

Brannoc's eyebrows rose a fraction. In forty years of ferrying, I suspect she'd rarely heard a thief say *not mine to spend* about inventory.

"There's a memory," I went on, and my voice did something humiliating halfway through, a crack, a wobble, the sound of a man's foundations shifting. "A kitchen. Bread. A woman singing. It's the only thing I own that I didn't take from someone else. It's the only proof I have that I was ever loved by anyone." I held out my hand, palm up, the way I'd done a thousand times over a thousand sleeping merchants — but this time offering instead of taking. "Take it. Settle the Lockridge debt with it. And be quick, before I find my courage again and become sensible."

For a long moment Brannoc just looked at me. Then she reached out — those great rope-muscled hands, gentle as a midwife's — and closed her fingers around my open palm without touching it, the way you'd lift heat from a stove.

The leaving of it was quiet. No tearing, no white pain, none of the ugly mechanics of my trade. One moment the kitchen was there behind my eyes, warm and yellow-lit; the next there was a shape where it had been, a kitchen-shaped absence, and I understood suddenly, horribly, what all my customers felt when I handed them back their empties. I had spent years selling that feeling and calling it a service.

"There," Brannoc said, and pressed her fist to the ledger — an actual book, oilskin-bound, swollen with damp, older than the boat, older maybe than the river's patience. The pages drank the memory the way ground drinks rain. Somewhere in that swollen mass, a woman sang off-key forever now, filed between a smuggler's broken truce and a bride-price unpaid in the reign of somebody's grandfather.

"Debt's settled," she said. "The *old* debt. Though—" she paused, and there was no kindness in the pause, "—the ledger shows more than debts, Lockridge. It shows entries. Want to see what your family wrote?"

I should have said no. A wiser man would have said no. But the kitchen was gone and the hole it left was shaped exactly like curiosity, and curiosity is the thief's native sin.

She opened the ledger to a page near the front, the ink brown with age, the hand formal and careful — a warden's hand, trained, disciplined, each letter a small act of duty. And there, in that dutiful script, an entry dated in a calendar nobody living used:

*Received of Aldous Corvess, of the house of Corvess, one-third part of the Third Clause of the Salt Covenant, in exchange for remission of the Crossing Debt incurred by the House of Lockridge. Let it be noted that the wardens sell what they were sworn to keep, and that the river sees, and the river waits.*

Corvess.

I read the name twice. Three times. The same name that burned districts in Greyharbor hunting a child. The same name the oath-breakers screamed into smoke. Vane Corvess, whose soldiers did not sleep, whose face was a rumour, whose hunger for forgotten oaths had emptied whole towns of their yesterdays.

My family had sold to *that* house. Had begun the liquidation of the covenant — the covenant Oren spoke in, the covenant cracking the sky above us — in a transaction recorded in a ferrywoman's ledger, and the buyer's line had spent generations collecting the rest.

"Small world," Brannoc said, dry as salt. "Or small ledger. Hard to tell the difference sometimes."

"It's not possible," I said. "I'm not — I'm nobody. I'm a cutpurse of other people's pasts. I'm not the heir to anything except bad decisions."

"Aren't you?" She turned the page, and there it was again, further down, a later entry, a different hand — shakier, hurried, frightened: *The remaining clauses conveyed to safekeeping. Location withheld even from this record. The heir will know, or the heir will not. So be it.* "The heir," Brannoc repeated. "That's you, boy. Whether you like it or not. Whether you remember it or not — and I notice you don't remember much of anything about yourself, which for a Lockridge is either tragedy or strategy."

Oren turned from the prow then. He looked at the ledger, and then at me, and something moved across his face that I had no name for — recognition, maybe, or pity, or the particular sorrow of a spirit watching a mortal discover the size of the trap he was born in.

"Mira," he said quietly — using, for the first time, a shortened form of my name, familiar, almost tender. "The clause. The final clause. The one Corvess wants." His eyes stayed on mine, steady, patient, terrible in their gentleness. "It names a warden's heir. It has been waiting a long time to be spoken by one."

The river carried us the last stretch to the far bank in silence. Brannoc shipped her oars, tied off, and looked at me the way you look at a man you've just dismantled.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the kitchen memory was good payment. Best I've taken in twenty years. Whoever she was — the singing woman — she'd have approved of you paying your own debts with your own heart, finally." She hesitated, and for the first time all day her voice lost its flint. "One more thing, Lockridge, gratis, because you paid clean. Corvess's vanguard crossed the north ford at dawn. They're a day behind you, maybe less. And they aren't looking for the boy alone anymore."

"No?"

"They're looking for the heir." She tapped the ledger against her palm, twice, like a heartbeat. "Someone told them what I just showed you. Someone who reads ledgers."

* * *

We stood on the far bank as Brannoc poled back into the brown water, a broad grey silhouette shrinking into the mist, taking my kitchen and my ancestry and my comfortable ignorance with her. Behind us, the Thornfell passes. Ahead, the long wet miles toward the free cities. Around us, a world that had just gotten much larger and much colder.

Oren slipped his small cold hand into mine — a gesture that had become habit between us on the road, though neither of us had ever discussed it, and which I now understood was perhaps the least childish thing about him.

"You gave up the only memory that was truly yours," he said. "To pay a debt you didn't remember owing. Do you know what that makes you, Mira?"

"Broke," I said. "Broke and sentimental."

"It makes you a warden," he said. "The first one in three generations to act like it."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to argue. I wanted, most of all, to go back an hour and refuse the whole revelation, keep my kitchen, stay a simple thief with a simple trade and a simple hole in him. But the ledger was written, the payment logged, the name Corvess inked beside my own in brown fading script, and somewhere behind us, a day's march back through the passes, men without faces were marching toward the heir with a warrant older than any of us.

"Come on, then," I said at last, because it was all I had. "If I'm a warden, I suppose I'd better learn what I'm supposed to be guarding."

Oren smiled — and it was the wrong smile, briefly, an old smile in a young face, ancient and sad and certain — and pointed up the trail into the mountains.

"That," he said, "is the easy part. Guarding is just love with a schedule." He squeezed my hand. "The hard part comes at Hollow Vows. They've reached the pass already, Mira. I can hear them. They're singing."

And faintly, terribly, from somewhere up ahead where the trail narrowed between cliffs the colour of old bone, I heard it too: not a war-song, not a marching chant, but something lower and stranger — hundreds of voices humming in unison, tuneless, patient, the sound an oath makes when it's being digested.
