## 5. An Army of the Unkept

I have heard the deep sing before.

I want to be clear about that, because what came up the pass of Hollow Vows was not singing, no matter what I told Mirabel on the trail. The deep sings when it is fed — when an oath is kept so long and so faithfully that it wears smooth as sea-glass and slides down into the dark without breaking anything. That singing is beautiful the way glaciers are beautiful: enormous, patient, indifferent to you.

What Corvess's army made was not that. It was the sound of oaths being *chewed*.

I did not tell Mirabel this. He was already walking too fast, one hand pressed to the cliff-wall where the salt had fossilized into ribs of white stone, and fear travels between people like warmth in winter whether you mean to share it or not. So I kept my hands in my pockets and hummed along instead, quietly, tasting what they were eating.

It was Brannoc's ferry-oath. And a marriage from somewhere south of Thornfell, ninety years kept. And a smith's apprenticeship. And a promise someone had made to a dog.

They were eating a promise made to a dog.

That was the moment I understood how bad this would be.

* * *

The pass of Hollow Vows is named honestly, which is rare for mountains and rarer still for men. Long ago — before your grandmothers' grandmothers were memories themselves — two armies met there and neither would retreat, and their commanders swore an oath of mutual ruin rather than yield. The mountain took them at their word. The whole pass is the scar of it: cliffs fused where they should not touch, salt-veins running through rock like the white marrow of a bone that never learned to stop growing. Every road through it is a promise that hardened before it could be broken.

Which means every road through it can be *un*-hardened. But that comes later. That comes with blood and years, and I am getting ahead of myself, the way old things do when they are frightened.

We reached the narrows at dusk, where the trail pinches down to the width of four men abreast between walls of pale stone, and I stopped humming because there was nothing left to harmonize with. The sound ahead had changed. The chewing had stopped.

They knew we were coming.

"Mira," I said, and tugged his sleeve. "The soldiers ahead. They've sold everything."

He crouched beside me behind a fallen slab of covenant-stone, and even in the dying light I could see him doing the arithmetic he always does — exits, prices, odds. "Sold what? Food? Salt?"

"Themselves." I tried to find words small enough for him. Human words are such narrow little boats; you can only fit so much drowning in each one. "Their names. Their mothers. The taste of bread. Every memory that isn't useful for killing. They gave it all to Corvess, and he gave them back strength and sleeplessness and no pain."

"And in exchange they—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "They're hollow."

"Unkept," I said. "Like houses no one lives in. Like books no one reads." I looked up at him, and here is the thing about wearing a child's shape: children are allowed to say true things plainly, and adults forgive them for it because they assume the child doesn't understand. I used that permission shamelessly. "You've been selling pieces of people for years, Mira. You know exactly what's left over when the remembering goes. You just never had to look at a whole army of it."

His jaw tightened. I felt bad about that, mostly. Mostly I felt afraid, and afraid things lash out, and I am old enough to admit that being ancient does not make you kind. It only makes you tired in more directions.

"So we go around," he said. "There's a smuggler's cut, half a day east—"

"They'd hear us. They don't sleep, Mira. They stand in the dark and listen for heartbeats that remember anything worth taking." I swallowed. "But listen. Listen carefully, because this part matters. They're looking for *kept* things. For memories that are loved. A memory nobody holds onto — something stolen, something that belongs to no one — walks right past them. They can't taste it. It has no flavor."

Mirabel went very still. In my experience, humans go still in two ways: prey going still, and predators. His was the second kind, and it made me proud of him and sad for him in equal measure.

"You want me to walk through them," he said slowly, "wearing other people's memories."

"Your stolen goods," I said. "Your whole coat full of borrowed years. To them you'll smell like a warehouse. Like nothing alive."

* * *

Here is what I saw that night, and I will write it plainly because someone must.

Mirabel stood up out of our hiding place, brushed off his knees like a man heading to market, and walked into the pass of Hollow Vows with a hundred faceless soldiers around him — and they did not see him. Not truly. Their heads turned toward him and slid away, the way eyes slide off fog. One of them passed close enough that its elbow nearly touched his shoulder, and it smelled the air near him and found only the stale, unclaimed scent of stolen memory, and moved on.

I followed three paces behind, small and quick and quiet, wrapped in the oldest trick I own: I smell of the covenant itself, and unkept things cannot bear that scent any more than moths can bear daylight. They parted around me without knowing why.

And Mirabel — Mirabel *performed*. That is the word. I watched him do it. He squared his shoulders and set his pace to theirs, that tireless dead march, and he let his face go slack, and he hummed their tuneless hum under his breath, and he was so good at it that my stomach hurt.

Because I could see what it cost.

Every few steps, his hand drifted toward his chest — toward the place inside where he keeps the stolen memories, all those fragments of other people's lives he carries like a pickpocket carries watches — and every time, he caught himself and put the hand back down. But I saw his fingers tremble after. And once, passing a soldier who had been a woman once, tall, with a scar through her eyebrow that no longer meant anything to her, Mirabel flinched. Just barely. Just enough.

Because he recognized her. Or he recognized a piece of her — some memory of hers he had lifted in Greyharbor and sold to a grieving rival, or kept, or traded, and now here she was walking beside him with her name eaten out of her, and the piece he held was the only proof left anywhere in the world that she had ever been anyone.

He walked among them wearing their missing selves like a disguise. And every step was a small cruelty, and he knew it, and he kept walking anyway, because I was behind him and small and he had decided — God help him, he had actually decided — that I was his to keep.

Nobody keeps anything in this world without it costing. That is the first law of the covenant and the last law of love and I have watched ten thousand years of both and they have never once contradicted each other.

* * *

We were nearly through. I could see the far mouth of the pass, a grey wound of evening sky, when the trouble happened — because there is always trouble, and if a story ever runs out of trouble it dies on the spot.

A soldier stood at the narrows' end, alone, facing the cliff wall. Not marching. Not humming. Its helmeted head was tilted upward, and its gauntlet rested flat against the stone, fingers spread, the way you'd touch the face of someone sleeping.

And against the wall, beneath its palm, a vein of fossilized salt was glowing faintly gold.

An old oath. One of the pass's buried promises, sealed in the rock since before kingdoms. And the unkept thing was touching it, and the oath was answering — reaching toward it like a plant toward light — and I realized with a lurch that somewhere under all that emptiness, some splinter of the soldier remembered *wanting* something. Remembered that wanting had once been possible. The hollow remembers the shape of what was taken out of it. That is the cruelest engineering Corvess ever managed: he left the ache in, because the ache makes better soldiers.

Mirabel stopped. I grabbed his sleeve — too late, too loud, my heart hammering in my borrowed child's chest — and the soldier turned.

Its face under the helm was smooth as an egg. No eyes I could find. But it turned, and it faced Mirabel, and it stood there, and the whole column behind us went silent, hundreds of headless helmets tilting toward us by degrees, slow as a field of iron flowers finding the sun.

Then the smooth face split. Not into a mouth. Into recognition.

"*Lockridge,*" it said. The voice was a hundred voices agreeing badly. "*You hold what is ours.*"

Mirabel's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it hard, and his palm was slick with sweat, and his voice when it came out was steady as a ledger-line.

"I hold what I bought fair," he said. "Come and audit me."

And I thought — absurdly, terribly, with ten thousand years pressing down on me like snow — *oh no. Oh, you brave, stupid, magnificent man. You're enjoying this.*

Behind the pass, far off but closing, horns began to sound. Deep ones. The horns of the main host.

Corvess himself was coming to collect.
