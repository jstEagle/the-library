## 6. The Usurper's Offer

They did not bind my hands. That was the first insult.

The second was the tea. Someone had actually brewed it—black leaf, a twist of dried plum, the way they drink it in Greyharbor on mornings when the fog won't lift—and set it steaming on a table of black salt-glass in the middle of a fortress that smelled, faintly and everywhere, of old promises rotting. I sat across from it with my wrists loose at my sides, flexing fingers that could have been picking three different locks by now, and understood that Vane Corvess wanted me to know exactly how unnecessary struggle was. The tea was the message. *Sit. Drink. We have time, because time is mine.*

I drank the tea. It was very good. I resented it deeply.

The cell—if you could call a furnished room with a view of the pass a cell—held me the way a ledger holds a debt: patiently, with interest accruing. Through the window I could see the pass of Hollow Vows falling away into grey morning, and somewhere down there, I told myself, a boy with a voice that made stone remember was running, or hiding, or—

Or captured too. Let's not pretend I didn't consider it. Hope is just fear wearing nicer clothes.

* * *

Corvess came at dusk, alone, which frightened me more than an escort would have. A man who travels without guards has already decided no one in the room can touch him.

He was not what the rumors promised. No crown of thorned iron, no eyes like banked coals. He looked like a magistrate's clerk grown tired—a thin man in good grey wool, with a face you'd forget before he finished speaking to you. Which, I realized as he poured himself tea from the same pot, was precisely the point. You cannot fear what you cannot hold onto. I would know. It's how I make my living.

"Mirabel Lockridge," he said, tasting the name like the tea. "The thief of Thornfell. Do you know what I admire about your work?"

"My discretion."

"Your economy." He sat, unhurried. "Every thief in history steals gold or grain or virtue, things that exist. But you steal what doesn't. Absence. You take a memory and leave nothing behind—not even the knowledge that something was taken. The merchant wakes whole and hollow and never asks why." He smiled thinly. "That's not theft. That's covenant-craft. Degenerate, unlicensed, profaned—but the shape of it is older than any kingdom on this map."

"I'm just a tradesman."

"You're a warden's get playing at being gutter vermin, and doing it beautifully." He let that sit between us like a third cup nobody poured. "But we'll come to that."

"We'll come to the part where you let me go."

"Of course. First, though—" he reached into his coat, and every instinct I owned went taut as wire—"I brought you something."

It was small. A vial, no longer than my thumb, filled with something that wasn't quite liquid and wasn't quite light: a slow silver turning, like snow falling inside glass, like rain over Greyharbor seen from very far above. Even sealed, even cold on the table between us, I felt it. My chest hurt in a way I hadn't allowed myself to feel since I was—well. Since I stopped allowing myself to feel anything.

"What is it?" I asked, though my hands knew before my mouth did. They were shaking.

"A childhood," Corvess said. "Yours. Age four to age nineteen, give or take a fever dream. Your mother's laugh. The name of the dog you loved. Why you flinch at the smell of lamp-oil. The reason you left home, and the reason you never once went back." He turned the vial slowly, watching the light turn inside it. "Complete. Unspoiled. Painless to restore—one swallow and you are the man you were always supposed to be, instead of the man you made yourself into out of scraps."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below, the fortress groaned against the mountain like an old ship.

"That's not possible," I said. "Stolen memories degrade. Everyone knows that. You can't keep them fresh for—"

"For twenty years? No ordinary vessel can." He set the vial down between us, equidistant, obscene in its generosity. "But oaths preserve what they touch, Lockridge. And these were kept in a founding oath of this fortress until I cracked it open last winter. Every year of you, exactly as you were. Waiting."

"And the price?"

He smiled again, and for one flickering moment the tired clerk fell away and something much older looked out through his face—something patient and vast and terribly hungry, the way the deep beneath the salt roads must be hungry.

"The boy," he said. "And the final clause."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about temptation, since apparently I'm the authority now: it doesn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like a door you've been leaning against for years, finally swinging open.

I thought about my mother's laugh. That was the trap of it—I couldn't think about her laugh, because I didn't know it anymore; I only knew that there had been one, that its absence had a specific shape, a specific weight, like a missing tooth the tongue keeps returning to. Twenty years of that shape. Twenty years of reaching for a face that fog refused to give back, of waking some mornings certain I'd been someone else entirely and lying there until the feeling passed. And here it was, in glass, for the price of a child I'd known for a handful of weeks.

"He told you what he is yet?" Corvess asked gently. Too gently.

"He's a boy who speaks the old tongue."

"He's a fragment of the Covenant wearing a dead child's shape. It needed a courier. It chose a form you wouldn't abandon." He sipped his tea. "You've been carrying a key and calling it a friend. I'm offering to relieve you of the burden. The clause names a warden's heir, Mirabel—it always has. It names *you*. Your family held the wardenship of the Salt Covenant for eleven generations, and your grandfather sold his office to buy his son's life, and that betrayal is the crack through which everything I've built has flowed ever since. The clause can't be reforged while an heir lives who refuses to know it. You are the flaw in the pact. Hand the boy over, take your past, and walk away whole. No one will ever call you traitor again, because you'll have no memories in which you were anything else."

And God forgive me, I hesitated.

Not long. Not visibly, I think. But I hesitated, and in that hesitation I saw all of it laid out plain: the man I could be, the whole man, the one with a mother's laugh and a reason and a name that meant something—the self I'd been pickpocketing around for two decades, telling myself I preferred it this way. All I had to do was give up a child whose hand fits inside mine, who calls salt roads by their true names, who trusted me when trust was the only thing either of us had left to trade.

"You're wrong about one thing," I said finally.

"Am I?"

"The clause names a warden's heir. Fine. But it requires a warden's *sacrifice*, doesn't it? I've read enough of the old tongue off Oren's lips to guess the grammar." I leaned forward. "You don't want me to surrender him. You want me to surrender *willingly*—and you can't force willingness. So instead you're selling me my own life back and hoping I sign the deed without reading it."

Something moved behind Corvess's face—annoyance, maybe, or respect, or the brief ugly flicker of a plan meeting friction. "Clever," he said. "The family talent, I suppose."

"There's another thing you got wrong."

"Do share."

"You said it chose a form I wouldn't abandon." I stood up, slow, letting him see my hands empty and steady now. "You think that makes the boy a tool. A lure. Maybe it does. But I've slept beside that fire for weeks, Corvess, and whatever the Covenant intended—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked slightly, steadied, "—whatever it intended, what I feel is mine. I earned it. It's the only thing I own outright. So no. Keep my childhood. I've managed without it. Turns out I'm very good at going without."

Corvess regarded me for a long moment. Then he sighed, set down his cup, and rose—and the room changed around him, the way water changes when something enormous passes beneath the surface.

"Painless, I offered," he said, almost regretful. "Very well. There are other ways to open a man than kindness."

The door opened behind me. The oath-broken filed in, faceless, tireless, filling the room like grey water rising.

"One more thing before they take you downstairs," Corvess said, retrieving the vial and holding it up so the light turned inside it one last time—that slow silver fall of years, my years, my mother's laugh caught mid-note forever. "The clause needs a willing sacrifice. But grief, Lockridge—grief can be *made* willing. When you watch what my soldiers do to the boy, try to remember that you chose this."

They took my arms. I didn't struggle. Struggle was unnecessary, and anyway I was busy memorizing the vial—the exact tilt of the light, the color of everything I'd lost—because if I was going to be the man who traded his whole self for one small stubborn soul, then by God I was going to remember, for as long as remembering lasted, exactly what it cost.

They marched me down through the fortress, past cells where forgotten oaths whimpered in the dark, toward the sound of a child's voice speaking words that made the stones weep salt.

Down toward Oren. Down toward the choice I'd already made, being made real.
