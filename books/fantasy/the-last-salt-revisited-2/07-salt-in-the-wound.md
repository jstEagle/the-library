## 7. Salt in the Wound

Here is a thing they do not tell you about wearing a child: children are believed. That is my whole trick, and it has worked for nine hundred years. A child weeps and grown men soften. A child asks questions and is answered in plain words instead of careful ones. A child stands at a fortress gate in the rain, shivering, and the guards look past him to the man beside him — the thief, the criminal, the one worth searching.

I have been many children. I was a shepherd boy when the second road closed. I was a drowning girl on the coast of Vell once, though that was an indulgence and I regret it still; the mother who pulled me from the water deserved better than what she got. But I had never been *Oren* before Mirabel Lockridge opened his door, and I had never minded the wearing until that night.

Which is, of course, why it hurt him so much when the truth came out. But I am getting ahead of myself, and the dead tongue warns against that. Begin where the salt begins.

* * *

The fortress of Corvess had been called Karag Vow once, before its name wore smooth like a stone in a river. It sat above the pass of Hollow Vows with its roots sunk into the oldest oath in Thornfell: *let no hand be raised within these walls that has not sworn itself to keeping.* Every stone of it had been laid on that promise. Every gate, every chain, every lock answered to it.

Corvess thought he owned the fortress. Corvess thought he owned me, locked in a cell of black salt-iron with Mirabel bleeding quietly two paces away, because Corvess fed on forgotten oaths and believed himself their master.

But here is the other thing they do not tell you: an oath forgotten is not an oath destroyed. It only goes quiet, the way a word goes quiet in a mouth that has stopped speaking. And I am the tongue the covenant spoke with. The founding vow of Karag Vow had been waiting under the fortress for six hundred years, patient as salt, and all it needed was someone to say it aloud again.

The trouble was what saying it would cost.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the theme of this chapter and I failed so thoroughly at it before. I knew the cost going in. I have always known. The covenant-tongue does not spend words; it spends *self*. Each true speaking draws down the speaker's substance like water from a well. For Mirabel, who holds his memories loose as stolen coins in a pocket, the price would be pieces of himself. For me — for what I actually am, wearing this borrowed body like a coat three sizes too small — the price was time. Years of it. The shape I wore could not hold the weight of a founding oath spoken whole. It would crack. It would grow. And then Mirabel would see.

I lay on the cell floor in the dark and did the arithmetic anyway, because there was no one left to do it but me.

"Oren." Mirabel's voice came low across the cell. In the days since Hollow Vows he had learned to speak softly even here, where softness bought nothing. "You're doing your thinking-out-loud face."

"I don't have a thinking-out-loud face."

"You do. Your brow does this thing. Like a small angry weather system." A pause, wet and ragged — they had worked him over after the Usurper's Offer, and he had refused me again, and the refusing had cost him skin. "Whatever it is, don't. Whatever you're planning. I've seen that weather before and it always rains on me."

"Mirabel." I turned toward the sound of him. My eyes in this shape were a child's eyes, made for candlelight and kindness, useless in the dark. "How much do you remember of your childhood?"

Silence. Then, carefully, in the voice of a man stepping onto ice: "Nothing. You know that. It's the hole in me. Why?"

"Because I'm about to open a very large hole in myself, and I wanted to know if you'd recognize one."

* * *

They came for us at the hour the fortress slept worst — the deep watch, when even oath-broken soldiers dream, if faces like theirs can be said to dream. Two guards, keys clinking. They meant to take Mirabel alone, I think, to finish the persuasion the Usurper's Offer had begun. They barely glanced at me. Children are invisible in the dark. Children are invisible everywhere. It is the whole trick.

I stood up as the door opened and I spoke.

I will not write the words here. Not out of drama — although some drama, I confess, is owed — but because they were never meant for ink. The covenant-tongue lives in the space between breath and meaning, and a page can hold only its corpse. What I will tell you is what it *did*.

The founding oath of Karag Vow had held for six hundred years: *no hand raised unsworn.* I spoke it back into the stones, complete, exact, with its true ending — which its builders had never known, because its true ending was written in me. And oaths, when spoken truly, must answer. The walls remembered what they were made of. The locks remembered they were promises, not barriers, and promises can be *kept or broken*, nothing between. The chains on Mirabel's wrists considered the matter and chose breaking.

The guards made sounds like men hearing their own names for the first time. The door came off its hinges with a groan like a waking animal. Somewhere far below, the foundation stones shifted and settled, an old man turning over in bed, and every torch in Karag Vow burned green for three heartbeats.

And I grew.

It was not dramatic, if you can believe that. No light, no thunder. It was more like watching frost climb a window — the small body simply becoming less small, joints lengthening, the child-coat splitting at the seams of itself. I felt years pour out of me like sand from a torn sack. Nine hundred years I had spent being small enough to pity, and I spent thirty of them in that corridor alone. When it was done I stood taller than Mirabel, and my hands were a man's hands, and the last of the child's face was gone from whatever mirror of me the torches offered.

Mirabel was staring. Of course he was staring.

"You," he said. And then nothing else for a long time, because Mirabel Lockridge, who could talk a merchant out of his own grief, had finally met a silence he couldn't sell his way through.

"It's still me," I said, and heard how thin that sounded in a voice gone low and adult.

"What are you?" He didn't step back. I want that recorded fairly. He stood in a broken prison with the fortress groaning around us and he did not step back. But his hand found the wall behind him all the same, the way a man's hand finds a rail on a ship in rough water. "All of it. The language. The salt crystallizing. The memories landing in me that night at my door. Oren, what *are* you?"

"I told you my name means 'he who knocks.'"

"That's not an answer. That's a *door*."

He was right, and it was, and I had built it that way nine hundred years ago without knowing why. So I gave him the truth, plainly, because he had earned plainness and I had run out of tricks. "I am the covenant's voice. Its keeper. Its... remainder. When the Salt Covenant bound your realms to the deep, something had to carry the words back and forth across the border. Something that could wear any shape the roads required. I have worn shepherds and kings and one memorable innkeeper, and for the last year I wore a child, because a child was what would make you open your door." I stopped. Made myself go on. "The friendship wasn't a trick. I need you to know that. But the shape was a lie, and everything you trusted walked around inside the lie, and I'm sorry."

The alarm bells had begun somewhere above us. Running feet, the clangor of a fortress discovering its own unmaking. None of it touched him. He looked at me the way a man looks at a house he grew up in after it's burned — seeing the rooms, seeing the ashes, seeing both at once.

"The memories," he said finally. "That first night. When I touched you and they landed in me. Those weren't accidents, were they? You were *showing* me."

"I was testing whether anything of the wardens survived in you. Whether the blood remembered." I hesitated, and hated the hesitation, because hesitation was how liars sounded and I was done sounding like one. "It did. More than I hoped. That's why Corvess wants you, Mirabel. The final clause of the covenant names a warden's heir. It's always named one. And the ledger at Brannoc's ferry —"

"— says my family were wardens who betrayed their office." His laugh was short and awful. "So the child I dragged halfway across Thornfell turns out to be a nine-hundred-year-old spirit, and the thief turns out to be the heir of traitors. We're both frauds."

"We're both *unfinished*," I said. "There's a difference. Fraud knows what it's pretending to be."

Above us, boots on stairs. Many boots. Mirabel took my hand — my new hand, the stranger's hand — and I watched him decide. I watched the whole war happen behind his eyes: the boy he had carried, slept beside, lied for, bled for, versus the tall stranger standing in the wreckage of that boy. Grief is a strange country and he crossed it quickly, faster than most men manage, because thieves are practiced at appraising loss.

"You'll explain everything," he said. Not a question. "Every year of those nine hundred. Every lie by name. On the road, walking, starting now, and you don't stop until we reach the free cities."

"Yes."

"And Oren—" He caught himself on the name, tested it against my new height, and kept it anyway, which is the closest thing to forgiveness I have ever been given. "—the next shape you wear, you ask me first."

We ran.

* * *

We went up, because the gates were down and the walls were confused, and because Corvess's soldiers were oath-broken men and the fortress no longer knew whose side its stones were on. Twice we passed through halls where the very architecture fought for us — doors swinging shut behind us, staircases offering steps that hadn't existed an hour before. An unmade oath leaves a fortress homesick for its own purpose. Karag Vow had been built for keeping, and we were, in our crooked fashion, keepers.

At the north wall Mirabel stopped, breathing hard, and looked back once at the burning green of the great hall where Corvess had made his offer.

"He'll follow us," Mirabel said.

"He'll *hunt* us. There's a difference too." I flexed my new hands, feeling their strangeness, feeling the hollows where thirty years of myself used to be. "And Mirabel — what's left of the covenant, the part that matters, the final clause — I couldn't speak it tonight. I tried, while the walls were listening. It won't come. It isn't mine to say anymore."

"Whose is it, then?"

Below us, the pass of Hollow Vows opened onto the last stretch of the salt road, pale as bone in the starlight, already fraying at its edges where Corvess's hunger had gnawed it thin. Beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly near, the free cities glimmered.

I looked at Mirabel Lockridge, heir of wardens, keeper of a clause he did not yet know he carried, and I said:

"Yours. It's been yours since the night you opened the door."
