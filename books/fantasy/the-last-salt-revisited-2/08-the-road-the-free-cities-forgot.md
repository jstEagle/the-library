## 8. The Road the Free Cities Forgot

The salt road began to die at dawn, and it died the way most things die in Thornfell — slowly at first, then all at once, and with a great deal of complaining.

I noticed it as a sound. Roads, in my experience, are silent things, the way furniture is silent, the way promises are silent until they break. But this road had been a promise once — some smuggler's oath, some lover's vow sworn over a pack of contraband salt two hundred years dead — and now that promise was coming apart, and it came apart loudly. A grinding, a groaning, a sound like a millstone turning over in its sleep. Under our boots the white surface went grey, then translucent, then simply *absent*, and beneath it there was nothing at all. Not darkness. Not rock. Just the particular nothing that exists between one remembered thing and the next.

"Is it supposed to do that?" I asked.

Oren didn't answer. The child stood at the edge of the dissolution with his small fists clenched and his eyes gone the colour of deep water, and I could see the effort of him holding the road together the way you'd see a man holding a door shut against a flood — in the trembling, in the sweat, in the terrible economy of motion. He was spending himself. I had known that since Hollow Vows, since the fortress came down around our ears and he aged four years in a single night of speaking. He was a covenant-spirit wearing a boy the way I wore my coat: something to keep the cold out. And the coat was wearing through.

"Oren."

"It holds," he said. "For now. It holds where I stand."

"Then we'd better stand somewhere useful."

I am, by trade and temperament, a coward. I want that on the record. Everything I have ever done well, I have done well because I learned early that the world rewards a man who knows which way the wind of consequence blows and steps aside. But there are only so many times you can watch a child — a *spirit*, a *lie I'd befriended*, call it what you like — spend himself into nothing to keep you alive before the coward's arithmetic stops balancing.

I sat down on the last solid stretch of road, which was either very brave or very stupid, and I opened the coat.

* * *

You should understand what I carried. Twenty years a thief of memories, and I had never once sold the good ones. That was the whole architecture of my self-respect: I stole from the grieving, yes, but I kept my own hoard intact — a merchant's reserve, a dragon's bed of gold I never spent. A first snow seen from a window I could not place. A woman's laugh in a kitchen I could not find. The smell of bread and lamp-oil and someone singing, badly, with enormous conviction. Fragments. I'd told myself for years they were inventory. I'd told Oren they were nothing, just dross I'd skimmed off other people's sorrows.

I took out the little salt-glass vials — seven of them, each no bigger than my thumb — and laid them in a row on the dying road.

"What are those?" Oren asked. He crouched beside me, and for a moment the boy-mask slipped and I saw the thing underneath, old as the covenant, tired as the sea. Then it was a boy again, because that was kinder for both of us.

"Inventory," I said. "Apparently."

"Mirabel—"

"Here's the situation as I understand it." I held up a finger, because lecturing is the one form of courage available to men like me. "You are holding this road open by standing on it, which is a lovely image but a losing strategy. The road needs *remembering*. You said so yourself, back in the passes — a road is a promise made solid, and promises need keepers. You're the keeper, but you're running out of *you*. So." I nudged the vials toward him. "I'm told these are fragments of the covenant itself. Stolen, hoarded, carried across half of Thornfell by an idiot who didn't know what he had. Feed them to the road."

He looked at the vials. He looked at me. And then he did the thing that undid me, the thing I will not be able to describe to anyone ever again because by tomorrow I won't remember it: he looked at me *gently*. A spirit older than the mountains, wearing a stolen child, pitying *me*.

"You know what they are," he said. "Don't you."

"I know they're mine," I said. "I've known since the ferry. I've known since Brannoc read out the Lockridge name and I felt it in my teeth. These aren't stolen goods, Oren. They're *change* from something that was taken from me. And I have been carrying them around for twenty years like a man carrying the receipt for a house he can't remember living in." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Feed them to the road."

* * *

The first vial he poured out slow, the way you'd pour out a libation, and the memory went into the salt like water into cracked earth.

I felt it leave.

I want to be precise about this, because everyone thinks losing a memory is like losing a coin — there, then gone. It isn't. It's like a tooth coming out. There's the moment of resistance, the root holding on, and then a strange lightness, and then your tongue keeps going back to the gap for weeks, worrying at the absence, learning its shape. The first snow left me and I felt the cold of it go, felt the window-glass fog of it go, and in the gap it left behind — this is the part I had not been warned about — something else came *in*.

A door. A green door, salt-rimed at the bottom. A hand much smaller than mine reaching for the latch, and my own voice — my own, younger, unbroken — saying, *"Careful, it sticks."*

I staggered. Oren caught my arm with a grip far too strong for a child.

"What did you see?"

"A door," I said. "A green door." I was laughing again, or something adjacent to laughing. "Oren, I've never seen that door. I've never — except I have, I *lived* there, I—"

"Keep giving," he said. Not cruel. Just true. "The road takes what it needs, and what it needs is *you*. It always was. That's why the fragments came to you, Mirabel. That's why a thief of memories ended up carrying the covenant's own clause. The road has been calling you home for twenty years, and you thought it was indigestion."

The second vial. The woman's laugh, the kitchen, the bread and lamp-oil — that one hurt worst, and in its place came a courtyard, a training-yard, wooden staves, and a man's voice saying *"A warden does not keep. A warden keeps* for.*"*

The third. A storm at sea, and in its place: my mother's face, which I had not seen in twenty years and which I now understood I had *refused* to see, because seeing it meant admitting it had been taken.

The fourth, the fifth, the sixth. Each one a tooth out, each one a room in a house I'd forgotten I owned, lit and furnished and waiting. I wept, I think, through most of it, in the undignified way of men who have never practised. The road firmed beneath us, mile by mile, the white brightening, the grinding fading to a hum — the road *singing*, Oren said, though I heard only the hum, and the hum sounded suspiciously like the badly-sung song from the kitchen, which is the sort of coincidence that isn't.

The seventh vial I held in my fist and did not open.

Because the seventh was warm. And through the gap where the other six had been, I could feel what it was. Not a fragment. The *whole*. The clause. The final line of the Salt Covenant, the thing Corvess would burn three kingdoms to hold, the thing that named a warden's heir — and I knew, with the terrible clarity of a man who has just remembered everything, that the heir was not a title I carried. It was a name I *was*.

"Mirabel." Oren's voice. "The road is whole to the ridge. You can stop."

"I know."

"You're keeping the last one."

"I'm a thief," I said. "Keeping things is the one thing I've never learned to do properly." But my fist didn't open.

And then I looked up, past him, past the ridge the road had carried us to — and there they were.

The free cities. All of them, spread below like a spilled jewellery box, white walls and salt-towers and the morning light coming off the Inner Sea the way light comes off nothing else in the world. Twenty years I'd told myself I'd see them someday. They were beautiful. They were *right there*.

And between us and them, filling the valley like ash filling a hearth, was Corvess's host. Thousands of the oath-broken, faceless and tireless, and at their centre a darkness that was not an absence but an *appetite*, and even at this distance I felt it turn toward me like a head turning, and I felt it *recognize* the warmth in my closed fist.

"Mirabel," Oren said quietly. "How much of you is left to spend?"

I did the coward's arithmetic one last time. The boy, the road, the man. Three things, and a valley full of the unkept between them, and one vial warm in my fist that could not be spent on all three.

"You know," I said, "for a spirit of the eternal covenant, you really do pick your moments."

Below us, the host began to climb.
