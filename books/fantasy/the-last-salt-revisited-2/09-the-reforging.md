## 9. The Reforging

The covenant-chamber had been waiting a long time to be disappointed.

That was my first thought as we came down the last stair into it, and I want it noted that even then, at the bottom of the world with an army at our backs, I was still making jokes. It's what I do instead of screaming. I've made a career of it.

The chamber was round and vast and salted through like a frozen sea stood on end. Every surface glittered white — walls, floor, the ribs of stone arcing overhead — and the light didn't come from anywhere so much as from everywhere, a pale glow rising out of the salt itself, the way memory rises unbidden at three in the morning. In the center stood a basin the size of a grave, cut from a single block of grey stone, dry as dust.

Oren stopped at the edge of the stair and looked at all that waiting whiteness the way you'd look at a house you'd been born in and exiled from.

"It's empty," he said. "It's been empty since the wardens fell."

"Cheerful place," I said. "Very them."

"Mirabel." He turned to me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the child's wide wonder, not the ancient patience. Something closer to fear, which from a being who had watched mountains fossilize was frankly insulting. "Once we begin, the chamber will take whatever is offered. It does not ask twice. It does not give back."

"Yes, well," I said, "neither did my mother, and I turned out fine."

He didn't laugh. Spirits of the eternal covenant have no sense of humor. It's in the contract, presumably.

I set the vial on the rim of the basin. Inside it swam everything I had left that was mine: the ferrywoman's debt paid in a childhood summer, the pass of Hollow Vows walked blindfolded, the taste of rain on Greyharbor slate, Oren's hand in mine on a burning street. A thief's whole estate, inventoried. It fit in a vial. That should tell you something about thieves.

Above us, faint through a hundred fathoms of stone, the mountain began to ring. Boots on the salt road. Corvess's host, climbing.

"They'll be here before the rite finishes," Oren said.

"Then we'd best be rude and finish early."

* * *

I felt him hesitate at the threshold, and I understood why.

The free cities are built on forgetting — that is their great talent and their great shame. They forgot the deep, they forgot the pact, they forgot the wardens who kept it, and they paved over the door to the chamber because doors to important things make people nervous. Oren stood at that paved-over seam where the last stair met the city's foundations, and I could feel him listening to the stone the way I listen to a lock: reading what it wanted, what it remembered, what it had been told never to say aloud.

*They are not wicked,* he said to me in the tongue that made salt bloom. *Only unkept. There is no oath left in them strong enough to hold a shadow.*

*Then we'll have to be their oath,* I said, because it sounded noble, and because underneath the noble sound it happened to be true.

We went down together. He carried no torch. He didn't need one; the dark opened around him like a held breath. And I noticed — I had been noticing for days and refusing to say it — that he moved wrong now. Not like a child. The escape from Corvess's fortress had cost him years in a single night, and what walked beside me wore a boy's shape the way a coat wears out at the elbows: still recognizably Oren, but thinning at the seams, translucent at the wrists when the light struck badly. He was spending himself to keep me alive, and neither of us had said it, because saying it would have made it a debt, and debts between friends are heavier than debts between strangers.

At the chamber's mouth he stopped me with a hand on my arm.

"When you speak," he said, "you must mean it. Every word. The old tongue has no room for almost. Corvess speaks it fluently and means nothing, and that is precisely why the covenant rejects him. You lie beautifully, Mirabel Lockridge. Tonight you cannot afford to be beautiful."

"I've never once managed it," I said. "Ask anyone I've robbed."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the mountain rang again, nearer, and the smile died of exposure.

* * *

Corvess arrived the way rot arrives in fruit: quietly, from the inside.

One moment the chamber was ours and silent. The next, the far arch was full of him — Vane Corvess, usurper, oath-eater, walking down the stairs alone while behind him the doorway filled with the faceless ranks of the unkept, soldiers who had sold every memory of themselves for strength and now stood in perfect, patient rows, waiting to be pointed at things. Their faces were smooth as river stones. I had walked among them once by wearing their emptiness like a cloak, and I had promised myself I would never feel that again.

"You're late," I called up. "We saved you a seat."

Corvess smiled. He had a good smile. He'd bought it, presumably, from someone who'd needed the money.

"Mirabel Lockridge," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded like a ledger entry. "Warden's heir. Do you know how long I have hunted that sentence? Two hundred years I have eaten forgotten oaths, and every one of them tasted of this room. Every broken promise in Thornfell leads back here, down, to the clause. Give me the child and the words, and I will give you back your childhood entire. Every stolen year. Painless. Complete. You would walk out of here remembering your mother's face."

And there it was. The thing I had wanted since Greyharbor, offered on a plate, warm and painless and complete. My mother's face. I couldn't have told you her name. I couldn't have told you if she'd laughed or wept or simply gone away, because I had spent those memories coin by coin to feed myself, and the man standing in front of me was offering the whole sum back with interest.

Beside me, Oren said nothing. That was the worst part. He knew — he had always known — that this was a choice I had to make with no one holding my hand, because a choice made with help isn't a sacrifice, it's a transaction.

"My mother's face," I said slowly. "Yes. That's tempting. Let me counter-offer."

I picked up the vial.

"No."

* * *

He said no.

I had known he would. I had known since the night he gave me fragments of his own stolen hoard on the dissolving road, handing over pieces of himself the way misers hand over bread, and lied about how much each piece cost. Humans are strange. They hoard what doesn't matter and spend what does, and they call the spending love.

But knowing he would refuse and hearing him refuse are different weights, and when Corvess's smile curdled and the unkept began to pour down the stairs like water finding a crack, I understood at last why the covenant had chosen this particular hollow man as its vessel for the final clause.

Not because he was worthy. Because he was willing.

"Wardens of the Salt Covenant," I said, and my voice came out in two languages at once, mine and the old tongue braided through it, and the chamber woke. Light climbed the walls. The salt began, very softly, to sing. "Hear the clause. Hear it kept."

*What is held may be given,* I said, and Oren's voice moved under mine like current under ice, correcting, steadying. *What is given cannot be taken. What is sacrificed willingly outlasts the sacrificer.*

The unkept reached the floor of the chamber and stopped, all of them at once, as if the air itself had closed its hand. Corvess came on alone. He had to. Whatever he was now, whatever two centuries of eaten oaths had built him into, the chamber would only deal with the man.

"The clause requires a warden," he said. "You are no warden. You're a thief."

"Yes," I said. "A thief keeps faith with what he steals better than a warden ever did. Ask any salt-merchant in Greyharbor. Ask Brannoc and her ledger." I uncorked the vial. "My family broke their office and ran. I'm the last of them, and I'm done running, and I am going to pay the arrears in full."

"Mirabel—" Corvess began, and for the first time in two hundred years, Vane Corvess sounded afraid. "You don't know what you are giving up. You don't even know who—"

"That's rather the point," I said, and drank.

* * *

Memory does not leave quietly.

It went out of me like heat out of a dying fire — the rain on Greyharbor slate first, then the pass, then the ledger, then the boy's hand, then the boy, then the word *boy*, then the shape of my own name, unraveling thread by thread, and I spoke through the loss because stopping meant losing everything without meaning it, and meaningless sacrifice is just theft committed against yourself.

*What is held may be given.* My mother's face went, and I never saw it, and I gave it anyway. *What is given cannot be taken.* My name went, and I gave that too. The chamber filled with light like a struck bell fills with sound, and the basin drank, and the dry salt sea of the walls began to weep brine, and somewhere above us every salt road in Thornfell groaned awake like joints remembering motion.

Corvess lunged for the basin — of course he did — and the covenant declined him.

I saw it happen. I was mostly light by then, but I saw it. Every oath he had ever broken rose out of him at once, two centuries of them, the promises he'd swallowed to grow strong turning on their devourer, and they unmade him politely, thoroughly, from the edges inward, the way salt dissolves. He screamed with a hundred borrowed voices, and then with none at all, and then the chamber stood empty except for the light, and the singing, and a small figure kneeling beside what was left of me, which was not much, which was perhaps a man-shaped absence holding the ghost of a hand.

*I have you,* Oren said, in both tongues, in the only tongue that mattered. *Sleep now. I will remember for both of us.*

And I thought — with the last coin I had — *don't let me forget the child at the door.*

* * *

The chamber went quiet the way a bell goes quiet: reluctantly, and never entirely.

Oren knelt a long time beside the man who had been Mirabel Lockridge, holding what remained, which was breath, and warmth, and nothing else. The basin brimmed with given memory and overflowed, slow as honey, into channels cut ten thousand years ago for exactly this, and the light traveled up through the salt and out into the roots of the world, and far above, in the dark, every dissolved road began to set like new ice.

The covenant, remade, asked its keeper one question, in the old tongue, gently:

*And what will you do with what you were given?*

Oren looked down at the sleeping stranger who had chosen truth over self, who had drunk the cup to its dregs rather than sell a child for a mother's face, and who would wake knowing nothing — not his name, not his trade, not the boy whose hand he had held on a burning street.

"He'll need a start," Oren said, in the human speech, smiling with an ancient face that had finally earned the right to wear one. "Something small. Something true."

He reached into the light, and lifted out a single memory, and kept it safe: a rainy night in Greyharbor, and a knock at a door.
