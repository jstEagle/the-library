## 10. A Name Worth Keeping

I woke to the smell of salt and rain, and to the fact that I did not know my own name.

Not *forgot* it — forgetting implies there was something there once, a shape you could trace with your tongue, a groove in the floorboard of yourself where the name had stood. This was different. This was standing at a door and finding no door. I lay on a narrow bed in a room above a harbor tavern, listening to gulls argue over something dead, and searched for myself and found only rooms.

There was a word for that, probably. There were words for everything; I seemed to have kept all of them. Language had survived whatever had happened to me the way rats survive a fire — scurrying out afterward, unbothered, ready to move into the ruin. I knew what a keel was. I knew what grief was, in the abstract, the way a man who has never been burned knows fire is hot. I knew I had hands, because they were lying on the blanket, scarred across both palms in patterns that suggested work of some delicacy and some danger.

They were stranger's hands. I watched them like a man watching a dog that might or might not be his.

The window looked out on Greyharbor's lower quay, rain coming down in that fine grey needle-fall the town does so well, fishing boats knocking their hulls together in the swell. Somewhere below, a woman was singing a working song about a husband lost at sea, and every third line had been replaced with nonsense syllables, the way songs go when the words wear out before the tune does. It should not have made my chest ache. It did anyway. Apparently they'd left me the capacity for ache, which struck me as an oversight.

"You're awake."

I turned. He sat in the corner chair like he'd been sitting there for days — and from the state of the cups on the sill, he had. Tall. Grey-eyed, but not the flat grey of weather; the grey of deep water, the kind that looks calm and holds ships. His hair was dark going silver at the temples, and his face was the kind you stop trying to guess the age of after a while, because the answer keeps changing depending on the light.

"I don't know you," I said.

"No," he said, and smiled, and it was a sad smile worn smooth, like a coin carried too long. "You don't. That's rather the point. My name is Oren."

"Oren." It sat strangely in my mouth, and then it didn't. Like a key that fits a lock you didn't know you owned. "Did we — do I owe you money?"

He laughed. It transformed him entirely, ten years falling off his face, and for one heartbeat I saw someone else underneath — smaller, younger, a child's laugh in an adult throat — and then it settled back into the man.

"Nothing so grim," he said. "You saved the world, as it happens. Or the roads, at least, which is most of the same thing."

"The roads." The words came up from somewhere below memory, below even the absence of memory. Salt roads. I said it aloud: "Salt roads," and tasted it, mineral and old.

"They hold," Oren said. "Every league from here to the free cities. The covenant stands reforged. You did that." He paused, and honesty crossed his face like weather. "*We* did that. Though the willing part, the part that cost — that was yours alone. No one could have done it for you. Believe me, I looked for a way. For years, I looked for a way."

"Years?"

"Mm. Sit up slowly. There's tea. You'll want the tea."

* * *

The tea was terrible, which apparently is a universal constant, because some part of me knew exactly how terrible tea was supposed to be and judged this cup accordingly. We drank it by the window while the rain worked through its repertoire. He told me things in careful doses, the way you feed a fire too recently built: that the town was called Greyharbor, that the war — if it had been a war — was over, that the man called Vane Corvess was gone, unmade, unwritten, that people who remembered me — and there were such people, he said, watching me carefully when he said it — would be arriving within the week to see whether I still existed.

"Do I?" I asked. "Still exist?"

"That depends entirely on what you think existing requires."

"Don't dodge. It's irritating, and I can tell you're good at it, which means I'm probably worse."

He laughed again, that same double-laugh, man and boy. "You exist," he said. "You are, at present, mostly potential. A blank page is still a page, Mirabel."

Mirabel. My own name, handed to me across a chipped cup, and it landed in me and found — nothing. Not an echo. Not a shiver. A stranger's name wearing my face like a borrowed coat.

"It doesn't fit," I said.

"It will. Names are like boots that way. Or you'll grow into it. Or—" he shrugged, elegant, ancient, tired, "—you'll choose another, and the world will learn it, and that will be truer than anything inheritance ever gave anyone."

"Inheritance," I repeated, and something moved in the empty rooms of me. Not a memory. The ghost of a doorway where a memory had been bricked over. "There was something about my family. Wasn't there? Something I paid."

Oren went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "Yes."

"And you're not going to tell me."

"I can't. What you gave, you gave whole. If I hand the story back piece by piece, it isn't sacrifice anymore — it's a transaction with extra steps. And the covenant—" he tapped his chest, where under the shirt something faintly luminous shifted, like light through ice, "—the covenant and I keep strict accounts these days. We've learned what happens to those who don't."

* * *

Three days passed. I learned the tavern keeper's name (Marda), her dog's name (Bastard), and the precise angle at which Greyharbor's rain turns from needle-fall to honest downpour around midmorning. I learned that my hands remembered things my head did not: they could pick a lock in the dark, mend a net, shuffle cards beautifully, and once, reaching for the sugar, they drew a small precise sigil in spilled salt without consulting me at all. Marda saw it. She went white, then went into the back room, then came back and put a free dinner in front of me and refused to discuss it.

On the fourth evening, Oren came to my room carrying a small clay pot sealed with wax, and set it on the table between us like a man setting down something fragile and explosive in roughly equal measure.

"What is it," I said.

"A memory. Yours. One only." He turned the pot slowly; the wax caught the lamplight. "When the reforging took everything, I was permitted to keep a single fragment in trust. Custody, not ownership. I've held it since, waiting to see what kind of man woke up."

"And what kind did?"

"The jury's still deliberating." He pushed the pot toward me. "It's the night we met. A child knocking on your door in the rain, speaking a dead tongue, salt crystallizing on your windowsill. Your first sight of me. The moment this entire road began."

My hands, traitors that they were, were already moving toward it.

And stopped.

Because here is what four days of emptiness had taught me: I knew what I was now, precisely because I wasn't anything else. Every kindness I'd offered Marda, every hour I'd spent at the quay learning knots from fishermen who didn't know my name, every time I'd given my last coin to the beggar by the fish market — none of it was habit. It was choice, naked and unpropped, made by a man with nothing behind him but the doing of it. Whatever Mirabel Lockridge had been — thief, warden, traitor's son, savior — that man had been built out of material I no longer possessed. And the man sitting at this table, reaching for the pot, had been four days old and already better than I suspected the original ever was.

If I took the memory back, I wouldn't be adding to myself. I'd be replacing myself. And I would never again know whether any kindness I did afterward was mine, or merely his, echoing forward through a borrowed past.

"Keep it," I said.

Oren didn't move. "You don't know what's in it. You don't know what you felt. You might be refusing the best night of your life."

"Then it's a generous gift to give away." I turned the pot once more, feeling its weight — lighter than a life, heavier than a mountain, all at once. "Whoever I was, he chose truth over comfort at the end. Everyone keeps telling me that. So let me start the way he finished. Let me earn it."

For a long moment Oren just looked at me, deep-water eyes going bright in the lamplight, and then he did something I hadn't seen him do in four days of careful storytelling. He bowed. Low, formal, the bow of one office to another — and though I had no idea what office he thought I held, my spine straightened to receive it like it had been waiting for exactly that.

"The covenant acknowledges you, warden," he said quietly. "Clause-final, oath-kept, name unspent." He straightened, and the sadness in his smile had changed quality — less mourning now, more pride. "You know, I wore a child's face for three hundred years waiting for someone to make the choice you just made. Most men would have grabbed the pot with both hands."

"Most men haven't spent four days being nobody. It's clarifying."

"It is that." He rose, tucked the pot inside his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "The merchants' guild arrives tomorrow. They'll want to thank you. Some of them owe you apologies you won't remember receiving. And there's—" he hesitated, and for the first time the ancient creature looked genuinely uncertain, "—there's a girl in the free cities who's been asking after you. Brannoc says she carries a debt against your name. She insists on paying it herself. In person."

"A debt," I said. "To a stranger. With my old ledger burned, how would anyone even prove what I'm owed?"

Oren grinned, and beneath the grin, briefly, the child showed through — rain-wet, knock-kneed, hopeful. "That," he said, "is exactly the sort of question you used to love. Welcome back to the world, Mirabel Lockridge. Try to stay out of the ledgers this time."

He left. I stood at the window a long while, watching the rain stitch the harbor shut, my strange scarred hands resting on the sill, and below me the salt roads gleamed under the moon — whole, unbroken, running east toward cities full of people I'd have to meet as a stranger, debts I'd have to reckon as a novice, a name I'd have to spend wisely because it was the only thing I owned.

Somewhere out there was a girl with a grudge and a grievance, and a guild full of apologizing merchants, and a life I hadn't lived yet.

I flexed my hands, picked up the terrible tea, and started making plans.
