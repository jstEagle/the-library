## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tonight

The mule's name was Duchess, and she was a liar.

Astrid had known this for three years — ever since the night she'd pretended to founder at the bottom of the Widow's Stair so that Marta would unload her and let her walk the last mile free. She lied about being tired, about being hungry, about whether there was a cliff on her left. Tonight she was lying about the path ahead, planting her hooves and rolling her eyes back with theatrical despair, as though the switchback before them led directly into the mouth of some mountain god.

"Duchess," Astrid said, "you have walked this pass forty times."

Duchess stared at him with one wet, innocent eye.

"You walked it in fog. You walked it in sleet. You once walked it while wearing an entire stolen chandelier."

Duchess snorted, which in mule meant *I remember nothing and regret less*, and finally stepped forward onto the switchback, and the whole convoy behind her — eleven people, nine more mules, and one extremely nervous goat named Prelate — creaked into motion like a necklace of beads pulled over a head.

Astrid Oyelaran walked point, as he always did on the Thornfell roads, and did not exist, which was his particular talent and the reason Marta paid him in first pick of the dried apples.

He didn't vanish, exactly. Vanishing was loud. What Astrid did was quieter: he became the sort of thing a person's eye slid off without comment. A shadow among shadows. A rock that happened to be shaped like a young man. Wardens could ride within arm's reach of him on a narrow trail and feel nothing but the vague unease of having forgotten something — usually their own names, briefly, or where they'd left their tea. His old master Yew had called it *unremarkability* and had once spent an entire winter teaching him to be boring on purpose, which Astrid felt was the cruelest thing anyone had ever done to a sixteen-year-old, and also the most useful.

Tonight the talent mattered more than usual. The convoy carried salt — real salt, crown-stamped salt, lifted from a warehouse in Lowmarsh and bound for buyers who preferred their transactions unrecorded. If a borderwarden patrol caught them in the open passes, it wouldn't be a fine. It would be ropes, or worse, because witch-finders had come into the mountains two weeks ago asking questions, and everyone knew what questions from witch-finders cost.

So: no existing tonight. Astrid was very good at it. He'd been practicing since he was seven years old, when he'd learned that the surest way to survive an angry man was to be furniture.

The eve of his birthday, and his gift to himself was to be nobody at all. It felt, if he was honest, appropriate.

* * *

They made the ruined waystation at Hollowmere an hour past moonrise, which by Astrid's count was also an hour past the moment he officially turned seventeen, though nobody had marked it. The waystation had been a tollhouse once, back when kings taxed the passes instead of merely bleeding them; now it was a stone shell with a collapsed roof on its north side and a hearth that still drew, which was the only luxury smugglers required from architecture.

Marta took one look at the sky, then at the roofless corner, then at Astrid.

"Snow by morning?"

"Snow by midnight," Astrid said. "Heavy. We'll want the animals in the old stable bay and double watch on the east road."

"You're certain?"

"The air tastes like iron and my knees have been complaining since the Widow's Stair. Also Duchess told me."

"Duchess is a liar."

"She's honest about weather," Astrid said. "It's the only thing she doesn't gamble on."

Marta grinned — she had a gap-toothed grin that had charmed three customs officers out of their posts and one husband out of his life savings — and started barking orders in the low, musical cant of the crew. Fenn went for wood. Old Sefa began the argument with the goat that constituted Prelate's nightly lodging. Within twenty minutes the ruin smelled of stew and wet wool, and the storm arrived precisely on schedule, throwing fistfuls of white against the standing walls like a creditor who'd found them at last.

Astrid ate his stew, accepted a cup of Sefa's terrible plum brandy — "medicinal," she insisted, though for what ailment remained unspecified — and listened to the crew argue about whether the witch-finder in Lowmarsh wore his rings on both hands or only the left, which was apparently a matter of doctrinal importance. He laughed in the right places. He existed, for these hours, in the small warm way that only happened indoors, in winter, among people who'd feed you before they asked your business.

Then, near midnight, Fenn said: "Astrid. There's a door in the cellar."

"There's no cellar."

"There's a cellar now. The snow's drifted against the north wall and I fell through what I thought was floor." Fenn rubbed his elbow. "It's not floor. It's a trapdoor. And under it there's stairs, and a room, and a smell like a library that lost an argument with a swamp."

Every head turned to Astrid, because finding things was the other half of his work. Roads, caches, water in dry season, doors in cellars. Marta liked to say the boy could find a needle in a haystack, and then find the haystack's secret second haystack.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

The stair went down twelve steps into cold air that tasted of dust and old tallow. Fenn handed him a lantern, and Astrid held it high, and the little room resolved itself out of the dark: a scholar's bolt-hole, probably, from the tollhouse days. A desk. A chair with three legs. Shelves carved into the stone, mostly empty, their contents long since rotted or looted.

Except one shelf, at the back, behind a fall of rubble, where a leather case sat upright like a patient guest.

Astrid knew better than to touch found things in the mountains. Found things in the Thornfells were sometimes traps, sometimes curses, and occasionally — per Sefa — the property of a witch who would invoice you later. He circled the case twice. He held the lantern close and looked for wires, wards, the glint of hexwork. Nothing. Just old leather gone green at the corners, stamped with a device he almost recognized: a crown, and beneath the crown, lines like falling rain.

Salt-rain. The sigil of the Salt Court, a century dead.

His hand moved before his caution did. Later he would think about that — how his fingers closed on the clasp the way a reader's thumb finds the edge of a page, eager, already turning.

Inside was a book.

It was not beautiful. It was a working book, thick as a brick, its spine patched twice, its margins crowded with annotations in at least four hands. The title page read:

***THE BRINE CONSIDERED: Being a True Account of the Hunger in the Crown, with Prognostications***
*Set down in the Year of the Long Frost, 101 years past.*

"A prophecy book," Fenn whispered, from the top of the stairs, because Fenn had the courage of a much braver man standing somewhere safely behind him. "Astrid, those are illegal. Like, *burn-the-house-around-it* illegal."

"I know."

"We should burn it."

"We should absolutely not carry it anywhere, is what we should do," Astrid said, and heard the lie in his own voice even as he spoke it, and hated it a little.

Because he'd opened the book. Because it had fallen open — books do that, they fall open where they're handled most, and someone had handled this page hundreds of times — to a page of prognostications, dense columns of cramped script, and his eye had done what eyes do, sliding down the columns hunting for sense, and had stopped.

Stopped hard, the way a cart stops when the axle breaks.

*In the hundred-and-first year after the setting-down of this account,* the line read, *on the eve of the turn of winter, the Brine shall name its undoing, and its undoing shall walk the Thornfell roads unseen.*

Astrid stood very still.

The eve of the turn of winter. Tonight. His birthday, which fell on the eve of the turn of winter, because his mother had had a sense of humor about calendars and Marta had never let him forget that he'd been born during the worst blizzard in a decade, delivered by candlelight in the back of a wagon while Sefa swore in three languages.

And *unseen*. Walking the Thornfell roads. Guiding a convoy, invisible, unremarkable, a shadow among shadows.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the book, to nobody.

Fenn's boots came halfway down the stairs and stopped. "What?"

"It's nothing. Old book. Wrong dates." He was already flipping backward through the pages, hunting the colophon, the scribal note, anything that would say *copied in such-a-year* and prove the arithmetic harmless. Prophecy books were like almanacs; you could make them fit anything if you squinted. Yew had taught him that too. *Prophecy is a hook,* she'd said, in her dry smoke-of-a voice. *And readers are bait. Anyone who finds themselves in a prophecy has been found on purpose.*

But the colophon didn't cooperate. The colophon said, in a careful antique hand:

*Set down and finished in the Year of the Long Frost. Let it be read in its proper season, or not at all.*

One hundred and one years. This year.

Astrid sat down slowly on the three-legged chair, which wobbled but held, the way his entire understanding of himself did not. He read the passage again. Then the paragraph beneath it, which he hadn't wanted to read the first time and read anyway, because that is the nature of paragraphs.

*Its undoing shall bear no crown and seek none. The Brine shall reach for him as it reached for kings, and find that it cannot hold what refuses to be held. On that night the hunger shall know fear, and fear shall make it careless, and careless it may be broken — or bound, or bargained with, or fed, according to the wisdom of the hour. The book does not say which. The book was never permitted to know.*

"Well," Astrid said to the empty room, "that's reassuringly specific."

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Here is the thing about Astrid Oyelaran, and the thing he himself understood least about himself: his whole life, his invisibility had been a comfort. While other children dreamed of being seen — cheered, crowned, sung about — Astrid had perfected the opposite art and called it safety. Nobody sees the furniture. Nobody burns the furniture. Nobody writes prophecies about the furniture.

And now some century-dead monk with cramped handwriting had looked down the long throat of time and written his name on tonight.

Well — not his name. Astrid checked, twice, heart hammering, scanning every line of the passage. No name appeared. *Its undoing.* *He.* *The unseen walker.* Anonymous. Which meant, said the desperate clerk in his head, that it wasn't really him. It couldn't be. It could be anyone who happened to be walking the Thornfell roads unseen on the eve of the turn of winter — a smuggler, a fugitive, any one of perhaps four people in the world —

The lamp guttered again.

And in the gutter-light, for half a heartbeat, the page changed.

Astrid never managed to explain this afterward, not even to himself, and he tried often. But in that flicker, the ink seemed to move — not smearing, not shifting, but *settling*, the way sand settles in water, as though the words were only now deciding what they had always said. And in the margin, beside the line about the unseen walker, in a hand smaller than the rest, newer than the rest — a hand that made the back of his neck prickle with a familiarity he could not place and did not want to —

in that margin, faint as breath on glass, three words had appeared that had not been there before:

*Not anyone. You.*

The flame steadied. The ink was ordinary ink. The margin was empty.

Astrid sat in the cold cellar of a ruined tollhouse with snow howling overhead and a stolen salt cargo sleeping above him, and did the arithmetic of terror. Someone had wanted this book found. Someone had put it behind rubble in a cellar under a waystation that smugglers used every season, on the one night of the year that matched the page. That wasn't prophecy. That was *bait*, laid with a fisherman's patience, and he had picked it up with his own eager hand.

Which meant that whatever came next had been planned by someone who knew his routes, his habits, his birthday.

Someone who had been watching him be invisible, and had finally, patiently, learned to see.

Above him, through twelve steps of stone, he heard Duchess begin to scream.

* * *

Astrid took the stairs three at a time.

The stable bay was chaos — mules plunging, Prelate the goat achieving altitudes previously theoretical, and Duchess backed into the far corner with her ears flat, screaming that particular saw-blade scream mules reserve for the end of the world. Snow blew sideways through the roofless corner. The fire had scattered embers across the hearthstones.

And standing in the doorway of the waystation, outlined against the storm, was a figure in oilcloth that shed the snow like water.

For one merciful instant Astrid thought: *warden, just a warden, we'll bribe him.* Then the figure pushed back its hood, and the lantern light caught a face Astrid knew from a dozen frightened campfire descriptions — a long face, gray-eyed, patient as arithmetic, with a ring on each hand.

Witch-Finder. Here. Tonight. At the exact coordinates of the page.

The man looked across the ruined room, past the plunging mules, past Marta rising from her bedroll with a knife already open in her fist, past all of them —

and his gray eyes found Astrid as easily as a hand finds a face in the dark.

Found him. Held him. *Saw* him.

Seventeen years of being nobody, and it had failed him on the one night it mattered most.

"Ah," said the witch-finder, in a voice like a key turning. "There you are."
