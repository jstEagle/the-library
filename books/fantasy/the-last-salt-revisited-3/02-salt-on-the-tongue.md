## 2. Salt on the Tongue

Astrid did not sleep.

This was not unusual — a smuggler's apprentice learned to sleep in snatches, like a cat on a wall — but tonight he lay in the ruined waystation's loft with the book open on his chest, rising and falling with his breath, and every time he closed his eyes he saw the same two lines of cramped brown ink:

*And the boy Oyelaran, who walks unseen, shall come to the Thornfell crossing on the eve of his nineteenth year.*

*He shall be the Brine's undoing.*

His name. His birthday. Tonight.

The loft smelled of old hay and older mouse, and through the broken slats of the roof he could see the stars doing what stars always did: nothing useful whatsoever. Below him, the convoy slept in a tangle of blankets and mules. Marta snored like a landslide deciding whether to happen. Old Piet talked to the ceiling in his sleep, negotiating rates with someone who was clearly winning.

Astrid sat up.

*It's coincidence,* he told himself. *Oyelaran isn't so rare a name. There must be dozens of us. Whole villages of us, probably, all named by some great-grandmother who liked how it sounded.* And as for the date — well. Books were full of dates. Somewhere in that book there was probably a prediction involving the fourth of Frostmonth and a man named Hedwig, and if a man named Hedwig happened to be born on the fourth of Frostmonth, would that mean anything?

No. It would not. He was being foolish. He was going to close the book, put it under the floorboard where he'd found it, and go back to pretending he was an ordinary invisible person leading an ordinary invisible life.

He looked at the book.

The book looked back, the way books do when they've already won.

"All right," he muttered. "One test. Just to prove you're rubbish."

Because that was the thing about prophecy — everyone knew the stories. The oracle speaks, the hero tries to dodge it, and by dodging it, fulfills it. But those stories always assumed the prophecy was *true*. Nobody ever tested the wretched thing first. Nobody ever said, *hmm, before I let this ruin my life, let's see your track record.*

Yew would have. Yew had rules for everything. *Rule one, Astrid: never trust a door that opens too easily. Rule four: never trust a man who offers you soup twice in one day. Rule nine:* —

Rule nine. He could almost hear her voice, dry as chalk dust: *Rule nine: prophecy is a hook, boy, and readers are bait. Anyone who writes down the future wants something. Ask yourself what they're fishing for before you swallow.*

She'd said it once while gutting fish, which had lent the lesson a certain vividness.

"Fine," Astrid told the book. "Track record first."

He turned to the beginning, because a proper con started at the beginning, and read the earliest predictions with the flat professional skepticism Marta had drilled into him over six years of appraising "genuine relics." Most were vague enough to be worthless — *a harvest of ash, a winter of knives,* whatever that meant. Some were dated. A handful were specific.

He found one near the front, dated ninety-one years past, that made him sit up straighter:

*On the third day of Emberfall, a white hind will drink from the millpond at Hallowford, and the miller's daughter will see it and tell no one.*

Specific. Checkable. Ninety-one years ago.

"That's cheating," he whispered. "You picked things that already happened."

But it wasn't cheating, exactly. It was evidence. If the early predictions matched the past, then either the author had been writing history backwards and calling it foresight — which any forger could do — or the book was genuine, in which case the later pages deserved considerably more respect. And considerably more fear.

He needed recent ones. Things written decades ago that pointed at days *he* had lived through, so he could check them against memory.

He hunted through the middle pages, past prophecies of floods and coronations and a suspicious number of crows, until he found:

*In the spring of the red thaw, the bridge at Marlow's Ford will fall, and a green cart will be on it, and the driver will live, and the horse will not.*

Astrid's mouth went dry.

Two springs ago. He'd been fourteen, running with Marta's convoy over Marlow's Ford in the gray week when the mountain snowmelt came down rust-colored from the iron bogs upstream. The bridge had groaned all day. They'd crossed anyway, because smugglers always crossed anyway, and the span had dropped out from under the last cart — Piet's cart, painted the exact shade of terrible green that Piet insisted helped them hide in orchards.

Piet had swum clear. The horse hadn't.

Nobody outside the crew knew about that horse. There'd been no ballads, no broadsheets, no reason on this earth for a stranger to record it. Astrid stared at the entry until the words blurred, then pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"Coincidence," he said. His voice came out thin. "Green carts are common. Bridges fall all the time."

*Then find another one,* said the part of his brain that sounded like Yew. *If it's chance, it won't repeat.*

He kept reading. It took him less than an hour to find the second one, and this time he laughed out loud, a single unhappy bark that woke one of the mules below.

*A thief with no shadow will steal the Sable Cup from the Vault of Saint Aldric in the autumn of the double moon, and the wardens will blame the rats.*

Three years ago. Autumn of the double moon — the year both moons went full together, which happened once a decade and had every astrologer in the country scribbling nonsense. Astrid had been thirteen, newly apprenticed to Yew, and she had taken him along on what she'd called *an educational outing* to the Vault of Saint Aldric, where they had stolen a cup worth more than the town Astrid was born in, and where the wardens — after hours of searching — had concluded that rats had gotten into the display cases somehow, and had been seen carrying off small valuable objects, which everyone agreed was very strange behavior for rats.

Yew had been delighted. She'd framed a drawing of the rat theory.

Astrid set the book down very carefully on the straw, the way you set down a snake you suspect of opinions.

Two for two, on things nobody else could have known. Plus the hind at Hallowford, if he wanted to count history. That was not a track record. That was a ledger.

"One more," he said hoarsely. "Recent ones. Days I can verify. If you're real, show me something happening *now*, tomorrow, something I can watch with my own eyes."

He flipped toward the end, toward the section where the ink changed color — the newer entries, the ones written in the last few decades — and there, wedged between a prediction about a bishop's scandalous boots (*which will be red, and much remarked upon*) and one about a hard frost in Highsummer (*the vines will die, and the vintners will weep, and deserve it*), he found:

*On the day after the unmaker's naming, a warden of the eastern marches will drown in air, on dry land, beneath a cloudless sky.*

Astrid frowned. *Drown in air* was the kind of phrase prophecy loved — dramatic, uncheckable, poetic. Except. Eastern marches. The convoy was in the Thornfell passes, which ran along the eastern marches like a scar. And he knew exactly one warden of the eastern marches.

Sergeant Dree Halloran. Borderwarden, twenty-two years' service, a broad weathered woman who pretended to hate smugglers with tremendous enthusiasm while accepting bribes of tobacco and turning a blind eye to precisely half of everything, chosen by a system of personal judgment Astrid had once described as *a coin flip, but warmer.* She'd taught him to whistle through cupped hands. She called him *lad* even though she'd long since worked out he was no such thing, because she said *lad* meant *small criminal I'm fond of*, and gender was irrelevant to the category.

Dree Halloran, who was stationed at the Thornfell crossing. Who was, right now, four hundred yards down the road at the checkpoint hut.

*Drown in air.*

"No," Astrid said aloud. "No, that's — that's just me seeing patterns now. That's how it gets you. You show a man two hits and suddenly every vague line looks like his own face."

But he was already pulling on his boots.

* * *

He told himself he was going down to the checkpoint for completely normal reasons. To wish Dree a happy birthday, actually, since she shared the day with him — an old joke between them, two people born on the same night finding themselves annually on the same frozen road, trading a flask back and forth and arguing about whose mother had worse taste in names. It was tradition. It had nothing whatsoever to do with prophecy. He was simply a boy walking down a mountain path at midnight to visit a friend, and if his hands shook a little, well, it was cold. That was all. Cold.

The checkpoint hut glowed with lamplight. Dree was on the porch in her greatcoat, whittling something that had started life as a spoon and was clearly destined to end as kindling.

"Oi," she said, without looking up. "If you're here to bribe me, the rate's gone up. Inflation."

"It's my birthday."

"It's my birthday." She squinted at him. "You look awful, lad. Rough night?"

"I couldn't sleep." True. "Bad dreams." Also true, though the dream had been a book.

Dree grunted and shifted on the bench, making room, and Astrid sat, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the best kind of conversation either of them knew. The mountains ticked and settled around them. Somewhere downslope, water moved under ice with a sound like whispering.

"You ever think about leaving?" Dree asked eventually. "This life. The roads."

"Where would I go?"

"That's what I always say." She pointed the ruined spoon at the dark valley below, where the lowlands spread out toward the capital, fat and lit and lawful. "People think the law's a different country. It's not. It's the same country with worse food." She paused. "Though I'll tell you something, lad. Lately the marches feel wrong. Animals moving strange. Wells tasting brackish, and we're a hundred miles from any sea. Old Tamsin at the mill says her salt keeps going damp in a sealed jar. Salt doesn't do that."

"Salt does do that," Astrid said. "If there's moisture—"

"In a sealed jar, lad."

The wind came down the pass and Astrid shivered, and told himself it was the wind.

They passed the flask. Dree told a story about a goat she'd arrested in '09 — *it headbutted a tax collector; frankly I sided with the goat* — and Astrid laughed in the right places, and watched her hands, and hated himself for watching her hands, checking them for tremors, for signs, like she was already a page he was reading ahead in.

"Dree," he said finally. "Do you ever get the feeling that something's... arranged? That the world's a card trick and somebody's palming the queen?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all his odd questions, which was complete.

"Every day of my life," she said. "That's why I stay out here. Out here, the only thing arranging itself is the weather, and at least the weather's honest about wanting to kill you." She knocked the last splinters off the spoon-stump and stood, joints crackling like green wood. "Go to bed, lad. Birthday rule: nothing important happens after midnight. Anything that seems important after midnight can wait till morning."

She went inside. The lamp dimmed. Astrid stayed on the porch a while longer, looking at the cloudless sky, the dry land, the air — ordinary air, breathable air, air with absolutely no water in it whatsoever.

"Drown in air," he repeated softly. "Rubbish. It's rubbish. Prophecies are always rubbish. That's the whole point of them."

He walked back up to the waystation telling himself this with every step, and almost believed it by the time he reached the loft, and lay down, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and businesslike. Marta had them packed and rolling within the hour, because Marta believed dawn was a moral position. Astrid drove the second cart, which gave him nothing to do but watch the road unspool below and jump at every crow.

Nothing happened. The sun climbed. The ice squeaked under the wheels. By noon he'd nearly managed to fold the whole night away into the part of himself where he kept other unbearable things — a respectable mental attic, well-organized, rarely opened.

Then, at the switchback above Miller's Rest, they met a rider coming up fast: one of the convoy's outriders, Bren, gray-faced, hat in hand.

"Marta," he called up. "The crossing. Sergeant Halloran."

Astrid's blood went to ice water.

"What about her?" Marta called back.

"Dead." Bren wiped his mouth. "Found her this morning on the checkpoint steps, sitting up straight against the door, like she'd nodded off. But—" He stopped, visibly wrestling with how to say it. "Her lungs were full of water, Marta. Clean water. Mountain-cold. The surgeon from Miller's Rest looked her over twice because he didn't believe his own fingers. Full of water. On dry land. Under a sky that hasn't seen a cloud in a week."

The convoy went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet, all the noise draining out of them at once. Mule bells kept tinkling stupidly on, indifferent.

Marta crossed herself with the abbreviated gesture of a woman who believed in God the way she believed in taxes — technically, resentfully, when required. "Twenty-two years on that crossing," she said. "Never sick a day." She looked around at her people. "We'll pay respects on the way through. Hats off, all of you. She was fair to this crew."

They rode down. Astrid rode with them, his body performing all the correct motions while the rest of him stood at a great distance screaming.

At the checkpoint, Dree sat propped against her own door exactly as Bren had described, someone having draped her greatcoat over her shoulders with more dignity than the crown ever showed its wardens. Her ruined spoon lay on the step beside her, finished at last. Astrid took his hat off because his hands needed instructions, any instructions, and that was the only one available.

*Lad,* she'd have said, if she could. *Stop looking like that. Nothing important happens after midnight.*

Except it had. It had happened in broad daylight, on schedule, on the page, in a book a century old that had known her death to the *idiom* — *drowned in air* — before Astrid had ever heard of the Brine, before he understood there was anything in the world worse than witch-finders.

He stood there while the others murmured, and felt the ground tilt, gently, the way the world tilts when you realize the floor you've trusted your whole life has been scenery all along.

* * *

That evening, camped in a hollow below the high pass, Astrid opened the book again by the light of a shuttered candle stub, because he could not stop, and that frightened him most of all. Not the predictions. The *reading*. The way his eyes went to the pages like a tongue goes to a missing tooth.

There was a third entry. He'd seen it last night and refused to finish it, and now he made himself read it through:

*Before the next new moon, salt will rise in the low wells of the eastern marches, and cattle will refuse the water, and the folk will say the sea has come inland, and they will not be wrong.*

He thought of Dree's voice: *Old Tamsin at the mill says her salt keeps going damp in a sealed jar. Salt doesn't do that.*

Salt going damp. Wells going brackish. Cattle refusing water. It was already starting — the prophecy hadn't even bothered to wait; it had simply described the world's slow rot with a bookkeeper's patience, and the world, obligingly, was rotting on schedule.

Astrid closed the book and held it shut with both hands, as though it might breathe.

*Prophecy is a hook,* Yew had said, scraping fish scales into the bucket, not even looking at him. *Readers are bait.*

He'd laughed then. He wasn't laughing now. Because he finally understood what the sentence meant, the whole ugly mechanism of it. A hook isn't just sharp. A hook is *shaped*. Someone bent the metal on purpose. Someone chose these words, these dates, this name — *his* name — and then hid the book in a ruined waystation on a route his convoy used twice a season, under a loose floorboard that any apprentice sent to search for leaks would find.

Books don't hide themselves. Books don't wait.

Someone had fished these waters before. Someone had known that a boy who could walk unseen — who had spent his whole life perfecting the art of not being noticed — would be the one person in the mountains unable to resist a story with his own name in it. You couldn't bait a trap for an invisible man with gold or glory. You baited it with the one thing invisibility starves you of: proof that you exist. Proof that somewhere, in ink, someone had *seen* him.

And he had swallowed it whole. He'd carried it out in his shirt, warmed it against his chest, read it by candlelight like a love letter.

"Who wrote you?" he whispered to the cover. "What are you fishing for?"

The book said nothing, which was answer enough. Hooks never talk. They just wait for the pull.

Below in the camp, Marta's voice rose, giving the night's orders, and Astrid looked toward it — toward the tangle of blankets and mules and terrible singing that had, without anyone ever saying the word, become the only home he had — and felt the vertigo come back stronger than before. Because the book didn't stop at Dree. The book had plans. The book had a whole final act with his name in the title, and every hour he spent holding it, he could feel the line going tauter, reeling him toward a capital city and a hollow king and a page he hadn't dared turn to yet.

Tomorrow he would burn it. Tomorrow, definitely. Tonight he wrapped it in oilcloth and put it inside his coat, against his heart, where it had apparently lived all along.

And in the morning, before he could find the fire, Marta caught his arm at the edge of camp — Marta, who missed nothing, who had been watching him watch the book all day — and said, quietly, in the voice she saved for customs men and funerals:

"Astrid. Lad. We need to talk about what you're carrying."
