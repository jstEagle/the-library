## 3. What the Convoy Owes

Marta Oduya had been running cargo through the Thornfell passes for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things worth writing down.

One: never trust a road that's too easy. Easy roads are roads somebody wants you on.

Two: the crown's men can smell fear, but they cannot smell salt pork, which is why every crate she owned was packed over a false bottom of salt pork. You could hide an altar to a forbidden god under salt pork and no witch-finder would ever look twice.

Three: family is not who you birth and not who you bury and not even who you swear to. Family is who you split the last apple with when the pass is snowed shut and everyone's hungry and nobody says anything about it.

She was thinking about the apple rule the morning Astrid came down from the waystation looking like a boy who'd seen his own ghost and hadn't liked the resemblance.

He was seventeen today. Seventeen, and he walked into camp carrying that century-old book against his chest like it was a coal he couldn't put down, and his eyes did the thing they always did when he was frightened — went vague, went soft at the edges, like a window fogging up. His one gift. Invisibility, or near enough. People looked at him and their gaze slid off like rain off waxed canvas. He'd used it for years to move through checkpoints untouched, and Marta had never once asked where the trick came from, because you don't ask a foundling about his pockets any more than you ask a river why it runs downhill.

But this morning the trick wasn't working. She could see him too well. Every freckle. Every sleepless night written under his eyes in ink.

"Sit," she said, and pointed at the flat rock by the fire, the one shaped like a loaf of bread gone wrong. "And eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're seventeen. Hunger is the only honest thing about you. Sit."

He sat. He took the bowl of barley porridge Tam had made — Tam made porridge the way other people made threats, thick and mean — and he ate four spoonfuls before his hands started shaking so hard the spoon rattled like a tiny tambourine announcing bad news.

Marta let him shake. She was a great believer in letting people shake until they were done shaking. Her mother had taught her that, back when her mother was still a woman who taught things instead of a name carved on a stone above the tree line.

"What's in the bag, Astrid?"

His shoulders climbed toward his ears. "Nothing."

"Astrid." She kept her voice level, the voice she used for negotiating tolls with men who carried knives and opinions. "You've been with us five years. In five years you have lied to me twice. Once about eating my honey bread, which I forgave, because the crime was small and the thief was starving. Once about knowing how to read, which I also forgave, because literacy is a hanging offense out here and I understand a man keeping his neck. That was your ration. Both of them. Spent."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind the supply wagons, Fen was arguing with the mules again, losing, as usual, on points.

Astrid set down the bowl. He reached into his coat and drew out the book, and Marta felt the morning change temperature.

It was old. Not old like a grandmother, old like a mountain. The leather of its cover had gone the color of dried blood, and the clasps were green with age, and when he set it on the rock between them the light seemed to fall into it slightly wrong, the way water bends around a stone.

"It was in the waystation," he said. "In the wall. Behind loose bricks, wrapped in oilcloth, like someone hid it there and meant to come back and didn't."

"People hide things in walls," Marta said. "Usually money. Usually I approve."

"It's a prophecy book." His laugh came out cracked down the middle. "It's — Marta, it has my name in it. My full name. Astrid Oyelaran. And tonight's date. Tonight. It calls me—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, the way men talk about a wound they haven't looked at yet. "It calls me the unmaker. Of the Salt King."

Marta looked at the book. The book, in some sense she could not explain, looked back.

Now. Here is the thing about Marta Oduya that the ballads would get wrong, if anyone ever wrote ballads about her, which she devoutly hoped they wouldn't: she was not brave. Brave people run toward fires. Marta ran away from fires very quickly and very intelligently, and then, once she was safely elsewhere, she thought hard about who had set them, and she went back later with friends and buckets and, on two memorable occasions, a mule named Duchess who hated witch-finders on a personal, spiritual level.

So she did not do anything dramatic. She picked up the book. It was heavier than it should have been, the way guilt is heavy, the way promises are heavy. She turned its pages with a thumb gone leathery from thirty years of rope work, and she read.

Her reading was slow and private, a skill she'd traded a bolt of silk for at fourteen and told no one about, but she could read. And the book could write, apparently, a hundred years ahead of itself, and it wrote things like *the unmaker walks the smuggler's road* and *the Brine shall know his step* and other sentences that made the back of her neck prickle like someone reading over her shoulder.

Then she got to the part about the borderwarden. Bren. The young one at Cold Ford who looked the other way for a cask of brandy and asked after Tam's bad knee every time they passed, because his own father had limped the same way.

"*The warden at the cold ford drowns on dry land,*" she read aloud, because she wanted to hear how terrible it sounded in the open air. "*On the eve of the unmaker's naming.*"

"That was last night," Astrid said. Very quietly. "I checked. This morning. A rider came through — Bren slipped on wet stones crossing the ford, hit his head. Drowned in six inches of water. On dry land, more or less." His hands were in fists now, pressed against his knees. "Three predictions. Three. The miller's second son, the frost out of season, and Bren. All true. All within a day. Marta, what am I supposed to do with that except believe the rest of it?"

What are you supposed to do with that.

Marta closed the book. She held it shut with both hands, like pressing down a lid on something that scratched.

What she was supposed to do, according to thirty-one years of rules, was simple. There was a standing offer from the crown, posted in every garrison town, renewed every spring like a subscription: *any person delivering intelligence regarding agents of the Salt Court's enemies, or persons bearing prophetic materials, shall receive pardon for all past offenses and a letter of amnesty under royal seal.* Marta Oduya had a past long enough to hang from three times over. An amnesty letter under royal seal would buy her crew legal lives. Legal lives. Tam could walk into a market in daylight. Old Sef could see a doctor about his chest without checking the door first. Fen could stop arguing with mules and start arguing with tax assessors, which was his true calling.

And all it cost was handing a frightened boy to the witch-finders, who had methods, plural, and patience, singular and infinite.

She looked at Astrid. He was watching her face the way a man watches dice roll — already calculating the worst number, already grieving it.

"You knew," she said. "You knew what I could do with this. Sell you, sell the book, collect the letter. That's why you nearly shook the porridge off the rock."

"I thought about it," he admitted. "That you might. I'd think it too. If I were you." He tried a smile and it fell off his face halfway up. "I keep waiting for you all to realize what I'm worth dead versus alive. Dead, I'm nothing. Alive, I'm either treasure or plague, depending on who's counting."

"Fen's alive and he's neither," Marta said. "It's possible to be alive and merely annoying. Aim for that."

She sent him to sleep in the third wagon — "not because I don't trust you, but because I want to count spoons while you're gone" — and called the crew together around the fire. Tam. Sef. Fen. Little Wren, who was nineteen and sharp as broken glass. Old Nettie, who was seventy-three and sharper than that. Nine souls in all, plus Duchess the mule, who stood at the edge of the firelight listening the way she always listened, with an air of having heard better meetings elsewhere.

Marta laid the book on a blanket between them and told them everything. The name. The date. The drowned warden. The word *unmaker*, sitting in century-old ink like a knife left point-up in the grass.

Then she said the other thing. The thing about the amnesty letter.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The fire worked. Fen opened his mouth, and Marta raised one finger, and Fen, wisely, closed it.

Finally Tam said, "How much is a legal life worth?" Tam was practical. Tam had buried two husbands and a smuggling partner and had developed a relationship with death best described as professional respect.

"Everything we've ever wanted," Marta said. "Shops. Names in church records. Grandchildren who aren't bastards by law." She paused. "And it costs us Astrid."

"And if we don't?" Wren asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking, the way you ask about weather.

"If we don't, then whatever's coming for him comes for whoever stands near him. That's what the book says, more or less. The Brine knows his step. And the witch-finders read these books, Wren. They've been reading them for twenty years. Sooner or later, someone official reads that page and starts asking who else has read it."

"So we're already guilty," Nettie said. "Just by sitting here." She spat into the fire, a habit she claimed was medicinal. "Good. I hate being innocent. Innocent people get hanged surprised."

Sef coughed, deep and wet. "I've been guilty since the reign of the old king's grandfather. I'd hate to break the streak over a boy who does his share of the loading and never once complained about my snoring."

"He complains constantly," Tam said.

"He complains *quietly*," Sef amended. "There's a difference. It's called manners."

Fen cleared his throat. Everyone braced. "I'll say it, then, since nobody else will. We're not his blood. Five years is five years, but a crew's a crew and a son's a son, and when the rope's around somebody's neck, 'he did his share of the loading' doesn't cut a single fiber of it." He looked around at them, jaw set, doing the arithmetic of survival out loud because somebody had to. "I love the boy. You all know it. But I've seen what witch-finders leave of the people they question, and I've seen what's left of the crews that sheltered them. I'm asking: what does the convoy owe? Exactly. To the letter."

Silence again. Duchess shifted her weight and blew air through her lips, which from Duchess constituted a formal opinion.

Marta reached inside her coat.

She'd been carrying the amnesty offer for eleven years. Not the letter itself — she'd never been offered one — but the shape of it. The idea. *Someday,* she'd told herself, walking cold passes with hot cargo, *someday I cash out. Someday some scared clerk in some garrison office stamps a piece of vellum and Marta Oduya becomes Margaret Odell, respectable widow of nowhere, and every name on every warrant goes soft and gray.*

Eleven years she'd fed that idea scraps from every job. It had grown fat and comfortable, curled up in her chest like a cat, purring *soon, soon.*

She looked at the book on the blanket. At the fire. At her crew — her loud, thieving, snoring, mule-arguing crew — and at the third wagon, where a boy lay awake pretending to sleep, listening to every word, certain as sunrise that he'd overhear his own betrayal.

Here was the truth Marta arrived at, and it arrived not gently but like a cart hitting a rut: the amnesty letter had never actually been hers to want. It was a thing the crown held out the way you hold meat out to a dog whose teeth you want to check. And a woman who sold a child of her own household to buy her freedom wasn't free afterward. She was just owned by something quieter.

"Well," she said, standing, joints cracking in the cold like green wood in a fire. "Here's what the convoy owes."

She walked to the cook wagon, rummaged beneath the false-bottomed salt pork crates — rule two — and came back with a tin box. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was paper, ink, and the good wax. She sat, and she wrote, right there in front of all of them, a letter addressed to the Witch-Finder's Office at Greyharbor Garrison.

*To the honored officers of the crown: I possess knowledge of a prophetic text concerning the unmaker of the Salt King. I will deliver the bearer to you. Name your price.*

She signed it. Marta Oduya, in her careful, hidden, expensive handwriting.

Then she held the corner of it to the fire.

Tam made a sound — half gasp, half laugh. Wren leaned forward. Fen's mouth hung open like a barn door in wind. The paper caught, flared orange, and Marta held it until the flame kissed her fingertips, then dropped the last burning scrap onto the stones and ground it out under her boot, thoroughly, the way you kill anything you mean to stay dead.

"There," she said, flexing her scorched fingers. "That's what we owe him. Nothing less than the whole stupid future we might've had. Because that's how debts work in this crew — you don't pay people back what's convenient. You pay them what it costs."

"Marta," Fen said hoarsely, "that letter was worth—"

"I know what it was worth. I've known for eleven years. Worth more than any of you, which is exactly why none of you were allowed to know I had it." She looked around the circle, daring anyone to argue. Nobody did. Duchess sneezed, which was close enough to applause. "We don't sell our people. We don't sell them when they're useful and we don't sell them when they're cursed and we especially don't sell them when selling is *smart*. Smart gets you hanged eventually anyway, and at least the dumb way, you get to look at yourself in a mirror first."

Nettie cackled. "Speak for yourself. I haven't met a mirror I liked in forty years."

"The point, Nettie."

"The point is you burned eleven years of wanting," Nettie said, softer now, because under all the spite Nettie had a heart like a banked coal. "That's a real funeral. Somebody ought to say words."

"I'll say words," Marta said. "They go like this: fetch the boy. He's been listening to the whole thing and his heart's going like a rabbit's, and I'll not have him spend one more minute believing he's cargo we're pricing."

Astrid came around the wagon slowly, red-eyed, trying to arrange his face into composure and failing at roughly the same rate Fen failed with mules.

"You heard," Marta said. Not a question.

"Some." His voice was wrecked. "The burning part."

"Then you heard the important bit. The rest was just insults aimed at Fen."

"Mostly deserved," Fen called from the fire.

Astrid stood there, holding his elbows, and Marta watched the thing happen to him that she'd watched happen to every stray that ever washed up in her convoy — the exact moment the belief that they were alone cracked. Not broke. Cracked. Belief like that doesn't shatter clean; it splits slow, over months, like ice going out on a spring river. But the first crack, the loud one, that happens all at once.

"I don't know how to be worth this," he said.

"Good," Marta said briskly, because tenderness applied directly to a wound makes it worse; you have to pack it in something rough. "Worth isn't a skill, it's an invoice, and you'll be paying it off for years. Starting tomorrow, you take double watch. And you teach Wren to read. Properly. None of that sounding-out-words nonsense."

"She'll be insufferable."

"Yes. It'll be wonderful." She put her hand on the back of his neck, brief and hard, the only embrace she issued. "Listen to me, Astrid Oyelaran. Whatever's in that book, whoever wrote it, whatever hunger wears the king like a glove — none of it knows who it's dealing with. Books make plans. Books don't plan on mules."

Behind her, Duchess, hearing her name, bit through the lead rope and wandered off toward the feed wagon.

"See?" Marta said.

But Astrid was looking past her shoulder, up the dark throat of the pass where the road bent north, and his face had gone still in a way she didn't like at all.

"Marta," he said. "Last night, at the waystation. The book was hidden in oilcloth behind fresh mortar. Someone sealed it in recently. Within the year." He swallowed. "Someone wanted it found. Someone wanted *me* to find it. And this morning there were hoofprints on the high trail — shod horses, military pattern, moving slow. Searching."

Far off, down in the valley, a horn sounded. Once. Then again — and the second time, another horn answered it, farther east, and Marta's thirty-one years stood up inside her spine and paid attention.

Corvane, the riders would learn to call him, though she didn't know the name yet. All she knew was the sound of hunters coordinating across a valley at dusk, and that the boy beside her had stopped breathing.

"Right," Marta said, turning, voice dropping into the register that moved nine souls and one mule without a wasted word. "Strike camp. We run the low road tonight, the one that isn't on any map, and Astrid—" she caught his eye, and held it, and gave him the only inheritance she had left after burning the rest— "you lead."
