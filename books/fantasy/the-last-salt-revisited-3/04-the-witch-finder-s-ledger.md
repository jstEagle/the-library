## 4. The Witch-Finder's Ledger

Witch-Finder Corvane had read four hundred and eleven prophecy books in his twenty years of service, and he had burned all of them.

Not because they were false. That was the common misconception among the ignorant — that the witch-finders destroyed prophecy out of fear it might come true. Fear was for peasants. No: he burned them because a prophecy left lying about was like a hook left baited in a stream. Someone always swallowed it. And the fish that swallowed this particular hook had been swimming toward Thornfell for three weeks now, and Corvane intended to be waiting at the end of its line.

He made the entry himself, in the ledger, by candlelight, in his small precise hand. *Volume 412. Untitled. Leather boards, water-stained. Provenance: seized from the estate of the late Aldous Merrin, bookbinder, Lowmarsh. Contents: apocalyptic verse concerning the Salt King and "the unmaker." Disposition:* — and here he paused, pen hovering, because disposition was supposed to be simple. *Burned.* He wrote it, and then he looked at it for a long moment, and then he crossed it out.

*Disposition: retained for operational use.*

The crossing-out troubled him more than he let it trouble him on the surface, which is to say not at all, which is to say considerably. Corvane was a tidy man. Tidy desk, tidy boots, tidy soul. Twenty years ago, as a young clerk with ink-stained fingers and a widowed mother to support, he had been assigned to catalog the confiscations of the Office of Inquiry, and he had believed then what he believed now: that somewhere in those mountains of seized paper there was an order, and that order could be found by anyone patient enough to read every page.

He had been patient enough. He had read every page.

* * *

His junior, a nervous young man named Pell with a cowlick and a tendency to apologize to furniture, stood in the doorway of the inn's best room holding two cups of tea.

"Sir? The landlord sent up — well, he said it was tea. I think it might be mostly hot water with a leaf in it."

"That's what tea is, Pell."

"Yes sir. Sorry sir." Pell set the cups down and hovered, which was his natural state of being. "Sir, may I ask — and forgive me, sir — why we're stopping here? In Thornfell? The warrant list has us down for Lowmarsh, then Harrowgate, and Thornfell isn't —"

"The warrant list is a month old," Corvane said pleasantly, turning back to the book spread open on the table before him. "And the man who wrote it reads warrants the way you read them, Pell, which is to say top to bottom without once asking who benefits from the reading. Sit down. Lesson time."

Pell sat. He always looked faintly alarmed when lessons were announced, as though learning might be catching.

Corvane turned the prophecy book around so the boy could see it. It was, outwardly, nothing. A century old, water-bloated, its spine held together with twine and hope. The verses inside were the usual doggerel — salt and shadow, crown and hunger, the sort of thing every failed poet in every generation produced when the wine ran out.

"Now," Corvane said. "Tell me what you see."

Pell leaned in, squinting. "An old book, sir?"

"Everyone sees an old book. Look again. What don't you see?"

A long pause. Pell's brow furrowed with heroic effort. "...Marginalia?" he guessed at last. "Old books usually have marginalia. Notes. Doodles. Someone's grandmother complaining about the price of candles."

"*Very* good." And Corvane meant it; the boy was slow but he was teachable, which was rarer than cleverness. "Four hundred and eleven prophecy books, and every single one of them had marginalia, because readers are vermin and cannot leave a page alone. This one doesn't. Which means one of two things. Either it was written by someone who never read it — impossible — or someone cleaned it. Scrubbed it. Removed every trace of every hand that touched it except the hand that wanted it found."

"But why would anyone —"

"Because a prophecy is a hook, Pell, and hooks want fish. Somewhere out there is a person — or something wearing a person — who wrote these verses a hundred years ago and then spent a hundred years arranging for them to arrive exactly where they needed to be. And last month, they did. A bookbinder in Lowmarsh dies of natural causes, his estate is inventoried, and lo — one volume of apocalyptic verse, naming no name, dated to no night." Corvane tapped the page. "*Except it does name a night. Read the ninth verse again. Slowly. Aloud.*"

Pell read aloud, stumbling over the archaic meter:

*When the thief-child walks the smuggler's road*
*and cannot make himself unseen,*
*when salt lies white on Thornfell stone*
*on the eve of his seventeenth year,*
*then comes the unmaker, crown-breaker,*
*and the Brine shall know its hour is near.*

Silence. Outside the window, the wind came down off the Thornfell passes with a sound like a great wet cloth being wrung out.

"I don't understand, sir. There's still no name. 'Thief-child.' That could be anyone."

"It could be any *thief-child*, yes. But look at the rest of it. A smuggler's road. Thornfell stone. Salt frost on the eve of a seventeenth birthday. Three conditions, all narrow, all checkable." Corvane rose and went to the window. Below, the town of Greyhollow clung to the mountain's knee like a burr, all slate roofs and smoking chimneys, the kind of place where everyone knew everyone and strangers were counted twice. "And here's the beautiful part, Pell. Here's why whoever wrote this book was either a genius or a fool. They gave us a date. Not a day — an eve. A birthday. Do you know how many children turn seventeen in the Thornfell passes in any given season?"

"A... fair number, sir?"

"Dozens. And do you know how many of them walk the smuggler's roads? Fewer. How many of them have the talent of invisibility — real invisibility, hedge-craft, the vanishing trick? Fewer still. And how many of *those* would notice, on the very eve named in the verse, that their trick had stopped working?" Corvane smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "The book isn't addressed to us, Pell. It's addressed to *him*. Every line of it is designed so that only one boy in the world will read it and know, beyond any possibility of doubt, that it means him. The prophecy doesn't predict the unmaker. It *manufactures* him. It walks into a frightened child's hands on the worst night of his life and says: you. It was always going to be you. And a frightened child who believes he is doom incarnate will do exactly what doom is expected to do."

Pell stared at him. "Then... the prophecy's false? Sir?"

"Oh, I expect it's perfectly true." Corvane returned to his chair and took up his tea. "That's the tragedy of it. False prophecies are harmless. True ones are merely weapons. The question is never whether the words come to pass — the question is who sharpened them, and what they mean to cut." He drank. The tea was, as advertised, mostly hot water with ambitions. "We're in Thornfell because the fish has taken the hook. Reports say a convoy went through the high passes three nights ago moving faster than cargo justifies. A borderwarden at Cold Ford died suddenly — heart failure, the local physician wrote, though the physician also spelled 'physician' wrong twice, so we'll allow ourselves some skepticism. And yesterday, a farmer outside Greyhollow reported frost on his south field. White salt-frost, in autumn, on ground that hasn't seen the Brine's touch in living memory."

"You think the boy passed that way."

"I think," Corvane said, "that the Brine is stirring, and the Brine only stirs when it senses the knife coming. Order, Pell. Let me tell you about order."

* * *

Here is what Corvane believed, and had believed for twenty years, and would believe at the end of everything, when belief was all he had left:

He believed that the world before the Brine had been chaos. He had grown up in that chaos — the border wars, the famine years, the petty lords burning each other's granaries while their peasants ate bark. His father had died in one of those wars over a bridge nobody needed. His mother had died of a fever that a functioning state would have quarantined. Chaos had eaten his childhood the way a tide eats a sandcastle, patiently, grain by grain, and Corvane had watched it happen and sworn, at fourteen, standing over two fresh graves, that he would spend his life building something the tide couldn't touch.

Then he'd become a clerk. Then a reader. Then, the first time he'd stood in the vaults beneath the capital and felt the wards hum — the deep, ancient wards that predated every dynasty, the wards that ran on salt and silence — he had understood what the tide actually was, and what kept it out.

The Brine was not a monster. That was the vulgar understanding, the tavern understanding: a hungry god under the mountain, wearing the King like a glove. Superstition. No — the Brine was *pressure*. It was the ocean's weight given intent, the vast indifferent force that pressed against the walls of the world at all times, wanting in. And the crown — the crown was the dam. For nine hundred years, some vessel had held the pressure: absorbed it, contained it, suffered it, so that the land behind the wall could have harvests and laws and children who lived past five. Yes, the vessel wore down. Yes, the King hadn't spoken in years. Yes, there were rumors — Corvane had read the rumors, catalogued the rumors, burned the rumor-adjacent pamphlets personally.

But what was the alternative? Open the wall? Let the tide take the lowlands? He had seen what happened in the provinces where hedge-witches meddled with bindings, where amateur binders cracked vessels they didn't understand. Whole villages gone white. Wells that ran brackish. Crops that came up glittering and dead. He had walked through those villages. He had carried the ledgers of the dead.

Order wasn't cruelty. Order was the levee. And if the levee required maintenance — if occasionally a binder who knew too much, or a prophecy-book that baited too well, had to be quietly removed for the structure's sake — then that wasn't villainy. That was *engineering*. Somebody had to be the engineer. Everyone else got to sleep at night precisely because somebody didn't.

Corvane did not think of himself as a hunter of children. He thought of himself as the man who checked the wall.

It is worth saying, in fairness, that most monsters do.

* * *

The knock came at midnight, which pleased him; competent informants kept competent hours.

"Enter."

Sella slipped in the way she always did, as if the door were a formality she tolerated — a lean grey woman with river-country vowels and eyes that priced everything. She ran his network in the passes: three smugglers, a toll-collector, a widow who sold pies and information in equal measure and seasoned both identically.

"Well?" Corvane asked.

"He's real," Sella said, dropping onto the window seat uninvited. "Or something is. Marta Voss's convoy came down the high road two nights past, running dark, no lanterns. And here's your salt-frost for you — the waystation at Miller's Rest, the ruined one? Shepherd found it glazed white inside. Floor, hearth, the lot. Like something had *wept* in there." She smiled thinly. "Also, and this is the part you'll like: Voss's crew is short one boy. Took him on last spring, they did. Quiet lad. Good with horses. Nobody can tell me where he came from, and in a smuggling crew, *nobody knows* means everybody knows and nobody's paid enough to say."

"Name?"

"Nobody uses one. The pie-widow calls him 'the ghost' — says he can stand in a room full of excise men and not one of them will remember he was there. Hedge-talent. Vanishing trick." Her eyes glinted. "Vanishing trick that stopped working, maybe. Because Marta Voss has been acting like a woman guarding something ever since. Doubled her outriders. Changed her route twice. And tonight—" Sella paused, savoring it, a connoisseur of other people's secrets. "Tonight she bought passage through the Deep Ways. The old mine tunnels under Raven Scar. Nobody buys the Deep Ways unless they're running from something they can't outrun."

Corvane stood and went to the ledger. Under the lamp he wrote: *Subject located. Convoy of M. Voss, twelve souls plus subject. Route: Deep Ways, Raven Scar, thence —* and stopped.

Thence where? To the capital, surely. If the boy believed the book — and the frost on Miller's Rest said he believed — then he'd go to the source. To the King. To the throne room, with whatever half-learned hedge-binding his frightened little mind had assembled, and he'd crack the wall open thinking he was saving the world.

Unless.

Corvane set down the pen. Something itched at the back of his thoughts, the same itch he'd felt crossing out *burned*. Four hundred and eleven books, and in all his years he'd never once encountered a prophecy engineered this cleanly. Whoever wrote it had understood readers. Had understood witch-finders. Had built a trap with two jaws — one for the boy, and one, he was beginning to suspect, for whoever came hunting the boy.

"You're frowning," Sella observed. "You never frown. Frowning costs money."

"Ride ahead tomorrow," Corvane said. "Get me eyes on the Deep Ways entrances, both ends. And find out who else knows Voss is taking them — a route like that has a keeper, and keepers can be bought." He hesitated. "Gently, Sella. I want the boy alive and the convoy unharmed if it can be managed. Whatever else he is, he's seventeen, and someone taught him to vanish instead of fight, and I'd like very much for this to end at a table rather than in a ditch."

"You've gone soft."

"I've gone *competent*. Soft is what happens when you break things you later need whole." He closed the ledger. "One more thing. The book — volume four-twelve. Keep it wrapped in oilcloth, away from salt, and never read the final page aloud. Even alone. Especially alone."

"Why, sir?"

"Because I read it," Corvane said mildly, "and I'm still deciding whether I dreamed what it said."

Sella left. The candle guttered. Corvane sat a while in the dark with his tidy hands folded, listening to the wind worry the eaves, and told himself, as he told himself every night, that the wall would hold, that the tide could be held back one more winter, one more year, by any means necessary —

— and did not quite manage to stop thinking about the final page, and the handwriting on it, which was fresh.

Which, on a book a century old, was impossible.

Which he had therefore decided, provisionally, not to have seen.

Far below, in the black throat of the Raven Scar tunnels, a convoy of thirteen moved without lanterns through the dark — twelve of them trusting, and one of them, paid in coin and fear, memorizing every turn.

Corvane slept soundly. He was, after all, the hero of his own story, and heroes sleep well right up until the chapter where they learn otherwise.
