## 5. Down the Smuggler's Road

Marta gave the order to move an hour after sundown, when the moon was still a rumor behind the clouds.

"No lanterns," she said, standing in the middle of the yard at Hollowmere Farm with her arms crossed like a woman daring the dark to argue. "No pipes, no singing, no whistling that tune Fenric thinks I don't know he's been practicing. If you must sneeze, sneeze into your hat."

"What if it's raining?" asked Fenric.

"Then drown quietly."

Astrid stood at the edge of the gathering with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, bound there by two straps of his pack like something between a newborn and a bomb. Three days had passed since the waystation. Three days of watching every prediction in that book come true like stones dropping into a well, each splash closer than the last. And last night, by firelight, Marta had read the page about the witch-finders aloud in a voice flat as a frozen lake, and then she'd said one word: "Run."

So they ran.

The convoy was eleven souls, four wagons, and a mule named Duchess who had opinions about everything and expressed them only through her eyes, which were enormous and judgmental. They took the low road out of Hollowmere, the one that didn't exist on any crown map because Marta had personally bribed the cartographer into forgetting it, and Astrid walked point with Yew's lessons unspooling in his head like thread from a dropped spool.

*The mountains have bones, boy. The smuggler roads are just the places where the bones show through. Learn to feel them under your feet and no witch-finder alive can follow.*

He felt for them now. The Thornfells at night were a country of textures: pine duff that swallowed a bootfall whole, scree that announced you to three valleys, the strange packed silence of old game trails where even the wind seemed to walk politely. Every fifty paces he stopped and listened — not with his ears, which were ordinary, but with that other sense Yew had beaten into him over six years of apprenticeship, the one that read the mountain the way a blind man reads a face.

Behind him, the wagons creaked their slow music. Old Tam drove the lead wagon with his pipe long cold in his fist, chewing on the stem out of pure habit. Beside him rode Sefa, twelve years old and furious about being awake, wrapped in a blanket like an indignant moth.

"Are we going to die tonight?" Sefa whispered to Astrid as he passed.

"Statistically unlikely," Astrid whispered back.

"That's what you said about the bees."

"The bees were a statistical anomaly."

"You cried."

"I was *stung*, Sefa."

She grinned and burrowed deeper into her blanket, and Astrid felt the familiar warmth of it, the little gravity this crew exerted on him, the pull of people who knew his worst stories and kept him anyway. He'd spent six years learning to be invisible — first as Yew's apprentice, slipping through towns unnoticed, then here, among smugglers, where being forgettable was a professional skill. He was good at it. He'd built his whole life on it.

And then a book had looked up at him across a century and said *you*, and invisibility had died of embarrassment somewhere in the Thornfell passes.

They made the first waypoint — a hollowed oak the size of a chapel, hollowed by lightning two generations back — a full hour ahead of schedule. Marta allowed ten minutes of rest. People ate hard bread and harder cheese in the dark, passing things by touch, and Fenric nearly fed his own fingers to Duchess before realizing the shadow near his hand had teeth.

"How far behind us are they?" Marta asked quietly, appearing at Astrid's shoulder.

"I don't know yet." He closed his eyes and listened down the road they'd come. "There's something. Not close. A weight on the roads, like... like a lot of boots all agreeing with each other."

"Riders?"

"Worse. Organized riders."

Marta spat with feeling. "Corvane." She said the name the way other people said *plague*. "Tam told me about him once, years ago. Twenty years reading confiscated prophecy books, they say. Can recite the Brine's catechism backward. Believes every word of it." She shook her head. "Give me a greedy man every time. Greedy men you can buy. Believers you can only outrun."

"Then let's be fast," Astrid said.

* * *

The second half of the night was where Yew's lessons earned their keep.

There was a place where the official smuggler road bent east around a cliff called the Widow's Elbow — a bend so reliable that anyone following them would follow it too, because maps agreed, habit agreed, and the cliff itself made a fairly persuasive argument. But below the Elbow, hidden behind a curtain of hanging birch, there was a path that wasn't on any map, not even Marta's. Yew had found it decades ago and taught it to exactly one student, on the grounds that a secret shared twice was a rumor.

Astrid found the birch curtain by feel, counting paces from a lightning-split boulder shaped, if you squinted, like a sleeping dog. He held the branches aside and whispered the count — forty-one steps down, thirty across the ledge, mind the third step, it lies.

The convoy came through single file, wagons dismantled of their cargo and carried piecewise, the frames themselves lowered by rope. It took two hours. Nobody complained. Smugglers understood that the difference between a hard night and a dead one was usually just patience.

On the far side, the path rejoined the world through a sinkhole meadow where sheep grazed in baffled silence at this parade of nocturnal furniture. Astrid looked back up at the Widow's Elbow and permitted himself one small, private smile. Let Corvane's riders take the long way. Let them spend their night going around the mountain while his family went through its pocket.

He should have known better than to feel clever. Cleverness, Yew always said, was the sound a trap makes just before it closes.

* * *

It happened at the river crossing, an hour before dawn.

The ford was shallow and gravel-bottomed, and they crossed it in good order, wheels hushed with wrapped sacking, Duchess expressing her views on water via her eyes. On the far bank the land rose into a long pine ridge, and Astrid paused at the tree line to do what he'd done all night — listen back along their trail.

That was when he felt it. Not the riders. Something smaller. A wrongness, thin as a hair across a page.

He cast back along the meadow, the ford, the track beyond. And there — faint, but unmistakable to a mind trained by Yew — a trail of attention. Someone had been stopping. Marking. The broken branch wedged in a fencepost at the ford's edge, placed too neatly to be accident. A stone turned pale-side-up on the track, three miles back. A strip of cloth, gray on gray, hung in the birch curtain they'd come through — he remembered brushing past it and thinking nothing, because he'd been busy feeling proud of himself.

Someone was leaving bread crumbs. Not for themselves to find the way home. For the wolves to find them.

Astrid stood very still in the cold, and the cold moved inside him.

Every prediction in the book had come true. Every one. He hadn't dared look for a passage about betrayal — he'd told himself the book couldn't possibly have gotten that specific, that petty, that cruel. But standing in the wet grass with dawn graying the ridgeline, he understood that of course it could. Prophecy didn't deal in grand abstractions. It dealt in nights exactly like this one.

"Astrid." Marta's voice, low. She'd come back for him, which she did sometimes without ever admitting she did it. "You've got the face. The one you had with the bees."

"There's a marker at the ford," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Another one back on the track. Somebody in the convoy is leaving signs for whoever's following us."

The silence between them lasted four heartbeats. He counted them, because counting was better than feeling.

"Marta—"

"I heard you." Her jaw worked. In the half-light her face looked carved rather than lived-in, all planes and stubbornness. "You're sure. Certain-sure. Not tired-sure, not scared-sure."

"Tired and scared," Astrid admitted. "But sure."

She nodded slowly, and what frightened Astrid more than the markers themselves was how unsurprised she looked. Not shocked. Just grimly rearranging a world she'd already half-expected.

"Say nothing," she said. "To nobody. Not yet."

"Marta, if we keep walking, we're walking behind a signpost with our names on it—"

"*I said say nothing.*" It wasn't loud. That was the terrible thing; it never needed to be. "Boy, I've run cargo past crown wardens, tax men, two feuding baronies, and one extremely persistent bear. You know what sinks a crew faster than any of those? Accusing each other in the dark. You point a finger wrong once and this family eats itself before Corvane ever catches us. So we do this my way." She was already turning back toward the wagons. "We change the route. Quietly. We make it look natural. And you and me, we watch everybody, including each other, and we're wrong as long as we need to be until we're right."

"And if we're right about who it is?"

Marta paused. When she answered, her voice had gone somewhere old and tired.

"Then that's tomorrow's grief. Tonight we're still a convoy. Walk."

* * *

They changed the route at the ridge, taking a fork south that added four hours and shed the marked trail entirely. Astrid watched everyone.

He hated it. That was the part nobody warned you about — not the danger of treachery, but the tax it levied on everything else. He watched Old Tam spit off the wagon seat and thought *is that a signal?* He watched Fenric fall asleep sitting up against a wheel and thought *is that exhaustion or theater?* He caught himself studying the way Sefa tied her bootlaces, and had to stop and breathe and feel disgusted with himself, because Sefa was twelve and had once cried over a bee, and if his suspicion had a face like hers then the prophecy had already won.

The crew felt it anyway, the way families feel weather changes they can't name. Conversations got shorter. Jokes landed a beat late. By midmorning, camped in a fold of hills under canvas while the daylight hours burned off their exhaustion, the convoy had gone quiet in the specific way of people listening for something.

It was Tam, of all people, who broke it.

"Right." He sat down heavily by the cold cookfire, pipe unlit, and addressed the group at large with the air of a man opening a ledger. "Somebody's been leaving marks on our backtrail. Don't look shocked, I've got eyes older than most of your opinions. Marta knows. Astrid knows. Which means the rest of you now know, and we can all stop pretending this is a camping trip."

Fenric sat up. "Marks? What kind of marks?"

"Branches. Stones. Little courtesies for whoever's chasing us." Tam's gaze went around the circle, mild and devastating. "Now, I'm not going to guess out loud, because guessing out loud is how crews die. But I'll say this much: whatever's driving whoever did it — fear, coin, a knife at somebody they love — it doesn't change what they did. We're all of us carrying something heavy these days. Question is whether we're carrying it together or spending it."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above, a bird made an accusation and flew off.

Astrid looked around the circle — Tam, Fenric, Sefa asleep against her mother, the others, faces he'd trusted with his life a hundred times on a hundred bad roads — and felt the book press against his chest like a second heartbeat. Somewhere in this circle, the prophecy had already chosen its instrument. Or perhaps, whispered a voice that sounded unpleasantly like Yew, the prophecy hadn't chosen anything at all, and it was simply true that fear makes traitors of ordinary people, and books merely write down what fear was always going to do.

*Prophecy is a hook,* his master had said. *And readers are bait.* He'd thought she meant the words on the page. Now, watching the faces of his family arrange themselves around a suspicion none of them would speak, he wondered if the hook was bigger than the book. Maybe the hook was the future itself, and everyone who knew one was already on the line.

* * *

Dusk. They moved again.

Astrid led them onto the last stretch — a high shelf road above the Grelle valley, narrow enough that the wagons hugged the inner wall and the outer world dropped away into blue dark. Below, far off, torchlight moved along the valley floor. Corvane's riders, organized as a hymn, sweeping the low country they'd cleverly abandoned.

"We're ahead of them," Marta murmured beside him.

"For now."

"For now is the only tense smugglers get." She clapped his shoulder once, hard, which from Marta was equivalent to a sonnet. Then she dropped back to her wagon, and Astrid walked on alone into the wind.

That was when he saw the last marker.

It was fresh — minutes old, sap still wet where a twig had been snapped and wedged into a crack in the shelf road's retaining wall, angled deliberately toward the valley, toward the torches. Placed within the last hundred paces, which meant placed during this march, which meant—

Astrid turned.

Twenty feet back, a figure was adjusting the strap on one of the lead mules' harness, hands moving with the practiced calm of someone doing absolutely nothing suspicious. The hood was up against the wind. The face beneath it, when the figure glanced up and met his eyes, went carefully, perfectly blank — and Astrid knew that blankness, had worn it himself for six years, the invisibility of a person deciding in real time how much of themselves to show.

Neither of them spoke. The wind poured down off the peaks, carrying the smell of snow and, very faintly, salt.

Far below in the valley, the line of torchlight paused. Then, unhurried, terribly, it began to climb.
