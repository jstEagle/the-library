## 6. The Hollow Court

There were three hundred and eleven servants in the Salt Palace, and Tam Broadstairs knew every one of them by name, by habit, and by which of them stole sugar. He had been a servant there since he was nine years old — first carrying water, then carrying linens, and now, at forty-one, carrying the particular burden of being the only man the King's chamber would tolerate.

Not that the King ever said so. The King never said anything.

Tam had stopped counting the years since the last words at some point, the way you stop counting how many stairs there are when you climb them every day of your life. It had been something small, he remembered. Something domestic. The King — younger then, with color still in him and a laugh that could be heard from the orangery — had been eating breakfast and had said, "Tam, this honey is off," or possibly "Tam, this honey is *awful*," and Tam had laughed and promised to speak to the kitchens.

And then the King had gone quiet. Not sulking-quiet, not grieving-quiet. Quiet like a room where someone has just left.

The honey, for the record, had been fine.

* * *

Every morning began the same way, because mornings in the palace were like tides: they came whether you wanted them or not, and they left things on the shore.

At half past five, Tam let himself into the royal apartments with his ring of keys and his bucket of warm water and his heart already braced, the way you brace yourself before opening a cellar door in winter. The antechamber first — dusting the shelves of ceremonial gifts from foreign courts, most of them salt-themed, because foreign courts have a sense of humor and no shame whatsoever. A salt crystal chandelier from the Meridian Isles. A salt-in-glass paperweight from Vhalen, which was just salt in glass with a fancier name. A carved salt duck, gift of the Ambassador of the Lake Countries, which Tam privately considered the ugliest object in the kingdom and kept on the highest shelf so it couldn't hurt anyone.

Then the bedchamber door.

You knocked, even though he never answered. You waited three breaths, even though it changed nothing. Then you went in.

King Aldous the Third sat in the window seat as he always did, facing east, wearing whatever nightshirt Tam had laid out the evening before. His hands rested on his knees. His eyes were open. They tracked movement — they always tracked movement, which was somehow worse than if they hadn't — but nothing behind them answered the door when you knocked.

"Morning, Your Majesty," Tam said, because you talked anyway. That was the first rule of the hollow court, unwritten and unspoken and understood by everyone who lasted more than a week: *you talk to him anyway.* "Damp one today. Fog off the river thick enough to spread on bread."

He worked as he spoke. Warm cloth for the face. Comb through hair that had gone the color of old frost, white shot through with grey like veins in marble. The King was fifty-three and looked ninety, and everyone pretended this was normal, the way everyone pretended the crown wasn't strange.

Ah. The crown.

Here was the thing about the Salt Crown that Tam had learned not to say out loud, because saying it out loud made the other servants look at you with that flat, careful expression that meant *perhaps you should take some air:* the crown moved.

Not dramatically. Not like a thing alive. But if you set it on its stand facing north at night, by morning it faced the door. If you turned it back, it faced the window. It faced whichever direction the King was in, always, the way a sunflower faces the sun, except sunflowers don't do it in the dark and the crown did it in the dark, Tam was sure of it, sure down in the part of himself that didn't sleep well.

And sometimes — this was the part he'd told exactly one person, his wife Bess, over brandy, after which she'd slept with the lamp lit for a month — sometimes the crown *hummed.* A low note, felt in the teeth rather than heard, and the King's jaw would tighten around it, and thin lines of white would creep up from his collar like frost climbing glass.

Tam combed the King's hair and told him about the fog and about the cook's new apprentice who had salted the porridge twice, once by accident and once out of spite, and about the sparrows nesting in the west gutter again, and the King watched the fog with eyes the pale grey of weathered stone, and did not answer, and Tam loved him anyway.

That was the second rule of the hollow court: *you love him anyway.*

* * *

The court had adapted around its empty center the way water adapts around a stone. There were still councils, still banquets, still petitions read aloud in the presence chamber while the King sat on the throne and looked through everyone. The Chancellor ruled in all but name, and the Princess Isolde — sharp-eyed, sharper-tongued, twenty-six and already tired in a way Tam recognized because he saw it in mirrors — signed what needed signing and smiled at what needed smiling at and spent her nights, if the gossip below-stairs was true, in the archive rooms with lamps burning past midnight.

Nobody danced at court anymore. Not really. There were balls, technically — Tam had carried enough trays of champagne through enough masques to know the difference between dancing and people moving their feet while watching each other. The last time anyone had truly danced, properly danced, drunk-and-laughing danced, had been years ago, before the King went quiet, and nobody could quite explain why the music had died with his voice, only that it had, the way birds go silent before weather.

The servants had their own theories, traded in whispers in the linen room where even the walls seemed too polite to listen.

Cook said it was an illness of the mind, poor lamb, and fed him extra honey on principle.

The under-butler, Pell, who was ambitious and therefore stupid, said it was a weakness of blood and that the Princess should be grateful for her regency.

Old Nanny Sef, who had nursed two kings and buried one, said nothing at all, which from Nanny Sef was louder than anything, because Nanny Sef had opinions the way other people had hands.

Tam's own theory he kept to himself. His theory was simpler and worse: the King was still in there. Somewhere. Behind those tracking eyes, behind the tight jaw, somebody was home — and couldn't get to the door.

He based this on small things. On the way the King's hand twitched toward his daughter when she passed, always, every time, a little jerk of the fingers like a man reaching for something just out of grasp. On the way his breathing changed when music played, quickening slightly, as though listening. And once — only once, three winters ago, and Tam had never told a soul — once, as Tam had finished trimming his beard, the King's mouth had moved. No sound. Just a shaping of lips, slow and effortful, like speech in deep water.

Tam had leaned close, heart hammering, and read it as best he could.

It might have been *help.*

It might have been *honey.*

He chose to believe the second one, because the first one would have broken him, and the palace needed at least one unbroken servant, and Tam had appointed himself to the post.

* * *

The morning of the stranger's arrival began like any other, which is to say it began with fog, porridge, and one-sided conversation.

"—and Bess says the cat has taken to sleeping in the flour bin, which is either affection or ambition, and knowing that animal I'd wager ambition—"

The comb snagged. Tam murmured apology, worked it free. The King's hair had been getting coarser lately, drier, as though the life were draining out of it grain by grain, and Tam had started mixing oil into the wash water and telling no one, because if the physicians noticed they'd write a report, and reports led to questions, and questions at the Salt Palace had a way of leading somewhere cold.

It was mid-morning when the visitor came through the servants' gate.

Tam noticed him because noticing strangers was instinct down here. The boy — young man, rather, nineteen maybe twenty, dark-haired, travel-worn, with the specific posture of someone trying very hard to look like they belonged somewhere — came in with the delivery carts, carrying a crate of river fish that Tam happened to know contained no fish, because Tam knew everything that moved through his palace. He wore the grey livery of a courier's assistant, wrong at the collar, right at the cuffs, the sort of disguise assembled by someone clever who had never actually worn a uniform in his life.

Their eyes met across the kitchen yard. The young man had the look of a stray dog deciding whether to bolt, and Tam, who had fed strays all his life, gave him the smallest nod in the world — *I see you, I'm not going to make noise about it* — and went back to his ledger.

Strangers came to palaces. Most of them were harmless. Some of them were interesting.

This one, Tam thought, watching him drift through the great hall with the studied aimlessness of a man following instructions memorized from a map, this one was looking at the throne dais like a man looks at a grave.

* * *

The strange thing about grief in a palace is that it has nowhere to go. In a cottage you can weep at the table and it's just Tuesday. In a palace, sorrow gets scheduled, formalized, embroidered onto cushions. The whole building was built out of somebody's ancient grief — the long galleries, the memorial windows, the tomb-quiet of the east wing — and the living grief, the King's, sat in the middle of it like a stone in a shoe that the entire country had learned to walk on.

Which is why what happened in the gallery that afternoon broke every unwritten rule at once.

Tam found the young man first — lurking near the pillar, hood up, doing a spectacularly bad job of impersonating a servant carrying nothing — and was about to steer him firmly toward the exits with some fiction about deliveries when the sound started.

A sound like sand pouring.

Soft. Continuous. Coming from the throne dais, where the King sat in state for the afternoon petition hour, flanked by Chancellor and guards, while a delegation of wool merchants droned on about tariffs.

The King was crying.

Except he wasn't crying, not properly, because tears are wet and these weren't. They ran from the corners of his eyes in thin white lines, glittering, catching the light from the memorial windows — grains, tiny grains, rolling down his cheeks and dropping from his jaw onto the stone steps of the dais, where they accumulated in little heaps, little white dunes, *piling up*, faster than anything natural, faster than any body could produce anything, and the wool merchants droned on, and the Chancellor stared straight ahead with the rigid face of a man refusing to see, and the guards stared straight ahead with the rigid faces of men who had seen it before.

Tam had seen it before too. Twice. Both times he'd told himself it was a trick of light, and both times he'd swept the salt up afterward with shaking hands and buried it in the garden, because you couldn't put it in the refuse, you simply couldn't, some instincts run deeper than sense.

But this time was worse. This time the King's hands had come up — slowly, terribly, like branches bending under snow — and pressed against his own cheeks, and his mouth was working, shaping words without voice, and his whole frame shook with the effort of something that was either sobbing or fighting, and Tam could not tell which and had never been able to tell which and suspected, with a sick lurch, that it was both.

*He's in there,* Tam thought, with absolute clarity, standing frozen among the petitioners. *He's been in there the whole time. Something is crying through his eyes and it isn't him and he knows, he knows—*

And the young man — the false courier, the stray — had gone pale as the salt itself, and was staring at the dais with an expression Tam recognized, because he'd worn it himself, three winters ago, reading lips in lamplight.

Pity. Horror and pity, in equal measure, and horror losing.

The boy stepped forward. One step, two, hood slipping, before Tam caught his sleeve — firm, practiced, the grip of a man who had steered drunks and diplomats and one memorable escaped bear — and pulled him back into the shadow of the pillar.

"Not here," Tam breathed. "Not ever, here."

"But he's—" The boy's voice cracked. Young. Scared. *Furious*, underneath, which interested Tam enormously. "He's trying to *say* something."

"He's trying to say something every day of his life, lad." Tam glanced at the dais; the Chancellor had risen, smoothly, loudly, drawing all eyes to a dispute about wool duties while two attendants drifted toward the King with cloths and a covered basin, professional as altar boys. The salt was already vanishing, swept, hidden, denied. By tonight it would be as if it never happened. It always was. "And every day, this palace helps him keep it private. That's the arrangement. Nobody asked me whose arrangement."

The boy looked at him — really looked, and there was something in the look, some quality of attention, that made Tam feel briefly, absurdly *seen*, seen down to the ledger of him — and said, quietly:

"You sweep it up. Don't you. After."

Tam said nothing, which was an answer.

"I need to see the King," the boy said. "Alone. Before the frost comes."

"The frost?"

But the boy's gaze had gone past him, up to the high windows, where the afternoon light had turned strange — thin, white, faintly glittering, as if the air itself had begun, very gently, to remember it was made of salt.

Down on the dais, unnoticed by all but Tam and the thief-hearted stranger, the Salt Crown turned. Slowly, on the King's bowed head, it turned — until it faced the boy in the shadows directly.

And hummed.
