## 7. Lessons in Being Bait

The capital smelled like a hundred things at once — tallow smoke and river mud, roasting chestnuts and the sharp mineral tang that clung to everything near the palace district, as though the Salt King's grief had soaked so deep into the stones that even the gutters wept brine.

Astrid hated it immediately, which was how he knew he'd feel at home eventually.

He'd come down from the Thornfells with a false name stitched into his collar and Marta's warning ringing in his ears: *Don't be clever, boy. Be dull. Clever gets people hanged.* He'd been careful. He'd been invisible, or he'd thought he was — until the waystation, until the book, until the night his one talent failed him like a lamp running out of oil. Now witch-finders were reading faces in every market crowd, comparing them against a description in a confiscated ledger: *young man, dark-skinned, unremarkable except when you look directly at him, at which point he becomes difficult to see.*

It was, Astrid reflected, a deeply unfair way to describe a person. It made him sound like a magic trick gone slightly wrong. Which, to be fair, was more or less what he was.

So he did what Yew had taught him to do, years ago, in a different life: he went down. Down past the honest streets, past the dishonest streets, down the crooked stair behind the tannery where the smell got so bad it circled back around to being useful — nobody searched for anyone in a place that smelled like that — and out into the undercity, the old drowned quarter where the river had changed its mind a century ago and left half a town standing empty beneath the new bridges.

Lamplighters didn't come here. The crown pretended it didn't exist. Which meant everyone who actually ran the city existed here instead: debt-brokers and fence-menders, a woman who sold pigeons by the rumor, three separate men claiming to be the same dead forger. And somewhere in the wet dark of it, if half the whispers were true, a teacher of hidden things who had vanished from the Thornfell roads two winters ago and left nothing behind but a cold hearth and a note that said *gone fishing.*

Astrid had spent two days asking after her in the way you asked after dangerous people — obliquely, politely, never using her real name. He'd bought drinks he couldn't afford. He'd listened to a man describe a witch who lived underwater and charged by the question, and he'd nodded along even though he was fairly sure the man was describing a pike.

On the third evening, a child tugged his sleeve outside a chandlery — a girl of maybe nine, selling matches, with the flat unblinking stare of someone who had been paid not to blink.

"You're looking for the fisherwoman," she said.

"I'm looking for—" Astrid began, and stopped, because lying to children was a sin even smugglers kept. "Yes."

"She says you eat too fast." The girl held out her hand. Astrid put a coin in it, feeling like a man paying a toll to a very small troll. "Pier nine. The one that fell down. She says don't knock, just hum."

"Hum?"

"Anything. She likes sea songs but she'll settle for badly whistled hymns." The girl shrugged with the weariness of a much older woman. "She says humming means your hands are empty. Mostly."

*Mostly.* That word again. His whole life lately was built on mostly.

* * *

Pier nine had fallen down in some forgotten flood and been left where it lay, a skeleton of black timber jutting into the green water like the ribcage of something enormous and patient. At its end, half-sunk, stood a boathouse leaning at an angle that violated several principles of architecture and possibly gravity itself. A single lantern burned in the window, the flame a strange color — pale, salt-white, steady as a held breath.

Astrid stood at the broken edge of the pier and hummed.

He chose a smuggling ballad about a girl who robbed a tax collector and married his horse, which was not technically a sea song, but it had waves in it, metaphorically. He got through two verses before the door of the boathouse creaked open and a voice came out across the water, dry as old paper and twice as sharp:

"Verse three is better. Come in, then. Mind the third plank. It lies."

He crossed carefully, testing each board, and found the third plank indeed tilted treacherously over open water, and understood that this was Yew's idea of a greeting card.

Inside, the boathouse was warmer than it had any right to be. Books everywhere — stacked, wedged, propping up a table that was itself propping up a kettle. Dried herbs hanging from the beams. A cot. A chair. Two cups already set out, which meant she'd known he was coming since at least this morning, possibly since the day he was born, which was the sort of thing Yew would do and then refuse to explain.

And there she was. Smaller than he remembered, or maybe he'd grown. Grey now in her braid where there had only been threads of it. Same ink-stained fingers. Same expression — the one that looked like she was deciding whether to be pleased to see you or disappointed, and had settled, provisionally, on both.

"Astrid Oyelaran," she said, "runaway apprentice, convoy guide, and according to a book older than my grandmother's grave, the doom of kings."

"You've heard."

"I've heard everything. I live under a city; the city tells me things." She poured tea from the kettle — bitter, smoky, faintly salty, which should have been wrong and somehow wasn't. "Sit down before you fall down. You look like a rope that's been walked on."

"It's good to see you," he said, and meant it so hard it came out crooked.

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, quickly buried. "Is it? Last I saw you, you were stealing my good knife and leaving a note that said *sorry, needed it more.*"

"You said yourself I'd need it more."

"I say many things. Most of them are tests." She sat, folded her hands, and looked at him the way she used to look at a page she suspected of lying. "You went back to the roads. After everything I taught you about staying small, you went and became a legend. There's a song about you in the passes. It's a bad song. The tune doesn't match the words."

"There's a song about me?"

"There's a song about a ghost-boy who leads caravans through the fog and charges bandits double to be haunted by him. The convoy invented it to drum up business. Marta sang four verses of it to a tax inspector once and got the whole shipment waved through." She smiled, briefly, genuinely, and Astrid felt the old warmth of her — the warmth that had always sat strangely close to her edges, like fire wrapped in wet wool. "Marta Voss could sell a drowning man a swimming lesson. Of course she could sell you."

Then the smile shut off like a tapped keg.

"Now. The book."

Astrid pulled it from inside his coat — wrapped in oilcloth, guarded like a heart. The prophecy book from the waystation. Century-old leather, pages soft as cloth, and on the first page, in faded brown ink, a date. Tonight's date, a hundred years early. And a name. *Astrid Oyelaran, called the Unmaker, who comes out of the high passes in the season of white frost.*

He set it on the table between them and watched her face while she read. He'd had a week to get used to the horror of it. He wanted to see it land on someone else, someone wise, someone who would tell him it was nonsense.

Yew read for a long time. Then she turned to the margins.

That was the part Astrid hadn't shown anyone. The margins were full of writing — cramped, slanted notes in newer ink, commenting on the prophecy like a scholar annotating scripture. *This line is mistranslated.* *The binder's name has been scraped away here — look at the scratch marks.* *Whoever bound the Brine last time used a crown because crowns hold power; any vessel large enough would do.* Page after page of them, in handwriting that looped and hooked in a way Astrid had seen somewhere before and couldn't place, the way you can't place a smell until suddenly you can.

"Do you know whose hand this is?" Yew asked quietly.

"No. That's why I came to you. I thought — you know old books. Old hands."

"Mm." She turned another page. Another note. *Do not let them make you a vessel alone. Alone, the cage becomes the keeper.* And beside the final prophecy, the one about the unmaker taking the Brine's place: *There is another reading. There is always another reading. Look for who else stands in the room.*

"You wrote these," Astrid said.

It came out flat, not accusatory. He was too tired for accusation. But the floor of his stomach dropped anyway, because he'd carried this book through witch-finder country believing it was a weapon pointed at him by strangers, and it turned out the strangers had help.

Yew didn't flinch. "I wrote some of them," she said. "Two winters ago. Before I left the passes."

"Why?"

"Because I read the book. Properly, all the way through, which almost no one does — prophecy books are like contracts; everyone reads the exciting parts and skips the clauses." She tapped the scratched-out name on the third page. "Someone bound the Brine a century ago and then erased themselves out of the record. Do you understand what kind of fear that takes? To win, and to be so afraid of what winning costs that you scrape your own name out of history?" Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't. "I started writing in the margins because I was angry. And then because I was frightened. And then I realized the book had started doing something to me — I'd read it six times and I was beginning to arrange my life around it. So I left. Gone fishing. You don't stop reading a hook by holding it tighter."

"But you kept a copy of the notes."

"I kept the notes because someday someone was going to find that book, and I wanted them to find a friend in the margins." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "I just didn't think it would be you. I hoped it wouldn't be you. I liked you, Astrid. Liking people and prophecy don't mix."

"The book names me."

"The book names whoever reads it, eventually. That's the trick of prophecy. It's a net woven to fit whatever fish swims in." She set down her cup. "But this one — this one fits you well enough that I'm not going to insult us both by pretending otherwise. The date matched. The borderwarden died the way it said. You're here, aren't you? In the drowned quarter, drinking my terrible tea, exactly where a certain reading of page twelve says you'd be."

Astrid felt the vertigo rise again, the same sick lurch from the waystation. "Then it's true. I'm the unmaker. I'm supposed to—"

"Stop." She said it softly, which from Yew was louder than shouting. "Lesson one, and we are starting lessons tonight whether you like it or not, because you have the survival instincts of a moth and roughly the same relationship with bright lights. Lesson one: a prophecy is not a prediction. It is an *offer*. Written by someone who wants a particular future, phrased so it sounds like fate, delivered so it finds the right reader. Prophecy is bait. The question is never *is it true.* The question is *who tied the fly, and what are they fishing for?*"

"And who tied this one?"

She paused. Sipped. Looked at the wall.

"Yew."

"Astrid." She put the cup down with great care, aligning the handle with the table edge, buying herself a moment in porcelain. "Some questions cost more to answer than others. Some answers, given at the wrong time, become the very thing they describe. If I tell you, tonight, what the last page of that book costs — if I say it out loud, in this room, to your face — then you will spend every hour between now and the ending trying to avoid it, and in trying to avoid it you will walk straight into it, because that is how bindings work. They close around the shape of your resistance." She spread her hands flat on the table, palms down, pinning the book between them like a specimen. "So no. Not yet. When you need to know, you will know, and not one hour sooner, and you will hate me for it, and you'll be right to."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Fairness is for markets. This is magic."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue. And then he remembered the possessed hound story — the one she'd told him years ago, casually, as a cautionary tale, never explaining why she knew so much about what happened to binders who learned too much too fast — and he closed his mouth again. Yew's evasions had never been cowardice. They'd been load-bearing.

"All right," he said. "Teach me, then. Whatever you can teach me without telling me."

"Good lad." She cleared a space on the table, sweeping three books onto the floor without ceremony. "Salt first. Everything begins with salt. Do you know why?"

"Because... it preserves?"

"Because it remembers." She produced a shallow dish of coarse grey salt from somewhere among the clutter. "Salt remembers being the sea. Every grain is a memory that refused to dissolve. That's why it holds bindings, why it holds ghosts, why it holds grief — the Salt King hasn't spoken in years, but the whole capital tastes salt on the wind, because his sorrow won't dissolve either. Power in this world is memory under pressure. Bindings are memories that agree to stay put." She pushed the dish toward him. "Put your finger in it. Tell me what you taste."

"Salty."

"Wrong. Try again. Slower. What *kind* of salty?"

He dipped his finger properly this time, closed his eyes, and paid attention the way she'd taught him to pay attention to a lockpick — not with force, with patience. Under the salt: something mineral and vast and old. Cold. A depth-pressure feeling, like the deep end of a lake. And underneath that, faintly, horribly, a hunger. Something in the salt was *wanting*, reaching, tasting him back.

"Oh," he said.

"Yes." She withdrew the dish with alacrity. "That was a binding-grade salt I bought from a widow whose husband drowned thirty years ago. There's a trace of the Brine in it — there's a trace of the Brine in everything coastal, everything that ever loved the sea too much. It tasted you, Astrid. It knows your flavor now. Congratulations. You've been introduced."

"Should I be worried?"

"Immensely. But later." She was already moving on, pulling a stub of chalk from her braid, drawing a circle on the tabletop. "Lesson two: names. A binding is a contract between a name and a container. The Brine eats kings because a king is the largest named object in any room — a whole kingdom's worth of memory and power, packed into one title. Crowns are containers. Thrones are cages with cushions. Whoever bound it last time gave it a king to live in, and the kingdom has been paying the rent ever since, in salt."

"So the King—"

"Is a prisoner wearing his own body. Yes. And the Brine wears him. And the court salutes the Brine. And the witch-finders call it peace." Her chalk paused mid-circle. "You see why I don't sleep much."

They worked until the lantern burned low and the tide under the pier turned. He learned to draw a circle that actually held — hers were clean; his wobbled like a drunk's signature until she made him draw forty of them, and he complained, and she said *good, complaining means you're taking it seriously.* He learned that his invisibility wasn't a talent at all, really — it was a binding he'd done to himself as a child without knowing, a small contract, his name traded for absence, and that it had frayed the night he read the book aloud because the book had *renegotiated.*

"You bound yourself once already," Yew said, with something between pride and pity. "At eight years old, alone, terrified, no teacher. Do you understand how rare that is? Most mages study twenty years to botch what you improvised." She looked at him across the guttering light. "Which is exactly why they'll want you. All of them. The Brine, the crown, the witch-finders, the prophecy itself. Everyone in this story is fishing, Astrid, and you are the best bait on the river."

"Charming."

"It's the highest compliment I know how to pay. Bait survives by being *wanted* and *unreachable* at the same time." She banked the lantern. "Sleep. Tomorrow we start on debts, which is the third lesson and the one that kills most students, so try to be a disappointment."

* * *

He slept on the cot under a quilt that smelled of cedar and old smoke, and dreamed, and the dream was salt.

In the morning she fed him smoked eel and hard bread and made him draw circles until his wrist ached, and taught him the shape of debts — how every binding is a loan, how power borrowed must be repaid with memory, with time, with name. She showed him how the traitor-hounds of the witch-finders were bound animals, debts walking on four legs, and how to read the terms of a binding by touch, the way a blind man reads a coin.

And all day, in the corners of the lessons, she avoided one thing with the grace of a dancer avoiding a spilled drink. Whenever he edged toward the last page — whenever he asked what the unmaker *becomes*, what the vessel pays, what the erased name on the third page had lost — she steered away. Not clumsily. Beautifully. With jokes, with tea, with a sudden fascinating digression about the pigeon-rumor woman. He caught himself admiring it even as it maddened him.

On the second evening he tried a different approach. He waited until she was comfortable, tea in hand, feet on a crate, and he said, gently:

"You wrote in the margins because you were frightened of what the book would do to you. What did you see, Yew? When you read it all the way through?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Long enough that the lantern flickered twice.

"I saw myself," she said finally, "in a room I haven't entered yet. Holding a door I haven't reached. And I saw that I stay." She swallowed. "That's all you get. That's all I get, some nights."

"And the last page?"

"Ask me again when you can hold a circle in a gale," she said, "and mean it."

He laughed — short, startled, real. She laughed too, and for a moment the boathouse was warm in the ordinary way, the old way, two people who had missed each other sitting in the smell of eel-smoke and ink, and the prophecy could wait, and the Brine could wait, and the witch-finders could circle the city above them all night long and never find the drowned quarter where a runaway and his teacher were eating hard bread and telling bad jokes about tax collectors.

Then footsteps crossed the fallen pier. Careful ones. Testing planks.

Astrid heard them before Yew did — his frayed invisibility had left his hearing suspiciously good — and he turned, and the lantern-light showed him a figure coming down the black timbers: broad-shouldered, hooded, moving like someone who knew the route but not quite well enough.

"Yew," he said quietly. "Someone's coming."

"I know," she said, and did not seem alarmed, which alarmed him. "That's Ferren. My other student. She brings news from above." A pause, and then, in a tone that made the hair on his arms stand up like startled cats: "And it seems she's brought more than news. Astrid — how many people know you're in the capital?"

"Marta. Isolde. You."

"Then we have a leak," Yew said, "or a tail. Because Ferren's face says the witch-finders found something tonight. Something with a name on it."

She snuffed the lantern with two fingers, unhurried, and in the sudden dark her voice was calm as deep water.

"Lesson three starts early, boy. Tonight you learn what your debts cost other people."
