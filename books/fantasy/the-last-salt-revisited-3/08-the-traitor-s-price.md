## 8. The Traitor's Price

The mark on the doorpost was a fishhook, drawn in tallow soot.

Sera Voss had invented it herself, three nights ago, standing outside the Gilded Eel with her heart hammering like a debtor at the door. A fishhook meant: *the boy sleeps here.* A fishhook crossed with a line meant: *he moves tomorrow.* She had never imagined she'd need a third sign, but tonight, pressing her thumb into the cold soot beneath the hook, she drew one: two short strokes, like gills.

*They're closing in.*

She pulled her hand back and looked at it. Soot to the second knuckle. Somewhere in the dark behind her, the capital breathed and muttered and counted its coins, indifferent as the sea.

"You'll wash that before you come home," said a voice in her memory — her mother's voice, dead eleven years now, still the strictest person she knew. "You'll wash your hands, Sera, or everything you touch turns to what you are."

Her mother had been talking about lying, mostly. But it worked for treason too.

* * *

Three weeks earlier, they had come for her in a tea shop.

That was the thing about witch-finders nobody warned you about: not the chains, not the white cloaks, not even the ledger Corvane carried under his arm like a hymnal. It was the politeness. Witch-Finder Corvane had sat down across from her at a table by the window, ordered two cups of smoked tea without asking what she drank, and said:

"Sera Voss. Rigger, Marta Kell's crew, nine years. Widow of Tam Voss, lost on the Saltmere run. Mother of one."

Every word landed like a coin placed precisely on a scale.

"My daughter," Sera said, because there was no use pretending otherwise, "is seven."

"Nettie," Corvane agreed. "Bright girl. Draws on walls." He turned his cup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the table edge, and something about the smallness of the gesture frightened Sera more than any threat could have. This was a man who straightened things. Who believed the world could be set right if only everyone held their handles at the proper angle. "She is currently in the care of the Order's foundling house in Greyharbour. Excellent porridge. Terrible hymns. She'd be released to your custody immediately upon conclusion of certain matters."

"What matters?"

Corvane opened his ledger. He did not show her the pages; he simply rested his hand on them, the way a man rests his hand on a sleeping dog. "There is a boy with Marta Kell's convoy. Young. Unremarkable. Good at not being noticed — which is itself remarkable, when you think on it. I want to know where he sleeps. Where he goes. Whom he trusts." He looked up, and his eyes were not cruel. That was the horror of them. They were patient, and clean, and absolutely certain. "I'm not asking you to harm him, Mistress Voss. I'm asking you to be accurate."

"And if I say no?"

"Then in six years, Nettie ages out of the foundling rolls and enters crown service. Laundry, likely. Or worse, if her aptitude testing runs... interesting." He rose, settled his cloak, aligned his chair with the table edge out of what seemed to be pure compulsion. "I don't believe you'll say no. Mothers are the most reliable instrument I know. Better than chains. Chains rust."

She had thrown her tea at him. He had paid for the cup, left exact change plus tip, and been waiting outside when she finished crying in the privy.

* * *

Now, three weeks and four marks later, Sera climbed the stairs to her rented room above the chandler's shop and let herself fall onto the bed without unlacing her boots.

The ceiling had a water stain shaped like a fat bird. Nettie would have named it. Nettie named everything — the stain, the cat behind the fish market, the one-eyed gull that stole her bread every Tuesday. When Sera closed her eyes she could see the whole litany: *that's Admiral Splashes, Mam, he only robs rich people.*

"He's a gull, love. They're all criminals."

Guilt was a strange cargo, Sera thought, staring at the fat-bird stain. It didn't weigh anything until you tried to put it down. Then it weighed everything.

Because here was the truth of it, laid out plain as contraband on a customs table: the boy was kind. That was the worst part. If Astrid had been arrogant, or cruel, or even just ordinary-stupid the way most apprentices were, it would have been easy. But he'd sat with her through a night storm on the Saltmere road holding her lantern steady while she re-rigged a torn line, and when her hands shook too badly to knot it, he'd done it for her without a word, without looking at her, without making it a debt. He had that way about him — the invisibility, Yew called it, though Sera suspected half of it wasn't magic at all. Half of it was just a boy who'd learned early that being unnoticed kept you fed.

He trusted her. He'd told her once, laughing over bad stew, that the convoy was the first place he'd ever slept without one ear open.

And she had sold his sleep for a bowl of foundling-house porridge.

"No," she said aloud to the ceiling bird. "Don't dress it up. You didn't sell his sleep. You sold his throat."

The ceiling bird declined to argue.

She should have gone to Marta. That was the thought that came every night, punctual as the harbor bell. Marta Kell, who took in strays the way other people took in cold — who'd buried two husbands and a son and still laughed louder than anyone at her own jokes. Marta would know what to do. Marta always knew what to do, even when what she knew was terrible.

But Marta couldn't fix this. Nobody could fix this. Because Corvane hadn't asked for the boy dead or alive or delivered. He'd asked for accuracy, and accuracy was already given, and somewhere in Greyharbour a seven-year-old was drawing on walls while her mother drew fishhooks in soot, and between those two facts stretched a distance no smuggler road could cross.

Sera rolled over, and her elbow hit something on the bedside table.

A parcel. Small, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the particular double knot that meant *Marta's crew, handle with care and also curiosity.*

She hadn't seen anyone come up the stairs.

Her blood went cold and then hot in the space of one heartbeat. She sat up, checked the window (latched), the wardrobe (empty), the space under the bed (a mouse, unaffiliated). Then, slowly, she unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside lay a strip of dried fish, an apple with a bite taken out of it, and a note written in a hand she knew too well — the careful, slightly cramped letters of someone who'd learned to write in secret and never quite believed he was allowed.

*Sera. I know about the marks. The fishhook is good work — Ferren nearly missed it, and she misses nothing. Meet me at the old rope-walk at moonrise. Come alone. Bring the apple back if you don't want it, it was getting soft anyway.*

*Astrid*

Underneath, smaller, almost shy:

*I'm not angry. Please come anyway.*

* * *

The old rope-walk was a long roofless corridor where they'd once stretched hemp from wall to wall, and now it stood empty except for pigeons, moonlight, and one slim figure sitting on a bollard with the studied casualness of someone who had rehearsed sitting casually.

"You followed me," Sera said.

"For four days." Astrid hopped off the bollard. In the moonlight he looked very young and very tired and not at all like the unmaker of kings. He looked like a boy who'd found a fishhook on his doorpost and spent a night learning what it meant. "Yew taught me the countersigns. Lesson two: *the trail you leave is the trail you eat.* Which sounds profound but mostly means check behind yourself."

"I'm sorry—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Please. Let me do this in order, because I've been practicing it in my head for a day and a half and if you interrupt I'll lose my place and start apologizing instead, which is apparently something I inherited from nobody, since I never met my parents."

Sera shut her mouth.

"First," Astrid said. "I'm not going to hurt you, and I'm not going to tell Marta, and neither is Yew, and neither will Isolde once I explain it to her, which I haven't yet, so that's a promise I'm making on credit. Second — I need to hear it from you. Not from a ledger. From you. Why."

And because he asked plainly, and because he had brought her an apple, and because three weeks of carrying it alone had worn her thinner than any rope ever had, Sera told him. All of it. The tea shop. The aligned cup handle. The foundling house with its excellent porridge and terrible hymns. Nettie drawing Admiral Splashes on the wall of a room with grey sheets, and Corvane's clean, patient certainty that mothers were more reliable than chains.

When she finished, the pigeons had all flown off, offended by something, possibly the silence.

"So sell me," Sera said finally. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Turn me in to Marta. Cut me loose in some port town. I'd deserve it. I marked your door for men who burn books and the people who read them, and I did it for—"

"For your daughter," Astrid said. "You did it for Nettie."

"That's not an excuse."

"It's not supposed to be." He was quiet a moment. Moonlight made silver seams of the old rope-grooves in the stones. "My master — Yew — has this list of laws. Lesson one is *every binding is a debt.* I used to think that meant debts were traps. Debts are how they get hooks in you." He looked up. "But I think maybe it means the opposite, too. That being bound to people is the only thing keeping any of us from coming apart. And the Brine — the thing in the prophecy, the thing that eats kings — it doesn't understand that. It only knows how to take. It can't imagine choosing to owe someone."

"That's very pretty," Sera said, "for a boy about to tell me why he isn't furious."

"Oh, I'm furious." He smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked and exhausted. "I've been furious for a day and a half. At Corvane, mostly. A little at myself, because I liked you, which means I stopped checking behind myself, which means lesson two exists specifically to describe me. And a little bit at the world for building a place where a witch-finder can hold a seven-year-old like a pawn and call it order." He stepped closer. "But here's the thing, Sera. Here's what I keep arriving at no matter how many times I walk around it. You didn't betray us because you wanted to. You betrayed us because someone built a trap where every road out cost somebody. And I have recently learned — it's in a book, it's a whole book, I hate the book — that I'm going to be facing a lot of roads like that. So I'd like to practice. Starting with this one."

"Practice what?" Sera said warily. "Forgiveness? I haven't asked for it."

"Not forgiveness." His eyes caught the moon. "Logistics. Tell me about Greyharbour. The foundling house. Guards, walls, rotations, where they keep the children at night, whether Nettie has a window room. Everything."

Sera stared at him. "You can't be serious. You're a runaway apprentice with one spell and a price on your head."

"I'm a runaway apprentice with one spell, a price on my head, a smuggler family that owns every tunnel on the coast, a princess inside the palace, and a witch who owes me lessons." He counted them off on his fingers, then seemed briefly startled by how many fingers that was. "Also Marta, once I convince her — and I will, though she'll shout, and possibly throw something. Probably something heavy. We get the child out, Sera. Not traded. Not bargained. Out. And then Corvane's hook comes out of you, and you stop drawing fish on doors forever."

For a long moment Sera Voss, rigger, widow, traitor, could not speak. Three weeks of guilt sat in her chest like swallowed ballast, and this boy — this invisible, impossible boy — had just offered to help her jettison it, free of charge, no debt recorded.

"You understand," she managed, "that if this goes wrong, it won't be you in the cells. It'll be me. And her."

"I know." He said it simply, without flinching, and she understood suddenly why Marta had taken him in, why Yew had come out of hiding for him, why a princess was poisoning salt for him in a hollow court. It wasn't the prophecy. Prophecies were cheap; every market stall had one. It was that when he said *I know,* he actually did — he priced the risk honestly and chose anyway.

"Then we do it properly," Sera said, and heard her own spine come back into her voice, the old rigging-taut certainty of a woman who had once walked a swaying line between masts in a gale because the alternative was losing the sail. "Greyharbour's a wet dock town. Foundling house sits on the old customs quay. There's a coal chute on the north side the children use to sneak out and watch the ships — Nettie's mentioned it twice in her letters, which the wardens censor, badly." A plan was assembling itself in her head, plank by plank, and it felt so much better than soot on her fingers. "We'll need tide tables. And someone small enough for the chute. And—"

"A distraction," said a voice from the shadows of the rope-walk.

Sera spun. Astrid sighed the sigh of a boy whose evening was not going according to rehearsal.

Ferren stepped into the moonlight — sharp-faced, gray-cloaked, arms crossed — and beside her, filling the rope-walk doorway entirely, came a broad shape in a sea coat that Sera recognized the way you recognize a storm front.

"Marta," Astrid said weakly. "How long have you—"

"Long enough." Marta Kell advanced down the rope-walk like weather. Her eyes fixed on Sera, and Sera braced for fury, for the cut rope, for the long fall.

Instead Marta stopped in front of her, reached out, and took Sera's chin in one huge hand, tilting her face up to the moonlight the way you'd inspect a hull for rot.

"They have my Nettie?" Sera whispered.

"They have *our* Nettie," Marta said. "Girl draws on my charts every time we dock at Greyharbour. Calls the Kraken Strait 'Mr. Squiddles.'" Her grip tightened, gentle and iron both. "You should have told me. Whatever he held over you — you should have come to me, and we'd have burned his tea shop down around his tidy little cup handles."

"I know," Sera said. "I know."

"Well." Marta released her, turned to Astrid, and cracked her knuckles with the sound of a ship coming about. "No help for it now. Boy, you said we'd need tide tables and a distraction. Lucky for you, I happen to own a smuggling crew, a grudge, and a cargo of very illegal fireworks currently disguised as pickled herring."

"Ferren told her," Astrid muttered.

"Ferren tells me everything," Marta said. "Lesson two, boy. Check *in front* of yourself too."

And high above them, on the ruined rope-walk wall where the moonlight fell, Sera saw — or thought she saw, though she'd swear to it later — grains of salt settling out of the air, tracing a line of writing none of them had made:

*The cage opens from the inside. Hurry.*

Astrid went very still, staring at it.

"That's new," he said quietly. "That's not Yew's hand."

Below in the city, a bell began to ring — not the harbor bell, not any hour. The alarm bell at the Order's gate, thin and urgent, rolling across the rooftops toward Greyharbor.

Toward the foundling house.

"They're moving her," Sera breathed. "Tonight. He knows I'm burned — he's cutting his losses—"

Marta was already moving, hauling both of them by the collar like wayward cargo. "Then we move faster. Ferren! Wake the crew! Everyone rides tonight!"

As they ran, Astrid fell in beside Sera, breathless, and gave her a wild, terrified, luminous grin.

"Lesson four," he said. "*Some debts you pay by breaking things.*"

Behind them, in the empty rope-walk, the salt words hung gleaming a moment longer — and then, grain by grain, began to fall.
