## 9. Yew's First Law

Yew's first law was this: *everything owes something.*

She said it on my first morning in her cellar beneath a tannery that hadn't operated since before I was born, while she measured out grains of salt onto a scarred table like a woman counting coins she didn't trust.

"You were taught to vanish," she said. "Invisibility is just a debt unpaid. The world stops noticing you because you stop owing it attention. Elegant trick. Cowardly, but elegant." She pushed the salt line toward me. "Binding is the opposite. Binding is *insisting*."

"Insisting what?"

"That things stay where you put them." She looked up at me, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw the woman who had trained me to hide, ten years ago, when I was small enough to fit inside a flour sack and frightened enough to want to. "Salt remembers debts. Names are the ledger. And power—" she tapped the table, "—power is whoever holds the biggest purse."

I wrote it down, because writing things down was how Yew had taught me to survive lessons. Then I underlined it twice, which was how I taught myself to survive being afraid.

* * *

The cellar smelled of oak bark and old lime, and it was cold in the way cellars are cold — not cruelly, but thoroughly, like an honest opinion. Yew had strung it with drying racks now hung not with hides but with jars: salt of seven colors, chalk ground fine, thread pulled from funerary shrouds ("don't ask whose"), and a small clay pot labeled, in her crabbed hand, **DO NOT**.

I asked about the pot every day.

Every day she said, "Good instinct. Keep asking."

The lessons began at dawn and ended when my hands shook too badly to continue, which by the third day was noon, and by the fifth was barely mid-morning. Binding, it turned out, was not dramatic. It was bookkeeping. You named the thing. You named what it owed. You named what would be paid if it broke terms. Salt held the agreement the way wax holds a seal — inert until pressed, then stubborn as sin.

"Say the hound's name," Yew instructed.

"It doesn't have one."

"Everything has one. It just hasn't been told yet. That's your job." She nodded at the corner of the cellar, where a dog lay curled — a big brindled thing, gray-muzzled, breathing too slowly. Too evenly. Like a bellows worked by someone patient. "That animal died three weeks ago in a ditch outside Marrowgate. Something moved in. Something hungry but small. Call it by what it wants to be called."

"And if it doesn't want to be called anything?"

"Then you've learned something too." She settled onto her stool with the air of a woman preparing to enjoy a long afternoon of my failures. "Go on, apprentice. Introduce yourself."

I crouched near the hound. Up close its fur was wrong — not matted, but *settled*, lying flat in patterns no wind had made, like water after something large passes beneath. Its eyes tracked me without its head moving.

"Hound," I said.

Its lip curled.

"No," Yew said from behind me, dry as the lime on the walls. "That's what it is. Try again. What does it think it is?"

I thought about the Brine wearing a king. A hunger that dressed itself in whatever container it found. This thing in the dog was the same appetite, only smaller — a minnow beside the leviathan, but made of the same water.

"You're the dog," I said quietly. "You're still the dog. Whatever else got in, it's borrowing. The teeth are yours. The breath is yours." I reached out — stupidly, Yew later said, though she also said it approvingly, which from her was the same thing — and laid my palm on its flank. Warm. Thank all the quiet gods. "So I'm calling *the dog*. Come back and collect yourself."

The hound shuddered. The evenness of its breathing broke — stuttered — and for one heartbeat there was a sound underneath the sound, a wet whispering like tide through gravel, and something in the room turned its attention from the dog to *me*.

It looked at me. There is no other word. Nothing moved, nothing changed, and yet I felt seen the way a coin feels seen by a hand reaching into a pocket.

Then the hound barked. A real bark, rusty and indignant, and rolled onto its feet, and scratched itself, and was — as far as any dog ever is — entirely a dog again.

Yew let out a breath. "Huh," she said. "Faster than I did it."

"How long did it take you?"

"Four days and most of my left eyebrow." She touched the brow in question, which had never grown back properly. "Don't preen. You caught it sleepy. The little ones sleep between feedings. Now—" she tossed me the jar of white salt, "—do the seal anyway. Unbinding isn't done until the door is locked behind it."

* * *

The days found their shape. Dawn: salt-work, lines and circles, learning how many grains made a promise strong enough to keep and how many made it brittle enough to break. Midmorning: names, which Yew taught like a language with no dictionary. "Names aren't labels," she said. "A label sits on top. A name goes *down*. To know a thing's true name is to hold its mortgage." Afternoon: debts.

Debts were the part that unsettled me, because debts turned out to be everywhere once you knew to look. The chair owed the tree. The tree owed the soil and the rain and, in some long chain I couldn't follow, the sun. Every binding Yew showed me was, at bottom, a renegotiation: *you owe the world your shape; here are new terms.*

"Why does the Brine eat kings?" I asked one evening, over stew Yew made from smuggled eels and stolen leeks. "Marta's crew kept asking. If it's hunger, why not eat anything? Why climb all the way to the capital and sit in one man for a century?"

Yew chewed thoughtfully. "Why does a thief rob banks?"

"Because that's where the money is."

"There's your answer, then. Crowns are the largest containers of power in the land." She set down her spoon. "Think about it. Every oath sworn to a king flows into him. Every law signed, every tax collected, every soldier's loyalty, every prayer said in his name at his priests' insistence — it all pools. A crown is a reservoir with a man-shaped dam holding it back. Most men can't hold much. That's why they're ordinary. But put a hungry thing inside a full reservoir..." She shrugged. "It doesn't have to hunt. It just drinks."

"The King," I said. "He's been drinking for a hundred years."

"He's been *being drunk* for a hundred years. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and we'll get there when you're ready." Her voice gentled in a way I distrusted immediately. "Ask me tomorrow."

I asked tomorrow. She said, "When you can hold a circle through a night without sleeping."

So I held circles. My hands learned the grammar of salt the way a sailor's learn rope. And every night I sat in a ring of my own making and felt the cellar press against it — felt the world notice me, insistently, the opposite of invisible — and somewhere in the small hours of the fourth night I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what Yew wasn't saying.

If crowns were reservoirs, then the prophecy's *unmaker* — the one meant to pour the Brine out of its cup — would need a cup of his own. And the book had named me a century early, which meant someone had counted me as a vessel worth waiting for.

I didn't say any of this aloud. But Yew watched me across the stew pot that night with her sharp black eyes, and I think she read it off my face like marginalia.

* * *

The hound — who I had started calling Bracken, because he objected to nothing and ate everything — became our practice partner, to his visible resignation. The thing inside him had gone, but the shape of its passage remained, a groove worn in him like a cart-track, and Yew showed me how to find such grooves in anything. "Possession leaves seams. So do curses, so do bargains, so do old griefs. Half of all binding is finding the seam. The other half is having the nerve to put your finger in it."

On the ninth day, she decided I'd earned the nerve.

"We're going to practice unbinding," she said. "Taking apart an existing binding without killing what it holds together. It's delicate work. It's the work, actually — everything else is preparation." She looked at Bracken, who looked back with the weary dignity of a professional. "There's a residue-binding in him still. The thing that wore him stitched itself in a little on the way out, the way a tick leaves its head. We're going to take the stitches out."

"What happens if I do it wrong?"

"Then we'll both learn something memorable."

"Yew."

"Astrid." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "You want the truth? Every binder who ever lived has done it wrong at least once. The ones who survived are the ones who did it wrong in a cellar with a teacher watching. Consider this a kindness."

The working took two hours to prepare and four seconds to ruin.

I found the seam easily — that much I could say for myself, afterward, when I was capable of saying anything for myself. The residue sat along the hound's spine like a row of bad stitching, faintly cold, faintly *tidy*, and I set my salt-circle around us both, and I named the binding, and I named what it owed, and I began to unpick.

And the binding — the small, dying, leftover binding — *took hold of my name*.

I want to be precise about this, because people always imagine losing your name means forgetting it. It doesn't. Forgetting is soft. This was the opposite of soft. It was as if someone had hooked a finger into the exact center of my chest and pulled, and everything that made me *Astrid* — the runaway years, Marta's kitchen, my mother's face, the taste of the word *home* — came loose all at once and started sliding toward the little dying knot in the dog's spine, because a starving binding will grab any name in reach, and mine was closest, and I had been careless enough to leave it lying about unattended.

The cellar tilted. The salt circle flickered. Somewhere very far away Yew was shouting my name —

— except she wasn't shouting it *at* me, she was shouting it *for* me, hurling it like a rope across a chasm, "ASTRID OYELARAN, SON OF NOBODY, BROTHER OF THE CONVOY, THIEF OF BREAD AND HEARTS," naming me piece by piece, rebuilding me aloud, and I clung to each title like a drowning man clinging to driftwood, and my hand — my own hand, I would know it anywhere, it had stolen bread and buried friends and held a dying borderwarden's — my hand came down hard on the seam and tore the stitches out clean.

Bracken sneezed. Shook himself. Wandered off to his water bowl.

I sat down in the middle of the broken circle and shook for a long time.

Yew crouched next to me. She didn't touch me. She knew better; skin carries names, and mine had been handled roughly enough for one day.

"Now you know," she said quietly. "That's what the final page costs. Not death. Death is easy; death is just the last debt. What the page costs is *you*. Someone has to stand inside the binding and be the thing that keeps it shut. And bindings are hungry, Astrid. Bindings always grab the nearest name." She paused. "Especially the largest container in the room."

I looked up at her. "You've known since the beginning. Since the flour sack. You looked at me and saw a vessel."

"I looked at you," Yew said, "and saw a boy who deserved to choose. There's a difference, and I have spent twenty years making sure nobody erased it." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and busied herself sweeping the scattered salt into her palm — salvaging, always salvaging. "Tomorrow we start the hardest lesson."

"Harder than nearly dissolving into a dog?"

"Different." She poured the salt back into its jar and sealed it with a thumbprint. "Tomorrow I teach you how to walk into a palace full of people who can smell magic, wearing none at all, and smile. Tomorrow we make you bait."

I laughed — I couldn't help it; it came out cracked and startled, the first laugh since the borderwarden died. "You always said prophecy readers were the bait."

"Yes." Yew glanced at the prophecy book where it sat chained in its iron box in the corner, patient as a trap, and her mouth went thin. "But sometimes, love, the fish gets big enough that somebody ought to warn the hook."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep. I went up the cellar stairs into the tannery yard, where the frost was starting — early frost, wrong-month frost, the kind the oldwives mutter about — and breathed white clouds and tried to feel the edges of my own name, checking them the way you check your pockets after a crowd.

They were all there. Astrid. Oyelaran. Son of nobody, brother of the convoy, thief of bread and hearts.

But under the frost-light, when I closed my eyes, I could feel it — far away, past the mountains, past the smuggler roads, sitting in a throne room at the heart of the capital: an enormous, patient attention, turning over in its sleep.

And for just one moment — one heartbeat, one grain of salt's worth of time — I felt it notice me back.

Not see. Not recognize. Just *notice*, the way the sea notices a single warm drop falling into it.

I stood in the yard until my toes went numb, and then I went downstairs and woke Yew, because some things you don't sit on overnight, and she listened with her eyes going flatter and harder by the sentence, and when I finished she was already pulling her boots on.

"Frost in the tenth month," she said. "It's feeding faster. Which means the King is emptying quicker. Which means—" she grabbed her coat, "—we're out of time for lessons, love. Tomorrow night there's a masque at the palace. Half the underworld has been forging invitations for a month. Isolde's people leave a door unlatched on the east colonnade — don't ask how I know, ask how *she* knows I know, that's the interesting question." She shoved a forged invitation against my chest. "Congratulations, Astrid. You're going to a ball."

"I don't dance," I said.

"Then you'd better learn fast," Yew said, "because I promise you, the thing in the crown already knows your steps."
