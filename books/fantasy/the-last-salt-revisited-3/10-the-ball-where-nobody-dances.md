## 10. The Ball Where Nobody Dances

The invitation cost Astrid a week of wages, a tooth, and most of his dignity.

"You're certain this is the one?" he'd asked Yew, holding up the card she'd fished from the pocket of a drunk herald three nights prior. *His Serene Majesty requests the honor of your presence at the Masque of the Turning Year.* The ink was gold. The paper was heavy enough to stun a fish.

"Certain as I am of anything," Yew had said, not looking up from her mortar. "The Brine feeds heaviest on ceremonial nights. All that power gathered in one room, all those oaths and toasts — it's a banquet table laid out for something with no manners. If you want to see what's wearing your King, you go when everyone else is wearing a mask."

Which was how Astrid Oyelaran came to be standing in the servants' corridor of the Winter Palace, dressed as a minor functionary of the Office of Ceremonial Grains, holding a tray of salt.

Not metaphorical salt. Actual salt. His cover story — Yew's invention, and therefore flawless in its tedium — was that he belonged to the obscure bureau responsible for blessing and distributing the ritual salts used at state occasions. Nobody questions a man carrying salt. Nobody wants to be near him long enough to try. He had walked past four guards and two actual members of the Office of Ceremonial Grains, and both of the latter had given him the weary nod of men who understood each other's suffering.

The disguise itself was simpler than the paperwork. Invisibility had been his one gift since childhood — the talent of being so unremarkable that eyes slid off him like rain off waxed canvas. It had failed him the night he found the prophecy book, and it had stayed failed ever since, which meant that for the first time in his life, Astrid had to *work* at going unnoticed. Posture slightly stooped. Expression faintly apologetic. A walk like a man perpetually on his way to fetch something for someone more important.

It turned out he'd been overqualified for invisibility all along. He'd just never had to do it manually before.

* * *

The ballroom made him stop, tray or no tray.

He had expected splendor. What he hadn't expected was the silence inside the splendor — a hundred nobles in masks of silver and glass, an orchestra playing a waltz so slow it barely qualified as motion, and nobody dancing. They circled the floor's edge like moths afraid of the candle. Conversations happened in murmurs. Laughter, where it occurred, had the bright brittle quality of ice about to crack underfoot.

And at the far end of the hall, beneath a canopy of white silk, sat the throne, and on the throne sat the Salt King.

Astrid had seen portraits. Portraits lied by omission. The man on the throne was not old — or rather, he was every age at once, his face smooth as river stone and just as expressive. He wore the crown the way a fence post wears ivy. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, as if waiting for something to be placed in them. His eyes were open too, fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, on everything.

Every few minutes, a chamberlain would lean close and murmur something. The King's head would tilt a precise fraction. Sometimes his right hand would rise and fall, and the chamberlain would interpret this as assent, and a law would pass, and a tax would change, and a family somewhere in the provinces would have a very good year or a very bad one.

Nobody danced. Astrid began to understand why. Dancing required turning your back to the throne, and something in the room — something under the taste of salt that coated the back of his throat the moment he entered — suggested that this would be unwise.

He drifted along the wall, tray balanced, doing the manual labor of being invisible. He was perhaps thirty feet from the throne when he felt it: a pressure against his mind like a thumb testing fruit. Faint. Curious. Old.

The Brine had noticed him.

Astrid did what any sensible person would do. He looked down at his tray and became intensely interested in salt.

*Don't look up,* he told himself. *You are a boring man with a boring job. You have done this job for eleven years and you have never once been promoted. You have opinions about grain coarseness.*

The pressure withdrew — slowly, reluctantly, like a cat deciding the mouse wasn't worth the pounce. Astrid exhaled through his nose and discovered his hands were shaking hard enough to make the tray whisper.

"Careful," said a voice beside him. "If you spill that on the marble, they'll make you lick it up, and then we'll both be embarrassed."

He turned. The woman addressing him wore a mask of pale blue enamel worked with silver waves, and a gown the color of deep water, and an expression of such perfectly calibrated boredom that Astrid felt an instant kinship. She held a glass of wine she clearly wasn't drinking. Her other hand rested lightly on her fan, and her fan, he noticed, was positioned with casual precision between herself and the throne.

"I beg your pardon, my lady," he said, defaulting to functionary. "I'll be careful."

"Mm." She studied him over the rim of the untouched glass. "You're new."

"The Office of Ceremonial Grains has a—"

"A high turnover rate, I know. Everyone says so." She sipped nothing. "You hold your tray wrong."

Astrid looked down. "I... do?"

"Left hand under the rim. Right hand never touches it. That way, if someone demands you show them your palm — and someone will, eventually, because the witch-finders have gotten ambitious lately — you don't have salt residue on your sword hand." She said it lightly, the way one comments on the weather. "Also, real grain-blessers flinch when people get close. You didn't flinch when I approached. Either you're very brave or you've spent years making yourself small enough that closeness stopped frightening you."

The orchestra swelled into a movement even slower than the last. Somewhere across the hall, a countess laughed her brittle laugh.

"And you," Astrid said quietly, "know entirely too much about trays for someone who is drinking nothing and watching the throne through a fan."

For the first time, the mask's painted smile was matched by a real one underneath it. Small. Sharp. Almost relieved.

"My lady Isolde," she said. "Though tonight I'm merely 'the girl in blue whom nobody asks to dance,' which suits me fine. Walk with me toward the east colonnade. Slowly. We are going to admire the tapestries, and you are going to keep holding that tray like your life depends on it, because it does."

* * *

They admired the tapestries.

They depicted, in thread faded to ghosts of themselves, the founding of the realm: kings kneeling, rivers running silver, and everywhere — borders, hems, halos — lines of white that could only be salt. Astrid counted seven separate scenes of a monarch receiving salt from the hands of the common folk. In none of them did anyone look happy about it.

"They stopped weaving new ones eighty years ago," Isolde said, gazing at the oldest panel with the flat affection of someone regarding a relative who had done something unspeakable at a wedding. "The guild said the dye kept failing. Do you know what actually happens to dye near the palace wards?"

"It fades."

"It fades unevenly. In patterns." She turned to face him fully, and though her voice stayed light as conversation about weather, her eyes were not light at all. "My father hasn't spoken in six years. Not a word. Not to me, not to the council, not to his own reflection. The court calls it grief for my mother. Grief is convenient. Grief explains everything and requires no action." She touched the tapestry, one finger tracing a line of salt-white thread. "But I grew up in this palace, and I remember my father's laugh, and whatever sits on that throne now does not grieve. It *consumes*. Slowly. Patiently. Like damp spreading through a wall."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "My lady, I—"

"Don't." She raised one finger. "Don't tell me why you're here. Don't confess anything. There are ears in these walls that aren't attached to heads. Just answer me one question, and answer it honestly, because I have asked it of every stranger who has passed through this palace in ten years and I have never once gotten the answer I'm waiting for." She leaned closer. Behind the enamel mask, her eyes were the exact gray-green of the sea before a storm decides. "Have you read a book? Old, water-damaged, bound in gray leather, with a date in it that shouldn't exist?"

The bottom of the world dropped out, quietly, the way it always did.

"How—" Astrid managed.

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes."

Isolde closed her eyes. For three full seconds — an eternity at a royal masque — she simply stood there, breathing, like a swimmer surfacing after longer underwater than she'd planned. When she opened them again, they were wet, and she was smiling, and the combination was devastating.

"Ten years," she said softly. "I found a fragment of it when I was fourteen. One page, hidden in my grandmother's things, quoting a prophecy about an unmaker who would come in the turning of the year. I thought I was mad. Then I thought I was chosen. Then I thought I was mad again, but usefully." She laughed — a real laugh, quickly smothered. "Do you know how lonely it is, knowing a thing the entire kingdom refuses to see? Every council session, watching that thing wear my father like a coat? Every ceremony, pouring the blessed salt and wondering if anyone else noticed that the King doesn't blink?"

"The King doesn't blink?"

"Not once. Go on. Watch. It takes a while to catch, and then you can never un-see it, and you'll be as ruined as I am." She glanced back toward the throne room, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There's something else you should know. Something I've told no one living."

Astrid waited.

"For nine years," Isolde said, "I have been poisoning it."

* * *

She led him — still carrying the absurd tray — through a service door into a narrow gallery above the kitchens, where the heat rose up around them like a blanket and the noise of the masque fell away to a distant ocean-sound. Here, finally, she pulled off her mask, and Astrid saw her properly: mid-twenties, tired around the eyes in the specific way of people who perform competence daily, with a small scar through one eyebrow she made no effort to hide.

"Poisoning it," he repeated. "The Brine."

"Every state ceremony involves consecrated salt. Who do you think oversees the consecration?" She leaned against the wall, arms crossed. "The rite requires the salt to sit overnight in the chapel of the tides. Nine years ago I started adding things. Ground ironbark. Ashes from a pyre of drowned-man's-nettle. Nothing lethal — I'm not trying to kill it, mostly because I don't think you can. But salt is how it tastes the world, Astrid—" she caught herself using his name, and paused, and he realized he must have given it to her somewhere in the blur of the last quarter hour, or perhaps she'd simply taken it, the way royalty took things "—and I have been making the world taste bitter for nearly a decade. Slowing it down. Starving it by inches."

"Why didn't you tell anyone? Your council, the—"

"The council?" She smiled thinly. "Half of them are hollow already. Little hollows. The Brine doesn't only take kings — it takes whoever holds power long enough, and a councillor's power is smaller, so the hollowing is slower, subtler. Councillor Vane has voted exactly as the crown wishes for eleven years and cannot tell you why. Chancellor Merek cries salt tears and believes they're sentiment." She shook her head. "No. I told no one, because everyone I might have told was either eaten or frightened, and because telling people gets them killed. I learned that at nineteen, with a friend whose name I don't say anymore."

The warmth in the kitchen gallery suddenly felt fragile, a lamp in an enormous dark. Astrid set the tray down on a barrel, finally, and immediately felt lighter in body and more exposed in every other way.

"I have friends like that," he said. "Smugglers. In Thornfell. They took me in when I ran, and I've spent months trying to decide whether knowing me is going to destroy them." He laughed, without much humor. "The evidence so far suggests yes."

"And yet you came anyway. To the capital. To the ball." Isolde tilted her head. "Why? If the prophecy names you — and I can see it does, you have the face of a man carrying something breakable — why walk into the seat of the thing that wants you?"

"Because I need to know what I'm facing." He looked at his hands. "And because I read the rest of the book. And there's a page at the end that I haven't been able to make myself read twice, and I thought — I thought if I came here, if I saw it, the whole thing might turn out to be a mistake. Books lie. Prophecies especially. My master always said prophecy is a hook and readers are bait."

"She sounds wise."

"She sounds alive, which is better." Astrid hesitated. Then, because lying to her suddenly seemed both impossible and obscene: "The page is in my handwriting, Isolde. A century before I was born."

She didn't gasp or recoil, and he loved her a little for it. She just nodded slowly, the way one nods at news that confirms a long suspicion.

"Then you understand why I poison the salt," she said. "Someone wrote the ending. I intend to make sure the ending arrives late, hungry, and weakened." She pushed off from the wall and extended her hand — not the court gesture, but a real one, palm open, the same posture the King held on his throne, except hers was a choice. "So. Here is what I propose, unmaker. You want to reach my father. I can get you within arm's reach of him — there is a moment, during the Rite of First Salt at the turning of the year, when the crown is removed for the blessing, and whatever is left of the man comes closest to the surface. Three minutes, maybe four. I've watched it nine times and never had the means to use it."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you let me help you rewrite your ending." Her grip was cool and firm. "I've had ten years to study how bindings work from the inside of one. I know where the cage is weak. And unlike everyone else in your life, apparently, I have absolutely nothing left to lose, which makes me extremely dangerous."

Astrid took her hand. Somewhere below them, the orchestra began the final waltz, and upstairs, in the glittering hall, nobody moved toward the floor.

"One condition," he said. "When this is over — however it ends — you teach me the poisoning recipe. I have a feeling I'm going to need to be bitter too."

That earned a real smile. "Done. Now come. We have until the toast, and there's something in the archives beneath the chapel you need to see before you can plan anything — records of every binding ever attempted on that thing." Her expression flickered, and he saw, for just a heartbeat, fear cross it like a cloud. "Fair warning. The last binder's name is in those records. And the date of his death is written in a hand I think you should compare against your book."

Astrid picked the tray back up out of pure reflex.

Isolde was already moving toward the stair, mask back on, spine straight, every inch the princess nobody asked to dance.

"Keep up, Master Oyelaran," she said over her shoulder. "And do try to look bored. In this palace, bored is the safest thing to be."

He followed her down into the dark, salt gritting softly under his boots with every step.
