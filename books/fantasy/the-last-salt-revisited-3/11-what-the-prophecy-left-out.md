## 11. What the Prophecy Left Out

The royal archives smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of salt — that last one Isolde had stopped noticing years ago, the way one stops noticing a limp or a lie told daily. She carried two lanterns, a ring of keys she was not officially permitted to own, and a boy who walked like a man trying to be furniture.

"You can stop doing that," she said over her shoulder.

"Doing what?"

"Trying to be invisible without using your magic. It doesn't work. You keep apologizing to doorframes."

Astrid straightened, which helped only slightly. He was still wearing the livery of a minor palace under-clerk, three sizes wrong for him in ways that made him look less like a servant and more like a laundry basket that had learned to walk. Isolde had dressed him herself, partly for disguise and partly because it had been the first genuinely funny thing she'd seen in a decade, and she'd hoarded the laugh like coin.

"The archives are beneath the east wing," she said. "Officially they're flooded. Unofficially my grandfather diverted a river to make them flooded, because he didn't like being contradicted by paper."

"Your grandfather sounds charming."

"He once declared war on a duck." She unlocked the third door past the wine cellar — the wine cellar being the official archive, stocked with documents selected for their agreeability. "The duck won. We don't discuss it."

Down, then. Down past the level where the torches were kept trimmed, down stairs worn into shallow smiles by centuries of clerks' feet, until the air went cool and close and the lanterns threw shadows that seemed to arrive slightly before their owners. Isolde counted doors out of habit. Eleven. Twelve.

Thirteen.

She unlocked it and stepped aside. "Welcome," she said, "to everything the crown would prefer everyone forget, arranged alphabetically by embarrassment."

* * *

The archive proper was a long vaulted hall, shelves climbing into darkness, and Astrid stood at its mouth like a man at the edge of cold water.

"How much is here?" he asked.

"Four hundred years of binding records. Every attempt to cage the Brine. Every binder who tried." Isolde set her lantern on the reading table — a slab of black oak scarred with candle wax and, near one edge, something that might have been fingernails. "My tutors taught me the Brine appeared sixty years ago. A calamity. An invasion from outside. That's what the histories say."

"And?"

"And the histories are the thinnest books down here." She pulled a ledger from the shelf nearest her hand, blew off its dust, and dropped it in front of him with a thump that raised a small ghost of grey. "Read the colophon."

He read. His lips moved. She watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out.

"'Bound previously,'" he quoted. "'Anno—'" He looked up. "This date is four hundred years old."

"Two hundred and eleven years before that, there's another record. And another before that. The Brine isn't an invader, Astrid. It's a tenant. It comes, it finds the largest container of power in the land, it moves in, and eventually the container cracks. Then someone binds it again, and we get — oh, forty, sixty years of quiet while it chews through the walls." She turned pages with a clerk's efficiency and a gravedigger's gentleness. "Here. Binding of the Brine, year 311. Binder: Queen Maravel the Second. Outcome: successful. Note: binder did not survive."

"That could be coincidence. Queens die all the time. Especially ones who do things like declare war on ducks."

"Year 268. Binding performed by the Circle of Nine." She let him read the outcome line himself this time.

Astrid read it twice. "'Outcome: successful. Note: none of the Nine survived.'" His voice had gone very careful, the voice of a man walking across ice and trying not to inform the ice that he was there. "All nine."

"All nine. And the circle before them, and the lone hedge-witch in year 190, who at least has the decency of a margin note saying she knew exactly what she was buying." Isolde closed the ledger. "Every unbinding kills the binder. Not sometimes. Always. It's not a cost of failure. It's the fee."

"Then how does anyone ever—" He stopped himself. She watched him arrive at the answer and hate it: *someone has to.*

"There's more," she said, hating herself a little for how gently she said it, and more for knowing she couldn't soften it. "There's always more. That's what archives are for."

* * *

She led him deeper, to the restricted shelf — locked behind iron mesh, though the lock had been broken since her father's reign and no one had bothered, because who sneaks into a room full of grief? There she took down a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, and set it beside his prophecy book, which he had carried against his ribs like a second heartbeat for two days now.

"Where did you find yours?" she asked.

"A ruined waystation in the Thornfell passes. Hidden under a loose flagstone, wrapped in this same kind of cloth."

"Mm." She unwrapped hers.

It was the same book. Same cracked leather, same salt-stained edges, same title stamped in flaking gilt. Different text inside — older, fuller — but when she laid them side by side, open to their final pages, the correspondence was unmistakable. Hers was the parent volume. His was... she still didn't have a word for what his was. Neither did the archive. She'd checked. Four hundred years of records, and no one had ever catalogued a book that wrote itself forward.

"Mine ends mid-sentence," she said. "Year unknown. Look at yours."

"I've looked."

"Look again. Properly. Out loud."

He sighed — the sigh of a young man humoring an older woman, which she allowed because she'd earned decades of being humored and intended to spend every one — and began to read the final page aloud.

"'And the unmaker shall come to the throne in the hour of white frost, and the hunger shall know its name—'"

"Stop. Whose hand is that?"

"It's the same as the rest of the book. It's been the same the whole way through, I checked after Marta pointed out the dates—"

"Whose hand, Astrid. Look at it the way you'd look at a forged seal. Letter by letter. Where does the tail of the 'r' go? How does the 'k' cross itself?"

He frowned and bent closer, and she watched him do it — really do it — watched the frown deepen from irritation into concentration into something colder. He was good at this. Of course he was; Yew had trained him, and Yew had been a forger among her other crimes, and forgery begins with learning to truly see a letter rather than merely recognize it.

"The 'k,'" Astrid said slowly. "The crossbar starts inside the stem and kicks out. Nobody writes a 'k' like that. It's awkward. It's..." He went quiet.

"Go on."

"It's how *I* write it." He said it the way you'd report a symptom to a physician. "Yew beat it out of me for copying. Said it was a signature, not a letter. But I never stopped. Under pressure, my hand goes back to it." He looked up at her. "Isolde. This whole book — the prophecies that came true — were they..."

"Compare the early pages too. Take your time."

He took his time. The archive clock — some brass mechanism that had outlived three dynasties and ticked out of pure spite — measured out a quarter hour in small dry clicks. Astrid turned leaves. Held pages to the light. Tilted them. Once he laughed, short and awful, and she pretended not to hear, because some laughs are private property.

At last he sat back. His hands were flat on the table as if steadying it, or himself, or the world.

"It's mine," he said. "Not similar. Mine. The ink changes weight the way mine does when I'm tired. The margins get wider toward the end the way mine do when I stop caring about neatness. Isolde—" His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I didn't write this. I've never seen it before tonight. I found it under a rock in Thornfell a week ago."

"I believe you."

"Then what am I looking at?"

Isolde poured water from the flask she'd brought, set it in front of him, and waited until he drank, because grief goes down easier diluted. Then she told him the truth she'd assembled alone, in this room, over ten patient years.

"I think you wrote it later. Or — will write it. Will have written it." She winced at her own grammar. "Tenses break around things like this. My father's astrologer went half-mad trying to diagram sentences about the future perfect. But the simplest explanation is this: at some point ahead of us, you take this book — this book, or its twin — and you write in it. Everything in it. The parts that already came true, because you needed them to come true. The parts that haven't, because you need them to happen. And then you hide it in a waystation in Thornfell a century before tonight, and you arrange — somehow — for yourself to find it."

"Why? Why would I—" He stopped. She saw him work it, the way she'd worked it a hundred nights: the hook, the bait, Yew's law. *Prophecy is a hook, and readers are bait.* "Because if I just told myself, I wouldn't believe it. Because a stranger's warning gets ignored, but a prophecy gets obeyed." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Because I needed me to walk every step of this road, and I knew I wouldn't do it for free. So I paid myself in fate."

"Now you understand why I stopped sleeping properly," Isolde said. "Congratulations. It's terrible."

He laughed again, and this time it was almost human. "The final page. What does it actually say? Not the prophecy-speak. You've had ten years with the parent volume."

She hesitated. This was the part she'd rehearsed in the dark, and it still stuck in her throat like a fishbone. But he'd earned it. He'd drunk the water.

"In plain words? The binding requires a vessel. A single vessel. One person must wear the Brine — carry it, contain it, hold it — for the rest of a life that does not end until the vessel breaks. Every binder before you thought that meant: put it in the strongest container available and let the container burn slow. Crowns. Queens. Kings." She touched the black oak table, the nail-scars near its edge. "My great-grandmother tried it. Read her note."

Astrid picked up the scrap of vellum she slid toward him. The handwriting was beautiful and getting worse line by line.

*To whoever reads this: it does not hurt, which is the cruelest part. It simply eats. Tell my daughter I was not afraid. Tell her I was not anything.*

Astrid set it down very carefully. "That's what happened to the Salt King."

"That's what's happening now. Faster, since the frost started." Isolde folded her hands. "So. Now you know what the book left out, and what it was written to make sure you'd find anyway. Every unbinding kills the binder, and the binding makes the binder a prison until death, and the handwriting on the wall is yours."

For a long moment he said nothing. The spiteful clock ticked. Somewhere above them, the palace went about its hollow business.

"Then I'll change it," Astrid said finally. Quietly. Not bravely — she noted that with respect. No flourish. Just a man deciding, the way you decide to stand up when your legs have been kicked out from under you.

"Change a page your own hand wrote?"

"Change what it means. If the binding needs a vessel—" He stopped. Frowned. Something moved behind his eyes, some idea not yet fully born, and she recognized the expression because she wore it most days herself: the look of a person holding a shape they can't yet name.

"If?" she prompted.

"If the cage is a person, maybe that's the flaw. Maybe the flaw is *alone*." He shook his head. "No. Forget it. It's not finished yet."

"Most true things aren't," Isolde said. "Finish it fast. Because—"

The bell began.

Not the chapel bell, not the alarm — the *court* bell, deep bronze, rung by hand only when the witch-finders demanded entry. Its sound reached even here, four floors down, muffled to a heartbeat in the stone.

Isolde was on her feet before the second toll, snuffing lanterns, mind already running the corridors ahead of them. "Corvane," she breathed. "He's at the gates. Tonight. With warrants." She grabbed Astrid's sleeve. "And somewhere in this city, I'd wager everything left of my family's honor, there's a convoy of smugglers he intends to trade for you."

Above them, faintly, the bell rang on.
