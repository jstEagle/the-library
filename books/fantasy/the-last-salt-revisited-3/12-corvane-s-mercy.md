## 12. Corvane's Mercy

Witch-Finder Corvane had a theory about mercy, and he kept it the way other men kept a good knife: honed, oiled, and rarely shown in public.

The theory was this — mercy was not the absence of punishment. Mercy was punishment, precisely measured, with the arithmetic shown. A man who was flogged without knowing why learned only fear. A man who was flogged and told *this is the price of your crime, and here is the door back to grace* learned something worth having. Corvane had spent twenty years in the witch-finders' service learning the difference, and twenty years watching his colleagues never learn it at all.

His colleagues burned. Corvane *read*.

That was the other thing he kept like a knife: the ledger. Forty-one confiscated prophecy books, catalogued in his own cramped hand, cross-referenced, annotated, their contradictions noted in red ink. The other finders treated prophecy like kindling. Corvane treated it like testimony. Every book was a witness, and witnesses, in his long experience, always wanted something. You only had to work out what.

The book from Thornfell wanted Astrid Oyelaran.

He had read it eleven times now, by lamplight, in the back of a jolting wagon that smelled of wet leather and the horses he did not trust. Eleven times, and each reading the same passage had risen up to meet him like a fish rising to a hook:

*On the eve of his nineteenth year, the unmaker walks the salt roads, and the Brine shall know his step.*

The eve of his nineteenth year. Which, according to the boy's apprenticeship papers — Corvane had read those too, all of them, he read everything — was tonight.

"Finder," said Sergeant Hallow, reining up beside the wagon. She was a broad, sensible woman with a face like a closed door, and she was the only rider Corvane had ever permitted to ask questions twice. "The scouts say the convoy's stopped at the Drowned Chapel. Old smuggler waystation, up on the ridge. Nine wagons, twenty-some souls. They've lit fires."

"Of course they've lit fires. It's cold, and they believe the mountains are theirs." Corvane closed the ledger and tied it shut with the little waxed cord he used for nothing else. "Do they know we're behind them?"

"Hallow thinks they spotted our dust an hour back. They'll run at moonset."

"They'll run at moonset," Corvane agreed, "and they will run directly into the Narrows, because the Narrows are the only road that fits nine wagons, and the Narrows have exactly one mouth." He smiled, briefly, the way one might smile at a well-constructed sentence. "We will be at that mouth before they are. Sergeant, I want the wagons across the road and the men in the rocks. No one fires unless fired upon."

Hallow's closed-door face opened a crack. "No one fires? They're smugglers, Finder. And if the unmaker's among them—"

"Then he is a nineteen-year-old apprentice who has never killed anyone, riding with people who love him, and if we put a crossbow bolt through one of those people we will teach him exactly what the crown is, and he will spend the rest of his life agreeing with the Brine." Corvane swung down from the wagon and began buckling on his riding coat. "We are not here to make the prophecy true, Sergeant. We are here to prevent it. Kindly do not help it along."

It was, he reflected as he mounted, a genuinely radical position for a witch-finder to hold. His colleagues believed prophecy was a threat to be burned. Corvane believed prophecy was an *argument* — one side of one, at that — and arguments could be won.

He intended to win this one with mercy. Precisely measured. Arithmetic shown.

* * *

The trap at the Narrows closed at the second hour past midnight, and it closed beautifully, which Corvane noted with the modest satisfaction of a man whose plans usually closed adequately.

The convoy came down the defile with their lanterns hooded and their wheels greased, and found the road blocked by two overturned wagons, thirty riders, and Corvane himself standing in the lantern-light with his hands visible and empty, which unnerved people more than a drawn sword ever had.

"Good evening," he said, in the voice he used for reading aloud — warm, unhurried, a voice that made people lean in before they remembered they ought to be running. "My name is Corvane. I am a witch-finder of the crown, and I have come a very long way, and I would like very much not to do any of the things you are currently afraid I will do."

A woman came forward from the wagons. She was sixty-odd, grey-braided, built like something the mountains had personally quarried, and she walked with the particular rolling gait of a woman who had spent forty years on ship decks and mountain paths and had decided, at some point, that both were her territory.

"Marta Voss," Corvane said, before she could introduce herself. "Matriarch of the Voss convoy. Smuggler for thirty years, never once caught, and I want you to know that I have read your file and I consider that record genuinely admirable."

"You've got a strange way of paying compliments, Finder." Her voice was gravel over iron. Behind her, the convoy had gone very still — twenty-some faces in the lantern-light, and among them, at the back, half-hidden behind a salt barrel, a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and the specific rigid posture of someone trying very hard to be furniture.

Corvane did not look at him. Looking was a mistake; looking was how amateurs lost their quarry. He kept his eyes on Marta and let the boy believe, for one more minute, that invisibility was still working.

"I do pay compliments," Corvane said. "I find they oil the machinery. Now. Here is the arithmetic, Mistress Voss, and I will show all of it, because I believe you of all people will respect a clean ledger."

He drew out the amnesty writ — the one signed by the Chancellor's own hand, the one that had cost him three months of bureaucratic warfare to obtain — and held it up to the lantern.

"This paper pardons every soul in this convoy. Every debt to the crown, every cargo, every year of every crime. It is signed, sealed, and it becomes valid the moment one condition is met." He let the pause stretch. "Astrid Oyelaran steps forward, comes with me to the capital, and answers the crown's questions. No chains. No cells. He rides in my own wagon and eats at my own fire. Everyone else walks away free, tonight, with the crown's paper in your hand and its riders at your back for protection."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Corvane had heard that silence before, in a hundred village squares, and he knew what it was made of: hope and horror grinding against each other.

"And if he doesn't step forward?" Marta asked.

"Then I am required to take the whole convoy to Thornfell gaol, and the arithmetic gets ugly. Some of you will hang. Not all — I will argue for as few as the law allows, and I am *very* good at arguing — but some." He folded his hands. "I do not want that. I have read every page of your file, Mistress Voss. I know about the orphan you took in during the fever year. I know about the widow you carried over the pass for nothing. I have no interest in hanging any of it. I am offering you the cheapest price that will ever be asked of you, and I am offering it once."

"You talk like a man selling salt at a famine," Marta said. "Cheap, and you know we're thirsty."

"I talk like a man who has read forty-one prophecy books," Corvane said, "and I am trying to stop a famine. The boy is in danger, Mistress Voss. Not from me — from the thing that wants to wear him. Every day he runs, it gets a better grip. I am not his enemy. I am the only man in this kingdom who has read the whole argument and wants him to *survive it*."

And there — there it was. The boy moved.

It was small. A shift of weight behind the salt barrel, a half-step that no one else would have caught, but Corvane had spent twenty years reading people the way he read books, and he caught it, and he looked — finally, deliberately — directly at the place where Astrid Oyelaran was failing to be invisible.

"Ah," Corvane said gently. "There you are."

* * *

The boy did not come forward.

That was the thing Corvane had not fully prepared for, and he was honest enough with himself, in the privacy of his own skull, to note it as a planning failure. He had expected fear to break the boy, or guilt, or the simple crushing arithmetic of twenty lives against one. Instead the boy stood frozen behind the salt barrel with his face doing something complicated, and Marta Voss stepped *sideways* — deliberately, insolently — into the line of Corvane's sight, cutting him off.

"He's not here," Marta said. "You're looking at an empty stretch of road, Finder."

"Mistress Voss."

"You're looking at *nothing*." Her chin came up. "Whatever you think you've got in this convoy, it's salt and smuggled brandy and twenty liars, and you can hang the lot of us before you get one name out of—"

"Marta." The boy's voice. Quiet, cracked, and absolutely clear in the mountain air. "Stop."

Corvane watched, and this time he did not pretend not to.

Astrid Oyelaran came around the salt barrel like a man walking into deep water — slowly, with the terrible steadiness of someone who has decided drowning is better than watching others drown. He was thinner than his papers suggested. His eyes went across the convoy — the widow, the orphans, the big gentle carter with the bad knee — counting them, Corvane realized. Doing his own arithmetic.

"You'd really let them go," the boy said to Corvane. "All of them. If I go with you."

"I give you my word, and my word is the only currency I have ever traded in."

"Your word." Something flickered in the boy's face — not fear, Corvane thought. Recognition, almost. As if he'd heard the sentence somewhere before, in a book, in a margin, in a voice. "And if your questions in the capital go badly? If I don't answer them right?"

"Then I will argue for you," Corvane said, "with everything I have. Which is considerable."

The boy almost smiled. It was the worst thing Corvane had seen all night — worse than the traitor's confession, worse than the burned waystation — because it was the smile of a boy who *wanted* to believe him, and wanting was the hook, and Corvane knew better than anyone alive that hooks were baited with exactly this.

And then Marta Voss spat.

Not at Corvane — past him, into the dirt at his feet, a long deliberate arc of contempt that every smuggler in the convoy watched land. And when she spoke, it was not to the witch-finder at all. It was to the boy, in the voice she must have used for forty years to send crews through storms.

"Astrid Oyelaran. You look at me." The boy looked. "You take one step toward that wagon and I will never forgive you, and I don't mean the way old women say it. I mean I will spend what's left of my life *knowing* you, and knowing you chose to believe a man with a ledger over the people who fed you. You want to save us?" Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, Corvane could see her hating it. "Then *run*. Run, you stubborn, invisible, maddening boy. Run so far they can't spend us buying you. That's the only mercy you can do us tonight. *Run.*"

The word went through the convoy like a spark through dry grass. Corvane heard it pass from mouth to mouth, soft and fierce — *run, run* — and he understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching his own careful arithmetic catch fire, that he had made a category error.

He had offered a trade. They had heard a threat. And the boy — the boy was looking at Marta with an expression that Corvane had seen in exactly one place before: on the final page of the Thornfell book, in the marginalia of a hand he had never been able to identify. The look of someone who has read the ending and is trying to decide whether to believe it.

"Go," Marta said again, softer now, and it was somehow worse. "Go on, love. Go."

The boy ran.

Not down the road — that would have been sensible, and sensible was clearly not in his inheritance. He went *up*, over the rocks, into the black throat of the defile, with a speed that spoke of years of learning exactly these mountains from exactly the wrong sort of teacher, and Corvane stood in the lantern-light and did not give the order to shoot.

"Finder—" Hallow began.

"Let him go." Corvane's voice came out level, which cost him something. "A man who runs into the Narrows at night with no lantern breaks his neck, or he comes out the far side, and if he comes out the far side he'll need the smugglers' roads, and the smugglers' roads—" he turned, and looked at Marta Voss, and for a moment the warm reading-aloud voice was gone entirely and what was underneath was tired, and old, and almost kind, "—the smugglers' roads all belong to you, don't they, Mistress Voss. Every one of them."

Marta said nothing. She stood with her crew gathering around her like a wall, and her eyes on Corvane were the eyes of a woman doing sums of her own, and the sum, he suspected, was how many of her people she could spend to buy that boy one more day.

"You'll hang for this," Corvane said, and heard, to his own disgust, how much of it was ritual. "The writ is void. Bind them. Wagons, people, all of it. We ride for Thornfell gaol at first light."

"And the boy?" Hallow asked, as the riders moved in.

"The boy," Corvane said, "is going to do something magnificent and terrible, and I am going to spend the rest of my life trying to be in the room when it happens." He looked up at the black defile, at the place where a nineteen-year-old had vanished into the dark with twenty lives' worth of guilt on his back. "Get the chains off the widow and the children. We are not *barbarians*."

It was, he told himself, as the convoy was bound and the fires were kicked apart — mercy. Precisely measured. Arithmetic shown.

But arithmetic, he knew, was a two-way street, and somewhere up in the dark the boy was doing his own sums, and Corvane had a sick, certain, scholarly suspicion that the boy's sums would come out owing — and that Astrid Oyelaran was the sort of debtor who always, *always*, came to collect.

By dawn, the first frost had crept down the Narrows like a white tide, and it was salt.
