## 13. The Salt Cells

The witch-finders' cells sat beneath the Hall of Edicts like a stone heart under a stone chest, and Isolde drew me a map of them on the back of a laundry list, which she claimed was the only document in the palace no one would ever bother to read.

"You'll want the coal chute," she said, tapping a smudge. "It's how they bring in fuel for the branding irons. Nobody guards a door that smells of work."

"Nobody except whoever's standing behind it," I said.

"That's why you're bringing Yew."

I looked at her. She had the particular stillness of someone who has already decided something and is waiting to see if you're clever enough to argue. I wasn't. Not yet.

* * *

Yew took the news the way she took everything — with her hands busy. She was re-salting the seams of my coat when I told her we were going down into the witch-finders' own cellar to pull out Marta's crew, and she didn't stop stitching, just said, "Corvane will have left a door open somewhere."

"That's what worries me."

"It should." She bit the thread clean. "A man who believes he's merciful is more dangerous than one who knows he's cruel. The cruel man watches his back. The merciful one sets a table."

We went in through the coal chute at the hour before dawn, when even the rats take a shift off. It was exactly as foul as promised. I have smuggled salted eels through worse, but not by much. Isolde came as far as the chute's mouth and then stopped, because a princess crawling out of a coal heap is a story that ends kingdoms, and she had a council to build by winter.

"Bring them out," she said. "All of them. Even her."

Even Sefa. The traitor. The woman who'd sold our route because Corvane held her daughter, little Wren, six years old and missing two front teeth, whom I'd once carried on my shoulders through the Thornfell passes while she sang a song about a goat. I hadn't forgiven Sefa. I'm not sure forgiveness was mine to give — half the crew wanted her flogged and the other half wanted worse. But Marta had said something on the night they took her: *You don't cut off a hand because it was forced open.* And so here I was, breaking into a prison to steal back both the hand and the thing it had been forced to trade away.

The chute opened into a fuel yard, and the fuel yard opened into a corridor, and the corridor smelled of cold iron and old prayers. Witch-finder cells are built like churches upside-down — all the devotion poured downward, into the dark, where the heretics are kept.

Yew moved ahead of me with a hooded lantern shuttered to a coin of light. She'd gone grey since the last time I'd seen her walk a road, but her hands were as sure as ever, reading locks the way other people read weather.

"Third door," she whispered. "Feel the hinges."

I felt them. Warm. "Someone's been through recently."

"Recently or often. Warm means traffic." She picked the lock anyway, slow as syrup. "If Corvane set a trap, he'd want it warm. Traps should feel lived-in. That's the whole art of it."

The door swung. Inside: four of Marta's crew, chained to the wall in pairs, bruised but breathing. Doss. Old Tam. A boy called Finch who couldn't have been more than sixteen and who cried when he saw my face, which I pretended not to notice so he could keep some dignity about it.

"Astrid," Tam rasped. "She told them things. Sefa. They knew your name before you ever gave it."

"I know."

"And you came anyway?"

"Marta would have come for me," I said, which was true, and also easier than saying what I actually felt, which was that I didn't know what I felt and there was no time to find out.

* * *

Getting four chained men out of a witch-finder cell is mostly a problem of noise. Chains are loud. Locks are loud. People who've been beaten are loud when they move, in the way that old doors are loud — every joint announces itself.

Yew solved it with salt.

She'd brought a pouch of coarse grey crystals, blessed or cursed depending on which church you asked, and she laid a thin line across each threshold we crossed. "Sound hates an untidy surface," she murmured. "Give it gravel to argue with and it forgets to shout." It worked well enough that we got three cells cleared before anything went wrong, and what went wrong was this:

In the fourth cell, the one furthest down, where the light from Yew's lantern barely reached, there was a small figure sitting on a cot. Small. Six years old. Missing two front teeth.

Wren.

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. Because Wren was supposed to be at Corvane's estate outside the city, held as leverage against her mother. She was not supposed to be forty feet underground, alone, in a cell with straw on the floor and a bowl of water that had frozen overnight.

"She's bait," I breathed. "This whole thing —"

"Yes," Yew said. She didn't sound surprised. She sounded tired, the way you get tired of being right. "He wants you down here. He wants you holding the child when the doors close. A man can't run with a six-year-old in his arms, and a man won't leave her, and either way he has you."

"Then we go now. We take her and we run."

"Through the coal chute? With four grown men who can barely stand?" Yew looked at me over the lantern, and for a moment I saw her clearly — not the sharp-tongued teacher, not the master of bindings, but an old woman doing arithmetic with lives. "There's another way out. The drain channel, south wall. It's narrow. The crew fits. You and the child fit. I fit, if I don't mind ruining a perfectly good coat."

"But?"

"But someone holds the door behind us while we go. Someone quiet. Someone who knows how to make a corridor disagree with soldiers." She smiled, and it was the smile she used when she was about to teach me something I didn't want to learn. "That's a binding job, Astrid. Salt, names, debts. I've been laying lines down here since we entered. This whole corridor is already half mine."

"No." I heard my own voice crack on it. "No, we go together, we find another way, we —"

"Astrid." She put her hand on my cheek. Her fingers were cold and dry and I loved them more than almost anything in the world. "You told me once that your master taught you invisibility so you'd never be caught. Do you remember what I said?"

I did. I wished I didn't. *Invisibility isn't hiding, boy. It's making yourself small enough that the world agrees to lose you.*

"Be small," she said. "Let the world lose me tonight. That's all this is. One more trick."

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened next, because later — after — everyone will tell it differently, and some will say I was brave and some will say I was fooled, and both will be wrong.

I carried Wren. She woke as I lifted her and, bless her, she didn't scream; she pressed her face into my shoulder and whispered, "Are you the goat song man?" and I said yes, and she said, "Your beard's longer," and that was the entire conversation, because some mercies are small and perfect.

Doss carried Finch. Tam leaned on the walls. We went south along the drain channel, which was exactly as horrible as it sounds — knee-deep, freezing, roofed with iron grates through which we could see the boots of the city passing overhead, unaware. Behind us, Yew walked backward, laying salt.

At the channel's mouth, where it opened into the storm culvert beneath the river road, I stopped. I shouldn't have. Every instinct I owned said *keep moving*, but I turned around, and I looked back down the long black throat of that passage, and I saw, far off, a coin of lantern-light. Her light. Still burning. Still moving.

Then the light went sideways. Then it went out.

And the sound that followed — I will hear it in my sleep until I die. Not screaming. Yew wouldn't have screamed; it wastes breath and gives away position. It was the sound of a corridor full of soldiers discovering, all at once, that the walls had opinions. A grinding, a rushing, a crack like the mountain clearing its throat. Salt does that, when it's asked firmly enough. Stone remembers being stone, and briefly objects to being architecture.

Then silence, and cold air pushing up the channel against us like a held breath finally released.

Tam grabbed my arm. "Move," he said gently, "or she died for nothing, and she'd haunt you for that worse than the witch-finders ever could."

So I moved.

* * *

We came up through the storm culvert into the fog on the river road, and Marta was there.

Of course she was there. Isolde had sent word, and Marta had brought the convoy's remaining hands and a cart disguised as a fish delivery, and she stood in the fog with her arms crossed like a woman waiting for a late ferry, and when she counted heads her jaw tightened with each one until she reached Wren, and then something in her face broke and reformed, quick as ice cracking on a pond.

"Sefa's girl," she said.

"Wren," I said. "Her name's Wren."

"I know her name, boy." Marta lifted the child out of my arms and held her the way you hold something you intend to defend with your teeth. Then she looked at me, and read me, the way she reads weather and ledgers and lies. "Where's Yew?"

I couldn't answer. My throat had closed like a fist.

Marta nodded slowly, once, the way she nods when a shipment goes bad and there's nothing to do but write off the loss and survive the season. She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She didn't say *it wasn't your fault*. She said, "She held the door."

"She held the door."

"Then we owe her a door's worth of living. That's how it works in my family. Debts don't die with the dead; they transfer." She handed Wren to Doss and gripped my shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Grieve on the road. Cry if you need to. But cry walking."

* * *

We cried walking. All of us, in our own ways. Finch sobbed openly. Tam went silent in the way that frightens people who know him. Doss laughed at nothing, twice, which is its own kind of grief. And I walked at the back of the cart with Yew's salt pouch in my hands — she'd made me carry it since the chute, saying a student should always hold the tools — turning it over and over, feeling the grains shift inside like tiny, patient bones.

Somewhere past the tanneries, Wren appeared beside me. She'd escaped Doss's watchful eye the way children escape everything, silently and completely. She looked up at me with her gap-toothed solemnity.

"The lady with the lantern," she said. "Is she gone?"

"Gone," I said.

"Was she your mama?"

"No. She was my teacher."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a judge. "My mama says teachers go inside you and stay. Like seeds." She patted my arm, once, very seriously, and wandered back to the cart.

I stood in the fog a moment longer than the column could afford.

Then I caught up, because Marta had told me to grieve walking, and because Yew had left me one last lesson whether she meant to or not: the salt pouch was nearly empty. She'd spent almost everything she carried, down there in the dark, buying us a corridor. Which meant that whatever came next — Corvane, the Brine, the throne room, the ending written in my own handwriting — I would face it with her craft in my head and almost none of her salt in my hands.

Behind us, deep under the city, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bells. The other kind — the ones the witch-finders rang when they wanted the whole capital to know that something precious had been taken from them, and that the taking had only just begun.

Marta cracked the reins. "Home first," she said. "Then war."
