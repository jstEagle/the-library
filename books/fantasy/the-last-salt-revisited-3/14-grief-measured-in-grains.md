## 14. Grief, Measured in Grains

The safehouse Isolde had found them was a tanner's loft above a shuttered chandlery in the Tallow Quarter, which meant the whole building smelled faintly of old fat and new beginnings, and the floorboards creaked like an old man complaining about weather. It was the kind of place nobody looked at twice. Astrid had spent his whole life being the kind of person nobody looked at twice. He had never expected to find it comforting. He did now.

He hadn't slept.

He'd tried. He'd lain on the pallet Marta's people had dragged up three flights of stairs, staring at the rafters, and every time his eyes closed he saw the door of the witch-finders' cells — that iron door, that corridor, Yew standing in it with her walking stick planted like a fence post and her chin lifted the way she did when she was about to say something devastating and knew it.

*Hold the line, boy,* she'd said. Not *goodbye*. Yew didn't believe in goodbyes. She believed in debts, and she had always said she'd settle hers when she was good and ready.

She hadn't been ready.

Astrid got up before dawn, when the Tallow Quarter was still the color of ash and the lamplighters were just beginning their rounds, and went through Yew's satchel the way you go through a wound: slowly, wincing, unable to stop.

There wasn't much. That was the thing about Yew. She'd owned a hundred books once, back when she'd been someone whose name appeared in other people's marginalia instead of writing it. Now she owned a satchel, a stick, a knife with a bone handle worn smooth as river stone, seven packets of salt wrapped in waxed linen, a tin of tea she'd been saving for an occasion that had apparently turned out to be dying, and a wooden bird.

The bird was the worst one. Or the best one. Astrid couldn't decide. It was a wren, carved from applewood, no bigger than his thumb, with wings folded as if it were waiting for permission to fly. He'd seen it a thousand times without ever asking about it. Yew kept things the way she kept secrets — close, unremarked, load-bearing. You only learned what mattered to her by what survived her.

He sat on the floor of the loft with everything laid out on a cloth, and he did what smugglers do with cargo, which is weigh it. Not in silver. In meaning.

The salt packets he could account for. Seven packets, because Yew taught him bindings came in sevens — six to hold, one to seal, and the seventh always cost more than the first six together. She'd carried enough for two bindings. She'd died holding a door with none of them spent. He didn't know yet whether that was thrift or heartbreak or some third thing she would have named with a snort and refused to explain.

The tea he decided was for the living. Marta drank tea like it owed her money. He set the tin aside for her.

The knife he kept. The stick he kept, though he was twenty years too young for it and would look ridiculous, and he suspected that was exactly why Yew would have wanted him to have it. She'd enjoyed making him look ridiculous. It was how she loved people: by refusing to let them take themselves too seriously while they did serious things.

And the wren.

He held the wren up to the grey light coming through the slatted window and turned it over. On its belly, so small he'd never noticed it, someone had scratched a single letter. A *Y*.

His master's name was not Yew. That had been a road-name, a smuggler name, the kind of name you took because it was short and bitter and easy to shout across a ravine. Her real name — the name in the confiscated ledgers, the name Corvane's people had hunted for twenty years — began with a Y. Astrid had known it once. He'd promised never to say it aloud, and he wouldn't start now, not even alone, not even to a wooden bird. But she had carved it herself, or someone who loved her had, and she had carried it hidden under her own hand all these years the way she'd carried everything.

"Right," Astrid said out loud, because the loft was empty and grief makes talkers of quiet people. "Right."

* * *

They buried her effects at noon, which is to say they buried what couldn't be kept, which is to say Astrid buried a packet of salt and a lock of hair he cut from her own satchel lining where she'd hidden it, because Yew would have considered a body an extravagance. Corvane's people had taken her. There would be no grave. There would be a ledger entry, probably. *One binder, elderly, disposed of.* Twenty years of reading confiscated prophecy books, and this was what Corvane thought people were: entries.

Marta came. Marta, who had a convoy to run and a city full of frost creeping toward her tunnels and every reason in the world to be elsewhere, stood in the little yard behind the chandlery with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and watched Astrid dig.

"You're digging like a boy who's angry at the ground," she said.

"I am angry at several things," Astrid said. "The ground is nearest."

"Aye." She let him dig. Then, when the hole was deep enough and he knelt to lay the salt in it, she knelt beside him and put her hand flat on his back between the shoulder blades, the way she did with frightened new crew on their first night run. Steady. Heavy. Warm.

"We don't do funerals on the roads," Marta said. "Roads don't keep graves. What we do is we say what the dead person owes us, and what we owe them, and then we carry both until we can't carry anymore, and then we hand some of it to whoever's standing next to us." She paused. "You're standing next to me, boy. So here's mine. I owed her a debt from before you were born, and I never paid it, and now I'll pay it to you instead, and I don't even know what it was, and neither did she, probably, and that's how debts work in families. Say something nice about your teacher and let's get out of the cold."

"She taught me that invisibility isn't hiding," Astrid said. His voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through it. "It's choosing what deserves to be seen. She said most people spend their whole lives visible and never once choose anything." He pressed the salt down into the earth. Six measures to hold, one to seal. "And she taught me that if anyone ever tells you prophecy is fate, you should charge them double."

Marta barked a laugh that was half sob, which Astrid had learned was the family sound for grief. They filled the hole. They left the yard. Somewhere above them, on a rooftop, a real wren sang four notes and stopped, as if it had said its piece.

* * *

That night he raged.

He tried not to. He'd been raised better — Yew had a whole curriculum on the management of temper, delivered mostly through pointed silences and the phrase *"the salt will still be there after you've finished shouting."* But the book was sitting on the table where he'd left it, that century-old prophecy with his name in it like a hook through his cheek, and the candle was burning, and he was alone, and grief plus anger plus exhaustion is a chemistry no curriculum covers.

Because that was the thing. That was the worm in the fruit. Yew hadn't just died. Yew had died *on schedule.*

He'd gone back through the pages that afternoon, hating himself, checking. Chapter nine of the prophecy — the chapter he'd read a dozen times and skimmed past a dozen more, because it was about "the teacher of the unmaker" and he'd assumed it meant lessons, syllables, boring stuff. It wasn't boring stuff. It said, in that dry archival voice that made doom sound like a shipping manifest:

*The teacher holds the door. The door is held but once. When the unmaker passes beyond, the teacher's page is turned, and no debt remains unpaid.*

No debt remains unpaid. She'd told him once, drunk on festival wine, laughing: *"Everyone dies with debts, boy. The trick is dying with the right ones outstanding."* And the prophecy had gone and closed her accounts for her. Had arranged her. Had used her love for him as a hinge and swung her into a doorway and shut it behind him.

He picked up the book.

It was heavier than a book should be. It had been heavy since the waystation, since the night his invisibility failed him for the first time in his life and he understood that the reason he'd always been able to slip through the world unnoticed was that the world had been saving him for something. Every page was a weight. Every fulfilled prediction was another grain in a scale tipping toward a final page written — and he had checked, in the archives, by candlelight, with Isolde watching him go pale — in his own handwriting.

His handwriting. In a book a century old.

He stood in the middle of the tanner's loft with the book in both hands and the candle guttering, and he thought: fire solves this. Fire solves most things eventually. Burn the book, burn the page, burn the handwriting, and whatever hasn't happened yet can't happen, because prophecies are just words and words burn beautifully. Yew had taught him that too. Words were the cheapest magic there was and the most flammable.

He crossed to the hearth. The coals were low but alive. He opened the book to the last page — his own looping script, the letters leaning forward like runners — and held it over the embers, and his hands were shaking, and he told himself it was the cold.

"Don't."

Isolde's voice, from the stair. He hadn't heard her come up. Of course he hadn't; she'd grown up in a palace full of people listening at doors, and she moved like someone who'd learned that the safest place to stand is where nobody expects anyone to be. A princess and a smuggler's apprentice, both fluent in the same language.

He didn't turn around. "It's my handwriting," he said. "It's my book. It's my—" His voice did something ugly. "She's dead, Isolde. The book scheduled it. It wrote her death down a hundred years ago and then it waited for me to walk into it, and I did, I walked her straight into it, and you want me to just *keep reading?*"

"I want you to think," Isolde said, "for exactly ten seconds, which I know is eight seconds longer than you usually manage—"

"Not now."

"—about what burning it does." She came into the light. She wasn't dressed as a princess tonight; she wore a servant's smock and her hair was bound up plain, and somehow that made her more herself, not less. "If the last page is your handwriting, then either you wrote it a hundred years ago — impossible — or the book reached backward and borrowed your hand — possible, horrible — or *you haven't written it yet*, and the book is simply patient, and somewhere ahead of you there's a night when you sit down with a pen and copy those words onto that page yourself."

The coals ticked. Astrid's arms ached from holding the book steady.

"And if that's true," Isolde said quietly, "then the question isn't whether the words exist. The question is *when* they get written. And maybe — I'm not a mage, Astrid, I'm a poisoner and a liar, so correct me — but maybe a page that hasn't been written yet can be written differently."

"Burning it writes it blank."

"Burning it might be the moment you write it. Fire as pen. You'd never know until it was done." She tilted her head. "Also, practically speaking: Corvane has read that book. If it vanishes, he doesn't stop believing in it. He just stops believing *you have it*, and starts believing you destroyed evidence, which in his doctrine is confession. You'd trade a page for a manhunt."

"I already have a manhunt."

"Yes, but this one would be *justified*." Almost, almost, a smile. It didn't arrive. Grief had gotten to her too — Yew had liked her, had pronounced her "the only useful royal in three generations" within an hour of meeting her, which from Yew was a coronation.

Astrid lowered the book. His arms dropped to his sides like cut rigging. He looked at the last page again — his own handwriting, patient as sediment — and felt the despair shift underneath him, the way ice shifts under a sled runner. Not breaking. Moving.

"I can't change it," he said. "I've read it forty times. I know it by heart. *And the vessel shall wear the crown alone, forever, and the hunger shall sleep in him, and the land shall forget his name.* Forty readings and it never changes. It's the most stubborn thing I've ever met, and I was apprenticed to Yew."

"Then stop reading it."

"What?"

"Stop reading it." Isolde crossed the room and took the book from his hands — carefully, the way you take a blade from someone, handle first — and set it on the table, closed, under the candle where it cast a small stubborn shadow. "You keep reading it like it's instructions. Like it's a recipe and you're checking your work. But it's a *letter*, Astrid. From you, to you. And I have received letters from people who thought they knew how things would end." Something flickered across her face — her father, salt-tracked and hollow, ten years of ceremonial poison measured in grains. "Every single one of them was wrong about the ending. Because the ending depends on who else reads the letter."

He stared at her. "That's not how prophecy works."

"How would you know? Your education came from a woman who faked her own death twice and taught you that names are doors." She shrugged. "Mine came from a court where everyone believes the future is fixed, and I have spent ten years proving them wrong one grain of salt at a time. One of us has data."

Despite everything — despite the hollow where Yew should be, despite the frost creeping through the city below the window, despite his own signature waiting at the end of the book like a trap already sprung — despite all of it, Astrid laughed. Once. Short and cracked and real.

"Yew would have hated you."

"Yew would have charged me double," Isolde said, "and then taught me for free. I know the difference. So did she."

* * *

He didn't sleep that night either, but it was different.

He sat at the table with the book closed in front of him and did the hardest thing he had ever done with a piece of paper, harder than forging manifests, harder than signing false names: he left it shut.

Instead he took out Yew's knife — the bone-handled one, smooth as river stone — and a sheet of plain paper, and he wrote, in his own handwriting, deliberately, watching each letter form:

*The vessel shall not wear the crown alone.*

It was a bad sentence. Prophecies had rules, cadences, the dry rhythm of things that come true, and his sentence had none of that. It was clumsy. It was a smuggler's forgery next to a master engraver's plate. He wrote it anyway. Then he wrote beneath it:

*The cage shall be a chorus.*

Worse. Clumsier. He wrote it anyway.

He understood, finally, what Isolde was telling him, what Yew had been teaching him all along without ever saying it plainly: that a binding is just a sentence that people agree to carry, and that sentences can be added to. That the prophecy wasn't a wall. It was a *ledger* — and ledgers have margins, and margins are where Yew had lived her whole life, writing in the edges of other people's certainties, changing what the words meant without changing the words.

He was his teacher's student after all. He would write in the margin.

He fell asleep at dawn, at the table, the book closed under his folded arms like a child guards a stolen loaf, and dreamed — for the first time since the cells — not of the iron door, but of salt falling. Salt falling upward. Grains rising off the ground against their own weight, which was impossible, which was the sort of thing that happened in dreams precisely because it was impossible, and which he woke from with his heart pounding and his hands smelling of brine that wasn't there.

Below the window, in the street, a lamplighter was dousing the last of the night's flames. And on the sill outside the slatted glass, where nothing could have climbed and nothing should have settled, someone had left a message in salt.

Five words, fine as flour, precise as Yew's own penmanship, spelling out a sentence no smuggler, no servant, no princess could have written:

*The crown is the cage. Break the cage, not the man.*

Astrid stood very still, and then he reached for the book, and opened it, and turned to the last page, and read his own handwriting one more time — not as instructions this time, but as a rival.
