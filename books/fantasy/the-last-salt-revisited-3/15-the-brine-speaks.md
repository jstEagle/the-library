## 15. The Brine Speaks

The first thing Aldric Vane knew, on the morning his mind surfaced after eleven years underwater, was that someone had moved his chair.

Not the throne. The throne was a fact of the universe, like gravity or grief. He meant the small reading chair that had once stood by the east window of his private study, where he used to sit with his boots off and his crown hanging on its stand like a hat nobody wanted to claim. In his memory the chair faced the window. Now it faced the wall.

*Eleven years,* he thought, and the thought came out whole and round and unmistakably his own, and he nearly wept with the simple luxury of it.

He did not weep, because he was not certain what his body was doing at present. That was the difficulty of surfacing in one's own mind: you came up through layers, like a diver rising through cold water into warmer and warmer strata, and until you broke the surface you could not always tell which movements were yours. His hand, for instance. Was his hand moving? He looked down — looking down being another luxury, the first in over a decade — and found his hand resting on the arm of the throne, perfectly still, fingers curled with ceremonial grace.

Not his grace. The Brine's grace. Aldric had never been graceful in his life; his fencing master had once described his footwork as *a man arguing with the floor*. Whatever wore his body now had spent years perfecting a stillness Aldric could never have managed, and the unfairness of that stung more than he expected. Eleven years of captivity, and the thief had improved on the house.

*All right,* he told himself. *Inventory.*

He was aware. That was new. Or not new — that was the horror of it — because as he settled into himself he began to remember other moments of awareness, brief and drowning, scattered through the years like coins at the bottom of a well. A moment when the Brine, wearing his mouth, had greeted the ambassador from Vellichor and Aldric had screamed inside his own skull and heard nothing, not even an echo. A moment during his daughter's twelfth birthday feast when something like him had risen high enough to see her face across the table, flushed and laughing, and then been pressed back down, down, down into the dark where the hunger lived.

She would be twenty-two now. Isolde. He counted the years the way a man counts the bars of his cell.

And the Brine was there, all around him, vast and patient and *attentive*, the way the sea is attentive to a swimmer. It was not asleep. It was never asleep. But it was occupied this morning — he could feel its attention flowing outward, through his eyes, through the wards woven into the palace stones, following some thread of magic that had brushed the outer walls in the night and slipped away again.

Aldric reached along that thread of attention, careful as a man reaching past a sleeping dog, and felt what the Brine felt:

Salt. Young salt. Unbound salt, wild and frightened and *familiar*, moving through the lower city with a signature he knew the way you know a song your mother sang.

*That's a Ravensworth binding,* he thought helplessly. *Who is doing Ravensworth work? Yew is dead — I signed the warrant myself, gods forgive me —*

But the signature wasn't Yew's. It was younger. Rougher. A student's copy of a master's hand, with the ink still wet on it.

And underneath the signature, threaded through it like a name stitched into a coat lining, was something else. Something that made the Brine's attention sharpen and lean, the way a starving man leans toward a lit window.

The Brine knew this magic. Had been waiting for it. The hunger's whole long patience — decades of wearing a king like a coat worn to shreds — had a shape now, a purpose, and the purpose had just walked into the city wearing a smuggler's coat and a stolen face.

*No,* Aldric thought, with all the force a drowned man can muster, which is none, which is exactly nothing, but he thought it anyway. *No, you don't. Not that one. I don't know who he is. I don't care who he is. You will not have him.*

The Brine did not answer. It never answered. It simply continued being enormous, the way the ocean continues being wet, and somewhere beneath its attention Aldric felt the faintest flicker of something that might, in a creature less ancient, have been called irritation.

Good, he thought. Be irritated. I've got nothing but time.

Which was, of course, a lie. The frost on the windowsills had told him otherwise — white frost in midsummer, creeping a finger's width further up the glass each week. The Brine was finishing its meal. When the last of King Aldric Vane was consumed, the hunger would need a larger plate.

He had perhaps a season left. And for eleven years he had had no way to spend it.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about being hollowed, Aldric reflected that afternoon, as the Brine sat through a petition from the Weavers' Guild wearing his body and nodding at intervals it had clearly rehearsed: it was not sleep, and it was not death, and it was very much not peace.

It was audience.

Eleven years of audience. Watching through eyes that were technically his own while his hands signed things, while his voice pronounced sentences in a flat ceremonial drone that no courtier ever questioned, because kings were permitted to be strange. Watching the Brine learn statecraft faster than he ever had. Watching it dismiss ministers who grew suspicious — quietly, politely, with letters of retirement and pensions that their widows collected without comment. Watching it eat, slowly, patiently, the way rot eats a beam: from the inside, leaving the paint untouched.

And watching, always, for anyone who might see.

Because here was the thing the Brine did not know — could not know, being a hunger and therefore incapable of imagining that anything might refuse to be eaten — Aldric had been leaving messages for eleven years.

Small ones. The Brine controlled his hands, his voice, his waking hours. But salt was the medium of the binding, and the binding was the cage, and a cage — Aldric had learned this the hard way, over a decade of practice — a cage has bars, and between bars, gaps.

When the Brine wept — and it wept often, thin lines of salt running down the royal face during ceremonies, which the court had learned to interpret as majesty — those tears were not entirely the Brine's. Grief is a pressure, and pressure finds cracks. Some fraction of every salt-tear the King shed was Aldric's, pushed up from below like a diver's last bubble, and Aldric had spent years learning to shape what fell with them.

He could not write. The Brine watched his hands too closely for writing. But salt falls where it falls, and if a man had ten thousand hours of captivity and nothing else to do, he could learn to make salt fall *interestingly*.

There was a hairline seam in the flagstones of the chapel where two stones met imperfectly, and Aldric had taught himself, tear by tear over four years, to guide the runoff so that it pooled in shapes. Letters, eventually. Words. He had written *ISOLDE RUN* on the chapel floor in the winter of her sixteenth year, and she had found it — he'd seen her find it through the Brine's distracted eyes — had seen her go white, look around, kneel as if praying, and then, with her sleeve, sweep the letters away before anyone else entered.

And she had never spoken of it. And she had begun, that same season, to taste the ceremonial salt before each rite. And the Brine had grown subtly sicker at every communion since.

*My daughter is poisoning my captor through the sacraments,* Aldric thought, with a pride so fierce it hurt worse than the hunger. *Gods keep her. She gets that from her mother.*

Queen Maren would have liked Astrid Oyelaran, he decided, though he could not have said how he knew the boy's name. It arrived with the magic-thread, the way a scent arrives on wind: *Oyelaran, Astrid*, and under it, older, the prophecy-book's cadence, *the unmaker*. Names traveled strangely near bindings. You picked them up the way your sleeve picks up burrs.

The Brine had spent the day circling that name like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. Aldric let it circle. Meanwhile he planned.

Because the boy was coming. You didn't walk into a capital wearing that signature unless you were coming to the palace, and you didn't come to the palace with that signature unless you meant to unmake something. Every instinct Aldric had left screamed the danger: the boy would try to kill the Brine, and killing the Brine was impossible, and the attempt would do exactly what the hunger wanted. It would crack the old cage wide open at the moment of maximum power, and the Brine would pour out — or pour *across*, into the nearest vessel large enough to hold it, and the largest vessel of power in any room where the King stood was, historically, the King.

Or whoever came to take his place.

*Break the cage,* the boy's book probably said. *Slay the Salt King.* Prophecies always said that. Prophecies were written by people who had never been the cage.

And that — that was the thought that finally made the plan whole — that was the instruction that mattered. Whoever came for the throne must understand what Aldric had taken eleven years to learn from inside it:

The crown was not a symbol. The crown was a *vessel-clause*. The original binder — three hundred years dead, poor ambitious bastard — had bound the Brine into the line of kings, thinking to make the monarchy a prison guard. But a clause that says *the crown contains the hunger* does not distinguish between wearers. It was not a cell with a prisoner; it was a cell with a vacancy sign. Kill the current Salt King while the binding held, and the Brine would simply flow into whatever container stood closest with enough power in it. The throne itself. The regalia. A witch-finder full of borrowed doctrine. An apprentice full of unbound salt.

The crown is the cage. Break the cage, not the man.

He had to get the words to the boy. Not the chapel — the boy might never enter the chapel. Somewhere on the boy's path. Somewhere high, and exposed, and impossible.

Aldric turned his attention inward, down through the strata, to where the pressure lived, and began, carefully, painfully, the work of building a grief large enough to crack a window.

* * *

It took him three days.

Grief on demand is a terrible craft. He had raw material — eleven years of it, compressed and salted — but the Brine monitored the deep pressures the way a jailer monitors a rattling door, and twice he had to abandon a rising surge and let it sink back down, unpoured, unused, while the hunger prowled the borders of his mind and found nothing but the usual despair, which was permitted, which was furniture.

On the third night the Brine went deep into the wards, hunting the boy's thread through the lower city, its attention stretched taut and far, and Aldric rose through the strata all at once, diving-reversed, and put everything he had against the thinnest wall of himself.

The tears came hot and fast, and he rode them, shaped them, spent them like a dying man spends his last coin — and they were his last, nearly; the effort left him thinner than he had been in years, scraped down to a whisper of a whisper.

Through the Brine's distant eyes he saw the window ledge of a garret over a chandler's shop, saw it only for a heartbeat as the hunger's gaze swept past — and saw the salt fall true. Five words, fine as flour.

Then the pressure closed over him again, and the dark came down, and the Brine settled back into its wearing of him, satisfied, patient, one season from done.

But as it sank, Aldric felt something he had not felt in eleven years.

The boy had stopped. Down in the lamplighter's street, in the cold, the thread of wild young salt had gone absolutely still — the stillness of someone reading.

*Read it again,* Aldric begged the dark, from the bottom of his prison, in a voice that was no longer a voice at all. *Read it twice. And boy — listen to me, whoever you are, whatever the book told you, listen —*

*Don't break the cage open. Don't kill the King. And whatever the last page says, whoever's handwriting it shows you —*

*— the cage doesn't care whose hand writes the next page. Only that a hand does.*

Far above, through layers and layers of hunger, he felt the Brine pause.

Felt it notice, for the first time in eleven years, that the King was awake.
