## 16. Corvane Unmasked

The thing about hiding in a city that was slowly turning to frost was that everyone's breath gave them away.

Astrid had learned this the hard way over three days of dodging through the capital, because every witch-finder on the streets now walked with his collar up and his eyes down and a little cloud of steam trailing after him like an accusation. You could disguise your face. You could disguise your gait. You could not disguise the fact that you were, regrettably, alive.

So he'd taken to moving at dusk, when the breath-clouds of a thousand other people made his own just one more candle in a crowded room. Invisibility, Yew used to say, isn't the absence of being seen. It's the art of being *unremarkable*. Astrid had once been so unremarkable he'd walked out of his master's tower with the prophecy book under his coat and two guards had held the door for him. Those were simpler days. Those were days when the only person hunting him wanted him for smuggling, which was at least an honest profession.

Now the whole city knew a face was coming. Corvane had made sure of it. There were broadsheets pinned to every wall — crude woodcuts of a young man with a scarred brow, beneath letters that read THE UNMAKER. REWARD. REPORT ALL SIGHTINGS.

The woodcut made him look handsome, which was frankly insulting. His actual face, in the cracked mirror of a chandler's back room where Isolde had stashed him, looked like someone who hadn't slept properly since finding his own name in a dead man's book. The scarred brow was accurate, though. He touched it out of habit. Marta said it made him look trustworthy. Yew had said it made him look like a boy who fell off things, which was also true.

"You're brooding," said Pip, from the doorway. Pip was one of Marta's runners, fourteen years old, and possessed the unshakable conviction that everything happening to Astrid was tremendously exciting. "Isolde says you're not to leave before the second bell."

"Isolde says a lot of things."

"Isolde is a princess."

"Isolde is a liar who happens to have excellent posture." Astrid pulled his hood lower. "Where's the second bell crowd thinnest?"

"Fishmarket. But—" Pip's face did something complicated. "There are greycoats there. Two of them. Asking about a boy with a scarred brow who buys tallow candles in bulk."

Astrid paused. "Do I buy candles in bulk?"

"You bought forty yesterday. I told her it was suspicious behavior for anyone, prophecy or no prophecy."

"Forty candles is *operational security*," Astrid said with dignity. "You can't do binding-work by moonlight alone, and lamps attract—"

"Greycoats," Pip said. "Lamps attract greycoats."

This was true, and it was one of those small humiliations of the fugitive life that Astrid had stopped arguing about around the time he'd started eating rats' tails in stew without noticing. He sat back down on the crate. Outside, somewhere past the frost creeping up the window glass, the second bell began to ring across the white-rimed rooftops, and the sound came out strange, muffled, as if the city itself had been wrapped in wool.

Or salt.

He tried not to think about salt. He thought about salt constantly.

* * *

It went wrong at the fishmarket, because of course it did. Astrid's life had developed a rhythm lately, and the rhythm was: plan carefully, execute competently, be betrayed by some detail nobody could have predicted, run.

In this case the detail was a dog. A small, wet, enthusiastic dog that took one look at Astrid hurrying between the stalls and decided, with the flawless judgment of its kind, that he was its new best friend. Astrid had spent the last week practicing unbinding on animals — Yew's lessons, the hound, the terrible night he'd nearly unpicked his own name from the world — and something in him must have still smelled of that work, because the Brine-marked creatures of the capital all drifted toward him like iron filings toward a lodestone. This one wasn't marked. This one was just friendly. But it barked at him with joy, twice, three times, and a greycoat turned around.

Astrid did what he did best, which was nothing. He became furniture. He became a stack of unsold mackerel. He let his shoulders slump into the universal posture of the tired working poor and fixed his gaze on the middle distance with the thousand-yard stare of a man thinking exclusively about fish prices.

The greycoat walked past him. Stopped. Walked back.

And then, behind the greycoat, unhurried as a funeral, came a tall man in a black coat with silver clasps, and Astrid's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Witch-Finder Corvane did not look like a villain. That was the worst of it. He looked like a schoolmaster, or a kindly uncle, or one of those priests who remembered your name and asked after your mother. He had a soft, lined face and spectacles and the patient air of a man who had read every book in the world twice. When he spoke to the fishmonger, he thanked her. Astrid watched him drop a coin in the beggar's cup by the fountain. He watched him pause to straighten a child's crooked scarf against the cold.

Then the child pointed — not at Astrid, thank all the drowned gods, but at the dog — and Corvane smiled, and the smile was warm, and Astrid felt his stomach drop through the cobbles anyway, because he'd seen that smile before. On the day Corvane had arrested Marta's convoy. He'd offered them mercy then too. Warmly. Kindly. With every appearance of meaning it.

And then he'd marched them into the salt cells anyway.

Astrid turned, very slowly, and began to walk away, and that was when the alley chose its moment.

* * *

Later, Astrid would try to reconstruct how it happened, and fail. One moment he was three stalls from the alley mouth; the next, hands had closed on his arms from either side — not greycoats, they moved wrong for greycoats, they moved like people who'd done this in the dark a hundred times — and the world folded sideways, and there was brick and rot and lamplight, and when the bag came off his head he was sitting in a chair in a room that smelled of old paper and lamp oil, and Witch-Finder Corvane was pouring tea.

Two cups.

"One sugar?" Corvane asked, without looking up. "I've been wondering. Everyone assumes the young take sugar, but you strike me as someone with an ascetic streak."

"I don't want tea."

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "But you'll drink it, because refusing would require you to believe this is a negotiation, and we both know it isn't." He set the cup down within Astrid's reach and settled into the chair opposite, folding his long hands over his knee, and regarded Astrid with the gentle, bottomless patience of a man about to explain arithmetic to a slow student. "There. Now. Do you know why you're not in chains?"

"Because you don't know who I am."

"Oh, Astrid." And the way he said the name — softly, correctly, every syllable in its place — was worse than shouting. "Of course I know who you are. I've known since the fishmarket. Possibly since the chandler's. Certainly since the ball." He smiled again, rueful this time. "You dance like a smuggler. All the court training in the world can't teach hips that learned to count coins under a table."

Astrid's mind raced. If Corvane had known at the ball, then Isolde was burned, then the cells rescue, then Yew—

"Your friends are safe," Corvane said, reading him with irritating ease. "For the moment. The woman Marta is stubborn and her crew has more tunnels than sense; my men are chasing shadows in the undercity. The princess is untouchable while her father lives, even such a life as his is. And the old binder—" something flickered across his face, quickly smoothed, something that might have been regret, "—Yew was always going to die holding a door for someone. She'd have wanted it that way, if wanting entered into it."

"You're lying."

"About which part?"

"About all of it. About caring. About the tea. About—" Astrid's voice cracked and he hated it. "You put children in cells."

"I put *binders* in cells." Corvane leaned forward, and for the first time the warmth in his face didn't quite reach his eyes, and Astrid saw what lived underneath it, and wished he hadn't: exhaustion. Twenty years of it. "Sit still. Listen. You're owed an explanation, and I find I want to give one, which surprises me as much as it will surprise you."

He rose, crossed to a shelf crammed with ledgers — dozens of them, spines numbered in a careful hand — and drew down the oldest. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as cloth.

"Do you know what I was doing twenty years ago? Before the witch-finders, before the ledger, before any of it?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I was a curate in Saltmere. Little coastal parish. And one winter the Brine surfaced there — a small surfacing, a fishing village worth of hunger, nothing like the palace — and it got into the rector. Good man. Kind man. I watched it wear him for eleven days. I watched him stand at the altar and smile and bless his congregation in a voice that wasn't his, and I was the only one who could see it, and no one — not my superiors, not the diocese, not the crown — believed me." He set the old ledger on the table between them. "Eleven days, Astrid. Then a traveling binder came through and cut it out of him, and the cutting killed him as surely as the hunger would have, and the village called it a mercy, and I called it a lesson."

"What lesson?"

"That the Brine cannot be fought. Only *managed*. Only *fed* in small ways so it doesn't feed itself in large ones." Corvane opened the ledger. Pages of cramped entries. Names. Dates. Small villages, mostly. "The order promised me peace. Not power — I never wanted power. Peace. A land where nothing wears anyone. Where no child watches her father smile with another mouth. And the price—" his voice stayed level, terribly level, "—the price was small. Minor binders. Wanderers, hedge-witches, the sort whose deaths the world barely records. Fed quietly to the hunger, here and there, the way you bait a trap so it doesn't spring on your own family."

Astrid stared at him. "You fed people to it."

"I *saved* people by feeding it others." Corvane said it simply, without heat, and that simplicity was the most frightening thing Astrid had ever heard. "Every year the King hollows further, and every year the Brine grows hungrier, and every year I keep it satisfied with drops instead of letting it drink the sea. Twenty years, Astrid. No village has burned since Saltmere. No family has watched. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am not the villain of this story. I am the *tax collector*. Someone pays. I decide it's the fewest possible someones."

"And the cells? Marta's crew? They weren't binders. They were smugglers."

"The convoy was leverage." A shrug, unapologetic. "You care about them. Leverage works."

"And Yew?" Astrid's voice came out low and dangerous. "Was she a drop?"

Corvane was quiet a moment. "She was a debt," he said finally. "From a long time ago. She knew what I was before I knew it myself." He closed the ledger gently, like closing a coffin. "But none of that is why you're here. You're here because of this."

He reached into his coat and drew out a book.

Astrid knew it instantly — of course he did, he'd have known it blindfolded, he'd slept with it under his head for months — except that it wasn't his copy. It was older. Fatter. Its pages were foxed and its spine was repaired with wire, and when Corvane laid it open on the table beside his tea, Astrid saw the same prophecies, the same dates, the same marginalia in Yew's sharp slanted hand — *and underneath them*, in different ink, in different centuries, annotations by other readers, other hunters, other frightened souls who had found their own names in the text.

And there, on the page headed with tonight's date — the date that was no longer tonight, the date the frost had nearly finished eating — a portrait. Tiny, exquisite, drawn in ink finer than hair. A young man. Scarred brow.

Him.

"Not a copy," Astrid whispered. "The original."

"The book you found was made from this one. A hundred years ago, by whoever wrote it." Corvane tapped the tiny face with one finger. "I have carried this book for twenty years, Astrid Oyelaran. I have read it until I can recite it. I have prayed over it. And in all that time, the face on this page was blank — smudged, unfinished, waiting. Until four nights ago, when I woke and found the ink had come." He looked up, and behind the spectacles his eyes were wet, and Astrid realized with a lurch that this broken old man meant every word. "Do you understand? I didn't find you. The book *finished*. The prophecy completed itself, and it completed itself wearing your face, and I have spent twenty years believing that whatever the prophecy names as the Brine's undoing must be the instrument of the order's triumph — or its end. Either way, it must be *handled*."

"Handled," Astrid repeated. "Like a drop. Like a tax."

"Like the only hope I have left." Corvane stood, and paced, and his composure frayed at last, showing the frantic thing beneath. "You think I haven't doubted? Twenty years of doubt, boy! Every name in this ledger is a doubt I buried! And now the book opens itself and shows me *you* — a smuggler's brat with hedge-magic and a found family — and every doubt comes roaring back and asks me: what if it's all been waste? What if the feeding, the cells, the ledger — what if it was all for nothing, and salvation walks in off the fishmarket smelling of tallow candles?" He laughed, short and awful. "I could almost laugh. Salvation should smell of incense. It should arrive on a white horse. It should not need me to buy forty candles."

"They were operational security," Astrid said faintly, and Corvane actually chuckled, and for one horrible heartbeat they were two tired people laughing in a lamplit room, and Astrid understood exactly how twenty years of feeding people to a hunger could feel like kindness from the inside.

Then Corvane stopped laughing.

"So here is what happens now." He straightened, and the schoolmaster returned, and the warmth went back on like a mask. "Tomorrow night the frost reaches the palace wards. The King will be gone inside a week — the Brine is devouring him faster than ever before, panicked, because it knows you're coming. After him it spills into the land. Unless." He held up one finger. "Unless the vessel it takes instead is *prepared*. Bound. Delivered willingly, with rites, with structure — so the hunger goes into a cage instead of a flood." His eyes settled on Astrid, calm and certain and utterly mad. "You are the unmaker. Very well. Come to the throne room tomorrow night and unmake it — into yourself, under my supervision, by my rites. Your friends walk free. The princess keeps her father's memory clean. The land keeps its skin. One boy pays, and thousands live. It's the best trade on the table, Astrid. It's the only trade. It's the same trade I've offered for twenty years, and every soul who took it saved more than they cost."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I put you in a cell, and I feed the Brine the convoy instead, and the frost takes the city, and I spend the rest of my life knowing I had the answer in my hands and let doctrine—" he faltered, just slightly, just for a word, "—let *doubt* win."

"There it is," Astrid said quietly. "Doubt. You almost said it."

Corvane's hand trembled, once, as he gathered his ledger and his ancient book and his two decades of buried graves. "Sugar," he said, nodding at the untouched tea. "I guessed right, didn't I? Ascetic streak."

He knocked on the door, and the greycoats came, and Astrid was escorted — gently, respectfully, like a guest, like a sacrifice — out into the frosted street, where the snow had begun to fall and each flake glittered white as salt.

Behind him, through the closing door, he heard Corvane speaking softly to himself, the way men do when they're praying or bargaining, and couldn't tell which:

*"One boy. Thousands. It's arithmetic. It's only arithmetic."*

Astrid flexed his fingers in his cuffs and felt, faint and far away, through stone and frost and the walls of the world, something vast turn its attention toward him like an eye opening — hungry, patient, and already counting him among its vessels.

Tomorrow night. The throne room. The trap inside the trap, snapping shut.

He just had to make sure that when it shut, he was the one holding the door.
