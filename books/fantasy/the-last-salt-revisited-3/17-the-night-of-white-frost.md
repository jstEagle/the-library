## 17. The Night of White Frost

The King had a name once.

He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a rope that has already slipped through his hands — not because it would save him, but because letting go felt like a decision, and he had made his last decision eleven years ago, when he had drunk from the ceremonial cup at his own jubilee and tasted, beneath the honey wine, the sea.

*Edric*, he told himself now, in the small white room where he still lived. *Your name is Edric. You were born in a house with a crooked door in Harrowmere, and your mother sang off-key, and you hated the taste of fish.*

The Brine did not care for the name. The Brine wore him the way he had once worn a coronation robe — heavily, ceremonially, without love. Tonight the wearing was faster. He could feel it in the way his memories went first: the crooked door, gone; the off-key singing, gone; Harrowmere itself dissolving like sugar in warm water. Something outside had frightened it. Something approaching. And when the Brine was afraid, it ate.

He watched through eyes that no longer fully obeyed him as frost bloomed across the bedchamber window — white, branching, crystalline, and wrong, because it was not cold. It was salt. It crept down the glass in feathers and ferns, and beyond it the city lay under a sky the color of pewter, and along every gutter and doorstep of the capital the same white bloom was opening, petal by petal, like a flower made of everything the sea had ever swallowed.

*Soon*, said the Brine, in the voice that used his throat when it pleased, which was always now. *The unmaker comes.*

*Let him come*, Edric thought, and for the first time in years the thought had teeth. He had seen the message he'd left, scratched into the crust on the chapel floor by fingers he had briefly borrowed back. *The crown is the cage. Break the cage, not the man.* He did not know if the boy would find it. He did not know if the boy could read desperation when it was written in salt.

But he knew one thing the Brine did not know, one thing it had never bothered to learn in all its centuries of eating kings: kings are not the only people who keep their word.

He gathered what remained of himself — a name, a mother's bad singing, a crooked door — and he held it all in two cupped hands behind his ribs, like water carried across a desert.

And he waited to be spent.

* * *

Witch-Finder Corvane stood on the wall above the River Gate and watched the frost come up the streets like a tide running backward, and for the first time in twenty years of service he admitted to himself that his ledger had failed him.

Not his faith. Never his faith. His faith was a stone worn smooth by two decades of handling, and he gripped it now as he had gripped it through every burning and every cell and every long night of reading confiscated prophecy books by tallow light, cataloguing madness so that the sane might sleep. But the ledger — the great index of names and signs and bindings — had an entry for the unmaker, and the entry did not say *when the frost comes, lock the gates*. The entry said nothing at all about frost.

"Seal it," he said. "Every gate. River Gate last."

His riders moved. Below, in the city, bells began to speak — not the alarm bell, which Corvane had forbidden as panic-making, but the curfew bell rung hours early, then again, then again, a stammering iron voice saying over and over: something is wrong, something is wrong, something is wrong.

He understood, distantly, that he was doing the wrong thing. That was the horror of it. The gates had been built to keep enemies out, and the enemy was already inside every gutter and windowpane, blooming white. Locking the gates would only trap the citizens in with it. He could see the logic of his own error with terrible clarity, the way a man falling can see precisely which handhold he missed.

And yet his hands gave the order anyway, because the alternative — the open gate, the unknown pouring through — was a void, and Corvane had spent his whole life paving over voids with doctrine.

"The boy," said Sister Vael beside him, breath steaming though the air was not yet cold enough to explain it. "If the unmaker reaches the palace—"

"He will not." Corvane watched the white creep along the rooftops below, beautiful and patient, and thought of the face in the prophecy book, the young face with the watchful eyes. *You were bait*, he thought. *Someone wrote you into a hook a century ago, and you took it, and I am sorry for what must happen next.* Grief and certainty sat together in his chest like old married couples, comfortable with each other's weight.

"Double the guard at the Salt Cells," he added. "And bring me the smuggler woman. If her crew knows ways into this city that my maps don't, I want them named before midnight."

Sister Vael hesitated. "She refused the amnesty again, Finder. She asked instead whether we'd considered that locking the gates was—"

"I know what she asked."

Corvane looked out at the frosted city, at the tiny figures in the streets below standing perfectly still, staring up at the white on the walls as though reading it.

"She's right," he said quietly, to no one who could help him now. "Open the River Gate."

* * *

Marta Oduya had been thrown into a witch-finder cell twice in her life, and both times she had gotten out by being more stubborn than the lock. The third time she got out because the lock froze.

It happened just past midnight. The iron of the cell door went white-rimed, then brittle, then simply gave way with a sound like a biscuit snapping, and half the corridor's doors came down with it in a cascade of salt-crusted metal, and forty prisoners stood blinking in the wreckage wondering whether they had died and this was a very poorly organized afterlife.

"Well," said Tam Wicker, spitting salt off his lips. "That's new."

"It's him," Marta said. Her heart was doing something complicated in her chest — fear and fury and a fierce, unreasonable pride all fighting for space. Only one person she knew could leave salt where he passed like a snail leaving slime. Only one person was walking toward this city tonight through the frozen dark with witch-finders and worse on his heels. "It's Astrid. He's close enough that his magic's bleeding through the walls."

"And the Brine's scared," said Fen, the youngest of the crew, pressing her palm to the frosted stone. "Look how fast it's spreading. It's gorging itself because it's frightened. My gran used to say that about wolves."

Marta's gran had said it about tax collectors, but the principle held.

They came up out of the cells into a capital gone strange — streets furred white, lamps burning yellow inside halos of frost, citizens standing at their windows watching the slow bloom with the particular stillness of people deciding whether to pray or riot. Witch-finder patrols clanked past, shouting orders nobody understood, and the smugglers of the Oduya road melted into alleys with the ease of water finding cracks, because that was the whole trade, wasn't it? The world built walls; Marta's people learned where the walls were thin.

Which was why, an hour later, Marta stood in the cellar of a chandler's shop on Sable Lane, pulling aside a rack of hanging candles to reveal a wooden door older than the dynasty, and addressed her assembled crew.

"You all know the tunnel under the wall here. Grandfather Sal dug it, my mother widened it, and I've run silk and brandy through it since before some of you could walk." She put her hand flat on the ancient wood. "Tonight we run a boy through it. A boy who got himself taken by witch-finders on our account, whose master died holding a door so our people could walk out of the Salt Cells, and who is about to walk into that palace alone because he thinks — gods forgive me for letting him think it — that he has to do everything alone."

Tam shifted. "Marta. The palace. Even if we get him to it, that's—"

"That's the point, Tam. Listen." She turned and looked at them, forty faces lit by one candle, faces she had fed and bled and buried kin for, faces that had taken her in when she was a girl with nothing but a stolen knife, faces that had taken in a runaway apprentice because Fen cried about it and Tam pretended not to agree. "For fifteen years the crown called us criminals and the witch-finders called us filth, and we survived by staying small and staying hidden. Well, look around. The night itself has gone small and hidden. Everything's hiding tonight. So here's what I know." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Nobody survives alone. Not smugglers. Not kings. Not boys written into prophecies by their own future hands. We go through that tunnel, all of us, and we put ourselves between Astrid Oyelaran and whatever's waiting for him, because that is what family *does* — not because it's smart. Because it's ours."

There was a silence, the good kind, the kind that comes before work.

Then Fen said, "I'll carry the crowbar," and Tam sighed the sigh of a man who had known, since Fen first started crying about a stranger in the rain, exactly how this would end, and began checking the load in his crossbow.

They opened the old door. Cold air breathed up out of the earth, smelling of salt and deep stone.

"Oduya road rules," Marta said, and stepped through first.

* * *

Isolde had spent ten years learning to be invisible inside her own home, and tonight invisibility finally paid its wages.

She moved through the palace's servants' corridors in a scullery maid's smock, hair bound and greyed with ash, carrying a laundry basket full of nothing but nerve. The frost had reached the palace hours ago. It grew on the state portraits now, whitening the faces of dead monarchs until they looked like a row of ghosts judging the living ones. In the Great Hall, the ceremonial salt — the salt she had spent a decade lacing with everything from blessed ash to ground grave-iron, her quiet decade-long poison drip against the thing wearing her father — had risen from its dishes overnight into delicate white spires taller than candelabra, and courtiers were crossing themselves before it, mistaking the Brine's hunger for a miracle.

Her father's wing was sealed. Guards at every door — Corvane's guards, not hers. So she took the corridor behind the kitchens, the one that smelled of tallow and old smoke, the one her nursemaid had shown her when she was nine and already asking too many questions, and came out through a priest-hole into the little chapel where the royal confessor had once heard sins.

The floor was white.

Not dusted white — *written* white. Letters of salt a finger high covered the flagstones in a sprawling, urgent hand, and Isolde knelt among them with her heart hammering and read:

*THE CROWN IS THE CAGE. BREAK THE CAGE, NOT THE MAN.*

Beneath it, smaller, shakier, almost human:

*I HELD HIM OFF AS LONG AS I COULD. HE IS COMING. DO NOT LET THEM PUT IT ON HER.*

Isolde stared at that last line for a long moment. *Her.* Not me. He didn't mean me. Which meant he meant—

"Oh," she said softly, understanding arriving like ice water. "Oh, you clever, dreadful book."

The binding wanted a vessel, and vessels wanted thrones, and thrones wanted bloodlines, and there was exactly one bloodline left in this palace with royal blood in it, and her father — whatever was left of him, whatever small drowned king still paddled beneath the surface — had spent his last strength warning her away from it.

"All right, Papa," she whispered to the empty chapel, and touched the salt letters as gently as she'd touch a sleeping face. "I hear you. No thrones. We'll break the cage another way."

Somewhere far below, faint through the stones, came a sound she should not have been able to hear and did anyway: a grinding, groaning shift, like a mountain settling, like a door that had been locked for three hundred years remembering it could open. The wards. Someone was testing the palace wards from underneath — from the old ways, the smuggler roads, the routes no map admitted existed.

And threaded through the ward-stones, unmistakable to anyone who had spent ten years studying the flavor of magic in secret, came a signature she knew from a single meeting in a masked crowd and one impossible conversation afterward: bright, quick, careful, and utterly unwilling to be noticed.

Astrid was in the tunnels. And he was not alone.

Isolde stood, brushed the salt from her knees, and pulled off the scullery smock to reveal the plain black traveling clothes beneath — ten years of preparation condensed into a costume change.

"Hold on, Papa," she told the letters on the floor. "Help is coming. It's just coming the dishonest way."

She blew out the chapel candle, which was the closest thing to a blessing this family had managed in generations, and went to meet her allies in the dark.

* * *

Astrid felt the city before he saw it.

The tunnel ran beneath the river and beneath the wall and beneath, at its deepest point, the roots of the palace itself, and the whole way the stones sweated salt. It beaded on the ceiling overhead and fell in slow, glinting drops that did not splash but *bloomed* where they landed, little white flowers opening on the packed-earth floor behind them. His own magic answered the pressure of the place — rising in him unbidden, prickling along his arms, his one talent turned inside out. All his life he had made himself unseen. Now every ward and stone in the capital could feel him coming, and there was nothing whatsoever he could do about it except walk faster.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said, matching his stride. "The guilty-face thing."

"I'm doing the *everything-is-my-fault* thing, yes."

"Good. Save some for later. Guilt keeps." Tam glanced sideways at him, and his voice dropped its roughness for exactly one sentence. "Your master bought us our lives with hers. That debt's yours now too, boy, and Oduya debts get paid, not carried. Walk straighter."

Ahead, Marta raised a closed fist, and forty smugglers stopped as one in the dripping dark.

Above them — Astrid could feel it through the soles of his boots, a vast pressure like a whale passing under a boat — something enormous was moving in the palace foundations. The Brine. Feeding. Panicking. Eating its king alive faster than it had ever dared eat before, because it could feel the unmaker coming and it was afraid.

*Good*, Astrid thought, and then immediately, *no, not good, that's Yew's law, fear makes it stronger* — and he breathed, and centered, and did what Yew had taught him in the weeks that felt like years: he stopped pushing his magic down and let it sit openly in his chest, a lamp in a window, steady.

Below him, through sixty feet of ancient stone, the grinding pressure paused. Turned. Noticed.

And somewhere in the dark ahead of them, a hatch cover lifted, and a girl in black leaned down into the lantern-light with salt dust on her knees and the expression of someone who had been waiting a decade and intended to enjoy the next five minutes enormously.

"Took you long enough," said Princess Isolde. "Follow me. And try to look like you belong in a palace. It's not hard — half of them don't."

Behind Astrid, Tam muttered to Fen, "Told you the fancy ones always know the back stairs," and Marta laughed — low, fierce, unafraid — and the Oduya road walked up into the heart of the hollow court, forty smugglers, one traitor's daughter, one princess, and one boy written into the end of the world, while overhead the frost climbed the walls and the last king of the line spent his final hoard of self getting ready to pay.

The throne room doors stood ahead of them, tall as gallows, frosted white to the lintels.

From behind them, faintly, came the sound Astrid had been dreading all night: boot heels. Many. Moving fast.

Corvane had found the tunnel after all.
