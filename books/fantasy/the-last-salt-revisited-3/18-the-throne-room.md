## 18. The Throne Room

The palace at night smelled of salt and old candles, which was fitting, Astrid thought, because so did everything else in his life lately.

He had come up through the servants' stair with Isolde two steps ahead of him, moving through the dark the way she moved through everything — like a woman who had spent ten years memorizing a house she was never allowed to leave. Her ceremonial robes were swapped for a guard's cloak that didn't quite fit across the shoulders. She'd stolen it from a man who owed her a favor, and by "favor" she meant "gambling debts," and by "debts" she meant "I have his ledger."

"Left here," she whispered.

"You said left at the tapestry."

"I said *past* the tapestry. The tapestry is a decoy. My father had it hung to confuse assassins. It confuses everyone, including assassins, who tend to get lost and give up."

"That's the stupidest security system I've ever heard."

"It's the only one in the kingdom that works," Isolde said, with the particular pride of someone whose family had produced it.

Astrid pressed himself against the wall out of habit — invisibility, his oldest trick, the talent that had failed him on the worst night of his life and kept half-failing ever since. It wasn't true invisibility anymore. Yew had taught him that nothing was. It was *unremarkableness*, a magic of being skipped over, and it worked best when he genuinely believed there was nothing worth looking at.

Tonight that belief was hard to come by. Tonight every corridor of the palace hummed against his skin like a struck wire. He could feel the wards — Yew's lessons lived in his fingers now, salt and names and debts — and beneath the wards he could feel something else, vast and patient and *hungry*, turning over in its sleep the way a whale turns under a boat.

*It knows I'm coming,* he thought. *Good. Let it lose sleep for once.*

"The throne room is through the double doors," Isolde murmured. "The guards change at midnight. You'll have perhaps four minutes before—"

"I know. You've told me twice."

"Because you have the survival instincts of a moth, and I want it on the record that I told you twice."

Astrid grinned despite himself. Two months ago he'd been a runaway hiding in smuggler roads, convinced that no one in the world would notice if he vanished. Now a princess was bullying him toward safety and meaning it. Marta would have liked her. Marta liked anyone who bullied Astrid correctly.

He thought of Marta then — in the witch-finders' cells below the city, or what remained of them after last night's rescue; of Yew, who wasn't anywhere anymore; of the child, Nessa, asleep in a safehouse with salt drawn in a ring around her cot, because Astrid had learned you can't undo the habit of protecting people even when your hands are shaking. Grief was supposed to slow you down. Nobody warned you it could also be fuel.

"Four minutes," he said. "Go."

Isolde hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and then did something she had never done in all their scheming. She took his face in both hands and kissed his forehead, quick and fierce, like sealing a letter.

"For luck," she said. "And because if you die, I will be extremely annoyed, and I don't have time to be annoyed. I'm abolishing a monarchy this week."

Then she was gone down the corridor, and Astrid pushed open the doors.

* * *

The throne room was not what the ballads promised.

Ballads gave you gold and banners and trumpeters. What Astrid got was a vast hall of white stone gone gray with salt-frost, crystals creeping up the pillars like ivy in fast-forward, chandeliers furred white as if winter had gotten inside and decided to stay. The air tasted of the sea — but the dead sea, the pickling sea, the sea that keeps things forever by making sure they're never quite alive again.

And on the dais, the Salt King.

Astrid had seen him once before, from across a crowded masque: a hollow figure in a hollow crown, weeping salt without knowing why. Up close it was worse and stranger. The King sat perfectly straight, hands on the armrests, and frost radiated off him in slow pulses, like breathing. His eyes were open. That was the horror of it. They were open, and wet, and somewhere far behind them a man was watching.

*I've been aware,* the King had written in salt, in the message that had changed everything. *The crown is the cage. Break the cage, not the man.*

Astrid walked up the dais steps and stopped an arm's length away.

"Your Majesty," he said. "My name is Astrid Oyelaran. I'm the one the book talks about." A beat. "Sorry about that. I didn't write it. Well. Apparently I did. It's complicated."

The King's throat moved. A sound came out — not words, just a rasp, like wind through a door left ajar. But his eyes tracked Astrid's face, and one finger twitched against the armrest, and Astrid chose, generously, to interpret it as *get on with it.*

"On with it, then."

He knelt and drew the circle.

Yew had drilled him on this until his knees ached. Unbinding looked, from the outside, almost disappointingly simple: a ring of salt, a name spoken into its center, and then the patient work of finding where a binding's threads crossed and pulling them loose the way you'd ease a knot out of a fishing net — not cutting, never cutting, because cut threads whip back and take flesh with them.

But the Brine's binding was not a fishing net. It was a cathedral of knots, centuries deep, and every thread ran through the crown.

"The crown is the cage," Astrid murmured, pouring the salt in a perfect unbroken line. "Break the cage, not the man. I hear you. Let's see if I'm any good at listening."

He set his palms flat against the stone and spoke the first name.

Not the King's name — Yew had been clear on that; names were doors, and you opened the outermost door first. This was the binding's own name, the word the original binders had used when they caged the hunger a thousand years ago, recovered from the archives at the cost of Isolde's composure and three nights of Astrid's sleep.

The word left his mouth and the whole hall shuddered.

Salt-frost crackled up the walls. Somewhere above, a chandelier shed a fine white dust that hung in the air instead of falling. And Astrid felt the binding answer him — felt it *notice* him — the way a sleeping dog notices a hand near its bone.

He pulled the first thread loose.

It came free with a sound like ice cracking on a pond in spring, and the Brine screamed.

Not aloud. Worse than aloud. It screamed *inside* — inside the wards, inside the stones, inside Astrid's own teeth, a pressure that made his vision swim. For one lurching moment he was seeing the room from everywhere at once: from the rafters, from the drains, from the frozen fountain in the courtyard, from a hundred small crystals of salt scattered through the palace like eyes.

Then it was gone, and he was kneeling on the dais with blood in his mouth, having bitten his tongue without noticing.

"Right," he panted. "You're awake. Good. Second thread."

*Little thief,* said something that was not a voice, because it had no need of voices. *Little empty boy. Do you know what you are?*

"A moth," Astrid said grimly, and pulled again.

* * *

The third thread was the hard one. Yew had warned him: bindings nested like secrets, and the innermost knots were tied through living things. To reach the Brine's anchor in the crown, he had to pass through the King — gently, gently, the way you'd move through a sickroom.

He climbed the last step and laid his hands on the King's shoulders.

Up close, the man underneath was still there. Astrid could feel him the way you feel warmth behind glass — faint, flickering, furious to survive. An old man now. A father. Someone who had once, presumably, laughed.

"Hold on," Astrid whispered. "I'm not here to kill you. Whatever the book says. I'm going to break the cage and let you out, or I'm going to die trying, and honestly at this point those are my only two plans, so."

The King's cracked lips moved. Sound scraped out of him, barely a word, barely shaped:

"...girl..."

"Isolde?" Astrid's chest did something complicated. "She's alive. She's safe. She sent me. She's been poisoning this thing through the ceremony salt for ten years — you raised a terrifying daughter, Your Majesty, and I mean that as the highest compliment."

Something happened to the King's face. It took Astrid a moment to recognize it, because it had clearly been a long time since the muscles remembered how. It was a smile. Small and ruined and real.

Astrid reached past him, into the crown, and found the anchor.

It was enormous. That was the thing nobody could have prepared him for. Crowns were the largest containers of power in the land — Yew's law — and a crown worn for a century by a god-hungry parasite was less an object than a well, a shaft going down and down into cold black water. At the bottom of it, coiled around itself, the Brine waited. It was not a monster in any shape Astrid knew. It was a *want*. Pure appetite, wearing patience like a coat.

*You cannot unmake me,* it said, and for the first time it spoke directly into his skull, warm as bathwater, kind as a lie. *No one has ever unmade hunger, little thief. They have only starved it, and starvation makes it cleverer. Look at your book. Look at your last page.*

"Don't need to," Astrid said. "I wrote it."

*Yes.* The pleasure in that word made his skin crawl. *And do you know what it says yet? Have you read your own hand?*

"I've read enough." He gripped the third thread. "Here's the part you didn't read, though. The part I haven't written yet."

He pulled.

The thread tore loose, and the world went white.

* * *

For a moment there was no hall, no throne, no night. There was only the well, and Astrid falling down it — except he wasn't falling, he was being *poured*, and the difference mattered enormously.

The Brine had let go of the King.

Astrid felt it happen the way you feel a great weight lift off a bridge — relief and catastrophe arriving in the same instant. Behind him the Salt King sagged in the throne like a puppet with its strings cut, gasping, alive, *free*, and Astrid wanted to shout with joy for him, he did —

Except the freed weight hadn't gone anywhere. It had turned around.

*The largest vessel in the room,* it said, delighted, hungry, intimate. *Did you think I hadn't noticed? A king is only power by inheritance, little thief. But you — you carry an unbinding in your hands and a death in your pocket and grief enough to fill a harbor. Do you know what you taste like?*

Astrid did the only sensible thing, which was to panic quietly and work anyway.

His hands were still on the crown. The circle of salt at his knees was broken — his fall had smeared it — but Yew's voice was steady in his memory, calm as a woman counting stitches: *When the knot pulls you, Astrid, don't pull back. Knots win pulling contests. You go slack, and you find the crossing point, and you wait.*

So he went slack.

He let the Brine pour into him.

It was like swallowing the sea. Cold filled him from the boots up — his feet went numb, his knees, his spine, a rising tide of not-Astrid climbing toward the place where Astrid kept his name. And in that rising cold he felt what the King had felt for a hundred years: the watching-from-inside, the scream with nowhere to echo, the terrible clarity of being furniture.

*There,* the Brine crooned, settling into him like a hand into a glove. *Oh. Oh, you are better than the old man. So much more room. We will keep the kingdom together, you and I. Order. Peace. Forever.*

"Yeah," Astrid thought back, through gritted non-teeth, from the small shrinking room at his own center. "Funny thing about that."

Because he had gone slack, yes. But slack isn't surrender. Slack is the fisherman's trick, the smuggler's trick, the trick every person on Thornfell mountain knows: you let the load swing, and you watch for the moment it swings *back*.

And at the bottom of his own self, at the crossing point of every thread the Brine had thrown into him, Astrid found what he was looking for.

The Brine had poured itself into a vessel that contained an unfinished unbinding.

Its threads were tangled in his. Its hunger was wrapped around his name. And his name, thanks to a prophecy written by his own future hand and a year of Yew's merciless teaching, was the one object in creation the Brine could not digest.

*Oh no,* Astrid said sweetly, into the dark inside himself. *You've swallowed the hook.*

He began to pull.

* * *

That was when the doors banged open.

Astrid couldn't turn his head. His body stood rigid on the dais, frost streaming off him in banners, and through the corner of his eye he saw figures enter — guards, witch-finders in their gray coats, and at their center, unhurried, a tall man with a ledger under one arm and twenty years of certainty on his face.

Witch-Finder Corvane.

Astrid's heart sank through the floor. Of course. Of course he'd come now, at the worst possible moment, to—

But Corvane didn't draw a weapon.

He walked up the dais steps through the drifting frost, stepped over the broken circle of salt without a glance, and studied Astrid's frozen, glowing, half-devoured body with the expression of a man inspecting a delivery.

"You're doing it wrong," Corvane said.

The words landed in the wrecked silence like a dropped plate.

"The binding," Corvane continued, conversationally, circling Astrid as if he were a statue in a gallery. "The old rite. Single vessel, single keeper, absolute authority. It fails every three generations — did you know that? Every three generations the vessel cracks, the hunger leaks, and we burn another village cleaning it up. Twenty years I've read confiscated prophecy books, boy. Twenty years I've understood what the Brine actually offers." He smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Not conquest. *Order.* One will. One keeper. No more cracks."

He raised one hand, and the witch-finders behind him knelt.

"So no," Corvane said softly. "I'm not here to stop the ritual. I'm here to finish it. Properly. Under supervision." His eyes glittered. "Hold still. This will only take the rest of your life."

Inside, in the drowning dark, Astrid felt the Brine stir with something horribly like satisfaction — and felt, beneath his terror, a smuggler's stubborn arithmetic begin to run.

Corvane wanted to control the trap. The Brine wanted to wear it. And Astrid, hanging between them by his own name, realized with a wild, giddy, terrified clarity:

They needed him whole. Both of them. Neither could finish this without the vessel staying exactly where it was.

Which meant, for the first time all night, Astrid Oyelaran had leverage.

Outside, distantly, through stone and frost and the ringing in his ears, he heard a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: a whistle, low and looping, threading up through the palace tunnels.

Marta's convoy. Coming through the walls.

Astrid closed his eyes, gripped the hook, and began, very carefully, to bargain.
