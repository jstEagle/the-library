## 19. The Last Salt

The first thing Astrid learned about being inside a binding was that it was very much like being swallowed.

Not the dramatic kind of swallowing — no teeth, no gullet, no long dark slide toward a stomach that digested you over centuries. It was more like being dipped in cold seawater that had opinions. The Brine was everywhere and nowhere, pressing against his skin from all directions at once, tasting him the way he might taste a strange coin: thoughtfully, with an air of deciding what he was worth.

*Small*, the Brine said. Its voice was not a voice so much as an ache behind his eyes, a pressure in the roots of his teeth. *You are small for such a large ambition.*

"I get that a lot," Astrid said, because terror had always made him flippant, and Yew had never managed to beat it out of him. "My master used to say I was built close to the ground so the wind couldn't take me."

*Your master is ash on a cell floor.*

The words landed like a fist. Astrid let them land, let the grief move through him the way Yew had taught him to let salt move through water — not resisting it, only choosing where it pooled. "Yes," he said quietly. "She is. And she stayed behind holding that door so I'd have time to do this. So let's not waste her time, shall we?"

Around him — though *around* was a generous word in a place that seemed to be made of pressure and pale light — the binding shimmered. He could feel it the way he felt his own heartbeat: the unbinding he had begun in the throne room, threads of salt and name and debt woven outward from his hands into the dark. Somewhere beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly near, the throne room still existed. Isolde's wards held. Corvane shouted doctrine at people who had stopped listening. And on the dais, the old King lay dying with his hand pressed flat against the crown, as if he were holding down a lid.

Astrid turned — or did the equivalent of turning, which in here meant reorienting his attention until *forward* agreed to exist — and looked at the Brine.

It had no face. That was the horror of it, and also, strangely, the pity. A hundred years of wearing kings had worn away whatever the Brine had once been, the way river stones wore smooth. What remained was appetite arranged in the shape of a hunger, vast and patient and, when he looked closely at its edges, faintly *tired*.

"You've been doing this a long time," Astrid said.

*I am older than your word for long.*

"Yeah, everyone in this story is ancient, it's very intimidating." He crouched, which was absurd, there was nothing to crouch on, but his mind insisted on giving his body habits and he'd decided long ago to humor it. "Here's my question, though. The honest one. Do you even remember wanting anything besides eating?"

The pressure around him tightened. For a moment the Brine said nothing, and the nothing was louder than anything it had said yet.

*Wanting,* it said at last, in a tone that might have been wonder in a smaller creature. *Is that what the small ones call it.*

"That's what we call it, yes." Astrid kept his voice steady. Inside, his heart was performing something ambitious and percussive. "Because I read your book. Well — the book you inspired, the one some poor terrified monk copied out a century ago. And here's the thing about you that nobody ever says out loud: every king you've eaten, you wore them like coats. And a coat doesn't get to want anything. You've spent a hundred years being worn yourself."

*Careful, unmaker.* The ache behind his eyes sharpened. *I have unmade better than you.*

"Name one," Astrid said. "Go on. One thing you've destroyed that you didn't immediately need to replace, because destroying is the only thing keeping you — " he gestured at all of it, the pale light, the pressure, the endless gray sea of itself — "*occupied*."

The Brine surged. For one white instant Astrid felt what it felt: the hollow under the crown, the centuries of sitting in another man's skull watching hands that weren't yours sign laws you couldn't read, the terrible arithmetic of a hunger that grew exactly as fast as it fed. He staggered under it. It was loneliness. It was loneliness the size of a country, and it had teeth.

He breathed. Salt on his tongue. Yew's first lesson, her last law, her voice steady in his memory even now: *Salt remembers what it was before the sea. So do you. When the magic gets big, get specific.*

"Okay," he whispered. "Okay. Specific."

And he looked down at his own hands, where the final page of the prophecy book hung glowing in the space between them — because of course it was here, of course the ending had followed him in. He hadn't needed to open the book since the archives; he knew the words by heart now, had read them so many times the ink might as well have been carved into his eyelids.

*And the vessel shall be crowned, and the crowning shall be forever, and none shall take it from him, and he shall sit alone while the world forgets his name.*

His handwriting. His own handwriting, looped and slanted the way his letters went when he wrote fast, the way they went on smuggler manifests and apology notes to Marta. He had written his own ending and mailed it a century back through whatever broken, desperate magic future-him had scraped together, and now present-him stood inside it reading the terms of service.

"There it is," Astrid said softly. "The fine print."

*You knew,* the Brine said. There was satisfaction in it, thick as brine-fat. *Some part of you knew when you began the unbinding. You cannot kill me, little thief. You can only hold me. And holding requires a vessel, and the largest vessel in this room is already opening its mouth.*

"It's not the largest anymore." Astrid lifted his chin toward the dying light that was the throne room, toward the figure on the dais. "That honor's currently disputed."

*The King is empty. I emptied him.*

"You emptied his *crown*. Turns out those are different things." Astrid stood. His legs shook. He let them. Fear wasn't the enemy; fear was just information with bad manners. "That's the flaw in your whole arrangement, you know. You keep pouring yourself into the biggest container you can find, because big containers hold more. But a crown isn't just a container. It's a claim. It reaches down bloodlines like a root and it says *this power belongs to whoever sits here, forever, by right*. You've been living in real estate. And real estate has deeds."

*You are babbling.*

"I'm *negotiating*," Astrid said, "with a deadline," and reached for the binding.

* * *

From inside, the unbinding looked nothing like magic looked from outside.

Outside — in the practice yard, in Yew's cellar lessons, on the night he'd unwound the possession from the hound and nearly lost his own name doing it — magic had been threads, visible and tuggable, knots to be found and loosened. Inside the binding, it was architecture. The whole structure of the Brine's imprisonment-in-waiting rose around him like a cathedral made of debt: who owed what, to whom, since when, sealed in salt. And at the center of it, load-bearing, ugly, and absolutely essential, sat the clause. The vessel clause. The forever clause.

*Alone,* the Brine whispered, curling around the clause like a cat around a warm hearth. *You will sit alone. As I have sat alone. As the king sat alone. It is the shape of power. It does not come in any other shape.*

"That," said Astrid, "is where you and I disagree, and where a hundred years of kings disagreed with you too, they just never survived long enough to say so."

He put his hands on the clause.

It burned. Of course it burned. Everything true in binding-magic burned; Yew had called it the receipt. He could feel the whole cathedral leaning on his hands, could feel the weight of the words he himself had written, and beneath the weight, the trap inside the trap snapping shut exactly as designed — by him, for him, with love, presumably, from some future self who had run out of better ideas.

"Hey," Astrid said, not to the Brine. To the light. To the far end of the binding, where the throne room flickered like a lamp behind cloth. "Your Majesty. If you can hear me. It's time."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the light changed.

* * *

The King came into the binding the way an old man enters a cold room: slowly, deliberately, having decided in advance that he would not complain about it.

Astrid felt him before he saw him — a presence like a candle carried carefully through wind. Not large. Not powerful, not anymore, not in any way the Brine would measure. But *specific*, in Yew's sense of the word. A man who remembered what he was before the sea.

He was smaller than his portraits. His beard had gone the color of old frost, and his eyes — when they focused, which took effort, which cost him — were brown and kind and appallingly tired. The crown hovered above his head rather than resting on it, the way a yoke hovers over an ox, and Astrid realized with a lurch that the King had been holding it up. All these years. Holding the crown up so it wouldn't quite touch him. Spending everything he had left on that one small rebellion, decade after decade, and no one had ever known.

"Boy," the King said. His voice was gravel and dust and, underneath, astonishingly dry humor. "You're the one making all that racket in my wards."

"Sorry about the wards."

"Don't be. Best noise they've made in twenty years." The King looked past Astrid at the Brine, and something moved across his ruined face that was not fear. It took Astrid a moment to identify it, and when he did, his throat closed. It was recognition. The look of a man greeting a cellmate he'd shared a wall with for thirty years.

"You're aware," Astrid said hoarsely. "You've been aware. This whole time."

"Every day." The King smiled, and it cracked him like old paint. "Every single day, watching it wear my face to sign treaties. Do you know how humiliating it is to watch yourself give a speech? I gave *terrible* speeches. It has my cadence all wrong." He paused. "The message in the salt. That was mine. Took me four months to push three words through. Felt like passing a kidney stone made of language."

"The crown is the cage," Astrid quoted. "Break the cage, not the man."

"Mm. And have you worked out how?" The King turned his tired eyes on the glowing clause. "Because I've had thirty years to think about it, lying in the dark of my own head, and I'll tell you what I concluded. You can't break the cage by force. Force feeds it. Every binder who tried to smash the vessel clause died, and their deaths went into the pot, and the Brine got stronger. No." He raised one trembling hand. "You don't break a cage. You *amend the deed*."

"The crown's claim—"

"—reaches down bloodlines. Yes. Whoever wears it, inherits the duty. Father to son to daughter, forever, whether they choose it or not. That's the trap. That's why the Brine loves thrones." The King's hand closed, shaking, into a fist. "But a claim is only as strong as its wording. And I am — " he laughed, wet and thin — "I am, as of roughly an hour ago, the last legal authority on the wording. Dying kings may spend their will any way they like. It's the one privilege left to us."

"Your Majesty." Astrid's voice came out strange. "What you're describing — spending your last will to rewrite the claim — that's everything. Whatever's left of you."

"Yes," said the King, pleasantly. "Isn't it marvelous? I've been saving up."

*No,* said the Brine, and for the first time in perhaps a century, the hunger sounded afraid. *Old man. Hollow thing. You have nothing left to spend.*

"I have thirty years of saved-up spite," the King said, "and the finest legal education in the realm, which you ate around but never through, because you needed my signature." He stepped forward, one foot, then another, each step visibly costing him, and pressed his palm against the vessel clause beside Astrid's hands. "Boy. Your name."

"Astrid Oyelaran."

"Astrid Oyelaran. Witness me." The old King's eyes glittered, fierce and alive and entirely, unmistakably *his own*. "Let the record show that Aldric the Third, by grace of absolutely nobody, wearer of a hat I have hated since childhood, hereby amends the deed."

He spoke the amendment.

Astrid felt it happen the way you feel a great tree fall — through the soles of your feet, through the roots of your teeth, through the whole standing weight of the world adjusting itself around a new absence. The crown's claim tore free of bloodlines with a sound like ice releasing a shoreline. Forever became *for as long as chosen*. Inherited became *accepted*. And *alone* —

*Alone* dissolved.

In its place, written in the King's last strength and Astrid's own handwriting and salt older than both of them, a single new condition settled into the deed like a keystone:

*The vessel shall be borne by the willing, and the bearing shall be shared, and no keeper shall carry alone.*

The crown above the King's head stopped hovering.

It simply — opened. Unclasped itself along a seam no jeweler had ever found, and fell, not as metal but as meaning, dissolving into grains of light that drifted down through the binding like the world's slowest, most dignified snowfall. The cage was gone. What remained was portable. A choice, folded small enough to fit in a human hand.

The King sagged. Astrid caught him, or the idea of catching him, arms full of dying light.

"Thank you," Astrid said, and found he was crying, and found he didn't care. "You didn't have to—"

"I absolutely had to. I've been *bored*." The old man's smile was fading at the edges, going soft as sea-glass, but his eyes stayed sharp to the last. "Listen to me, unmaker. It will come for you now. Without a throne to pour into, it will take the biggest willing vessel in reach, and that's you, and it will hurt, and you will be tempted to bear it alone because you've been practicing lonely since you were small enough to hide in doorways." His grip tightened with the last of his strength. "Don't. Whatever you do — however it ends — don't write *alone* back into the page. Promise an old boring man."

"I promise," Astrid said.

"Good lad." The King exhaled. It sounded like relief. It sounded like a tide finally going out after thirty years of holding. "Now run along and be devoured. Politely."

And he was gone — not violently, not with any drama at all, just a candle carried out of a cold room by someone who had decided, decades ago, exactly how he wanted to leave it.

* * *

The Brine hit Astrid like the sea hitting a breakwater.

He had expected rage. What came was worse: *gratitude*, vast and starving and utterly sincere, the hunger pouring toward him with something like joy, crying *willing, willing, the willing one, at last* as it flooded into the space where the clause used to hold it. The binding caught it — barely, groaning, the amended deed flexing like a sail in weather it was built for but had never met — and funneled it, and Astrid stood at the mouth of the funnel with his arms spread and his teeth gritted and let the ocean try to become a man.

It hurt the way birth hurts, probably, he thought wildly. It hurt the way every ending hurts that is also a beginning. The Brine filled him to the brim and kept coming, and somewhere in the roar he heard it say, almost tenderly, *alone, alone, we will be alone together forever* —

"No," Astrid said through the flood. "Actually. About that."

Because he had heard it. Underneath the roar, underneath his own cracking resolve, he had heard it the moment the King finished speaking — footsteps, many of them, running, and a whistle he'd know anywhere, the convoy's three-note signal that meant *coming, hold on, family inbound*. Marta's whistle. Isolde's battle-yell, which she would deny having. Even, high and fierce, a child's voice shouting a word that was probably a curse she'd learned from smugglers.

They were coming into the binding after him.

The prophecy had accounted for everything — the crown, the cage, the vessel, the forever, the alone. Written in his own hand, sealed a century deep, airtight as a tomb.

It had not accounted for anyone bringing friends.

"Hold that thought," Astrid told the Brine, as the light of the throne room burst open behind him and five sets of footsteps crossed the threshold of the ending. "We're expecting company. And they're rewriting the page."
