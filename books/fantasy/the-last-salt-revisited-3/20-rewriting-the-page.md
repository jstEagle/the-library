## 20. Rewriting the Page

The Brine did not pour so much as it *arrived*, the way winter arrives — all at once and having been coming for a long time.

Astrid stood in the ruin of the throne room with salt to his knees and the crown's shattered claim still ringing in his ears like a struck bell, and he felt himself being filled. Not with water. With *wanting*. The Brine had spent a century eating kings because kings were the largest vessels in the land, and now the dying man on the throne had spent his last will unmaking that arithmetic, and the largest vessel in the room was a runaway apprentice from Thornfell who had once made himself invisible for three days straight just to avoid a scolding.

*Oh,* said the Brine, in a voice like tide against a hull. *Oh, you are roomy.*

"That's," Astrid managed, "deeply rude."

The hunger laughed. It had a hundred years of laughter stored up, and none of it was kind.

He could feel what it felt. That was the worst part. The Brine was not cruelty; cruelty would have been simpler. It was appetite, pure and patient and *lonely*, a thing that had been poured into vessel after vessel and had never once been asked how *it* felt about any of this. It ate because eating was all it knew. It wore kings because crowns were containers, and containers were the only shape of company it had ever been offered.

And it was already learning him. He felt it brush against his memories the way a tongue finds a cracked tooth: Yew at her workbench, laughing at her own jokes before she finished them. Marta's hand heavy and warm on the crown of his head. The waystation book open on his knees, his own name in century-old ink. The taste of salt on his tongue that wasn't grief, not yet, but might be soon.

*You will be a good cage,* the Brine murmured. *Better than the old one. He fought me for forty years. You'll last longer.*

"Flattery," Astrid said through his teeth. His fingers were whitening at the tips, frost creeping up his knuckles, and somewhere beneath the flood he could feel the binding taking hold — the one Yew had taught him, the one the prophecy demanded, the one that ended with an uncrowned king alone in a room forever. *Someone must wear the crown as its vessel.* The final page. His own handwriting, waiting at the bottom of a century-old book like a trap he'd set for himself and forgotten.

He reached for the binding anyway. Because the alternative was the Brine spilling out into the land, and because Yew had died holding a door so that he could stand here, and because — gods help him — some stubborn smuggler-trained piece of him refused to die owing anyone anything.

That was when the doors banged open.

Not the great doors. The little door behind the dais, the servants' door, the one Isolde had shown him on their second night in the capital with the offhand air of a woman who had spent ten years memorizing every secret her prison held.

Marta came through it first, which figured. She had a smuggler's cutlass in one fist and blood on her sleeve that Astrid prayed was not hers, and behind her came Isolde with her skirts hiked up and salt-frost melting off them in streams, and behind *her* —

Behind her came Rilla, gray-faced and shaking, with Nell perched on her hip like the child weighed nothing at all, which Astrid knew for a fact was a lie, because Nell had eaten half the convoy's honey-cakes on the road south and had the grip strength of a bear trap.

"You," said Marta, surveying the throne room — the frost, the dying king, the boy standing in the middle of it glowing faintly at the edges like a lamp about to go out — "look terrible."

"I'm being devoured by an ancient hunger," Astrid said.

"Well, don't make excuses. Isolde! Tell me what to break."

"There's nothing to break." Isolde was already wading toward him, frost climbing her shins and falling away again where her own small poison-work — ten years of bitter salts slipped into ceremonial bowls — gave her some tiny immunity. Her eyes were wide and fierce and wet. "There's nothing left to break. There's only the binding. And the binding wants—"

"One vessel. I know. I've read the ending." Astrid laughed, and it came out wrong, crackling, two voices in one throat. "I *wrote* the ending. Apparently."

"Then we're here to spoil it," said Rilla.

Everyone turned to look at her. Rilla, who had sold their route to Corvane for her daughter's life. Rilla, who had knelt in the snow outside the witch-finders' cells while Astrid put Nell into her arms and said, without a flicker of judgment, *go home, you're done running.* She shifted the girl on her hip and lifted her chin, and there was something in her face Astrid had never seen there before, because it had been buried under guilt for months.

"The book says one name," Rilla said. "One keeper, alone forever. That's what Corvane believes. That's what the Brine expects. It's what *you* expect." She swallowed. "But I've been thinking. On the road. About cages."

"Rilla," Astrid started, "you can't — the binding takes a keeper whole, it eats everything you are, I watched it nearly take my *name*—"

"I know what it takes." Her voice didn't shake. Much. "You told us. In the tunnels. You talk too much when you're scared, did you know that?" A wet, ragged ghost of a smile. "It takes a whole person because one person has to hold all of it. All the hunger, all the weight, all the *alone*. But—" she hoisted Nell higher, "—when the cells opened and there were too many of us to carry out and I thought we'd all die in there, do you know what happened? We linked arms. Sixteen people, one chain, and the dark couldn't pull any single one of us loose. Because you can't drag away a link that's holding and being held."

Astrid felt the Brine go very still inside him. It had noticed something too.

Isolde had gone pale in a different way — the way of a scholar watching an impossible sum come out right. "The binding is a container," she breathed. "Yew's first law. Salt, names, debts — a binding is whatever shape you give it. The prophecy assumed a crown because crowns are what bindings have always been. One head. One bearer. But if the vessel isn't shaped like a crown—"

"—shaped like a *chain*," Marta finished flatly. "Then it needs links." She looked around at all of them, and then at Astrid, and her expression did something complicated and tender and furious all at once. "Boy. You were going to do this alone."

"It was sort of the whole point of the—"

"Don't." One word, and it landed like an anchor. "Don't you dare. You sat at my table for six years. You fixed my boots. You taught Tammy to read and lied beautifully about whose idea the honey theft was. You think we raised you just to watch you walk into a cage by yourself?" She spat — an old smuggler's oath, the foulest blessing she knew. "We're your family, and family means nobody carries the heavy end alone. Now tell me where to sign."

Astrid wanted to laugh. He wanted to weep. He did both, and the Brine flinched from the sound of it, because grief and joy braided together made a noise it had no shelf for.

"It's not signing," he said, when he could speak. "It's — names. The binding runs on names. Mine anchors it, but if — if you give yours freely, knowingly—" He groped for Yew's voice, for her lessons, for the hound he had unbound in the cellar and the terrible vertigo of almost losing *Astrid* to the unmaking. "It has to be given, not taken. That's the only law the Brine can't break. It can eat power. It can't eat a gift."

"Then we're generous people," said Isolde.

She knelt first, right there in the frost, princess of a hollow court, and took his frost-white hand in both of hers. "Isolde Veyra Saltborn," she said clearly, "who poisoned her father's cage for ten years because no one would tell me the truth, gives her name to this work. Freely. Knowing the cost." Her eyes came up and caught his. "Whatever the cost turns out to be. Even if it's monthly meetings in a damp cellar for the rest of my life. Even if it's *committees*."

"Gods," Marta muttered. "She means it. She'd sit through committees."

Marta knelt beside her, joints cracking like green wood, and gripped Astrid's hand so hard his fingers creaked. "Marta Oyelaran's-daughter-was-I, called the Mule of Thornfell, called worse things by better men than deserve the breath. I give my name. Freely." A pause. Her voice roughened. "Knowing full well that if this hurts you, boy, I will find whatever's left of this Brine after and haunt it personally."

"Marta," Astrid whispered, "the prophecy didn't account for you. None of it accounted for you. It was written by a lonely fool in a tower who thought love was a variable that always came out zero—"

"Then the lonely fool," said Marta, "was bad at sums."

Rilla came last, Nell on her hip, and for a moment she just stood there, and Astrid saw her deciding — really deciding, the same way she'd decided in the snow, the same way everyone in this cursed beautiful story kept deciding against every sensible instinct.

"Nell," Rilla said quietly, to her daughter, not to the room. "Do you remember what I told you about promises?"

Nell, four years old and solemn as a magistrate, nodded. "*Promises is heavy. You share 'em or they squish you.*"

"That's right." Rilla kissed the top of her head, set her down — and Nell immediately grabbed Astrid's frost-white hand with both of hers, because children have no sense whatsoever of dramatic timing, and also because Nell had decided months ago, on the strength of honey-cakes and piggyback rides, that Astrid belonged to her. "Careful, love. Cold hands."

"*Warm heart*," Nell corrected severely, and did not let go.

So it was a child's small warm grip that anchored the circle when Rilla knelt and gave her name — Rilla Marsh, called Sparrow, called traitor, called forgiven — freely, knowing the cost, and the circle closed.

Five names. Five links.

Astrid spoke the binding.

He had expected it to be like the cellar, like the hound — a fight, a tearing, the sensation of being unwritten. Instead the words went out of him like water finding its level, and the Brine rose up in him roaring—

—and found five doors where it had expected one.

It threw itself against Astrid's name and Marta's met it there, stubborn as a mountain pass in winter. It surged toward Isolde and found ten years of quiet poison-work, a patience like bedrock. It tried Rilla and found a mother's guilt transmuted, god help them all, into *load-bearing devotion*. It probed at the smallest link, the child, and recoiled as if burned — not from weakness but from the sheer uncomplicated ferocity of a four-year-old's affection, which turned out to be the one substance in creation the Brine had no teeth for.

*What,* it said, and for the first time in a hundred years it sounded small. *What is this shape.*

"A chorus," Astrid said.

And the strangest thing happened. The Brine, pressed into five vessels instead of one, diluted past any single point of failure, *sighed*. Astrid felt it — felt the hunger settle into them like silt settling in still water, felt the frost recede from his fingertips, felt something vast and ancient and terribly tired curl up in the shared space they'd built for it like a stray finally allowed indoors.

It had never been offered company that wasn't a meal. It had never been *shared*.

The frost fell from the walls in sheets. The white bloom on the windows melted to clear glass and ordinary moonlight. And on the throne, the Salt King — old, emptied, nine-tenths gone — opened eyes gone milky with centuries of salt and looked at the five of them kneeling in a circle on his floor, and smiled.

It was a terrible smile, cracked and crusted. It was the most beautiful thing Astrid had ever seen.

"The crown," the King rasped, each word costing him visibly, "is broken. Good. Good." His gaze found Astrid. "You didn't kill it."

"No," Astrid said. "I'm sorry. The page said—"

"The page," said the King, with the ghost of dry humor that must once, a lifetime ago, have made courtiers laugh, "was written by someone who'd never had friends. I remember the feeling." His hand lifted, trembling, and pointed at the crown lying dead on the dais steps — just gold now, just metal, its claim on bloodlines shattered. "Burn it. Smugglers like fires, I'm told."

"Oh," said Marta, rising with her joints crackling, "now *that* I can arrange."

They burned it in the great hearth while the King watched, and Isolde held her father's hand, and Nell insisted on throwing in one stick, and the fire turned strange colors for a moment — green and silver and one brief impossible flash of blue — and then it was only a fire, and only metal, melting.

Afterward the King slept, really slept, for the first time in years, and his breathing was slow and human and did not fog the windows white.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, in a palace suddenly full of smugglers arguing cheerfully about whether the wine cellar counted as salvage — Astrid sat on the throne room steps with the prophecy book open on his knees.

The pages were blank now. Every prediction, every dread date, every line of doom: gone, dissolved like salt in warm water, as though the book had only ever been a letter waiting to be delivered. All except the final page.

His own handwriting still waited there. He'd dreaded it for weeks — the sentence that had sentenced him, the future reaching back to close its fist. He made himself look.

*and the unmaker made instead,* it read.

But beneath it, in ink that was still drying, fresh as the hour:

*—unless the page is wrong. It was wrong. Five names. Ask them.*

Astrid stared at it for a long moment. Then he laughed — a real laugh, rusty and disbelieving, startling a pair of looting smugglers on the far side of the hall.

The handwriting wasn't his.

Well. It *was* his — the slant of it, the cramped e's, the way he crossed his t's like he was stabbing them. But it wasn't the handwriting of the man who'd written the rest of the book, the lonely tower-fool who'd believed love summed to zero. This was newer. This was *after*. Which meant somewhere ahead of him — a year? a decade? — he was going to sit down with this blank book and write back across time to a frightened boy in a ruined waystation, and the first thing future-him had chosen to say was *ask them*.

"Heavy reading?" Isolde dropped down beside him, passing him a cup of the salvaged wine. "You look like you've seen a ghost with excellent penmanship."

"Something like that." He tilted the book so she could see, and watched her eyebrows climb. "The last page changed. Or — I suppose it hasn't happened yet. Whichever way you slice it, apparently I get the last word after all."

Isolde studied it, then bumped her shoulder against his. "Good. Someone in this family should."

From across the hall, Marta's voice boomed over a dispute about candlesticks: "BOY! When you're done communing with your spooky book — we've got a Brine to meet with monthly and a *schedule* to argue about. First council session's at dawn, and I intend to be insufferable at it."

Dawn. The first renewal of the binding. Five names, five voices, a cage that was learning to sing.

Astrid closed the book, and held it against his chest, and went home to the argument.
