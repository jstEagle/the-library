## 21. The Uncrowned

The salt cellar beneath the ruined waystation had once stored contraband brandy, and Marta insisted this was the only reason the binding worked.

"Nothing evil survives in a room that's smelled of good smuggled liquor for a hundred years," she said, setting the fifth candle with her boot heel. "It knows it's outclassed."

Astrid smiled the way he'd learned to smile on these nights — tired, grateful, a little afraid — and opened the book to its final page.

One year since the throne room. One year since the Brine had poured into him like cold water filling a cup, and then kept pouring, and then — impossibly — stopped, because five other voices had spoken his name back at him and refused to let him hold it alone. One year of monthly meetings in cellars and smuggler dens and once, memorably, a hayloft while a thunderstorm tried very hard to kill them all.

The page read differently now than it had that first night in this same ruined room, when he'd been a runaway with invisibility as his only talent and dread as his only companion. The old prophecy was still there in his own handwriting, the terrible letter from his future self:

*And the unmaker shall take the crown, and wear it alone, forever.*

But beneath it, in five different hands, crowded and overlapping like guests at a table set for more than expected:

*Not alone.*

*Not alone.*

*Not alone.*

*Not alone.*

*Not alone.*

Marta's hand was blunt and certain. Isolde's was elegant, small letters, a princess trained to take up as little space as possible. Sera's — the traitor who had become something better than loyal, because loyalty that survived betrayal was forged, not found — slanted hard to the left, impatient even on paper. Little Wren had signed hers in a wobbly scrawl two years her senior, because she had argued, with devastating six-year-old logic, that if she'd been bait once she got to be a keeper too. And Astrid's own name sat first among them, not as a king but as one voice among five.

He touched each name in turn. Salt rose to meet his fingertips, warm as skin.

"All right," he said. "Everyone ready?"

"Been ready since noon," said Sera. "Some of us have jobs."

"Some of us," Marta corrected, "have *empires*. Yours is a stall."

"My stall feeds half the river district, thank you."

Isolde laughed — quietly, always quietly, though less quietly than she used to — and took Astrid's left hand. Marta took his right. Sera clasped Marta's free hand, Wren scrambled into Sera's lap and seized whatever fingers were available, and the circle closed.

The Brine came up to meet them, as it did every month: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of deep water, an ancient hunger turning over in its sleep and finding itself, once again, uncomfortably full. For a moment Astrid felt it press against the walls of them — five hearts, five names, a cage made of chorus — and felt it test the bars the way a prisoner tests them, without hope, almost without malice.

*You could be so much,* it whispered, in a voice like tide over drowned things. *You could be one.*

"We could be five," Astrid answered, and spoke the words Yew had taught him, the words she'd died holding a door open for:

"Salt remembers. Names divide. Debts are shared."

The candle flames bent inward, steadied, and stood up straight. The pressure receded, sullen, diminished by exactly the amount it was diminished every month — which was to say, measurably. Yew's notes, which Astrid now kept in a leather case against his heart, had charted its decline. At this rate, in thirty years the Brine would be a whisper. In fifty, perhaps a memory. In a hundred —

"In a hundred years," Marta said, when the candles guttered out and everyone exhaled, "some other poor fool finds a book in a ruin and we do this all over again."

"Not if I have anything to say about it," Astrid said.

"You won't," said Wren comfortably. "You'll be dead. That's what a hundred years means."

"Wren."

"What? Sera explained it."

Sera examined the ceiling with great interest.

They packed up the candles and the salt and the book — Astrid carried it now; he had stopped being afraid of carrying it somewhere around the fourth month — and climbed the broken stair into evening light. The waystation looked much as it had that first night: roofless, wall-less, a skeleton of a building on the smuggler road through the Thornfells. But someone had planted flowers in the foundation stones. Sera's doing, though she'd deny it. Purple ones, stubborn, growing out of rubble.

"I still say we should meet somewhere with a roof," Marta grumbled.

"The book was found here," Astrid said. "Yew said bindings like coming home."

"Yew," said Marta, "liked dramatic nonsense." But she said it gently, the way you say the names of dead friends, and she put her arm around Astrid's shoulders as they walked down toward the convoy camp, where the smell of stew and the sound of off-key whistling promised that some things, at least, would never change.

* * *

Corvane walked out of the capital on a Tuesday, because he believed Tuesdays were honest days.

That was the sort of man he had been: a man who believed in honest days, honest ledgers, honest hierarchies. Twenty years he had served the witch-finders' office, twenty years reading confiscated prophecy books by lamplight, and he had never once doubted that order was the highest good a man could serve. He had fed minor binders to the Brine believing he was feeding peace. He had locked the city gates believing he was saving it.

Then he had stood in a shattered throne room and watched five people — a smuggler matriarch with dirt under her nails, a disgraced princess, a traitor, a child, and a boy who should have been nothing — share a burden that doctrine said only a crown could carry, and every ledger in his soul had failed to balance.

He did not rage. Rage was for men who thought they'd been lied to. Corvane understood that he had lied to himself, carefully, for twenty years, and that discovery deserved a quieter response.

He took only three things from the witch-finders' headquarters. The first was his cloak, because the mountains were cold. The second was a letter of resignation he left on his own desk, addressed to no one, because there was no longer anyone with authority to receive it — Isolde had seen to that.

The third was the ledger.

Twenty years of confiscated prophecy books were recorded in it: titles, dates, marginalia, the fates of their readers. It was, he understood now, a catalogue of everything he had gotten wrong. A sensible man would burn it.

Corvane had spent his whole life being told he was sensible. He tucked it under his arm and walked north, into the Thornfells, where the smuggler roads wound like veins through the stone. If shared power could unmake what he had built, then he wanted to understand it — not as a believer, not anymore, but as a scholar. He would read every book in that ledger again, in the places they were written, and this time he would pay attention to the margins.

On the third day out of the city, a convoy passed him on the high pass — salt traders, by their wagons, flying no colors and therefore every color. A woman with gray braid called out to him.

"You look like a man walking away from something heavy."

"I am."

"Got room in your pack for bread? We've too much, and my crew eats like gulls."

It was, he would reflect later, the first act of unearned kindness anyone had offered him in twenty years, and it came from the very class of person his doctrine had called chaos. He accepted the bread. He ate it sitting on a milestone, and it was good, and he could not finish it, and a smuggler boy laughed at him without malice and finished it for him.

Corvane wrote that night, in the ledger's margins, in a hand that shook only slightly:

*Day three. The theory of necessary cruelty does not survive contact with free bread. Further study required.*

* * *

Princess Isolde — no, she corrected herself daily, *councilor* Isolde, though the court couldn't seem to stop curtsying — stood before the empty throne and told another delegation that no, really, she wasn't going to sit in it.

"But Your Highness," said the ambassador from the coastal leagues, wringing his hat. "The throne is *there*. You are *here*. The arithmetic is distressing."

"The arithmetic," Isolde said, "is precisely why the throne must stay empty. My father wore it alone. The thing in the crown grew fat on solitude. Every king before him wore it alone, and every one of them was eaten a little. I decline to be dinner."

She gestured instead to the long table that now dominated the great hall — plain oak, scandalously unadorned, thirteen chairs. "The council sits here. Nine seats for the provinces, two for the guilds, one for the smuggler roads, because apparently they've been running half the country's actual commerce for centuries and it's time someone admitted it."

"And the thirteenth?"

"Empty," Isolde said. "For whoever comes next that we haven't thought to invite yet. There's always someone."

The ambassador looked at the throne — tall, gold-veined white stone, gathering dust — and then at the table, and Isolde watched him perform the same calculation she'd watched forty delegations perform. Power was a habit. People reached for crowns the way hands reached for railings in the dark.

Her father had been the hardest part. He lived now in a sunlit room in the east wing with nurses who adored him, a man mostly hollowed out, mostly quiet, but present in a way he hadn't been in years — as if the removal of the Brine had let a little of *him* seep back into the vacancy. Some days he knew her. On those days he held her hand and wept, though not salt anymore, just ordinary human tears, and told her he was sorry, and she told him there was nothing to forgive, and both of them knew it wasn't entirely true and said it anyway, because some mercies are meant to be spoken rather than earned.

On the best days, he asked about the council. He had been a king once, before the hunger, and something in him recognized what she was building.

"Thirteen chairs," he'd murmured last week. "Your grandmother tried for seven. They called her mad."

"They called her a lot of things," Isolde agreed. "I'm having her portrait hung in the council chamber. Over the empty chair."

Today, after the ambassador finally bowed his way out — unconvinced, but outnumbered by nine provinces' worth of signatures — Isolde stood alone in the great hall a moment. Dust motes turned in the light from the high windows. The throne waited, patient as stone, patient as hunger.

She picked up the ceremonial salt cellar from beside it — the vessel through which she'd poisoned the Brine by grains for ten lonely years — and carried it out under her arm like a laundry ticket.

There was work to do. Councils didn't run themselves, though Wren had offered.

* * *

The last meeting of the year happened in the hayloft after all, because December in the Thornfells was not to be trifled with, and Marta declared that any ritual requiring frostbite was a ritual designed by someone who hated joy.

They renewed the binding by lantern-light, five hands and five names, and the whisper of the Brine was smaller than it had ever been — a tide far out, a hunger dreaming of a table it would never sit at again. When it was done, they stayed, because they always stayed. Marta produced mulled wine from somewhere structurally impossible. Wren fell asleep against Sera's shoulder mid-sentence, defending her right to remain awake. Isolde told a story about the ambassador and the hat that made even Sera laugh.

Astrid sat a little apart, the book open on his knees, and read the final page by lantern glow.

Five names. Five *not alones.* And below them now, added over the months in visits and messages, a sixth line in careful, scholarly script — Corvane had sent it by post, of all things, along with a page of his marginal studies and a request for correction that Astrid had answered twice already:

*The cage is strongest where it is widest. — C., studying.*

And below that, in wobbly capitals, Wren's permanent addition: *AND ALSO THE BRINE IS A COWARD.*

"It's going to be the strangest book anyone ever finds," Isolde observed, settling beside him. "A hundred years from now. Some apprentice hiding in a ruin, and they find this, and it starts as a horror story and ends as a guest list."

"That's most stories," Marta said from across the loft. "People just don't write down the second part."

Astrid closed the book and held it a moment, feeling its weight — the weight of a future he'd written and then, with help, unwritten; the weight of a teacher who'd held a door; the weight of a name that belonged to four other people now, which meant it finally, properly, belonged to him.

Outside, snow was beginning to fall on the Thornfell passes, softening the smuggler roads into silence. Somewhere down the valley, the convoy's watchman began to whistle the old route-song, the one that meant *all safe, come home.*

Astrid stood, brushed straw from his coat, and went home.
