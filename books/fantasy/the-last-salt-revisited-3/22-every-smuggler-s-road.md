## 22. Every Smuggler's Road

The pass had not gotten any kinder in a year.

Astrid stood at the mouth of the Thornfell high road with the wind pushing at his back like an impatient hand and thought: *good*. It shouldn't be kind. Kind roads bred careless travelers, and careless travelers ended up in ditches, or worse, in ledgers. The mountain had teeth, and he loved it for that. You could trust a thing with teeth to be honest about what it was.

He'd come up alone this time — well, mostly alone. A mule named Duchess plodded behind him under a load of salt, which still made him laugh when he let himself think about it. Salt. He hauled salt now. Five keepers in the land, and one of them spent his springs ferrying the stuff over passes on a mule's back like any other smuggler.

Duchess stopped beside him and breathed warm against his neck, smelling of hay and opinion.

"Yes, we're nearly there," Astrid told her. "No, you don't get extra oats for sulking."

She flicked an ear, unconvinced.

He shouldered the lead rope and started down. His boots knew the way without him. That was the strange gift of this year — not power, exactly, though there was plenty of that coiled quiet in his chest where the Brine slept its distributed sleep. The gift was *knowledge*, the body's own kind. Where the loose stone sat on the switchback below Widow's Ledge. Which stretch of scree sang underfoot and which kept silent. Where Yew had once crouched, seven years ago, and pressed two fingers to his shoulder and said, *you walk loud for an invisible boy.*

He still missed her in sudden ambushes. A smell of ink. A certain slant of light through pines. He'd learned to let the grief arrive, tip its hat, and move along, rather than fighting it in the doorway. Isolde had taught him that, or tried to; she was better at it than he was. Marta's method was simpler — grief got a cup of tea and then it got put to work.

The book rode against his ribs in its oilskin wrap, as it always did. Blank now. Every page drained white as frost, except the last, where five lines crowded together in five different hands above the single line beneath them, written small and sure:

*and the unmaker made instead.*

He took it out sometimes at night, by firelight, and read nothing at all. It was the best book he'd ever owned. Not a word of prophecy left in it. Whatever else the Brine had taken from the world, it couldn't have this one back.

* * *

The waystation ruin came up on his left just past midday, its walls softened by another year of moss. Astrid didn't need to stop. He stopped anyway.

This was where it had all turned, though "turned" was too gentle a word for what happened here. Here he had opened a dead man's book and read his own name in a century-old sentence and felt the world tilt beneath him like a deck in weather. Here he had learned that invisibility — his one talent, his one armor — had quietly stopped working, because you cannot hide from a story that has already found you.

He crouched by the hearthstone out of habit. Nothing there but ash older than him and a mouse skeleton arranged with some dignity. But he touched the stone anyway, the way you touch a doorframe you were married under.

"I know," he said to it. "I know."

Because the truth — the truth he'd only been able to say aloud in the last few months, usually to Duchess, who kept secrets better than most priests — was this: he had never wanted to be invisible. He'd wanted to be *unseen*, which is a different hunger entirely. Unseen until someone chooses to see you. He'd spent seven years as Yew's shadow, four years as Marta's ghost on the convoy rolls, perfecting a talent for vanishing, and every hour of it he'd been waiting. Standing very still in the dark of himself, waiting for someone to come find him.

Nobody had come. So eventually, catastrophically, a book had.

He laughed a little at that, breath smoking in the cold, and swung back up into the wind. The road bent north toward the valley, and somewhere beyond the bend, if his nose wasn't lying, someone was frying onions.

* * *

He heard the convoy before he saw it — he always did now, though he pretended otherwise, because hearing things three switchbacks early was one of the few perks of sharing your chest cavity with an ancient hunger and he intended to enjoy it.

The whistle came first. Three notes rising, two falling — Marta's signal, unchanged since before he'd joined her crew, meaning *road clear, come in honest*. Then wheels on stone, the creak and complaint of a dozen loads, the low argumentative murmur of mules, and threaded through all of it, voices he knew the way he knew his own pulse.

They came around the bend in their usual beautiful disarray. Tam and Oskar walking the wheel-mules and arguing about dice debts from a game last autumn. Fen perched atop the second wagon with her crossbow across her knees and a look that said she'd counted every bird on the ridge twice. And at the head, riding the lead wagon like a figurehead, Marta Vos — greyer at the temples than a year ago, broader through the shoulders, because grief and rescue work both built muscle — scanning the road ahead with eyes that missed less than a witch-finder's ledger ever had.

Her gaze found him and stopped.

Astrid raised one hand. Just that. No hiding, no slipping off the road into the bracken the way he'd done his first two years with the convoy, materializing at the fire already seated as though he'd always been there. These days he walked straight up the middle of the road like a man who paid taxes, or would if smugglers did.

"Well," Marta said, reining in. "Look what the mountain coughed up."

"The mountain and I have an arrangement."

"Does the arrangement involve those onions I'm smelling, or are you going to stand there being poetic while they burn?"

"They're your onions, and yes, they're burning."

"Then get up here," she said, "before Oskar eats my share out of spite."

Oskar, twenty feet back, looked deeply offended at a slander he absolutely deserved.

Astrid handed Duchess's rope to Tam — who greeted the mule by name, because everyone in this family greeted everything by name, dogs, mules, favorite boulders — and swung up onto the wagon bench beside Marta. She looked at him sidelong for the length of a switchback. He'd learned to read her silences the way other scholars read archives. This one said: *you're thinner, you're sleeping badly again, you're smiling anyway, good.*

"You've been to the waystation," she said finally.

"How could you possibly—"

"There's moss on your knee. Only one place on this road has that moss, and only one fool kneels in ruins to say hello to hearthstones." She clicked her tongue at the mule. "Say hello to it from me next time. It kept us dry the night Corvane's riders passed, and I pay my debts."

"I'll tell it."

"Do." A pause. Wheels turning, mules complaining, the whole long music of the road. Then, quieter, in the voice she used perhaps four times a year: "How is it? The carrying."

Astrid considered the question honestly, because Marta could smell a dishonest answer at forty paces and had once made a man eat his own dishonest answer, metaphorically, over a very long supper.

"It's quiet," he said. "Quieter than it was. We meet on the dark of each month, all five of us, and we sing the binding new — you know that part. What nobody tells you is how much of the work is just... remembering to be ordinary. The Brine feeds on weight, on concentration, on one person holding everything. So we hold a little each, and we put it down, and we go make supper." He shrugged. "It turns out the great spell against ancient evil is a shared chore list."

"Your master would be insufferable about being right."

"Yew was insufferable about being right *before* she died. It's one of the ways I know she trained me properly."

Marta snorted. Below them, Fen called up something about a rider on the far trail, then amended it to a rock, then to a rock shaped disappointingly unlike anything interesting. The convoy rolled on through the gold afternoon, and Astrid sat on the bench of the lead wagon in plain view of the whole sky, and let the belonging settle over him like a coat cut to his measure.

Not found. Earned. There was a difference, and the difference had cost a door in the witch-finders' cells, and a ledger full of names, and Yew's last stand at a threshold, and Marta spitting *run* at him through the bars of a cage so he would save himself to save them all. Belonging bought at that price you wore carefully, the way you wore a blade you'd actually had to use.

* * *

Camp that night was in the hollow below Shepherd's Rest, where a spring came up sweet between the rocks and the pines broke the worst of the wind. Fires lit. Onions avenged. The child — Sefa, the traitor's daughter, eight years old now and ruler of the camp by unanimous terror — commandeered Astrid within minutes of his arrival, on the grounds that he owed her a story and she had been counting the days.

"That's a lie," Astrid said, sitting down on the log she indicated with the gravity of a throne room. "You can't count past thirty."

"I count *in circles*," Sefa said witheringly. "Thirty-one days is circle one. I'm on circle eleven. Story. The one with the crown."

"The one with the crown has a sad middle."

"All the good ones do." She folded her arms, a gesture so precisely copied from Marta that several nearby smugglers turned away to hide their faces.

So he told it. He told it the way he'd learned to, over the winter — true enough to honor, soft enough to sleep on. Once there was a king who caught a hunger, and the hunger ate his words and wore his face, and the kingdom thought the king had simply gone quiet, because kingdoms will believe almost anything rather than look closely at a crown. And once there was a boy who could vanish, who read his name in a dead book and thought it meant he'd die alone. And the boy went to unmake the monster and found out the monster was a prisoner, and the king was a prisoner, and the only question worth asking was not *who wears the crown* but *why does anyone wear it alone*.

"And then?" Sefa demanded, at the part where the friends arrived in the throne room with their names ready.

"And then they wrote their names in," Astrid said. "All of them. Even the one who'd sold the road, because — this is the important part, pay attention — even the one who'd been most afraid. Especially her. Fear doesn't disqualify you. It's just proof the thing matters."

Sefa considered this with the ferocious seriousness of the very young. "And the hunger?"

"Dwindles. Gets smaller every month. One day it'll be a whisper, and then maybe a memory, and then maybe just a bad taste in old people's mouths when it rains." He tapped her nose. "But it only stays small as long as nobody tries to hold the world alone again. That's the moral. Don't let anyone talk you into carrying everything by yourself. Not crowns, not cargoes, not other people's sins."

"That's two morals."

"I'm an epilogue. I contain multitudes."

Around them the camp settled into its night sounds — Tam losing at dice with theatrical outrage, Fen cleaning her crossbow, Oskar eating Marta's share after all, the mules sighing their heavy sighs. Isolde was a week's ride east, refusing a throne with tremendous energy and convening her council with more; her letters arrived monthly, dense and funny and signed, lately, *your colleague in governance, a title worse than any curse*. The King — no, the man who had been the King — tended bees now in a coastal village that didn't ask questions, and wrote twice a season, brief notes in a hand growing steadier: *the hive is calm. I spoke today. It was nothing important. It was wonderful.* Corvane was gone, ledger under arm, walking wherever exiles walk; the last report had him teaching children in some border town to read, which Astrid chose to believe was penance working itself out the only way it could.

And tomorrow, or the day after, the five of them would gather — Astrid and Marta, Isolde arriving dusty and delighted, the traitor Brenna with Sefa asleep on her hip, and old Hollis the ferryman who'd joined in autumn, saying he was too old for fear and too stubborn for anything else — and they would sing the binding new, five voices around one table, splitting the weight like a loaf.

But tonight there were onions and dice and a child demanding sequels.

"One more," Sefa said. "What happened to the boy?"

Astrid looked at the fire. Looked at the faces around it, rough and loyal and alive. Put his hand flat against his own chest, where an ancient hunger slept in a cage built of company, and where — underneath everything, before and after and regardless — a boy who had once perfected the art of disappearing sat warm and visible and thoroughly, stubbornly found.

"Oh," he said, "he came home."

Sefa nodded, satisfied, and demanded the sequel anyway, and got it, and the fire burned down to stars, and on the road behind them the Thornfell passes lay open and honest under the moon — a road with teeth, a road worth trusting, a road that whistled three notes rising, two falling, calling anyone still out there to come in, come home, come in honest.

**THE END**
