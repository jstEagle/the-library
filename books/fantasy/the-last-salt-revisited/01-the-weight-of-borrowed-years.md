## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The smuggler's name was Hobb Fenwick, and he was dying the way old sailors die — slowly, stubbornly, and with a great deal of complaint about the quality of the blankets.

Rafael Fairweather sat at his bedside in a room that smelled of lamp oil, dried fish, and the particular staleness of a house where someone has been ill long enough for the illness to become part of the architecture. Rain ticked against the shutters of the cottage above Saltmere Cove. A kettle muttered on the stove downstairs. Somewhere below that, waves were doing what waves do against a cove like this: arriving, giving up, trying again.

"Coin first," Hobb said. His voice had gone thin as gull-bone, but his eyes were still sharp enough to count with.

Rafael set six silver marks on the blanket, one by one, so he could watch them land. "Six. As agreed. Half now, half after."

"After." Hobb snorted, which turned into a cough, which he rode out with the practiced ease of a man who'd been coughing since before Rafael was born. "You think I'll be in any state to argue afterward?"

"That's rather the point of the arrangement, yes."

Hobb laughed then — a dry rattle like a shutter in wind — and Rafael felt something in their chest do the small, familiar twist it always did at moments like this. They were good at this work. That was the trouble with being good at it: you got very fluent at smiling while your hands did the delicate part.

"Tell me again what I'm selling," Hobb said. He'd asked twice already. Dying men liked to check their arithmetic.

"You're not selling anything," Rafael said, settling into the chair beside the bed and pulling off their gloves, finger by finger. "Selling implies you lose it. You keep every scrap of tonight, Hobb. You'll remember this conversation, the rain, the terrible blankets. What I take is *copy*. The original stays right where it is."

"And mine's the one you want? Out of everything?"

"It's the one you offered." Rafael flexed their bare fingers. Warmth gathered in them the way warmth gathers in hands held over coals — except Rafael's hands drew from somewhere else entirely. "A man on his deathbed doesn't usually offer me the memory of his worst day. You want the summer of the *Gullwing*, and you want someone to have it when you don't anymore. That's not a sale. That's a bequest."

Hobb looked out the window, toward the sound of water he couldn't see. For a long moment he didn't speak, and Rafael let him not speak. Patience was half the trade. The other half was knowing exactly how much pressure a memory could bear before it tore.

"I was twenty-three," Hobb said finally. "Best crew on the coast. We ran salt and silk past the Thornfell cutters, and we never once lost a cargo. Not once. And there was a girl in Marrow Bay who used to wait on the pier —"

"— with her hair tied back with fishing twine," Rafael said gently. "I know. You told me last week, when you were well enough to shout about the price."

"I shouted because you quoted me a thief's price."

"I am a thief. It seemed thematically appropriate."

That earned another laugh, weaker than the first. Hobb closed his eyes. "Do it, then. Before I talk myself out of it."

Rafael leaned forward and laid two fingertips against the old man's temple, feather-light, the way you'd touch a bird you meant to release rather than keep.

The world went quiet and silver.

This was the part Rafael had never been able to describe to anyone, mostly because describing it would require admitting they did it at all. Memory-theft wasn't grabbing. It wasn't even, really, taking. It was more like — walking into a house where all the lamps are lit and finding the one room the owner loves best, and sitting down inside it, and letting it pour itself into you. Memories *want* to be remembered. That was the secret nobody knew, the secret that made Rafael's trade possible and profitable and, on nights like this, unbearable. All those years locked in one skull, going over and over themselves, waiting for an audience.

The *Gullwing* opened around them. Salt spray. Rope burn. A hold full of contraband silk and four sailors singing badly. And the girl on the pier at Marrow Bay, hair tied back with fishing twine because she'd broken her ribbon and refused to be late, laughing as the ship came in, laughing as if the ship coming in were the most wonderful thing the sea had ever done —

Rafael lifted it whole. Gently. The way you'd lift a sleeping child.

Then they were back in the sickroom, rain on the shutters, Hobb breathing slow beneath their fingers, a smile still moving on his face like a tide going out.

"Well?" he whispered.

"It's a good one," Rafael said. Their voice came out slightly rough. They cleared their throat. "The singing alone is worth three of your six marks."

"Off-key?"

"Catastrophically. There's a verse where everyone forgets the words and just yells 'the *Gullwing* flies' at increasing volumes. I intend to treasure it."

Hobb smiled up at the ceiling. "Good. Good." His hand found Rafael's wrist and gripped it, stronger than Rafael expected, stronger than it should have been. "Keep it safe, Fairweather. That's all. Keep it safe."

"I will," Rafael said. And meant it, which was the other unbearable thing.

They took the remaining three marks' worth of silence — no, they took nothing; the payment was already made — they gathered their coat and gloves and let themselves out into the rain.

* * *

The path out of Saltmere Cove climbed through gorse and slate, and by the time Rafael reached the ridge, the rain had thinned to mist and the sky had gone the color of pewter holding its breath. Below, the cove shrank to a gray comma. Above, Thornfell mountain shouldered into cloud, wearing the fog like a collar it disapproved of.

Rafael walked. Walking home after a pull was ritual, and rituals mattered in a trade like theirs. First mile: say nothing, think nothing, let the borrowed thing settle. Second mile: review the goods. Third mile onward: try very hard not to feel anything, fail immediately, repeat until home.

By the second milestone they were humming the *Gullwing*'s song under their breath, including the verse where everyone yelled.

It was a fine memory. That was the problem. Rafael had carried eleven years of fine memories that belonged to other people — a baker's pride in her first perfect loaf; a bride's terror and delight watching her mother cry during the vows; a shepherd boy's whole afternoon spent teaching a dog to count sheep (the dog could manage three). They kept them the way a miser keeps coins, catalogued and polished and never spent. Some thieves sold what they stole. Rafael had tried that once, early, and had lain awake all night hearing the buyer laugh with Hobb Fenwick's girl's laughter, laughter that belonged to no one now, drifting loose in a stranger's head. Never again. What Rafael lifted stayed lifted, but it also stayed *kept*. A museum with one visitor.

Which raised the question that walked beside them on every road home, patient as a dog: whose life was Rafael living, exactly?

They ate other people's favorite meals because they remembered loving them. They hummed songs learned secondhand. They had opinions about weather in towns they'd never seen. And underneath it all, their own history sat behind a door they had nailed shut themselves, painted over, bricked in, guarded.

Because that was the rule. The only rule, the one carved so deep into them it might as well be bone:

*Never steal your own memories back.*

Every thief in the trade knew the temptation. Your childhood is a vault you were locked out of — why not pick the lock? Why not walk back into the house of yourself and turn up all the lamps? Rafael had stood at that door exactly once, aged nineteen, drunk on cheap wine and worse courage, fingers hovering over their own temple in a cracked mirror.

And had seen, in the mirror's depths or their own imagination, something looking back that was not them. Something that had been waiting in the dark of their skull since they were nine years old, since the night they couldn't remember, the night their life became a hallway with every door closed. Whatever lived behind that door, it wasn't a happy summer on a sailing ship. Rafael had known that the way you know the weight of a body under a floorboard.

They'd put the wine away. They'd never touched their own head again. Nine years old and earlier: sealed. Everything before that a smooth white wall, and Rafael a person built entirely out of rooms furnished with other people's furniture.

"Cheerful," they said aloud to the mountain. The mountain declined to comment.

The third mile brought them to the fork below the old tollhouse, and here — as arranged — a lantern hung from the crooked signpost, hooded against the wind, blinking its agreed pattern: three flashes, pause, two.

Rafael's contact. A fence named Odile who dealt in information the way Rafael dealt in memory, and who charged rates that would embarrass a bishop. Rafael uncovered the lantern and waited in the lee of the tollhouse wall while footsteps crunched up the goat track.

Odile appeared out of the mist like a rumor: small, round, wrapped in a gray cloak that made her look like a boulder with opinions. She was chewing something. She was always chewing something.

"You look terrible," Odile said, by way of greeting.

"You look like a rock that's learned to talk."

"One of us is aging gracefully." She spat out a cherry pit with remarkable accuracy into the gorse. "How's the sailor?"

"Dying comfortably. He gave me his best day."

"Mm. Sentimental trade, yours." She dug into her cloak and produced a folded paper, which she did not hand over. "Before I give you this. Word of advice, free of charge, which should alarm you."

"Deeply."

"The cache I'm about to tell you about — the Vane brothers' vault under the ruined chapel at Greywater — everyone says it's unguarded because everyone says the Vane brothers are dead. But nobody's *robbed* it. Thieves don't leave money lying about, Rafael. When a vault sits open and full for a year, either it's empty, or it's bait, or it's cursed." She shrugged. "Or it's waiting."

"For whom?"

"If I knew that, I'd charge double." She held out the paper. "Map's inside. Combination's inside. My fee's inside my usual arrangement, which you'll settle by month's end, and don't give me that face, you've stolen enough rich men's nostalgia this season to buy the chapel outright."

Rafael took the paper but didn't unfold it. "Why sell me this at all? If it's cursed or baited, you're spending my blood to make your coin."

Odile looked at them for a moment, and the humor drained out of her face, leaving something older and sadder, the way fog drains off a hill at dusk.

"Because a fortnight ago," she said, "a courier came through here asking after you by name. Full name. Fairweather. Nobody's used your full name in years, and this fellow wasn't from any guild I know. Paid too much, tipped too well, asked too politely." She chewed. "And then yesterday, a different courier, same questions. Two people looking for you in one month, after ten years of nobody caring. So either you've made enemies —"

"I haven't. Enemies require being remembered."

"— or you've made *something* curious. And I thought: better the thief hears it from a friend than finds it in a vault." She tapped the paper in Rafael's hand. "There's a line written on the back. Read it when you're somewhere with walls."

"Odile, you're being theatrical."

"I'm being paid extra to be theatrical. There's a difference." She was already turning back down the goat track, a gray shape folding into gray mist. Her voice drifted back: "Walls, Fairweather! And lock your doors!"

* * *

Rafael's cottage clung to the mountainside two hours further on, a squat stone place with a green roof and a view of nothing but more mountain, chosen precisely because nothing and no one came up this high without meaning to. They lit the stove, fed the cat — a large, judgmental gray creature called Ledger, acquired through means Rafael preferred not to examine too closely — and only then, sitting in the yellow lamplight with a cup of tea going cold beside them, unfolded Odile's paper.

The map was clean work. The combination was cleaner. Rafael almost smiled.

Then they turned the page over.

Three lines, in a handwriting that was somehow both cramped and elegant, ink gone brownish-red like old wine:

*Rafael Fairweather.*

*What was sold beneath the mountain remembers its price.*

*Come and read before the moon finishes counting.*

Rafael read it four times. The fire popped. Ledger stared at them with the flat golden patience of a creature who has watched humans panic before and expects nothing less.

"What was sold," Rafael murmured. Their voice sounded strange in the small room, thin as Hobb's. "Sold beneath the —"

Thornfell rose black against the stars outside the window. Beneath it, the old miners' tunnels went down and down, past the abandoned salt workings, deeper than any map showed, into places the smugglers wouldn't dig and the shepherds wouldn't graze. Places Rafael had spent nine years — no. No, they hadn't spent nine years avoiding them. They'd spent their whole life avoiding them, for reasons the white wall in their memory declined to explain.

Their hands, they noticed distantly, had gone cold. Both of them. The hands that could walk into any mind on the coast and come out carrying its treasures.

*Never steal your own memories back.* The rule rang in them like a struck bell.

But this wasn't stealing. This was reading. Someone had written their name — their full name, the one nobody used — and written it before tonight, and sent it by courier through a fence who owed them nothing, and the moon outside the window was rising fat and pale over Thornfell like an eye opening.

Counting.

Rafael looked at the map. Greywater chapel, the Vane vault, a day's walk north. Unguarded. Waiting.

Ledger jumped onto the table, stepped directly across the map, and knocked the cold tea over.

"Yes," Rafael agreed. "That's about how it feels."

They sat up a long time after that, listening to the mountain breathe, the brown-red ink drying on the table between them like something freshly wounded. In the morning, they told themselves, they would decide sensibly.

In the morning, they packed a bag.
