## 2. A Date Written in Salt

The tip had cost Rafael three coppers and a lie, which by Thornfell standards was practically a gift.

*Old Vetch's cache, the one under the leaning pine past the second switchback. He's dead a week and nobody's claimed it.* The teller of this tale had been a one-eyed fur trader named Hobbs, who delivered it while pretending not to watch Rafael eat stew. Rafael had let him pretend. It was kinder that way, and kindness — borrowed or otherwise — came cheap when you were full.

Now they stood at the base of the leaning pine in the last blue light of evening, breath fogging, and considered the slope below its roots with professional suspicion.

Nothing about the scene said *trap*, exactly. But nothing ever did. Traps, in Rafael's long and occasionally humiliating experience, announced themselves the way beautiful strangers announced themselves: with an absence of warning so complete it became its own kind of warning.

"Right," they told the mountain. Talking to yourself was another thing the passes made normal. A person could go days up here without a conversation that wasn't with a goat or their own reflection in a frozen stream, and Rafael had long ago decided that narrating your own burglary was preferable to the alternative, which was silence, which was where the loneliness lived.

They knelt and pulled back the mat of frost-stiffened moss.

The door beneath it was smuggler-standard: iron-banded oak, no lock worth the name, but rigged — ah, there it was — with a hair-fine tripwire threaded through the hinge gap, attached to something behind the wall that clicked softly when Rafael breathed on it. A whistle-flask arrangement. Crude. Old Vetch had been losing his touch before he lost everything else.

Rafael lifted the wire free with two fingers and a patience learned over twenty years of taking things from places that didn't want them taken. The door swung open on air that smelled of lamp oil, cedar shavings, and old paper.

Not coin, then.

That was fine. That was *fine*. Rafael had come all this way because Hobbs had said cache, and cache meant value, and value could be paper if the paper was right. Smugglers' manifests sold to fence-brokers for more than the cargo sometimes. Ledgers sold for more than that. A man like Old Vetch, dying slow in a room that smelled of camphor, might have kept records worth a winter's firewood money, and Rafael needed winter's-firewood money the way most people needed lungs.

They lit the stub of candle they carried for exactly these occasions and went down into the earth.

* * *

The cache was a single stone room, low-ceilinged, dug into the mountain's shoulder sometime in the last century and never improved upon. Barrels gone soft at the staves. A crate of tallow candles fused into one sad mass. And on a table against the far wall, centered with a care that made the hairs on Rafael's arms stand up, a book.

Everything else in the room was neglect. The book was attention.

It lay closed, wrapped in oilcloth that someone had folded with crisp, deliberate corners, and even before Rafael touched it they knew two things. One: this was what the tip had been about, the whole point of Hobbs and his three coppers and Rafael's half-day climb. Two: whoever had placed it here wanted it found — wanted it found *by them*, specifically — and that knowledge arrived not as thought but as a cold drop of water down the spine, the body understanding before the mind consented to.

Because here was the thing about Rafael Fairweather, the thing they'd built a whole self on top of: they were never the mark. They were the hand. Twenty years of lifting memories out of heads like plums from a market basket, and in all that time Rafael had never once felt the particular vertigo of being inside someone else's plan.

They stood in the doorway a long moment, candle guttering, and listened to the mountain breathe.

Then, because rent was real and fear was theoretical, they crossed the room and picked up the book.

Heavy. Heavier than paper had any right to be. The oilcloth came away dry and crackling, and underneath was leather the color of driftwood, scuffed white at the edges, stamped with no title. When Rafael opened the front cover, the smell hit them — salt, unmistakably, sea-salt, though the nearest coast was four hundred miles away — and the first page bore a single line of handwriting so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood:

*The Salt Codex. Kept, not read.*

"Well," Rafael said to the empty room, "I'm about to ruin that."

Flipping forward was easy. Reading was not.

The pages weren't paper. They were vellum of some kind, thin as onion skin, and every inch of them was covered edge to edge in a cramped, slanting script — hundreds of entries, maybe thousands, each one dated in a column down the left margin. Dates going back a hundred years. Dates going *forward*. Rafael's eye snagged on next spring, on the year after, on entries marked for days that hadn't happened yet, and the candle flame leaned sideways though no draft moved.

Prophecies. Of course. Wren Ashvale's people, the keeper order, the ones who burned books and exiled their own — this was their breed of nonsense. Rafael had heard enough about prophecy-keepers from Wren's occasional bitter monologues to know the shape of the thing: an order that believed the future could be written down, filed, and guarded, like cargo.

Rafael didn't believe in written futures. Rafael believed in hands and hinges and what people would pay to forget. Believing otherwise seemed like a good way to end up living alone in a hole in a mountain, which — they glanced around the cache — apparently it was.

But the dates. Someone had bothered to write tomorrow, and the day after, and the whole of the coming year, in a book locked in a dead smuggler's cellar, and whatever else that was, it was *curious*, and curiosity was the other thing Rafael had built their self on top of.

They turned pages faster now, skimming. Entries about harvests and floods, about a bridge falling, about names — always names, names of people who sounded invented, *Merrin Dole*, *the twins at Harrow Ford*, *the girl who counts crows*. Small prophecies. Village-sized futures. The kind of thing that was either a century-long con or a ledger of small tragedies, and either way not Rafael's problem.

Then the date caught them.

Tonight's date. Not approximately. Not a near miss. The exact day, month, and season, written in ink older than Rafael's grandmother, and beneath it, in that same brown slanting hand:

*A thief comes to the cache beneath the leaning pine.*

Rafael's mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude.

They read it again. They read it a third time, aloud, because saying things made them less true somehow — "a thief comes to the cache" — and their voice came out wrong, too high, a stranger's voice. There were perhaps six thieves in the Thornfell passes with the skill to find this vault, and only one of them had been fed the location by a one-eyed man who wouldn't meet their eyes over his stew.

Hobbs hadn't been selling a tip. Hobbs had been *delivering*.

Below the line about the thief, the entry continued, and Rafael made themselves read it.

*She comes for coin and leaves with her name. The reader is found; the reading begins. What was sold beneath the mountain stirs in its sleep, for the bargain nears its thirteenth turn, and the soul that was traded for a child's escape remembers the hand that spent it.*

The candle chose that moment to gutter out.

Darkness, total, the kind the mountain keeps. Rafael stood absolutely still in it, heart going like a fist on a door, and did the arithmetic they very much did not want to do. *A child's escape.* There had been an escape, once, when they were nine — a running-away so total they had never once looked back, had made a craft of not looking back, had become, professionally, a person who took other people's pasts precisely so that they never had to sit still inside their own.

And there had been a sale before that. A thing Rafael knew the way you know the shape of a scar without looking at it: something had been given away so that they could run. Their mother's face at the door. A word they'd never let themselves finish remembering. *Sold.* They had spent twenty years not finishing that sentence.

The book finished it for them.

In the dark, faintly, the pages whispered — the sound of a leaf turning, though Rafael's hands were at their sides.

"No," Rafael told it. Firmly. Like a dog on a bed. "Absolutely not. You don't get to be creepy at me in the dark, I have a reputation."

The whispering stopped. In its place: light. A pale, moon-colored glow rising off the open pages, illuminating the little stone room in shades of pearl and shadow, and on the page — Rafael saw it clearly, wished violently that they hadn't — the ink was moving.

The letters of tonight's entry were crawling, rearranging, gathering themselves into new words as Rafael watched, the way ants reform after you disturb the line. The old sentence dissolved. New letters surfaced through the vellum like fish rising to feed:

*Rafael Fairweather.*

Their name. Their actual name, spelled correctly, including the flourish their mother used to add to the final *r* that Rafael themselves had dropped at age eleven and never told anyone about. The ink wrote it slowly, lovingly, a hand practicing a signature, and then beneath it, in smaller letters, almost gentle:

*Tonight.*

Every instinct Rafael owned — twenty years of them, hard-won, load-bearing — screamed the same instruction: *put it down, walk away, burn the passes behind you.* You don't steal from a trap you can see. You don't touch the bait after you've identified the hook. This was Theft, First Lesson, the one even bad thieves learned before they died of learning it late.

Rafael reached out and touched the page.

Later, they would try to explain this to themselves and fail. The closest they ever got was this: the entry had their mother's flourish in it. And there is a kind of loneliness so old it stops feeling like loneliness and starts feeling like architecture, like load-bearing walls, like simply *how the building stands* — and when something reaches into that, past all the competence and the cleverness and the narrated burglaries, down to the nine-year-old who ran and never looked back, well. Hands move before wisdom catches up. That's how thieves are made in the first place.

Their fingertip met the vellum.

The salt smell surged — oceanic, overwhelming, the whole drowned world arriving in a cellar under a mountain — and the page *pulled*. Not the ink. The page itself, the space behind it, a door opening where a door had no business being, and Rafael felt their weight shift forward onto their toes, felt the room tilt, felt the stone floor go distant under their boots like the far shore of a lake.

"Oh," they said, with the distinct clarity of a person watching their own mistake complete itself. "Oh, you absolute —"

The moonlight from the pages rose around them like water closing overhead. The last things Rafael saw of the ordinary world were the barrels, the fused candles, the leaning-pine dark above the stair — and then those, too, dissolved, and the Salt Codex began, with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had waited a hundred years, to read its reader.

Somewhere very far away, on the slopes of Thornfell, a one-eyed man counted three coppers and felt briefly, unaccountably ashamed.

And somewhere much closer than that, a disgraced prophecy-keeper who had spent ten years hunting this exact book felt the pull of its opening like a hook behind her ribs — and started to run.
