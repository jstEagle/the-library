## 3. The Keeper Who Failed

The trouble with tracking a stolen prophecy for ten years, Wren Ashvale had learned, was that everyone assumed you were the villain.

She had been chased out of three towns, one monastery, and a remarkably pleasant bathhouse in Lowmarsh — that last one on the grounds that a woman who read omens for a living must also, by local logic, be reading the bottoms of other patrons. She had slept in haylofts, hedgerows, and once, memorably, inside the hollow statue of a saint who had been a keeper himself, and whose stone hands had curled around her shoulders like an apology.

None of it had dulled her purpose. That was the problem with guilt: it kept better than salted fish. It did not rot, it did not fade, it simply sat in the chest, patient as a debt, waiting to be called in.

Wren had been calling herself in every day for a decade.

* * *

She came down out of the Thornfell passes at dusk, when the mountain turned the color of old pewter and the wind smelled of snow that hadn't committed yet. The Codex was near. She felt it the way you feel a storm behind a hill — not seeing it, just knowing the light had gone strange.

Ten years ago she had been Sister Wren of the Order of the Turning Page, third-ranked keeper of the Vault of Hours, entrusted with more prophecies than any keeper under forty. And ten years ago, on the worst night of her life, she had stood in front of the Salt Codex with the destruction rite half-spoken and let herself be argued out of it.

By herself, which was the humiliating part.

*It's the last one,* young Wren had said to old Wren, in the voice keepers used for dangerous thoughts. *After this, nothing new. Nothing living. The Order will have finished its great work and there will be no more readers needed, ever. Let this one stay unread. What harm can a closed book do?*

Old Wren, standing now in the freezing pass with her boots wet through, would have liked five minutes alone with young Wren and a sturdy stick.

Because the Codex was not a book that waited to be read. The Codex was a book that *read*. It required a living reader — that was its design, laid into its binding by founders who had believed, with the serene confidence of people who never expected to be wrong, that they could aim prophecy like an arrow. Find the right reader, open the right page, and the future would unfold exactly as written.

And the final prophecy — the one Wren had glimpsed in a single terrible night before her exile — required a reader with a particular shape of soul. A soul that had been sold. A soul that could be *called home*.

Which meant somewhere beneath Thornfell, right now, someone was being pulled into a story older and crueler than they knew, and every hour Wren spent feeling guilty about it was an hour the ink got closer.

"Move faster," she told herself, and slid down the scree toward the smuggler's cache, because self-flagellation was best done at a jog.

* * *

The cache was easy to find, once you knew what you were looking for. That was the thing about smugglers: they trusted locks, and locks could be reasoned with, but they never trusted the obvious, and so the obvious was where everything hid. The door was behind a curtain of hanging roots, keyed to a mechanism older than the smugglers themselves — Order work, Wren realized, and her stomach dropped a full inch. Her order had seeded these mountains with caches generations ago. This wasn't a smuggler's vault that had swallowed a book. It was a larder that had been stocked on purpose, and the book had simply been waiting for the right thief.

The lock opened for her the way locks had always opened for keepers, with a click that sounded almost relieved.

Inside: dust, crates, the ghost-smell of salt and lamp oil. And on a plain wooden table, lit by the moon through a ventilation crack, a book bound in grey leather that seemed to drink the moonlight rather than reflect it.

"Oh, you're still here," Wren said. "Good. We need to talk."

Talking to books was, technically, against protocol. So was most of what she'd done since her exile. She crossed the room anyway, and the moment her hand hovered over the cover, the air changed.

It changed the way air changes over deep water. Pressure first, then pull.

"No," Wren said firmly. "No, we are *not* doing this. I know exactly what you are. I have read your sisters. I burned two of them myself."

The Codex did not answer, because it was a book, but the pull deepened, gentle as a tide, and beneath it she heard — felt? imagined? — something that was not quite a voice. Not words. More like the memory of words, the way you remember warmth after leaving a fire. It said, in the language of pressure and longing: *come see how it ends.*

"I know how it ends," she snapped. "I've read the ending. It's awful. Everyone dies of it."

But even as she spoke, she understood the trap she was standing in. The Codex didn't need her. She was a failed keeper, a decade stale, her reader's gift worn down to a nub from disuse and cheap wine. The Codex wanted the thief. The tip she'd followed — the rumor of an unguarded cache, passed through three mouths in a border tavern, each mouth more helpful than the last — had been bait, and she had walked straight into it like the earnest fool she remained at thirty-four years old.

Which meant the reader was already coming. Which meant—

Boots on gravel outside. Light footsteps, careful, professional, the walk of someone who had robbed enough places to stop performing caution and simply embody it.

"*Now?*" Wren hissed at the book. "You couldn't wait until I'd—"

The door opened.

The thief who slipped in was younger than Wren expected, lean and quick, dressed in dark clothes that had been mended many times by someone who cared about them. They moved like water finding a crack, and their eyes — sharp, amused, already cataloguing exits — swept the room, found the table, found the book.

And then, a beat too late, found Wren.

To her lasting embarrassment, neither of them reacted like a professional. The thief yelped. Wren knocked over a crate. A jar of pickled something rolled across the floor and came to rest against the thief's boot, gazing up at them both with briny indifference.

"Don't touch it," Wren said, recovering first, because guilt sharpened fast into authority when given half a chance. "Step away from the table."

"It's not yours either," the thief said, and their voice did something complicated in the space between defiance and curiosity. "You've been following me."

"I've been following the *book*."

"For how long?"

"Ten years."

Something flickered across the thief's face — respect, maybe, or the professional horror of learning you'd been worth a decade of someone's life without noticing. "That's either flattering or deeply sad."

"Both," Wren admitted. "Mostly sad. Now listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then events are going to take it out of my hands: that book is a prophecy that reads its reader. If you open it, it opens you. Whatever it shows you, whatever it promises, do not—"

The thief had, of course, already touched it.

Wren saw it happen — saw the exact moment the thief's fingers brushed the cover and the moonlight in the room bent inward like light approaching a lens. The thief's eyes went wide, then distant, then fixed on some middle distance that was not the middle distance of this room at all. Their breath slowed. Their pupils dilated into twin doors.

"No no no," Wren said, crossing the space between them in three strides. "I told you. I *just told you.*"

The pull hit her a heartbeat later, and it was worse than she remembered. Ten years had softened the memory of it into something survivable; the reality was a hand closing around the base of her skull, kind and absolute. The room went thin. The crates, the pickle jar, the roots beyond the door — all of it receded like a shoreline seen from a departing ship, and ahead of her, going down into the ink, was the thief, arms loose, face slack with wonder, sinking into a story that had been written to consume them.

Here was the arithmetic of Wren Ashvale's life, laid bare in that moment:

Her order had written this book. Her order had chosen its reader, or built its trap around whoever wandered close. She had been the one who let it survive. For ten years she had tracked it, and in all those years she had told herself that when the moment came, she would act — decisively, cleanly, the way keepers were trained to act.

And now the moment was here, and the decisive, clean action was to step back. To let the thief go down alone. Because the prophecy needed a reader, yes — but a reader pulled in blind, unprepared, unaccompanied. Anyone who grabbed on would be dragged in too, an extra weight on a line already straining, and the Codex would *use* that. It would fold her into the text however it pleased. Keeper law said, in the oldest phrasing of the oldest rule: *let the ink take what it catches; mourn, record, move on.*

Mourn, record, move on. She had mourned for ten years. She had recorded everything. She had moved on precisely nowhere.

"Damn it," Wren said, with feeling, "I am *so bad* at my job."

She grabbed the thief's wrist.

* * *

Falling through a book, it turned out, felt like falling through winter. Cold everywhere, but a clean cold, a cold with intention. The thief was beside her — above her? below? directions had resigned — and as the grey rushed past them both, Wren wrapped her grip tighter and shouted words she wasn't sure carried.

"You! Thief! Listen!"

The thief turned, and even mid-fall, even with the ink streaming off them like rain running backward, their expression managed to convey: *this is your fault.*

"It is absolutely my fault," Wren agreed, because honesty cost nothing here. "My order wrote this. I let it live. Every bad thing about to happen to us is a bill arriving ten years late. But hear me—" the grey tore at her voice, so she leaned close, forehead nearly touching theirs, and put every scrap of certainty she owned into the words, "—whatever it shows you, whoever it says you are, whatever bargain it claims you already made — *none of it is finished.* Prophecy is a draft. I know the editors. They're fallible, they're vain, and one of them is currently holding your wrist."

The thief's eyes searched hers. There was fear in them, real fear, but underneath it something else — a quickness, a calculating gleam, the look of a person already pricing the exit routes of Hell.

"You're insane," the thief said.

"Frequently. It's load-bearing."

A pause, the length of one falling breath.

"What's your name?" the thief asked.

"Wren Ashvale. Failed keeper of the Order of the Turning Page. You?"

Something crossed the thief's face then — hesitation, the instinct of a lifetime of trade secrets — and then, absurdly, generously, they gave it up as the dark took them both:

"Rafael."

And the Codex closed around them like a mouth, like a door, like a page turning, and far above them a pickle jar rolled gently to a stop in an empty room, the sole witness to the second-worst decision of Wren Ashvale's life — and the first one, she thought as the ink took her, that she had ever made on purpose.
