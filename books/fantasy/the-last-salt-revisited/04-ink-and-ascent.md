## 4. Ink and Ascent

The ink did not pull so much as *insist*.

Rafael had been pulled out of their own head before — by grief, by cheap wine, by a memory of a door they'd never opened twice — and this was nothing like that. That had been falling. This was being read. The Codex turned its pages through them the way a wind turns pages, and somewhere in the turning, the smuggler's vault with its salt-crusted beams and its dead man's ledger simply stopped agreeing to exist.

The last thing Rafael saw of the world was Wren Ashvale's hand closing around their wrist.

"Let go," Rafael said, or tried to say. Their mouth was full of ink. It tasted of iron and rain and, absurdly, of their mother's kitchen.

"Absolutely not," said Wren, who was also dissolving and somehow still managing to sound disapproving about it. "I have chased this book for eleven years. You don't get to fall into it alone."

"You don't even know me."

"I know your name is in my book," she snapped, "which makes you my problem, which makes you *mine to keep track of*, which—" The vault went white. "—hold on."

They held on.

* * *

Falling through a page, it turns out, feels like being remembered by someone who barely knew you: imprecise, affectionate, and full of small errors. Rafael felt themselves smoothed over, simplified, corrected. For one horrible half-second they were nine years old again, standing in a doorway with salt on their tongue — and then the moment passed, or was taken from them, and they landed on stone.

Real stone. Cold as a debt.

"Everyone alive?" Wren's voice came from somewhere above them, breathless.

"No," Rafael said into the rock. "I'm dead. Leave me here. Write it in your book."

"Noted." A boot appeared beside their face, then a hand. Wren hauled them upright with the brisk efficiency of someone who had clearly dragged bodies before and no longer found it interesting. "Look up."

Rafael looked up.

They stood at the foot of a stair that rose into darkness — not the ordinary darkness of a cellar or a cave, but the deep, patient dark of night sky seen from underneath. Each step was broad enough for two people and cut from something pale and faintly luminous, like moonstone that had been told to behave itself. And every step bore an image, etched shallow into its surface, silver lines glowing softly against the pale.

A moon. Full, round, serene.

"One," Rafael counted aloud, because counting was a thing hands could do while the rest of them screamed quietly.

"Keep going," Wren said.

Two steps up: a moon with a sliver bitten from it, like bread. Three: a half-moon hanging over a line of hills that looked uncomfortably like Thornfell. Four showed rain falling upward. Five showed a door standing open in a field, with no house attached to it. Six—

"Stop." Rafael crouched. On the sixth step, the etched moon hung low and swollen, and beneath it stood two tiny figures rendered in three strokes each — one tall, one shorter, both unmistakably mid-argument. "That's us. That's *now*."

"That's now," Wren agreed quietly.

"How many steps are there?"

Wren didn't answer. She had gone very still in the way people do when they already know the number and wish they didn't. Rafael climbed past her, step by step, reading moons. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. At eleven, the figures on the stair were walking downward, and there was a third figure among them, small. Twelve showed a mountain's mouth, open. Thirteen—

Thirteen showed a full moon again, but this one was carved deeper than all the others, and around it ran a ring of words too worn to read, except for two that survived: *sealed* and *forever*.

And above the thirteenth step, the stairs stopped. There was nothing but dark, and in the dark, very far off, a light the color of candleflame behind vellum.

"Thirteen," Rafael said. "Turns of the moon."

"The bargain seals on the thirteenth turn." Wren came up beside them, arms crossed against the cold that poured down from the dark. "Whatever soul was sold beneath Thornfell — after thirteen moons, it stops belonging to anyone who might want it back. It becomes court property. Permanent. Inalienable." She said the last word like it tasted bad. "That's the law of the Undervault. Their whole civilization runs on it. Nothing kept can ever be unkept."

"Then we have a year."

"We have until the next full moon turns waning, and then twelve more after it. Yes. A year." Wren was already pacing, measuring the stair with her stride. "Which sounds generous until you remember we're inside a prophecy written by people who *invented* deadlines."

Rafael sat down on the sixth step — on their own argument, apparently — and put their head between their knees.

"All right," they said. "Let's review. Last night I broke into a smuggler's cache expecting unguarded brandy and possibly some coins. Instead I found a century-old book with my name in it. My *name*, Wren. Spelled right. Nobody spells it right; I've spent thirty years training people to get it wrong. And tonight's date. Tonight's actual date, written in salt-ink by someone dead before my grandmother learned to walk." They lifted their head. "Then I touch it, and instead of the book coming with me, *I* go with the book. So. Questions. Who wrote this? Why me? And why does a disgraced prophecy-keeper grab hold of a stranger rather than let them be swallowed by cursed literature?"

Wren had stopped pacing. In the stairlight she looked younger than she had in the vault, and more tired, and considerably less in control of events — which, Rafael was beginning to suspect, was her natural state dressed up in good posture.

"I'll answer the second one first, because it's the only one I can," she said. "Why you. Because the Codex requires a reader. Not a keeper — readers. Keepers guard it, copy it, argue about it. But the prophecies only *unfold* when someone reads them who isn't supposed to. Living eyes. That's what my order called them. Living eyes." Her jaw tightened. "We used to recruit them. Volunteers, mostly. Mostly."

"And the volunteers?"

"That's the first question, isn't it. Who wrote it." She exhaled slowly. "My order wrote it. The Order of the Kept Page. We wrote the Codex, we hid it, and then — when hiding wasn't enough — we lost it. I was the one carrying it when it was taken. Eleven years ago. I've been following rumors of it across four countries since." She looked at Rafael, and there was something raw under the composure, badly plastered over, like paint on a cracked wall. "So when I say you're my problem, I mean it in the most literal sense available. You are holding a mess I made."

"You didn't make it tonight."

"I made it eleven years ago, which is worse, because I've had eleven years to imagine exactly this."

Rafael studied her. Memory-thieves learn to study faces; it's most of the trade. What they saw was guilt that had been carried so long it had become structural — load-bearing guilt. The kind you couldn't remove without the whole person collapsing.

"Well," Rafael said at last, "the good news is I'm a thief, and thieves are excellent at other people's messes. It's the entire profession."

"The good news," Wren repeated flatly.

"You wanted a different reaction? Fear? I'm doing fear internally. Very efficient. Externally I'm doing competence, because fear doesn't climb stairs."

Something happened to Wren's face then — a twitch at the corner of her mouth, quickly suppressed, like a mouse caught under a cup. "You're afraid," she said. "Of course you're afraid. You just hide it in sarcasm."

"I hide everything in sarcasm. It's a large closet. There's room if you need it."

"I'm *fine*."

"You've been saying 'my problem' like a prayer for ten minutes. Take the closet."

To Rafael's mild astonishment, Wren laughed. It was a short laugh, rusty, clearly out of warranty, but real. She sat down on the seventh step beside them — the step with the upward rain — and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the dark above held its candle-colored light, and the stair hummed faintly under their boots like a struck bell that had decided to be patient.

"Can I ask you something thief-shaped?" Wren said eventually.

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question and my mood."

"When you touched the page. Before I grabbed you. What did it feel like?"

Rafael considered lying. It was their native language, after all. But the stair was listening — they could feel it, the way you feel a confessor listening — and lies seemed likely to be transcribed.

"It felt like being *known*," they said. "Not understood. Understood would have been fine. Known. Like something had a complete list of me and was checking items off. Every name I've used. Every job. Every—" They stopped.

"Every what?"

"Nothing."

"Every stolen memory," Wren said, not unkindly. "I know what you do. Fairweather. It's a known name in certain circles. You take memories out of heads and sell them to people who want lives they didn't earn."

"I prefer 'broker of pre-owned experience.' It has fewer legal implications."

"And you're good at it."

"I'm the best at it." No false modesty; modesty was for people with something left to protect. "Dying men give me their happiest hour and I leave them coin and quiet. Grieving women buy their wedding days back for a season. It's honest work, comparatively. Everything I sell, someone genuinely lived."

"But you never steal your own memories back."

Rafael went still. That rule lived in them the way a splinter lives under skin — small, old, and aching whenever pressed.

"Who told you that?"

"The trade talks. Even the trade talks about Fairweather. The best there is, and the one line they won't cross." Wren tilted her head. "Why not?"

Because, Rafael did not say, because once, when they were small, someone took everything they loved and sold it, and Rafael had run rather than watch, and the running had cost them every memory of *what* they'd run from — and there was a hole in them shaped like a childhood, and they had filled it with thirty years of other people's Tuesdays, and they were terrified — truly, bone-deep terrified — that if they ever reached into that hole, they'd find out what they'd traded away, and whose fault it had been.

"Professional discipline," they said.

"Liar," said Wren, without heat. "Good. Liars make better allies in prophecy-country. Honest people walk into traps because the trap doesn't match their self-image."

"And you're honest?"

"I'm a failed keeper with a guilty conscience. I'll tell you the truth at the worst possible moments. It's my defining flaw."

"Mine's stealing things," Rafael offered. "Between us we should cover most contingencies."

They shook on it — Wren's grip dry and firm, Rafael's deliberately casual — and it occurred to Rafael, shaking the hand of a stranger inside a book, that this was the first bargain in years they hadn't drawn up terms for. It felt either very foolish or very new. Possibly those were the same feeling.

* * *

They climbed.

There was, it developed, nothing else to do. Behind them the stair ended in blank wall — smooth, featureless, smug. Above, the dark waited with its far-off vellum-light. Between lay thirteen steps of moonlit future, and the future, it turned out, was heavy going.

Not physically. Physically the steps were kind, each one lifting them gently toward the next, an escalator of destiny. It was the *reading* that wore them down. Because you couldn't help reading. Each step showed its turn of the moon, and each turn showed things, and the things got harder to look at.

Step eight: the moon over water, and in the water, a reflection of the moon that was wrong — too many points, like a crown.

Step nine: a market lit with green lanterns, stalls selling bottles, and in each bottle a small swirling light. Rafael looked at that one a long time. Wren pretended not to notice how long.

Step ten: two figures on a stair, and one of them flickering, drawn in lines that wouldn't hold still, as though the artist couldn't decide whether to include them at all.

"That's cheerful," Rafael said, and stepped on it quickly, before it could develop.

"Don't," Wren said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Treat it like a heist. I can hear it in how you talk. *Entry point, exit point, contingency.* This isn't a vault, Fairweather. Prophecies aren't locks. They're — weather. You don't pick weather's pocket. You dress for it and you keep moving."

"Everything's a lock," Rafael said. "Some locks are just bigger and wetter than others. Weather has patterns. Weather can be *read*. You of all people should know that — reading's literally your order's whole thing, and look how well that went."

Wren shot them a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces. Then, grudgingly: "...That's fair. That's appallingly fair. Fine. Treat it like a lock. But hear me on one thing." She stopped on the twelfth step — the mountain's open mouth — and pointed down at it. "When we get to whatever this is. Whatever the mountain's mouth means. If the plan goes wrong, we go wrong *together*. No heroics, no splitting up, none of this 'you go on without me' nonsense that thieves think is charming. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Rafael said. "Though for the record, 'you go on without me' *is* charming. It's tragic and charming. It's why ballads exist."

"Ballads are written by survivors," said Wren, "and I intend for neither of us to qualify."

They climbed the final step together.

* * *

The thirteenth step was different underfoot. Warmer. The ring of worn words around its moon glowed brighter as they stood on it, and Rafael crouched, brushing the grit of centuries from the letters with a thumb. Salt came away on their fingers — fine, glittering, familiar in a way they refused to examine.

"Sealed forever," they murmured. "Forever's a strong word for a book to use."

"Books are where strong words live," Wren said. "It's why people burn them."

Above them, the dark had changed. The candle-behind-vellum light was closer now, and steadier, and as Rafael squinted up at it, shapes resolved: a landing, a doorway, and beyond the doorway — impossibly, unmistakably, seen from above and yet somehow also ahead — the green-lanterned market of step nine, stalls and bottles and small swirling lights, full of movement and murmur and life.

The market of the Undervault. Already waiting. Or perhaps always waiting; time was clearly negotiable here.

"Down, then," Rafael said, and heard their own voice steady itself by force. "Into the court that buys souls. Where someone bought mine — or near enough to mine that a book wrote my name in salt a century early." They flexed their empty hands. Empty hands, they'd learned long ago, were either a tragedy or an opportunity, depending entirely on nerve. "One year on the clock. Thirteen turns. A disgraced keeper and a memory-thief, versus an entire underground court of soul-brokers."

"When you phrase it like that it sounds almost winnable," Wren said.

"Oh, I phrase everything winnable. It's the closet thing again." Rafael started down — for the stair, having delivered them upward, now plainly intended to deliver them onward, the thirteenth step sloping gently into descent, the moon-images flowing past beneath their feet like a river of nights. "Tell me about the Steward on the way. Everyone with a ledger has a Steward, and everyone with a Steward has a weakness."

"They're creatures of contract," Wren said, falling into step beside them. "Bound by every word they've ever signed. Which means—"

She stopped.

Below them, on the ninth step, where the green market shimmered, a small figure had appeared — a child, standing at the edge of the etched lantern-light, looking *up* the stair directly at them. Too far away to see a face. Close enough that Rafael felt the gaze like a fingertip on the back of the neck.

And faintly, threading up through the stone, through the salt, through thirteen steps of moonlight —

Rafael knew the tune.

They had never heard it before in their life.

They knew every note of it.
