## 5. The Court Beneath

The Stair of Turns let them off at the mouth of a tunnel that smelled of cold iron and old rain, and Wren was grateful for it. Grateful for anything, really, that wasn't the sensation of being a sentence in someone else's book.

Rafael stood a few paces ahead of her, shaking out their hands the way people did after touching something they shouldn't have. The light of the last moon-turn still clung to them faintly — a silver dusting along the shoulders, like flour on a baker who'd been working all night.

"Right," Rafael said. "So when I asked you earlier what we were walking into, and you said 'a complication,' and I said 'define complication,' and you said 'I'll explain on the way' — this is the way. We're on it now. So. Explain."

Wren adjusted the strap of her satchel and began to walk. It was easier to confess while moving, she'd found. Harder for either party to look at each other.

"The Undervault," she said, "is a court. A real one — thrones, heralds, taxes. It sits beneath Thornfell the way a stone sits at the bottom of a well: quietly, and with great patience."

"A court of what?"

"That's the question everyone asks first." She ducked under a low rib of rock. "Not of the dead, if you're worried. Not of demons, either, whatever the fireside stories say. They call themselves the Kept Court, because everything in their keeping is kept — debts, promises, names. And souls. Especially souls."

Ahead of her, Rafael's stride hitched, just slightly, before resuming. "They *buy* souls," Rafael said. "That's what the page said. 'A soul sold beneath the mountain.'"

"They buy them the way moneylenders buy houses. Gently. With paperwork. Nobody down there drags anyone screaming; there's no need. They wait until a person is desperate enough to sign, and then they hold the signature forever." Wren's boot found a slick patch of moss and she steadied herself against the wall. "Desperation is their harvest. They've never once had to chase anything. Things come to them."

"Charming business model."

"It's very stable, as institutions go. They've never had a war, because they've never needed one. Why fight for what people hand over freely?"

"And the moon-turns? Thirteen? What happens at thirteen?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the silence itself became an answer. Rafael stopped walking and turned around. In the dim glow of the lichen along the walls, their face had gone carefully blank — the expression of someone who had learned young that showing fear was a form of currency spent at a loss.

"Wren. What happens at thirteen?"

"The bargain seals," Wren said. "The soul becomes theirs outright. Before that, ownership is — contested. Conditional. A soul under contract can still be ransomed, traded, argued over by lawyers, which is why courts employ lawyers. After the thirteenth turn, it's simply... theirs. Like a stone at the bottom of a well."

"You like that image."

"I find it honest."

Rafael looked at her a moment longer, then turned and walked on. "Fine. Thirteen turns. That's — what, a year?"

"Thirteen full moons. Roughly a year, yes."

"So we have time."

"We have less than you'd think. Time moves strangely near the Vault. But yes. Some."

They walked. The tunnel descended in long, patient switchbacks, and the air grew warmer and wetter, and somewhere below them — faintly, at the edge of hearing — came a sound Wren knew well and dreaded: bells. Small ones. The sound of an inventory being taken.

* * *

It was perhaps an hour later, at the bottom of a stair carved so wide it might have been made for processions rather than feet, that Rafael sat down abruptly on a step and said, "All right. Your turn."

"My turn?"

"You know things about me I haven't told you. You knew my name before I gave it to you. You grabbed onto me back there like you'd been waiting for me specifically." Rafael's voice stayed level, but Wren could hear the thief underneath it now, the professional, doing sums. "Nobody tracks a stolen book for ten years unless the book matters. Nobody grabs a stranger mid-pull into a prophecy unless they know what the pull does. So. Your turn. Who are you, and how much of this did you already know?"

Here it was, then. Wren had rehearsed this confession on the road, in three versions: the short one, the merciful one, and the true one. She had always known she would end up telling the true one, because Rafael had a face that made lying feel like throwing coins into a well, and because — though she would never say it aloud — she was tired. Ten years tired.

She sat down on the step beside them, leaving a careful gap of air between them.

"My order is called the Order of the Salt Page," she said. "You may not have heard of us. That was deliberate. We were keepers of prophecy — not fortune-tellers," she added quickly, catching the look on Rafael's face. "Nothing so useful. We didn't tell futures. We recorded them. There are texts in the world that write themselves, or nearly — books that fill their own pages as events approach. The Salt Codex is one. My order's founding purpose was to find such books and guard them. Copy nothing. Change nothing. Let them be read only by—"

"By whom?"

"Readers." The word came out flat. "Living readers. A self-writing text needs eyes, Rafael. Ink that moves must be witnessed moving, or it doesn't move at all. That's the oldest law of prophecy. A prediction unwitnessed is just ink."

Rafael was very still. "And the readers. Where do they come from?"

Wren looked down at her hands. They were a keeper's hands, ink-stained and steady, hands that had turned a thousand pages without ever once being asked whether the pages should exist at all.

"We recruited volunteers," she said. "Mostly. People who believed, as we believed, that witnessing a prophecy helped steer it — that a watched river runs cleaner. Some of our readers were devout. Some were scholars. A few were..." She exhaled. "A few were poor, or desperate, or owed us something. We told them the risk was dreams. Bad nights. A heaviness behind the eyes. And for most prophecies, that was true."

"And the Codex."

"The Codex is not most prophecies." She made herself meet Rafael's eyes. "The Codex doesn't describe events. It *requires* them. Every prophecy in it has a reader written into it, and the reader becomes part of the working. We lost eleven readers to that book over ninety years, and we called it devotion, and we buried them with honors, and we kept sending new ones. Because the Codex promised knowledge of every bargain ever sealed beneath Thornfell, and my order decided that knowing was worth the cost."

Rafael's voice, when it came, was almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Eleven people."

"Twelve, if tonight counts."

"Oh, it counts," Rafael said. "Believe me. I was there."

* * *

The confession, having started, would not stop — Wren had learned this about truth the hard way, that it behaved like water finding cracks. She told Rafael about the night the Codex was stolen from the order's vault, ten years ago; about the keeper who had carried it out through the snow and vanished, and the inquiry that followed, and how she had been the youngest keeper on duty, and how the elders had decided, in their wisdom, that the theft proved the book too dangerous to recover — that it should be left wherever it lay, unread, unwitnessed, inert.

"And you disagreed," Rafael said.

"I disagreed," Wren said. "Because I'd read the margins. The Codex doesn't go inert. It goes *looking*. An unwitnessed prophecy doesn't sleep, it hunts for a witness. I petitioned for ten years to mount a recovery. I was refused nine times. On the tenth year I resigned my post, took what records I could carry, and went looking for the book myself."

"And you found it in a smuggler's cache."

"I found its trail in a smuggler's cache. Empty shelf, salt residue, a lock picked from the outside by someone small and quick." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "I've tracked a lot of things, Fairweather. You were the easiest trail I ever followed. You leave fingerprints like other people leave footprints."

"Flatterer." But Rafael didn't smile back. Rafael was doing arithmetic again; Wren could see it moving behind their eyes, columns of figures adding up to something they didn't like. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it." Wren nodded slowly. "The rest of it is that when I caught up with the cache, two days ago, I found the Codex open to your page. Your name. Tonight's date. And I understood that the book hadn't been waiting for a keeper. It had been waiting for *you*. Written ninety years ago, waiting for you, and I —" Her voice snagged. She pushed through it. "I could have closed the cover. Taken the book, buried it in salt, done what my order never had the spine to do. Instead I waited in the dark of that vault for six hours to see if you'd touch the page. Because I needed a living reader to get inside where I couldn't go alone, and because I told myself the prophecy named you anyway, so what difference did my waiting make —"

"What difference," Rafael repeated softly.

"— and that was a lie I told myself," Wren finished, "and I've been carrying it up the whole Stair of Turns hoping you wouldn't ask. You asked."

The tunnel dripped, somewhere. The far-off bells chimed again, patient as tide. Rafael sat with their elbows on their knees and stared at the middle distance, and Wren recognized the posture because she'd seen it on eleven faces in illuminated portraits in her order's hall: the reader, realizing the book had read *them* first.

"You knew," Rafael said at last. "When you grabbed onto me. You knew what touching the page would do."

"Yes."

"You knew I'd be pulled in."

"Yes."

"And you let it happen. You stood in a smuggler's cellar and let a stranger open a book you knew would swallow them, because your order wrote themselves into a corner and you needed a key." Rafael's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it — it got quieter, and more precise, the way a lockpick gets thinner. "My order steals memories. Yours sacrifices readers. And you want to know the funny part, Wren? The genuinely funny part?" They laughed, short and airless. "We're the same. Both of us spend other people to buy our own clean consciences."

"I'm not spending you."

"No?"

"No. I'm—" Wren stopped. Took stock. Every argument she'd prepared — that the prophecy named Rafael regardless, that she'd have warned them eventually, that she intended to stand between them and the worst of it — every one of them sounded, in the echoing dark, exactly like what it was: an accounting. So she set them down. "I don't have a defense. I chose wrong. I told myself a story with myself as the rescuer and you as the instrument, and the fact that I'm fond of you already, after four days, doesn't launder it. Fondness makes excellent camouflage for using people. Ask any courtier down there."

Rafael studied her for a long moment. The lichen-light made their face half silver and half shadow.

"Why are you telling me all of it, then? Now? You could have kept the version where you're the hero. I might even have believed it."

"Because you're going to walk into a court that buys desperate people," Wren said, "and I will not send you down there having bought anything from me without your signature on it. Whatever else I am, I am a keeper. Records should be accurate. Even records about me."

Another silence. This one had a different texture — not the silence of a door closing, but of a door left ajar, which was more than Wren had dared hope for.

"Four days," Rafael said finally, standing, brushing off their coat. "You said you'd known me four days and you're already fond of me. That's fast, even for me. People usually take longer to decide I'm worth betraying."

"It wasn't a decision. It crept up on me. Like damp."

That earned her half a laugh — reluctant, cracked down the middle, but real. Rafael offered a hand and pulled her up from the step, and the contact lasted exactly as long as it had to, and no longer. Wren noted the difference. Before the confession, Rafael had touched her shoulder twice, casually, easily. Now there was a ledger between them where the shoulder had been.

Fair, she thought. More than fair.

They walked on, down the processional stair, and the bells below grew clearer, and the air took on a sweetness that didn't belong in a place like this — honey and candlewax and something floral, the smell of hospitality, which in the Undervault was the most dangerous smell of all.

"One question," Rafael said, as the tunnel opened ahead into a glow of lamplight and the murmur of a market that shouldn't exist underground. "These readers your order sacrificed. Eleven of them. Did the book ever — do the records say what happened to them? At the end of their prophecies?"

Wren hesitated on the threshold of the light.

"Some of the records end with the words 'the account balances,'" she said. "We never learned what it meant. I used to think it was hopeful."

"And now?"

"Now I think," Wren said, as the lamps of the border market swam into view ahead of them, and the first of the court's servants turned toward them with a smile that was a little too welcoming, "that we're about to find out."
