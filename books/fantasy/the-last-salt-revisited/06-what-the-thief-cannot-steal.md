## 6. What the Thief Cannot Steal

The border market of the Undervault smelled of wet stone and candle smoke, which Rafael decided was at least an improvement on the smell of the Stair of Turns, which had smelled exclusively of Wren's pessimism.

They had come up out of the deep passages that morning — or what passed for morning down here, a grey luminescence seeping from veins of moonstone in the ceiling like the sky had been buried alive and was still faintly glowing. The market spread through a cavern the size of a cathedral's graveyard: stalls of black wood, lanterns of green glass, and everywhere, everywhere, salt. Salt in bowls beside every door. Salt crusted along the stall edges in careful white lines. Salt strung on cords like garlands at a festival, except this was clearly not decoration.

It was lockwork.

Rafael had spent twenty years learning to read locks. This was the same language written in mineral.

"You're doing the face," Wren said beside them. She wore a hooded cloak borrowed from a very indifferent smuggler two turns back, and beneath it she had the expression of a woman who had catalogued every possible disaster and found all of them plausible.

"What face?"

"The one where you look at something complicated and decide it belongs to you."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Rafael. You looked at a prophecy book that had your name in it and your first instinct was to pick it up."

"That," Rafael said, "was research."

They drifted into the market proper, and Rafael let their professional instincts do what Wren's warnings could not: map the place. A thief's eye sorts a crowd automatically — who watches, who is watched, who has pockets worth the trouble. Here the sorting came up strange. The courtiers moved through the market like people strolling through their own memories: unhurried, proprietary, bored. They were tall in the way that things are tall when they've had centuries to practice, with skin like candle wax and eyes that caught the lantern light and held it a half-second too long. Their servants — human servants, living ones, which sat in Rafael's stomach like a swallowed stone — kept to the edges with their eyes down and their hands busy.

And every servant wore a thin collar of braided cord, pale as bone, at the throat.

"Bought souls," Rafael murmured. "You said the court buys souls. That's what that looks like?"

"That's what ownership looks like." Wren's voice had gone flat, the way it did whenever her guilt got loud enough that she needed to smother it under procedure. "The body stays. The soul is held as collateral against the contract. The person walks around, works, breathes — but there's something missing underneath. Something quiet."

"How can you tell?"

"You can't. Not by looking." She paused. "Not by looking at anyone else."

Rafael didn't answer that. They had been not-answering it for six days now: the fact that somewhere under this mountain, wearing some body they'd never seen, walked a soul Rafael had sold before they were old enough to understand what selling meant. All they knew was what the Codex had shown them in its moving ink — *a soul sold beneath the mountain* — and the sick certainty, growing daily, that they would know it when they saw it.

Which was ridiculous. Souls didn't have faces.

Rafael found a vantage point — a stack of crates beside a wine merchant's stall whose owner was deep in negotiation with a courtier over vintage years measured in dynasties — and did what Rafael Fairweather did better than anything else in any world, above or below.

They watched for a mark.

* * *

The courtier took an hour to choose, because Rafael was being picky and they knew it. Too old and the memories might be buried too deep; too young and there might be nothing worth taking yet; too important and the theft would be noticed before they cleared the market. What Rafael wanted was mid-level: someone with access to the court's business, unremarkable enough that no one guarded their mind, and — crucially — someone who had been present when the bargain was struck. Someone who had *seen the bought soul*.

Because here was the plan, such as it was, and Rafael admitted privately that it was a bad plan, but bad plans were the only kind Rafael had ever been good at: find the soul. Confirm it. Then figure out step two, which currently consisted of the single word *somehow*.

Wren had vetoed three versions of this plan already. So Rafael hadn't told her this version. They'd told her they wanted to "get a feel for the market," and she'd gone off to argue with an archivist about marginalia, which was apparently how Wren Ashvale relaxed.

The mark was a courtier of middle rank — if a creature that might be four hundred years old could be said to have a middle rank — with rings on six fingers and a merchant's fidgety way of counting his own coins. He stopped at a stall selling bottled dreams, haggled badly, and stood at last beneath a lantern with his eyes half-lidded in the particular trance of the rich and self-satisfied.

Rafael slid alongside him. Close enough to smell the candle-wax skin. Close enough to work.

The theft itself was almost gentle, when done right. No pain, no tearing. You found the thread of the memory you wanted — everyone's mind wore its memories like threads on a loom, bright ones and dull ones — and you drew it out the way you'd draw a ribbon from a spool. Rafael had lifted dying men's happiest hours without waking them. They'd taken a queen's coronation out of her head while she slept through the whole ceremony a second time. They were, they thought with the modest pride of a craftsman, very good.

Rafael found the thread. It shimmered — recent, vivid, the memory of a transaction. *The bought soul.* Perfect.

Rafael pulled.

Nothing happened.

Rafael pulled harder.

The memory didn't budge. It wasn't hidden or tangled or defended. It was — Rafael searched for the word and hated the word the moment they found it — *nailed*. Fastened down through its center with something cold and absolute, the way a specimen is pinned in a case. And around the pin, packed tight, gleaming faintly in the lamplight of the courtier's mind:

Salt.

Rafael tried again, delicately, professionally, with all the finesse of twenty years. The salt simply *was*. It wasn't a defense so much as a property, the way stone isn't soft. These memories hadn't been locked away. They'd been preserved. Fixed forever, exactly as recorded, unalterable and untouchable, like flies in amber or saints in glass.

The courtier's eyelids fluttered. He turned his head.

For one long moment he looked directly at Rafael, and his eyes were the eyes of a thing that had watched a thousand thieves and been amused by most of them.

"Careful, little pickpocket," he said pleasantly. "Everything here is kept."

Rafael withdrew the way they'd been taught to withdraw from everything in life: smoothly, smiling, already three steps away in their mind. "Forgive me, lord. I thought I recognized you."

"From where?" the courtier asked, mild and terrible. "You've never been here before. I remember everyone who's ever been here before." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "I would remember."

Rafael bowed and melted into the crowd, heart going like a drum in a parade, and did not stop walking until they reached the far edge of the market, where the stalls gave way to a wall of veined moonstone and a narrow stair leading down toward the servants' quarters, and where they leaned against the cold rock and breathed and told themselves very firmly that the courtier remembering *everyone* was fine, was probably fine, was a problem for future Rafael.

*So,* Rafael thought. *The memories are nailed shut with salt. Of course they are. Of course.*

Every trade had its rules, and rule one of Rafael's trade was: know the material. You couldn't lift a memory from someone who didn't keep memories — really keep them, hold them loosely, let them live and shift. The Undervault didn't *remember* anything. It *recorded*. Fixed, filed, salted down like fish for a winter that never ended. There was nothing loose here to steal. Nothing human enough to steal.

Except —

Rafael became aware of music.

Faint. Threaded through the market noise like a silver wire through grey cloth. Someone humming, low and absent, the way people hum when they think no one is listening — a servant girl somewhere behind the wine stalls, scrubbing something, her tune rising and falling in small patient phrases.

Rafael knew the tune.

That was the wrong sentence. Let Rafael try again: Rafael knew the tune the way you know your own front door in the dark. Not remembered — *known*, in the body, in the hands, in the hollow behind the breastbone where certain songs live. The melody rose and Rafael's breath rose with it, and when it fell, something in Rafael's chest fell with it, and they were humming the next phrase before they had decided to, matching the girl note for note across forty feet of crowded market like two halves of one conversation.

The humming stopped.

Rafael stopped.

A silence opened between the stalls, and in it Rafael heard Wren's voice in their memory, six days old and getting older: *You can't tell by looking. Not by looking at anyone else.*

Rafael should go get Wren. That was the sensible thing. Wren would say something about caution and salt and the courtier who remembers everyone, and she would be right, she was always right, it was one of the most annoying things about her.

Rafael went toward the music instead.

* * *

She was small. Nine years old, maybe ten, with dark hair cut practically short and sleeves rolled to the elbow, scrubbing the inside of a great black cask with a brush nearly as big as she was. Her collar of braided cord sat pale at her throat. She hummed as she worked, and when Rafael stepped into the lantern light she glanced up without fear — servants here, Rafael realized, had probably learned fear was a luxury item — and said:

"You hummed my song wrong."

"I did not."

"You did. The third part goes down, not up." She demonstrated, and her voice was clear and unselfconscious, and the third part went down, and Rafael felt the correction land somewhere physical, like a hand pressed flat against their sternum. Because the girl was right. The third part had always gone down. Rafael had known that since before they could read.

"What's it called?" Rafael asked. Their voice came out strange. Careful. The way you walk on ice.

The girl shrugged, brushing suds off her forearm. "It doesn't have a name. My mother sang it. Or—" she frowned, the frown of a child trying to remember something through fog, "—someone sang it. I don't remember who. I don't remember before the vault." She said it lightly, the way children report weather. "Nobody remembers before. That's the salt."

"Does it have words?"

"A few." She hesitated, then sang, barely above the sound of her own scrubbing:

*"Sleep, my salt, sleep, my own,*
*the mountain keeps what can't be known.*
*Sleep till the thirteenth moon is grown,*
*and then, my love, I'll bring you home."*

The last word hung in the cavern air, and the market noise seemed to bend around it, and Rafael stood very still among the crates with their whole body ringing like a struck bell.

*Then, my love, I'll bring you home.*

Someone had sung that. To Rafael. Before. Before the running, before the sale, before nine years old, before everything Rafael had traded away to escape — someone had knelt at a bedside in a house Rafael could not picture, in a town Rafael could not name, and sung those exact words down, and the song had sunk so deep that not even the selling could burn it out. It had survived in Rafael the way a coal survives under ash.

And now here it was, worn smooth, coming out of the mouth of a child in a bone-white collar, in the market of the court that had purchased it.

"What's your name?" Rafael asked.

"Marisol." She resumed scrubbing. Then, without looking up, with the devastating directness of children: "Why are you crying?"

"I'm not."

"There's salt on your face." She considered her own statement, and something flickered across her small face — a shadow with no shape she could catch, there and gone like a fish under ice. "That's funny," she said slowly. "That's how the memories are kept."

Rafael crouched down to her level, slow, the way you approach any wild thing you're afraid of breaking. Up close her eyes were brown and ordinary and entirely unremarkable, and Rafael looked into them anyway, searching, the way the Codex had taught them to search, and found — beneath the child, beneath the fog, beneath the salt — something old. Something patient. Something that looked back.

"You should go," Marisol said kindly, before Rafael could speak. "The Steward counts us at moonrise. If you're standing near me when he counts, he'll ask questions." She returned to her cask. Then, softly, almost to herself, in the tone of a girl repeating something she'd been told so often it had lost its meaning:

"Everything here is kept."

Rafael straightened. Somewhere across the market, the green lanterns burned, and the courtiers glided, and the salt lines gleamed white and perfect in their careful rows, and Rafael understood — with the dread blooming open in their chest like ink dropped in water — exactly what had been bought beneath the mountain, and exactly whose it was, and exactly why the lullaby had known the way home when Rafael had not.

They had to find Wren. Now.

Behind them, faint and patient, Marisol began to hum the third part again — down, not up — and the sound followed Rafael into the dark like a hand on the shoulder.
