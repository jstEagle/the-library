## 7. Thirteen Turns of Moon

The Undervault border market never truly slept, but it did something worse than sleep: it pretended to. Somewhere past the salt-stained awnings, a fiddler played the same eight bars over and over as though hoping they'd eventually become a different song, and vendors called out prices in a language that had no word for *free* and eleven words for *yours now*. Wren Ashvale sat in the corner of their rented room above a dye-shop that smelled permanently of bruised indigo, with the Salt Codex open on her knees, and tried not to feel like a woman reading her own death warrant by candlelight.

She had read it forty times since the Stair of Turns. She would read it forty more before dawn if she had to.

*When the thirteenth moon turns full beneath Thornfell's root,* the page said, in ink that shifted like oil on water whenever she blinked, *the bargain made in blood and hunger shall be sealed, unless the reader pays the whole of what was taken.*

She had underlined nothing. Keepers didn't underline. Underlining was for people who believed they'd remember, and Wren had spent ten years learning exactly how much faith could be placed in remembering.

"Rafael," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying names was a keeper's habit — you spoke a thing to bind it, and Rafael Fairweather was a thing badly in need of binding. "Rafael is going to ask me what the prophecy says. And I am going to lie."

It sounded monstrous when spoken aloud. She said it again anyway, quieter, the way you test a floorboard before trusting it with your weight.

The trouble — and there were many troubles, Wren thought, cataloguing them the way other people counted coins — the trouble was that *the whole of what was taken* was not ambiguous. It was not one of those prophecies her order had loved so dearly, all mist and mayhap, where a clever reader could squint and see whatever mercy they wanted. The old keepers had written plainly when it suited them. *Whole* meant whole. *Taken* meant taken. And what had been taken from Rafael Fairweather was everything: every memory they had ever lifted from another head, every borrowed year, every stolen love folded into themselves like letters tucked into a coat pocket. Years of other lives. Other people's weddings and funerals and first kisses, all worn so long they fit like Rafael's own skin.

Pay it back, and what remained? A child's worth of self, and even that was gutted — sold at nine years old, traded away beneath this very mountain by someone who must have been desperate enough to make a devil look generous.

Wren pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until colors bloomed.

And if Rafael didn't pay it — well. Then Marisol died. That was the shape of it, wasn't it, once you stripped away the poetry. A flickering girl in a servant's smock, humming a lullaby she shouldn't know, held together by a contract written before she could write her own name.

Unless the reader pays.

Unless.

There was a third option, of course. There was always a third option; her order had built an entire theology on the third option. Wren turned the pages backward, past the thirteenth-moon prophecy, past the Stair of Turns, to the marginalia — those small, crabbed notes in the margins that the keepers had written to each other across a century, arguments and corrections and, occasionally, jokes so dry she'd laughed at them alone in archives and then felt ashamed of it. Keeper humor. You had to have been there. Preferably dead.

*The price binds the reader only,* one note said, in faded iron-gall ink. *No other may pay it. No other may be told the whole of it.* And beneath that, in a different hand, angrier: *Why? What good is a warning the warned cannot hear?* And beneath *that*, in the first hand again, so small Wren had needed candlelight and stubbornness to read it: *Because told, it becomes a choice between two griefs. Untold, it remains one.*

She stared at that line for a long time.

*Untold, it remains one.*

Her order had believed that. Had written it into law. *A keeper shall not burden the reader with the full weight of the payment, lest the knowledge itself become the theft.* They had dressed it up as mercy. Wren had spent ten years in exile telling herself she'd left because they were cowards, because they burned readers' names off their rolls rather than admit the Codex ate the people who opened it, because Grandmother Veyl had smiled with all her teeth while she explained why the ninth reader's family hadn't been notified.

And here she sat, doing the arithmetic, and finding that the arithmetic agreed with them.

"No," Wren said to the ceiling. "Absolutely not. I am not becoming Veyl."

The ceiling, being indigo-stained plaster, declined to argue.

But the thought had its hooks in her now, the way true thoughts do. Because there was a difference — wasn't there? — between Veyl's silence and hers. Veyl had hidden the costs to protect the *order*, to keep the machinery running smooth, to spare herself the unpleasantness of watching readers weep. Wren would be hiding it to give Rafael time. Time to find another way, or time to decide, or simply time — unweighted, unbent time, the kind you couldn't buy in any market on either side of the mountain. If she told Rafael tonight, every plan Rafael made afterward would be made inside the shadow of the price. Every kindness would be a farewell. Every joke would have a funeral standing behind it.

Whereas if she said nothing —

If she said nothing, and the thirteenth moon rose, and Rafael learned the truth with hours instead of weeks remaining —

Then Rafael would learn that Wren had known. Would count back through every shared meal, every night watch, every moment of the growing, terrifying tenderness between them, and find it poisoned at the root. Would understand that every *we'll figure it out* had been spoken by someone holding the answer behind her back like a card marked on its face.

Wren looked down at the Codex, and the Codex, being a book, did not look back, which was somehow more unnerving.

"You're enjoying this," she told it.

* * *

Rafael came back an hour past midnight smelling of rain and someone else's tobacco, which meant they'd been working the market crowds, lifting memories off drunk sailors and homesick merchants the way other people picked pockets — lightly, professionally, with a flourish only the victim never saw.

They came through the door without knocking, because knocking was a courtesy Rafael considered decorative. "You're still awake."

"I'm always awake. I'm a disgraced exile haunted by a prophetic book. Sleep and I are in a fight." Wren closed the Codex — too quickly, she realized a half-second too late, and Rafael's eyes tracked the motion the way a cat tracks a falling spoon.

"What's it done now?" Rafael dropped onto the bed across from her, boots and all, and began unlacing them with the absent efficiency of someone who had slept in worse places than boots. "Rewritten itself? Tried to eat you? Last week it tried to eat me, or possibly I dreamed that. Hard to say. Everything down here has teeth."

"It's been quiet." This was true. This was also, Wren noted with a fresh spike of guilt, exactly the sort of sentence liars favor. "Quiet books worry me more than loud ones."

"Mm." Rafael tipped their head back against the wall. Up close, in the candlelight, they looked tired in a way that went deeper than the heist-work — tired somewhere under the competence, in the load-bearing walls. "I went looking today. For the soul. Or for anything about the soul. Asked around the market, quiet-like." A pause. "There's a girl. Servant in the Steward's household. Young — younger than us, much younger. She hums when she thinks no one's listening."

Wren felt the cold arrive in her chest before the meaning did, the way winter comes through a door ahead of the man carrying it. "Hums what?"

"That's the thing." Rafael frowned at their own hands. "I don't know the song. But I knew it. The way you know a word that's on the tip of your tongue — except my tongue has never been to wherever that song lives. It was like hearing my own voice come out of a stranger's mouth." They shook their head, laughing a little, and the laugh had cracks in it. "Which is nonsense. Obviously. I steal memories for a living; I know what borrowed feels like, and that wasn't—" They stopped. Looked up. "Wasn't mine. Was it."

It was not a question. Rafael Fairweather, who had built a whole self out of other people's yesterdays, knew precisely what theirs felt like, and had just heard something underneath the floorboards of their own childhood.

"The Codex says the soul was sold," Wren said carefully. "Souls carry things. Songs. Names. Lullabies are old magic — mothers sing what their mothers sang, all the way down. It doesn't mean—"

"Don't manage me, Wren."

The words landed gently and cut deep, which was Rafael's gift. Wren closed her mouth.

"I'm not asking you to tell me what the book says," Rafael went on, softer. "I know you've got keeper business in your head you're not sharing — you get a particular wrinkle right there—" they tapped the spot between their own eyebrows, "—when you're deciding how much truth I can afford. It's insulting, by the way. I can afford plenty. But fine. Your secrets are your own; I respect a professional. Just don't stand between me and the girl."

"She might not be the soul at all."

"And if she is?"

Wren thought about the margin note. *Told, it becomes a choice between two griefs.* She thought about Rafael walking toward the Steward's household tomorrow with light feet instead of heavy ones, and whether that lightness was a gift or a debt being quietly run up in her name.

"Then we get her back," Wren said. "Whatever it takes."

Rafael studied her for a long moment, and Wren watched them decide — visibly, the way you watch weather change — to let it go. "Whatever it takes," they repeated. "That's a dangerous thing to say in this town. Everything down here takes."

"Yes," Wren said. "I'm beginning to notice that."

Rafael grinned then, sudden and crooked, and some of the tiredness burned off. "Look at us. Two disasters in a rented room over a dye-shop, planning to rob a moonlit court of a child's soul. My mother always said I'd end up respectable or dead. She never mentioned a third option." They lay back, boots finally off, and within minutes their breathing slowed into something that was almost trust.

Wren sat with the book on her knees and listened.

* * *

She did not sleep. She worked instead, because work was the only prayer she'd ever trusted.

By the grey hour before dawn she had gone through the marginalia twice more, cross-referencing hands and dates, and had assembled the truth into its least merciful shape:

One: the payment was *the whole of what was taken* — every stolen memory, returned to its origin, poured back into the world. Not offered. Given. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

Two: it bound the reader only. Wren could not pay it for Rafael. She had checked three separate annotations hoping to find a loophole, a substitution clause, anything — and had found instead a margin note from 214 years ago, in Grandmother Veyl's precise, beloved, hateful script: *We looked. There is no substitute. Write that here so no fool looks again.* So some earlier version of Wren had already searched this same passage for the same escape, and had found the same wall, and had left a message for her across two centuries: *don't bother.*

Three: the prophecy did not say *who* freed the soul. Only that the reader must pay. Which meant — Wren turned this over slowly, feeling its edges — which meant the Codex required a reader, yes, but it did not require the reader to *survive* the paying. It did not even require the reader to remain a person afterward. Her order had written *unless* into the text and let generations assume it meant salvation. It might mean salvage. Dragging wreckage ashore.

And four — the item she kept circling back to, the splinter under the nail — Rafael did not know. Rafael lay three feet away, sleeping the sleep of someone who believed their companion would tell them if the ground ahead opened, and Wren held the map of the chasm behind her back and called it protection.

*Told, it becomes a choice between two griefs.*

"But it's *their* grief," she whispered to the candle. "That's the whole point. It was always supposed to be theirs."

The candle guttered and recovered. Outside, the false-dawn bells of the market rang their flat bronze note, and somewhere below, the dye-shop's first apprentice began hauling water, bucket after bucket, a rhythm like a heart deciding to keep beating.

Wren looked at the Codex. The ink on the thirteenth-moon page had shifted again in the night — she was almost sure of it — the words rearranging themselves by fractions, the way ice shifts on a river before it takes the bridge. Prophecies weren't predictions; her order had taught her that much honestly. They were *arguments*. The book wanted something. Books that wanted things were the most dangerous things in any world, and this one had waited a century for Rafael Fairweather specifically, had arranged a smuggler's cache and an unguarded vault and a thief's curiosity like a fisherman arranging bait.

Which raised a question she had been refusing to ask, and asked it now because the candle was low and lies need darkness to grow and she was trying to starve hers:

If the Codex had arranged everything else — the cache, the date, the pull — had it also arranged the girl? The lullaby? Had it hung a child's soul in front of Rafael like a lantern, knowing exactly what a thief who'd sold their own childhood would do?

And if so — whose design was she protecting Rafael from? The court's? The book's?

Or her own order's, reaching out of the grave through her hands?

Wren wrote none of this down. Writing made it real, and real things could be found on your body if the court took you, and the court took people; she'd seen the ledgers. Instead she fed the last of the candle to its own flame and memorized the passage one final time, the way you memorize the face of someone you're about to betray, so that later — whichever later came — you'd be able to say honestly that you had looked at them properly.

Below, the apprentice dropped a bucket. Rafael stirred, muttered something in a language Wren didn't recognize — one of the stolen tongues, surfacing like a fish — and rolled over.

In the grey light, the Salt Codex lay open on the table where Wren had left it, and she would have sworn, would have sworn on her order and her exile and every name she'd ever burned, that the page had turned itself to the one about the servant girl who hummed.
