## 8. The Lullaby in the Dark

I had stolen a thousand songs and never once been able to keep one.

That's the trouble with my trade that nobody warns you about. I could lift a memory whole — a wedding dance, a mother's voice, the smell of rain on a roof you grew up under — and carry it out of someone's head like a coin from a pocket. But it never sat right in mine. Borrowed memories are like borrowed coats: warm enough, but cut for somebody else's shoulders. So the songs I stole from dying smugglers and lovesick courtiers always faded by morning, leaving nothing but the shape of where they'd been.

Which is why the lullaby stopped me dead in a corridor of the Undervault at what I judged — though judgment down here was a fool's game — to be two hours past midnight.

It wasn't that I knew the song. That was the horror of it. I didn't know it, and yet my mouth was already shaping the words before my mind caught up. My feet had turned down a side passage without consulting me. Somewhere beneath my breastbone, something old and patient had heard its name called and started walking.

The corridor sloped downward, lit by lamps of pale glass that burned without flame. Salt crusted the walls in long white veins, and when I dragged my fingertips along them — a habit Wren had scolded me for twice, since everything down here was probably load-bearing, or sacred, or both — the salt came away warm.

*Warm.* Everything in this place was wrong in small ways that added up to one large way: it was all *kept*. Nothing here belonged to itself.

The singing drew closer. A child's voice, thin and clear as a wire.

I should have gone back for Wren. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the person who made them has sold off the parts of himself that would care. I should have gone back for Wren, because Wren had spent ten years studying this book and this court and this whole sunken machinery of bargains, and I had spent ten years stealing memories from people too tired to notice, and between us only one of those educations was going to be useful tonight.

But the lullaby pulled, and I followed. Memory-thieves make terrible listeners to their own good sense. We're built out of other people's impulses; ours never fit either.

* * *

She was in a linen closet.

Not a cell, not a shrine, not any of the ominous chambers I'd been bracing myself for since we'd come under Thornfell — a linen closet, tucked behind the servants' stair of some wing of the Undervault that even the salt seemed to have forgotten. Shelves of folded grey cloth rose on either side of her, and she sat cross-legged on the floor among a drift of socks she was ostensibly pairing, singing to herself because there was no one else to sing to.

She was small. Eight, maybe nine years old, with dark hair chopped short in a way that suggested scissors applied by an impatient adult, and a smudge of ash on one cheekbone. She wore the grey livery of a servant child, sleeves rolled three times over. Her hands were quick and clever, sorting socks by some private taxonomy known only to her.

And when she saw me standing in the doorway, she didn't startle. She looked up with the flat, unimpressed calm of a child who has already been disappointed by every adult available and sees no reason to extend credit to a new applicant.

"You're not the laundry steward," she said.

"No," I agreed.

"He sends people. When he thinks I'm hiding." She held up a sock, considered it, put it down. "I am hiding. But he sends *stewards*, and stewards knock like they're sorry. You didn't knock."

"I got lost," I said, which was true in the way most of my honest statements were true — technically, and beside the point.

"You don't look lost." She tilted her head, studying me with an attention that made my skin prickle, the way a lockpick feels a tumbler shift. "You look like you followed my song."

My mouth went dry. "What makes you say that?"

"Because everyone does." She said it simply, without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "The song goes through the walls. People hear it and then they come stand in the doorway and look sad and then they leave again." She resumed sorting. "You can leave now if you want. I won't count it against you. I've stopped counting."

I should have left. I should have gone back up the passage and found Wren and told her I'd found a child and let the keeper's trained mind do whatever keepers' trained minds did with children found humming impossible songs in soul-courts.

Instead I sat down on the floor across from her, among the socks.

Her eyebrows went up. It was, I suspected, the first genuinely new thing to happen to her in some time.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Marisol." Then, quickly, like a price tag she'd learned to display: "That's what they call me. Here."

"And what did they call you —"

"Nobody calls me anything else anymore." She paired two socks with a decisive snap. "Before doesn't have a name. Before is just before."

*Before.* The word landed somewhere under my ribs and sat there, heavy, familiar, a stone I recognized without knowing why I recognized it.

"Marisol," I said carefully. "Can I tell you something strange?"

"You already did. You sat down."

Fair. "When you were singing. The lullaby. Where did you learn it?"

She shrugged. "I've always known it. Since before." Her fingers stilled on the socks. "Have *you* heard it before? Outside, I mean. Up-top?"

"No," I said. And then, because lying to children is a debt that compounds: "But I knew it anyway. The second verse. The part about the sea."

Something moved in her face — hope, maybe, or the scar tissue where hope used to live. She began to hum, low, testing, and when she reached the end of the first verse she stopped and looked at me with her chin lifted, daring me.

And my mouth, traitorously, gently, kept going.

"*Sleep, the tide will hold your door,*" I sang, in a language I could not name and did not need to, "*sleep, the stars have seen this shore; whatever's lost, whatever's sold, the moon keeps watch, the moon keeps hold.*"

Marisol stared at me.

Then she threw herself forward and hugged me, hard, arms around my neck, face buried in my shoulder, and she was shaking — not crying, exactly, or not only crying, but shaking the way a rope shakes when something at the far end of it has finally been tied off after years of slack.

I held her. I didn't know why my eyes were burning. I didn't know why holding this stranger-child felt like coming home to a house I had no memory of ever living in — and that phrase snagged in me, *a house I had no memory of*, because that was supposed to be my specialty, wasn't it? I was the one who remembered things. Other people's things, yes, but I remembered *everything*. My head was a warehouse of a thousand lives. There should not have been room inside me for a hole this shape.

There was. The hole fit the song perfectly.

* * *

We sat among the socks for a long while, and she talked, and I listened, and everything she said made the hole wider.

She didn't remember arriving at the Undervault. She remembered light, and cold, and a voice reading terms aloud in a language of clauses. She remembered being told she was *bought*, and not understanding, and then understanding, and then deciding — with that same flat practicality she'd aimed at me — that understanding changed nothing, so she might as well fold linens.

"They're very particular about the linens," she confided. "If you fold them wrong the Steward knows. He always knows. He says the salt remembers." A pause. "Do you think salt remembers things?"

"Yes," I said, thinking of a century-old codex with my own name drying in its margins like a fly in amber. "I think salt is the worst possible thing to be made of, if you're a secret."

That got a laugh out of her, small and startled, as if she'd surprised herself with it.

"Your song," I said. "Where you're from — before. Was there a sea?"

"I think so." Her brow furrowed. "There's a door in my head that opens onto water. Just water and a porch rail. And someone's hands." She held up her own small hands, turning them over. "Big hands. Brown. They're braiding something. Grass, or hair. I can't see the rest. Every time I try to see the rest, it's like the door gets smaller."

I knew that door. Gods forgive me, I knew that door from the inside — knew the sensation of a memory worn smooth as riverglass from handling, a scene revisited so many thousands of times it had gone soft-edged and holy. Except I didn't know it. I couldn't. I had no porch, no sea, no braiding hands anywhere in my inventory. My earliest memory — my genuine, owned, first-hand earliest memory — was a road. Rain on a road, and a woman's voice saying *run*, and nothing before it, a wall where a childhood should be.

Wren had asked me about that wall once, early on, in her careful keeper's way, and I'd given her the answer I gave everyone: *I traded it away. That's how the trade works. You sell your worst years.*

I had always assumed the wall was made of the bad stuff. The beatings, the hunger, the nights I didn't ask about. That's what I told clients, when they flinched at the price. *You'll lose the nightmares. Consider it a kindness bundled into the fee.*

Sitting on a closet floor with a child's head against my shoulder, listening to a lullaby rise through my chest from some cellar of me I hadn't known I owned, the truth came up through that hole like water through a cracked hull:

I hadn't sold my worst years. I'd sold my best ones. All of them. Everything I loved before the age of nine, poured out in one transaction on a rainy road, to buy a child named Rafael Fairweather a running start.

And it hadn't gone into the void. Souls and their contents don't go into the void. They go where they're sent.

They'd been sent *here*.

* * *

"Hey," Marisol said, poking my arm. "You went somewhere."

"Just a long way," I managed. "Short trip, long way."

"Wren does that too. Your friend. She stands in rooms and her eyes go all —" Marisol squinted, searching for the word, "— *archived*."

"You've met Wren?"

"She came by the laundry. Asked questions about the Steward's schedule. I told her the schedule is that he's always there, because he's a *creep*." The word was delivered with tremendous satisfaction, clearly a recent acquisition and a prized one. "Is she your wife?"

"What? No. No, we — she's —" I floundered. Ten years of stolen vocabularies and not one of them contained a word for what Wren Ashvale was to me. "She's the person who grabs on," I said finally. "When I fall into things. She grabs on instead of letting me vanish alone."

Marisol considered this with the gravity it deserved. "So your wife."

"I'm not having this conversation with a nine-year-old in a linen closet."

"That's what someone with a wife would say." She returned triumphantly to her socks.

And I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since a book of prophecies had reached out of a smuggler's vault and taken me by the throat — and the laugh broke something loose, and what came through the break wasn't funny at all.

Because I looked at her — at the quick clever hands, at the stubborn set of her chin, at the way she'd organized even her captivity into a system she could master — and recognition went through me like a blade finding a seam.

Those were my hands. Not literally, not in any sense the world would certify, but I knew that grip, that economy of motion, the instinct to impose order on small controllable things when the large uncontrollable ones loomed. I knew that flat-eyed courage. I had watched it in mirrors my whole life and called it temperament, called it nature, never asking what a *nature* was made of or where it had been purchased.

A soul wears a body the way fire wears a lamp. The lamp was Marisol's. The fire —

The fire had been mine, once. Sold on a rainy road before I was old enough to understand that *everything I love* is not an abstraction but a list, and lists get read aloud in courts beneath mountains, and items on lists can be kept.

"You're staring," Marisol said, not looking up.

"I know."

"You're doing the archived face."

"I know." I swallowed. "Marisol. If I told you that I think — that I might be able to take you out of here. Away from the Steward, away from the linens. Would you come?"

She looked up at that. And for a moment the flat practicality fell away and underneath it was just a child, lit by flameless lamps, in a kingdom of kept things, wanting so badly it frightened her.

"The song people always say that," she whispered. "In my dreams. *Come away, come away.* But dreams don't have hands."

"This one does." I took hers. Small, warm, calloused from work no child should do. "This one has hands, and a bad reputation, and a friend who's smarter than me, and thirteen minus however-many turns of the moon left to figure out how."

Her fingers tightened around mine. And beneath our joined hands, faint as breath, I felt it — the pull, the old tidal tug, the song recognizing itself. Her soul leaning toward me through her skin like a plant toward a window.

Like a daughter toward a father she couldn't remember.

Or a brother. Or simply the other half of one ruined bargain, finally in the same room.

Down the passage, boots on stone — measured, unhurried, the tread of someone who knocked like he was sorry and was never, ever sorry. The salt veins along the wall flickered, dimmed, brightened, as if the mountain itself had drawn a breath to announce him.

Marisol went still. "He knows you're here," she breathed. "The salt tells him everything. Hide —"

Too late. The lamplight in the corridor bent, and the Steward's shadow arrived before he did.

Behind me, faint and far above, I heard another set of footsteps take the stairs at a run — Wren, coming fast, and I prayed to every god whose names I'd stolen that she'd gotten to the archives first, because I had a sudden, sick certainty that the answers to all of this — the sale, the rainy road, the parent who signed — were written down somewhere in this court, in a ledger, in salt.

And that the Steward was coming to make sure I never read them.
