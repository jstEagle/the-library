## 9. Bargains Remembered

The archivists of the Undervault were, Wren had to admit, a well-organized people. Everything in their domain was filed, cross-referenced, and stamped — souls, debts, grudges, the names of everyone who had ever wronged them and precisely how much interest had accrued. It was the most orderly place she had ever been, and it was built entirely out of other people's misery.

She found that she hated it less than her own order's archive, which was saying something. At least here the cruelty was honest.

She had come down from the border market alone, leaving Rafael above with Marisol and a flimsy excuse about checking salt routes. Rafael had accepted it with the particular squint of a thief who knew a lie when they heard one but was too tired to audit it. Wren had felt the lie settle in her chest like a swallowed stone. She was getting good at those lately. She didn't want to be good at anything anymore, if this was the skill set.

Now she stood before an archivist named Brother Quillon, who had the face of a man who had spent three centuries being disappointed by paper. He was small, gray, and precise, with ink stains up both forearms like old tattoos, and he regarded Wren over half-moon spectacles with the weary patience of someone who had processed every kind of supplicant and expected nothing better from her.

"You want," he said slowly, as though tasting each word for rot, "the original record of a sale."

"A soul-sale," Wren said. "Beneath Thornfell mountain. Roughly twenty years ago."

"Soul-sales are sealed records." He folded his hands atop his desk, which was so clean it gleamed, which meant he did no work at it, which meant his real work happened somewhere else. "Sealed by court law. Sealed by buyer's privilege. Sealed, I might add, by common decency, if we believed in such a thing down here, which we do not, because it doesn't file properly."

"I'm invoking keeper's privilege."

Something flickered behind the spectacles. Not surprise — archivists were never surprised; they merely updated their ledgers — but a kind of grim relish, the way a customs officer perks up when someone declares something interesting.

"Keeper's privilege," he repeated. "There hasn't been a keeper down here since—" He stopped. His eyes went to her hands, to the faded scar across her left palm where, years ago, a younger and stupider Wren Ashvale had pressed her thumb into wet wax to swear an oath she would spend a decade failing to keep. "Oh," he said softly. "You're one of *those*. You're Ashvale order. The ones who wrote the thing."

"The Codex wasn't written by my order," Wren said. "It was written by four keepers who then lied about it to the rest of us. I've spent ten years cleaning up their handwriting."

"And yet you carry their seal." Quillon tapped his long fingers together, fingertip to fingertip, forming a little cathedral of bones. "You understand my dilemma. If I show you the record, I am complicit. If I refuse you, you will simply steal it, because your kind always does. Thieves of memory make the worst patrons. You take the reading and leave the book."

"I'm not going to steal from you, Brother Quillon."

"Everyone says that." He sighed, and the sigh had the weight of a man opening a drawer he'd hoped never to open again. "But you'll notice I'm already standing up. That is what a lifetime of filing other people's sins does to a person. You develop an instinct for when resistance is futile, and yours arrived the moment you walked in smelling of surface air and bad decisions."

* * *

He took her deep into the stacks, past shelves of contract-scrolls bound in pale hide that Wren refused to look at too closely, past jars of salt labeled in a script that predated the court itself, down a stair so narrow they had to go single file. The air grew colder and tasted faintly of iron. Somewhere below them, water dripped in a rhythm almost exactly like a heartbeat, and Wren chose not to think about whose heartbeat the architects had been imitating.

"Twenty years ago," Quillon said, talking to fill the dark, or perhaps because silence down here felt like eavesdropping. "A hard winter. Do you remember the hard winters? Of course you don't, you're barely old enough to have opinions. The passes closed early. The mining town at Thornfell's foot lost its vein and its road in the same season. People do desperate mathematics in seasons like that." A pause. "They start counting children as currency."

Wren said nothing. Her boots found each step carefully. She had known, in the abstract, what she would find down here. Knowing a thing abstractly, she was learning, was a completely different animal from knowing it with your hands.

The chamber at the bottom was round and low-ceilinged, lit by lamps that burned without flame, giving off light the color of moonlight through ice. In the center stood a single pedestal, and on the pedestal lay a scroll case of black lacquer, banded in silver, stamped with the Undervault's sigil: a crescent cradling an eye.

"Contract of sale, soul of a minor, executed beneath Thornfell," Quillon recited, unsealing the case with a key from around his neck. "Seller: one parent, name withheld per standard practice — we find sellers live longer when the court can't look them up. Buyer: the Office of the Steward, acting for the moon-court. Consideration rendered: passage out of debt, grain for two winters, and the erasure of the seller's ledger." He unrolled the parchment with the tenderness of a man handling something that might bite. Which, Wren reflected, it might.

"Show me," she said.

Quillon turned the scroll toward her, and there it was, in the court's beautiful, terrible hand — the same flowing script as the Salt Codex, every letter a small act of elegance performed upon someone's ruin.

And beside the formal clauses, in a different ink, a marginal note in a cramped, hurried script. An addendum. Filed by whoever had witnessed the transaction.

Wren read it. Then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had slid off it the way eyes slide off things the mind isn't ready to carry.

*"Sale contested at execution. The child was present. Nine years of age. When the mark was made, the child struck the broker, bit through their own thumb to the bone — see physician's note, appended — and fled into the mountain rather than let the soul be taken whole. Pursuit failed. The soul was taken in part, the remainder fleeing with the child. Court ruling: partial extraction sufficient. The child carries the difference. Note: the child remembered nothing of what was lost. This is recorded as mercy. The undersigned records it as theft."*

The signature beneath was small and sharp. Wren recognized the hand. Every keeper would. It belonged to Mother Cassarine, founder of the Ashvale order, the woman whose portrait hung in the great hall with the inscription *SHE KEPT WHAT OTHERS COULD NOT.*

"She was there," Wren said. Her voice came out strange, flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "Cassarine witnessed the sale. She wrote this."

"Cassarine," Quillon echoed. "Yes. Your order's saint. She did a great deal of witnessing, back in the day. Witnessing is how orders like yours get started. Someone sees something unbearable and decides the correct response is to build an institution around it." He rolled the scroll closed with a soft, final sound. "You didn't know this part."

"No."

"Then your ten years of cleaning up after her just became considerably longer." He resettled his spectacles. "Shall I tell you the rest? There is a rest. There is always a rest; documents are like onions that way, and equally likely to make you cry."

"Tell me the rest."

* * *

They sat, because Quillon insisted that difficult readings be done sitting, on the grounds that standing readers fainted onto his scrolls and he was tired of replacing them. Two stools. One lamp. Between them, the black lacquer case, empty now, still smelling faintly of salt and old silver.

"The partial soul," Quillon said, "was brought here and placed in holding, per custom. Souls in holding need vessels — raw soul-stuff burns through anything inert, don't ask me how I know, it involved a very regrettable decade — so the court places them in borrowed flesh. Usually volunteers. Sometimes not. In this case—" he tapped the scroll case "—a servant-child of the court, an orphan, age-matched. Standard practice. The vessel grows up around the held soul the way a tree grows around a fence post. Mostly harmless. Occasionally the two of them hum the same songs without knowing why."

Wren thought of Marisol in the market courtyard, humming a lullaby in a language she couldn't possibly know, and had to put her hand flat against her knee to keep it steady.

"The child who ran," Quillon continued, watching her with those dry, filing-cabinet eyes. "Grew up, presumably. Somewhere above. Took a trade, I'd wager — children who lose themselves young tend to go looking in other people. And now, unless my instincts have gone entirely to mold, that child has come down my stairs following the smell of their own missing piece."

"How—"

"You flinched at the word 'lullaby' forty minutes ago and you haven't stopped touching your left palm since. Also you asked for the record of a soul-sale before asking whether the court could be bribed, which means you're doing this for someone else, which means someone else is here, which means—" he spread his hands, a gesture of professional completeness "—archivist. We connect entries. It's the entire job."

Wren laughed. It came out cracked down the middle, half humor and half something that wanted very much to be grief but hadn't finished applying for the position.

"So the soul in the vault," she said. "It's Rafael's. Half of it. They've been walking around all these years with half a soul and no idea what's missing."

"Worse than no idea." Quillon's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse, the way rain is worse than hail because it takes longer. "The court ruling extracted the memory along with the soul. Everything the child loved before age nine — parents, home, the town, the sound of their own mother's voice — pulled out with the piece that fled toward it. That's how the extraction worked, you see. The soul clings to what it loves. Pull the soul, the loves come with it, like burrs on a coat." He paused. "The child didn't run away from the sale. The child ran away carrying everything the sale tried to steal, and the court caught up anyway and took it out of them on the road. There are records of the pursuit. Would you like—"

"No." Too fast, too loud. She moderated herself. "No. Thank you."

Because she could picture it. That was the problem with being a keeper; her whole life had trained her to picture things accurately. A nine-year-old on a frozen road, running with everything they loved stuffed inside them like contraband, and then hands, and then — emptiness. Waking up somewhere warm with strangers telling them they'd had a fever, and no mother's face in their head at all. No lullaby. Nothing.

And growing up into a person who stole memories for a living. Who walked through other people's happiest hours like a beggar pressing their face to bakery windows. Who had built an entire self out of rented rooms.

"Oh, you *idiot*," Wren whispered, and it took her a moment to realize she wasn't talking to Rafael. She was talking to Cassarine. To the order. To the whole elegant machinery of witness-and-institution that saw an unbearable thing and responded by writing it down beautifully and doing nothing. *This is recorded as mercy.* Four hundred years of keepers quoting that line as wisdom, and it had been a confession all along, signed by the woman who watched a child bleed on a mountain road and filed the paperwork.

"Keeper Ashvale," Quillon said quietly. He had used her name-title for the first time. "I'm going to say a thing now that I am not permitted to say, and I would appreciate it if you'd forget my face afterward, which shouldn't be hard, everyone does."

"Say it."

"You're deciding right now whether to tell them." He nodded upward, toward the market, toward Marisol, toward the flickering candle-end of a person who had hired Wren's guilt and was paying her in trust. "The whole truth. What was taken. Who sold them and why. Whether the parent lives — and before you ask, that entry is sealed even from me, so don't waste the breath. You're weighing truth against mercy, and I can see it costing you, because your order trained you to believe those are opposites." He folded his hands. "Here is my professional observation, offered free of charge, worth what you paid: the people in these records who were spared the truth did not thank anyone later. The mercy reads well in the margin. It reads terribly in the life."

"They're nine years of memories," Wren said. "Their whole childhood. If I tell them what's missing, they'll feel the shape of it forever. Every day. A hole with edges."

"Yes," Quillon agreed. "Whereas if you don't tell them, they'll simply be a hole with no edges, wandering around, stealing other people's shapes to fill it." He shrugged, a small, gray, devastating shrug. "I file both outcomes. Neither files neatly."

* * *

She climbed back up through the stacks alone, Quillon having returned to his desk to pretend she had never existed, and the stair was narrow and cold and gave her far too much time to think, which was the trouble with stairs; they were essentially thinking machines built by people with too much masonry and too little sense.

Truth or mercy. Her whole life, the order had answered that question the same way: *mercy*, always mercy, mercy meaning *we decide what you can bear*, mercy meaning *the margin note instead of the scream*. Cassarine's mercy. Look how that had aged. Look at Rafael Fairweather, thirty-some years old and hollow as a flute, playing other people's music because none of their own remained.

And yet.

She thought of Rafael's face in the market when Marisol had first hummed the lullaby — the way they'd gone absolutely still, the way a man goes still when he hears his own name spoken in a language he doesn't recognize. They already suspected. Thieves always suspect; suspicion is the tool of the trade. What they didn't have was the shape of it. The edges.

*A hole with edges,* she'd said. But maybe that was wrong. Maybe an edge was a place you could push against. Maybe a hole with edges was, in the end, a door.

By the time she reached the top of the stair, her mind was made, and then immediately unmade, and then made again, which she supposed was what deciding actually looked like when you did it honestly instead of institutionally.

She found them in the courtyard behind the salt-market, sitting on an overturned crate with Marisol nearby, teaching the girl a clapping game with the fierce concentration of someone who had learned it yesterday from somebody else's memory and was passing it on like contraband kindness. The girl laughed at some fumbled rhythm, and Rafael laughed too, easy and bright, and Wren stood in the doorway and felt the truth sit in her chest like a stone she was supposed to either swallow or spit out, and could do neither.

Rafael looked up and saw her, and their smile shifted into that squint again — the auditor's squint, the one that priced lies on sight.

"You've got a face on," they said. "The specific face of a person who found out something and is deciding whether I can afford it. I know that face. I've worn it in mirrors." They stood, brushing off their coat, and their voice stayed light but their eyes didn't. "So. Out with it, keeper. How bad is the ledger?"

Behind them, Marisol had stopped clapping. The girl was watching Wren with those wide, patient eyes, and humming under her breath, barely audible, the tune threading through the cold air of the courtyard like smoke finding a draft.

Wren opened her mouth.

Closed it.

And what came out instead — God help her, what her mouth chose, on its own, while the rest of her was still voting — was:

"There's something I have to show you. In the Steward's wing. Tonight."

Which was true. And also a coward's way of buying one more hour, one more afternoon of clapping games and laughter, before she handed Rafael the knife and asked them to hold it themselves.

Rafael studied her a moment longer. Then, softly, with terrible gentleness:

"Wren. Whatever it is — you're a terrible liar, and I'm a wonderful thief. Don't make me steal it from you."

The lullaby drifted between them, sweet and small and unbearably familiar, and Wren realized the hour she'd bought had just expired.
