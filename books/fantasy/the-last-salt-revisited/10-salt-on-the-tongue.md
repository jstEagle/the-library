## 10. Salt on the Tongue

The Steward received me in a room that smelled like a bank vault and a church had argued about who got to keep the smell.

I'd been expecting something grander, honestly. When you're summoned — and make no mistake, I was summoned; two salt-gray servants had appeared at my door in the border market with the particular politeness of people who would drag you if polite didn't work — you expect a throne. You expect shadows and a voice like grinding stone. Instead, the Steward sat at a plain desk of black wood, small and neat as an accountant, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on a nose so sharp it could have opened letters.

He was writing. He let me stand there while he finished his sentence, which was its own kind of message: *you are not urgent*.

"Rafael Fairweather," he said at last, without looking up. "The memory-thief. Do you know what we call your kind down here?"

"I imagine it's not 'darling.'"

"Tourists." He set down his pen and removed the spectacles, folding them with a small click. "Thieves who come down for a season, lift a little wonder, sell it up top for coin, and go home thinking they've seen the deep water." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm even, which frightened me more than any snarl could have. "But you're not a tourist, are you. You came for inventory."

"Marisol," I said. "The girl."

"The girl." He said it the way a jeweler says *the stone*. "You've met her. Of course you have. The lullaby found you." He tilted his head, studying me the way I'd studied a hundred marks across a hundred crowded rooms — looking for the seam, the place where a person opens. "Tell me, thief. What did it feel like? Hearing a song you sold before you were old enough to write your own name?"

My hands wanted to shake. I kept them still through a trick I learned young: hold something invisible, grip it hard. A rope, maybe. Or a hand.

"It felt," I said, "like someone reading my mail."

That surprised a laugh out of him — a real one, brief and unguarded, gone almost before it arrived. "Good. Honest. You'd be amazed how many people lie to me in this room. They think flattery is currency down here." He gestured to the chair across from his desk. "Sit. Let's do business. That's why you came, isn't it? Not to grieve. To trade."

I sat. The chair was uncomfortable in a deliberate way — just enough wrong to keep you from relaxing, from settling into the conversation. Everything in the Undervault was like that. Nothing let you forget where you were.

"Here is what I know," the Steward said, lacing his ink-stained fingers. "A child's soul was sold beneath this mountain twenty-six years ago. Contracted, sealed, filed. It has been — I want to say *content*, but that's not honest — it has been *kept*. Recently it was placed, per court custom, into a vessel. A servant girl. Marisol. And now a famous thief walks into my market asking questions with her whole face." He spread his hands. "So. Make me an offer."

"I'll work for you," I said. It came out steadier than I felt. "One year. Every soul-contract in this court, I can read the memories bound into them — who sold, who bought, what was promised. Debts get disputed because no one remembers the terms anymore. I remember them. Give me access, and I'll settle every contested ledger you have. In exchange, you tear up one contract and give the girl back her name."

He listened all the way through, which I hadn't expected. Most powerful men interrupt. They can't bear to let a sentence finish without their fingerprints on it.

Then he said, quietly, "No."

"No?"

"You misunderstand what you're offering. You think skill is rare. Skill is common. This mountain is full of creatures who can pull a memory out of a head — some of them do it with their teeth." He leaned forward, and for a moment the accountant fell away and I saw what sat underneath: something old and patient and very, very hungry. "What's rare is *willingness*. A human who will open another person's skull-matters and take, and take, and sleep soundly after. Do you know how many souls in my ledgers belong to people who said yes exactly once? One yes. That's usually all it takes to end up in a book."

"I'm not selling you my soul."

"You sold it once already," he said gently. "At age nine. Your parents signed; but a sale requires consent of the goods, and you — clever child — ran. Ran up the stairs, out of the dark, out of the deal. The contract held anyway, mostly, enough. But there's been a loose thread in you ever since, hasn't there? A feeling like a coat that doesn't fit. Like a room missing furniture." His smile returned, softer now, almost kind. "You've spent your whole life taking other people's pasts because yours has a hole in it shaped like everything you loved before you were nine years old. Am I close?"

I gripped the invisible rope harder.

"You're a liar," I said.

"Constantly," he agreed cheerfully. "But not about this. Here is my counter-offer, since you came to trade and I am nothing if not courteous." He slid a sheet of paper across the desk. Blank. Just blank paper, cream-colored, waiting. "Wren Ashvale."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped stone.

"She carries the Salt Codex's sister-volume — or she did, before her order burned half their archive and exiled her for failing to destroy the rest. Her head holds ten years of marginalia, corrections, cipher-keys. She is, at this moment, the most valuable unread text on this side of the mountain." He tapped the blank page with one stained finger. "Steal her memories of the Codex. All of them. Cleanly, completely — I'll know if you leave change. Bring them to me, and I return the soul to the girl. Whole contract torn, no tricks, sealed by court law, which — whatever else you believe about us — binds us absolutely."

"That's not a trade. That's asking me to gut her."

"It's asking you to choose," he said. "Which is what trades are. Everything else is haggling over price." He rose, and up close he was taller than he'd seemed, unfolding like something that had been pretending to be small. "You should know the shape of the choice before the moon does it for you. Thirteen turns, thief. We're past seven now. On the thirteenth, the bargain seals itself, no one's choice required, and the girl goes into the vault forever — and so does every scrap of soul in her, including the part that used to hum lullabies in a kitchen you can't remember. Unless."

"Unless," I repeated. My mouth tasted of salt. I hadn't eaten anything salty in days. Down here, I suspected, everything did.

"Unless someone pays full price early." He walked me to the door himself, which somehow made it worse, his hand light between my shoulder blades like a friendly uncle seeing a guest out. "Take your time. Well — take what time there is. And Rafael?" At the threshold he stopped me, and when I turned, his face had gone gentle in a way I genuinely hated, because it meant he knew exactly which knife he was holding. "The keeper knows what your order's prophecy costs. Ask her sometime what *the whole of what was taken* means. Ask her why she never told you." He patted my shoulder twice, briskly, and closed the door.

* * *

I walked the long way back through the Undervault's upper galleries, which was stupid, because walking slowly just gave the salt more time to seep in.

The galleries were beautiful, I'll give them that. Vaulted ceilings hung with lanterns that burned cold blue, and along the walls, niches upon niches, each holding a jar. I tried not to look at the jars. I looked at the jars. Some glowed faintly, like fireflies caught napping. Some were dark. One, near a turning I took by mistake, flickered in a rhythm I recognized before I understood it — a rhythm like a song, four notes rising, three falling, a pause like someone drawing breath to start again.

Marisol's lullaby. Kept in glass.

I stood in front of it longer than was wise, doing sums I didn't want to do. Twenty-six years in a jar. A kitchen. Hands kneading dough — whose hands? I couldn't see them, only feel the ghost of flour between my own fingers, which wasn't memory, just the hole where memory had been cut out, edges sharp as cut paper.

"Staring at inventory, tourist?"

I turned. Wren stood at the gallery's mouth, arms crossed, her keeper's gray traveling cloak dusty from wherever she'd been — the archives, probably, prying at marginalia, hunting the same answer I'd just been handed by a man with ink on his fingers. She had a talent for appearing exactly when I least wanted a witness.

"How much did you hear?"

"Enough. He offered you the standard cruelty." She came closer, eyes moving over my face the way she did — cataloguing damage, deciding what needed bandaging. "Whatever he asked, the answer is no. Whatever he showed you, it's bent. The Steward doesn't lie, which is worse than lying, because you can't catch him at it."

"He said you know what the prophecy costs." I watched her carefully. I'm good at watching people; it's most of my trade. "He said to ask you what *the whole of what was taken* means."

And there it was. The flinch. Small — Wren's flinches are small, trained down to almost nothing by an order that punished hesitation — but I've built a career on small things. Her jaw tightened. Her eyes went to the jar behind me and away again, too fast.

"He told you that," she said flatly.

"He told me that."

"Rafael—"

"Ten years, Wren." My voice came out lower than I intended, rougher. "You've chased this book for ten years. You decoded its margins in your sleep — I've seen you wake up writing. You knew what touching that page would do to me before it did it. So here's my question, and I'd like the truth, because I'm apparently the only person under this mountain who deals straight." I stepped closer, close enough to see the salt dust in her lashes. "When you grabbed my hand on the Stair of Turns — was that rescue? Or was that a keeper securing her reader?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere below us, the court made its small cold sounds — pages turning, jars chiming faintly against each other like distant bells.

"What did you tell him?" she asked finally.

"Don't answer my question with a question. It's a coward's move and beneath you."

"What did you *tell him*, Rafael." Louder now. And then I understood — not from her words, from her fear. She thought I'd taken the deal. She thought she was already talking to a version of me that had emptied her out, and she'd come looking anyway, because apparently that's who Wren Ashvale was: the woman who shows up for the person who betrayed her.

"Nothing," I said. "I told him nothing. I walked out."

Some of the tension left her shoulders. Not all. Keepers don't have *all*.

"But he'll ask again," she said. "He always asks again. And next time you'll be more tired, and the moon will be closer, and he'll be right about one thing—" She stopped herself.

"Finish it."

"He'll be right that you have to choose." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "That's what this whole court is, Rafael. It's a machine for making people choose between the people they love and the selves they've built. And it wins. It almost always wins."

"Not this time."

"You can't know that."

"I stole from a dying smuggler last month," I said, "his happiest memory, a morning with his daughter fishing, and I paid him for it, and he thanked me, and I slept fine — mostly — and I told myself that's the job. Take what people can spare, pay fair, walk away clean." I shook my head. "You're not spare, Wren. You're the opposite of spare. You're the first thing I've wanted to keep instead of take, and I don't even know how to do that, so forgive me if my technique is ugly."

She stared at me. For a long moment the whole cold gallery seemed to hold its breath, jars and all.

"That," she said finally, "is the worst confession I have ever heard, and I once audited a monk who confessed in rhyme."

"Genuine emotion makes me sloppy."

"Evidently." But she was almost smiling, and her hand found mine — briefly, hard, a grip like a promise with a short term — and then she stepped back into keeper-mode, brisk and armored. "Come on. There's marginalia I haven't shown you. And Rafael—" she paused at the gallery's turn, not looking back, "—whatever he offered you, whatever the price turns out to be, decide it *with* me. Not for me. Promise me that much."

I promised.

It was easy to promise. Promises are cheap; I've collected thousands of them secondhand and they weigh almost nothing.

It was only later, alone in the narrow room they'd given us — separate beds, a candle, the Codex open between us like a third guest neither of us had invited — that I understood the trap the Steward had actually built. Not the offer itself. The offer was crude; he'd known I'd refuse it. No, the trap was simpler and crueler: now the idea lived in me. *Steal from Wren.* Every hour I spent beside her, every laugh, every 3 a.m. argument over cipher-keys, the thought sat in the corner like a patient guest: *you could. You know how. It would save her — no, it would save the girl, it would cost Wren ten years she barely remembers fondly, it would be almost merciful—*

Almost. The most dangerous word in any language, and I'd stolen it in six others.

Across the room, Wren slept sitting up, the Codex on her knees, one hand resting on the page like she was holding the world's saddest pet. Moonlight — the eighth turn, rising — came through the salt window and lay across her face.

I looked at her a long time. Then at the Codex. Then at my own hands, which had never once failed me.

And beneath the floor, faint as a heartbeat, I heard the jars chiming. Four notes rising. Three falling. A pause, like breath drawn to start again.

The ninth turn was coming. And I was running out of ways to refuse to choose.
