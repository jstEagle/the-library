## 11. The Turn of the Seventh Moon

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years telling herself that the Order of the Salted Page had been mistaken — not wicked, merely mistaken — and she had gotten very good at believing it. Belief, she had learned, was a craft like any other. You practiced it daily. You kept your tools sharp. You ignored the splinters.

Then the seventh moon rose over the Undervault, and the Codex began rewriting itself in her hands, and every careful year of practiced belief came apart at once.

She was sitting in the alcove they had claimed off the Gallery of Kept Things — a niche behind a curtain of hanging salt-crystals that hummed faintly when anyone lied nearby, which meant it hummed at Rafael roughly once an hour — when the pages moved under her fingers.

Not turning. *Rewriting.*

The ink lifted from the parchment like insects startled from grass and resettled elsewhere, forming new lines while she watched. She had seen the Codex update before. It had done so on the first turn, adding their names. On the third turn, sketching the Steward's face. But those had been small revisions, marginalia shuffling like a card player squaring a deck. This was different. This was the book deciding what it wanted to be.

"Wren." Rafael's voice came low through the crystals. "You've gone the color of old cheese. The unappealing kind."

"I'm reading."

"You're gripping. There's a difference, and the difference is usually blood loss."

She loosened her hands. Beneath them, the page settled into new text, and she read it twice because the first time could not possibly be correct:

*On the seventh turn the reader will have passed the last gate outward. What remains is descent.*

"Last gate outward," she said. Her voice sounded very far away, as though someone else were borrowing it. "There were six gates out of the Undervault. Market gate, ferry gate, the Stair itself, the salt-flats road, the pass, the mountain door. I counted them on our way down. I counted them *twice*."

"And?"

"And we are standing inside the seventh."

* * *

They went to look, because Rafael Fairweather had never in their life accepted bad news without personally inspecting it, and Wren followed because following Rafael had become, somewhere around the fourth moon, a thing her body did without consulting her.

The market gate stood where it had always stood — a tall arch of black basalt carved with the court's motto, *ALL THINGS RETURN TO THE VAULT*, which Wren had initially taken for poetry and now understood to be a legal notice. Except that where the gate had opened onto the long climb toward the salt flats, there was now simply more gallery. More shelves. More corridors curving away into lamplight, as though the Undervault had quietly swallowed its own exit and belched up architecture in its place.

Rafael put a hand against the wall where the archway used to be. "Huh," they said.

"Huh," Wren agreed.

"That's a load-bearing huh," Rafael said. "I want you to know I'm keeping it together extremely well, and if you contradict me the crystals will start humming."

The crystals hummed. Gently. Like a chaperone clearing her throat.

"It rewrote around us," Wren said. She heard how flat her voice came out. "Around *your choices*. That's what the text says. The reader descends; the book makes room. Every path you didn't take—" she gestured at the seamless wall, "—gets paved over."

"So the Codex isn't predicting the future." Rafael's eyes had gone bright and narrow, the way they did when they were casing a room. "It's doing the opposite of predicting. It's *editing*. We're not reading a prophecy, Wren. We're living inside a draft."

"Yes."

"And someone holds the pen."

Wren did not answer, because the honest answer was lodged in her throat like a fishbone, and it had her name on it too — or rather her *order's* name, the Salted Page, keepers of the Codex, destroyers-of-nothing, who for three hundred years had sworn to guard the book and had, apparently, been guarding something else entirely: an instrument.

* * *

Back in the alcove, she laid the Codex open across her knees and made herself read it the way she should have read it years ago — not as a keeper, reverent, line by line, but as a thief would read it. The way Rafael read everything. Looking for the trick.

Prophecies, properly kept, were supposed to be stable. A true prophecy described what *would* be, and therefore could not change, because the future does not revise itself out of politeness. Wren had memorized that doctrine at fourteen, kneeling on cold stone while Master Ellery Voss tapped the lectern with one ringed finger. *The text is fixed. The reader is variable. That is why readers burn out, child — the book cannot bend, so the burden bends them instead.*

But the Codex bent. It had always bent. Every account in the Order's archives described revisions, additions, passages that appeared only when particular readers drew near. And the Order's response, generation after generation, had been to call this *mystery*, and to send more readers.

"Oh," Wren said aloud.

Rafael looked up from picking the lock of a salt-candy tin they'd acquired from Marisol — ostensibly as a gift for the girl, actually, Wren suspected, as a way to keep their hands busy while the world rearranged itself. "Oh good, or oh bad?"

"I need you to ask me a question."

"That's ominous. All right." They set down the tin. "Who wrote this thing?"

"The Order. My order. Not transcribed — *written*. Not recorded prophecy — composed. I think—" The fishbone turned. She swallowed past it. "I think the Codex doesn't contain prophecies. I think it contains *instructions*, dressed as prophecy, and the instructions require a reader, and the Order has been supplying readers for three hundred years the way you'd feed coal to a furnace."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. Then, gently, in the tone of someone approaching a skittish animal: "Why?"

"Because of the soul." She turned to the passage they both knew by heart now, the one that had started everything — *a soul sold beneath the mountain, redeemable at a price yet unnamed* — and looked at it until the words stopped being familiar. "Every keeper's manual agrees on one thing about bought souls: they don't lapse. A soul under contract stays contracted forever unless the contract is paid or broken. So if your order wanted a soul back — wanted it badly enough — they couldn't just take it. Court law forbids theft between signatories. But a *prophecy*—"

"A prophecy can make someone else do the stealing," Rafael finished.

"And take all the risk. And pay all the cost. And then hand the prize to the people who wrote the book." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "We didn't fail to destroy the Codex, Rafael. Destruction was never on the table. I wasn't sent to retrieve a dangerous book. I was sent to deliver it to the right pair of hands."

"Mine."

"Yours. A memory-thief. The single profession on earth capable of paying 'the whole of what was taken.'" She watched the understanding arrive in Rafael's face and hated herself for watching. "The prophecy doesn't say who frees the soul. I told you that weeks ago and thought it was mercy — that the book was leaving room for hope. It wasn't leaving room. It was leaving a *slot*. Shaped exactly like you."

* * *

The salt-crystals around the alcove had gone silent, which somehow felt worse than the humming, as though even the furniture was holding its breath.

Rafael stood at the mouth of the niche, arms crossed, looking out at the gallery lamps. When they finally spoke, their voice had the lightness Wren had learned to distrust — the brightness they wore like a mask at funerals.

"Well. Silver linings." They held up fingers. "One: my childhood trauma is apparently load-bearing to a three-hundred-year-old conspiracy, which explains a lot about my luck. Two: I've been conned by a book, which stings professionally but I'll survive. Three—" the third finger wavered, "—three is still loading."

"You don't have to perform for me."

"This isn't performing. This is coping. There's a difference and the difference is—" They stopped. Their hand dropped. And for a moment the mask slipped all the way off, and Wren saw underneath it a nine-year-old child standing at the mouth of a tunnel, running from a bargain they hadn't understood, carrying nothing.

"They knew," Rafael said quietly. "Your masters knew there was a child. Knew a family sold a soul to the Undervault. Knew the child ran. And instead of finding that child and helping them, they wrote a book that would find the child thirty years later and *spend* them."

"Yes."

"And you carried this thing for a decade trying to protect people from it."

"I carried it because I loved my order," Wren said. "That's worse."

* * *

Later — much later, when the gallery lamps had dimmed to their night-cycle and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark like a pair of shipwreck survivors on a very well-organized raft — Rafael spoke again.

"When we get out of here," they said, "assuming the traditional miracle occurs, what happens to the Codex? Your order will want it back."

"No," Wren said. "My order will want *you* back. Or what's left after the payment. The book can go in the sea."

"Burn it?"

"Drown it. Fire leaves ash, and ash can be read." She paused. "Salt water, though. Salt undoes salt."

Rafael huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Look at us. Planning the epilogue before the climax. Very healthy."

"Somebody has to be optimistic, and you're busy being flippant."

"I contain multitudes. Some of them are borrowed." They were quiet again, and then, so softly the crystals didn't stir: "Wren. If the payment is what you think it is — the whole of what was taken — promise me you won't pay it for me. Whatever it costs. Promise you'll let me choose it myself."

And there it was: the question she had been dreading since the fifth turn, arriving on schedule, patient as the tide. Because Wren had already read the marginalia further than she'd admitted. Already knew what *the whole of what was taken* most likely meant. Already knew that if it came down to Rafael unraveling versus some other way — any other way — she would lie, cheat, forge, and sacrifice without a moment's hesitation, and that this was precisely the flaw her masters had counted on in keepers for three hundred years: love of the mission wearing the costume of love for the person.

"I promise," she said.

The crystals hummed.

"Oh, come *on*," Rafael said to the ceiling.

* * *

Near dawn — if dawn could be said to exist four miles underground, which the court insisted it could, out of what Wren suspected was sheer stubbornness — a folded scrap of paper came sliding under the alcove curtain, moving with the peculiar self-importance of Undervault correspondence.

Rafael retrieved it, unfolded it, and went still.

"What?" Wren asked.

Rafael handed it over without a word. In Marisol's careful, childish hand, pressed deep into the paper:

*The Steward is asking the archivists about you. Both of you. He wants to know who taught the girl's lullaby to the thief. He says somebody sold it twice.*

Wren read it three times. Somewhere beneath them, deeper than the vaults, deeper than the ledgers, something vast shifted in its sleep — and the Codex, lying closed on her knee, grew warm as a living thing, and began, softly, to rewrite.

She looked at Rafael, whose face had gone white around the eyes.

"Sold it twice," Rafael repeated. "Wren — I never taught Marisol that lullaby. I *know* it. I've known it my whole life. Which means either the soul remembers, or—"

"Or someone else knew it too," Wren said, "and sold it to the court separately. Someone who was there the day your parents signed."

The Codex's warmth spread up her arm like a hand closing around her wrist, and the ink, she could feel it, was already moving — already making room.

The seventh turn was not half over. And the book, it seemed, had one more edit in mind.
