## 12. What I Took From You

The safehouse Wren had chosen was a smuggler's waystation cut into the bone of Thornfell itself — a low stone room with a hearth that smoked more than it burned and a single window that looked out on nothing but mountain dark. It smelled of old tallow and older secrets. Rafael had slept in worse. They had slept in considerably better, too, but those beds had belonged to other people, along with everything else about them.

Marisol slept in the alcove behind the curtain, curled small under a blanket that smelled of salt and lamp oil. She hummed in her sleep sometimes. That lullaby. Rafael still couldn't say where they knew it from, only that knowing it felt like a splinter working its way toward the skin.

Wren sat across the hearth with the Salt Codex open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. Rafael could tell because her eyes hadn't moved in an hour and nobody read a book like that unless they were trying to avoid someone sitting across the fire from them.

"You're going to wear a hole in that page," Rafael said.

"I'm studying."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Studying has more blinking."

Wren closed the Codex with exaggerated care, which was how she did everything — as if the world were a fragile thing she'd already broken once and didn't intend to break twice. "You've been staring at Marisol's curtain for two days," she said. "Every time she hums, you go white around the mouth. So. Either you tell me what you know, or I start guessing, and my guesses tend to be loud."

Rafael fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. This was the moment. They'd known it was coming since the market, since the salt-nailed memories, since the child who carried a soul that fit them like a key fits a lock no one admitted to owning.

They were a thief. They knew how to take things. What they had never learned was how to hand something over.

"When I was nine," Rafael said, "my parents sold my soul."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water dripped through stone with the patience of centuries.

Wren didn't gasp or clutch at her pearls. She went very still, the way people do when they realize they're standing on the edge of something deep, and Rafael — who had spent a lifetime reading faces for a living — watched her decide, deliberately, to listen instead of react.

"It was a bad winter," Rafael went on, because now that the door was open, the whole room came tumbling through it. "The year the passes froze early and the tolls doubled and my father's leg went bad. I remember — " They stopped. Frowned. Reached for it the way you reach for a stair that isn't there. "I remember there being a decision. I don't remember the decision. There's a shape where it should be. A dinner table, candlelight, my mother not looking at anyone, my father saying a name I can't hear. And then I'm nine years old and then I'm nine years and one day old and I'm running up the Thornfell pass alone with a bag of bread and the memory of a door closing."

"The sale bought the family's survival," Wren said quietly. "That's how the Undervault writes its contracts. The soul is collateral against ruin."

"My soul was collateral against *winter*." Rafael laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you understand what they actually took? Everything before that night. My mother's face. My father teaching me to whistle through my thumbs. Whatever that lullaby is, whoever sang it to me first. I have been walking around for thirty years with a hole in the middle of me shaped like home, and I told myself it was just how I was made. Light. Portable. Easy to carry." They spread their hands. "Turns out I'm not light. I'm just empty."

"Rafael —"

"And here's the part you're really going to love." They leaned forward, elbows on knees, wearing the grin they used for buyers who haggled too hard. "I know a trade that fixes this. I've seen it done. A memory that cuts you every time you touch it — you find someone skilled enough, and you have them lift it out. Clean extraction. You wake up lighter. I've done it for soldiers who couldn't sleep, for widows who wanted to stop drowning. It's good work. It's *kind* work, Wren, whatever you keepers think of my profession."

Wren set the Codex aside on the stone. "Say what you're asking me."

"I'm asking you to steal it." The words came out steadier than Rafael expected, which was its own kind of betrayal. "The memory of the sale. The table, the candles, the door. Lift it out of my head the way I've lifted a thousand others. You've got keeper's hands, you know how the deep things are stitched. I'll talk you through it. I'm very good at talking people through it." Their voice cracked on the last word, traitor that it was. "I can't function like this. Every hour I spend near that girl, I lose another piece of myself to grief over people I can't even remember. I walked into the Steward's court today and nearly wept at a *tapestry*. If I go back down there tomorrow carrying all of this —"

"No."

The word was soft. That was the worst part. Wren Ashvale, disgraced keeper, exile, woman who had lied by omission about prophecy-traps and confessed it with her chin up — she said *no* the way other people said *please*, gently, like it cost her.

Rafael blinked. "You didn't let me finish the pitch. I haven't even gotten to the part where I offer you something outrageous in exchange."

"There's nothing outrageous enough."

"Wren. I am literally a professional. This is my trade. People pay me —"

"People pay you to take their pain because taking things is easy and keeping them is hard, and somewhere along the way you decided that made you a healer instead of a dealer." Wren rose and crossed to the hearth, standing over Rafael, firelight making copper of her gray hair. "I have watched you work. You're extraordinary. You hold other people's memories so gently they never know they've been touched. But this isn't a memory you found lying in the road. This is the floor you're standing on."

"It's a wound."

"It's *you*. Do you hear yourself? You want to hand me the only true thing left in your head and have me dig it out so you can walk back into that court hollow and functional." She shook her head slowly. "I've read a hundred years of keeper's records about souls sold beneath the mountain. Do you know what the ledgers call the buyers' state afterward? *Unburdened.* That's the word. Unburdened. Like it's a blessing." Her jaw tightened. "It's not a blessing. It's a second theft. The first one took your childhood. This one would take your grief for it, and your grief is the last proof you ever loved anyone enough to mourn."

"They're dead, Wren. Probably. The ledger said —"

"I know what the ledger said. Your parents' names, held as collateral, status unknown. Unknown, Rafael. Not lost." She crouched to their level, and her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely immovable. "Unknown means there's something down there worth finding. And you want to carve out the only reason you'd go looking."

Rafael opened their mouth. Closed it. For thirty years they had talked their way through locked doors and armed checkpoints and the private vaults of men who killed for less than a raised eyebrow, and now they sat in front of a smoky fire with nothing.

"I can't do it," they finally said, and their voice came out small, unarmored, nine years old. "Carrying it. I can't walk around with a hole screaming at me every time that girl hums. I've outsourced every hard thing I've ever felt. It's the only skill I have."

"Then learn a new one," Wren said. "Tonight. Right now. With supervision."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm going to sit here while you hurt." She dragged her bedroll around the fire until it touched Rafael's, then sat down on it with the finality of a woman building a wall with her own body. "All night, if it takes all night. You're going to feel the whole shape of it — the table, the candles, the door — and you are not going to die, and I am going to be right here, and tomorrow you will still have all of it, and you will also have survived having all of it. That's the trade I'm offering. Take it or take it."

"That's not a trade."

"It's the only honest one on the table." A ghost of a smile. "Which, given our respective professions, may be a first for both of us."

* * *

The first hour was the worst.

Rafael talked, because silence made the hole louder. They talked about the heists that had gone well and the ones that had gone sideways, about the dying smuggler whose happiest memory had been of his daughter learning to walk — *he gave it up smiling, did you know that? People give up their joy smiling, like it's rent* — about the rule they'd never broken, never stealing their own memories back, and how they'd always told themselves it was ethics when really it had been fear of finding out exactly how much of them was missing.

Wren listened. Occasionally she asked a question, small and precise, the way she did everything. Mostly she just stayed, radiating heat and stubbornness like a stove with opinions.

Somewhere past midnight, Rafael ran out of deflection and hit the thing itself.

"I remember the bread," they said. The fire had burned low. Marisol's humming had gone quiet. "In the bag, running up the pass. It was walnut bread. My favorite. Someone packed it for me — I don't know who, my hands just *know* the weight of it, the warmth of it through the cloth. Someone loved me enough to pack bread for a nine-year-old running into the mountains, and I don't know their face. I don't know if it was goodbye or if they thought I'd come back. I don't know if they watched me go." Their breath hitched. "I built an entire life out of other people's memories, Wren. Forty-some lives, stitched together, and not one stitch is mine. And tonight I finally understand why I did it. I wasn't collecting. I was *furnishing*. Trying to fill the room."

Wren's hand found theirs in the dark. No speech, no lesson. Just her fingers, rough with ink-stains, laced through theirs, holding on.

Rafael cried. It was not elegant. It was ugly and silent at first and then not silent at all, thirty years of it coming up like floodwater, and through all of it Wren stayed exactly where she was, thumb moving slow across their knuckles, saying only once, quietly: "There it is. Keep going. I've got you."

"I hate this," Rafael managed.

"I know."

"I'm going to be insufferable about it later. I'm going to claim this never happened."

"I'll deny everything," she agreed solemnly. "We keepers are trained in discretion. It's the exiles who gossip."

A wet, cracked laugh escaped them despite everything. "You're terrible at comfort, you know."

"I'm excellent at presence. Comfort requires talent. Presence just requires refusing to leave." She shifted her shoulder against theirs. "Luckily, refusal is my best subject. Ask any of my former colleagues."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the window slit. Marisol emerged from the alcove rubbing her eyes, saw the two of them slumped shoulder to shoulder by the ash-cold hearth, and announced, "You look terrible."

"We had a conference," Rafael croaked.

"About feelings?"

"About *logistics*."

Marisol looked deeply unconvinced, accepted a strip of dried apple from Wren, and settled between them to hum her lullaby while she ate. And Rafael — Rafael braced for the splinter of it, the scream of the hole —

— and found that it hurt, yes, but it hurt like weather now instead of like a knife. Bearable. Real. Their grief, their own, unpaid-for and un-robbed.

"Well?" Wren murmured, watching their face.

"Still there. All of it." Rafael flexed their hands, surprised to find them steady. "And I'm still me. More me, maybe. Isn't that obscene. All these years I thought the emptiness was holding me together."

"Grief's strange that way. It weighs more when you put it down." Wren began rolling the Codex into its oilcloth. "Speaking of putting things down — I've been thinking about the Steward's ledger. Souls held as collateral get entered somewhere. Contracts, terms, payment schedules. Paper."

Rafael looked at her. Something old and bright kindled behind their eyes, the thing that lived there underneath everything else, the thing no sale had ever touched.

"Paper," Rafael repeated, "is my favorite kind of lock."

"Your parents' names are in that ledger. Terms of the original sale. Maybe — conditions. Loopholes." Wren's smile was small and sharp. "I believe you mentioned once that you're very good at talking people through things."

"I'm very good at getting into places I shouldn't." Rafael stood, joints cracking, hollowed-out and somehow fuller than they'd been in thirty years. "How do you feel about a little burglary?"

Wren tied off the oilcloth. "Disgraced, remember? Burglary is practically a career advancement."

Above them, through miles of stone, the Undervault waited with its nailed-shut memories and its salt-locked doors — and somewhere inside it, in a vault Rafael had already begun mapping in their head, a ledger held the names of everyone they had ever loved.
