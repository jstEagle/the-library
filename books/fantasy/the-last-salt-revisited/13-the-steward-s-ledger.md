## 13. The Steward's Ledger

There is a rule among thieves that I have broken exactly twice in my life, and both times I told myself it was the last time, and both times I was lying, which is a third kind of breaking.

The rule is this: never steal from someone who knows your face.

The Steward of the Undervault knew my face the way a fisherman knows the shape of a hook. He had looked at me across his polished table of black salt and smiled with all the warmth of a locked door, and he had named his price — Wren's memories, laid out on a plate like oysters — and he had expected me to either pay it or crawl away.

I did neither. I did what I do.

I came back for his ledger.

* * *

Getting into the vault was, I will admit, insultingly easy. I had expected wards, salt-lines humming with old law, doors that asked questions. Instead the Steward's counting-house sat above the soul-vault like a fat spider above its web, and fat spiders do not guard their webs. They trust them.

The corridor to his offices was patrolled by two courtiers with lantern-fish swimming in glass globes at their shoulders, their light a soft drowned green. I waited in the shadow of an alcove where a statue of some moon-blessed ancestor held a set of scales made of bone, and I listened to the courtiers complain about each other in the bored, intimate way of people who have been paired on too many night shifts.

"—told him the third turning is mine. He knows the third turning is mine. He took it anyway, the little eel—"

"You could petition."

"Petition the Steward? He'd charge me for the ink."

I almost liked them. That is the trouble with this place, I was learning. Everyone down here is someone's victim and someone's thief, and the ledger keeps turning, and nobody remembers who started it. Except, it turns out, the ledger remembers everything.

When their footsteps faded toward the turning-gallery, I went up the servants' stair — servants' stairs are always the honest ones, built for people carrying things, wide and unguarded because no architect imagines a thief wants to *carry* — and I let myself into the Steward's counting-room the way a draft lets itself under a door.

The room smelled of ink and cold candle-wax and, underneath it, faintly, of salt. Of course salt. Everything down here was salted shut. Wren had shown me how the court nailed its memories closed, and I had spent two days since then walking through the Undervault like a man in a museum of locked drawers, able to see every beautiful terrible thing and touch none of it.

But ledgers are not memories. Ledgers are only ink. And ink, thank every merciful moon, can still be read.

* * *

I found the vault door behind a tapestry of the First Bargain — some ancestor of the court kneeling before a mountain with his hands full of light — and the lock was a good lock, an honest lock, the kind a craftsman builds when he wants to be admired by other craftsmen. I picked it in the time it takes to hum four bars of a lullaby, which is the time I give every lock, because Marisol's tune has become my metronome and I have stopped fighting it.

Inside, the vault was not full of gold.

I had braced myself for gold. Gold is simple. Gold you can carry.

Instead there were shelves. Shelf after shelf after shelf, rising into a darkness the lantern I'd lifted off a wall didn't reach, and on the shelves, books. Not books like the Salt Codex — nothing so grand, nothing with prophecies breathing in the margins. These were plain. Bound in grey leather, spines numbered in a careful hand. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

I pulled one at random and opened it, and my stomach did the slow cold turn it does when a job stops being a job.

It was a soul. Or the record of one. *Held in trust,* said the first page, in that same careful hand, *against debt outstanding. Debtor: Odile Ferrante, weaver. Collateral: one soul, whole, surrendered the ninth of Harvestmoon, in the year of the Long Frost. Terms: redeemable by the debtor or her blood unto the seventh generation.*

*Redeemable.* As if it were a coat left at an inn. As if the weaver could simply walk in one day, count out her coins, and put her soul back on like a shawl.

I stood there in the green-dark with the little grey book in my hands and I thought about the weaver, Odile Ferrante, who I had never met, who had probably died generations ago with her soul still shelved here like a jar of preserves, redeemable unto the seventh generation, and her seventh generation down there in the market right now, haggling over lamp-oil, carrying the shape of an absence they couldn't name.

That's what the Undervault sells, I realized. Not souls. Absences. It sells you the hole where something used to be, and charges you rent on the hole.

And then — because I am who I am, because the thief in me works faster than the person, because my hands were already moving before my heart caught up — I went looking for my own name.

* * *

Fairweather. F-A-I-R. Between *Farrow, Tamsin, cooper* and *Fenn, Aldous, chandler*. My parents' ledger sat where the alphabet said it would sit, patient as a grave, and I took it down with hands that were not quite steady, and I sat down on the cold floor of the vault with my back against the shelf, and I read.

The theft was easy. Nobody had come. Nobody had even stirred. The hardest lock in the Undervault had been the one in my own chest, and I had picked it coming down the stair.

The reading was not easy.

*Debtor: Sera Fairweather, laundress, and Tomás Fairweather, mason. Collateral: one soul, whole, surrendered the third of Thawmoon, in the year —* my year, the year I turned eight, the year I have no memories of because I sold them, or thought I sold them — *in consideration of debt outstanding to the court, said debt being the price of one passage out of Thornfell passes for one child, Rafael Fairweather, age nine.*

There it was. In ink. The thing I had spent my whole life not quite remembering.

I had always told myself the story the way orphans tell it: they died, I ran, the road made me. A clean story. A story with no one to blame. And here was the truth, sitting in a grey book on a cold shelf, in a clerk's careful hand: my parents had not sold *my* soul out of greed or cruelty. They had sold it to buy me out. Passage for one child. They had paid the court with the only coin they had that the court wanted, and the coin was *me* — the part of me that loved, the part that was nine years old and knew my mother's voice and my father's hands and the smell of soap in the wash-house — and then they had put me on the road alone, because the bargain bought passage for *one*.

I did the arithmetic I had spent twenty years refusing to do. The debt they were clearing. The debt a laundress and a mason could fall into in a bad winter in a company town. It wasn't a fortune. It was *rent*. It was a winter's grain and a mason's fine and a laundress's medicine, and the court had looked at that little heap of trouble and said: we'll take the child's soul instead.

And my parents had said yes. Because the other choice was watching me die of it. Because love, when it is poor, has to pay in the only currency it has.

I sat in that vault and I shook, quietly, the way you shake when you're furious and there is nothing in the room to hit. The lantern-light didn't flicker. The shelves didn't care. Somewhere above me the court went on with its turning-gallery and its petitions and its little eels, and Odile Ferrante's seventh generation haggled over lamp-oil, and my parents' names sat in a grey book, *debt discharged, collateral retained* — retained, do you understand, *retained*, as though they had ever been given a choice about getting it back.

I had come down here to steal a ledger. I sat on the floor of the soul-vault of the Undervault and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade being taken out of me, that I had come down here to be *angry*, and that the anger had been waiting its whole life for an address.

* * *

Here is the thing about rage: it wants to burn. It wants to burn the shelf and the book and the court and the mountain down to the salt it's carved from, and it wants to do it *tonight*, and it does not care that you are one thief with a lifted lantern in a hole in the ground under a mountain.

I let it burn for exactly as long as it takes to read three more entries.

Because I made myself read three more. *Farrow, Tamsin, cooper.* *Fenn, Aldous, chandler.* *Ferrante, Odile, weaver.* And then I didn't stop. I read down the shelf, and along the next shelf, and I pulled books at random from the dark rows beyond the lantern's reach, and every one of them was the same. A name. A trade. A debt too small to be a crime and too large to survive. A soul, whole, retained.

Thousands. The careful clerk had numbered them, and the numbers ran past the point where numbers mean anything.

And somewhere in those rows — I knew it the way you know a lullaby you've never been taught — somewhere in those rows was a grey book with a girl's name on it. *Marisol.* Collateral: one soul, whole, surrendered — no. Not surrendered. *Transferred.* Sold once by a Fairweather and held ever since, moving from shelf to shelf like furniture, until the Steward put it into a child and set the child to carrying lamps in his market, because a soul in a body is worth more than a soul on a shelf. A soul in a body *works*.

She had been humming my lullaby because it was hers. Because it had been mine, before, and souls remember what children are made to forget.

I closed the last book. I put every book back exactly where it had been, spine to the line, because a thief who leaves disorder behind is a thief who has announced herself, and I had one more thing to do before I left this vault, and it was not burning.

The rage was still there. It had not gone quiet; it had gone *clear*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my trade. A memory stolen in fury is a memory you drop. A memory stolen in clarity is a memory you can carry. And what I carried out of that vault, in my hands and behind my ribs, was this:

The ledger was the contract. Not the Codex, not the prophecy, not the thirteen turning moons with all their ceremony and their dread. The *ledger*. Every soul in this mountain was held by a line of ink in a grey book, and ink — I have made my living on this, I have eaten on this, I have slept soundly on this — ink can be moved.

I could not steal a soul out of its contract. I had tried, with Marisol, and the trying had nearly unmade her, and Wren's warning had come too late and told me why: the contract must be *paid*, not broken. Paid in full. The whole of what was taken.

But a contract is a *record*. And a record is kept by clerks. And clerks, in all my years of lifting other people's pasts out of their skulls, have one thing in common with everyone else:

They can be robbed.

I did not touch the Fairweather ledger. I left it on its shelf, between Farrow and Fenn, debt discharged, collateral retained, because the time to move that line of ink is not tonight, alone, in fury, with a lifted lantern. The time is when the whole ledger comes down at once. When every Odile and every Aldous and every Tamsin walks out of this mountain with their own names in their own hands.

That is not theft. I stood in the dark among the shelves and admitted it to myself at last, and it felt like putting down something heavy I had carried so long I'd mistaken it for my spine.

That is *returning* what was stolen.

* * *

I was three steps from the tapestry when the lantern guttered — not wind; there is no wind down here — and a voice from the darkness of the far shelves said, conversationally, the way you'd greet a colleague:

"You read longer than most."

I did not scream. I am a professional. But my hand found the pick in my sleeve and my heart found the lullaby and hummed it very fast.

The darkness moved, and an old woman stepped into the light — a clerk's grey robe, a clerk's ink-stained fingers, eyes pale as washed paper. She looked at me the way you look at weather.

"Four hundred years I've kept this room," she said. "Thieves come for gold. There's no gold. Thieves come for their own names." She tilted her head. "You read *the others*."

"I read the others," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"And now?"

"Now," I said, "I'm going to need a bigger bag."

She smiled — a small, terrible, hopeful thing — and held out her hand, and in it was a key.

"The Steward's copy," she said. "The one with the *original* terms. The one that proves the court never held clear title to a single soul it bought." Her pale eyes held mine. "I have waited four hundred years for someone angry enough and careful enough. Tell me, thief — how do you feel about committing the largest theft in the history of the Undervault?"

I took the key.

"*Returning*," I said. "The largest *returning*."

And above us, faint through the mountain's stone, the moon began its eleventh turn.
