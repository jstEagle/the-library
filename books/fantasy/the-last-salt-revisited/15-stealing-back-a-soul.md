## 15. Stealing Back a Soul

The lullaby led me down.

That's the honest version, though I'd have laughed at it a week ago — Rafael Fairweather, guided by a child's humming instead of a map, a bribe, or a well-placed lie. But maps lie, bribes run out, and lies have a way of circling back to bite the liar. The lullaby never did any of those things. It just drifted through the Undervault's bone-pale corridors like smoke with an opinion about which way to go, and I followed it, and it was right every time.

Four left turns past the salt-gardens. Down the stair where the steps had been worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet that were never allowed to hurry. Through a corridor so narrow my shoulders brushed walls furred white with mineral bloom, like frost that had decided to live indoors. And always, always, the tune: six notes up, three notes down, a pause long enough to hold your breath in.

I knew the pause. That was the thing that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook. I knew where the pause went before it arrived. Some part of me that had been sold off at nine years old was still keeping time somewhere beneath this mountain, and when the melody paused, that part of me leaned forward, waiting for the next note the way you wait for someone you love to come around a corner.

*You don't remember loving anyone,* I told myself sternly. *You remember the shape of remembering. It's not the same.*

My inner voice did not believe me. My inner voice had been getting less and less convinced by anything I said lately, which is what happens when you spend two weeks learning that your entire childhood was a transaction and your best talent was the weapon used to close it.

* * *

The vault door was exactly as ugly as everything else the Steward owned, which is to say beautiful in the way of things designed to make you feel poor. Black salt-iron, tall as three men, carved all over with clauses. I mean that literally — the whole door was covered in fine script, contract-language, the kind of writing that exists not to be read but to be enforceable. I ran my fingers along a line of it and felt the words resist my touch like cats resisting a bath.

Above the handle, in letters that had been added later, cruder, almost apologetic:

**THE VAULT OF KEPT SOULS. NO ENTRY WITHOUT SETTLEMENT.**

"No entry without settlement," I murmured. "Well. Fortunately for both of us, I've never once entered anywhere with settlement."

The lock was a work of art, and I say that with professional bitterness. Most locks are puzzles: tumblers, wards, little mechanical egos waiting to be flattered. This one was a scale. Literally — a set of balance pans grown out of the door's iron like something organic, and when I pressed my palm against the mechanism, a voice like dry paper said:

***State what you bring of equal weight.***

"Oh, come on," I whispered.

***State what you bring of equal weight.***

I thought fast. What did I have? A handful of stolen memories, most of them already promised back to their owners in my private ledger of debts. Three coins. A hairpin Wren had left in my collar without mentioning it, which I was trying very hard not to read anything into. And —

And the lullaby. Humming in my head, patient, six notes up, three notes down.

"Equal weight," I repeated. "What's equal to a soul?"

***A memory freely given,*** said the voice, ***or a truth freely spoken.***

I looked at the door for a long moment. Somewhere behind me, back up the corridors, the court was stirring toward its night-business; I could hear the far-off chime of the turn-bells counting hours I didn't have. Thirteenth moon in three nights. Maybe less, the way things had been going.

"A truth freely spoken," I said. "Fine. Here's one." I put my mouth close to the iron, because it seemed only polite. "I sold my own soul when I was nine years old. I didn't know that's what I was doing. I thought I was buying my mother's freedom with a bad bargain I half understood, and I've spent twenty years telling myself I got away clean. I didn't get away clean. I got away empty, and I've been filling the hole with other people's lives ever since."

The pans of the great scale trembled.

"That's true," I added, "and it was free, because nobody made me say it, and I'd honestly rather have eaten glass."

The door considered this. Then, with a sound like a very large book closing very gently, it unsealed along a seam I couldn't see, and swung inward onto darkness that smelled of cold stone and old paper and, faintly, impossibly, of sea-salt and sun-warm wood — the smell of a harbor town I had no memory of visiting and knew instantly I had grown up in.

The lullaby swelled, just once, like a hand squeezing mine.

I went in.

* * *

Inside, the vault was a library that had committed a crime against itself.

Shelves rose into the dark, but instead of books they held jars, boxes, lanterns with their lights shuttered, birds' cages draped in cloth. Each container held a soft glow, dimmed to almost nothing, the color varying — gold, blue-green, one furious orange that buzzed faintly like a trapped wasp. Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All the Steward's collateral, all the kingdom's quiet bargains, sitting on shelves in the dark gathering dust that was probably also contractual.

I found the ledger first, because I'd come here before — three nights ago, in fact, in a different chapter of my life that had ended with me running. The ledger still lay open on its stand where I'd left it, my own fingerprints ghosted in the dust of its margin. I didn't need to look up the entry. I'd read it until the words burned themselves into me even deeper than memory usually goes:

**FAIRWEATHER, R. — one soul, entire, age nine. Sold by parent(s), desperate circumstances. Held as surety against further debt. Status: ACTIVE. Vessel: assigned.**

*Vessel: assigned.* Marisol. Nine years old herself, small and quick and always humming, carrying around inside her borrowed body a soul that had been waiting in the dark for twenty years to come home.

I found her jar on the third shelf of the seventh row. Of course I did. I didn't search; my hands simply went there, drawn by the same gravity that pulled the lullaby. It was a plain jar, salt-glass, stoppered with wax stamped by the Steward's seal, and inside it burned a light I can't describe without lying. Gold, I'll say, and then immediately want to take it back, because it wasn't gold the way coins are gold. It was gold the way late afternoon through a kitchen window is gold. It was warm. Even through the wax seal I could feel it wanting out, patient as tide.

"Hello," I whispered, absurdly. "I'm — well. I'm the reason you're in there. Which makes this either a rescue or a burglary, and I've decided it's both, because honesty has apparently become my whole personality now."

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. Six notes up, three notes down.

It was humming back at me.

My eyes stung, which was inconvenient and professionally embarrassing, and I wiped them on my sleeve and got to work.

Here's what I know about theft, and I know more than anyone living, which is not bragging, it's inventory. Every theft is a conversation. The lock tells you how it wants to be picked. The pocket tells you which seam will open. The memory tells you which thread to pull so the whole bright cloth comes away without tearing. You don't force. You *ask*, beautifully, with your hands.

So I asked.

I laid both palms flat against the jar, closed my eyes, and reached for the contract the way I'd reach for the edge of a memory — that silken border where a thing ends and its keeping begins. And there it was: the contract wrapped around Marisol's soul like a second jar, a lattice of clause-light, dense as chainmail. Every line anchored to another. The sale-deed below. The Steward's claim above. The thirteenth moon ticking somewhere in the weave like a clock inside a clock.

"Right," I breathed. "You're a big one. Fine. I've stolen cathedrals. Metaphorically. Once, nearly, actually, but we don't need to discuss that."

I found the loose thread. There's always a loose thread — a clause written by someone tired, an exception no one tested. Mine, as it happened. *Sold by parent(s), desperate circumstances.* Desperation was written into the very root of the deed, and desperation, I knew from a hundred sad nights lifting dying men's happiest hours, is the one thing courts never price correctly. They think it's cheap. It isn't. It's the most expensive material there is.

I pulled.

The contract resisted, then argued, then — with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, a note too low for ears and too wide for rooms — began to *unpick*. Clause by clause, the lattice loosened. The gold light inside the jar rose like bread dough, like a tide coming in, pressing up against the wax, pressing out against the thinning walls of its keeping, and I thought, wild and laughing under my breath, *it's working, it's actually working, somebody write this down, the impossible theft, performed by an idiot with good hands—*

The last anchor snapped.

And Marisol screamed.

Not here. Not in the vault — she was three corridors away, asleep in the servants' wing, I'd checked twice. But I heard her anyway, through whatever thread I'd grabbed, because souls don't respect distance and neither do the people carrying them. It was a scream with two voices in it: a child's terror, and underneath it something older, vaster, waking up after twenty years in the dark and finding the room it woke in wasn't its own.

I froze, hands still on the jar, the unpicked contract dissolving around my wrists like sugar in rain. "No, no, no — shh — I'm sorry — I'm doing it *for* you—"

But the damage was visible now, right in front of me, and gods forgive me, I should have seen it coming. The soul in the jar wasn't easing out of its keeping. It was *flickering*. One moment it burned steady and whole, a girl-shaped warmth, Marisol through and through. The next it stretched, thinned, spread wide like wings unfurling — older, freer, huge, a presence that made the shelf hum and the dust rise off the floor in slow spirals. Then it snapped back to girl-smallness. Then out again. Flicker. Flicker. Like a lamp with a failing wick. Like something being pulled between two shapes that both needed all of it.

Two vessels. One soul. A contract half-dissolved, which meant neither binding nor free, which meant — I did the arithmetic and hated every number — the soul was tearing itself in half trying to obey both.

"Stop," I begged it, stupidly, uselessly. "Stop, just — wait, let me think—"

The great light surged again, and this time it pressed outward through the glass hard enough to crack it — one silver line, hair-fine, running up the jar's curve, leaking warmth across my palms like sunlight through a keyhole. And in that leak I *felt* it. Twenty years of patience. A harbor at dusk. A woman singing over a stove. A small hand holding a smaller hand. Everything I'd sold, everything I'd spent two decades not-remembering, pushing against the inside of my chest from the outside, trying to recognize me.

It knew me. That was the worst part. It flickered into its vast old shape and bent itself, enormous and gentle, around my shaking hands, the way you'd cup a candle flame in a wind — and then flickered back to Marisol-small, and I heard her voice, faint and doubled, from three corridors away and from inside the jar at once:

*"It hurts. Why does it hurt? You said—"*

"I know," I whispered. "I know. I'm sorry. Hold on. Please hold on. I'll fix it. I fix things. It's the only thing I'm good at."

The crack in the jar widened, a whisper at a time.

* * *

I worked the rest of the night, and I'll spare us both the details, mostly because they consisted of me failing elegantly in eleven different ways. I tried re-sealing the contract partially, to stabilize the flicker; the clauses slid off the soul now like water off oiled cloth. I tried splitting the difference, feeding the vessel-shape enough anchoring to ease the pull; Marisol's doubled voice went quiet for ten minutes and then came back weaker, which frightened me worse than screaming. I tried, in one low moment, simply stuffing the jar back on the shelf and pretending I'd never come, and my hands refused. Actually refused. They stayed flat on the glass, and the lullaby hummed through my bones, six notes up, three notes down, and would not let go.

Somewhere in the smallest hours I sat down on the cold floor with my back against the shelf, the cracked jar cradled in my lap like the world's most dangerous egg, and admitted the truth to the dark.

"It worked," I said. "That's the joke. It worked, and working is going to kill her. Half-free is worse than kept. Half-free is a soul being torn like bread."

The gold light pulsed. Steady. Girl-small. Waiting. Trusting me, which was the cruelest thing it could have done, because I had built a whole life out of people trusting me with their memories and giving them back coin instead.

Think, Rafael. Think like a thief. When the theft works but the taking kills — what do you do?

There was an answer. It had been sitting in the margins of the Codex for weeks, in Wren's careful handwriting, in warnings she'd tried to give me sideways and I'd been too busy being brilliant to hear. Something about payment. About the whole of what was taken. About the price of unmaking a bargain being the return of everything the bargain ever touched.

I looked down at my hands. Thief's hands. Hands full of twenty years of other people's sunsets, other people's first kisses, other people's mothers singing over stoves. A hoard. A fortune. A life.

"...Oh," I said quietly.

The jar cracked a little wider, and the light within it flickered — girl, vastness, girl — and far above us both, through a mile of mountain, the moon turned one night closer to its thirteenth.

Then, from the corridor outside, footsteps. Quick ones. Familiar ones. And a voice I knew, ragged with having run a long way, calling my name into the dark.
