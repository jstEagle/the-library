## 16. The Tenth Turn Betrays Us

The archive of the Undervault smelled of salt and old decisions.

Wren had been a kept thing for three turns now — ten days, by the counting she did in her head against the rhythm of the water that dripped somewhere beyond the shelves — and she had learned the archive's moods the way one learns a difficult colleague's. It creaked when it was pleased. It went very, very quiet when it was listening. And it never, ever let her forget that every book on these shelves was a person who had once made a bargain and lost.

She shelved them now with both hands, as the Steward's clerks required. A living record was not permitted to touch the holdings casually; casualness implied ownership, and in the Undervault nothing was owned except by the court. So Wren held each volume like a sleeping bird and slid it home among its fellows, and if her fingers lingered a half-second longer than regulation allowed, well. The clerks were blindfolded. It was one of the court's little ironies that its archivists were forbidden to *read*.

Wren had been reading since she was four years old. She considered this her small nightly rebellion: she read everything she shelved, memorized everything she read, and carried it all behind her eyes where no ledger could reach. Her order had spent two centuries teaching keepers to hold whole libraries in their heads. They had not anticipated their knowledge being quite so useful for treason.

"Tenth turn," said the clerk at the desk, without looking up. He was called Brannoch, and he had the voice of a man describing weather in a country he'd never visit. "Mark your tally."

"I've marked it." She had. Ten strokes scratched inside her left boot heel, beneath the leather lining, where the moon-court's all-seeing ledgers apparently did not think to look. The Undervault catalogued souls, debts, names, and breaths. It had never once catalogued a boot heel. Wren found this professionally offensive and personally convenient.

Ten turns. Three remained.

And somewhere above her — past the vaults, past the market where memories hung nailed shut with salt, up the long dark throat of the Stair of Turns — Rafael Fairweather was doing something brave and stupid, and Wren could do nothing but shelve books about it.

* * *

She had pieced together what Rafael intended from fragments: a whispered word passed through Marisol, who came to the archive twice a week carrying books back from the Steward's private study and humming, always humming, that lullaby that made Wren's chest ache in a way she hadn't yet found time to examine. Marisol flickered now. That was the word the servants used, dropped like something hot. *The girl flickers.* One moment she was nine years old and afraid of the dark; the next her eyes went old and silver and vast, and she spoke in a voice that remembered oceans.

Rafael had gotten into the soul-vault. Rafael had gotten into the soul-vault and *touched* the contract, because Rafael Fairweather had never in their life met a lock they didn't consider a personal invitation.

Wren understood the impulse. That was the problem. She'd watched Rafael work — watched those quick fingers and that quicker grin, watched a thief talk their way past wards that had eaten better thieves whole — and she understood that Rafael stole not out of greed but out of a kind of desperate generosity. Everything taken was taken *for* someone. Even their own childhood, sold at nine years old to buy their family's survival, had been reframed over the years into an act of agency: *I chose. I paid. I saved them.*

And now Rafael meant to steal back the soul they'd sold, and Wren had read enough contracts in this archive to know what happened when you tried to repossess a soul without settling the debt.

She'd tried to send the warning. That was the bitter comedy of it — three nights ago she had stood before Brannoch's desk and asked, with the flat politeness of a clerk requesting ink, whether outgoing correspondence from the archive required review.

"Everything is reviewed," Brannoch had said.

"Everything?"

"The court keeps what is written." He'd turned a page. "You are written, Keeper Ashvale. You should understand how thoroughly."

So no letters. No notes smuggled in bread — the Undervault's bread contained no hiding places, being less food than formal apology. No whispers down stairwells; the walls here were salt and salt remembered everything spoken against it. Wren had lain awake on her pallet of ledger-cloth running through every channel and finding each one sealed, and had finally arrived, around the hour the false moonlight dimmed to grey, at the only door left open.

Books came back.

The Steward's study borrowed volumes from the archive the way a rich man borrows knives — carelessly, often, and always returning them slightly damaged. And returned books had to be reshelved, and reshelving was done by the living record, and the living record was, at present, a disgraced prophecy-keeper with excellent handwriting and a grudge.

* * *

The volume arrived on the morning of the tenth turn: *On the Custody of Perpetual Things*, fourth edition, its spine cracked, its margins annotated in the Steward's fussy violet hand. Wren received it from the courier with both hands and a bow, carried it to her station, and set it down as though it were nothing at all.

Then she waited for the clerks' midday census, when every blindfolded eye in the archive turned toward the tally-boards and counted what could not be counted anyway, because the Undervault refused on principle to believe its holdings could change.

Her tools were pitiful. A stylus of bone, issued, blunt as an insult. But a keeper learns early that any point will write if you hate it enough, and Wren had sharpened hers against the stone lip of her shelf for eleven nights, in the dark, in silence, pausing whenever the archive went quiet in its listening way.

She opened the book to the middle pages, where a returned volume would be handled least — clerks checked spines and seals, never middles, because middles were where truth lived and truth was not the Undervault's business. Then she wrote. Small. Angled. In the shorthand of her order, which no archivist alive could parse, because her order had been dead or scattered for a decade and its shorthand had died politely along with it.

*To R.F. — if the girl flickers, STOP. The theft cannot hold. A soul lifts from its contract only so far as the debt remains unpaid — you have pulled a thread, not cut it, and every pull now tears HER. The flickering is the contract collecting. It will take her apart between the girl she is and the soul she was unless the bargain is PAID IN FULL. Not stolen. Not broken. Paid. The price is written in the Codex margin — I hid it from you and I would hide it again and I am sorry for both. THE WHOLE OF WHAT WAS TAKEN. Whatever you took from the world, the world must have back. All of it. Understand me: ALL.*

She stopped. Her hand was shaking, and the stylus had bitten through one page into the next, which was either carelessness or honesty.

She looked at the line *I hid it from you* and hated it and left it. There was no time left for mercy-by-omission. She had learned that lesson the slow way, in chapter after chapter of her own complicity: the prophecy her order had written was never a prediction, only a trap with good manners, and she had walked Rafael into it holding the door. If she softened the truth now — if she let Rafael believe there was a clever third path, some thief's trick that would spare them — she'd be writing the trap's final page herself.

Below the warning she pressed one more line, smaller, in plain script rather than shorthand, because some things deserved to be readable even if only by accident:

*You once told me you'd never met a lock worth respecting. Respect this one. Come find me instead. I have an idea, and it's terrible, which means it's probably ours.*

Then she closed the book, breathed on the pages until the faint wet shine of the scratch faded to invisibility — salt-paper held ink the way the sea holds secrets, briefly and under protest — and shelved it where the return-trolley would claim it within the hour.

It would reach the Steward's study by evening. From the Steward's study, if Marisol dusted there that day — and Marisol always dusted there, because the Steward liked his servant frightened and visible — it would pass through the hands of the only other person in the Undervault who loved Rafael Fairweather.

Wren stood back and looked at the shelf, one slim spine among ten thousand, and felt the specific vertigo of having done everything right and knowing it might not matter.

* * *

The trouble with archives, Wren thought that evening, is that they tell you exactly how late everything is.

She was reshelving in the contract wing — punishment detail, self-assigned, because the contract wing was nearest the soul-vault's outer wall and she wanted to be close when whatever happened happened — when she heard the change.

The Undervault hummed. Everyone said so. A low note under the floor, the sound of ten thousand bargains being honored, minute by minute, forever. Wren had tuned it out weeks ago. Now it *shifted* — a half-tone upward, then a stutter, then a silence so total that Brannoch at his desk lifted his head like a deer.

Then the hum resumed, wrong. Skipping. Like a heart remembering it had business elsewhere.

"What," said Brannoch, "was that."

"Census error?" Wren offered.

Brannoch rose, and for the first time in her captivity Wren saw him move quickly. Clerks flooded the aisles, blindfolds pushed up — they could see fine, she'd discovered; the blindfolds were ceremonial, which was somehow worse — and someone shouted from the vault corridor, and the shouting multiplied, and the archive filled with the particular panic of bureaucrats discovering that reality had edited itself without filing forms.

Wren shelved faster.

Because she knew. Of course she knew. She had spent three turns memorizing this collection, and the hum skipping meant the contracts were straining, and the contracts straining meant someone was pulling a soul across a line drawn in salt, and there was exactly one thief in the Undervault with the skill to try it and exactly one lullaby-souled girl caught on the other end.

*Stop,* she thought at the ceiling, at the walls, at the whole salt-hearted mountain. *Heed the book. Stop.*

But had the book reached them? Had Marisol carried it up? Had Rafael opened it, read it, *understood* — or was Rafael, even now, halfway through the impossible theft, fingers deep in a contract, grinning the grin of a person who has never once believed the worst-case scenario applies to them?

A runner clattered past the contract wing. Wren caught his sleeve. "What's happening?"

"The vault," he gasped, delighted and horrified in equal measure. "The soul-vault's singing. The contracts are — the Steward's gone down — they say a soul's come *loose*, half loose, they say the girl's—"

He wrenched free and ran. Wren stood among the shelves with her heart going like a hammer on a nail.

Half loose. Half. The word confirmed everything and fixed nothing. A soul half-loose was a soul tearing, and the tearing would not stop for bravery or love or excellent shorthand. It would stop for payment, or it would stop for death, and Rafael — Rafael who sold their own childhood to save people who maybe never knew, Rafael who gave away pieces of themselves so long ago they'd forgotten what wholeness felt like, Rafael who had once told her, drunk on stolen courage in a border inn, *"the worst thing about my trade is I know exactly what everyone's happiness weighs"* — Rafael would not stop. Not now. Not with their soul — *their actual soul*, the nine-year-old self they'd mourned for twenty years — flickering in and out of a child's body like a candle in wind.

They'd pour everything in. Wren saw it coming the way she'd once seen prophecies come: not as prediction but as pattern. Every memory ever stolen. Every borrowed life. The whole hoard of a lifetime's thieving, poured back into the world to balance a ledger — and it wouldn't be enough, couldn't be enough, because the Codex's price was precise and cruel and *total*: the whole of what was taken. And what had been taken from Rafael was taken from *them*, too — every version of love they'd ever known, every warmth predating age nine — and Rafael didn't know that part yet. Didn't know the bill included the one thing they'd already paid once, as a child, and could not survive paying twice.

Unless they did it anyway. Unless they paid it anyway, empty-handed, hollow-eyed, and stood up afterward a stranger to themselves.

Wren pressed her palm flat against the shelf, against ten thousand kept things, and made the keeper's vow she'd failed to make a decade ago when it mattered: *this time I choose the reader over the text.*

There was one thing she could still do. One message left in her, and it wasn't ink.

*Hold on,* she thought, hard, as if thought could travel. *Both of you hold on. I'm coming. I'll bring the whole rotten library down if I have to.*

Behind her, the archive went quiet in its listening way.

She turned. Brannoch stood at the end of the aisle, blindfold hanging from one hand, violet eyes narrow.

"Keeper Ashvale," he said. "The Steward requests your presence in the vault. He says—" and here Brannoch's weather-report voice faltered, actually faltered, "—he says the girl is asking for you. Both of you. By name. In two voices."

Wren was moving before he finished, boots striking salt-flagstones, tally-marks hidden in her heel, the terrible arithmetic running in her head: three turns left, one soul half-free, one thief who would pay any price — and one memory, the last one, the one about staying, that Wren was suddenly, desperately afraid Rafael was about to spend without ever knowing they'd owned it.
