## 17. Everything I Ever Stole

The warning was still in Wren's hand — scratched into the flyleaf of a returned book, her letters slanting and hurried, as though she'd written it while someone's footsteps were already coming down the archive stairs.

*The flickering will kill her unless the contract is paid in full. The whole of what was taken.*

Rafael read it three times. Then a fourth, because reading had always been easier than understanding, and because there was a part of them — a large part, an increasingly large part — that had built its whole life out of not understanding things until absolutely forced to.

Marisol sat on the vault floor with her back against a pillar of salt, humming. Her outline kept going soft at the edges, like a candle flame seen through water. One moment she was nine years old and scowling at her own knees; the next she was something vast and patient looking out through a child's face with eyes that had watched moons turn for a hundred years. Then she'd blink, and be small again, and ask if Rafael had any food.

"The whole of what was taken," Rafael said aloud, to no one, to the vault, to the lullaby still hanging faintly in the air.

They knew what it meant. That was the terrible thing. They'd known since the moment they read it, the way you know a stair is missing before your foot finds the gap.

Everything they had ever stolen.

* * *

Let me tell you what a memory-thief actually carries.

People think we keep nothing. People think we're hollow men and women walking around with empty heads, taking from others to fill ourselves. And people are wrong in the specific way people are usually wrong about thieves: they imagine theft goes one direction.

When I lift a memory, I don't erase it. I *copy* it — I pull it loose from its moorings and carry it away, and what remains behind is a scar shaped like happiness. The dying smuggler whose happiest hour I took in Chapter One of my own sorry story — he remembered that something had been there. He spent his last weeks reaching for it like a man patting his pockets for keys he could hear jingling somewhere just out of reach. I told myself coin was fair payment. I told myself a great many things.

But here is the trade nobody warns you about: once a stolen memory lives in your head long enough, it stops being foreign. It puts down roots. It starts sending up shoots. You remember loving a wife you never met, in a house on a river you never sailed, and the love is *real*, because love doesn't check its paperwork. You remember a mother's hands kneading bread, a brother's laugh, a first kiss under a bad roof during a good storm — none of them yours, all of them worn smooth now from years of your keeping, fitted to the shape of you like stones in a riverbed.

Twenty-three years of thieving. Do you understand? I have carried perhaps four hundred lives inside me. I have loved more people than most priests have buried. I am a cathedral built entirely out of other people's stained glass, and every pane lets in light that was never mine, and I had convinced myself — truly, thoroughly, with the full confidence of a professional — that this made me rich.

I sat down on the cold vault floor across from Marisol and did the arithmetic.

To pay the contract in full, I would have to pour back everything I had ever taken. Every life. Every love. Every borrowed year. Not just give them up — *return* them, send each one home to whatever remained of whoever I'd taken it from, or scatter them into the world like seeds if their owners were ash by now. The Undervault didn't want a payment. It wanted restitution.

And what would be left of Rafael Fairweather when the cathedral came down?

A name. A date of birth. A childhood sold at age nine and a memory of selling it. Whatever I'd managed to grow myself, honestly, in the thin soil between heists. Probably not much. Probably a person roughly the size of a knife, a lockpick set, and a talent for walking quietly.

"You're making a face," Marisol said. She was small again. She had a smudge of salt on her chin. "Wren makes that face too. When she's counting something she doesn't want to count."

"I'm not counting anything."

"Liar." She said it comfortably, the way children do, without judgment, as though lying were simply another weather. "You're counting how much of you there'd be left. I can see the answer on you."

"And what's the answer?"

She considered me with those flickering eyes, old and young trading places behind them like cards shuffled by a patient hand. "Enough," she said. "Barely enough. But barely enough is how everyone starts."

I laughed. It came out cracked down the middle, but it came out.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about terrible clarity: it isn't dramatic. It isn't thunder and revelation. It's quiet. It's the moment you stop arguing with a sum because you've checked it eleven times and it keeps coming out the same.

I had tried, in the hours since finding Wren's message, to find another door. Of course I had. I am Rafael Fairweather; finding doors is my entire profession. I had gone through the ledger again — the Steward's ledger, lifted clean from his vault in a single night's work, the easiest theft of my career and the heaviest thing I've ever carried. Thousands of souls. My parents' names among them, held as collateral on a debt paid a lifetime ago, because the Undervault does not release, it only accumulates. I had looked for loopholes the way a drowning man looks for driftwood, and the ledger had looked back at me with the flat patience of accounting.

The contract on Marisol's soul specified its terms. Souls sold beneath the mountain could be returned by three roads: purchase, conquest, or repayment. The court's price for purchase was a soul of equal weight — and no soul weighed more than a whole life. Conquest required an army, and my army consisted of one disgraced keeper currently imprisoned in the archives for my sake, one flickering child, and me.

Repayment, then. *The whole of what was taken.* And what had been taken — what had *I* taken, all these years, hoarding other people's yesterdays like a dragon of secondhand gold?

Everything. I had taken everything I had.

There was a horrible elegance to it. The Undervault ran on salt and debt, and I was the greatest debtor it had never thought to bill. Twenty-three years of withdrawal from the world's account. Now the world was calling in the loan.

I thought about refusing. Let me be honest — I thought about it for a long time, sitting in that vault while Marisol hummed and flickered, and the thought wore my own voice: *You'll be nobody. You'll stand up from this floor and there won't be enough of you left to walk out. Is a child's soul worth the whole architecture of who you are?*

And then Marisol hummed the lullaby — my lullaby, the one sold out of me when I was nine, the one note of my own first life I'd ever recovered — and the argument collapsed, because I understood suddenly and completely that I had never owned any of it anyway. Not the smuggler's wedding day. Not the fisherman's dawn on green water. Not one hour of four hundred lives. A thief owns nothing. A thief only holds, and holding was never the same as having, and I had confused the two for so long that the confusion had grown bones and learned to answer to my name.

Terrible clarity. It feels like setting down a weight you forgot you were carrying, and discovering the weight was you.

* * *

I began with the smallest ones, because you learn to start small in my trade. Test the lock before the vault.

The pickpocket's memory of rain on a tin roof — a two-minute theft, my first ever, taken from a boy in Saltmarket when I was fifteen and greedy and stupid with power. I closed my eyes, found it where it lived behind my left ear, all rust-colored sound, and did the thing I had never done: instead of pulling it deeper, I pushed it *out*. I let go.

It went like water finding a drain. For half a heartbeat I felt the boy — man now, surely, if he lived — feel the rain return to him somewhere in the world, felt the tin roof ring again under his own sky. And then it was gone, and I waited to feel lighter.

I felt *smaller*. That was the surprise. Lightness and smallness together, like a coat removed in winter.

"Again?" Marisol asked softly. She had stopped humming. She was watching me with the old eyes, the patient ones, and I understood she knew exactly what was happening to me, had perhaps known longer than I had.

"Again," I said.

The baker's morning. Gone — and somewhere, I hoped, a man woke remembering why he loved the smell of yeast at four in the morning. The widow's dance with her husband, thirty years dead, which I had carried for a decade and treasured shamefully, my private proof that love outlasted bodies. I gave it back with shaking hands. If her ghost walked anywhere near that memory, let them dance again.

The soldier's letter home, unsent. The midwife's thousand first cries. The cartographer's awe at coastlines. The fisherman's green water. The smuggler's wedding day — the very first big theft, the one that made my reputation, the one I'd told myself a hundred times had been fair trade. I held it a long time. In it, a woman with flour on her sleeves laughed at a joke I had never understood because the joke belonged to a marriage I had never had, and I loved her, God help me, I had loved her for twenty years without her ever existing in my life at all.

"I'm sorry," I said to her, whoever she was, wherever she was. "It was never mine to keep."

I let it go. Somewhere in the world a woman laughed at a joke, and did not know why she was crying.

* * *

By the fiftieth memory I had stopped sitting up. By the hundredth I had lost track of which memories were stolen and which — the few, the precious few — were genuinely mine, and this frightened me more than anything, because the border between *me* and *what I carry* had been eroding for years and I had simply declined to look at the coastline.

Names went first. I reached for the name of the woman with flour on her sleeves — gone. Then faces blurred, then the order of my own life began to shuffle like a deck in careless hands. I remembered loving someone on a river and could not say if the river was hers or mine. I remembered grief for a brother and couldn't find the brother. The cathedral was coming down pane by pane, and the light coming through the gaps was strange and thin and honest, and I didn't know anymore whether what stood in that light deserved to be called anyone at all.

Marisol held my hand through it. Small hands, salt-smudged, steady. Whenever I flickered toward panic she hummed, and the lullaby was an anchor line, and I gripped it.

"Who taught you that song?" I asked once, between losses, clinging to the question the way you cling to a rail in a storm.

"A lady made of paper," Marisol said. "In the dark place. Before the girl-body. She sang it so I wouldn't forget there was an outside." She tilted her head. "It was your song first. Did you know that? I could always taste you on it."

"I know," I said. "I'm beginning to know everything too late. It's my one consistent talent."

That got a laugh out of her — the child-laugh, bright and startling in that vault of salt and debt — and I held onto the sound of it harder than I'd held onto any stolen thing, because it was happening *now*, to *me*, un-borrowed, and I was learning, at the bottom of the world, what a real moment weighed.

Two hundred. Three hundred. I poured lives back into the dark and the dark carried them home. With each one I got simpler. Quieter. The clever machinery of me — the angles, the aliases, the forty ways into a locked room — ground slower and slower, gears losing their teeth. I could feel the thief leaving, and with him the armor, and with the armor the excuse, and under all of it, emerging blinking into honest light like something dug out of a landslide:

A person. Very small. Very frightened.

Still here.

"Barely enough," I whispered, remembering.

"Told you," said Marisol.

* * *

Near the end — I knew it was near the end because I could no longer remember the number — I found the last of them, and my hands stopped.

Not the smuggler. Not the widow. This one was recent. Weeks old. Taken from a woman in a market town whose name I had already given back along with everything else, but the memory itself remained: a kitchen, evening, a stranger laughing at her own bad joke while she cooked, and the sudden fierce wish — *mine, that night, in that body, that borrowed heart* — *I wish I had someone to come home to.*

I had carried that wish out of her head like a coal in cupped hands. It had burned quietly in me ever since. It was the reason I'd stayed at Wren's side when running would have been smarter. It was the reason I'd learned how she took her tea. It was the seed of everything that had grown between us in the dark under Thornfell, and it wasn't even mine — it was a stranger's loneliness, transplanted, blooming in foreign soil.

And I loved what had grown from it. Truly. That was the cruelty of the final payment: to return this memory was to pull up the root of the only living thing I had.

"If I give this back," I said to Marisol, and my voice sounded like a stranger's, thin and new, "I might not remember her. Wren. Why any of this mattered. Why I'm lying on a floor giving away my whole self for a girl who hums."

"That's true," Marisol said, with the terrible honesty of the young and the ancient.

"Then why am I doing it?"

"Because you haven't done it yet," she said, "and you already know you will." She squeezed my hand. "That's the whole of what was taken, Rafael Fairweather. But it isn't the whole of you. The choosing isn't a memory. Nobody can steal the choosing. Believe me — I've been a kept thing for a hundred years. They kept everything except that."

The last memory hovered in my cupped hands like the coal it was.

I thought: *someone will come home to her someday, and it will be herself, and the wish will be answered, just not the way she imagined.* I thought: *whatever Wren and I are, we'll have to be built again from nothing, and maybe — maybe — built honestly this time, without any stolen parts.*

I opened my hands.

The wish went home through the dark, and the kitchen vanished, and the laughter vanished, and Rafael Fairweather sat in a salt vault holding almost nothing at all — a name, a fear, a song half-remembered, a stubbornness that had survived the flood — and above us, far above, through a mile of mountain, the twelfth moon was rising, and somewhere in the archives a keeper I could no longer quite picture was running out of time.

"What happens now?" I asked. The words cost effort, like lifting stones.

Marisol stood. Her edges had stopped flickering. She looked, for the first time, entirely and only like a girl — and she was looking at the vault door with an expression I recognized, because it used to live on my face.

"Now," she said, "we run."
