## 18. The Twelfth Turn

The archive let her go the way a fist opens — not all at once, but finger by finger, each one reluctant.

Wren had spent three turns of the moon as a kept thing in the Undervault's great library, shelved between a ledger of drowned sailors and a jar of somebody's last words. She had been catalogued. She had been *indexed*. A court archivist with too many fingers and no eyelids had pressed a salt-stamp to her palm and pronounced her "a minor holding, keeper-class, depreciating."

She had thought about that word — *depreciating* — for a very long time in the dark.

Now the shelves were screaming.

It began as a tremor in the floor, then in the books themselves, spines rattling like teeth in a cold jaw. Somewhere above, Rafael was unraveling — she could feel it the way you feel weather coming in an old scar, a pressure behind the eyes that meant something enormous was being given away. Every stolen memory Rafael surrendered went somewhere, and where it went was *into the record*, and every record in the Undervault was straining against its binding to hold it.

The salt-stamp on Wren's palm cracked.

"Unscheduled release," said the many-fingered archivist, hurrying past her with an armload of trembling volumes. "Nobody move. Nobody *breathe* on anything—"

Wren moved. Wren breathed on several things out of spite. And then she ran, up through the archive's throat, past the reading rooms where courtiers clutched their own memories of the commotion like frightened pets, out into the long corridor of kept things where the lamps burned blue and low, and toward the vault at the bottom of everything.

*Twelve turns,* she counted against her heartbeat as she ran. *Twelve of thirteen.* The twelfth moon would be rising over Thornfell even now, fat and white and merciless, sliding into its place in the sky like the last coin dropped into a merchant's palm.

One turn left. One night. And Rafael was emptying themself out like a jug poured to the dregs.

---

The vault doors were open.

That alone told her how bad it was. The soul-vault of the Undervault had stood sealed for longer than her order had kept records, guarded by law so old it had forgotten it was law. Now both doors stood wide, groaning on hinges furred with salt-crystals, and the air inside moved strangely — thick, sweet, wrong, like the inside of a struck bell.

Rafael knelt at the center of it, hands buried to the wrist in a contract that had stopped being paper and become something more like light. Marisol stood beside them, flickering — girl, then something older and vaster looking out through a child's face, then girl again, each transition a little harder to come back from.

And around them all, the Steward's court had gathered: the moon-crowned, the salt-veiled, the beautiful terrible bureaucracy of the Undervault, arranged in tiers like spectators at an execution who genuinely hoped for a pardon.

"—cannot hold," the Steward was saying, and his voice had the patience of a man reading terms aloud to someone already signed. "The contract frays because it is unpaid. You pour memories into the world, thief, and the ledger drinks them, but a soul is not made of memories. A soul is made of *debt*. Yours has never once been paid in full."

"I'm paying," Rafael said. Their voice came out sanded down, thin as the last page of a well-loved book. Wren's chest hurt to hear it. "Everything I ever stole. Everything I ever took. It goes back — all of it—"

"Not everything." The Steward turned his head, slow as a door, and found Wren standing in the doorway. Salt-veils rippled. The court murmured like a tide deciding whether to come in.

"*There* is what you have not returned," he said. "The reader. The keeper. The one your order sent into my vault a hundred years ago, carrying secrets she was sworn to burn. She still holds them. She has always held them." He smiled, and it was almost gentle, which was how Wren knew it was cruelty. "Bring me her records, thief, and I will consider the account settled."

"Don't," Wren said.

Rafael looked up. Their eyes were the worst part — not blank, not yet, but *thinning*, like frost on a window going clear. They were still in there. That was the horror of it. They were still in there, watching themself go.

"Wren?" they said. "Is that—" A frown, groping. "You stayed. Didn't you? You stayed the whole time."

"I did," Wren said, and crossed the vault floor with her chin up, because if she was about to do the largest thing of her life she was going to do it with decent posture. "And I'm about to stay a little longer. Everyone stay calm. Especially you," she added to the Steward. "You especially. I've read your ledgers. You're a middle manager with a crown."

A ripple of something that might have been laughter went through the lower tiers of the court before the salt-veils silenced it.

---

Here is what Wren Ashvale had carried for eleven years, wrapped in guilt and kept close as a second heartbeat:

Her order — the Keepers of the Turning Page, may their name be ash — had not written the Codex to *foretell*. Prophecy was the bait. The Codex was a trap built to reclaim the sold soul for the order's own use, a soul powerful enough to anchor any future they cared to write. Every reader drawn into its pages had been a retrieval attempt. Every reader who failed had been written off in the margins as *spent*.

And Wren had known. For ten years she had known, since the night she found her superiors' true marginalia hidden beneath the false ones, and instead of burning the Codex as commanded she had run with it — not to destroy it, but because she couldn't decide whose hands deserved it less. Her cowardice had delivered it to a smuggler's cache. Her cowardice had delivered it to *Rafael*.

She had told herself, in the archive dark, that confessing this would serve nothing. That mercy was kinder than truth. She had hidden the cost of the bargain from Rafael for seven turns while pretending it was protection.

Duty, her teachers called it. Duty, discretion, the keeper's burden.

It was fear wearing duty's coat, and she was done with the coat.

"Steward of the Undervault," Wren said formally, pitching her voice the way she'd been trained, the way that made even many-fingered archivists stand straighter. "I invoke the Rite of Substitution. Keeper's law, third covenant: *a debt may be paid by any holder of equal weight.*"

Murmurs. The Steward's smile thinned at the edges.

"You hold nothing," he said. "You are a minor holding yourself."

"I hold this." She reached into her coat and drew out the thing she had protected through exile and disgrace and three turns as a living bookend: her order's master record. Not the Codex — the *commentary*. A century of keepers' secrets, names of readers spent, souls weighed, futures hoarded like a dragon's bed. Every secret the Turning Page had ever kept from the world, kept instead by the one keeper who'd failed to destroy them.

"The whole of what was taken," she said quietly, "was taken from everyone. So here is the whole of what I have. Every secret my order ever wrote down. Every name they spent. Every future they stole in advance." She held it out flat on her palms, an offering, a surrender, a dare. "Weigh it against the contract. If it comes short, take me back to the archive after. I'll shelve nicely; I've had practice."

"Wren." Rafael's voice, cracked and small. "What are you doing?"

"Something my teachers would call heresy," Wren said, not taking her eyes off the Steward, "and what I call Tuesday."

---

The weighing took an hour, and it was unbearable, and it was also — she would remember this later, when there was later — oddly funny.

The Steward's clerks brought out scales the size of coffins. They weighed the commentary against the contract while the court watched, whispering behind veils, placing wagers she pretended not to see. At one point two clerks got into a genuine argument over whether a secret confessed freely outweighed a secret extracted under seal, and the elder clerk cited precedent from the reign of some drowned king, and the younger one said, with real feeling, "*That* precedent was overturned during the Silt Interregnum, everybody knows that—" and Wren caught herself thinking that Rafael would have loved this, would have had three cons ready before the scales settled, and had to press her hand flat to her sternum to keep breathing.

Marisol flickered beside the contract. Girl. Something vast. Girl. In the gaps between flickers the child looked at Wren with eyes far too old and hummed four notes of a lullaby, and Wren's throat closed, because those four notes had followed them down the whole mountain, patient as water.

Finally the head clerk stepped forward and bowed.

"The weight balances," the clerk announced, "to within one turn."

"One turn," the Steward repeated, tasting it. "You have purchased delay, keeper, not absolution. The thirteenth moon will rise tomorrow night regardless of your books. If the contract is not paid in full by moonset—" he spread his elegant hands, "—then everything returns to my keeping, including the girl, including the debt, including whatever is left of the thief."

"Then we'll pay it by moonset," Wren said.

"With *what*?" His composure slipped, just a hairline crack, and beneath it she saw something startled. "You have given your order's whole weight. The thief has given every stolen year. What remains in either of your hands but air?"

Wren looked at Rafael — kneeling, thinning, still stubbornly, stupidly *there* — and felt the answer settle into her the way the lullaby had settled into Marisol: not chosen, exactly. Recognized.

"That's not for you to weigh," she said. "Tomorrow night. Thirteenth moon. Be here." She paused, and allowed herself one small, unkeeperly satisfaction: "Bring your scales. You'll want them."

---

They put Rafael to rest in a side chamber off the vault, on a bench that had probably been a throne until the Undervault demoted it. Marisol curled beside them like a cat who had decided, on her own authority, that this person belonged to her now. The flickering had slowed. Small mercies.

Wren sat on the floor across from them, back to the wall, and talked, because talking seemed to be the one thing keeping the frost from closing over their eyes.

"So," she said. "You're probably wondering what you missed. Turns eight through twelve, a retrospective. Turn eight: I got filed under 'K.' There's a joke there I refuse to make. Turn nine: I taught the archivist a card game and he banned it, which I consider my finest achievement below ground. Turn ten—" her voice caught; she smoothed it flat, "—turn ten you tried to steal a soul out of a contract, which remains the most insane thing anyone has ever done in my presence, and I once watched a man argue with a prophecy and *win*."

Rafael's mouth curved. Barely. But it curved.

"Turn eleven," Wren went on, softer, "you started giving everything back. And turn twelve, tonight, I finally did the one thing I've been afraid of since I was twenty-three years old." She laughed, a little wetly. "Do you know what's funny? My entire order trained me for sacrifice. Ceremony, liturgy, the keeper's burden, all of it. And not one lesson covered how much it *itches* afterward. I gave away a century of secrets and my hands feel like I've set them down after carrying them upstairs."

"Wren." Rafael's voice was a thread. "Did I choose you? Before. When I couldn't remember. Did I—"

"You did," Wren said. "You chose to stay when every sensible instinct said run. It's in the record. I checked. I check obsessively; it's a flaw."

"Mm." Their eyes drifted shut. "Good choice. Tell me I was smug about it."

"Insufferably."

"*Good.*"

Their breathing evened. Marisol's small hand found Rafael's sleeve and gripped it. Above them, through stone older than language, the thirteenth moon began its climb toward tomorrow.

Wren sat in the blue-lit dark and did not sleep, and did not pray — her order had forfeited her prayers along with everything else — but she did something adjacent to praying. She took stock of her hands, empty now of every secret she'd ever kept, and found that they didn't feel empty at all.

They felt ready.

Somewhere overhead, the last moon of the year cleared the shoulder of Thornfell, white as a fresh page, and began, slowly, terribly, to rise.
