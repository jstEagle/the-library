## 20. Empty Hands

The ledgers began to empty at moonrise, and the sound they made was nothing like I expected.

I had imagined something grand — a tearing of chains, a thunderclap of law fulfilled. Instead it was a sigh. Thousands of contracts unbinding themselves in the Steward's vault, page after page going soft and gray as ash, and the whole Undervault exhaled like an old man setting down a load he'd carried too long to remember why.

I stood in the doorway of the soul-vault and watched names lift off the paper. Not fly — *lift*, the way dust lifts when you open curtains in a room no one has entered for years. Each name rose, hung a moment in the cold blue light of the vault lamps, and then went wherever names go when they are finally nobody's property.

Rafael stood beside me with their hands hanging loose and empty at their sides.

They did not watch the ledgers. They were watching their own hands, turning them over slowly, as though checking whether they still worked. Which, I supposed, from where they stood, was a fair question.

"Rafael," I said.

They looked up. Their eyes found mine, held them politely, and gave back nothing. It was not the flatness of the salt-nailed courtiers; this was emptiness the way a well is empty — deep, clean, waiting to be filled by rain or rope. Every stolen year poured out of them and into the world. Every borrowed love, every lifted grief, nine lives' worth of other people's pasts given back to whoever owned them first.

What remained was Rafael Fairweather, minus everything Rafael had ever taken.

Which was, I realized with a lurch that started in my chest and ended somewhere below my knees, nearly all of Rafael.

"Do I know you?" they asked.

Their voice was theirs. That was the worst of it, somehow. The same dry edge, the same faint amusement even now, as if the habit of irony lived deeper than memory, down in the bone where thieves keep their knives.

"You do," I said. "You did. You will again."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." I offered my hand. "Come on. We're leaving while the leaving's good. Courts that lose their ledgers get very sentimental about visitors."

They looked at my hand but didn't take it. "Where would we be going?"

"Up." I nodded toward the far wall, where the Stair of Turns wound away into darkness, each step still faintly marked with its moon. "There's a sky up there. You won't remember liking it, but trust me, you do."

* * *

The Undervault was dying the way a theater dies after the last performance — not collapsing, just emptying, its purpose gone out of it like heat out of a kettle.

We passed courtiers standing in the corridors with their hands pressed flat against the walls, feeling the mountain change around them. Some wept. Some laughed. One elderly woman in robes of gray salt-silk stopped me as we went by and gripped my sleeve with surprising strength.

"The ledgers are open," she said. "Do you understand what that means? Nothing is *held* anymore. Nothing is kept." She said the word the way her kind always had — *kept* — with reverence, and then, hearing herself, with horror. She let go of my sleeve. "Go. Go quickly, before someone remembers how to want things."

"I don't think anyone here remembers how to want anything else," I said, which was unkind, and true, and she nodded as if I'd paid her a compliment.

Marisol was waiting for us at the foot of the Stair.

She was sitting cross-legged on the bottom step, swinging a satchel that must have weighed more than she did, and when she saw Rafael she scrambled up so fast she nearly tripped over her own hem.

"You're empty," she said. It wasn't a question. Children of the Undervault learned early to see what adults pretended not to.

"Yes," Rafael said, agreeably, because whatever else had been poured out of them, courtesy appeared to remain. "It seems I've been robbed."

"No," Marisol said fiercely. "You *robbed yourself*. For me." Her chin was trembling. She had a soul nine-tenths restored and a body that was still eight years old, and she was trying to carry both at once, and doing better at it than most grown people manage. "That's not being robbed. That's —"

"It's arithmetic," Rafael said gently. "Someone subtracted from me. I'd like to know who, so I can decide whether to thank them or bill them."

Marisol looked at me, stricken.

"He doesn't remember," she said. "*Any* of it?"

"Not yet," I said.

"Can you —"

"No." I said it before she finished, and heard how hard the word came out of me, and softened it as best I could. "Keeper's law. A memory spoken into another mind is the one theft that can never be undone. If I pour my telling of him into his head, it becomes *my* story wearing *his* face. He deserves better than that. He deserves to earn himself back."

"That's stupid," Marisol said.

"Yes," I agreed. "Most honorable things are."

She glared at me for a long moment — a child's glare, total war, no reserves held back — and then she marched up to Rafael and took their empty hand in both of hers.

"Then I'll tell you one thing," she said, "and it isn't a memory, so it isn't against anybody's law. Your name is Rafael. You like the top step of any staircase, high windows, and honey in your tea instead of sugar. And you hum when you think nobody's listening, and you're bad at it."

Rafael looked down at her. Something moved behind their eyes — not recognition, nothing so generous, but interest, maybe, the first small fish returning to the emptied well.

"Honey," they said, testing it. "Hm. I'll take your word for it. You seem like a reliable authority."

"I'm the most reliable authority you know."

"You may be the only authority I know."

"Even better." She hoisted her satchel onto her shoulder and planted herself on Rafael's left like a small determined guard. "I'm coming up with you. My soul's mine now, and I'm taking it somewhere with weather."

So we climbed.

* * *

The Stair of Turns had been, on our descent, a calendar — thirteen landings, thirteen moons, each step showing us what was coming. Now, with the bargain broken and the Codex dead in the world above, the steps showed nothing at all. Just stone, worn smooth in the middle by feet that had climbed them long before either of our orders existed.

Rafael walked between Marisol and me, steady enough on the stairs, but every hundred steps or so they would stop and look around with the expression of a person who has walked into a room and forgotten the errand.

"Tell me something true," they said, the third time it happened. It had become a ritual already, in the space of an hour. People build rituals fast when they're building themselves.

"All right." I cast about. "Your full name is Rafael Fairweather. You chose the surname yourself, at fourteen, off a gravestone in the Thornfell passes, because it sounded like someone who'd survive."

"Did I survive?"

"Spectacularly. Mostly out of spite."

"That tracks," they said, and there it was again — that flicker of the old dry humor rising up out of the emptiness like a green shoot through rubble, and my heart did something undignified in my chest. "Gravestones. Spite. I feel like I'm getting a coherent picture of myself."

"You're getting the accurate one. The flattering version comes later, if you earn it."

"And who are you in this picture?"

The question landed harder than they could have known. I kept walking, kept my voice level. "Wren Ashvale. Formerly of the Order of Kept Hours. Currently unemployed, disgraced, and — as of tonight — the last keeper of prophecies in the world, unless you count that child, who mostly keeps secrets about stealing honey."

"She told you about the honey?" Marisol said, scandalized.

"She tells everyone about the honey. It's her best material."

Rafael laughed.

It was a small laugh, startled out of them, and they stopped mid-step and touched their own mouth as if checking where it had come from. "Oh," they said. "That works. Laughing works. Good to know."

And I thought: *they are discovering joy the way a child does, one sense at a time, and I am the only witness,* and I had to pretend to adjust my bootlace for a while until my face remembered its job.

* * *

On the ninth landing we rested. There was a niche there, cut into the rock, with a bench worn smooth — some older pilgrim's kindness, centuries back. Marisol fell asleep almost instantly, curled against Rafael's side with her satchel as a pillow, the way children sleep: completely, shamelessly, as if sleep were a country they held dual citizenship in.

Rafael sat very still, so as not to wake her, and looked at me across the little lamplit niche.

"You keep looking at me like I owe you money," they said.

"I'm sorry. I'm not — I'll stop."

"Don't stop. I'm curious. What's the sum?"

I considered lying. I considered silence. I had spent the entire climb telling them facts — their name, their trade, their habits, their crimes — carefully curated truths, each one load-bearing, none of them dangerous. But they were looking at me with those emptied eyes, patient as a well, and lying to a person with no memories felt like pickpocketing someone in a hospital bed.

"You gave up everything you had to free her," I said, nodding at the sleeping girl. "Every stolen memory. Years of other people's lives — loves, griefs, whole childhoods you carried so you wouldn't have to carry your own. You poured them all back. That's the sum. Everything. And you did it laughing at your own expense, because that's apparently how you do everything unbearable."

Rafael absorbed this. "And you watched?"

"I helped. Badly, at first. Better, later."

"Why 'badly'?"

"Because I knew what it would cost you and I let you find out anyway, because the alternative was letting a child die, and I chose, and choosing is the part of me I like least on most days."

They were quiet for a moment. Above us, the stair turned upward into dark.

"You talk like someone reading a confession," they said at last, "but you stayed. Through the bad parts and the later-better parts. Why?"

"Because —" And here I ran aground, thoroughly, on the oldest rock in my sea. Duty. Law. Guilt. All my life I'd had words ready for why I did things, the Order's whole vocabulary of obligation, and every one of those words felt like a coin from a country that no longer existed. "Because I wanted to. Because you make terrible choices bravely and I find that — compelling. Because someone should tell you your own story until you can tell it yourself, and I volunteered before anyone else could."

"That's three reasons."

"You asked a three-reason question."

"Mm." They leaned their head back against the stone and closed their eyes, and for a moment they looked so much like the Rafael of the seventh turn — exhausted, sharp, secretly terrified — that my chest ached with the unfairness of it. "Wren Ashvale. Unemployed, disgraced, last of your order."

"Yes."

"When we get to the top, and I presumably still don't know you — what happens then?"

"We figure it out," I said. "You meet me again. Properly. You'll be suspicious, probably. You're suspicious of everyone; it's one of your better qualities."

"And you'll put up with that?"

"For as long as it takes."

They opened one eye. "That sounds like devotion without expectation."

"It's the only kind I've ever been any good at."

Something crossed their face — not memory, not quite, but the shape memory leaves behind, like a dent in a pillow after the sleeper rises. They reached over, slow and deliberate, and took my hand. Their grip was careful, questioning, a stranger's grip — but it was warm, and it did not let go, and neither did I.

"For the record," Rafael said, "whoever I was, they had excellent taste."

* * *

We climbed through the twelfth landing as dawn approached underground — the Undervault kept its own hours, and its dawns were pale fungal lights dimming along the ceiling like a city going to bed. On the thirteenth step, the last step, the stone was different. Older. And carved into it, so worn you had to trace it with fingers to read it, was a line in the first keeper's script:

*WHAT IS GIVEN FREELY CANNOT BE TAKEN BACK.*

I had read that line on the way down and thought it a warning. Standing on it now, with Rafael's hand in mine and Marisol yawning on my hip, I understood it differently. It wasn't a warning. It was permission.

"Almost there," Marisol mumbled against my shoulder. "I can smell pine. Real pine, not the preserved kind."

"One flight," I said.

Rafael stopped at the threshold where the stair met the mountain road, where actual night air moved, cold and resinous and alive. They stood in it with their eyes closed like a man tasting rain after drought.

"There's a moon up there," they said. "I don't know how I know that."

"Because you've seen it ten thousand times."

"Will I remember seeing it?"

I hesitated. This was the hook of it, wasn't it — the thing I'd been circling the whole climb, the thing Marisol had asked and I'd refused. Keeper's law sat on my shoulders like a yoke I'd worn so long I'd forgotten it could come off. A memory spoken directly into another mind. The one theft beyond undoing. The one crime my order reserved its deepest cell for.

But my order was ash and ledgers now. Its vaults stood open. Its law lay in the dust with everything else the Undervault had kept.

And there was one memory I carried that had never belonged to the Order at all. One that was not stolen, not archived, not written in any book. The night on this very road when Rafael Fairweather, given every reason to run, had looked at me and chosen to stay. I had no right to give it. I had less right to withhold it — because without it, they would spend the rest of their life wondering why a disgraced stranger looked at them like that, and wondering, for Rafael, would curdle into suspicion, and suspicion into distance, and distance into the loneliest second childhood ever lived.

"Wren?" Rafael said. "You've gone strange. Stranger."

"I'm deciding something," I said. "Give me a moment. It's been a century since anyone in my family made a good decision; I want to break the streak properly."

Marisol snorted. Rafael waited, patient as a well.

Above us, through the crack of the cave mouth, the moon was rising — not the thirteenth, that bargain was dead, just an ordinary moon, silver and unaffiliated, climbing over Thornfell like it had nowhere better to be.

I turned to face Rafael fully, took both their hands in mine, and drew breath.

"Wren," they said, half laughing, half wary. "What are you about to do?"

"Something no keeper has ever done," I said. "Hold still. And if it works — welcome home."
