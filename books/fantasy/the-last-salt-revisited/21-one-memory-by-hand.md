## 21. One Memory, By Hand

The morning they came up out of the mountain, the world was so ordinary it hurt.

Grass. Actual grass, bent sideways by a wind that smelled of pine and thaw and nothing at all — no salt, no ink, no cold ledger-iron. Rafael stood at the mouth of the Stair of Turns with the sun full on their face and could not have said why their eyes were wet. They didn't remember crying. They didn't remember much of anything.

They knew that, at least. That was the strange mercy of it: they knew what they had lost even if they couldn't name a single piece of it. It was like standing in a house where every shelf had been emptied but the dust still showed the shapes of the things that used to live there. *Something* had stood here. Many somethings. Whole lives' worth of somethings, borrowed from strangers and worn until they fit.

"Rafael."

They turned. The woman behind them had dirt on her face and a fresh scar along her jaw and eyes that looked like she'd spent the last thirteen moons holding up the sky by main force. She was watching them the way you watch a candle in a draft.

"You know my name," Rafael said. Their voice came out rough. "I don't know yours."

"Wren." She said it carefully, like setting down something breakable. "Wren Ashvale."

"And I know you." Not a question. The knowing lived below the memory, in the body, in the way their chest turned toward her like a plant toward light. "I just don't know *how* I know you."

"That's fair," Wren said. "You paid for me with everything you had. I'd be insulted if you remembered me for free."

Rafael laughed — startled out of them, rusty, real. Wren's face did something complicated at the sound, relief and grief fighting for the same ground, and then she shouldered her pack and pointed up the switchback trail.

"There's a town two days east. Marisol's already ahead of us; she walks fast when she's got somewhere to be for the first time in her life." She hesitated. "You can walk with us. Or not. Either way is — either way."

"I'll walk with you," Rafael said, and watched something in her shoulders come down half an inch. "I don't know why, but I trust you. That seems worth following."

"It's more than I deserve," Wren murmured, and started climbing.

* * *

Two days on the road taught Rafael the shape of their own emptiness.

They were still themselves — that was the unsettling part. The hands still picked locks without being asked. When Marisol dropped her bread near the fire the second night, Rafael caught it without looking, and the girl stared at them and said, "You used to do that all the time," in a voice that suggested this meant more than it sounded like it meant.

"Did I?"

"You laughed after," Marisol said. Then, quieter, watching them: "You don't laugh now."

"I laugh," Rafael protested.

"You make the noise," the girl allowed, with the brutal honesty of nine-year-olds everywhere, or whatever age she was now, soul-grown and body-small and finally, finally her own. "It's different."

She hummed while she ate. The lullaby. Rafael felt it land somewhere beneath the emptied shelves, in the foundation, where nothing had been taken because nothing there had ever been stolen — only given away, long ago, by someone too young to know what a soul cost at market. The melody didn't bring back memories. But it brought back *warmth*, diffuse and sourceless, like sun through a curtain.

"Marisol." Rafael waited until she looked up. "What was I like? Before. You'd know better than anyone."

The girl chewed thoughtfully. "Scared," she said at last. "But you kept choosing not to run. That's the thing about you. Everybody thinks thieves are brave but you weren't, you were terrified the whole time, and you stayed anyway. Every time." She shrugged, went back to her bread. "Wren says that counts double."

"Wren says a lot of things."

"She stayed up all night talking to you once," Marisol said, sly as a pickpocket. "When you couldn't sleep. Ask her what she said."

"I'm asking you."

"Nope." The girl grinned, gap-toothed and unrepentant, and rolled into her blanket. "Some things you gotta get from the source."

* * *

That night, when Marisol slept and the fire had gone down to a red heart, Rafael found Wren sitting watch with the Codex's ash still under her fingernails and a book of her order's returned records open on her knees, reading by moonlight out of what looked like pure habit.

"You should sleep," Rafael said.

"So should you."

"I don't dream," Rafael said. "There's nothing to dream with. It turns out sleeping is just lying down and losing." They sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and studied her profile against the stars. "Tell me something true. About before. About us."

"Us," Wren repeated. Her ears went faintly pink, which seemed like an odd thing for a woman who had faced down a moon-court to manage.

"Was there an us? Everyone keeps looking at me like I broke something valuable. Even Marisol. *Especially* Marisol."

Wren closed her book. For a long moment she said nothing, and Rafael watched her weigh words the way she must once have weighed prophecies — turning each one over, checking for traps.

"There wasn't an us," she said finally. "Not the way you mean. There was a thief who kept refusing to leave, and a keeper who kept pretending that annoyed her." A pause. "And there was one night, on the seventh turn, when the Codex erased every exit and we both understood we might die in the dark down there. And you—" Her voice snagged. She cleared her throat. "You chose to stay. With me. Nobody made you. There was nothing left to steal, no bargain to strike, no reason in the world except that you looked at me across a vault full of other people's kept things and decided I was worth staying for."

Rafael's chest ached. The ache had no picture attached to it, no sound, no face — just warmth and loss fused into a single stone.

"I wish I remembered it," they said quietly. "I'd give — well. I don't have anything left to give. That's rather the point of me now."

"Yes," Wren said. "About that."

She set the book down. She turned to face them fully, cross-legged, and took both of Rafael's hands in hers, and her grip was steady but her breath wasn't.

"What I'm about to do," she said, "is forbidden. Not frowned-upon forbidden. *Cast-out-with-your-name-struck-from-the-rolls* forbidden. Keepers may record, keepers may preserve, keepers may read aloud — but a keeper may never give a memory directly into another mind. Memory has to be earned or remembered or it isn't yours. That's the first law. It's older than my order. It might be older than salt."

"Then don't," Rafael said. "Wren. Whatever it costs—"

"It costs my place in a dying order and I've been planning to quit since chapter twelve," she said, and the ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Listen to me. This is important. I can't give you back what you poured out — those memories belong to the world now, thousands of them, scattered back into every life you touched. Stealing them again would just be stealing twice. And I wouldn't. Even if I could." Her thumbs moved over Rafael's knuckles, slow and deliberate. "But the night on the seventh turn? You never stole that. You never sold it. It was never part of any bargain. It was yours, whole and free, from the moment it happened. Which means it's the one thing I'm allowed to hand back. The only thing."

"How?" Rafael asked. Their voice had gone very small.

"The way lullabies get passed down," Wren said. "By voice. By choice. By staying awake beside someone in the dark until they carry a little of your warmth away without meaning to." She leaned closer. Her eyes were wet and she let them be, which told Rafael more about her courage than the whole descent beneath Thornfell had. "Keepers speak memories into books. I'm going to speak one into you. Hold still. And Rafael — Fairweather, memory-thief, terror of three provinces —"

"Yes?"

"Don't fight it. You're going to want to grab for it. Don't. Just receive. For once in your life, let something be given."

* * *

Later, Rafael would try to describe it and fail.

It was not like remembering. Remembering is opening a door onto a room you already own. This was a voice in the dark, low and warm, telling them a story in second person:

*The Codex has eaten every way out. The walls are rewriting themselves around your choices and the ink smells like drowning and Wren Ashvale is standing three feet away, cataloguing doom, because cataloguing is how she stays brave. You are afraid — properly afraid, down in the marrow — and you notice that she is too, and that she is staying anyway, and that she has been staying anyway since the day she grabbed onto you mid-pull and refused to let you vanish alone.*

*You think: everyone I've ever loved, I loved on loan. Borrowed weddings. Borrowed funerals. Borrowed first kisses lifted out of strangers' heads and worn like coats. And here is this impossible, guilt-ridden, magnificent woman who knows exactly what you are and stayed.*

*"Hey," you say, and she looks up. "If we get out of this—"*

*"We will," she says, because she cannot bear unfinished prophecies.*

*"No. Listen. If we get out of this, I'm not running. I always run. It's the trade. But I'm not running from you. I choose this. I choose to stay."*

*And she goes very still, the way people do when a prophecy comes true in front of them, and says, "Say it again," and you do, and the Codex shrieks somewhere in the dark because there is one thing in the world it cannot write: a choice freely given.*

It ended there. It ended on her face, lit by vault-light and moon-turn, saying *say it again* — and Rafael surfaced back into the fireside night with tears running freely down their face and no idea when they'd started.

Wren was still holding their hands. Still watching. Braced, Rafael realized, for rejection — this woman who had traded her order, her law, her name for them, waiting to find out if the gift had taken root or fallen on empty shelves.

"Say it again," Rafael whispered.

Wren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "That's supposed to be *my* line."

"Please."

She leaned in until their foreheads touched. "Stay," she said.

"I remember," Rafael said, and it wasn't quite true and it was entirely true — the memory was hers now as much as theirs, spoken into them by voice and choice, and it didn't matter whose hands had carried it. What mattered was that it hadn't been stolen. What mattered was that it had been *given*. "I remember enough. And I choose it again. I choose you, Wren Ashvale. Freely. No bargains, no ledgers, no thirteenth turn coming to seal it. Just this."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in her blanket, Marisol pretended very hard to be asleep and smiled at the stars.

Wren kissed them then, soft as ink settling, and when she pulled back she was laughing and crying in equal measure and doing a poor job of hiding either. "You know my order would call this the gravest corruption of keeper's law in four hundred years."

"Good," Rafael said. "I'd hate to be conventional at anything."

They sat together until the fire burned to embers, shoulder to shoulder, her head finding the hollow of their shoulder like it had been measured for it. Rafael held her and marveled at the strangeness of it: they were, by any honest accounting, nearly empty. Every stolen year poured back out. Every borrowed love returned to its owner. And yet sitting here in the ashes of their old trade, holding the one thing they'd never taken and never could —

they felt fuller than they ever had wearing a thousand lives.

Above the ridgeline, the last stars were fading toward a dawn that belonged to no prophecy at all. In two days there would be a town that took in strays, and a girl with a restored soul who needed breakfast, and a life to build out of honest materials.

And in Rafael's pack, wrapped in oilcloth they had no memory of packing, something small and heavy shifted against their hip — and began, very softly, to hum.
