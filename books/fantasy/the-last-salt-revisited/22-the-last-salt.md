## 22. The Last Salt

The mountain road smelled of pine and morning, and for the first time in my life I wasn't looking over my shoulder to see who else was on it.

I had a theory about that. A small, unscientific theory, the kind of thing you develop when your head has been emptied out and slowly, imperfectly refilled by other people's kindness instead of other people's memories. My theory was this: thieves look over their shoulders because they are always leaving something behind. And I had finally stopped leaving things behind. Everything I owned fit in one pack, and none of it was stolen.

Well. Almost everything.

"You're doing the face again," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The face where you're having a thought and you think nobody can tell." She didn't look up from the map she'd been pretending to read for the last mile. We both knew these passes better than any cartographer alive — she'd kept them in prophecy and I'd kept them in escape routes — but Wren liked holding the map anyway. She said it made her feel useful. I suspected it actually made her feel like a person whose usefulness wasn't measured in centuries of accumulated guilt, but I let her have her version. It was kinder.

"I'm thinking about salt," I said.

"Of course you are."

"No, really. All my life, salt's been the thing that locks things down. Salt on memories in the Undervault. Salt in the Codex margins. Salt under my tongue before every job, so nothing could be taken back out of me." I turned a stone over with my boot, out of habit more than curiosity. "And now we're going to burn a book with it, and it's going to mean the opposite thing. Release."

Wren folded the map with the crisp precision of a woman who had once been responsible for the fate of nations and was now mostly responsible for not losing our only map. "Keeper doctrine says salt preserves."

"Keeper doctrine also said sacrificing readers to a book was acceptable practice, so maybe we don't cite keeper doctrine today."

"That," she said, "is why I was exiled."

"That is *one* of the reasons you were exiled. The other is that you're insufferable when you're right, and you were right about everything."

She smiled at the road ahead of us, and I watched her do it, because watching Wren Ashvale smile was a thing I had chosen to do with my time now, freely, with no contract attached. That was the whole revolution, as far as I was concerned. Choosing things.

Behind us, Marisol hummed.

It had become the sound the three of us walked to — that lullaby, worn smooth as river glass. Before the vault, before the thirteenth moon, I had known it without knowing it, a song living in a hollow where my childhood used to be. Now I knew its name. Now I knew my mother's voice singing it. Now I knew that Marisol knew it too, from the inside, from the nine years she'd carried a soul that was never hers and somehow made room for it gently, the way you make room for a stray cat that turns out to be the reason the house feels like a home.

"Rafael." Marisol caught up between us and took my hand, which she did now without asking, because children who have been property learn quickly which adults never treat them as anything else, and she had decided I was safe. I still hadn't recovered from it. I probably never would. Good. "When we get to Hollowmere, will they ask questions?"

"The town takes in strays," I said. "That's the whole entrance requirement."

"But they'll *wonder*. About me. Where I came from."

"They'll wonder," Wren agreed, "and then someone will feed you, and then someone else will decide you're theirs, and by the end of the first week the wondering will be gone and there will just be Marisol, who arrived one autumn and belongs here now. That's how towns like that work. I've read about them."

"You've *read*—"

"I've read *extensively*," Wren said with dignity. "The Undervault archive had eleven volumes on free towns. Nobody ever checked them out but me."

Marisol laughed, and the laugh had a flicker in it still — just occasionally, at the edges, when she got very happy or very tired, something older moving behind her eyes like a fish under ice. The healers in the last village had called it integration. Wren called it two songs learning to share one throat. Marisol herself called it *my business*, with the flat authority of a child who had spent a year as a bargaining chip and would spend no further portion of her life being discussed in the third person.

We crested the rise, and there it was: the flat stone shelf above the falls where the smugglers' cache had been, where I had cracked a vault a year and a lifetime ago and put my hands on a book that had been waiting for me since before I was born.

The cache was empty now. We'd seen to that weeks ago, clearing out the rest of the hoard — coin to the families whose names were in the Steward's ledgers, relics to the temples that had lost them, and one truly hideous jade statue that Marisol insisted on keeping and that now rode on top of my pack like a smug green passenger.

But the Salt Codex itself we had saved for here. For the road. For daylight.

Wren set her pack down and drew it out, and even now, even defeated, even with its prophecies spent and its trap sprung and broken, the book looked wrong in sunlight. Its pages were the color of old bone. Its cover drank the light. Under the thirteenth moon it had seemed inevitable; under the sun it just looked like what it was — a tool, made by frightened people, to hold onto power.

"There were forty-one names left in it," Wren said quietly. "Souls still contracted when the court fell. I checked twice."

"And the court released its ledgers," I said. "Bound by its own law. You told me that yourself, standing on the Stair of Turns, talking fast enough to outrun a collapsing kingdom."

"I did tell you that. I'm telling you again because burning things goes easier with a witness." She knelt at the center of the stone shelf and began arranging kindling with the same crispness she gave maps. "Also because I'm nervous."

"You? Nervous?"

"It's the last page of my order, Rafael. Whatever else the Keepers were — and they were arrogant, and cruel, and willing to spend lives like small change — they were *mine*. Ten years I hunted this book to destroy it. Let me be allowed to hesitate for exactly one minute."

So we let her. Marisol sat cross-legged on the warm stone. I stood at the edge of the shelf where the falls threw up a fine cool mist, and I let myself remember — really remember, with my own reclaimed grief and my own patched-together heart — the night I'd found this book. The ink moving. My own name rising through the salt like a body surfacing.

I had thought, that night, that I was a thief walking into someone else's design. I hadn't been. I'd been a soul walking home by the longest possible route.

"Wren," I said, when her minute was up.

She struck the flint.

Salt burns strange. Not blue, the way the stories say — more a deep gold, shot through with white, and it gives off a smell like the sea remembering itself. The Codex caught slowly, reluctantly, each page fighting the flame the way it had fought every reader it ever consumed. Then the fire found the ledger pages at its heart, the ones with the remaining forty-one names written in contracts older than the court itself, and the book *sighed*.

There's no other word for it. A long exhale, like something setting down an enormous weight, and the smoke went up off the shelf in a column that bent sideways in the wind and scattered over the valley — and I felt it go. Every name. Forty-one souls' worth of held breath, released all at once into a world that had mostly forgotten they existed. Somewhere beneath Thornfell, in the dark and emptying halls of the Undervault, doors were opening that had no keys anymore. Somewhere in ordinary villages, people who had felt a hollowness all their lives without knowing why were going to wake tomorrow feeling oddly, inexplicably lighter.

Marisol stood up and put her hand flat on the warm stone, and for a moment her flicker rose all the way to the surface — the sold soul and the girl who'd housed it, looking together at the smoke. "Goodbye," she said. Not to the book. To whatever part of her had waited a hundred years under the mountain.

Then she sat back down and asked if we had any food, because she was nine, and grief has a schedule that respects dinner.

* * *

We ate hard bread and hard cheese and soft apples, sitting in a triangle around the ashes, because none of us wanted to leave them yet. The wind picked at the grey powder, lifting little ghosts of it off the stone.

"Do you know what I keep thinking about?" I said.

"The jade statue?"

"*Apart* from the statue. I keep thinking about the rule. My rule. Never steal your own memories back. I built my whole life around it — around the idea that some things, once given away, should stay given. And then I spent a year breaking every other law of memory there is, and the funny thing is, the rule held. I never stole myself back. Everyone else handed me the pieces."

"Because you paid for them," Wren said. "With everything you'd stolen. That's the whole of what was taken, returned. You balanced a ledger a century old, Rafael. The rule didn't break. It just turned out to point somewhere larger than you thought."

I turned an apple in my hands. "And you broke the biggest rule of all. Keepers don't give memories by hand."

"Keepers don't," she agreed. "Which is fortunate, since I'm not one anymore. I'm a woman on a mountain with a map and no institution." She hesitated, and I saw her deciding whether to say the next thing, and I loved her a little extra for saying it anyway. "You know it isn't whole. What I gave you. A memory passed mouth to mind loses most of itself — the edges, the weather, the small sounds. You have the shape of that night, not the night."

"I know."

"You'll never remember the exact words you said to me."

"I know that too. But Wren — I remember that I chose. That's the load-bearing wall. The rest is decoration." I reached over and took her hand. Her fingers were still warm from the flint. "You gave me the one memory I couldn't have survived losing, and you gave it away from yourself to do it — because a keeper who speaks a memory loses her copy. I know what that cost. I carry both halves of it now. Yours and mine."

She looked out at the valley for a while. "Eleven volumes on free towns," she said finally, "and not one word about any of this."

"We'll write it ourselves. Volume twelve."

"*The Free Towns, With Practical Notes on Belonging.*"

"Chapter one: how to arrive. Chapter two: how to be arrived at."

Marisol raised her hand. "Chapter three should be about the statue."

"Nobody wants a chapter about the statue, Marisol."

"*I* want a chapter about the statue."

* * *

We came down off the mountain in the gold part of the afternoon, when the light comes slantwise through the pines and makes even a smuggler's road look like a road to somewhere good. Hollowmere showed itself by degrees — first the smoke of chimneys, then a bell tower, then dogs, announcing us to the entire population with the enthusiasm of creatures who have never once considered discretion.

A woman met us at the first gate with flour on her hands and an expression of frank appraisal. She looked at Wren, who stood straight and offered her exile like credentials. She looked at me, who had nothing to offer except empty hands, honestly held. She looked at Marisol, who looked back with the particular chin-lifted defiance of a child daring the world to object to her.

"Well," the woman said. "Supper's at dusk. Don't be late, and don't be strange."

"We're extremely strange," Marisol said.

"Then be strange quietly. There's a spare room over the bakery and a loft over the smithy, and you can fight about it among yourselves."

We didn't fight about it. Marisol took the loft — she said she wanted to sleep near horses, and after a childhood in a vault, I understood her completely. Wren and I took the room over the bakery, which meant waking to the smell of bread, which meant that I, a person who had spent thirty years waking in ditches and safehouses and one memorable season in a smuggler's hold full of contraband pepper, woke every morning believing I had died and been promoted.

I started stealing again within the week.

Not memories. Never again memories. But moments — the way the baker's boy concentrated when he scored his loaves, tongue between his teeth. The way the evening light hit the bell tower at precisely the hour Marisol came down from her loft humming. The way Wren looked when she thought no one was watching her be happy: unguarded, surprised by it, like a woman reading a prophecy that finally, finally turned out kind.

I took those moments and kept them, and gave nothing in return because nothing was owed, and that — that was the trade I'd been circling my whole life without knowing it. Everything I'd ever lifted had cost somebody something. These cost no one. They were free, and abundant, and mine.

On the last evening of autumn, the three of us climbed back up as far as the falls, because Marisol wanted to show Wren the spot where the mist makes rainbows when the sun hits low, and because neither of us adult had a single reason left to say no to her.

The stone shelf was bare. A year of weather had scoured the last ash into the cracks. Nothing marked the place where a book had held a kingdom's worth of souls and my name in salt — except, when I looked closely, a faint pale shimmer in the stone itself, as if the salt had sunk in and stayed.

Marisol crouched and touched it, then licked her finger and touched it to her tongue, which nearly gave Wren a heart attack.

"It tastes like nothing," Marisol reported. "Just rock."

"Good," Wren said fervently. "That's — good. Please never lick mysterious substances again."

"You licked the archivist's inkwell."

"That was *investigative*—"

They argued their way back down the path, Marisol's laughter scattering ahead of them through the trees, and I lingered a moment on the shelf alone.

Below me, the valley was lighting its lamps, one by one, the way valleys do when they have people in them who expect each other home. Somewhere down there was a loft that smelled of hay, a room that smelled of bread, a table set for three at a house that had decided without ceremony or contract that we belonged to it. Somewhere down there, in the emptying dark under Thornfell, the last of the freed souls were finding their ways back to bodies and lives that had learned to live without them — and would learn, now, to live with them again.

I had spent my whole life taking what wasn't mine and calling it survival. Here, at the end of the road, I had finally learned the harder trick: staying. Being taken. Letting a town, a child, a woman with flour-dusted hands at a gate and a keeper with a borrowed memory in her eyes — letting them have me, freely, no bargain attached.

My hands were empty. They had never been fuller.

I turned down the path after the voices I loved, toward the lamplight, toward supper, toward the plain and miraculous work of an honest life —

— and paused, just once, at the bend where the mountain disappears behind the trees, because the wind had come up the valley carrying something on it. Not smoke. Not salt. Just the faintest thread of a lullaby, sung by no one I could see, in a voice like my mother's and like no one's, and it sounded less like goodbye than like *go on*.

So I went on.

Down the mountain. Toward the lamps. Home.
