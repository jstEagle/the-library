## 1. The Smuggler's Almanac

The moon was a nail-paring over the Thornfell passes, and Emrys Winterbourne liked it that way.

They crouched on the shoulder of a ridge the maps called nothing at all—a blank space between two surveyor's marks where the ink of the Crown cartographers simply gave up—and watched the lantern-train of the customs road wind below like a string of sickly yellow beads. Nine riders. Lanterns lit, horses shod loud, moving with the confidence of men who believed every path in these mountains belonged to them.

Emrys let them pass. They always passed. That was the trick of it: not to outrun the law but to be somewhere the law had never thought to look.

Below their perch, half a mile of scree and shadow away, eleven mules stood hobbled in a fold of the hills with their bells muffled in wool. Their cargo—forty bolts of Saltwork silk dyed in colors the Crown's weavers' guild had outlawed forty years ago for reasons no one could remember, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp—shifted and sighed as the beasts dozed.

"You're grinning," said Doss, the caravan-master, from his seat against a boulder. He was a broad man built like something dragged up from a riverbed, and he chewed a pipe he never lit because light carried.

"I'm not."

"Your face does a thing. When you've found a road nobody else knows." He tapped his temple. "Does a thing."

Emrys turned back to the dark. The customs riders were rounding the shoulder of Wolfjaw Tor now, and in three minutes they would reach the narrows where the road bent along the cliff—and there they would stop, because there was a shrine to some forgotten saint wedged into the rock there, and every rider stopped at the shrine. Not out of piety. Out of habit. The habit had been planted thirty years ago by a smuggler whose name Emrys had never learned, who had paid a stonemason to carve the saint's face just slightly too beautiful, just slightly wrong, so that no rider could pass without looking twice. Thirty years of looking twice. Thirty years of two minutes lost at the same bend of road, while the real traffic moved through a dry riverbed two miles north that appeared on no chart because the river had died before anyone thought to draw it.

Emrys knew that riverbed the way other people knew their mother's kitchen. Knew the way the gravel sang underfoot if you stepped wrong, knew which stones were honest and which rolled. Knew the smell of it in wet weather, iron and old stone, and the smell tonight: dust, cold, the faint mineral bite that gave the whole range its name. Thornfell. Even the mountains here tasted faintly of salt, though the sea was four hundred miles west and below the horizon of every living memory.

"Two minutes," Emrys murmured. "Maybe three if the shrine's got frost on it. They hate touching frost."

"And then?"

"Then we move. North fork, single file, mules nose-to-tail. Nobody speaks past the split rock—the acoustics carry down to the shepherd hut." They glanced at Doss. "And nobody looks up. There are eagles nesting on the crag this season and they'll spook the mules."

"Eagles." Doss sucked his teeth. "You know birds now."

"I know everything that lives in these passes. That's the job." It came out sharper than intended, and Doss raised his eyebrows in the dark, and Emrys made themselves soften. "Sorry. Long week."

Doss shrugged, unbothered, the way men get after twenty years of caravans. "You're the best guide I've ever hired and I've never once heard your name spoken whole. Half my clients think you're a boy. Half think you're a girl. One swore you were twins." He grinned around his unlit pipe. "How d'you manage that?"

"Carefully."

It wasn't entirely a joke. Emrys had spent nine years since leaving Maren's cottage building themselves into a shape the world couldn't grip. No surname used twice in the same valley. No face described consistently—hood up in taverns, hood down on the road, a smudge of ash or berry-stain across one cheek depending on what the witnesses needed to misremember. No debts, no lovers, no letters. They paid their mother's-old-debt in Coppersbridge every midwinter through a third party, sent coin to Maren through a fourth, and otherwise left no more mark on the world than wind leaves on water.

The name Winterbourne existed only inside their own skull, and even there they wore it lightly, like a coat that might have to be abandoned in a hurry.

*Unnamed, unhunted,* went the old smuggler's blessing, whispered before every crossing. *Unwritten, unfound.*

The lanterns reached the shrine. Emrys counted. One. Two. Frost glittered on the saint's carved shoulders; the lead rider dismounted with a curse audible even at this distance and began scraping it off with his glove, because a frosted saint was bad luck, everyone knew that, though why a saint would want frost scraped off him by a customs man no one had ever asked.

Three minutes and eleven seconds. Emrys felt the grin happen again and let it.

"All right," they said, rising. "Wake the mules. We're ghosts tonight."

* * *

The dead riverbed took them north through the spine of the mountains for six hours, and it was, Emrys thought more than once, the finest road in the world precisely because it did not exist.

They walked at the head of the train with a shuttered hand-lamp burning a sliver of blue light, reading the ground. Here the gravel was packed firm—good footing, keep the pace. Here a fan of finer silt marked where spring floodwater still occasionally remembered its old job—walk the high edge. Here, at the place where the canyon walls pinched together and overhead the stars were cut to a ragged ribbon, a slab had fallen sometime in the last year, and Emrys paused, listened to the muffled jingle of eleven mules behind them, and hated the slab with a personal intensity.

"We go over?" Doss whispered.

"We go under. There's a gap at the base, left side. Big enough for the mules if we unload the tall crates and roll them through separate."

Which took an hour, in the dark, in the cold, with a mule named Buttercup expressing her opinions about the entire enterprise at a volume Emrys found frankly impressive. But the gap was there, because Emrys had found it two years ago and memorized it, and the train passed under the fallen mountain one beast at a time, and on the far side the canyon opened into a high hanging valley where the stars came back all at once like a struck match.

They camped at dawn in a hollow ringed with juniper, and Emrys walked the perimeter out of habit, checking sightlines that hadn't changed in years, and then sat down among the mules and let the tiredness arrive all at once, like a debt collector.

The client was waiting at the far end. They always were.

His name this time was Master Corvane, a wool merchant out of Greyharbour with soft hands and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they touched, including people. He met them at the mouth of a defile near the northern watershed with two wagons, six guards who pretended to be drovers, and the particular smile of a man about to pay less than he'd promised.

"Winterbourne." He pronounced it carefully, having extracted it weeks ago at great effort, and wore it now like a borrowed coat he suspected didn't fit. "Eleven mules. You said twelve."

"One ate bad vetch at the staging ground. She'll live. Your silk didn't." Emrys swung down from the lead mule and began unbuckling the cargo straps as they talked, because watching hands work made men like Corvane comfortable, and comfortable men bargained worse. "Count it."

He counted, of course. All forty bolts, unwrapped and rewrapped in the grey early light, while his guards stood around looking tough and Emrys stood around looking bored. Then the payment, which was where it always got interesting.

"Coin as agreed," Corvane said, producing a strongbox. "Minus the mule discount, naturally. And—" he gestured, and one of his guards brought forward a wrapped bundle, long and flat and heavier than it looked, judging by the guard's wrists, "—the balance. As discussed."

Emrys took the bundle without enthusiasm. Part-payment in goods was common enough; coin was traceable, goods were not. Usually it was spice, or good boots, or once a truly magnificent bolt of stolen lace that Emrys had traded within the day. Corvane unwrapped it himself, fussily, revealing a book.

A large book. Leather gone the color of old tea, cracked at the hinges, corners worn round as beach pebbles. The clasps were brass, green with age, worked in a pattern of interlocking waves. Emrys turned it over. No title. No author. Just the wave-tooling, and a small stamped device on the back cover: a circle bisected by a vertical line, with three dots beneath.

"What is it?"

"A curiosity," Corvane said. "Came out of an estate sale in the Salt Marches. Old family effects. The appraiser couldn't read most of it—some cipher, he said, or a private shorthand. Worth nothing to me." He smiled his arithmetic smile. "But a clever person might make something of it. Consider it interest on your patience."

Everything Corvane gave away, he gave away because it profited him somehow, and Emrys turned that fact over while turning the book in their hands. The pages, when they risked a glance at the edges, were dense. Handwritten throughout. Columns of figures, tables, marginal notes in at least two different inks. An almanac, maybe. A ledger.

"Salt Marches," Emrys said.

"The estate was. Family originally from further east, I gathered." Corvane waved a soft hand vaguely toward the mountains behind them—which was to say, toward the heart of the range, the blank space on the maps, the territory that every chart for two hundred miles filled with the words *THORNFELL PASSES (UNSETTLED)* in small apologetic italics, as if the mountains themselves had failed to file the proper paperwork. "Old families out that way, they kept odd books. Records. Claims." His eyes flicked up, quick as a fish. "You'd know better than I. You know those hills."

"I know the roads," Emrys said. "Roads don't keep records."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Of course not."

They parted before full sunrise, wagons rolling south toward Greyharbour, mule train dissolving back into the high country, and Emrys rode alone with a strange heavy book strapped behind the saddle and the feeling—not strong, not yet, just a hairline crack in a wall—that they had been given something on purpose. That *interest on your patience* had been a sentence with a second meaning, and the meaning was: *I know something about you.*

They put the feeling away. Feelings like that were weather; you noted them and traveled on.

* * *

That night Emrys camped in a shepherd's bothy above the tree line, one of a dozen such shelters scattered through the passes that belonged to everyone and therefore no one, and built a small fire because the bothy's stone walls hid it and the altitude hid the smoke. Stew. Boots drying. The book on their knees.

By lamplight, it got stranger.

It was an almanac—or had been, once. Dates ran down the margins in a fine clerk's hand, decades of them, each entry paired with tide-tables, market prices, weather auguries. But threaded through the mundane entries ran a second text, written in that cipher the appraiser couldn't read, dense and deliberate, crowding the margins and sometimes running vertically up the page edges as though it couldn't wait its turn. And every few pages, a symbol recurred: that circle bisected by a line, three dots beneath. The same stamp as the back cover. A signature? A seal?

Emrys had broken ciphers before—it was a guide's trade craft, reading trail-marks and toll-marks and the chalk signs smugglers left for each other on gateposts—and this one yielded slowly, stubbornly, the way ice yields to a boot heel. A substitution system, but layered; the same glyph meant different things depending on whether it sat above or below the ruled lines of the page. By midnight they had perhaps one word in twenty. By the second hour past midnight, those words had stopped being random.

*Inheritance. Salt. Door. Turn.*

And names. Names recurring like knots in rope. *Vane.* That one appeared often, always in connection with the word *watch*, or *find*. And others, older, struck through with single clean lines as though crossed off a list: *Ilsa.* *Corwyn.* *Thorn.*

Emrys sat very still in the firelight, and outside the wind moved through the junipers with a sound like pages turning.

The almanac's dates were the thing that finally raised the hair on their arms. Because the entries weren't history. They couldn't be—the handwriting was consistent across a span of years no single life could cover, unless the writer had begun young and never stopped, and the ink shifted, and the hand tightened and loosened, generation after generation, as though the book itself were the constant and the writers merely tenants. Prophecies, then. Or predictions. Entries dated forward, into years not yet come, written in a hand that did not tremble, describing things in the flat confident grammar of record-keeping rather than fortune-telling:

*On the twelfth turn of the dark moon, the salt-marked one will taste power and find it sweet.*

*On the thirteenth turn, the Door opens. What the last salt chooses, endures.*

Emrys turned pages with fingers that had gone careful. Past entries dated decades ago, matching—appallingly—events they half-remembered from Maren's fireside stories: the flood at Coppersbridge, the year the customs house burned, a comet. The book had known. The book had *scheduled* them.

Then they reached the final section, the newest pages, the freshest ink, and the entries stopped being about floods and comets.

*The child flees the witch's cottage in the spring of its ninth year. It will make itself nameless. This will avail it nothing.*

Emrys read it twice. Their own history, nine years ago, rendered in clerk's shorthand: the fight with Maren, the door slamming, the road south with everything they owned in one bag. Written down before it happened. Written down by someone who had been *waiting* for it.

Their hands were not quite steady when they turned to the last written page.

There, alone in the lower corner, in ink darker than the rest, someone had drawn a small circle. Bisected by a vertical line. Three dots beneath.

And inside the circle, a date.

Emrys knew dates the way they knew trails. They knew what season it was, what turn of the moons, how many nights since the last new darkness. They did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you count the drop when you look off a cliff, and the answer assembled itself in their mind with terrible tidiness.

The circled date was today.

Not the season. Not the month. Today—the night of a cold camp in a shepherd's bothy, the night a wool merchant had pressed this book into their hands with a smile full of arithmetic. Somewhere, whoever held the pen, whatever order wrote in circles and threes and words like *salt* and *door* and *last*, had known that on this night the book would be here, in these hands, open to this page.

*This will avail it nothing.*

Emrys closed the book. The clasps clicked shut like a lock, or a manacle, and the fire had burned low, and the wind outside had found a higher note, and for the first time in nine years of being no one, nowhere, unwritten—Emrys Winterbourne lay awake in the dark knowing, with total certainty, that somewhere in the world there was a page with their name on it.

And that someone was holding the pen.
