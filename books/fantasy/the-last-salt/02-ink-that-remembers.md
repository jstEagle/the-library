## 2. Ink That Remembers

Maren's cottage sat at the crook of the Latchwater, where the stream bent like a finger beckoning travelers onward and then forgetting them. Emrys had walked the path a hundred times as a child—over the plank bridge with its missing rail, past the bee-boxes that hummed even in winter—and they knew every stone of it the way you know the back of your own hand: intimately, and without ever choosing to.

They had not chosen to come back now, either. Not really. Their feet had decided somewhere past the second milestone out of Harrowgate, while their mind was still arguing that the book was nothing, that old merchants pressed strange gifts on their guides all the time, that a circled date in a century-old almanac meant a market day or a hanging or someone's birthday and nothing more.

The argument had lost. Their feet had won.

The cottage looked smaller than it used to. Its turf roof had gone shaggy and silvered with early frost; smoke curled from the chimney in a thin gray thread, unhurried, as if Maren herself were exhaling. A row of dried herbs hung under the eaves—yarrow, wormwood, something darker that Emrys didn't recognize and suspected was meant not to be recognized. The garden beds lay fallow under their straw blankets, but the fence posts still wore the little knotted charms Maren tied every spring, frayed now, half-swallowed by ivy.

Emrys stood at the gate for a long moment, the book heavy against their ribs where it rode inside their coat.

Three years. Three years since the shouting, since Maren had said *you'll come crawling back when the world chews you up*, and Emrys had said things no apprentice should say to the woman who'd taught them letters, herb-lore, and how to walk through a room without being remembered. Since Emrys had walked out with a bindle and a grudge and a hunger for roads that didn't have her voice at the end of them.

The world had chewed, some. It hadn't swallowed. But the book—

The book weighed more than paper should.

They unlatched the gate. It creaked exactly as it always had, and something in their chest cracked along the same old seam.

* * *

Maren opened the door before they knocked.

She'd gone grayer at the temples, and there were new lines around her mouth, but her eyes were the same: quick, brown, unimpressed. She looked at Emrys standing on her step with frost in their hair and three years of silence between them, and she said, "You look half-starved."

"Hello to you too."

"I said what I meant." But her gaze flicked over their shoulder to the empty road, then up to the tree line, the way an old soldier checks a horizon, and Emrys understood she'd been watching the path all morning. Maybe longer. "Well. Are you coming in, or are we going to have our reunion in the cold like a pair of gulls fighting over a fish head?"

The cottage smelled the way it always had—smoke and tallow and bitter herbs, with an undertone of wet wool. The same crooked shelves. The same kettle on its iron hook. The same cat, or a cat's identical descendant, regarding Emrys from the windowsill with yellow suspicion. Everything unchanged, which made the changed thing—the absence of three years, sitting between them like a third guest—all the sharper.

Emrys set the book on the table without ceremony, because ceremony would have required words, and they hadn't found any yet.

Maren poured tea first. That was her way: never let a crisis arrive on an empty stomach. She moved about her kitchen with the deliberate calm of someone buying time, and Emrys let her have it, because their hands weren't steady enough to be seen doing anything useful.

Only when the cups were down and steam rose between them did she touch the book.

She touched it the way you'd touch a sleeping snake. Two fingers, light, at the corner—as if proximity alone might wake it.

"Where did you get this?"

"A client. Part payment on the pass run. He called it junk." Emrys turned their cup but didn't drink. "Old ledger-style binding, water-stained. He swore it wasn't stolen."

"He would swear that either way. Thieves always do." Her fingers hovered above the cover. "Did he tell you where it came from?"

"Said it came out of an estate sale south of the Saltworks. An old clerk's house. Why? What is it?"

Maren didn't answer. She opened the cover.

Emrys had spent one night with the book—had read perhaps a tenth of it by candlelight, enough to find tonight's date circled in faded brown ink and feel the floor tilt under them—but watching Maren read it was different. Watching Maren read it was like watching someone walk into a house where they know everyone has been murdered.

Her lips moved slightly. Her thumb traced the margin notes, those small, cramped annotations in a hand too precise to be human-hasty, too human to be printed. And then she reached the page where the script changed—where the neat merchant's alphabet gave way to something angular and dense, letters stacked like stones in a wall—and Emrys saw her go pale.

Not flushed-pale. *Drained*-pale. The color left her face from the center outward, like water down a drain, and her hand flattened against the page as if the book might fly open to further pages without permission.

"Maren."

"Where—" Her voice came out wrong. She cleared her throat. "Where did you see your name in this?"

The question landed in Emrys's stomach like a dropped stone.

"I haven't," they said slowly. "It's cipher after the first few pages. I can't read it."

"No," Maren agreed softly. "You couldn't." She turned the book around and pushed it across the table.

There, halfway down the page, in the middle of the dense stacked script, two words stood out—not because they were larger, but because Emrys's eyes went to them the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth, helplessly, again and again:

**Emrys Winterbourne.**

Written in ink older than they were. In a book bought yesterday from a man who didn't know what he was selling.

"That's not possible," Emrys said. Their own voice sounded distant. "That's—I'm twenty-two. This book is a hundred years old if it's a day. Look at the binding. Look at the—"

"I'm looking." Maren's hand had not left the page. "Emrys. Listen to me. This isn't merchant cipher. This isn't smuggler's shorthand, it isn't guild code, it isn't anything your client could've faked for a joke, because I have only ever seen this script in one context, and that context got people hanged." She drew a breath. "This is Royal Saltwork cipher. The administrative hand of the kingdom of Thornfell."

The word landed and rang.

Thornfell. Every child learned the word the way they learned the shape of the mountains—without ever being taught it directly. Thornfell, the drowned kingdom. Thornfell, whose passes Emrys had guided contraband through for five years without once thinking of them as anything but passes. Thornfell, erased from maps and law a century ago, dissolved by treaty, its name struck from the rolls so thoroughly that saying it aloud in a Crown office could get a clerk dismissed. A kingdom that existed now only in the negative space of documents—in clauses like *all former territories north of the Latchwater*, in phrases like *lands of no former sovereignty*, in the careful, embarrassed silences where its name should have been.

"It's a forgery," Emrys said. "Some antiquarian's game."

"With your name in it. Your exact name. Spelled the way you spell it, with the *y*." Maren finally lifted her hand from the page, and Emrys saw that it trembled. "Child, I need you to think clearly, because I am finding that difficult. What else does it say? The parts you could read."

Emrys told her. About the prophecies dressed up as weather entries. About the marginalia. About last night, sitting in a hired room over the Gilded Ewe with the shutters closed, turning pages by candle stub, feeling increasingly like the book was turning them instead. About the circle drawn around today's date, and beneath it, in plain alphabet, four words:

*The salt comes home.*

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a crow complained and was silent.

"The salt comes home," Maren repeated. She laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it whatsoever. "Do you know why they call it that? The Saltwork cipher. The clerks who wrote in this hand were called salters. Salt preserves. Salt corrodes. The royal archivists chose the symbol deliberately—it keeps what it touches and eats away at everything else." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "I taught you your letters, Emrys Winterbourne. I taught you which mushrooms lie and which rivers freeze honest. I thought I was arming you against the world. And the whole time, the world had already written a page with your name on it before you were born."

"You're jumping ahead. One line in an old book doesn't—"

"One line?" Maren stood abruptly, chair legs shrieking on stone. She crossed to the shelf, pulled down a jar, put it back, pulled down another—her hands needing work her mind wasn't giving them. "One hundred years ago the Crown dissolved a living kingdom. Do you know how you dissolve a kingdom? Kingdoms don't die of embarrassment. Somebody wanted Thornfell gone badly enough to erase it from every map, every register, every church record, and then—apparently—to write a prophecy about its return, in secret, in cipher, and hide it in a book that would drift around the border country like a leaf on a stream until it washed up against—" her hand jerked toward Emrys, accusatory, "—against *you*."

"So I follow the current. So what? Books wash ashore everywhere. Coincidence finds a name and—"

"Read the rest of the page."

Emrys looked down. Below their name, the stacked cipher continued for several lines—and then, at the bottom, in that same precise hand, a short phrase in plain letters again, as though the writer had wanted to make sure this part could never be misread:

*Let none stand between the heir and the Door.*

"What door?" Emrys asked.

"I don't know." Maren sat back down, heavily, all the fury gone out of her posture and something worse in its place. Weariness. Old fear. "But I know what happens to people who stand between powerful men and the things those men want. I've buried friends for less." She wrapped both hands around her teacup as if it were warm, though it had long gone cold. "Who else knows you have this?"

"The client. Maybe his factor."

"Names."

"Harrowgate's not big on names. He goes by Master Ellery." Emrys hesitated. "There was a woman at the inn asking after him. Grey cloak. Asked the stable boy questions."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, the decision had already been made behind them, and it frightened Emrys more than anything in the book.

"Then you leave tonight," she said.

* * *

They argued for an hour.

Maren laid it out with terrible practicality: pack light, take the northern track, avoid Harrowgate entirely, cut east before the ford, keep off the high ground where riders could silhouette you against the sky. She talked about sightlines and informants and the Crown's reward schedules with the fluency of someone who had done this before—for others, at least. Emrys recognized the lesson underneath the lesson. Maren had been hiding people since before Emrys was born. Hedge-witches always were. The ones who survived, anyway.

"And go where?" Emrys said at last. "North is salt-flat and customs houses. East is the Marquisate, which extradites. West is sea. You want me to run blind, on the word of one line of ink, for the rest of my life. I've lived unnamed for five years, Maren. I got *good* at it. But I can't outrun my own name written in a hundred-year-old book. There's nowhere to run *to*. Whatever this is, it already knows me better than I know myself."

"Then let it chase a ghost. Change your name. Burn the book—"

"Burn it?" Emrys pulled it across the table and held it against their chest, and was surprised by their own ferocity. "It knows things. Whatever game this is—prophecy, trap, joke, curse—it's a game with rules, and this book is the rulebook. You want me to flee into the mountains carrying nothing but my ignorance? They'd run me down in a week, and I'd die not knowing why." They heard their own voice harden into something they recognized from the passes, from negotiations with armed clients: flat, stubborn, immovable. "I don't run blind. I've never run blind. Blind runners break their necks in the first ravine."

Maren studied them across the table for a long moment. The cat jumped down from the sill, stretched, and left the room, as if it wanted no part of what came next.

"You sound like your mother," she said quietly.

"Don't."

"She had the same look. Same jaw." Maren sighed, and the sigh seemed to age her ten years in the space of a breath. "Fine. Then we do this properly. If you won't run, you'll at least understand what's hunting you before it arrives. Give me the book until morning. There are texts in my keeping that might decipher the cipher—old correspondences, a salter's primer I've had cause to hang onto. If we can read what it says about you—about the heir, the Door, the thirteen turns it keeps muttering about—then you can choose your ground. Fight smart instead of blind."

"Thirteen turns?" Emrys hadn't mentioned any turns. "What thirteen turns?"

Something crossed Maren's face—there and gone, fast, but Emrys had spent years reading faces across campfires and card tables, and they caught it. Not surprise. Recognition. As if a piece she'd been dreading had clicked into place.

"Give me the book," she said. "And bar the door behind you when you go up to the loft. And Emrys—" she paused, hand resting on the cover, "—whatever you hear in the night, however the wind sounds, don't come down until dawn. Some hours are for working, and some hours are for being afraid, and it's best not to mix them."

* * *

The loft was low and smelled of stored apples and dust. Through the gap under the eaves, Emrys could see the first stars coming out over the Latchwater valley, sharp and cold as pinpricks in black cloth.

Below, lamplight moved. Pages turned. Once, very late, Emrys heard Maren speaking—too low to catch the words, in a rhythm that wasn't quite prayer and wasn't quite conversation, the way hedge-folk talked to things that weren't people.

Sleep wouldn't come. Emrys lay on the pallet with the blanket to their chin and replayed the day backward: the client's oily smile as he counted out coin and slid the book across like an afterthought. *Thought you might like it—old junk, but it felt like yours somehow.* The woman in the grey cloak at the inn, standing too still among the crowd. The circle of brown ink around a date that had been fixed before Emrys drew breath.

*The salt comes home.*

Home. Emrys had never had one, not really—orphan raised loose in a hedge-witch's kitchen, then gone, then roads. Unless home was a drowned kingdom they'd never seen, waiting at the end of a prophecy written by strangers. That wasn't home. That was a hook, baited with belonging, and Emrys had gutted enough fish to know a hook.

And yet.

And yet their name was in the book. Written in ink older than their grandmother. Whoever had written it had known—not guessed, *known*—and had waited a century for the page to find its way here. That took patience. That took power. And both patience and power, in Emrys's experience, were always aimed at someone.

Somewhere below, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps crossing the main room toward the door—not Maren's tread, which Emrys knew, slow and deliberate as a pendulum. These steps were quicker. Lighter.

Emrys went cold from the scalp down.

They eased to the loft's edge and peered through the gap in the boards.

Lamplight still burned below. At the open door of the cottage, framed against the dark, stood a figure in a grey cloak, and even at this distance, even upside down and through a finger-width of shadow, Emrys knew the stillness of that stance. The woman from the inn. She held something in one hand—a folded paper, a warrant, a list—and as Maren stepped into the doorway to meet her, the wind shifted and carried a fragment of the exchange up through the eaves.

"—asking after a child marked with salt," the grey figure said. "The town records show a hedge-witch licensed in this parish. Licensed women remember things. Names. Births."

Emrys's heart slammed against their ribs.

Below, Maren's voice came up calm as still water. "I remember lots of children. Most of them grow up and forget me. That's the arrangement."

"This one wouldn't have forgotten. Not this one." A pause. Paper rustled. "We're prepared to pay well for recollections, mistress. Or to punish lapses in them. The choice of memory's price is, of course, yours."

Emrys lay flat in the dark above them, barely breathing, the book's words burning behind their eyes—*let none stand between the heir and the Door*—and understood, with total clarity, that the running had already started whether they chose it or not, and that the only question left was whether they ran ahead of the truth or behind it.

Dawn was four hours away. Maren had said not to come down.

Emrys started counting minutes instead.
