## 3. The Grey Inquisitor

The grey man came into the valley on a horse too fine for the road, and the road knew it.

Emrys watched him from the ridge above the mill, where they'd stopped to water the mule, and felt the wrongness of him before they could name it. It was the way the mist behaved. Valley fog in autumn moved like sheep—slow, grazing, following the contours of the land it had known for ten thousand years. But around this rider the mist drew back, parted, made room, as if the air itself had been shown papers and found them in order.

His coat was the grey of slate before rain. His saddlebags were the grey of ash after. He rode without outriders, without banners, without hurry, and that was worst of all. Soldiers hurried. Soldiers were afraid of being late, of being caught, of being blamed. This man rode as though the world would wait for him, because it always had.

"Vane," Emrys said aloud, testing the name they'd heard whispered at the last three way-stations, and the word sat strangely on their tongue, like a stone fished out of cold water.

*Inquisitor Halvard Vane. Asking after a salt-marked child.*

They hadn't stayed to hear more than that. They'd left their half-poured ale sweating on the table of the Drowned Ox and gone out through the kitchen, past the scullery girl who owed Maren a favor, and taken the high path north with their heart going like a drum in a parade.

Now, watching from the ridge as the rider descended toward the town, Emrys did the arithmetic they were good at—the smuggler's arithmetic of distance and time and risk.

Maren's cottage was an hour's walk from town, up under the alder holt where the spring came out of the hillside. An hour behind them, maybe less for a fresh horse. And Maren would not run. That was the thing that turned Emrys's stomach even as they turned the mule back down the track. Maren had not run from anything in sixty years—not the fever that took her husband, not the bailiffs who came for her herbs during the Purist years, not the slow collapse of her own eyesight. She would sit in her chair by the window with the prophecy book open on her knees, if Emrys had been fool enough to leave it there.

Emrys checked. They'd carried it out of her cottage themselves two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth inside their coat, because something in Maren's voice when she read her own name in the margins—

No. She hadn't read her name. She'd read *theirs*. Written in cipher older than the Crown, in a book paid to them by a client who'd never once looked them in the eye.

*The last salt shall come when the salt is spent,* the page had said, and beneath it, in a different hand but the same cipher: *and his name will be neither his father's nor his mother's, for he will refuse both.*

Neither his father's nor his mother's. A name refused. A child who chose what to be called, or chose nothing at all.

Emrys had laughed when they first saw it—a short, ugly laugh in Maren's dark kitchen. Then Maren had gone white as whey and said, *Child, there are people who have waited a hundred years to make that sentence true,* and Emrys had stopped laughing.

Now the grey man was in the valley, asking innkeepers about a salt-marked child, and Emrys rode down through the alders with the book against their ribs like a second heartbeat, telling themselves they were going to warn Maren, only to warn her, only to get her on the road south before—

The smoke told them they were too late before the bend in the track let them see the cottage.

It stood black against the green of the holt, and the fire had already eaten its way through the thatch, and the flames were the wrong color. Not orange, not gold. They burned a pale, chemical blue-white, like salt thrown on a spirit-lamp, and they made no heat that Emrys could feel at forty paces. This was not a cooking fire escaped its hearth. This was fire with paperwork behind it.

And in the yard, patient as a man waiting for bread to prove, stood Inquisitor Halvard Vane.

* * *

Halvard Vane had burned eleven cottages in his career, and he remembered every one.

Not the people—he made a point of not learning names he intended to orphan or widow; sentiment was a debt the job could not afford. But the houses, yes. The house at Marlow Ford with the carved door lintel and the child's chalk drawings still on the pantry wall. The longhouse above Tarn Beck whose owner had built the chimney himself, brick by brick, over thirty winters, and whose wife had screamed at Vane not as they burned it but while she packed her baskets, screaming not words but pure animal grief. The croft outside Dunmarrow where the hedge-witch had hidden contraband scripture inside a hollowed-out psalter, which Vane had considered, briefly, almost clever.

He kept the list because forgetting was how men like him became monsters, and Vane had no interest in becoming a monster. Monsters enjoyed their work. Vane merely completed it.

This one, the twelfth, belonged to a woman named Maren Ostrey, aged somewhere between sixty and the grave, registered with the Guild of Hedgewrights in the year Vane had been born, cited twice for unlicensed physicking and once—this was the relevant entry—for possession of pre-Dissolution texts. The informant's deposition ran to four pages and included a description of a visitor: young, slight, road-worn, carrying a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and marked.

Salt-marked.

Vane had heard the phrase perhaps six times in nineteen years of service. It was old vocabulary. It came from documents so far up the chain of custody that they didn't technically exist, and the first five times he'd heard it, the trail had ended in nothing: a dead child in a plague year, a runaway drowned in a canal, a case closed for want of evidence and reopened, quietly, by men in a room Vane had entered once and never been invited to again.

But this time there was a book. The informant swore to it. An old book, bought and sold along the smuggling roads, and the hedgewitch had opened it and read from it and gone the color of tallow, and then she'd sent the boy away north and stayed behind alone in her cottage with her herb racks and her bees, as if staying were some kind of answer.

Vane dismounted in her yard and knocked on her door, because procedure mattered, and because he wanted, just once, one of them to tell him plainly what he was hunting.

She opened the door herself. Small woman, straight-backed, eyes clouded but aimed at him with terrible precision, like two fingers pointed across a table.

"You're the grey man," she said. "I can smell you. You smell like ledgers."

"I'm here regarding certain documents," Vane said. "Texts predating the Dissolution of Thornfell. You're harboring them."

"Harboring." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was not unkind either. It was the smile of someone who has finally met a person she'd been expecting all her life and finds him smaller than advertised. "That's a shepherd's word, isn't it. Harboring. Like I've got a lamb in the kitchen."

"Do you?"

"No." She stepped aside, deliberately, letting him see the clean-swept hearth, the bare table, the empty shelves where the herb bundles had left their pale ghosts. "You'll find nothing, Master Inquisitor. I gave away everything worth taking days ago. To someone worth giving it to."

"And where is that person now?"

"Gone," said Maren Ostrey, "where your ledgers don't reach. Which is further than you think, and nearer than you'd like."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. He thought about the list. Twelve houses. He thought about the men upstairs, the ones with the non-existent documents, who had taught him, in his first year, the single most important lesson of his service: *the law is a story we keep accurate.* Accurate, not true. Accuracy was achievable. Truth was for priests.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that I'm required to burn it regardless."

"I know," she said. "I built it knowing you'd come. That's why I built it small. Easier to carry the memory of a small house." She looked past him, at the alders, at the light coming down gold through them, and her voice went softer, almost fond. "Tell me one thing before you do your work. This child you're looking for. When you find them—what happens?"

"That depends on what they choose."

"No," said Maren. "It doesn't. That's the whole point of prophecies, Master Inquisitor. Nobody chooses anything. That's what makes them prophecies instead of plans." She folded her hands. "Well. Burn it, then. And mind the bees—they'll be angry, poor things. Smoke them first if you can. There's no sense making them suffer for my opinions."

* * *

Emrys lay flat in the bracken on the holt's edge, forty paces from a burning house, and could not move.

Not couldn't. Wouldn't. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole shape of their life. Emrys had spent seventeen years learning to be smoke—to move through cracks, to leave no print, to owe no face to any ledger anywhere. Every instinct they owned was screaming the same lesson now: *stay low, stay nothing, survive.* The book was safe against their ribs. Maren had given it to them precisely so they could run.

But Maren was walking out of her own front door at her own pace, carrying nothing but her stick and a covered basket, and Vane was holding the door for her like a servant, and Emrys lay in the bracken and discovered that smoke could break.

The blue-white fire climbed through the thatch. The bees were screaming—or that was Emrys, or that was the wind through burnt alder leaves—and Maren came down the garden path and stopped at the gate and looked back at her house with her head tipped, listening to it die.

Then she did the worst thing. She turned, slowly, scanning the treeline, the bracken, the hollows—her blind eyes moving across the landscape like a hand feeling for a pulse—and she raised her basket, and set it down on the gatepost, and left it there.

For Emrys. Of course. Even now. Even with the sky raining cinders, she was provisioning a fugitive.

Emrys pressed their face into the wet bracken and breathed until breathing was possible again, and when they lifted their head, grief had changed its chemistry. It had gone from a soft thing, a wound-thing, to something with edges. Something you could hold by the blade.

*I gave away everything worth taking days ago. To someone worth giving it to.*

Maren had known Vane was coming. Had known, and stayed anyway—not to protect the book, which was already gone, but to stand in her own doorway and waste the Inquisitor's hour and send him up the mountain empty-handed and angry. She had stayed to buy time. Her life for a head start, traded as calmly as a woman paying tolls.

Emrys would not spend that coin cheaply.

Below, Vane was speaking to one of his men—when had the others appeared? Two more riders, grey as their master, materializing from the mist the way authority always does—and then the Inquisitor mounted, and paused, and turned his head. Just slightly. Toward the bracken. Toward the holt.

He couldn't see Emrys. No one could see Emrys; that was the entire art of them. But for one long moment the grey man's gaze rested on the hillside like a hand on a shoulder, and Emrys understood, with total clarity, that he knew. Not where. Not yet. But that the valley held what he sought, and that the book was out there, and that the hunt had a name now whether Emrys ever spoke theirs or not.

Then Vane turned his horse north, and his riders fell in behind him, and the mist closed over them like water over a dropped knife.

* * *

In the loft of the Drowned Ox, by the light of a shuttered lamp, Halvard Vane wrote his report.

*Twelfth property destroyed per Directive 9. Subject Ostrey, M., declined evacuation assistance (note: unusual). No contraband recovered. Informant's account corroborated in part: subject confirmed prior possession of pre-Dissolution text and confirmed transferring same to unidentified party, direction of travel north.*

He paused, pen hovering.

There was more to write. The part that wouldn't fit any form. That the old woman had asked him a question no subject had ever asked: what happens when you find them? And that he had answered honestly, *it depends on what they choose*, and that she had looked at him—blind, condemned, composed—with something that was not pity and was worse than contempt, and said:

*Then you don't know whose side you're on yet, Master Inquisitor. When you find out, I hope it keeps you warm.*

Vane had served the Ledger's interests under the Crown's seal for nineteen years, and he had never once written the word *Ledger* in any report, and he did not write it now. Instead he finished the entry, blotted it, sealed it, and sat for a while in the lamplight listening to the inn below—the ordinary sounds of ordinary people drinking, arguing, laughing, alive—and felt the question settle into him the way grit settles into a boot: small, ignorable, and guaranteed to raise a blister eventually.

What happened when he found them?

Upstairs, the informant's deposition lay in his satchel, and beneath it, wrapped in black oilcloth, the object the Ledger had couriered to him at Marlow Ford with instructions he was not permitted to copy down: a plain iron box, locked, keyless, warm. Inside—so his orders claimed—was a means of identifying the salt-marked. He had not opened it. Nineteen years of discipline, and he had not opened it, because some part of him understood that opening it was a door, and doors, once opened, do not close again.

Tomorrow they rode north. The smuggler's roads forked at the river country, and Vane knew the forks, and he knew that whatever moved along those roads would move at night, alone, avoiding the toll-stations and the muster-lists and every other net a lawful kingdom strings for its lawful fish.

A child who refused both names. Who lived outside every ledger Vane had ever kept.

Vane snuffed the lamp and lay down in the dark with the iron box on the floor beside his bed, warm as a sleeping animal, and did not sleep.

North, then. Into the country the maps forgot.

And in the bracken above a dying cottage, Emrys took Maren's basket off the gatepost, shouldered it, and walked into the trees—north, the only direction left—while behind them the blue-white fire settled into embers, and the bees, smoked mercifully senseless, slept on in their scorched hive, dreaming of flowers that no longer existed.

In the basket, under a cloth, beside bread and a skin of water and a twist of dried lavender, lay a scrap of paper in Maren's crabbed hand. Four words, no more:

*Find the ferryman. Trust him.*

And beneath that, smaller, as if added later, hastily, with the pen shaking:

*Eleven turns of the moon, love. Count them.*
