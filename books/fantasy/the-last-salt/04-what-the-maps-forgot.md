## 4. What the Maps Forgot

Emrys walked north for three days without letting themselves think.

Thinking was dangerous. Thinking meant Maren's face, the way she'd looked at the door as the first stones came through the window—*run, child, and don't look back*—and the orange bloom of the cottage burning behind them on the ridge road like a second, spiteful sunrise. Thinking meant the book in their pack, heavy as a sin, whispering against their spine with every step.

So they didn't think. They walked. They ate hard bread and harder cheese, drank from streams that tasted of iron and snowmelt, slept in ditches and under rock overhangs with one hand always on the knife. They moved the way Maren had taught them years ago, when they'd been a runaway apprentice with nothing but bruised ribs and a name they were already trying to shed: *Be weather, Emrys. Be rain, be fog, be nothing anyone remembers by morning.*

They had gotten very good at being weather. Now the weather was running out of places to go.

On the third evening the valley narrowed into a gorge where the map in their head said there should be a toll road, a checkpoint, at least a milestone. There was nothing. The gorge walls rose sheer on either side, grey stone streaked white with old salt blooms, and the path—if it could be called that—followed a stream down into deeper and deeper shadow.

It was the light that made Emrys stop. Or rather, the absence of it. The sun had gone down behind the western peaks twenty minutes ago, and yet ahead, around the next bend of the gorge, there was a soft gold glow, steady and domestic, like lamplight in windows.

No village sat here. Emrys knew every smuggler's route from the Thornfell passes to the coast, knew which valleys held farms and which held wolves and which held nothing but the wind's opinion of itself. This gorge was empty. It had always been empty. It was empty on every map they'd ever memorized, and Emrys had memorized maps the way other children memorized prayers.

The pack shifted against their back, and the book seemed to press closer, almost eager.

"Fine," Emrys muttered to no one. "You win."

They dug it out right there, kneeling on wet stone in the failing light, and turned to the pages Maren had marked before everything went to ash. The Royal Saltwork cipher still swam half-legible across the vellum, but the margins—ah, the margins. Someone, long ago, had written in a plain, hurried hand, the ink browned with age:

*North past the Grey Stair. Where the gorge forgets its own name. The village that pays no tax and owes no crown. Ask for the ferryman.*

Below it, smaller, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the page:

*They are still there. They never left. We made sure no one would ever look.*

*We*, again. The same we that circled dates. The same we that had written Emrys's own name into prophecy like an entry in a ledger.

Emrys closed the book, stood, and walked toward the impossible light.

* * *

The village appeared all at once, the way things appear in dreams—one moment gorge wall, the next a cluster of low stone houses with slate roofs furred green with moss, chimneys smoking, windows glowing. A lane ran between them, cobbled with stones worn round as river eggs. Laundry hung on lines. A dog lifted its head from a doorstep, considered Emrys with ancient amber eyes, and did not bark.

That was the first wrongness, though it took Emrys a moment to name it. Dogs barked at strangers. That was what dogs were *for*. This one simply watched, then lowered its head again with something that looked terribly like pity.

The second wrongness was the people. They were doing ordinary things—carrying firewood, drawing water from a well, sweeping a step—and they were doing them in clothes from a hundred years ago. Not costume-old. *Old*-old: high collars gone soft with wear, a cut of coat that hadn't been fashionable since before Emrys's grandmother's grandmother, colors faded not by fashion but by decades of sun and soap. And they moved strangely. Smoothly. Like figures in a music box, like water flowing downhill along paths worn so deep the choosing had gone out of them.

A woman swept her step. She had been sweeping her step, Emrys realized, watching longer than was polite, for a very long time. The broom moved. The dust did not.

"Hello," Emrys said, because silence felt worse.

The woman looked up. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, and when she saw Emrys she went very still—not frightened, exactly. More like someone who has waited so long for news that the news has become frightening.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're *warm*."

"I—" Emrys took a step back. "I'm sorry. I'm looking for a ferryman."

"Everyone is, eventually." The woman set down her broom. It stayed upright, leaning against air. "Down the lane, past the church that isn't there anymore, to the water. He'll see you. He sees everyone who finds us." Her gaze drifted over Emrys's shoulder, toward the south, and her voice dropped. "Did they burn much, this time?"

Emrys's throat tightened. "A cottage. One cottage."

"One is enough." She picked up the broom again—or the broom returned to her hands; it was hard to say which came first. "Go on, then. Warm thing. Go on."

* * *

The church wasn't there anymore. Emrys understood this literally only when they reached the spot: a foundation of pale stones, roofless, walledless, yet somehow still *shaped*, the way a scar shapes skin. Wildflowers grew in rows along aisles that had once been floored. Ghosts knelt in the grass among them—a dozen dim figures, heads bowed, mouths moving in a liturgy no living ear had heard in a century. Their prayer rose like heat-shimmer.

Beyond the churchless church, the gorge opened onto water.

Emrys had expected a river. This was more than that—a broad, dark lake filling the valley floor, black as poured ink, holding the last grey light of the sky in its middle like a coin at the bottom of a well. And moored at a rickety jetty, a flat-bottomed ferry boat, and leaning against the jetty post, an old man with a face like driftwood and eyes that were, unmistakably, entirely alive.

"You're breathing," the old man said, before Emrys had taken three steps onto the planks. "Good. Saves time." He pushed off the post and straightened, joints crackling like green wood in a fire. "Book in your pack. Salt cipher. Marginalia told you to ask for me."

"How did you—"

"Because you're the fourth warm thing to come down this lane in a hundred years, and the first three had the same book." He held out a hand, calloused and cracked. "Tammas. I keep the ferry."

"Keep the ferry," Emrys repeated. "Where does it go?"

"Nowhere, mostly." Tammas smiled, and the smile was tired in a way that made him seem older than his body, older perhaps than anything else in the valley. "And everywhere, on the nights that matter. Sit. You look like you haven't eaten since the burning."

Emrys flinched. "How do you know about—"

"The dead hear fires, girl or boy or neither-thing, whatever you are. Smoke carries. Grief carries further." He rummaged under the jetty seat and produced bread, cheese, a flask of something that smelled like apples and regret. Emrys's body betrayed them completely; their stomach roared. They ate while Tammas watched the water with the patience of someone who had watched it for a very long time indeed.

When the bread was gone, Emrys wiped their mouth and asked the question that had been sitting in their chest since the sweeping woman.

"What is this place? Why isn't it on any map?"

"Because maps are how the Crown says the world is," Tammas said, "and the Crown says this world ended." He tapped the jetty plank with his boot. "This is Thornfell, or what's left of it above water. The rest is under the lake."

"Thornfell's a story. A drowned kingdom, a fable mothers use to make children mind the water. It's not—it isn't a *place* with a *ferryman*."

"No?" Tammas gestured at the village behind them, the glowing windows, the woman forever sweeping her step. "Tell them."

Emrys turned. In the blue dusk, the ghosts had come out of their houses. Dozens of them now, standing at garden gates and lane's ends, keeping a careful distance—the distance of people who have learned that warmth burns. A child ghost peered around her mother's skirts, and even faded as she was, even translucent as morning mist, her curiosity was so ordinary, so achingly human, that Emrys's chest hurt.

"They're still paying taxes," Tammas said quietly.

"What?"

"Taxes. Tithes. Toll." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "Every year, on the feast day, they bring out the census rolls and the tithe ledgers and they mark themselves down. Names, households, livestock that died a century ago, grain that was never threshed. They pay their dues to a kingdom that no longer exists, to a crown that dissolved itself, because paying is the last thing they have left that looks like living." He spat into the dark water. "To no one. They pay taxes to no one. That's the joke the Ledger wrote, and they don't even know they're the punchline."

"The Ledger," Emrys said. The word came up from the book's margins, from Maren's grey lips. "You know that word."

"I know more than the word." Tammas sat down heavily on the jetty edge, and Emrys sat beside him because standing suddenly felt like too much. Below their boots, the lake breathed against the pilings. Somewhere far out, beneath the black water, something vast and patient waited—the shape of a country drowned.

"A hundred years ago," Tammas said, "Thornfell wasn't a fable. It was a kingdom. Poor, stubborn, salty—our wells ran brackish, our soil grew more stone than wheat, but it was ours. The Crown wanted it. Not the land—there's nothing here worth conquering—but the *treaty rights*, the salt trade concessions, the mountain passes. War was expensive. So the Crown found another way."

"The Ledger."

"Clerks. Just clerks, at first. Men and women with ink-stained fingers who understood that a kingdom can be killed with armies but *erased* with paperwork. They poisoned the wells—slowly, cleverly, so it looked like the land itself was turning against its people. Then they wrote a prophecy. Oh, they dressed it up in mystery, gave it a hundred years of dread, made it sound like fate. But it was a contract, Emrys Winterbourne. A contract with a deadline." He turned those driftwood eyes on them. "Dissolve the kingdom lawfully, by treaty, signed by a lawful heir. No conquest. No crime on any record. Just paper. The cleanest murder ever committed, and the weapon was a book."

Emrys's hands had gone cold around the flask. "The heir. The last salt."

"That's what they called whoever the bloodline thinned down to. The one whose signature would make the erasure legal. Make it *just*." His lip curled. "Justice, written in triplicate. Filed properly. Witnessed by the right clerks."

"And the ghosts? If the kingdom dissolved by treaty, why are they still here?"

Tammas was quiet for a long moment. Out on the lake, mist was gathering, thickening, taking shapes that might have been rooftops, might have been towers, might have been nothing at all.

"A treaty dissolves a kingdom's *law*," he said finally. "Not its people. The dead of Thornfell signed nothing. Nobody asked them. Nobody ever asks the dead." He nodded at the village. "So they remain. Bound to fields that grow phantom wheat, tithing to a crown that's ash, waiting for a Door that opens once a generation—for the heir who was promised to them in that cursed book. Every hundred years, the Door opens. Every hundred years, the last salt is supposed to come home and choose." His voice roughened. "Last time, the heir never came. Died on the road, or was caught by Vane's predecessors, or lost their nerve. Doesn't matter. The dead waited, and the Door shut, and the Ledger filed the delay and adjusted the schedule."

"How do you know all this? You're alive. You're—" Emrys stopped. Looked at him properly. At the way the lantern light passed through the edges of his coat. At the way his shadow fell on the water but not on the planks.

Tammas smiled sadly. "Ferryman's a job title, not a heartbeat. I've been dead sixty years, child. Drowned when the waters rose after the treaty—when the Ledger's engineers broke the dam and flooded the heartland, so no one could ever prove Thornfell had been real. I kept the ferry in life. Seemed rude to stop in death." He shrugged. "Besides. Someone has to be here when the last salt finally comes asking questions."

The night had gone fully dark around them, and the village lights behind seemed very small, and very far away, though they were fifty steps off. Emrys hugged their knees and felt the book press against their spine like a hand between the shoulder blades, pushing them forward down a road someone else had drawn.

"Maren died for this book," they said. "An Inquisitor burned her cottage with her in it, or close enough. For harboring it. And all it is—all it's ever been—is a *contract*. A trap with a ribbon on it."

"Yes."

"And my name is in it."

"Yes."

"So the smart move is to throw it in the lake and let Thornfell stay forgotten."

Tammas didn't answer. Behind them, the ghost-child had crept closer, close enough that Emrys could see her face clearly now: a gap where a tooth had been, a scratch on her cheek, the universal grime of childhood. She was looking at Emrys the way children look at doors.

"She thinks you're the one," Tammas said softly. "They all do. Hundred years they've waited. Another hundred before that, and another before that. They don't know about the Ledger. They don't know their suffering was *filed*. They just know the book promised them a choice, someday, and you walk warm and smell of salt and came out of the north with the book in your pack." He sighed. "So no, child. I don't think you'll throw it in the lake. People rarely do. Hope is heavier than it looks—it sinks straight to the bottom and drags the whole world after it."

Emrys looked out at the mist on the water, where drowned towers might have been, where a whole country lay sleeping under a century of cold black silence. Wonder filled them to the brim—and underneath the wonder, rising slow and cold as the lake itself, something else. Not fear. Anger. The specific, patient anger of someone who has just learned that their whole life—their invisibility, their hunger, their careful unnamed wandering—was a line item in somebody else's accounting.

"Eleven turns," Emrys said. "Maren said the ferryman could read the frame of it. Eleven new moons until the Door opens."

"Twelve," Tammas corrected. "Count tonight. You're already late." He stood, joints cracking, and laid a hand—half-solid, chill as lake-water—on Emrys's shoulder. "And you should know: the book's frame isn't just a date. It's a *summons*. When the count runs short, things start moving. Clerks, riders, men in grey with warrants written by dead authors." He nodded south, toward the gorge, toward the burning memory of a cottage. "Inquisitors don't give up on an asset. Especially not one that just watched their fire from a hayloft and lived."

Emrys went still. "How do you know about the hayloft?"

"The dead hear fires," Tammas repeated gently. "And tonight, child, I heard hooves. A lot of them. Coming up the south road, riding hard through the night, following smoke and rumor straight to the only village that isn't anywhere at all." He turned toward his boat and began untying the mooring line, unhurried, as if the world weren't tilting. "So. Question for the last salt of Thornfell, and I'd answer quick if I were you."

He looked back at them, driftwood face calm, eyes bright as the coin of sky sinking in the lake.

"Do you want to learn what this ferry can really do—or would you rather meet the Grey Inquisitor on solid ground?"
