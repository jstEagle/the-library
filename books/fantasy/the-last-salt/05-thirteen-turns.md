## 5. Thirteen Turns

The ferryman's name was Old Brack, and he smelled of river mud and pipe smoke, and he did not look up from his nets when Emrys came down the bank with the prophecy book clutched to their chest like a wounded bird.

"You'll be the one the maps forgot," he said. "I can always tell. Folk who belong to places walk like they're owed something. You walk like you owe everything."

Emrys stopped three paces from the water. Behind them, up the valley road, the village of Unmarked sat in its hollow of morning fog, its chimneys smoking for no king, its fields plowed by hands that had been dead for a hundred years. They had slept there one night under a roof that cast no shadow, and woken feeling more watched than they had ever felt under Inquisitor eyes.

"I need to cross," Emrys said.

"Everyone needs to cross." Brack drew a net through his knuckles, mending a tear that may not have existed. "Question's what you're carrying across. That book's heavy for its size. I can see it dragging your shoulders down like ballast."

"You know what it is."

"I know what it smells like." The old man spat into the current. "Salt and ink. Worst combination there is. Ink lies, salt remembers, and between them they've drowned better folk than either of us."

* * *

The crossing was not a crossing at all, at first. Brack made Emrys sit on the shingle while he finished his net, which meant an hour of cold wind off the grey water and the sense of being weighed by someone who never once looked directly at them. The river here had no name on any map — but then, no map admitted this stretch existed at all. It ran wide and slow and the color of tarnished pewter, and on its far shore the hills rose dark and folded, hiding roads Emrys's own almanac had never marked.

"Thornfell water," Brack said at last, coiling rope. "Treaty called it the Greyrun. Said it marked the new border. But rivers don't care about treaties, do they. River was here before Thornfell. River will be here after."

"After what?"

Brack looked at them then, properly, for the first time. His eyes were the same pewter as the water.

"Sit down properly, salt-child. This is going to take longer than a crossing."

* * *

He talked while he worked, and the work was endless — nets, mooring lines, the tarred seams of a flat-bottomed boat so old its planks had gone silver. He talked the way old men talk when they have been waiting a long time for someone worth talking to.

"The treaty wasn't a defeat," he said. "That's what nobody understands. There was no war lost, no army broken. Thornfell fell to a signature. The Crown sent clerks, not soldiers — a hundred years back now, my grandmother's grandmother watched them come over these hills with their ledgers chained to their wrists like scripture. And the kingdom signed itself away. Every noble put seal to wax. Every guild. Even the crown itself — Queen Ilsa's own hand, they say, though she wept doing it, if ghosts weep."

"Why would a kingdom sign its own death?"

"Because the alternative was worse, and the clerks made sure everyone knew it." Brack tested a knot and found it wanting. "The wells went bad first. Then the crops. Then the children sickened. A kingdom that cannot feed or water itself is already dead; the treaty just wrote the date on the grave marker. In exchange: safe passage out, pensions, new lands in the south. Most took it. Some didn't believe it could be real — a whole country, erased? Borders redrawn, the name struck from every registry, every map, every church record?" He shook his head. "But ink is patient, salt-child. Ink outlives everything except what it's written about."

"And the ones who stayed."

"Stayed dead or stayed stubborn. Sometimes both." He nodded across the water. "You saw the village."

"They paid taxes," Emrys said. "To no one. I watched them leave coins on a stone altar. The coins were old mint. Thornfell mint."

"Ghosts keep habits longer than the living keep memories. They pay because paying is what they did. The stone takes the coin and the coin sinks into the earth and next spring the fields grow a little greener than they should." Brack shrugged. "Don't ask me how. I only ferry."

Emrys set the book on the shingle between them, finally, like laying down a weapon. "Then tell me what I'm carrying. Maren read enough to be afraid. I want to understand the fear."

* * *

Brack did not touch the book. He leaned over it the way a man leans over deep water, hands clasped behind his back as though even proximity might pull him in.

"Open it to the frame," he said. "Not the prophecies. The structure around them. The part that looks like decoration."

Emrys turned the pages slowly. The cipher-script ran in columns, dense as rain, and around the columns wound borders of interlaced knots and moons and small crowned figures — the kind of ornament a scribe adds when the text itself means nothing to anyone.

"This?"

"There. Count the moons."

Emrys counted. Their breath slowed without their permission. "Thirteen."

"New moons. Look at the spacing. Twelve close together, and then—" 

"The thirteenth stands alone. Set apart. Drawn larger."

"Now count the little doors in the border. Bottom margin."

Emrys's finger traced the gilded edge of the page. Small arched shapes, repeated in the knotwork like stitches. Thirteen again. Twelve shut — each drawn with a tiny line beneath, a threshold crossed, a seam closed. The thirteenth stood open, and within its arch was drawn not a door but an eye, or a keyhole, or both.

"Twelve turns are spoken of in the text," Brack said quietly. "Twelve chances, twelve warnings, twelve names the prophecy tries on before it settles on the right one. But the thirteenth isn't written in the script at all. It's built into the frame. Because whoever wrote this wanted the last turn kept secret even from readers of the cipher. On the thirteenth new moon after the book wakes, the Door opens. Once. For the space of one tide." He finally lowered himself onto the shingle beside Emrys, joints cracking like the boat's old timbers. "And only one person can choose what passes through. The frame says so plainly enough, if you know where the plain words hide. *Only the last salt may open, and only the last salt may choose.*"

"Choose what?"

"What passes through, child. What comes out. Or what goes in. Restoration or unmaking — the Door doesn't care. The Door has no opinion. It's the one honest thing in this whole affair." Brack's voice dropped low. "Everything else in that book was written by men who very much had opinions."

Emrys stared at the thirteenth arch. The little eye-keyhole seemed to look back.

"When does the book wake?"

"It woke when it found you. Books like this don't have dates, they have conditions. Someone paid in that book knowing exactly what they were paying with — or being paid to pass along. Either way, the clock started the night you read your own name." Brack reached out, almost touched the cover, thought better of it. "So. When was that?"

Emrys did the arithmetic in their head and felt it land in their stomach like a stone through ice. "Eleven nights ago. Maybe twelve. I haven't been keeping—"

"Count," Brack said. "From tonight forward, you count. If eleven turns have passed since the waking, you have two left. If ten, you have three. But I'd wager my boat it was eleven, because things like this run tight. They always run tight." He rose, groaning, and began untying the mooring line. "Which means you'd best get on my boat, salt-child, because whatever you decide to do with your choice, you'll need the far side of this river to do it."

* * *

The crossing was silent except for the water. Brack poled them out into the pewter current, and the far hills came nearer with agonizing patience, and Emrys held the book against their chest and tried to feel nothing, which had been their finest skill for nineteen years, and failed utterly.

Two turns. Perhaps three. A lifetime spent staying unnamed, unmapped, unwritten — and now a book had written them, a mark might be finding its way toward their skin even now, and somewhere behind them a man in grey was asking innkeepers questions with a voice like a filing cabinet closing.

"Do you know what's on the other side?" Emrys asked. "Of the Door?"

"No one living knows. No one dead will say." Brack poled steadily. "Some say the kingdom's still in there, whole, the way it was the day the treaty sealed it. Wells sweet, harvests tall, all the people who wouldn't leave still walking their streets. Some say it's empty as a drummed well. Some say it's the Ledger's warehouse, and everything Thornfell ever was is stacked in crates with price tags on it." He smiled without humor. "Me, I think it doesn't matter. I think what matters is who's standing in front of it when it opens, and what they've become by then. Crowns change people, salt-child. Even crowns you refuse. Even crowns you carry in a bag for thirteen turns."

"I don't want a crown."

"Nobody wants a crown. That's why they give it to the ones who don't." The pole bit deep. "Doesn't mean you won't take it. Doesn't mean taking it would even be wrong. That's the trap inside the trap — the book doesn't offer you evil. It offers you justice, with interest."

The far shore slid close. Reeds, mud, a path climbing into the dark folds of the hills. Emrys stepped off onto soil that no law claimed, and their boots sank half an inch, and the ground held them.

"Old Brack." They turned back to the boat. "Why help me? Whose side are you on?"

The ferryman rested on his pole and considered them across three feet of grey water.

"I was born on this bank," he said. "My family stayed when the rest signed. We ferried the ones who fled, and we ferried the ones who came back to look, and for a hundred years we ferried nothing at all, just kept the boat tarred and ready, because the frame of the prophecy says the Door opens near water, and water keeps its promises even when kingdoms don't." He pushed off from the shore. "I'm not on anyone's side. I'm on the river's side. Go north, salt-child. Follow the high path past the split crag. And count your moons."

* * *

Emrys climbed. The path was narrow and old, older than the treaty, cut into the hillside by feet that predated every kingdom the Crown currently recognized, and they walked it fast with the book banging against their hip and the arithmetic running in their head like blood.

Eleven turns gone. Two remaining. Two new moons between them and a door that would open once and never again, and somewhere on the road behind—

They heard it before they saw it. Hooves. Not one horse — six, seven, riding hard, coming up the valley road along the south bank of the Greyrun, and Emrys flattened themselves against the split crag Brack had named and looked down through a screen of gorse.

Riders in Crown grey, but not Crown livery. Their cloaks bore no sigil at all, which was its own kind of signature. At their head rode a lean figure Emrys knew from a single glimpse through a burning cottage's smoke: Halvard Vane, the Grey Inquisitor, sitting his horse like a man who had never once needed to hurry and was hurrying anyway.

Behind him, roped to a saddle, stumbling, hood thrown back — Sable Quill. Alive. Bruised at the mouth. Still, even at this distance and this angle, somehow smirking, as though captivity were a joke they intended to get the better of eventually.

Emrys's heart did something complicated and painful. They pressed harder into the rock.

The riders drew rein at the water's edge. Vane looked across the Greyrun for a long moment — not searching, Emrys realized, but reading it, the way Maren had read pages. Then he dismounted, crouched, and pressed two fingers into the mud of the far bank. Into the print of Emrys's boot, still fresh, still holding the shape of a foot that belonged to no registered soul.

He looked up. Not at the crag. At the hills beyond it — north, along the high path, exactly where Emrys had been heading.

"He knows," Emrys whispered. The wind took the words. "He knows where the Door sleeps."

Below, Vane gave an order too quiet to carry across the water. The riders turned south, back the way they'd come, unhurried now, and Sable's horse followed, and the hoofbeats faded into the ordinary sounds of the river.

But Vane himself remained on the bank a moment longer, alone, looking north. And then — deliberate, unmistakable — he raised one hand, not in threat, not in salute, but in something like acknowledgment. *I see you. Come find me when you're ready.*

Then he mounted and was gone, and Emrys stood alone on the wrong side of a nameless river with two new moons left, a stolen inheritance in their bag, and the sudden, unwelcome knowledge that the thief they were going to have to rescue was also the thief who knew where all the relics were buried.

The first new moon was four nights away.

Emrys started north at a run.
