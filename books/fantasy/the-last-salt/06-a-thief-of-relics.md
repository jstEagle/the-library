## 6. A Thief of Relics

Sable Quill had three rules, and they had kept him alive and moderately wealthy for eleven years: never steal from the desperate, never sell to a buyer who smiles too much, and never, under any circumstance, learn what the merchandise means.

The third rule was why he'd slept well for a decade while moving crates of Thornfell junk through half the borderlands—salt-glazed crockery, coins stamped with a crown that no living king recognized, little lead tokens shaped like fish. Junk. Pretty junk, old junk, but junk. He sold it to collectors in Greyharbour and antiquarians in Vellum Cross with a straight face and an invented provenance, and if any of it had ever mattered, someone would have come looking for it long ago.

Then a stranger with storm-grey eyes and a stolen almanac's worth of hidden roads walked into his shop in Marrowgate and laid a coin on his counter that made Sable's mouth go dry.

* * *

"I want to know where you got this," Emrys said.

It wasn't a question. Sable appreciated that in a person. Questions could be dodged; statements had to be answered or refused, and refusal told you something too.

"Where I got *what*?" Sable said, because rule two applied even when rule three was already bleeding out on the floor.

Emrys turned the coin over with one finger. It was silver once, black with age now, and stamped with the sigil Sable had seen on a hundred pieces of Thornfell scrap—a circle bisected by a vertical line, like an eye that had been closed. Except this one wasn't dead. When the lamplight caught it at the right angle, the line inside the circle shimmered faintly white, like salt crystals catching sun.

"You know what it is," Emrys said. "I can see you know."

Sable leaned back in his chair and put his boots on the counter, which was a habit designed to make people underestimate him. "Friend, I sell a lot of old metal. If I stopped to know what every piece meant, I'd be a scholar, and scholars starve." He smiled his best smile, the one that had talked two customs officers out of searching his mule cart and one magistrate's wife out of reporting him. "Tell me what you think it is, and I'll tell you whether you're right."

Something flickered across the stranger's face—not annoyance. Recognition. The look of someone who had spent their whole life being underestimated and had learned to see the trick coming.

"You're not going to tell me anything," Emrys said. "But you're going to help me anyway."

"Am I?"

"Yes." They reached into their coat and produced a second object, and this one Sable did not pretend not to react to. A book. Old, swollen with damp, its cover cracked leather gone the color of driftwood. He'd seen books like it exactly twice in his life, and both times he'd sold them within the hour because keeping them made his skin crawl.

"That's—" he started.

"A prophecy book. Royal Saltwork cipher. It has my name in it." Emrys set it down on the counter between them, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping snake. "And it says there are relics. Three of them. And it says—" they paused, and Sable watched the first crack appear in their composure, watched them decide to say it anyway, "—it says whoever gathers them can open a door that hasn't opened in a hundred years. Into Thornfell."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, Marrowgate went about its evening: cart wheels, gull cries, a fishwife shouting prices. Inside, Sable Quill sat with a coin that glowed like a closed eye and a stranger who spoke of doors into drowned kingdoms as though they were discussing ferry schedules.

"How much?" he said finally.

Emrys blinked. "What?"

"For helping you. You clearly need someone who knows the trade. Someone who knows which chapels have false floors and which monasteries keep their reliquaries honest and which lords buy relics without asking questions. That's me. That's been me for eleven years." He swung his boots down and stood, and he saw the stranger tense—he was taller than them, broader, and he made a point of not closing the distance, of keeping his hands loose and visible. "So let's talk terms. Because I'm not doing this for a story about magic doors. Stories don't eat."

For a long moment Emrys just looked at him, and Sable felt examined in a way he hadn't since his mother died—the feeling of being read all the way down to the binding.

"You're frightened," Emrys said.

"I'm a businessman. Fear is overhead."

"You're frightened, and you're going to help me anyway, because that coin has been sitting in your strongbox for six years and you've never once tried to sell it." Emrys tapped the glowing sigil. "You know what it is. You've known for years. Rule three of whatever code you live by—don't learn what the merchandise means—you broke that rule the day you bought this, and you've been waiting ever since for someone to walk through your door and tell you why."

Sable's smile died on his face. It didn't happen often, and he hated how it felt—like a shutter blowing open in a storm.

"Who *are* you?" he asked, and heard how rough his voice came out.

"My name's Emrys." A beat. "Just Emrys, for now. Do we have terms or don't we?"

* * *

They had terms, hammered out over bad wine in the back room while rain ticked against the shutters. Half up front, half on delivery. Sable's name stayed off everything—no ledgers, no notes, nothing written. Emrys paid in coin that was mostly honest and entirely untraceable, which told Sable things about their recent activities that he decided not to ask about.

In exchange, Emrys got the thing Sable had never sold: his map memory. Thirty years of back roads, false floors, bribed sextons, and drunken monks' confessions, organized in his head better than any ledger.

"The circlet," Emrys said, tracing the prophecy's marginalia with a finger. "The Salt Crown's circlet. The book puts it in a chapel. Which chapel?"

"Depends which century you ask." Sable pulled a stub of charcoal toward him and, after a moment's war with himself, drew. "There were eleven salt-chapels along the old Thornfell marches. Crown-funded, before the Dissolution Treaty. Most were stripped when the kingdom went under—anything holy gets looted first, that's just physics. But there's one that never got touched. Chapel of Saint Aldhelm-at-the-Crossing, out past the fen road. Consecrated twice, once to the Crown's saints and once to older things, and the two consecrations argue with each other, so nobody goes there. Not priests, not thieves." He looked up. "Except one thief. Me. Twice. And I never found the crypt, because there isn't one. I checked."

"But?"

"But." Sable turned the charcoal over. "The floor's wrong. Aldhelm's has a checkerboard tile pattern, standard issue, but the tiles near the altar run nine-by-nine instead of eight-by-eight, and the extra row doesn't match the wear pattern. Somebody relaid those tiles. Recently, geologically speaking—within the last hundred years, which around there counts as yesterday. And underneath, when I lay flat and put my ear to the stone, there's a space. Hollow. Maybe two feet deep. Big enough for a box."

"Big enough for a circlet."

"Big enough for a circlet," Sable agreed. "Now here's my professional concern. Chapels like that don't get hollow floors by accident. There'll be a ward. Nothing dramatic—wards that announce themselves aren't wards, they're theater—but something that bites. Are you prepared for that?"

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Rain filled the silence.

"No," they said at last. "But I'm going anyway."

"Honesty. Good. That's rarer than honesty should be." Sable drained his wine and stood. "We leave tomorrow, before dawn. Fen road's passable at low water only, and the tide doesn't care about your prophecy."

* * *

The Chapel of Saint Aldhelm-at-the-Crossing crouched in its marsh like something waiting to be forgotten properly. Sable had always thought of it that way—as unfinished business, a building that hadn't quite gotten permission to fall down. Its walls were grey fieldstone furred with lichen, its roof patched with turf, its single bell long since stolen except for the yoke that held where a bell should be. The fen breathed around it. Reeds hissed. Somewhere a bittern boomed like a dropped plank.

They arrived at dusk, waded the last quarter-mile through thigh-deep water, and stood dripping in the nave while Sable lit two candles he'd brought for the purpose.

"You pray?" Emrys asked.

"I pay," Sable said. "Old habit. You leave a candle and a coin for whatever lives in a place before you take from it. Doesn't matter if anything does. Costs less than finding out." He set a copper on the altar, next to a dozen other coppers left by generations of people who also weren't sure what they were appeasing. Then he knelt at the ninth tile from the altar, third from the wall, and pressed his palms flat against the stone. "Hello again," he murmured. "Still hollow?"

He couldn't hear anything this time—the fen was too loud—but he didn't need to. He knew the floor the way he knew his own teeth.

"The ward first," Emrys said. They had gone strange in the last hour, quieter, watchful, eyes moving over the chapel's shadows like they were reading them. "Before we break anything. Where would it sit?"

"Thresholds and treasures. Doorway and the box itself, if I were setting it." Sable rose, brushed mud from his knees. "Which is why I do thresholds. You do the box. Whatever happens when that tile comes up—it's yours."

Emrys nodded. In the candlelight their face was all angles and patience, and Sable thought, not for the first time since Marrowgate, that he had made either the best deal of his career or the last one.

The threshold ward was a salt-line under the doorstep, laid in a groove worn by centuries of feet—old salt, grey and fused, but still humming faintly against Sable's senses like a plucked wire. Breaking it was delicate work: he ground the line to powder with a pestle borrowed from a very surprised apothecary, mixed it with grave-dirt and lamp oil, and painted the paste across the threshold in the shape of a keyhole. Superstition? Probably. But eleven years of superstition had kept his skin unburnt, so he took his time.

When he finished, the chapel exhaled. He felt it in his back teeth—a pressure lifting, like a held breath released after a hundred years.

"Done," he called softly. "Your turn."

Emrys knelt at the ninth tile. They didn't have tools; they used their fingers, finding the seam, working it, patient as water. The tile came up with a sucking sound and a smell that hit Sable like a memory he'd never made—cold stone and sea-wind and something sweet underneath, something like myrrh cut with brine.

Below the tile: a cavity lined in lead, and in the cavity, a coffer of black oak bound in corroded silver.

And rising out of the coffer, before anyone touched it, light.

"Oh," Sable said. His voice came out very small. "Oh, that's not junk."

The circlet lifted—or seemed to lift; later he'd argue with himself about whether it moved or the light did—a ring of pale metal worked into overlapping scales like fish or feathers, set with stones that weren't stones, that were salt given permanent crystal form, each one burning with a soft white fire that threw no shadows. It was beautiful the way ice is beautiful, the way deep water is beautiful. Beautiful and utterly indifferent to you.

Emrys reached for it.

"Wait—" Sable said, too late, and not knowing what he'd say after *wait* anyway.

Emrys's fingers closed around the circlet, and the world flinched.

Not loudly. No thunder, no shattering glass. Just a sound like the whole chapel inhaling, and every candle flame bending sideways though there was no wind, and the salt-stones in the circlet blazing up so bright that Sable threw his arm across his eyes—and then dark, and silence, and the reeds outside gone abruptly quiet, as if the fen itself were listening.

"Sable." Emrys's voice, steady. Too steady. "Don't panic."

"That sentence makes people panic. That's the first rule of sentences."

"Look at my hand."

Sable lowered his arm. In the guttering candlelight he saw the circlet sitting in Emrys's palm, dim now, almost humble. And he saw Emrys's hand—the back of it, the wrist, climbing toward the elbow—marked.

A sigil, fine as frost-work, white as salt-rim on black stone: the circle bisected by the line. The same mark that glowed on the coin in his strongbox. Except this one was alive. This one moved, subtly, the way heat-shimmer moves, and it covered skin from knuckle to forearm in branching filigree like frost growing in fast-forward.

"It's the mark from the book," Emrys said. Their voice held, but their eyes had gone somewhere else—to some interior room where fear lived. "The salt-mark. The ward didn't trigger *on* me. It *recognized* me. It—" They stopped. Started again. "It knew its owner."

Sable stood very still in the ruined chapel with the candles dying and the fen holding its breath, and he ran the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for two weeks. A stranger who navigated smuggler's roads like scripture. A stranger with a prophecy book and their own name in it. A stranger whose skin now wore the sigil of a kingdom that had been erased from every map Sable had ever forged or fenced.

"The last salt," he said quietly. "The heir of Thornfell. You're not collecting relics. You *are* one."

Emrys looked up at him, and in the dark their eyes were the grey of deep water, and they did not deny it.

"The Inquisitors will be able to see this," they said instead. Flat. Factual. "From now on. Any hedge-witch, any scryer, anyone with a drop of the craft will look at me and see exactly what I am. I can't hide anymore. I needed you to know that before you decide—" Their jaw tightened. "Before you decide whether you're still in."

Sable looked at the marked hand. At the circlet, dull as pewter now, waiting. At the copper coin he'd left on the altar for whatever lived in places like this, which suddenly seemed like a very small payment indeed.

Rule three, broken beyond repair. Rule two, smiling at him from the shadows. Rule one—never steal from the desperate—hanging in the balance, depending entirely on what he said next.

He thought about six years of a glowing coin in a strongbox. About how tired he'd been, lately, of selling meaning without knowing it.

"Well," he said, and heard his own grin arrive like a rescue party, ragged but real. "Good news is, I know every blind route from here to the coast, and I've smuggled things far uglier than a marked fugitive." He stuck out his hand. "Partners. Full shares. And Emrys—the first rule of partnership is you tell me the rest of it. All of it. On the road."

Emrys took his hand, and the salt-mark flared briefly white where their skin touched, warm as a struck match.

That was when Sable heard it—far off down the fen road, carried on the water: hooves. Many horses, riding hard, and the thin high note of a horn being blown in signal.

"They're already looking for you," he said.

"They were always looking for me," Emrys said. "Now they can find me. Run."

They ran.
