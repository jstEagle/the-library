## 7. The Chapel Heist

The chapel of Saint Aldhelm-the-Drowned sat at the crook of the river like a bone caught in a throat, and for three days Emrys watched it the way a fox watches a henhouse—patiently, hungrily, counting the comings and goings of everything that thought itself safe inside.

They lay in the bracken on the far slope with Sable beside them, close enough that Emrys could smell the thief's habitual disguise: tallow pomade and cheap rosewater, the scent of someone determined to be forgettable. Below, the chapel's squat bell tower threw a shadow across its own graveyard, and the evening light turned the slate roof the color of old bruises.

"Three services a day," Sable murmured. "Dawn, noon, vespers. Priest is deaf as a post but pious as a fever. Sacristan locks the reliquary at dusk. And there—" they lifted one gloved finger toward a lean figure stacking hymnals, "—our friend the verger, who sleeps in the vestry with the poorbox key around his neck and dreams, I'd wager, of nothing more ambitious than breakfast."

"You've been watching longer than me," Emrys said.

"I've been watching since spring." Sable rolled onto their back, arms folded behind their head, and grinned at the darkening sky. "I sold a candlestick from that sacristy in Marrowford last autumn. Paid for a very good winter. If the circlet's truly under the floor, this chapel has been sitting on a fortune for a century, singing psalms over it twice on Sundays."

Emrys didn't answer. They were watching the light fail on the water below, thinking about what the book had said—or rather, what its margins said, in that cramped cipher Maren had taught them to read before sending them north. *The circlet lies where the drowned are remembered by those who never knew them.* A chapel full of saints' bones fit well enough. The drowned kingdom's dead, mourned by strangers who didn't know whose bones they sang over.

It should have felt like theft. It did feel like theft—that was the trouble. But it wasn't gold Emrys was after, or not only. The relics called to each other. They'd learned that much from the chalice-shaped absence in the prophecy's text, the way the pages warmed near certain places, the way Emrys's own blood had begun to hum when they crossed running water heading north. Whatever Thornfell had been, some part of it recognized Emrys walking its forgotten roads, and Emrys hated how much they wanted to be recognized.

"We go tonight," they said. "Vespers ends at moonrise. The priest retires. The verger drinks."

"The verger drinks," Sable agreed happily. "God love him."

* * *

They went in through the crypt, because every chapel built on a drowned man's grave keeps a crypt, and every crypt keeps a drain, and every drain can be persuaded.

Emrys went first. That had become their arrangement without discussion—Sable for the fine work, Emrys for the passages no honest person would fit through. They stripped to their shift, greased their shoulders with mutton fat from a jar Sable carried for exactly such purposes, and slid into the drain's mouth feet-first, feeling the stone close around them like a fist.

The dark down there was total. Emrys breathed slow and counted, an old smuggler's trick—count your breaths, count your turns, trust your body's memory over your eyes. Left at the second junction. Down where the water sound deepened. The air tasted of iron and old prayers.

And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse through a wall, something else. A warmth. A *waiting*.

*There you are,* Emrys thought, and immediately wished they hadn't, because the warmth answered. Not words. Just a settling, like a dog hearing its name.

They came up through the crypt floor grate into air that smelled of incense and stone dust, hauled themselves out, and stood dripping grease in the dark while Sable followed, unfolding from the drain like a cat from a sack, immaculate even now, somehow.

"You're disgusting," Sable whispered fondly.

"You're next time."

"Not a chance. I'm the brains." Sable produced two slivers of candle, lit them, cupped the flames. Light bloomed small and golden across the crypt: niches of stacked skulls, a stone effigy of a bishop with his hands folded on a sword—which struck Emrys as telling—and, along the eastern wall, a slab of flooring newer than the rest. Newer by a century, which was to say, older than anything else here except what lay beneath it.

The mortar had been re-cut. Someone had laid that slab deliberately, carefully, and carved into its edge—worn nearly smooth—a line of symbols Emrys had seen once before, inked in the almanac's margins.

Saltwork cipher. Royal script.

"It's true," Emrys breathed.

"Course it's true. Why else would we be lying in grave grease in the dark?" But Sable had gone quiet too, crouched at the slab's edge, running fingers along those worn glyphs. "What does it say? You read it. You've been reading it the whole way north, don't think I haven't noticed."

Emrys hesitated. There were things they hadn't told Sable—the name in the book, the circled dates, the word *salt*. Some habits of invisibility ran deeper than others.

"'Here lies the crown that no head should bear,'" they translated slowly. "'Kept by faith against the heir's return.'"

"Heir," Sable repeated. "Hm." They looked up, and in the candlelight their expression was unreadable in a way Emrys had learned meant *filed away for later.* "Well. Let's not keep any heirs waiting."

Getting the slab up took an hour. Sable worked the mortar with a blade and patience while Emrys levered with a pry-bar wrapped in wool to keep it silent. Twice the verger stirred overhead—they froze, hearts hammering, listening to him mutter and roll over—and once a mouse ran across Emrys's hand and they very nearly betrayed everything with a yelp.

Then the slab came free with a sigh of stone, and cold air rose out of the earth beneath it, and both of them leaned over the opening without breathing.

Below, in a lead-lined hollow packed with wax-sealed cloth, lay a circlet of pale metal. No jewels. No ornament worth naming. Just a band worked into overlapping scales, each scale etched with a single glyph, and the whole of it giving off that faint warmth Emrys had felt through six feet of stone—as if it had been sitting by a fire all this time, waiting to be worn.

"That's it?" Sable whispered. "That's the great treasure? Looks like a fisherman's hat."

"It's salt-silver," Emrys said. Their voice came out strange, thickened. "Smelted from sea-ice and ash, the ferryman said. There's none left in the world. There hasn't been for a hundred years." They reached down.

"Don't touch it bare-handed," Sable said sharply. "Rule one of my trade: never take a thing with your skin if you can help it. Things remember hands."

But Emrys's fingers were already closing on the metal, and it was warm, warm as a living brow, and the moment skin met salt-silver Emrys understood exactly why Sable's rule existed—because the circlet *did* remember hands, thousands of them, crowned heads and cut throats and oaths sworn over wells, and all of it poured up Emrys's arm at once like floodwater finding a breach.

They gasped. The candles guttered.

"Emrys." Sable's grip found their shoulder. "Emrys, look at me—"

"I'm fine. I'm—" Fine was wrong. Something was moving under their skin, a cool prickling spreading from the right hand upward, across the wrist, and when Emrys raised their arm into the candlelight they saw it: a mark rising through the flesh like frost climbing a windowpane. Lines of white, branching, precise—a sigil they knew from the book's first page, from the treaty seals in the ferryman's stories, from the brand the Crown's Inquisitors burned into traitors' doors.

The salt-sigil. The sign of Thornfell's royal line.

Not inked. Not painted. *Grown.*

"No," Emrys said aloud, and clawed at it, and of course scratching changed nothing. It spread past the elbow now, delicate and terrible, filigree of white beneath the brown of their skin, glowing faintly where the candlelight touched it. "No, no—"

"What is that." Sable's voice had lost all its play. "Emrys. What is that. What did you take."

"I don't—I didn't know it would—" Emrys wrapped their arm in their shirt sleeve, useless, the glow bleeding through linen like a lantern through a sheet. The ward. There had been a ward on the slab, in the carving, in the very act of taking; the crown had been kept *by faith*, and faith apparently kept accounts. It had marked its heir. Or marked its thief. From inside, Emrys couldn't tell the difference anymore, and that frightened them worse than the mark itself.

Because they could feel the difference dissolving.

Above them, the chapel bells began to ring—not vespers, not matins, an alarm pattern, cracked and rusty, rung by no human hand. Somewhere in the walls, wax seals older than the town were breaking one by one.

"Time to leave," Sable said.

* * *

They made the drain going back faster than either of them liked, squeezing through in the dark with the alarm still tolling above and dogs starting up in the village beyond the churchyard wall. Emrys went first again, and this time the drain's darkness offered no comfort at all, because their own arm glowed faintly ahead of them, lighting the wet stone, announcing them to every shadow they passed.

They came out through the culvert into the willow-dark of the riverbank, soaked to the waist, and crouched there gasping while the chapel bell rang itself hoarse behind them.

Sable pulled off their gloves—one, two—and stood looking down at Emrys with an expression Emrys had never seen on them before. Not fear. Worse. Recognition.

"Show me," Sable said quietly.

"Sable—"

"*Show me.*"

Slowly, Emrys pushed back the sodden sleeve. The sigil had climbed to the shoulder now, its lines settled into their final shape: the scaled band of the crown itself, circling the arm like a brand, complete, permanent. It pulsed gently, in time, Emrys realized with a lurch, with their heartbeat.

Sable crouched. Didn't touch it. Looked instead at Emrys's face, long and level, and Emrys watched the arithmetic happen behind those quick grey eyes—the book Emrys wouldn't show them, the cipher Emrys read alone, the way ghosts' roads opened for them, the way Emrys had known which villages weren't on any map. Every oddity of the last weeks rearranging itself into a shape.

"The heir," Sable said. "The margin said *heir's return.*" A pause. "You're not just reading the prophecy, are you. You're *in* it."

Emrys's mouth was dry as altar stone. Every instinct screamed to lie—to laugh, deflect, invent, do what they had done their whole life, be nobody, be a guide, be a smudge on the road. But the mark burned on their arm like a lit window in a night country, and somewhere south, they knew without knowing how, men were already turning their horses north.

"My name's in the book," Emrys said. "Has been since before I was born. The date I found it was circled a century ago. There's a word they use for me. *Last salt.* Last heir of Thornfell." They heard their own voice shake and hated it. "I didn't ask for it. I didn't want any of it. I wanted to move cargo and stay unrecorded until I died comfortable and anonymous in somebody's barn."

Sable said nothing for a long moment. The bell finally cracked mid-toll and gave up, leaving a silence full of dogs and river.

"You know what I did, before you?" Sable said at last. "Before you hired me in Marrowford?"

"You stole things."

"I stole things *carefully*. Small things, far-flung, nothing anyone would cross a border for. Because I grew up in a border town that got eaten by a war over whose king owned it, and I decided early there was no object in the world worth being *known* for." Sable laughed, short and without humor. "And then I signed on with a person who turns out to have their name written in a hundred-year-old book, and I helped them dig a crown out of a chapel floor, and now every hedge-witch and Inquisitor between here and the capital can see you coming by the light of that—" they gestured at the glowing arm, "—that *lantern* you're wearing under your skin."

"I'm sorry." Emrys meant it more than they'd meant anything in years. "I should have told you. I should have let you walk away."

"You should have," Sable agreed. Then, to Emrys's astonishment, they sat down in the mud beside them, pulled their ruined gloves back on, and sighed the sigh of a person committing to a very bad decision with open eyes. "But here's the thing about being careful your whole life. Sooner or later you meet something worth being careless about. I spent six weeks watching you navigate passes that aren't on maps and talk your way past a toll-warden who hates everyone, and I thought: this one is interesting. Now it turns out this one is *prophecy*." They shook their head. "I ought to leave tonight. Any sensible person would."

"But?"

"But whoever wrote that book circled your date a century before you drew breath," Sable said, "which means somebody planned all this, and I have never once in my life let a con run unchallenged. Somebody wrote you into a story, Emrys. I want to know who. And I want to know what they get out of the ending." They stood, brushed mud from their knees, and offered a hand up. "So. Where's the next relic, Your Highness?"

"Don't call me that."

"Your Saltiness, then."

Emrys took the hand and let themselves be pulled upright—and stopped. Because standing there in the willow-dark with the sigil pulsing warm along their arm, they felt it again, stronger now, clearer than ever: a pull, north and east, over the mountains, steady as a compass needle. The chalice. Or something else. Something that had felt the crown come out of the ground and was calling to it across the drowned miles.

And threaded through the pull, fainter, another sensation entirely—the prickle of attention. Of being *looked for*. Somewhere behind them, days south, a grey rider had perhaps just paused at a crossroads, tilted his head like a man hearing distant music, and turned his horse.

"What is it?" Sable asked. "You've gone white. Whiter."

"It knows I'm marked now," Emrys said. "Everything knows. The relics. The wards. And—" they swallowed, "—the people hunting me. I'm not invisible anymore, Sable. I haven't been since my hand touched that metal."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then they turned north, toward the mountains, and pulled their hood up against the river damp.

"Well," they said. "Best put that lantern to work, then. Which way?"

Emrys looked at their glowing arm, then north, where the pull beat like a second heart.

"Northeast," they said. "Over the Salt Stair. Three days, if the weather holds." They started up the bank, and did not say—because they were only now admitting it to themselves—that with every step the mark felt less like a wound and more like a fitting. "And Sable? Walk fast. From tonight on, we're not the only ones who know where we're going."
