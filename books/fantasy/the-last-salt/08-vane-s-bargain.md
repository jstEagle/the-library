## 8. Vane's Bargain

The trap was simplicity itself, and Halvard Vane distrusted it for that reason.

He had set it in the market town of Harrowgate, where the north road pinched between a tithe-barn and the old salt warehouse—two buildings the Crown had seized a century ago and never thought of again. The relic-thief came through at dusk, as Vane knew he would. Quill could not resist a town with a chapel under renovation; half his inventory over the years had come from loosened floorstones and "salvaged" reliquaries. The man was a magpie with excellent manners and no sense of when he was being hunted.

Vane watched from the shadow of the warehouse eaves while four of his riders closed the alley behind Sable Quill with the quiet efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times. No shout, no drawn steel. Just the soft click of a crossbow being spanned in the dark.

Sable stopped walking. Even from thirty feet, even in the failing light, Vane saw the exact moment the thief understood—the small stillness, the weight shifting back onto the heels, the hands rising in a gesture that managed to be both surrender and mockery.

"Master Inquisitor," Sable called out pleasantly. "I'd know those boots anywhere. You've been following me since Duncarrow."

"Since Saltmere," Vane said, stepping into the open. "You sold a forged psalter to a widow there and told her it would keep her husband's ghost from walking. It did nothing of the kind. The ghost walked anyway. She wrote to the Crown."

"I refunded her."

"You kept the psalter money and charged her for the refund."

A grin flickered across the thief's face, quick and unrepentant. Then the riders took his arms, and the grin became something tighter, more careful. His eyes went past Vane, scanning the street, the rooftops, the dark mouths of the alleys—not looking for escape, Vane realized. Looking for someone else.

"Where is your companion?" Vane asked.

"I travel alone. It's cheaper."

"The salt-marked child travels alone, then." He let the words fall into the silence between them and watched them land. Sable's face did an admirable job of showing confusion, but he had spent twenty years reading faces in interrogation rooms, and admirable jobs were his trade too. There. The flicker. The almost-glance toward the river quarter.

"Take him to the tithe-barn," Vane said. "Alone. And if a single hair on his head is harmed before I speak with him again, I'll have the man responsible flogged for wasting my leverage."

* * *

The tithe-barn smelled of dust and old grain and, faintly, of the sea—a smell Vane had never been able to explain this far inland until he understood what these towns had once belonged to. Thornfell's salt roads had run through here. Everything ran through here, once. Now the maps showed only Crown territory, tidy and unhaunted, and the barns stood empty because there was no one left to tithe.

He sat opposite Sable Quill at a plank table by lamplight. The thief's wrists were bound to the chair, which was unnecessary—Sable's bindings were always unnecessary—but procedure mattered. Procedure was the spine of lawful work. Without it you were simply violence wearing a coat.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Master Quill?" Vane began.

"My charm?"

"Your consistency. Three years of records. You've sold forty-one objects attributed to Thornfell provenance—rings, coins, a candlestick, two dozen fragments of glazed tile. Every sale logged in your own meticulous ledger, because you are, ironically, the only honest bookkeeper in this entire affair. You never claimed they were worth more than trinkets. You never guessed."

"Guessed at what?"

Vane opened the case beside him and lifted out the circlet.

It was plain at first glance—a band of tarnished silver-white metal, unadorned, the sort of thing a farmer might find in a field and sell for the weight. But the lamp caught it wrong. The light slid off it the way water slides off oiled cloth, refusing to settle, and beneath the refusal there was a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in winter. Like salt drying on skin.

Sable looked at it for a long moment. Vane gave him credit: he didn't pretend not to understand.

"That came out of the chapel floor at Marrowdale," Sable said quietly. "Three days ago. There was a ward. My partner tripped it."

"Your partner tripped it," Vane agreed, "and now wears the mark of the last heir of Thornfell on their throat, visible to every hedge-witch and scrying-glass between here and the capital. Do you know how long the Order has waited for that mark to show itself?"

Sable's eyes came up sharply. "The Order," he repeated. "Not the Crown?"

And there it was—the question Vane had been carrying for eleven years, asked back at him by a common thief, in a barn, by lamplight.

"Interesting," Vane said, "that you should ask it that way."

"I've been followed before. Crown men, tax men, guild enforcers. They have a smell. Yours is different. Your warrant—" Sable tilted his head toward the seal-case at Vane's belt. "I got a good look at it in Duncarrow, when you weren't watching. That's not a royal seal. I've seen enough royal seals to forge one blindfolded. Yours has a balance scale on it, and a number underneath. Thirteen strokes. What office flies a scale and thirteen strokes?"

For a moment Vane considered lying. It would be easy. It was what the Order expected, what the Order trained into its interrogators: reveal nothing, extract everything, let the subject believe the Crown held their leash. The lie protected the Order. The lie had protected the Order for a hundred years.

But Halvard Vane was tired, and the barn was quiet, and somewhere in the last month—since Saltmere, since the cottage fire, since he had watched an old woman's hedge-witchery burn and felt nothing where satisfaction should have been—the lie had begun to chafe.

"What do you know about the dissolution of Thornfell?" he asked instead.

"What everyone knows. A hundred years ago the kingdom defaulted, the treaty dissolved it, the Crown absorbed the territory, end of story. History's dullest chapter. Nobody even fought."

"Nobody even fought," Vane echoed. He turned the circlet slowly on the table, letting its wrong light move. "Four hundred thousand subjects. A standing militia. Fortresses that had repelled three invasions. And it surrendered without a sword drawn, because overnight—literally overnight—every well in the kingdom went bitter. Salt-rot in the water. Livestock dying. Children sickening. A kingdom cannot fight when its own ground poisons it. So they signed. And within a decade, the wells ran sweet again. Curious, isn't it? As though the poison had never been natural at all. As though someone had needed it only long enough to get a signature."

Sable had gone very still. "Who poisoned the wells?"

"The Order of the Ledger." Vane heard his own voice go flat and distant, the way it did when he recited things he had sworn never to say aloud. "Clerks. Archivists. The most patient people I have ever served. They wrote a prophecy—a real one, functional, self-fulfilling—that promised a last heir would come to restore or unmake Thornfell. They seeded it into smugglers' almanacs and hedge-copies so it would spread like rot through the low country. Why? Because a dissolved kingdom needs no justice, Master Quill. But a kingdom with a living heir, a prophesied heir, a *lawful* heir—that kingdom can be bargained with forever. Every border dispute, every rebellion, every restless generation: 'Wait for the last salt. Wait for the Door.' Hope, manufactured in bulk, dispensed as sedative. And if the heir ever actually appears—" he tapped the circlet— "they intend to control him. Or her. Or whatever the child turns out to be. Crown in one hand, ledger in the other."

"And you?" Sable's voice was careful now. Genuinely careful, all the mockery burned away. "Grey Inquisitor Halvard Vane. Whose coat do you wear?"

"Both. Neither." Vane leaned back. "My warrant comes from the Crown. My orders come from the Ledger. The Crown believes I hunt a pretender. The Ledger believes I am their instrument. I believed, for eleven years, that I was the law's hand doing necessary, ugly work. Then I read the founding ledgers." He smiled without warmth. "They keep immaculate records, the Ledger. Costs of the well-poisoning. Names of the apothecaries who brewed the agent. Payments to the treaty negotiators. A hundred years of bookkeeping, and not one entry marked *crime*. Only *expenditure*. That is the horror of clerks, Master Quill. They can buy a kingdom's death and file it under operating costs."

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind moved through the empty grain-lofts with a sound almost like voices.

"So why tell me?" Sable asked. "You're the one holding the chains."

"Because tomorrow I'm going to offer your friend amnesty, and they're going to refuse, and then the Order takes over and does things I will not be permitted to prevent. Because you're going to see them again—I'm choosing to leave a door unlocked tonight, and you're clever enough to use it—and when you do, you're going to tell them what I told you. Not because you owe me. Because they deserve to choose with their eyes open, the way I never did." He rose, gathered the circlet back into its case. "Consider it the only clean entry I can still make in my own ledger."

"And the lock?"

"Third stall. The bolt sticks. Lift, then push." He paused at the barn door, lamplight behind him. "One more thing, Quill. When you give them my message—give them the warning too. Tell them the Order doesn't want the relics recovered. It wants the heir *desperate*. Desperate heirs accept bargains. Whatever corner they're driven into, tell them to check who built the walls."

He left the thief in the dark with his sticking bolt, and stood a while outside under the cold stars, listening to the wind in the lofts, waiting to feel damned.

He found, to his surprise, that he mostly just felt lighter.

* * *

The exchange was set for noon in the marketplace, in full public view—Vane's choice, because the Order preferred shadows and he wanted witnesses, even if the witnesses understood nothing. Emrys Winterbourne came alone, hooded, moving through the crowd with the boneless ease of someone raised on smuggler's paths. Vane noted the salt-sigil glowing faintly at their throat even through the wrapped scarf. Marked now. Marked forever.

They faced each other by the old market cross, and Vane laid out the terms plainly, loudly enough for the nearby stalls to hear: full amnesty. Pardon under royal seal. The relics surrendered, the inheritance formally renounced before a magistrate, and a new name, new papers, a life anywhere in the Empire's breadth. "No one need ever know what you were," he finished. "That is the Crown's mercy, and I am empowered to offer it."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice carried further than it should have, clear as a bell across the square.

"And Sable Quill? Your prisoner?"

"Released. Unharmed. Already gone, I expect."

"And the ghosts?" The hood tilted. "The dead farmers in the phantom fields, the ferryman on the unmapped river, the queen who begs not to be restored? Does the Crown's mercy cover them? Is there a clause for the people your treaty erased?"

"There are no such people," Vane said, giving the lawful answer. "Thornfell was dissolved. Dissolved things don't persist."

"Then you've nothing to fear from me," Emrys said. "If it's all stories, let me walk away with my stories."

"It isn't stories, and we both know it, and the moment you walk away the Order sends people who make me look gentle."

"Yes," Emrys said softly. "So Maren told me. Before you burned her cottage."

The words landed like a blade finding a gap in armor. Around them the market noise continued, oblivious. Vane did not flinch, because flinching was a luxury, but something in his chest shifted its weight.

"I have read the founding ledgers," he said. "I know what the Order did to build this trap a century ago. I know what they intend to do with you. Which is why I'm offering you the only exit that doesn't lead through their hands. Take the pardon. Renounce it. Live."

"No."

The word was quiet, and it rang louder than anything shouted in that square in forty years.

"They wrote a prophecy to own the future," Emrys said, and pulled back their hood, letting the whole square see the sigil burning at their throat, the face beneath it young and exhausted and absolutely certain. "They poisoned wells to own the past. And you—you're offering to let me sign my way out of my own existence, in triplicate, witnessed. I've read enough ledgers lately, Inquisitor. I know what gets filed under *expenditure*." They stepped closer, and lowered their voice to something meant for him alone, though the whole square seemed to lean in. "You told Sable the truth. I know you did—he wouldn't have escaped a sticking bolt otherwise. So I'll tell you one back. I'm not going to restore their kingdom for them, and I'm not going to renounce it for them either. I'm going to end it. Both of their options were cages. I'm choosing the door."

"There is no third option. The prophecy allows two."

"The prophecy," said the last salt, "was written by clerks. Clerks can be corrected."

They turned and walked into the crowd, and the crowd parted around them without knowing why, and Vane stood at the market cross with his warrant heavy on his belt and did not order his riders to follow.

Behind him, from the direction of the tithe-barn, came the unmistakable sound of a window opening that had been locked all night, and light footsteps on packed earth, and a familiar voice calling cheerfully across the square: *"Emrys! Over here! You will not believe what the scary man told me!"*

Halvard Vane closed his eyes.

Somewhere far to the north, in a hall lined with ledgers, the Order of the Ledger was already writing its response. And for the first time in eleven years of service, Halvard Vane understood exactly which column his own name would be entered under.

He turned north anyway, and began to ride.
