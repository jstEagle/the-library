## 10. Ghost Country

The border was not a line. That was the first thing Emrys understood, standing on the last true hill of the Crown's maps and looking north into a country that did not exist.

There was no river to cross here, no wall, no checkpoint where an Inquisitor's seal might turn them back. There was only a change in the quality of the light—a thinning, as if the world beyond had been washed one time too many. The grass grew grey-green instead of green. The wind came down off the high fells carrying the smell of woodsmoke and turned earth and something older beneath both, like the scent trapped in a house where no one had lit a fire in a hundred years.

"Tell me again why we're walking into the place the maps are afraid of," Sable said. They had their hood up against the wind, and their fingers were doing their nervous arithmetic—thumb across knuckles, thumb across knuckles—the way they always did when the road ahead looked like it wanted to keep them.

"Because the chalice is there." Emrys touched the strap of the pack where the Salt Circlet rode wrapped in oilcloth, cold even through three layers of wool. It had been cold since the chapel. It had been cold like it was *watching*. "And because the book says the second relic keeps what the first relic only points at."

"The book also says you're going to save or damn a nation, depending which margin note you believe."

"Then I'll read both margins."

Sable huffed—not quite a laugh, but near enough that Emrys counted it. In the two weeks since the chapel floor had opened under their hands and written its sigil into Emrys's skin, they had developed a rhythm: Sable talking enough for both of them, Emrys listening, the prophecy book riding between them like a third passenger who occasionally cleared its throat.

Emrys's forearm ached where the salt-mark sat beneath the sleeve. At night it gleamed faintly through the bandages, pale lines like frost ferns, and twice now—in dreams, in the half-second before waking—they had seen the pattern continue up past the elbow, branching, patient, waiting to be invited further.

They walked down out of the hills as the sun went the colour of old brass, and the country of Thornfell opened before them.

* *

It was farmland. That was what undid Emrys most completely—that it was simply farmland, rolling and gentle, hedged with drystone walls gone soft at the joints, threaded by lanes the width of a cart. Except every field was sown with light. Wheat stood in the furrows made of nothing but a suggestion, gold gone translucent, and figures moved among it—bent-backed, unhurried, hands passing through stalks that bent anyway, because a hundred years of harvest is its own kind of law.

The dead were farming.

"They don't see us," Sable whispered, though they'd said it three times already, each time as if needing fresh proof.

One figure straightened as they passed—an old man leaning on a scythe that was itself a memory of a scythe—and his eyes, milky as riverstone, slid across them without catching. But then he paused. Frowned. Looked again, and this time the look *landed*, and his mouth fell open, and he raised one hand—not in threat, in greeting, in something like *recognition*—and called out a word in a language Emrys did not know and yet understood perfectly in their bones:

"*Saltborn.*"

Other heads lifted along the field. Word moved through the phantom wheat faster than any runner could carry it, a shiver going from figure to figure, and Emrys felt it land on their own skin like rain—the salt-sigil warming, glowing faintly blue through the torn sleeve, announcing them to every dead soul in the valley.

"I thought they wouldn't notice us," Sable said. "I thought we'd be—"

"Ghosts among ghosts?" Emrys watched the harvesters set down their tools, one by one, and turn. "We're the most alive thing this country's seen in a century. Of course they notice."

An old woman came down the lane toward them, walking with the deliberate care of someone whose knees had opinions. She wore a smock a hundred years out of fashion and a wedding ring that had rusted to memory. When she reached Emrys she stopped, studied the mark on their arm, and made a gesture—forehead, lips, heart—that Sable later swore was the old Thornfell salute to royalty.

"You're late," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and patience. "A hundred years and a season late, saltborn. But the fields kept. The fields always keep."

"We're looking for the drowned cathedral," Emrys said. "Westwater. There's something there I have to—"

"There's everything there you have to." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone who has waited so long that comfort has become a stranger. "But you'll eat first. You'll sleep under a roof. You'll hear how we lived and how we ended, because a ruler who doesn't know what they rule over is just a thief with better manners." Her eyes flicked to the pack on Emrys's back, and something in her face tightened. "And you'll meet her. She's been asking after you since the moon turned."

"Her?"

The woman's expression closed like a door. "Come," she said. "The village keeps supper warm. It's all the village has ever been able to keep."

* *

The village of Harrowmere—if it had ever truly been called that; the name surfaced in Emrys's mind unbidden, the way names did here, the country whispering its own memory into whoever walked it—was a miracle of almost. Houses stood whole until you tried to lean on them. A well drew up water you could see the bucket through. Smoke rose from chimneys in slow silver ribbons that never dispersed, never faded, just climbed forever toward a sky that held them gently, like cupped hands.

And the people. There were perhaps sixty of them gathered in the long hall when the old woman led Emrys and Sable in, and every face turned, and every face held the same expression: hope worn down to something more durable than hope. Not joy. Joy would have broken them. This was the thing underneath joy, the load-bearing beam.

They fed their guests food that tasted like the idea of food—barley broth that warmed without filling, bread whose texture arrived a half-second after its flavour—but they fed it with such ceremony, such fierce hospitality, that Emrys ate every offered bowl rather than wound them with refusal. Sable, for once in their life, was quiet. Emrys caught them staring at a child by the fire, a ghost-child maybe eight years old turning a wooden horse over and over in her hands, and saw the thief do sums they didn't want to finish.

"How?" Emrys finally asked the hall at large. "How do you still—you died. Or you didn't die. What happened here?"

An old man with a smith's forearms answered, his voice rumbling like his forge must once have done. "The treaty dissolved us. That's the word they used. *Dissolved.* Like sugar in water, like a debt forgiven. One day we were Thornfell; the next day our king was a duke of nowhere, our laws were ash, our names were struck from the rolls, and the border moved south while we slept." He turned his hands palm-up. "But a country isn't paper. A country is the shape of itself, and shapes remember. So we stayed. We couldn't leave—we tried, the ones of us who woke early, and the roads carried us in circles back to our own doors. We couldn't be seen—living folk walk through our market square without a glance. And we couldn't die properly either, because dying had already happened, or hadn't, and no one could tell us which." He smiled without humour. "So we farm. Every year we bring in a harvest no one eats, pay taxes to no one collects them, and wait."

"For what?" Sable asked softly.

The smith looked at Emrys. All of them looked at Emrys. "For the salt to come home."

Emrys set down the spoon. Their hand was not quite steady. "I'm not—I'm not a ruler. I'm nobody. I ran away from an apprenticeship. I guide smugglers through passes because I'm good at not being seen. Whatever your book thinks I am—"

"The book," said a new voice, low and clear and tired, from the head of the hall, "is wrong about almost everything. But it is not wrong about you."

* *

She had been sitting apart, in the shadows behind the master's chair, and Emrys had taken her for another villager—a tall woman in a plain dark gown, greying hair braided simply, hands folded like a woman in prayer who had stopped expecting an answer. Only when she rose did the hall change around her. The villagers bowed. Not deeply—it was clearly a habit grown stiff with disuse—but they bowed, and the firelight found a circlet-shaped paleness across her brow, the ghost of a crown worn so long it had printed itself on the soul.

"Queen Ilsa," the old woman said. "Last of Thornfell. Though she'll tell you herself she was last of very little else."

"Don't kneel," Ilsa said, before Emrys's knees could make any decisions. "Please. I've had a hundred years of being knelt to and I can tell you honestly it never once helped anyone." She crossed the hall and stood close, studying Emrys's face with eyes the colour of deep water. "You have her chin. Maren's mother had it too—the Winterbourne jaw. Stubbornness made bone."

"You knew my family?"

"I knew everyone, in the end. That was the problem." Something moved through her face—grief, guilt, both wearing each other's clothes. She glanced at the hall full of her people, at the child with the wooden horse now asleep against her mother's shoulder, and lowered her voice. "Walk with me. What I have to say shouldn't be heard by people who might forgive me too easily."

They walked out into the phantom evening together. Sable started to follow; Emrys caught their eye and shook their head, and Sable—bless them, for once—stayed, folding themselves down by the fire with the wary air of a cat guarding a doorway.

Ilsa led them up the lane to a rise above the village, where a stone bench faced the drowned cathedral's distant silhouette, black against the last light. She sat. After a moment Emrys sat beside her, and the cold that came off the queen was not the cold of death but the cold of restraint, like sitting next to a banked fire.

"Do you know how Thornfell ended?" she asked.

"Dissolved by treaty. The ferryman told me. No conquest, no war—just signatures."

"Signatures." She laughed, briefly, bitterly. "Yes. And do you know what a signature is worth? It is worth exactly as much as the fear behind it." She turned to face Emrys fully. "I signed it. Understand that first, before anything else I say. I am not a martyr of Thornfell. I am its gravedigger. The clerks came to me with their treaty and their numbers—grain yields falling, wells souring, children sickening, the land itself turning against us—and they said, Majesty, the kingdom is dying, and the Crown offers mercy: dissolve now, with honour, with pensions for your lords, with your people fed from southern granaries. Or hold on, and watch them starve in the streets while history calls you a tyrant." Her hands tightened in her lap. "What they didn't tell me—and what I learned too late, and what cost me everything—was that the wells weren't failing. They were *poisoned*. Deliberately. Slowly. By the same clerks who wrote the treaty and the same order that wrote your prophecy. They manufactured a dying kingdom so they could legally bury it, and I—" her voice cracked, a hundred years of cracking in one syllable, "—I signed the paper that let them. I surrendered my people into erasure because I trusted the ledger over my own eyes."

The salt-mark burned cold on Emrys's arm. They thought of Vane's grey coat, of the word *Ledger* they'd pried loose from a dying rider's ravings, of the neat, patient handwriting in the prophecy's margins. *Power writes its own justice*, Maren had said. Apparently it also wrote its own crimes.

"So when they come for me," Emrys said slowly, "the Order of the Ledger—they want me restored. Crowned. Why restore a kingdom they destroyed?"

"Because destruction was only ever half the business model." Ilsa's voice went flat and precise, a clerk's own cadence. "A dead kingdom is worthless. A dead kingdom with a lawful heir, a prophesied restorer, a *chosen one* rising at the appointed hour—that is worth wars. Border claims. Taxes. A puppet on a throne the Ledger built, wearing a crown the Ledger forged, signing treaties the Ledger drafts. They don't want Thornfell alive, child. They want it *resurrected*—and resurrection requires a body, and the body they've chosen is yours."

Wind moved through the ghost-wheat below. Somewhere in the village a bell rang vespers, though there was no rope to pull and no bell to ring, only the memory of the hour keeping itself.

"Then I won't wear it," Emrys said. "Whatever they offer, whatever the prophecy says—I refuse. I'll find the Door and I'll—"

"And you'll what?" Ilsa asked, not cruelly. "Restore us? Put the crown on and break their game by playing it better?" She shook her head, and her braid shifted like a rope coming loose. "Listen to me. This is why I begged the old women to bring you to me before you went any further. The crown is not a tool, saltborn. It is an appetite. I wore it thirty years, and by the end I could not tell which thoughts were mine and which were its. I ordered men flogged and felt *relief*. I hoarded grain while villages starved and called it prudence. The corruption the clerks used to justify dissolving us—" she met Emrys's eyes, and there was no evasion left in her, nothing but the naked truth she'd carried alone through a century of harvests she couldn't eat, "—half of it was real, and I was its source. The crown curdled me. Then the clerks used what I'd become as evidence against everything my people were. Do you understand? My rot became their justification for a hundred years of exile for these—" her hand swept the valley, the fields, the sleeping child, all of it, "—for all of them. My failure is the foundation of their lie."

Emrys's throat was tight. "What are you asking me?"

"Nothing." Ilsa's voice dropped to something barely above the wind. "That's what I'm asking. Nothing. Don't restore me. Don't put me back on any throne, don't rebuild the kingdom I poisoned from its seat, don't resurrect Thornfell as it was, because as it was is the problem. If the Door opens and you carry those relics through it, the crown will offer itself to you the way it offered itself to me—gently, reasonably, as if refusing would be irresponsible. And you will feel how sweet it is. Everyone does. That's how it works." She took Emrys's marked arm in both her ghostly hands, and where she touched the sigil the cold sank all the way to the bone. "So I am begging you, heir of my heirs, last salt of a drowned country: whatever else you do on the thirteenth night—do not save me. Unmake me. Unmake the crown. Let these people go free, even if freedom means letting them fade. Better to end as released souls than to continue as the raw material of other people's empires."

"But if you fade—"

"We have been dead a hundred years, child. We are very good at it." She smiled, and this time it reached her eyes, and it was terrible and beautiful and sad as a song sung at a grave by the person in the grave. "What we cannot survive is being *useful* again. To the Ledger. To the Crown. To you, however kindly you mean it."

Emrys looked down at the village—at the lights that weren't lights, the smoke that climbed forever, the child asleep with her wooden horse—and felt compassion and duty grind against each other like millstones with nothing between them to be ground. Everything in them that had walked into this country wanting to *fix* it recoiled from what the queen asked. And everything in them that had spent a lifetime staying unnamed, unseen, ungoverned, recognized the truth in her words the way a lock recognizes its key.

"I don't know if I can promise," they said finally. "I don't know what I'll want when the crown is in reach."

"No," Ilsa agreed softly. "You don't. That's what makes you honest." She rose, and the darkness took her edges first. "But I can tell you what Maren Coldharbour's grandmother told me, the night before the treaty was signed, when I still had time to choose differently. She said: *Majesty, power isn't taken or given. It's agreed upon. And agreements can be refused.*" The queen's form was nearly gone now, woven into the dusk. "Refuse, saltborn. Refuse beautifully. Refuse for all of us."

Then she was only the smell of rain and the impression of a crown, and Emrys sat alone on the bench above the ghost-village, the circlet cold against their spine, watching the dead harvest a crop no one would ever eat.

Behind them, footsteps on the path—Sable, climbing, arms wrapped against the chill, stopping a careful few feet away.

"Well?" Sable said. "Do we go get your chalice?"

Emrys stood. Far to the west, the drowned cathedral's spire broke the surface of its black mere like a tooth. And beyond it, on the southern horizon, small and patient as handwriting, campfires had appeared in the pass they'd come through—too many, too orderly, too late to be coincidence.

"Yes," Emrys said. "Before they do."
