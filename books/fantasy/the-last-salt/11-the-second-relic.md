## 11. The Second Relic

Sable Quill had stolen a great many things in their life—a saint's fingerbone from a reliquary in Vess, a tax collector's ledger that turned out to be more valuable than any of the coins it recorded, a wedding ring off the hand of a sleeping bride who, Sable still liked to believe, had been grateful. They were good at theft because they understood its first principle: you did not take what you wanted. You took what no one was watching.

Nobody watched drowned places.

The cathedral had gone under eighty years ago when the millers upstream broke the old weirs to flood a valley the Crown wanted forgotten—another small erasure in the long ledger of them, though Sable hadn't known the word *ledger* meant anything yet, not then. Now it rose from the grey water like a drowned man's hand: spire tips breaking the surface, a rose window just beneath, its glass gone soft and green with silt so that light came through it in slow, dreaming columns. The locals called it the Belltower Deep and would not fish there. Sable could see why. The water held silence the way other water held fish.

"You're making that face," Emrys said beside them at the shoreline.

"What face?"

"The one where you're pricing something."

"I'm always pricing something." Sable crouched and let their fingers trail the water. Cold enough to ache. "It's a habit I'm trying to break, if you must know. You keep looking at me like everything I do is a confession waiting to happen."

"Is it?"

Sable grinned and did not answer, which was becoming, they noticed with some unease, their most frequent answer to Emrys Winterbourne. Three weeks on the road together, one chapel floor pried up, one circlet of black salt-iron wrapped in oilcloth against Sable's back, and already the thief had stopped lying about the small things—their name's true spelling, the scar under their jaw, how many knives they carried. It was the large thing they still lied about, and it sat in Sable's chest like a swallowed stone, growing heavier every mile north.

*They hired me before I ever met you.* Four words. Sable had rehearsed them in a hundred voices and could not make one of them survivable.

Emrys waded in first, as Emrys always did first, testing depth with a walking staff until the water climbed past the salt-mark on their forearm. That mark still unsettled Sable—the sigil faintly luminous beneath the skin, like a coin held behind flesh. Since the chapel, magic had begun finding Emrys the way iron filings find a lodestone. Moths landed on them. Rivers slowed around their ankles. Once, in a market town, an old woman had pressed her palm flat over her heart and bowed, weeping, before Emrys could speak—and Sable had watched the exact moment Emrys understood that invisibility was a garment they'd never wear again.

"The book says the chalice rests beneath the high altar," Emrys said. "Underwater, obviously, because Thornfell can't give anyone anything the easy way."

"Obviously." Sable shrugged the coil of rope and bladder-floats off their shoulder. "I've dived wrecks. Cathedrals are easier—they hold still."

They went down together through green light, following the nave's spine, and Sable kept their eyes moving the way training demanded while part of their mind drifted upward through the cold, thinking about the letter that had started all this.

It had arrived fourteen months ago, wax-sealed, unsigned, payment enclosed in full—which alone should have warned them. *Find the child marked by salt,* it said. *Follow, do not touch. Report monthly to the drop at the Sign of the Weighed Coin.* There had been a sketch: a young runaway, sharp-faced, wary-eyed, hair cropped unevenly. Sable had tracked a hundred marks and knew the type: someone running from a name. Easy money. They'd found Emrys within two months, reported three times, taken their pay, and told themselves it was nothing—just watching, just a job—right up until the night the almanac's prophecy opened itself under Emrys's fingers and the whole shape of the work revealed itself, and Sable understood they weren't a tracker anymore. They were a shepherd. And shepherds deliver flocks to slaughterhouses or to pens; either way, the flock does not choose the gate.

So Sable had stopped reporting. Eleven weeks now since the last letter went into the drop. Somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic, whoever wrote in Royal Saltwork cipher had noticed the silence.

Their lungs began to burn. Above them the rose window poured its columns of light down through the drowned nave, and between the pillars moved shapes—not fish. Figures, faint as breath on glass, standing in rows where pews had been. Congregations of the drowned, keeping their eternal service. One of them turned its head as Emrys passed, and Sable saw its mouth move in something that might have been a hymn, might have been a warning.

The high altar rose out of the gloom, marble furred white with mineral bloom. Beneath it, a crypt gate stood open—as if the cathedral had been waiting.

Emrys went through first. Of course they did.

* * *

The crypt below the altar was dry.

That was the wrongness of it, Sable thought later, hauling themselves gasping onto stone that should have been fathoms underwater—a low vaulted chamber lit by veins of pale crystal in the walls, air cold and tasting of iron and old incense. Water stood in the doorway behind them like a wall, quivering but not crossing, held back by nothing visible. Eighty years of the lake pressing down, and here: a room kept, a room expecting.

On the altar slab lay the Salt Chalice.

It was smaller than Sable expected—cup-sized, plain, made of grey glassy stuff that wasn't quite glass and wasn't quite bone. Salt crusted its rim in a rime that glittered even in the crystal-light. It looked worthless. It looked, Sable's trained eye noted with professional horror, exactly like the kind of object that kills people who underestimate it.

"Don't touch it," Sable said, too fast.

Emrys glanced back, one eyebrow arching. "You've never said that about anything in your life. You once licked a cursed ring to see if it tasted like curses."

"It did. That's how I learned." But Sable didn't laugh, and neither did Emrys, because the room had begun to hum—a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the fillings, in the marrow. The crystals in the walls brightened by degrees. And on every surface, blooming like frost-flowers, salt began to grow.

It crept across the floor toward the altar in branching patterns, and Sable recognized the pattern with a lurch: the sigil. The same mark that lived now under Emrys's skin, drawn ten thousand times in salt, converging.

"It knows you," Sable whispered.

"I know," Emrys said. Their voice was steady, but their hands, reaching for the chalice, were trembling—and Sable saw that the tremble wasn't fear. That was the worst part. It was recognition. Homecoming. Emrys lifted the chalice from the altar and the humming resolved into something almost like a chord, almost like a choir, almost like—

The water wall broke.

It came into the crypt not as flood but as fist—one columned surge, deliberate, aimed—and it caught Emrys mid-turn and hurled them backward into the doorway, into the dark beyond, down. Sable lunged, caught a wrist slick as riverstone, and for one heartbeat held on. Then the lake pulled, the way lakes pull when they are old and have been robbed, and the wrist slid free, and Emrys Winterbourne went down into eighty years of drowned silence with a crown-circlet and a chalice of salt, and the door of water sealed behind them smooth as glass.

Sable stood in the empty crypt holding nothing.

Later—much later, in every retelling—they would say they didn't think. It was nearly true. What actually happened, in the space of perhaps four seconds, was that every lie Sable had built between themselves and the truth collapsed at once, like wet paper. Because there was no version of the world in which Sable Quill, professional survivor, let Emrys drown. There never had been. That was the thing the stone in their chest had been trying to tell them for eleven weeks: somewhere between the Sign of the Weighed Coin and the chapel floor, the job had become a person, and the person had become the only fixed point Sable had.

They dove.

The water took them like a mouth. Green dark, pressure singing in their ears, the rose window above a dim ghost of color. Where? Down the nave, past the standing congregation—figures turning now, all of them, faces lifting toward something sinking in their midst. Sable swam hard along the pillar line and saw it: far below, at the drowned crossing where transept met nave, a small bright struggle. Emrys, tangled—rope, robes, some fallen beam of the rood screen—with the chalice still clenched in one fist because of course it was, of course they wouldn't let go, and the salt-mark on their arm flaring like a signal fire under the water, and their body beginning that terrible slow curl that bodies make when the fight goes out of them.

Sable had cut drowning men from nets. They knew the arithmetic: maybe ninety seconds of consciousness left, less with the cold. They dove down the column of the crossing, kicked off a capital carved with saints whose faces had worn to anonymity, and got an arm around Emrys's chest from behind.

Emrys fought them. That was the rule of drowning—you fought your rescuer, blind and animal—and Sable took an elbow to the cheekbone, saw stars in green water, held on anyway, hauled upward with legs burning and lungs screaming. The tangle of beam and robe dragged at them both. Below, the congregation of the drowned had begun to move, drifting down the nave in their silent procession, arms half-lifted—not menacing. Reaching. Like parents on a platform watching a train leave.

*Let go,* Sable thought wildly at them. *For once in your hundred years, let go.*

And whether the dead heard or the lake merely tired, the beam's grip loosened—rotted wood sloughing apart like wet bread—and they rose. Up through the columns of window-light, up past the rose window's green sun, up into air and grey afternoon and the shocking ordinary noise of wind on water.

Sable got them both to the shallows, dragged Emrys onto the shingle, and rolled them sideways as the water came out of them in long heaving spasms. Then Sable knelt in the cold shallows, shaking, cheek bleeding, and watched the salt-mark under Emrys's skin pulse slowly, slowly, like a second heartbeat learning patience.

* * *

Emrys woke coughing, which Sable counted as a professional success.

They'd gotten a fire going—driftwood, oily and smoky, but it burned—and wrapped Emrys in both their cloaks, and now sat close enough to share warmth while the lake behind them went gold and violet with evening. The chalice lay wrapped in oilcloth beside the circlet. Two relics. Two pieces of a crown that Sable had read enough marginalia to fear.

"Thank you," Emrys rasped.

"Don't mention it. Genuinely. My reputation can't afford heroism." Sable fed the fire a stick. "How much do you remember?"

"Drowning. Mostly drowning." Emrys pushed damp hair from their face. "There were people in the water. Standing in the church. They weren't trying to hurt me. They were—" A pause. "Reaching. Like they wanted to come with me."

"They reached for me too, after you dropped. All of them, moving at once." Sable kept their eyes on the fire. "This whole country does that around you. Ghosts, rivers, salt. Everything leans toward you like you're the only warm thing left in the room."

"That should worry us."

"It worries me constantly." Sable turned the stick in their fingers. The moment was here. It had been here for weeks, and every mile had made it worse, and now Emrys was alive and breathing and looking at them with those too-perceptive eyes, and there was no road left that didn't go through the truth. "Emrys."

"You're doing your serious voice. I hate your serious voice. Nothing good has ever followed it."

"Fourteen months ago I got a letter." The words came out flat, rehearsed-at-last, each one placed like a foot on ice. "Unsigned. Payment in full. Find the child marked by salt. Follow, don't touch, report monthly. There was a sketch of you." Sable finally looked up. "I found you in two months. I reported three times. To a drop box in Marrowgate. And then I stopped reporting, and I've been lying to you ever since about why I was on that road when we met."

The fire cracked. Out on the water, a bird cried once and was answered by silence.

Emrys didn't move. Didn't blink, even. Just looked at Sable with an expression Sable couldn't read at all, which frightened them more than anger would have.

"Hired by whom?" Emrys said quietly.

"The letters were cipher. Royal Saltwork script—same family as the prophecy book, Maren's notation confirmed that much before—" Sable stopped. Swallowed. "Before I burned her cottage rather than leave evidence. I know. I know what that cost."

"You said you found that page already translated. In a market archive."

"I lied. About that, and about how long I'd known the mark on your arm would show you to anyone with the sight, and about—" Sable laughed, a short ugly sound. "Stars, there's a lot of inventory. But the sum is this: someone has been paying to have you found. Not killed—found. Followed. Delivered. Every relic you take, every turn of the moons you walk, someone has known exactly where you were because I told them. Until eleven weeks ago. Then I went quiet, and I don't imagine they've forgiven the silence."

Emrys looked at the lake for a long time. When they spoke again, their voice had gone very controlled, the control itself a kind of grief. "The chapel ward. The mark. Did you know it would trigger?"

"No. I swear it on whatever I have left worth swearing on."

"But you knew who I was. From the start. The almanac, the date circled—all of it, and you stayed anyway, and you never said."

"I stayed because—" Sable's voice failed, restarted. "Because somewhere between Marrowgate and the chapel I stopped working for whoever writes those letters and started working for you. And I didn't say it because the truth was a debt I couldn't pay, and I thought—I stupidly thought—if I just kept you alive long enough, the debt would quietly cancel itself." They spread their hands. Blood from their split cheek had dried brown on their knuckles. "That's the confession. All of it. Do what you want with me. You'd be justified."

Emrys was quiet for so long that Sable began cataloguing exits out of pure reflex. Then:

"Maren used to say something," Emrys said at last. "About trust. She said trust isn't given or earned—it's spent. You spend it on someone and watch what they build with it." Their eyes came back to Sable's, and there was fury in them, banked but real, and something else underneath the fury that Sable would spend years trying to name. "You spent my life without asking. That's not nothing, Sable. I'm not going to pretend it's nothing because you pulled me out of a lake."

"No," Sable said. "You shouldn't."

"But." Emrys picked up a pebble and threw it into the darkening water. "You also dove into a drowned cathedral after someone you'd betrayed, knowing the dead were down there, knowing you might not come back up. People don't do that for a paycheck. People barely do it for love." Another pebble. "So here's what happens. You stay. And every day you choose to stay, honestly, no more drops, no more letters, no more clever omissions—the debt gets smaller. And the day I catch you choosing the other side, the debt comes due all at once. Agreed?"

Sable let out a breath they felt they'd been holding for fourteen months. "Agreed."

"And Sable? Whoever wrote those letters—whoever sketched my face and paid in advance—" Emrys turned to look east, where the mountains stood black against the last light, and the salt-mark on their arm caught the fireglow like buried lightning. "They'll send another. Or come themselves. We have one relic to go, and Maren to find, and eight turns of the moon, and now we also have an enemy who knows our road better than we do because you drew them the map."

"I did," Sable said simply. "So we walk a road they don't expect. Starting tonight, moving south instead of north."

Emrys blinked. "South? Everything points south—"

"Exactly. Nobody hunts you where they've already decided you aren't." Sable stood, wincing, and offered a hand. "Trust spent, Winterbourne. Watch what I build with it."

Emrys took the hand, and pulled themselves up, and for a moment they stood together in the firelight, two liars freshly converted to truth, while behind them the drowned cathedral settled back into its long silence—and beneath the water, unseen, in the dark of the crossing where the beam had rotted apart, the congregation of the dead turned as one toward the southern hills, and began, soundlessly, to follow.
