## 12. Maren's Last Lesson

They found her in the last place Emrys would have thought to look: not hiding, not running, but settled.

The hospice house at Greyhollow clung to the shoulder of a hill like a barnacle, all sagging thatch and walls patched with river clay, and it smelled of boiled willow bark and beeswax and the particular staleness of rooms where people came to stop being alive. Emrys stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, Sable shifting behind them with the restless energy of a person who distrusted stillness on principle.

"You're sure this is her?" Sable murmured.

Emrys didn't answer. They were looking at the small figure propped upright against pillows by the window—the only bed in the room, angled so its occupant could watch the road. Watching the road. Of course she was watching the road. Maren had always been three moves ahead of everyone, including death, and if she'd guessed Emrys would come north along the ferryman's route, then she had chosen the room with the window deliberately.

Her hair was white now where it had been iron-grey. Her hands, folded on the blanket, were translucent at the knuckles, the veins showing through like roots under ice. But her eyes—her eyes were exactly as Emrys remembered them from ten years ago, sharp and brown and faintly amused, as if the whole world were a slightly disappointing student.

"Took you long enough," Maren said.

Emrys crossed the room in four strides and then stopped, suddenly afraid that touching her would break something—that she was held together now by habit and spite alone, and one hug might scatter her like ash.

Maren solved the problem by grabbing their wrist with surprising strength and hauling them down onto the edge of the bed. "Sit. You hover like a heron." She looked them over with clinical thoroughness—the salt-sigil visible even through their sleeve, the new hardness around their eyes, the prophecy book slung against their back. "Hm," she said, which from Maren could mean anything.

"Maren—your cottage—I saw the smoke from the ridge, I thought—" Emrys's throat closed. "I thought you'd died."

"I did die, a little. The lungs mostly." She coughed, a wet, rattling sound that went on too long, and waved off Emrys's hand when they reached for her. "Inquisitor Vane is thorough. I'll give him that. Burned my library, my stills, forty years of notes." A dry smile. "He missed my second cellar. Witches keep second cellars the way smugglers keep second names. I got out with what mattered and took to the road until the road got tired of me. Greyhollow's sisters took me in. They ask no questions here. It's one of the few places left that doesn't."

"I should have come sooner. I should have—"

"You should have done nothing of the kind." Maren's grip tightened. "If you'd come sooner, you'd have led him straight to me, or me to you, and either way we'd both be cinders. You ran north. Good. That was the first smart thing you've done since you were nine years old."

Behind them, Sable cleared their throat. "I'll just—check the horses."

"No, you won't." Maren's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and Sable froze halfway to the door. "You'll sit down in that chair and listen, because you're the reason my apprentice didn't drown in a flooded cathedral, and because anyone fool enough to travel with a Winterbourne needs educating. Sit."

Sable sat. Emrys had never seen anyone obey Maren that fast. They made a mental note to enjoy it later, when there was a later.

* * *

The light moved across the floor while Maren talked—or rather, while she interrogated. She wanted everything: the almanac, the cipher, the ferryman, the village that paid taxes to no one, the chapel heist, the ward-mark blooming on Emrys's skin, Vane's bargain, the chalice, the drowning, Sable's confession. Emrys gave it to her in pieces, some of it haltingly. When they reached the part about the Ledger—the clerks who had written a century-old prophecy to manufacture an heir and an erasure both—Maren went very still.

"So it's true," she said softly. "I found fragments, years ago. Ledger marginalia in a Saltwork primer I bought for six coppers and never could decipher. I told myself it was administrative nonsense." She laughed, which turned into coughing. "Administrative nonsense. The most dangerous thing ever written, and I filed it under *boring*."

"They wrote me," Emrys said. "Before I existed. They named me in a book and set hunters on my trail and burned your home over it, and I never agreed to any of it."

"No," Maren said. "You never did."

"And now they've offered me the crown outright. Restore Thornfell, end the border wars, be their lawful king. And every day the circlet sits in my pack and whispers that it would be *easy*, that I'd be *good* at it, and the worst part is I think it's right. I think I would be good at it. That's what frightens me—not failing. Succeeding."

Maren was quiet for a long time. Outside, rain had begun, soft against the thatch. One of the hospice sisters passed the door with a candle, nodded to them, moved on without curiosity.

"Come here," Maren said finally. "Closer. My voice doesn't carry like it used to, and this next part matters more than anything I've ever taught you, including how to read a false trail and which mushrooms lie."

Emrys leaned in. Up close, Maren smelled of herbs and smoke and underneath it something faintly mineral—salt, Emrys realized. Old salt. She had been carrying it all these years, a pinch of Thornfell in a locket or a pouch somewhere, and had never once mentioned it.

"Everything you've been told about power is a lie," Maren said, "and I don't mean the pretty lie. I mean all of them. The Crown says power comes from bloodline. The Ledger says power comes from law, from ink and witness and proper form. The Inquisition says power comes from faith. The circlet on your hip says power comes from taking, and the ghosts say power comes from being given. All lies. Every one."

"Then where does it come from?"

"Agreement." Maren's eyes glittered. "That's the whole secret, child, the one the powerful spend fortunes burying: power isn't taken or given. It's *agreed upon*. Every crown in history is a story two parties tell each other—one agrees to lead, the other agrees to follow—and the moment either party stops agreeing, the crown is just metal. The Ledger understands this perfectly; it's why they need you alive and *willing*. A puppet dragged by strings rules nothing. They need you to agree, publicly, beautifully, with witnesses. They need your consent the way a fire needs air."

"The thirteenth moon," Emrys said slowly. "The Door opens once. Only the last salt may choose what passes through."

"Choose. Yes. And notice how everyone who wants something from you frames it as a choice between their option and their other option. Restore or renounce. Take the crown or refuse the inheritance. Those aren't choices, Emrys. Those are the same cage with two doors painted on it." She lifted one translucent hand and tapped Emrys's sternum, once, gently. "There is a third thing. There is always a third thing. Refusal isn't just saying no to the offer—it's saying no to the *frame*. No to the agreement itself. Walk out of the room where the deal is being struck, burn the contract, scatter the witnesses' certainty. Power that requires agreement can be starved by refusal. Not defeated—starved. Slowly. By people who simply stop consenting."

"That sounds like chaos."

"It sounds like justice," Maren said, "which looks like chaos from inside the cage." She sank back against the pillows, breathing harder now, and Emrys saw how much the speech had cost her. "The ghosts of Thornfell have been paying taxes to nobody for a hundred years because somebody forgot to collect their consent. The treaty dissolved the kingdom, but nobody asked the dead, and nobody asked the land. So the land kept agreeing—with itself. Phantom fields. Phantom ferries. The kingdom persists because somewhere, deep down, it never signed the dissolution. Do you understand? Even erasure is a kind of agreement, and even that one can be refused."

Emrys sat with that. Rain darkened the window. Somewhere below, a sister sang a work-song with no words in it.

"And if I unmake it?" Emrys asked quietly. "If I dissolve it truly—if I return the power and break the crown and close the Door forever. Is that agreement or refusal?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest and proudest expression Emrys had ever seen on a human face.

"That, my dear," she said, "is the question I'm leaving you. I'm an old woman with burnt books and wet lungs. I can teach you how to see the trap. Walking through it—that's yours."

* * *

She worsened after nightfall.

The sisters came and went with tinctures that smelled of opium poppy and something sharper, and each time they left, the rattle in Maren's chest was louder. Sable dozed in the chair with their boots on, having been formally informed that leaving was not among their options. Emrys sat on the bed beside their mentor and held her hand, careful of how thin it had become, thinner even than the morning.

"Tell me about Thornfell," Emrys said. "The real Thornfell. Before."

"Greedy," Maren murmured, eyes closed. "No—the kingdom wasn't greedy. The crown was. That's the lesson of Saltwork history, the one the Royal cipher was designed to hide. Thornfell was ordinary. Farmers, fishers, tin mines, quarrels over mill rights, weddings. A country like any country. Then someone forged the Salt Crown, and it whispered to whoever wore it, and within three generations the kingdom existed only to feed the crown's appetite. Wells poisoned—no wait, that came later, that was the Ledger's sin—but before that, levies and purges and a queen who drowned her own council when they disagreed." Her hand tightened on Emrys's. "Queen Ilsa was the last and the least of them. Weak enough to sign whatever the Ledger put in front of her. Don't let the ghosts romanticize her. Dead things remember themselves kindly."

"She begged me not to restore her."

"Good girl," Maren said. "Even at the end, she knew."

The candle guttered. Emrys fed it wax and watched the flame steady.

"Maren. Why did you take me in? Ten years ago. A runaway apprentice with no name worth writing down. Why?"

Maren opened her eyes. For a moment she seemed to look past Emrys, at something on the far wall, or the far side of a century.

"Because you asked," she said. "You stood in my doorway soaked to the bone and you said, *please teach me*, and you meant it. Most people want to be given things. You wanted to be taught. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." Her breath caught, steadied. "And because the first time I touched your hand, I tasted salt. Old sea-salt, in a valley a hundred miles from any coast. I knew what you were before you did. I hoped I was wrong. I taught you everything I could anyway, because if fate insists on using people, the kindest thing a teacher can do is make them dangerous to fate."

Emrys laughed despite the tears running freely down their face. "Dangerous to fate."

"Best ambition I know." She turned her head on the pillow. "Wake up, thief. You too."

Sable started awake, blinked, and had the grace to look ashamed of sleeping through a deathbed.

"Sable Quill," Maren said, studying them with the last of her strength. "You sold my apprentice to the Ledger for coin, and then you pulled them out of a flooded cathedral and have been guarding their sleep ever since. Which are you?"

"Both," Sable said hoarsely. "Working on it."

"Hm. Honest, at least. Listen, both of you, because I'm nearly done and I hate repeating myself." She gathered herself with visible effort, and for a moment her voice was the old classroom voice, the one that had drilled Emrys in herb-lore and lock-craft and the reading of weather. "Power is agreement. The Ledger holds its power because a hundred years of people agreed to believe their book. The Crown holds its power because a nation agrees to be governed. The circlet will hold *you* only as long as you agree to want it—and you must watch yourself, Emrys Winterbourne, because you are the sort of person who agrees easily, generously, with your whole heart. Your kindness is the door the crown walks through. Guard it."

"And when they come offering?" Emrys asked. "When the Ledger makes its final play?"

"Refuse the frame. Not the offer—the frame. Make them write a new prophecy. Make the world watch them do it." Her hand found the prophecy book where it lay on the blanket, and she pulled it toward her with trembling arms. It fell open across her lap, pages soft as cloth with age. "Now. Come here. Last lesson. Look."

Emrys leaned over the book. Maren's finger traced down the columns of Royal Saltwork cipher, past the framed prophecies, the thirteen turns, the diagrams of the Door—and stopped at the margin, where in faded brown ink someone had written a single line in a cramped clerk's hand:

*Let the last salt choose. Let the choosing bind.*

"There," Maren whispered. "Do you see it? The authors couldn't help themselves. They had to hedge. The prophecy gives you the choice—but the *choosing* binds you to whatever you pick. Restore, and you're crowned. Renounce, and you're erased, and the inheritance passes to whoever the Ledger appoints next. Either way, the book wins. Either way, the agreement stands." Her eyes lifted to Emrys's. "So don't choose inside it. Choose *against* it. Break the binding, not just the bargain."

"How?"

"How should I know?" Maren snapped—and then softened, her thumb brushing the page. "But I know this: a contract dies when its terms become impossible. And a prophecy is just a contract written in advance. Find the term they can't survive breaking, and break it *in public*." She began to laugh, low and delighted and terrible, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough became silence except for the rattle.

"Maren—"

"I'm not done." She pressed the book into Emrys's hands and folded their fingers closed over it. "One more thing. When I'm gone, burn me in the yard with rowan and salt, none of your sentimental weeping, and put a pinch of ash in your left boot so I can keep tripping you away from bad decisions. And Emrys—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—the page. Keep it open to your page. Promise me. Whatever happens at the Door. Keep the book open to the page where they wrote your name. Let them watch you read yourself."

"I promise."

"Say it properly."

"I promise. Open to my page. Ash in my boot. Rowan and salt."

"Good girl," Maren said again, though she knew full well Emrys was neither girl nor boy, and had spent ten years correcting strangers on the point. From her it was not a mistake. It was a benediction in her own private language, and Emrys would have fought anyone who tried to translate it.

She died an hour before dawn.

It was quiet, in the end—no drama, no last revelation, just a long exhale as if she'd finally set down something heavy she'd been carrying up a very long hill, and then the small, ordinary stillness that follows. The candle flame bent toward the window and stood straight again. Rain stopped.

Emrys sat holding her hand until it cooled, and then a while longer, memorizing it: the ink stains that had never quite washed out, the burn scar from a still that exploded in Emrys's ninth year, the callus on the middle finger from decades of quills and needles and picking locks for farmers locked out of their own barns by cruel landlords.

Sable stood at the foot of the bed, hat in their hands, uncharacteristically silent. At last they said, "She was something."

"She was everything," Emrys said. "And she was mine."

* * *

They burned her at noon, in the hospice yard, with rowan and salt as instructed. The sisters sang their wordless song. Half of Greyhollow came to stand at the wall and watch, because word traveled fast in small places, and apparently half the town owed Maren debts of one kind or another—a cured fever, a broken curse, a landlord persuaded to see reason. Emrys listened to them murmur her name with something like awe and understood, finally, what Maren had meant.

Maren had never held power. Had owned one cottage and forty books and a temper. And yet here were sixty people grieving her, because power was agreement, and Maren had spent her life making small, stubborn agreements with ordinary people—I'll teach you, you'll learn; I'll cure you, you'll pay what you can; I'll shelter you, you'll shelter someone else. An entire kingdom built of handshake-sized promises, invisible to every ledger ever kept.

Emrys scattered the ash and kept a pinch for their boot.

That evening they sat in the hospice kitchen with the prophecy book open on the table, open to the page where the Ledger had written *Emrys Winterbourne* a hundred years before Emrys was born. Sable sat across from them sharpening knives, which Sable did when thinking, and the whetstone's rhythm filled the silence like a slow heartbeat.

"The thirteenth turn," Emrys said. "Two turns left. Maybe less—the ferryman counted differently than the book. We need to reach the Door before they do, and they'll have every clerk in the order on the road by now."

"Then we ride tomorrow." Sable tested an edge against their thumb, satisfied. "What's the plan?"

Emrys looked down at the page. Their own name stared back at them, patient as a trap. Somewhere south, the Ledger was drafting its final offer, and somewhere in their pack the circlet hummed its sweet suggestion, and somewhere ahead a cliff face slept over a seam that opened once.

*Maren,* Emrys thought. *Open to my page. Let them watch me read myself.*

"We're going to give them a choice they didn't write," Emrys said, and closed their hand flat over the name. "Get your knives ready, Quill. Tomorrow we go make a prophecy impossible."

Far to the south, in a hall Emrys had never seen, a clerk dipped a pen and began to write.
