## 13. The Ledger's Summons

The invitation came on paper worth more than the town it was delivered to.

Emrys turned it over twice before they believed it. Thick cream stock, heavy as a shingle, edged in gilt that caught the lamplight of Maren's borrowed kitchen—the cottage Sable had found for them three days after the chapel, tucked behind a dyer's yard where the smell of mordant covered their trail from every hound but one. The seal pressed into its wax was not the Crown's crossed keys. It was a ledger-line: a single ruled stroke with small precise numbers marching along it like ants.

*You are summoned,* the letter began, *to an accounting.*

"They don't even pretend anymore," Sable said, reading over Emrys's shoulder. Their chin nearly touched Emrys's hair; they had stopped pretending to keep distance weeks ago, and Emrys had stopped noticing. "An accounting. Like you're a debt."

"I am a debt," Emrys said. "That's rather the whole point of me."

The letter named a place and a time. Harrowgate, the border town where the King's Road gave up and the mountain tracks began—a market town of slate roofs and stubborn people, famous for nothing except that three kingdoms' worth of treaties had been signed in its moot-hall and none of them had held. On the last day of the month, at the autumn assembly, when the whole valley gathered to settle tithes and grudges before winter closed the passes.

*Bring the relics or come without them,* the letter said. *Either way, come. What is owed will be settled in the open, before witnesses, as all lawful things must be.*

At the bottom, in a different hand—smaller, drier, a clerk's hand worn smooth by decades of entries—someone had written a single line beneath the formal text:

*They will offer you everything. Count what it costs them to give it.*

No signature. But Emrys knew handwriting the way other guides knew footprints, and Vane's was unmistakable: upright, joyless, each letter a small stone laid carefully on the next.

"He's warning me," Emrys said slowly.

"He's playing some game." Sable took the letter, held it near the lamp as if light might wring out its secrets. "Or he's cracking. People crack, Emrys. Even grey ones."

"Maren cracked too, at the end. She broke open and something true fell out." Emrys touched the locket at their throat—empty now, waiting—and thought of the circlet wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of their pack, and how, some nights, they could feel it through the canvas like a sleeping animal's warmth. "We go."

"We could not go."

"They'd burn Harrowgate to make the point. They burned Maren's cottage for less." Emrys stood, and the salt-sigil on their collarbone pulsed once, cold and bright under their shirt, as if the mark itself had opinions about the matter. "Besides. I want to see their faces when they say it."

* * *

Harrowgate in assembly-week was a town holding its breath. Bunting sagged between the eaves, half-hearted festival colors gone grey with coal smoke. Farmers drove lean cattle through the gate; tinkers shouted prices; children wove between cartwheels stealing apples. And everywhere, threaded quiet through the ordinary noise, the Inquisitors.

Not hunting this time. Standing post. Grey coats at the corners, grey cloaks on the rooftops, their faces empty as unsent letters. Emrys counted eleven before they reached the moot-hall square and made themselves stop counting, because counting felt too much like the old work—mapping exits, weighing odds—and today they wanted no exits. Today there was only the thing they had come to do.

Sable walked beside them wearing their best stolen coat, emerald wool, and kept up a low stream of commentary designed entirely to keep Emrys's hands steady. "That man's selling a bear. An actual bear. If we die today I want it noted I never got to see the bear fight anyone." A pause. "Your sigil's showing."

Emrys glanced down. The salt-mark had crept past their collar, pale lines like frost-ferns spreading along the side of their neck. Each use of the relics fed it; each day it grew a little on its own, patient as lichen. In the chapel, it had been a stamp. Now it was becoming a map—one Emrys did not recognize yet, but suspected ended somewhere inside Thornfell's drowned borders.

"Let it show," Emrys said.

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's usually both."

The moot-hall was older than any kingdom currently claiming the valley, built of black oak beams that had gone silver with age, and its great doors stood open to the assembly crowd. Inside, benches in tiers around a stone floor worn concave by centuries of argument. The townsfolk packed the galleries. Elders sat in the high chairs—not lords, Harrowgate had never tolerated lords, just seven gray-haired arbiters with staves of office—and beside them, uninvited and unmistakably in charge, sat five strangers in undyed linen robes.

Clerks. Emrys knew them now from Vane's stolen notes. No weapons. No insignia. Just ink-stained fingers and eyes like closed account-books, waiting to be opened.

The hall hushed when Emrys entered. Not the hush of fear—or not only that. The hush of a town that had heard rumors for a season: the salt-marked child, the ghost country, the Door. Rumors traveled the passes faster than riders. Emrys walked the length of the floor with the weight of two hundred stares and set down their pack upon the moot-stone, deliberately, the way one lays a bet.

A woman rose from among the clerks. Elderly, spare, her robe immaculate, her voice pitched to carry without strain—a voice trained by generations of reading verdicts aloud.

"Emrys Winterbourne." Not a question. A confirmation of entry in a ledger. "Once apprentice to a hedge-witch of the southern valleys. Once guide to smugglers. Now—" she paused, tasting it, "—heir of Thornfell. Last of the salt line. We have been keeping your place a long time."

"You've been keeping my place," Emrys said, "the way a jailer keeps a cell."

A ripple through the galleries. One of the arbiters rapped a stave, though whether against Emrys or the clerks was unclear.

"We are the Order of the Ledger," the woman continued, unmoved. "For a hundred years we have administered the Thornfell settlement. We dissolved a kingdom lawfully. We wrote the terms of its ending, and we wrote—" here she inclined her head toward the pack, toward the oilcloth shape within, "—the prophecy that would one day produce its lawful heir. Every step accounted for. Every outcome foreseen. You are not an accident, child. You are the final entry."

"And now?"

"Now the border wars end." She spread her hands, and behind her the other clerks produced documents—real ones, Emrys saw, seals and ribbons, the genuine machinery of state. "The Crown cedes all claim to the Thornfell territories. Our order dissolves itself. The ghosts of Thornfell are freed from treaty-limbo by royal decree. The Door opens under legitimate authority instead of smuggler's luck. All of it—" her voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty, "—in exchange for one thing. Take up the crown. Be restored. Be king of Thornfell, first and last of your line, and let the world finally have a name for what you are."

The silence afterward had texture. Emrys felt it press against their skin like deep water.

And beneath the silence, sweet as honey off a warm spoon, the circlet whispered from its wrapping. *Say yes.* It didn't use words. It used wanting. Emrys's own wanting, returned to them polished and enormous: the image of walking out of this hall crowned, of Sable's hand on their arm, of Maren's face if she could see it, of never again being nobody, unnamed, unrecorded, a smudge on the world's ledger—

Emrys laughed. It came out harsher than intended, and it rang off the silvered beams.

"Oh," they said. "Oh, it's beautiful. You've done beautiful work."

"Child—"

"No. Let me count, since we're doing accounts." Emrys stepped forward, and the salt-sigil blazed visible at their throat, frost-white, and nobody in the hall looked away. "You'll give me a kingdom. What does it cost you? Nothing. The Crown gives up land it can't hold and wars it's losing. Your order dissolves itself—which means the evidence of what you did dissolves with it. The wells you poisoned. The treaty you wrote at poison-point. A century of clerks' neat little crimes, all filed shut under 'amnesty.'" They turned, slowly, addressing the galleries now, the farmers and tinkers and slate-cutters. "They're offering me a crown the way you'd offer a rat a trap full of bread."

Murmurs. An arbiter leaned forward. The elderly clerk's composure did not crack, but her ink-stained fingers went still.

"A puppet doesn't know it's wood," Emrys went on. "That's the craftsmanship. You'd have me restore Thornfell—but restored *by your decree*, lawful under *your* law, existing because *you* permitted it. Every ghost freed on your paperwork. Every border drawn by your pen. And the moment I rule contrary to your interests, you'd write a new prophecy, find a new heir, and dissolve my kingdom a second time—with a better ending, one that reads cleaner in the histories." Their voice dropped, and carried anyway. "You didn't offer me a throne. You offered me a leash with a crown for a collar. And you did it in front of witnesses, because you wanted everyone to watch me take it and call it justice."

The hall was absolutely silent now. Somewhere above, a raven muttered on the roofbeam.

"So here is my counter-entry." Emrys opened the pack. Did not take out the circlet—only laid a bare hand flat on the wrapped bundle, and let the salt-light bleed up between their fingers so the whole hall could see it. "I refuse."

The word landed like a dropped anvil.

"Not renunciation—they taught me the difference, and thank you for the education. Renunciation means the inheritance goes back to whoever holds the ledger. Refusal means it goes to no one. I am not giving Thornfell back to you. I am not taking it for myself. On the thirteenth turn, when the Door opens, I will walk through it carrying every relic of that drowned kingdom, and I will unmake the inheritance entire—the crown, the prophecy, the debt, all of it—returned to the dead who earned it and denied to every living hand that schemed for it. Including mine."

"Unmake it?" The elderly clerk's voice lost its trained smoothness for the first time. "The kingdom's power cannot simply be *destroyed*—"

"Everything can be destroyed. That's the one prophecy I believe." Emrys picked up the pack and slung it on. "You wrote me as your final entry. Fine. Here's my amendment, written in front of two hundred witnesses and whatever ravens report to whom: *the last salt refuses the crown, refuses the ledger, and closes the account forever.*"

They turned for the doors.

It should have ended there. Later, Emrys would understand why it didn't—that power never concedes in public, that cornered orders reach for the tools they swore they'd retired. The youngest clerk moved first, hand slipping into his robe, murmuring a syllable of binding-script, and the air over the moot-stone thickened like cooling wax. Emrys felt it seize at them, at the sigil, a hook of borrowed law sinking toward their spine.

They didn't think. The circlet answered through the oilcloth, through the leather, through skin and bone, and Emrys *agreed* to it—one heartbeat's agreement, Maren's lesson turned weapon: *power isn't taken, it's agreed upon*—and spoke the refusal louder, with the salt behind it.

"*No.*"

The binding-script shattered like ice on a pond. The young clerk staggered back, nose bleeding. Every lamp-flame in the moot-hall burned white for a single breath, and the frost-map on Emrys's neck crawled another inch toward their jaw.

Then quiet. Then the galleries erupted—not in panic. In cheering. Slate-cutters on their feet, farmers pounding bench-backs, an old woman in the front row weeping openly and shouting something in the old valley dialect that Emrys didn't understand but felt in their chest like a struck bell. Harrowgate had signed three treaties in this hall and watched all three rot. Its people knew what paper promises weighed.

The arbiters rose as one body. The eldest, a woman with a shepherd's weathered face, planted her stave on the moot-stone with a crack like a closing door.

"The assembly has witnessed," she declared, in the tone of a law older than any Ledger. "Witnessed and *recorded*. Let it be entered: the heir of Thornfell appeared at Harrowgate assembly and refused the crown before the people. So witnessed. So entered. Let any who dispute it dispute us."

The elderly clerk stared at the stave, at the cheering galleries, at Emrys. Something moved behind her eyes—the first crack in a century of smooth pages. "You understand," she said quietly, "that we cannot permit an unclaimed inheritance to exist. If you will not wear the crown, someone else must. Or no one must—and we have ways to ensure—"

"I know your ways. I've read your notes. Ask your friend Vane how well they hold."

Something flickered across her face at his name. Doubt, Emrys thought. Or fear.

Outside, dusk had fallen blue and cold over Harrowgate. Sable fell into step beside them, emerald coat brilliant against the grey, and for once had nothing clever to say until they were clear of the square.

"That," they said finally, "was the most terrifying thing I've ever seen, and I once watched you fight a river."

"It gets worse."

"How?"

Emrys touched the sigil at their throat. Beneath the fingertips, the frost-lines had shifted—no longer random ferns, but pattern. Letters, maybe. Or borders. The outline of a coastline that existed nowhere on any living map.

"Because I meant every word," Emrys said, "and the crown *still* tasted sweet. Come on. Eleven days to the thirteenth turn, and I need to tell you what I'm about to ask of you—because if the corruption takes me before the Door does, you're the one who has to finish it."

Behind them, in the moot-hall, the clerks were already writing.
