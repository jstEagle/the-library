## 14. Turn Twelve

The high passes had a way of making you small.

Emrys had guided caravans through the Thornfell passes since they were fourteen, and they knew every trick of them—the way the wind funneled through the Col of Whistling Stones and could be heard three valleys off, the way snow lied about depth, the way a path that looked like mercy in summer became a jaw of grey ice by first frost. They knew how to read the sky, how to read rock, how to read the silence that meant something with lungs was watching from above.

But they had never made this crossing with one turn left on a clock no one could see.

*One turn*, the ferryman had said. *Thirteen new moons, and the Door opens once.* Twelve had come and gone while Emrys lay awake beneath an overhang counting their own heartbeat, feeling the salt-sigil pulse faintly under their collarbone like a second, slower heart. The mark had spread since the chapel. What began as a pale shimmer at the base of their throat now climbed in fine crystalline lines toward their jaw, visible only when firelight caught it—or when magic was near.

Tonight, magic was near. It had been near for two days.

"They're herding us," Sable said. They crouched at the mouth of a defile where the trail narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for one mule, and Sable was not looking at the trail. They were looking up, at the ridgeline, where the last light was going out of the world in long grey bands. "You don't chase someone through country like this. You don't have to. You just close your hand slowly."

"Then we go fast."

"You can't outrun a closing fist." Sable turned, and in the blue half-dark their grin was there but thinner than usual, worn down at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands. "There's a fork ahead—old shepherd's route, drops into the valley south of here. If Vane's smart, he's already there. If he's clever, he's on the ridge with crossbows and lets the mountain do his killing."

"And if he's neither?"

Sable's grin flickered back to life, defiant. "Then we get lucky, and I finally buy you that drink I keep promising."

They moved at dusk, because Emrys trusted the dark more than they trusted any road. The sigil pulsed with each step upward, and Emrys hated that they'd begun to know its rhythms—the quick flutter when danger breathed close, the deep slow toll when power did. It was learning them as much as they were learning it. That was the thing no one warned you about inheritance: it wasn't a coat you put on. It was a root. It grew *in*.

The fork came at moonrise—a thin sickle of a moon, twelve days dead and reborn wrong, hanging over the pass like a paring knife. Left went down. Right went along the ridge, exposed, stupid, obvious.

Emrys stood at the split and felt the pull of both roads in their chest. The left-hand path smelled of stone and old snow and nothing else.

The right-hand path smelled faintly, impossibly, of salt water.

"That's bait," Sable whispered.

"I know."

"It's the most insulting bait I've ever seen."

"I know." Emrys stared up the ridge path, at the darkness where the sea-smell hung like a ghost's perfume. Thornfell calling its heir home through the mountains. Or Vane, who had learned by now exactly what the heir wanted most. "Which is why we take the left."

They took the left.

It was nearly the death of them, because Vane was not clever or smart. Vane was patient, and patience is the only trap that doesn't look like one.

* * *

The ambush came an hour past midnight, in a place called the Throat—a slot canyon where the walls leaned so close overhead that the stars were reduced to a jagged white seam. Emrys heard it before they saw it: not hoofbeats, not voices, but the soft *click-click-click* of crossbow cranks, six of them, spaced along the rim like the ticking of some vast cold clock.

"Sable," they breathed.

"I hear it."

Down-slope, torchlight bloomed. And there he was, standing in the middle of the trail below with his grey cloak open and his hands empty at his sides—Halvard Vane, alone, unarmed, looking up at them with an expression that was not triumph. It was worse. It was *weariness*. The face of a man doing arithmetic he'd done ten thousand times and always gotten the same answer.

"Emrys Winterbourne," he called up. His voice carried strangely in the canyon, flattened by the stone, coming from everywhere. "I'm not here to take you. I'm here to offer what I offered before. Walk away from the relics. Walk away from the Door. Renounce, and live."

"You burned Maren," Emrys said. The words came out flat and strange, like someone else's voice. Below them, torchlight shifted; riders moved into positions Emrys couldn't see but could feel, the sigil prickling along their skin like frost forming.

"Yes." No denial, no justification. Just the word, laid down like a stone. "And I would burn her again, because the alternative was worse. That's the lesson of the Ledger, child. Every cruelty is a smaller cruelty wearing armor. Come down. Renounce. Let this end with everyone alive."

"Everyone except Thornfell."

Vane was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, and for just a breath it sounded less like an Inquisitor and more like a tired clerk. "There is no Thornfell. There hasn't been for a hundred years. There's only a story the Ledger needs told correctly, and you are standing in the margin of it, bleeding."

Emrys opened their mouth—

—and Sable shoved them hard against the canyon wall as the first bolt sang out of the dark.

It came from the ridge, not from Vane. From the flank, where the cranking had been quietest, where no rider should have been positioned for a clean shot at the ledge. A second crossbow team. Hidden. Not part of the bargain Vane thought he was striking.

The bolt meant for Emrys's throat found Sable instead.

They went down together, Emrys catching Sable's weight as they folded, and the sound Sable made was small and astonished, like someone interrupted mid-joke. Blood spread warm and shocking across Emrys's hands. Too much blood. Far too fast.

"No—" Emrys lowered them behind a shelf of rock, pressing hard on the wound below the collarbone. "No, no, stay with me, you absolute thief, you don't get to—"

"Technically," Sable managed, grey-lipped, grin intact by sheer bloody-minded will, "I stole... that bolt. Right out of the air. For you. That has to count... toward something."

"Shut up. Shut *up*." Emrys tore the sash from their own waist, wadded it, pressed. The sigil flared under their collarbone, white-hot, and they felt it reaching—not just burning, but *reaching*, tasting the blood on their hands, tasting the pain, tasting the fear, and cataloguing all of it the way the Ledger catalogued everything.

Above, chaos. Vane's voice cracking across the canyon: "*Hold! HOLD—who gave the order?*" A pause, then colder, quieter, and somehow more frightening: "That was not my command."

From the ridge, a voice answered, dry as ledger-paper. "Neither was yours, Inquisitor. The Order thanks you for delivering them to the ground."

*The Order.* Here. In person. Watching Vane as surely as they watched Emrys—and finding him as disposable.

Something broke open in Emrys's chest then, and it was not grief, though grief was in it. It was not rage, though rage was in it too. It was the thing underneath both, the thing Maren had spent years teaching them to name and set down again: *power isn't taken or given, it's agreed upon.*

Well. Emrys was done agreeing.

They reached beneath their cloak and drew out the Salt Crown's circlet—cold iron twisted with white crystal, recovered from the chapel floor, carried for weeks like a coal wrapped in cloth. They had sworn they would never wear it until the Door. They had sworn it to themselves, to Sable, to the memory of Queen Ilsa's sorrowful ghost.

Sable's blood was soaking through their fingers.

Emrys set it on their brow.

* * *

The world changed.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. There was no thunderclap, no pillar of fire. The circlet settled against Emrys's temples like it had always belonged there, like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was built into their own skull, and the night simply *opened*. They could feel the canyon—all of it. Six crossbowmen on the rim, two hidden flankers, four riders below, Vane standing in the torchlight with his hands still empty and his face going carefully blank. They could feel the mountain's bones. They could feel the snowpack on the southern ridge, heavy, unstable, waiting for permission.

*Permission*, whispered something that was either the circlet or Emrys's own voice grown deep and unfamiliar. *You need only give it permission.*

The flankers reloaded. Emrys felt the crossbow strings draw taut as though drawn against their own skin.

*No,* Emrys thought—and the thought went out of them like a wave.

The bolts shattered in the air. Not deflected—*shattered*, bursting into grains of white that hissed down through the torchlight like the world's coldest snow. Somewhere on the ridge a man screamed and dropped his weapon, and Emrys understood distantly that the salt-grains had found his hands, his eyes, that they burned the way brine burns a wound, and Emrys felt—

—they felt *good*.

That was the horror of it. Not the power itself, but the taste of it, arriving sweet on the back of the tongue like honeyed wine after years of ditchwater. The circlet drank their fury and poured it back out refined, concentrated, *righteous*, and every grain of salt that stung an enemy tasted like justice, like the exact justice Emrys had been starving for since Maren's cottage went up in flames. This was what the crown offered. Not conquest. *Certainty.* The absolute, corrupting certainty that whatever you wanted was what the world needed.

The snowpack on the southern ridge groaned. Emrys felt it leaning over the riders below like a held breath, felt the circlet whispering *let go, let go, bury the men who burned her cottage, let the mountain do your killing*—

Below, Vane looked up. Through the falling salt and the torch-smoke, his eyes found the iron and crystal on Emrys's brow, and for the first time since the valley, Halvard Vane looked afraid.

"*Take it off,*" he called, and his voice cracked on it. "Child—hear me—that thing does not serve you. It served Ilsa. Do you understand? It *unmade her*. Whatever you're feeling right now—it's lying to you—"

Emrys raised one hand.

The avalanche held.

Every rider below froze, staring up at a slope of snow that should have come down and didn't, suspended by nothing but the will of a runaway apprentice standing blood-soaked on a ledge with a stolen crown on their head. The silence was total. Even the wind stopped, as if the pass itself had agreed—for once—to obey.

"Go," Emrys said. Their voice came out doubled, resonant, ringing off the canyon walls with harmonics no human throat should make. "Take your Order's crossbows and your Orders' orders and *go*. Tell the Ledger the thirteenth turn comes whether they attend or not. Tell them the last salt remembers what was done to Thornfell's wells. Tell them—"

The circlet pulsed, hungry for more, hungry for a threat, a curse, a judgment—

"—tell them I'm coming."

Vane held their gaze a moment longer. Something passed across his face—not defeat. Recalculation. Perhaps even, terribly, *hope*. Then he turned and walked down the trail without a backward glance, and his riders followed, and the hidden crossbows on the ridge withdrew into the dark, and the mountain exhaled.

* * *

Emrys took off the circlet.

It was the hardest single act of their life. Harder than fleeing Maren's cottage. Harder than watching it burn. Their hands shook doing it—not with exhaustion, though exhaustion was there, but with *want*. Every nerve in their body screamed as the iron lifted away, a grief so acute it bordered on nausea, the sweetness draining out of the world and leaving only the cold and the blood and the boy—no, the thief—bleeding in their arms.

They knelt. Pressed the sash tighter. The salt-lines on their skin had spread in the minutes the crown had been on—crystal filigree now climbing their throat, tracing the line of their jaw, one bright thread touching the corner of their eye. When they caught their reflection in a lens of ice beside the trail, a stranger looked back: crowned in white veins, eyes lit faintly from within, beautiful in a way that made Emrys want to break the ice.

"Hey." Sable's voice, cracked but conscious. Their hand found Emrys's wrist, grip feeble but insistent. "Hey. Look at me. Not the ice. Me."

Emrys looked. Blood-loss-pale, sweat-slick, utterly foolish grin.

"There you are," Sable whispered. "Knew you were in there somewhere. Behind the... terrifying weather goddess."

"The bolt—"

"Missed anything vital. Barely. Hurts like a sermon." Sable coughed, winced, kept hold of Emrys's wrist. "Listen. I saw your face. When it was on. I've seen that face before—on collectors, on the Ledger's buyers, on anyone who's ever held something that holds them back. So here's the deal. You carry that thing. I carry *this*—" they tapped their own temple weakly "—the memory of exactly what you looked like when you liked it. And when we get to the Door, if you start enjoying yourself, I remind you. Deal?"

Emrys laughed—a wet, broken sound—and pressed their forehead to Sable's uninjured shoulder, careful of the wound, careful of everything.

"One turn left," they said against the wool of Sable's coat. "Tomorrow night, the moon dies. And when it's reborn, the thirteenth turn begins."

"Then we rest tonight," Sable murmured, eyes drifting shut, "and tomorrow we walk into the last valley in the world. Together. And you do *not* put that thing on again unless the whole sky is falling."

Emrys bound the wound properly by firelight, fed Sable broth from the travel kettle, and sat watch through the small hours with the circlet wrapped in three layers of cloth at the bottom of their pack—where they could still feel it, faintly, patiently, pulsing like a heart waiting to be asked to beat.

Near dawn, sleep finally took them, and they dreamed of a door in a cliff face, and behind the door a kingdom green and whole, and on its throne a crown that whispered their name in Maren's voice.

They woke with salt on their lips and one turn left, and the mountains silent around them, holding their breath.
