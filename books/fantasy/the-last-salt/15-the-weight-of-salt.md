## 15. The Weight of Salt

Emrys had meant to take the circlet off before sleep.

They told themselves so as they banked the fire in the ruined shepherd's hut below the pass, as they spread their bedroll on stone that still remembered warmth from some older fire, as they lay down with the Salt Crown's circlet—a thin band of white metal, salt-bitten and cold—still resting in its cloth beside their head. They told themselves they would sleep with one hand's distance between it and their skin, the way Maren had taught them to sleep near things that watched.

But the thirteenth moon was coming, and the chalice was still two days north, drowned in a cathedral under a lake that did not appear on any map, and somewhere behind them Vane was licking his wounds and learning patience, and the circlet hummed.

It hummed like a struck glass. Like a word held in the mouth too long. Emrys reached for it without deciding to reach for it, and the metal was warm now, warmer than their hand, and it fit the brow the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, which is to say: completely, and with an audible click that no one else heard but that Emrys felt in their teeth.

The dream took them before the fire burned down to embers.

* * *

Thornfell, whole.

Not the ghost-country Emrys had walked—the pale fields, the tax-paying dead, the ferryman poling his boat across a river that ran backward through memory—but Thornfell alive, in summer, seen from above. From the throne's height. Because that was where the dream put them: not walking the streets of the salt-city but looking down on them from the high window of the royal hall, hands resting on arms of carved whalebone, wearing a body that was theirs and not theirs.

They were Queen Ilsa. Or they wore her seeing.

Below, the market squares glittered. Salt-merchants in blue wool cried their wares; children ran races along the canal walls; the great tide-bells rang the hour, and every soul in the city turned at the sound the way wheat turns to sun, unthinking, unified. It was beautiful. That was the first thing Emrys understood, and the thing they would spend the rest of the night trying to unlearn: it was beautiful, and everyone in it was happy, or something shaped enough like happiness that from this height there was no telling the difference.

*I built the canals,* said a voice inside Emrys's own skull, calm as deep water. *I set the bells. When the plague came up from the southlands I closed the passes and let three villages burn rather than risk the port, and the port survived, and the port thanked me.*

Emrys tried to pull back from the window. The body would not let them. The body loved the window.

*You think power is a crown,* the voice went on—Ilsa's voice, or the shape the crown remembered of Ilsa's voice, worn smooth by a century of dreaming. *It is not. Power is attention. To rule is to see everything and be seen by everything until the difference dissolves. Watch.*

The view shifted. Now Emrys stood—not sat, stood—in a courtroom, and a smuggler knelt below them, a woman with rope burns on her wrists, accused of running goods past the salt-tithe. And Emrys felt what the throne felt: not anger. Something far worse. Recognition. The woman had hidden roads in her head the way Emrys carried maps of them now, and the queen saw herself in her, small and sharp and unnamed, and could have saved her with a word.

She pronounced the tithe-law instead. The guards took the woman away. The court murmured approval, and the warmth of that murmur moved through the royal body like wine.

*Why?* Emrys asked, inside the dream, inside the borrowed throat. *You knew her.*

*I knew her,* the voice agreed, without shame. *That is why. A ruler who saves the ones she recognizes rules by favor, and favor is just another name for appetite. The law was colder. The law was fairer. So I told myself.* A pause, long as a tide going out. *I told myself for forty years.*

The dream folded like paper.

Now it was winter, the last winter. Wells gone bitter. Cattle dying at the troughs with white froth on their lips. Emrys-Ilsa rode through villages where people looked up at their queen with faces already arranged into the patience of the dying, and Emrys felt the thought arrive in the royal mind fully formed, dressed in reason, wearing mercy's coat: *Better to sign the treaty. Better to dissolve us gently than watch us rot. Better my name erased than my people's suffering made a monument.*

And beneath that thought, so deep the queen herself might never have found it if she'd dug all her remaining days: relief. Relief that there was a lawful door out of the weight of all that attention. Relief that someone else—the clerks with their ledgers, the southern envoys with their clean ink—would hold the pen.

*They poisoned the wells,* Emrys thought, hard, hammering the truth against the inside of the dream. *The Ledger did it. You signed your kingdom away to the men who killed it.*

*I know,* said the voice, and for the first time it cracked, salt in a wound. *I learned it after. I learned it dead. That is the joke the crown tells every night, little salt: I traded a living country for a lie because the lie came with a signature, and signatures were the only god I'd ever worshipped. Do you understand yet? The crown does not corrupt you with cruelty. It corrupts you with reasons.*

The dream turned again, faster now, years sliding past like cards: the signing, the grey ships, the last tide-bell rung once and never again, the map-makers' pens drawing the new border straight through where the heart of Thornfell had been, as if a line could kill a place—

—and then, at the very bottom of the century, a single image, held and lit like a jewel: a door. A seam in a cliff, and beyond it green hills and ringing bells and the whole drowned kingdom waiting, patient as seed, for a hand worthy—or unworthy enough—to open it.

Emrys woke with the taste of brine in their mouth and their own hand pressed flat against the circlet on their brow, fingers curled around it like a lover's.

Dawn light came grey through the broken roof. Their cheeks were wet. They did not remember crying, and that frightened them more than the dream had: that the crown could spend their grief while they slept, spend it like coin, without asking.

*It corrupts you with reasons.*

They sat up slowly. Their body ached in the shape of sitting a throne—shoulders back, spine lifted, hands resting at precise angles—as though a hundred nights of queenship had soaked into the muscle overnight. They got up, walked to the rain-barrel, and looked at their reflection in the black water.

The salt-sigil had spread. It had been a small mark on the chapel floor, a white shimmer at the collarbone. Now it climbed their throat in fine branching lines, like frost grown inward, like coral, like the veins of a leaf rendered in salt. It did not hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like coming home.

"Two days," Emrys said aloud, to the water, to themselves. "Chalice. Then the Door. Then it ends." The reflection said it with them, and the reflection sounded regal.

They wrapped the circlet back in its cloth, and their hands shook, and they noticed—they made themselves notice—that part of the shaking was reluctance.

* * *

Sable was propped against the hut's one whole wall, blanket over their legs, whittling nothing in particular out of a stick. The bolt-wound in their shoulder had gone from angry red to bruised purple, which Sable insisted was "the wound apologizing," and they had talked their way back onto their feet three days ahead of any healer's schedule because lying down gave them time to think, and thinking, Sable claimed, was the one activity they'd sworn off professionally.

Emrys stood in the doorway a long moment before coming in. Long enough that Sable looked up, and looked again, and put the stick down.

"All right," Sable said. "Whatever it is, you've got the face for it. I've seen that face on rich men about to confess to their accountants."

"I wore the circlet last night."

"You—" Sable stopped. Emrys watched them decide not to make a joke. It cost them visibly, like setting down a full purse. "All night?"

"I meant to take it off." Emrys crossed the room and sat down opposite, cross-legged, close enough that honesty couldn't hide behind distance. "I dreamed Thornfell. From the throne. Her whole reign, Sable. The good parts and the parts she buried. I felt her feel it. The law she used on a woman who reminded her of me. The wells. The treaty." They swallowed. "The relief. She was relieved, at the end. Someone else would hold the pen. I've spent my whole life wanting the opposite of that—to hold nothing, owe nothing, be written on by no one—and I woke up understanding her. That's what it does. Not cruelty. Reasons. It makes every terrible thing feel like the reasonable thing."

Sable was quiet. Outside, wind worked at the pass, worrying the rocks like an old debt.

"And?" Sable said finally. Gentle. Terrible. "What did it make feel reasonable when *you* woke up?"

Emrys had rehearsed this part on the walk down from the ridge, and the rehearsal fell apart anyway. "The chalice," they said. "I want it. Not need—want. All night I could feel the gap where it should be, like a tooth gone missing. The circlet is half a thing without it. Half a crown. And some part of me—the part that grew those marks up my throat—wants to complete the set. Wants to sit in the Door with everything in place and feel what whole feels like." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I caught myself planning how fast we could travel. Not should. Could. I was measuring my friend's fever against my schedule."

"That's not corruption," Sable said. "That's Tuesday. Everyone measures their friends against schedules."

"Sable."

"No, listen." Sable leaned forward, winced, kept leaning anyway. "You know what I did for ten years? Sold relics. Half of them fake, most of the rest worthless, and I lied about all of them cheerfully, and I slept like a baby with a full stomach. And then I met you, and I watched you find out you're the heir to a murdered kingdom, and here's the thing I keep noticing: you haven't changed. Not really. You still count coins twice. You still give the extra ration to the mule. Corrupt people don't do inventory on their own souls at dawn, Emrys. They don't sit down across from a thief and say *watch me.*"

"They do at first," Emrys said. "That's the trick of it. The crown doesn't take the person who watches. It waits until they stop."

Sable held their eyes. Whatever charm lived in that face settled, underneath, into something older and plainer.

"So don't stop," Sable said. "Give me the job. Formally. I'm unreliable, greedy, and I lie beautifully, but I have never once in my life failed to notice when something valuable was about to be stolen—including, apparently, people. You want watching? I'm watching. Rules?"

Emrys breathed out. Some knot behind their breastbone loosened by a fraction. "Three rules. One: if I start talking about the crown like it's a solution instead of a problem, interrupt me. Don't be polite. Politeness is how kingdoms die. Two: if I touch the relics outside of necessity—if I catch myself wearing the circlet when we're not traveling, or holding the chalice when it's not needed—you take it. Physically. I'll hate you. Do it anyway."

"And three?"

Emrys looked at their own hands, at the faint salt-shimmer crawling now past the collarbone, toward the shoulder, mapping them like a coastline being surveyed for annexation.

"If I ever tell you I've changed my mind," they said quietly, "if I stand in front of the Door and say I want to restore Thornfell after all—that I've found a reason, a good one, a *reasonable* one—you don't argue with me. You get the ghosts out. Every one of them. And then you let the Door shut with whatever's left of me on whichever side it shuts on." They looked up. "Swear it."

The wind filled the pause. Sable's jaw worked.

"You know," they said at last, "most partners ask for a share of the take."

"*Swear it*, Quill."

"I swear." No flourish. No grin. Sable spat in their palm, an old road-custom, and Emrys took it, and the grip was warm and human and briefly the only real thing in a world filling up with salt.

"There's a fourth rule," Sable added, retrieving their hand and their whittling stick both, retreating into motion the way they always did after sincerity. "You eat. Queens skip meals; guides don't. If the crown wants you, it can wait until after breakfast."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, moving north along the old drover's road the maps had forgotten and the book's marginalia remembered, toward the lake and the drowned cathedral and the second relic. Emrys carried the circlet wrapped in three layers of cloth at the bottom of their pack, and every mile the pack seemed to grow heavier—not the metal, which weighed almost nothing, but the wanting, settling in like ballast.

By midday they could feel it even through the canvas: a low pulse, patient as tide, keeping time with their heartbeat. By evening, walking the ridgeline with the lake visible below, flat and black and waiting, Emrys realized they had begun humming a tune they didn't know, and that it was the same twelve notes the tide-bells had rung in the dream.

Sable heard it too. They didn't say anything. They just shifted closer on the narrow path, shoulder brushing shoulder, a small deliberate pressure—*I'm here, I'm watching*—and hummed something else, loudly and badly, a dockside song about a merchant's wife and a lighthouse keeper, until Emrys laughed despite everything and the twelve notes lost their hold.

Below them, as the last light failed, the surface of the lake broke once, smoothly, in a perfect ring, as though something enormous far beneath had turned over in its sleep and settled again.

Emrys stood at the edge of the overlook a moment longer than they meant to, the cold rising off the water like an invitation.

"Tomorrow," they said.

The word echoed strangely off the lake, doubled, and the second voice that said it back was not entirely their own.
