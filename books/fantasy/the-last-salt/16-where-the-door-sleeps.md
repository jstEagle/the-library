## 16. Where the Door Sleeps

The ferryman did not walk like the dead. That was the first thing Emrys noticed, watching him move along the riverbank with his pole across his shoulders, his boots finding the stones the way a living man's do—by habit, by long acquaintance. He walked like someone who had made this journey so many times that death had simply failed to interrupt it.

"You keep looking at my feet," he said, not turning around.

"I keep wondering why you don't drift."

"Drifting's for ghosts who've forgotten where they're going." He spat into the water, an old man's gesture, absurd and somehow holy. "I remember. That's all staying is, girl. Remembering harder than forgetting wants you to."

Emrys had stopped correcting him about *girl* somewhere in the drowned cathedral country. There were larger errors to fight. They walked north out of the ghost-fields at dawn, Sable a step behind with the Salt Chalice wrapped in oilcloth against their back, and the land changed around them the way a sentence changes when it approaches its end—the fields thinned, then the farms, then even the phantom hedgerows, until there was only grey scree and a valley that no map had ever bothered to insult with ink.

The ferryman called it the Hush.

"Not because it's quiet," he explained, though it was quiet, terribly so, the kind of quiet that pressed on the ears like deep water. "Because sound goes in and doesn't come back out. The kingdom's last breath went into that cliff." He raised his chin toward the far wall of the valley: a face of black stone five hundred feet high, unbroken except for one thing—one seam, hairline-thin, running vertical from crown to ankle of the rock, so fine that Emrys would have taken it for a crack, a flaw, a trick of light, if they hadn't spent eleven months learning to read marginalia.

The seam was the same shape as the line in the prophecy book. The one drawn beneath the words *and the last salt shall find the way in.*

Emrys stopped walking. Their hands, which had been steady through chapels and sieges and drowning water, shook now.

"That's it," they said. Not a question. Some part of them had known since the almanac, since Maren's kitchen, since the ward-flare on their skin. You didn't spend your whole life being invisible and then fail to recognize a door built for exactly one person.

"That's it," the ferryman agreed. He set down his pole and sat on a stone as though he'd been waiting a century to sit, which perhaps he had. "And before you ask—I can't open it. Can't touch it. None of us can. It's the one thing in Thornfell that never belonged to Thornfell. It belongs to whoever's left."

Sable caught up, breathing hard, and stood beside Emrys looking up at the cliff with an expression Emrys had learned to read over the miles: thief's assessment, measuring approach angles and guard posts, and finding nothing to steal because there was nothing here at all. No road led in. No road led out. Just the Hush, and the seam, and the sky going the color of pewter overhead.

"How long?" Sable asked.

"The moon dies tomorrow night," the ferryman said. "Thirteenth turn. The Door will open at full dark and stay open while the dark lasts. And only—" he looked at Emrys then, really looked, with eyes gone pale as riverwater, "—only for you."

* * *

They camped at the valley mouth, because there was nowhere else to go and because Emrys wanted, badly, to see anything coming.

Sable built the fire small and shielded it with stones, an old smuggler's courtesy, and sat sharpening knives that did not need sharpening. Emrys knew that tell by now. When Sable's hands needed work, their mind was working too.

"Say it," Emrys said.

"I've said it twice already."

"Say it again. I liked it better the third time than the second."

Sable sighed, laid down the knife. "We should leave. Tonight, now, before dark. Take the relics east over the passes, scatter them in six countries, let the Ledger chase shadows for another hundred years. You don't have to open that door. Nobody can make you be the last salt."

"Yes, they can." Emrys fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "That's what I finally understand about it. Vane could have killed me in Maren's valley. He didn't. The Ledger could have put a blade in me at any border town in a year of trying. They haven't. Because killing the last salt doesn't close the inheritance—it just leaves it unclaimed. And an unclaimed kingdom is worse for them than any king would be. So they need me alive, at that door, choosing what they want chosen. Which means every day I run, I'm doing exactly what they want. Running *is* their plan."

"And walking in?"

"Walking in might be mine."

Sable was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like voices, almost like a congregation, and both of them pretended not to hear it.

"When I was hired," Sable said slowly, "the brief said *recover or confirm destruction*. Four relics, one heir. I thought it was a collector. Rich men collect; that's just weather." They turned the chalice's wrapping over in their lap without unwrapping it. "But it was never about collecting, was it. It was about making sure everything arrived at the door together. Everything, and everyone. They weren't sending me after treasure. They were sending me after *you*, carrying their cargo, straight to the altar."

"They used your greed as a leash."

"My greed, my charm, my excellent taste in other people's property." Sable smiled, but it was the thin smile, the one that hurt to look at. "And the worst part is it worked. Every relic we took, I thought I was beating them. I was their courier."

Emrys reached over and took the chalice back, deliberately, and held it between them. "You're also the reason I'm still alive. Twice over. Three times counting the well in Ghost Country, which I know you'll deny." They turned the wrapped metal. "So here's what we do. Tomorrow night, when the Ledger comes—and they'll come, all of them, they can't risk leaving witnesses to whatever happens—we let them come. We let them watch. And when the Door opens, we choose something they didn't write."

"You've decided, then. What you'll choose."

Emrys looked at the seam in the cliff, a thread of darkness against darkness.

"I've decided I won't choose what they wrote."

* * *

Halvard Vane had besieged towns, fortresses, one stubborn monastery in the eastern fells whose monks had held out for nine days armed only with doctrine and slingshots. He knew siegecraft. He knew how to read a valley the way a clerk reads a ledger—where the weight sits, where the weakness runs, what column hides what sum.

The Hush read wrong.

He stood at the valley's northern rim in the grey afternoon with two hundred of the Order's company spread below him—clerks in their undyed wool, soldiers in Crown grey that fooled no one, the bound wagons of instruments he had spent thirty years pretending not to examine too closely. And the valley swallowed the sound of them. Two hundred men and horses made less noise than four. Orders passed hand to hand had to be shouted twice. The mountain ate everything.

"It's the salt," said Sub-Clerk Ovist beside him, young, eager, wrong. "Residue from the dissolution. Acoustic absorption. The founding texts mention—"

"I know what the founding texts mention." Vane did not look at him. He was looking down at the floor of the valley, at the tiny figures of two people and one dead man around a cookfire no bigger than a coin. "I want riders on both rims. Archers covering the seam at three hundred paces. The binding crews ready at dusk, but nobody touches the heir before the Door opens. Understood? Before. Not during. If the inheritance passes unclaimed because some fool put a bolt in the last salt early, the Founders' contract voids and every one of us hangs by our own articles. Am I clear?"

Ovist went white and went away. Vane remained, and let himself, for one unguarded minute, admit what he had been not-admitting since the chapel, since the bargain, since the bolt in the pass that had missed her by the width of a prayer.

She was going to stand at that door tomorrow night, and he was going to be one of the men who put her there.

He had told himself a story for thirty years, the way the Order told stories: that the Ledger was the brake on power, the cold clean instrument that kept kingdoms from bleeding. A hundred years ago, they said, Thornfell had been dying anyway—corrupt, cruel, its queen mad with the old magic—and the Ledger had merely written the mercy the age required. Dissolution instead of war. Erasure instead of massacre. Someone had to hold the pen.

But he had read the interrogation notes now. All of them. He had read the well-water reports from the year before the treaty, the ones marked with the Founder's cipher, the ones listing which villages received which shipments. He had read the margin note in the First Clerk's own hand: *Let the sickness do the conquering. We will supply the cure and call it law.*

Two hundred people below him believed they served justice. He had believed it longer than most of them, which meant he had betrayed it longer, which meant the debt ran deepest in him.

Vane took out the folded papers in their oilskin—the evidence, the real ledger inside the Ledger—and weighed it in his hand. It was lighter than it should have been, for something that heavy.

Tomorrow night, he thought. One way or another, tomorrow night the account closes.

* * *

The dead came to the fire that evening.

Not to haunt—to visit. That distinction mattered, Emrys was learning. They came singly and in pairs, out of the scree and the shadow-lines, farmwives and shepherds and one soldier still wearing a uniform of a style extinct a century, and they stood at the edge of the firelight the way the living stand at the edge of a sickroom, wanting to help and knowing there's nothing to carry in.

The ferryman sat among them like a host among guests, and translated.

"They want to know if it's true," he said. "That you mean to refuse the crown."

Emrys stood. Around the fire, faces turned—some blurred, some sharp as memory itself, all of them waiting. A hundred years of paying taxes to no one, of farming fields no one would harvest, of being a country that existed only in the insistence of its own dead. And here at last was the person the paperwork said could change it.

"I mean to refuse it," Emrys said, and their voice carried strangely in the Hush, flat and close, as if spoken inside a sealed room. "Not restore it. Not wear it. What Queen Ilsa asked of me, I intend to give. The power goes back to all of you—or it ends, whichever frees you more. Either way, it ends with me. I am the last salt, and I am done being brine for someone else's recipe."

Silence. Then an old woman near the front—a shepherd, crook still in her hands, her sheep long since scattered to legend—began, very quietly, to laugh. Not mockery. The other dead joined her, a low tide-sound of laughter and weeping indistinguishable, and the ferryman bowed his head as if receiving rain.

"They've waited a hundred years to hear a ruler say *no*," he said. "It's the only coronation they ever wanted."

* * *

Dawn came colourless. The moon was gone entirely now—not visible even as a fingernail, just an absence the sky wore like a missing tooth—and Emrys felt it in their blood, in the salt-mark that climbed their throat these days like frost climbing a window. Tomorrow night. Full dark. The thirteenth turn.

They spent the morning doing ordinary things with extraordinary care. Sable cleaned weapons. Emrys mended a strap on the chalice's wrapping, ate, drank, checked the circlet in its case for the tenth time and hated themselves for the tenderness in the checking. The ferryman walked the valley floor pointing out, in his dry way, where the ground fell away, where the scree would betray footing, the guide's litany of a country only he remembered whole.

Near midday, Emrys climbed alone to a ledge below the seam and sat with the book.

The prophecy lay open to the final page. The cipher had surrendered to them weeks ago, Maren's lessons and the circlet's dreams unlocking it letter by letter, and now they read the ending the Ledger had written a century before, in the neat hand of clerks who believed the future was just an unfilled form:

*And the last salt shall enter, and shall take up the crown, and the kingdom shall rise again under lawful heir, and the borders shall be drawn as the Founders have decreed, and it shall be good.*

*It shall be good.* As if goodness could be minuted. As if a kingdom could be resurrected by the same men who had poisoned its wells, and the resurrection would somehow wash the poison out of the record.

Emrys closed the book. Below in the valley, Sable looked up at the ledge, and even at this distance Emrys could see them mouth the question they asked every morning: *still with me?*

Still with me, Emrys mouthed back.

Then the wind changed, and the Hush broke.

Sound came pouring down the valley from the north—hooves, harness, the creak of wagons, the barked orders of men who did not care who heard them—and Emrys rose slowly to their feet and watched the Order of the Ledger come down into the Hush like ink dropped into water. Two hundred strong. Banners with no sigil, because the Order's sigil had always been blankness. At their head, riding alone, a grey figure on a grey horse whom Emrys recognized even at half a mile, the way you recognize a scar.

"He came," Sable said, arriving at the ledge, breathing hard. "All of them came. Emrys—that's not a pursuit force. That's a *coronation*. They brought the whole company. They brought the wagons. Whatever they think happens tonight, they think it's finished business."

Below, the company halted in perfect order at the valley's mouth, two hundred strong, and began, unhurried, to set their lines. Binding crews uncoiling cables of woven scripture. Archers loosing their bowstrings once, testing. Clerks opening books on portable stands, as if the siege were a service and they its congregation.

And at their center, Halvard Vane dismounted, looked up at the ledge where Emrys stood, and did not look away.

"He knows we can see him," Sable said.

"Yes."

"Do you think he's here to stop us?"

Emrys watched the grey man stand motionless in the failing light, one hand resting on his saddlebag as if it held something heavier than papers.

"No," Emrys said. "I think he's here to decide."
