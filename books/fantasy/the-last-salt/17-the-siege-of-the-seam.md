## 17. The Siege of the Seam

The valley had a name once. The book said so, in its cramped Royal Saltwork cipher: *Cwm yr Halen*, the Hollow of Salt, where the Door slept in a cliff face like a splinter under skin. The name was gone from every map, but the mountains remembered it. Emrys felt the memory pressing down as they crested the last ridge and looked back at what followed them.

Torches. Dozens of them, strung along the pass like a rosary of small fires, and behind the torches the Ledger's clerks in their grey coats, marching with the terrible patience of people who had waited a hundred years and could wait one more night.

"They're not hurrying," Sable said beside them. Their arm was still bound from Turn Twelve, the crossbow wound wrapped in Maren's last poultices, but their eyes were sharp as ever, cataloguing numbers out of habit. "Forty, fifty on the ridge. Another company holding the river mouth. They don't need to catch us. They only need to be here when the moon turns."

"The thirteenth turn is tomorrow," Emrys said. "New dark. The Door opens for one night."

"And they intend to open it themselves." Sable spat over the edge of the trail. "Or close it forever with you inside."

Below them, the ghost-valley spread out in its pale half-light — the phantom fields rippling though no wind touched them, the drowned village standing knee-deep in mist that was once the sea, the ferryman's lantern bobbing at his landing like a patient star. The dead of Thornfell went about their endless harvest, unaware, or pretending to be unaware, that the living world had finally come to collect.

Emrys had come here because there was nowhere else. That was what they told themselves. But some quieter part of them knew the truth: the Door called to the salt in their blood now, and answering it felt less like choice and more like gravity.

The circlet sat against their ribs beneath their coat, cold as a coin left overnight in snow.

*You could still leave,* said the part of them that was still Emrys Winterbourne, smuggler, guide, nobody. *Take Sable south. Vanish. You've done it your whole life.*

*And the ghosts?* said the other voice, deeper, older, wearing their own throat. *And the Ledger? And Vane?*

They started down into the valley.

* * *

They made the ferryman's landing an hour past dusk. Old Brannan stood at his pole as if he'd been waiting a century — which, Emrys supposed, he had.

"You brought them with you," the ferryman said. Not a question. His lantern lit the underside of his face, all hollows and river-light. "I can hear their boots on my water. Living boots. They haven't walked this shore since the treaty."

"Can you get us across before they reach the bank?" Sable asked.

"I can get you across whenever I please." Brannan's eyes settled on Emrys, and something in them softened and saddened at once. "It's the others I'm thinking of. My neighbors. My wife, who plants a field no rain has ever touched." He lifted the pole. "When the living come with chains, boy, who do you think they'll bind first?"

Emrys hadn't thought of it. That was the shame of it — three days of hard travel toward the Door, circlet humming against their chest, and they had thought of nothing but the seam in the cliff and the choice waiting behind it.

"They wouldn't harm ghosts," Sable began. "What would be the—"

But the answer came up the valley before they finished speaking.

It arrived as a sound first: a low, grinding hum, like a millstone turning underwater. Then light — not firelight, but the flat white glare of ledger-lamps, the cold illumination clerks used for checking accounts by. It swept across the phantom fields, and where it passed, the ghost-wheat stopped rippling.

Stopped. All at once, field after field, like birds falling out of the sky.

"No," Brannan breathed. He dropped the pole and waded into the shallows, lantern held high. "No, no — *Alys!*"

Emrys saw her then, at the field's far edge. A woman made of memory and mist, frozen mid-motion, her hands full of wheat she would never finish gathering. Around her, dozens of others stood locked in place — a shepherd mid-stride, children caught in a game of chase, an old man bent forever over a well. Bound. Held still as specimens in a collector's case.

Down the valley, the clerks' lamps moved among them like men counting stock.

"What are they doing?" Sable whispered.

"Binding them." Emrys heard their own voice come out flat and strange. "Ledger-work. Everything must balance. Every debt must have collateral." The words tasted like ash. "The ghosts are the debt. They're using them as surety against me."

Brannan turned from the water, and his face — the face of a man dead a hundred years — held more fury than any living face Emrys had ever seen. "They cannot hold us. We are dissolved. The treaty said—"

"The treaty," Emrys said quietly, "was written by the men who poisoned your wells. Did you think they honored anything else?"

The old ghost stared at them. Then, slowly, he picked up his pole again. "Get in the boat," he said. "Both of you. If the last salt is going to do something tonight, let it be done fast."

* * *

The crossing took no time at all, because Brannan willed it so — the mist parted, the water forgot itself, and suddenly the far bank rose before them, black cliffs stitched with white veins of salt. Somewhere up there, invisible in the dark, was the seam: the Door.

They climbed. Sable went first despite the wounded arm, finding holds with a thief's instinct, and Emrys followed, and the higher they climbed the louder the circlet sang against their ribs — a thin, eager tone, like a struck glass.

Halfway up, Emrys's hand closed on rock and the rock *answered*. Warmth flooded up through their palm, and they looked down and saw it: the salt-sigil, the mark the chapel ward had branded into them weeks ago, spreading.

It had been a faint shimmer before, a watermark visible only in certain lights. Now it crawled. White lines branched across the backs of their fingers like frost forming on a window, fine as script, and where the lines ran, the skin beneath glowed faintly, translucent, as if lit from inside by something that was not entirely them.

"Emrys." Sable had stopped above, looking down, and their face in the dark was careful. "Your hands."

"I know."

"Do you know? Because from here it looks like—" Sable stopped. Swallowed whatever the rest was. "How much of you is still yours?"

Emrys flexed their fingers. The sigil pulsed, and the pulse felt good, and that was the horror of it. Each time they drew on the relics' power, it took payment in kind — and the payment was *them*, grain by grain, traded away and replaced with something older, harder, crowned.

"Ask me again tomorrow night," Emrys said. "After the Door. Whatever's left will answer honestly."

They climbed on.

* * *

The ledge below the seam was narrow as a knife's spine. Above them the Door waited — a hairline fracture in the cliff, glowing faintly silver, too thin to slip a knife through. Tomorrow's dark moon would widen it. Tonight it only breathed, slow and cold, exhaling the smell of a country that existed nowhere: cut hay, woodsmoke, brine.

Below, torchlight spilled around the base of the cliff. The clerks had reached the valley floor.

And with them, unmistakable even at distance, came the flat voice of Inquisitor Halvard Vane, giving orders in the tone of a man reading aloud from someone else's script.

"—the bound ones are not to be harmed. Merely maintained. Move the binding-stones closer to the water. If the last salt comes down, the collateral stands until the Door closes."

Sable eased forward along the ledge to the lip and peered over. Emrys joined them.

From above, the scene arranged itself with terrible clarity: the clerks in their grey ranks, ledger-lamps planted like standards; between them, the bound ghosts standing motionless in the white glare, Alys and the shepherd and the children, held upright as hostages; and at the center, Vane, tall and gaunt, his grey coat indistinguishable from theirs except that he alone did not write anything down.

He was looking at the children. Even at this distance Emrys saw him looking — saw his jaw work once, side to side, like a man chewing on something that would not go down.

"He hates this," Sable murmured.

"He hated burning Maren's cottage too. He did it anyway."

"Yes. But look at him, Emrys. Truly look." Sable's voice dropped. "I've spent years reading marks — faces, locks, guards' tempers. That man is a lock starting to turn. Something in him has already broken loose and he hasn't admitted it yet. Men like that are more dangerous than loyal ones. Or more useful."

Emrys watched Vane turn away from the children sharply, as if the sight were a hand on his collar, and thought of the interrogation notes Sable had described — the Ledger, the founding crime, a century of clerks writing justice with ink made from other people's ruin. Vane had learned what his masters were. Learning and breaking were different things. But the crack was there.

"We can't fight fifty clerks," Emrys said.

"We can't fight ten."

"No." Emrys drew the Salt Chalice from their pack — the second relic, heavy as guilt, its bowl lined with mineral glitter that never dulled. "But we don't have to. We have to reach the Door, and the Door opens for one person. They know that. Their whole plan is leverage — bind the ghosts, force me to trade myself for them." They turned the chalice slowly in the sigil-marked fingers, watching light crawl through the translucence of their own skin. "So we take the leverage away."

"How?"

Emrys looked down at the bound ghosts, at the binding-stones — squat grey things, ledger-carved, humming their millstone hum. Four of them, one at each corner of the ghost-company, each attended by two clerks.

"Those stones hold the bindings," Emrys said. "Break the stones, break the bonds. The clerks can't relight four fires at once if I hit all four in a breath." A pause. The honest pause, the one they owed Sable. "And to move fast enough to do that, I'll need the circlet. Full strength. Both relics at once."

Silence, except the Door breathing above them.

"You'll change," Sable said quietly. "More than you already have."

"Probably."

"You dreamed with the circlet for one night and woke up talking about *completion* like it was a lover."

"I remember."

"So why—" Sable stopped. Started again, rougher. "Why does it have to be you?"

Emrys looked at their hands. The white script crawled almost to the wrist now. Somewhere under it, faintly, the old calluses from rope and lockpick and mountain stone.

"Because the Door knows me," they said. "Because the prophecy wrote my name and I'm the only one who can unwrite it. And because—" the deep voice stirred in their chest, wanting the sentence to end differently, wanting to say *because the power is mine by blood* — and Emrys clamped down on it, forced their own small stubborn voice through, "—because somebody has to carry it so nobody else gets hurt carrying it. That's the job. That's always been the job. Guide first. Last salt second."

Sable held their gaze for a long moment. Then they grinned — crooked, terrified, real. "Guide first. Gods help us both. Tell me where to stand."

* * *

They came down the scree in the last hour before dawn, when the ledger-lamps burned lowest and the clerks stamped their feet against cold and the binding-hum droned on, steady as tax-day. Emrys wore the circlet openly now, a thin band of white metal above their brow, and it fit as though it had been measured for them — which, in the way of cursed inheritances, perhaps it had.

Power came up through them like water through dry ground. The world sharpened. They could see the seams in the binding-stones, the clerk nearest the water biting his pen-nib, the exact moment when a sentry's gaze slid off them and slid back. And threaded through all of it, the ghosts: Alys, the shepherd, the children, held in their stillness, and beneath the stillness a vast faint pressure like a country holding its breath.

*Now,* said the deep voice.

*Now,* said Emrys, and meant it too, and for once the two voices agreed.

They went down the cliff face faster than falling, salt-script flaring along their arms like lightning learning to read, and hit the ground running.

The first binding-stone never knew what struck it. Emrys's hand — glowing, hardened, heavier than a hand should be — came down flat, and the stone split like a loaf, its hum dying mid-note. The two clerks beside it stumbled back, pens dropping. The second stone: Emrys crossed thirty feet of ground between heartbeats, and the world stretched sweet and slow around them, and they shattered the stone with a backhand sweep that should have broken their arm and instead sang up their shoulder like music.

Two down. The camp was waking now — shouting, lamps swinging, someone blowing a whistle — and the third stone's attendants had drawn short ledger-blades, grey steel, regulation issue.

Emrys did not slow down.

That was the thing they would remember afterward, in the long seasons after: not the fighting, but the *not slowing*. The way the power made other bodies seem soft, provisional, negotiable. The way the first blade that came at them simply — stopped, turned aside by a wall of salt-air they hadn't known they were raising, and the man wielding it went sprawling. The way they laughed. Briefly. Without meaning to.

The third stone broke. The fourth.

And the bindings died.

It went through the ghost-company like dawn going through fog. Alys's hands completed their gesture at last, wheat scattering; the shepherd's stride carried him forward; the children shrieked — joyfully, furiously — and scattered into the mist. A hundred still figures became a hundred moving ones, and the clerks stood in the middle of them holding lamps and ledgers that suddenly accounted for nothing, and the terror on those grey faces was, Emrys noted distantly, extremely satisfying, and the satisfaction itself frightened them more than the blades had.

"EMRYS!"

Sable's voice. High. Emrys spun — too fast, the world smearing — and saw the trouble at the waterline. The clerks had fallen back, but their captain had not: a broad woman with a binding-chain coiled in both hands, and she had abandoned the ghosts for a better prize. She had Sable. Chain around his wounded arm, wrenching, and Sable on his knees in the shallows with his face white.

Everything in Emrys went very quiet and very large.

They crossed the distance without deciding to. Later, they could not reconstruct the crossing — only the arrival, their hand closing on the chain, and the chain *freezing*, frost blooming down its links, and the captain letting go with a scream as the cold climbed her gloves. Emrys stood over Sable in the wrecked shallows, and the salt-sigil blazed from throat to fingertip now, white script crawling up their neck, and when they spoke their voice came out doubled, harmonized, theirs and something vaster underneath:

"*This one is under my protection.*"

The words rang off the cliffs. Off the Door. Off a century of silence.

The captain fled. The clerks fled, dragging their useless lamps. And Emrys stood in the emptying camp, chest heaving, tasting the power on the back of their tongue — and it tasted like cold springwater, like the first sip after thirst, and they wanted *more*, and knowing they wanted it was the only thing that kept them from taking it.

Slowly, shaking, they pulled the circlet from their brow. The world shrank back to human size. The sigil dimmed — but did not fade. It never faded anymore.

"Emrys." Sable, soaked, gasping, clutching his freed arm. He was staring at them with an expression Emrys had never seen on him before: not fear exactly. Grief. "Your neck. Your *face*. Look at yourself."

There was water pooled at the shore's edge, mirror-still. Emrys knelt and looked.

A stranger looked back. Their features, yes — Winterbourne bones, their mother's jaw — but overlaid now, unmistakably, with a lattice of white script climbing from collar to cheekbone, one line just touching the corner of their left eye. The eye itself had gone pale as sea-ice.

"Not long now," Emrys said quietly. "Tomorrow night, either way. Restoration or unmaking — after that, none of this matters."

"That's what frightens me." Sable hauled himself upright, dripping. "You say 'either way' like you've already stopped caring which."

Before Emrys could answer, a sound came from up the beach: footsteps. Unhurried. One set.

Halvard Vane walked out of the mist alone, unarmed, hands visible, his grey coat unbuttoned like a man halfway out of his own skin. He stopped ten paces off, and his eyes went from the shattered binding-stones to the fleeing clerks to the sigil blazing on Emrys's skin, and when he spoke, his voice was low and stripped of everything official.

"A word," Vane said. "Not as an Inquisitor. What my masters ordered tonight — binding children as collateral — I've served the Ledger twenty-two years, and tonight I find I cannot account for myself anymore." His jaw worked. "You'll want to know what they poisoned besides wells. I can prove all of it. Every page. But we have until the moon turns, and not one hour more."

Behind Emrys, the Door breathed in its cliff, one night from opening.
