## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The dark moon rose as a hole in the sky.

Emrys felt it before they saw it — a pulling behind the breastbone, like a stitch drawn tight — and knew without counting that this was the turn. Thirteen new moons since the night Maren's fire had lit the valley and the book had first spoken their name. The almanac had been right about everything else. It would be right about this too.

They stood at the cliff face where the seam slept, Sable beside them with an arm still bound from Turn Twelve, and the ghost-ferryman at their shoulder like a draft of cold air that had learned to be kind.

"It won't look like much," the ferryman said. "It never did. Even when I was living."

"What will it look like?" Emrys asked.

"Like a door." He almost smiled; his face was river-water and memory, so the smile came and went like light on current. "That was always Thornfell's trouble. We kept expecting our salvation to be enormous. Mountains of gold. Hosts of angels. And all the while the real thing was standing there looking like something you'd walk past on your way to market."

Emrys pressed their palm flat against the rock.

The salt-sigil beneath their skin woke at once — it had been waking more easily these last weeks, answering before they asked, and each time it did Emrys noted the sweetness of it the way a sober woman notes the taste of wine: carefully, with a small alarm underneath. The mark spread up their forearm, pale lines like frost ferning across glass, and where their hand met stone the cliff grew *thirsty*.

No. Not thirsty. *Recognizing.*

The seam opened.

Not with thunder. That was the horror of it, or the mercy — Emrys could never afterward decide which. There was no roar, no earthquake, no parting of the mountain. The rock simply became less certain of itself, the way ice becomes water not by breaking but by remembering another shape, and then there was a doorway where a cliff had been, tall as a chapel arch, edged in faint white light like the rim of a winter morning.

Beyond it: green hills. Terraced fields going gold with harvest. A river the color of hammered tin running through a valley that no map had carried for a hundred years, and above it all — impossibly, unbearably — a city on its hill, towers white and grey, banners moving in a wind that had been stopped for a century.

Thornfell. Whole. Alive.

Emrys's breath left them in a long, shaking thread.

"That's not possible," Sable whispered. They had seen ghosts, wards, drowned cathedrals, the Ledger's bound clerks chanting over chained dead — and still their voice cracked like a child's. "The treaty. The wells. The—"

"The Door doesn't open onto what is," the ferryman said quietly. "It opens onto what was agreed to be. This is the country as it was written down the day the Ledger sealed the treaty. Every field, every roof, every soul — held in trust. Waiting for someone to come claim it or refuse it." His voice roughened. "A century of waiting, in there. A single evening, out here. Time runs different across a threshold. You know that much, guide."

"I know passages," Emrys said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "I've never known one that led backward."

* * *

They stood at the threshold a long time without crossing it.

There was no rush. That was the strange gift of the thirteenth turn — the Door would hold until the moon set, and the moon, newly dark, had the whole night ahead of it. The Ledger's company was hours behind them still, Vane somewhere among the passes either hunting them or hunting his own masters, and the valley below held only sheep, stars, and the two of them and their dead companion. So they waited, because Emrys had learned on every road between here and Maren's burned cottage that the moments before an irrevocable act deserved their full weight.

"You don't have to go in," Sable said. "You know that. We could walk away. Let the moon set. Let it close forever unopened."

"And the ghosts?"

"The ghosts fade free anyway, if it closes. You said so yourself. The ferryman said so. Unclaimed inheritance reverts." Sable's jaw worked. "But if you open it and take the crown—"

"If I take the crown," Emrys said, "I stop being me."

They said it plainly, without drama, the way you'd report weather. But inside, saying it aloud, they felt the truth of the last weeks settle into place. The circlet in its wrapped bundle against their back had been whispering since Turn Twelve. Not words, exactly. *Appetites.* The chalice in its case at their hip hummed a lower note, a hunger for completion. And under the sigil, along their arms and throat, the salt-pale lines had crept further than they'd admitted to anyone — to their collarbones now, fine as cracks in porcelain, and when Emrys caught their reflection in still water they sometimes didn't recognize the face looking back, not because it had changed but because it wore an expression Emrys had never chosen. Something patient. Something *entitled*.

Kings were entitled. That was the whole disease of kings.

"No crown," they said. "Whatever waits in there offering itself. No crown."

Sable exhaled. "Good. Because I've got a speech prepared and it's terrible."

Emrys laughed — short, surprised, real — and the sound seemed to steady them both. Then they stepped through the Door.

* * *

Crossing felt like walking into memory made solid.

The air changed first. Mountain air outside, thin and resinous and cold. Inside: warm bread-smell, woodsmoke, river-mud, hay, the thousand layered smells of a country at harvest, and under it all salt — clean sea-salt, the smell of the drowned kingdom's name. Emrys's eyes stung.

Sound came next. Distant bells. A dog barking far off in the terraces. Children — actual children, voices high and quick — somewhere down the hillside, though when Emrys turned to look, the hillside paths were empty. Everything here was *almost* occupied. Fields with scythes leaning against fenceposts, mid-stroke. A loom with thread still in the shuttle. A table set for supper, three chairs, one pushed back as if someone had just risen to answer a knock.

"They're all here," the ferryman murmured, materializing fully now that he was home — and oh, he was *home*, his outline sharpening, his coat gaining buttons and patches and a mended elbow, a man instead of a rumor. "Every soul the treaty dissolved. Held in the shape of the day we signed it away. Not suffering. Not exactly. Just—" he searched for the word, "—*paused*. Like a letter half-written, waiting to see how it ends."

"How does it end?" Emrys asked. "If I restore it?"

He looked at them with eyes that had ferried the living for a hundred years and said nothing, which was its own answer, and then Queen Ilsa was there.

She came down the white road from the city alone, no retinue, dressed not in regalia but in a plain travelling gown gone soft with a century of pausing, and Emrys saw immediately why the ghosts loved her and why she begged them. She looked like a woman who had done arithmetic her whole reign and finally gotten the sum. Her crown she carried in her hands — not worn. Carried, the way you carry something heavy you are not permitted to put down.

"Last salt," she said. Her voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way true authority does, which is nothing like the way borrowed authority does. "You've come on the thirteenth turn. As written."

"As written," Emrys agreed, and hated the book for making even this moment feel like recitation. "And I want you to hear me say it plain, before anything offers me anything: I haven't come to be crowned."

Ilsa studied them. Ghost-light moved in her like weather.

"When my husband died," she said, "the Ledger's clerks came to me with pages. Prophecies, they called them. A lawful heir, foretold, who would return Thornfell to glory — or end it lawfully, if the heir proved unworthy. Either way, the kingdom's fate would pass through *law*. Through paper. Through them." She turned her hands over; the crown sat in them like a sleeping animal. "I was young and grieving and flattered that history had a role for me. I let them write my country into their ledger. By the time I understood that whoever holds the pen owns the ending, the wells were already bitter and the treaty was already signed and I was already—" her mouth twisted, "—*pause*. Do you understand what I'm telling you, child?"

"That the prophecy was never a prophecy," Emrys said. "It was a contract. With the ending pre-agreed."

"With the *ending* pre-agreed," Ilsa said, "and the middle left blank for whoever came last. That blank space is you. They need a last salt who accepts, so their theft becomes restoration. Or a last salt who refuses wrongly — violently, selfishly — so their theft becomes justice. Those are the endings they wrote. Crown or ruin. Both theirs." She stepped closer, and her ghost-eyes were fierce and terribly tired. "So I am begging you again, as I begged you in the heartland: do not restore us. Do not take it. And do not let them make your refusal into their story either. Find the third thing. The thing they didn't think to write down."

"There is no third thing," Emrys said. "I've looked. Every road ends at the crown or at the closing of the Door."

"Every road *they mapped*," said Ilsa. "You're a smuggler's guide, last salt. Your whole life has been roads that aren't on any map." Almost gently: "Look at what you're carrying. Look at what it wants."

* * *

The crown offered itself, as promised.

Emrys felt it happen — felt the circlet wake against their back and the chalice sing against their hip and the sigil blaze from wrist to throat, and the whole paused country lean toward them like wheat toward sun. The Door behind them filled with white light. The bells rang. Somewhere in the terraced fields ten thousand paused souls turned as one toward the girl-shaped question standing on the white road, and the crown in Ilsa's hands lifted, weightless, turning slowly, and it was beautiful, that was the unforgivable part, it was *beautiful* — silver and salt-white, wrought like frozen foam, humming the note Emrys had been hearing in dreams since Turn Twelve, the note of being *answered*, of mattering, of a hundred years of erasure undone by the simple fact of their consent—

*Take it,* said the country, in a voice like the whole valley breathing. *Take it and we live. Take it and the maps must redraw themselves. Take it and no one was ever drowned, no wells were ever bitter, no one ever waited—*

And there it was. The trap inside the trap. Not power — *amnesia.* Restoration not as resurrection but as revision. Take the crown and the Ledger's crime would never have happened, which meant the Ledger's crime would never need accounting, which meant the clerks would go on writing their lawful worlds forever with one more proof in their ledgers that paper beats people.

"No," Emrys said.

The word fell into the valley like a stone into the tin-colored river.

The crown kept turning. The country kept leaning. The offer didn't withdraw — offers like this didn't understand *no*, they only understood refusal made permanent.

"Ilsa." Emrys turned to the queen. "When the Door closes tonight, what happens to all of you? Truly. Not the pretty version."

Ilsa smiled, and it was a real smile, sad as salt.

"We end," she said. "Properly. The pause lifts, and whatever a soul has left unfinished finishes itself in the finishing — some will simply fade, glad to. Some will say the goodbye they never managed. My steward has been trying to tell his daughter he forgave her for thirty years; he'll get to say it. That's not death, last salt. That's *completion*. We've been dead a century either way. The only question was whether we died owned or free." She held out the crown to them, both hands, an abdication a hundred years overdue. "Free, please. Whatever it costs you. Free."

Emrys took the crown.

Not to wear it. To hold it — and holding it was like holding a live coal and a sleeping infant at once, warmth and hunger and terrible smallness, all that appetite packed into silver foam, and through their palms Emrys felt the whole architecture of it at last: the crown wasn't a thing. It was an *agreement*, exactly as Maren had taught them in her last hours. Power isn't taken or given; it's agreed upon. The crown was a century of agreement compressed to a point, the Ledger's signature and Ilsa's grief and ten thousand paused souls' consent all braided into one object, waiting for one final yes.

Agreements could be refused.

Behind them, far off in the outer darkness of the real world, torchlight bloomed in the pass — the Ledger's company, descending on the valley, hours out but coming, clerks in grey and Inquisitors in black and Vane somewhere among them carrying whatever he'd chosen to become. The bells inside the Door rang on. The moon outside hung dark and patient, halfway down the sky.

Sable touched Emrys's shoulder. "They're early."

"No," Emrys said. "We're on time. There's a difference." They closed their fingers around the crown, felt it press its offer one last time against their skin, sweet as honey and cold as wellwater, and heard themselves say, steadily, to the queen, to Sable, to the whole listening country, to the clerks still marching in the dark:

"Now we finish it."

The Door's light bent inward, like an eye narrowing — and beyond the seam, the first grey clerk stepped into the starlight of the valley.
