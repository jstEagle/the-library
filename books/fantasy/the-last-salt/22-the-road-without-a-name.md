## 22. The Road Without a Name

The first snow of the season came down soft as flour, and Emrys walked ahead of the caravan with their hood back because they had stopped caring who saw their face.

That was the strangest part, in the end. Not the mountains. Not even the ghosts, though there were no ghosts now, not since the Door had closed and the thirteenth moon had passed into story. The strangest part was being *seen* — a name spoken aloud at the waystation fires, *Emrys Winterbourne*, sometimes followed by *the one who unmade Thornfell*, sometimes by nothing at all but a nod, as if they were only another guide with good boots and a reputation for getting people over the passes alive.

Both things were true. That was what no ballad seemed able to hold: that a person could be both.

"Emrys." Sable's voice drifted up from the rear of the line, warm and dry as always, pitched to carry without carrying too far. "The Kellish merchant wants to know if we'll reach the high col before dark."

Emrys turned in the falling snow and looked back down the column of travelers — six wagons, eleven mules, a family from the river villages with everything they owned roped under oilcloth, two surveyors with their instruments wrapped in fleece, the Kellish merchant whose silks were legal this year because the border wars were over because the Ledger was ash. All of them trusting the person at the front to know where the mountain kept its teeth.

"We'll make the col," Emrys called back. "Tell him if he complains about the pace, he can carry his own silks."

Sable laughed — Emrys heard it even across thirty yards of snowfall, that particular laugh, bright and crooked — and turned back to the wagons.

Emrys faced forward again and climbed.

* * *

The pass had no name on any map made south of the valley, which suited Emrys fine. The maps that mattered were the ones the mountain folk drew themselves, charcoal on hide, chalk on slate, scratched into the lintels of waystations: here the slope slides after thaw, here the spring runs sweet, here you can see weather coming three hours off if you know how to look. When Emrys had first come through these heights, fleeing north with Maren's warning ringing in their ears and an old book heavy in their pack, the maps had shown nothing. *Here be the drowned kingdom*, the Crown cartographers wrote, or worse, they wrote nothing at all, as if erasure could be completed by silence.

Now there were new maps. Not drawn by clerks. Drawn by shepherds, by the ferryman's grandchildren, by Sable, actually, who had turned out to have an astonishing hand for it once they'd stopped using that hand to lift other people's relics.

"You're smiling," Sable said, drawing level. They had come up the line quietly, the way they did everything, snow gathering in their dark curls. "You only smile like that when you're thinking about something and refusing to say it."

"I'm thinking about maps."

"A thrilling subject."

"The ferryman's granddaughter sent me one. From the river village. She's twelve now. She's mapped the whole eastern shore, and she put the ford where the ghost-taxes used to be collected, and she labeled it *here nobody pays anybody*." Emrys pulled the folded scrap from inside their coat, careful of the damp, and let Sable see it — the wobbly ink coastline, the little fish drawn in the shallows, the defiant letters. "A hundred years ago the Ledger wrote a prophecy so they could erase a country and call it law. Now a child draws maps because she likes the shape of the water."

Sable studied the scrap longer than the joke required. "You keep that in your coat?"

"I keep several things in my coat."

"Mm." Sable handed it back, and their fingers brushed, deliberate. "I know. One of them rattles."

* * *

They made the col as the light went copper behind the western peaks, and camped in the lee of the old cairn — not the Salt Cairn, that was gone with everything else, just a plain pile of stones raised by drovers generations ago to mark safe ground. The travelers built fires, and Emrys walked the perimeter out of habit, checking the snow-lines, the wind, the small sounds of a mountain settling into night.

There was nothing to check for. No Inquisitors. Vane had stood trial in the border town square two winters past — not before a magistrate, not before the Crown, but before the assembled people of the valley towns, the descendants of those whose wells had been poisoned, whose kingdom had been signed away on paper while they slept. He had answered every question. He had named names until the Ledger's surviving clerks had scattered like cockroaches, some to prison, some simply to lives of terrified anonymity. And then he had asked the assembly what should be done with him, and Emrys had watched him stand there, grey and old and stripped of every office, waiting to be sentenced either to the gallows or to nothing.

The people chose neither. They gave him the road. They told him to spend the rest of his life walking through the country he'd helped unmake, and looking at what remained, and remembering. Last spring a shepherd had reported seeing a grey-haired man repairing a collapsed drystone wall above the lake, alone, unpaid, whistling. Emrys liked to think it was him.

The mountain was quiet. Emrys stood a moment longer anyway, breathing the cold, listening to the fires crackle below.

Then they took out the locket.

It was a plain thing, tin, dented, bought from a peddler for two coppers the winter after everything ended. Inside, on a bed of faded cloth, lay a single grain of salt — coarse, grey-white, ordinary-looking. It had come from the chalice. When the Door closed and the power went home to the people it belonged to, the relics had crumbled like sugar in rain; Emrys had caught this grain on their palm without thinking, the way you catch something falling that you cannot bear to let hit the ground.

They didn't wear it openly. It wasn't a crown. It was memory — that was all, and it was enough. Some nights, in bad dreams, Emrys dreamed of the throne room whole and living, dreamed the sweetness of the circlet's weight, woke with their hands aching to hold something again. On those nights they opened the locket and looked at the grain and remembered what it had cost, and what they had chosen instead, and why. *Power isn't taken or given,* Maren had said, her hand going cold around theirs. *It's agreed upon.* The grain of salt was the agreement refused. The agreement, forever, refused.

"Snow's easing," Sable said beside them. Emrys hadn't heard them climb up. They never did, and after four years had stopped pretending to mind. Sable settled onto the stone next to them, shoulder against shoulder, and looked at the locket without asking. They always knew what it was. They had been there when the world went white.

"The Kellish merchant asked me today," Sable said, "what's through the pass. On the far side. Whether the old stories are true — that there was a kingdom there once."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him there's a valley with good grazing, a river that runs clean, and forty families who'll sell him mutton at honest prices and won't thank him for calling their home haunted." Sable paused. "He looked disappointed. People want the story. Ruins. Wailing. A curse they can survive and boast about."

"They got the truth instead. Forty families."

"They got the truth instead." Sable tipped their head back and looked up through the thinning snow at the stars crowding out between clouds. "Do you ever miss it?"

"The crown?" Emrys laughed, short. "No."

"That's not what I asked."

Emrys was quiet. Below, someone's fire popped; a mule stamped; the ordinary music of an ordinary night. What Sable meant was the *purpose* — the terrible gift of having one's whole life aimed at a single point, a Door, a choice, a moon. Since the thirteenth turn, life had spread out again into seasons and wages and weather, into the long unremarkable work of walking people safely through mountains. Some days that felt like freedom. Some days it felt like standing in a field after the battle, wondering what all the sharpening was for.

"Sometimes I miss knowing," Emrys said finally. "Not the salt. Just — knowing what I was for."

Sable nodded slowly. Then they reached into their own coat and produced a folded paper, travel-worn, sealed with plain wax. "Then it's fortunate," they said, "that somebody appears to have written you a letter."

* * *

Emrys broke the seal by firelight while Sable pretended not to watch, which was Sable's way of watching very closely indeed.

The hand was young and careful. The ferryman's granddaughter — Liss, her name was Liss now, she'd grown into it — wrote that the river village council had met, all forty families plus the newer ones from the valley, and voted on a matter they wished to put to Emrys Winterbourne directly, because, she wrote, *you're the one who ended it and it seems wrong to ask anyone else.*

The matter was the ruins.

With the ghosts gone free, the phantom fields had faded, but the real remains remained — the drowned cathedral's bones above the waterline, the foundation-stones of the palace, the old treaty-stone where the Ledger's clerks had signed the dissolution a century ago. The council couldn't decide. Some wanted the stones left as they were, a lesson. Some wanted them quarried for the new schoolhouse. Some wanted a marker carved, naming what happened and who had done it and who had ended it.

And then, Liss wrote, old Tam the ferryman — older now than anyone could say, still poling his boat because he claimed the river would sulk otherwise — had stood up at the meeting and said that there was one person alive who carried the last of the kingdom in their pocket, and that the question ought to go to them. *Not because you ruled us,* she wrote, copying his words exactly, Emrys suspected. *Because you refused to. Grandfather says that's what makes it yours to answer.*

Below the signature, in smaller writing, almost shy: *We would pay your guide's fee. But also we would feed you, and my grandmother makes plum wine that people have fought over, truly fought.*

Emrys read it twice. Then a third time, slower, feeling something loosen in their chest that had been tight for four years without their quite noticing.

"Sable."

"Mm?"

"They want to build something. Where Thornfell was. Or not build — they can't agree. They've asked me to come and help them decide." Emrys looked up. "Me. Not a king's commission. A village council and plum wine."

Sable's grin arrived, slow and crooked, the same one from a chapel floor a lifetime ago, from a cell door, from a hundred camps like this one. "Well," they said. "It's a hard road. Rivers in flood season. Bandits, possibly. Terrible company the whole way."

"Terrible," Emrys agreed.

"We'd want a good fee."

"Plum wine," Emrys said. "Reportedly worth fighting over."

Sable pressed a hand to their chest, wounded. "Done. When do we leave?"

"After the Kellish merchant is delivered. After thaw, maybe." Emrys folded the letter along its worn creases and slid it into their coat, next to the map, next to the locket. "Liss says the treaty-stone's still standing. Nobody's dared touch it."

"And what will you tell them to do with it?"

Emrys considered. Snow ticked softly on the canvas of the nearest wagon. Somewhere below, the travelers' voices murmured around their fires, strangers becoming temporary neighbors the way people did when they trusted the road and the person leading them along it. Nothing rang. Nothing prophesied. The future lay open as fresh snow, unmarked, unwritten, waiting for whoever chose to walk out into it and leave their own prints.

"I don't know yet," Emrys said, and found that saying it felt like the freest thing they'd ever done. "But I think I'd like to sit down with forty families and a jug of plum wine and find out together."

Sable leaned over, blew out the candle between them, and grinned at Emrys through the dark.

"There's the last salt," they said. "Refusing to decide anything alone."

The stars came out all the way to the tops of the mountains, and in the morning there would be a col to cross, and after thaw a river, and after the river a valley full of people deciding, slowly and stubbornly and together, what to build on the ground where a kingdom used to be.

Emrys touched the locket once, through the cloth. Memory, not crown.

Then they went down to the fire, to the ordinary warmth, to the work of the road — and slept, for the first time in longer than they could count, without dreaming of thrones at all.
