## 1. Charms for a Magicless City

There are three rules to selling magic in a city that has outlawed it, and I follow all of them religiously, which is to say: I don't believe in them at all, but breaking them feels like tempting something with teeth.

Rule one: never call it magic. Call it craft. Call it tradition. Call it *what my grandmother did*, delivered in the tired voice of someone who'd rather be doing anything else. Grief is an excellent disguise. Nobody interrogates a grieving woman's salves.

Rule two: never make anything that works too well. A luck-knot that turns a bad day into a good one is evidence. A luck-knot that turns a terrible morning into a merely unpleasant afternoon is folk art.

Rule three — this one matters most — never let anyone see what you actually are.

I had kept all three rules for three years, and if you'd asked me on any of those nights whether I was happy, I would have said yes in the same practiced voice I used for everything else. That should have been my first clue. Happy people don't rehearse their answers.

* * *

Greyhollow's night market opens when the wardens' shift changes, which is to say it opens on a lie, like everything else in the city. The lantern-sellers come first, then the pie carts, then the fortune-tellers who are legally required to be frauds and mostly honor that requirement with enthusiasm. My stall sits between a mender of umbrellas and a woman who sells pickled things that glow faintly, which she insists is chemistry, and which the wardens have decided not to examine too closely because her brined eggs are, frankly, delicious.

My own stock is arranged with the studied carelessness of a person who spent two hours arranging it. Luck-knots in river-grass, red thread through the centers, each one tied with exactly nine loops — eight for show, one for the whisper of intent I breathe into the knot while my hands are busy and my face is bored. Weather-salves in clay pots, beeswax and comfrey and a single drop of something I will not name, stirred counterclockwise under a waning moon. Dried storm-fern bundles for people with aching joints. Nothing that glows. Nothing that hums. Nothing that would make a warden's detection-glass so much as fog.

"You look like a woman who's counting the hours until she can close," said Mrs. Halloway from the pickle cart, fanning herself with a ledger she never actually wrote in.

"I'm always counting the hours until I can close," I said. "It's called having a personality."

"It's called being young and wasting it."

"It's called being thirty-one and tired." Which was true in the way that all my truths were true — accurate, verifiable, and missing the entire interesting part.

The truth, if we're doing truths tonight, is that Greyhollow suits me the way a well-fitted coat suits a thief. The city banned magic sixty years ago after some catastrophe I only half remember hearing about — the official story changes depending on who's telling it and how much they've had to drink — and now the wardens patrol the streets with detection-glass lanterns and a mandate and a truly impressive budget. Magic doesn't exist here, officially. Which means it exists everywhere, unofficially, the way water exists in a wall: invisible until it ruins everything.

I sell charms to a city that believes charms are superstition, and the city buys them by the fistful, because everyone knows they're nonsense, and everyone knows they work, and no one says either out loud. It's the most honest dishonest place I've ever lived. For three years it had been enough.

That was the night Wren Vale bought a charm neither of us needed.

* * *

I saw them before they reached the stall, the way I see weather before it arrives — not consciously, but as a pressure behind the eyes, a change in the taste of the air. Most people read as nothing. Some read as faint static. This stranger read like the moment before lightning, except muted, wrapped, folded down small — like thunder trying to be polite.

They were tall-ish in a way that suggested they'd been told they were tall and had decided to be modest about it. Dark hair, wind-tousled though the night was still. A coat that had been good three years ago and was now merely loyal. And a smile that arrived a full second before the rest of their face, as though it had been sent ahead to scout.

"Evening," they said. "I'm told you're the one who sells luck."

"Told by whom?" I said. "Luck isn't real. Ask anyone official."

"Then I'm told you're the one who sells knots." They picked up a luck-knot, turned it over with fingers that were careful in a way craftsman's fingers are careful, or soldier's. "Nine loops."

"Traditional number."

"Is it?" Their eyes came up, and I made the mistake of meeting them. Brown, ordinary brown, except that ordinary brown eyes don't make your chest feel like a held breath. "Where I'm from, nine's the number for binding. Eight loops is a blessing. Nine's a promise you can't take back."

"Where are you from?"

"Far enough away that I should know better than to ask leading questions of charm-sellers." They set the knot down, then — here's the thing I keep returning to, the splinter in my memory of that night — they set it down and picked up a different one. "This one. No, wait." Third knot. "This one."

"They're identical."

"They're absolutely not." They smiled again, and this time the smile looked almost surprised at itself. "This one's quieter. Like it's been waiting longer."

I didn't answer, because what could I say? *Yes, that knot has been sitting at the back of the tray since midsummer, because every time I try to sell it my hands refuse.* I said nothing and rang up the sale, and took their coin, and our fingers touched over the exchange for perhaps half a heartbeat, and the static under their skin met whatever it is that lives under mine, and for one appalling moment the nearest lantern flickered.

Neither of us mentioned it. Both of us noticed. I could tell they noticed the way you can tell someone's noticed a sound — the small recalibration, the listening that happens behind the face.

"Storm coming," they said lightly, pocketing the knot.

"Not according to the glasshouse. Clear skies all week."

"Mm." They glanced up, past the market awnings, toward the mountains north of the city — the mountain, really; Greyhollow pretends its neighbors are plural the way it pretends about everything — and something moved across their expression too fast for me to name it. "Clear skies," they agreed. "Well. Thank you for the luck."

"You'll need more than a knot," I said, because I am constitutionally incapable of letting a customer leave without undermining my own product. "You'll need sense, and probably a map."

"A map." They considered this with genuine gravity. "Do you sell maps?"

"No."

"Pity. Then I suppose I'll just have to come back." And they said it plainly, without flirtation's usual armor, which is precisely why it landed like flirtation never does — direct, unguarded, a door left open on purpose. "Same time next week? Assuming the skies stay clear."

"That depends entirely on whether you buy anything."

"I'll buy something." They were already backing into the crowd, and the crowd parted around them in that way crowds do around people who matter somewhere else. "I always do."

I watched them go and then stood there feeling absurd, holding coins that suddenly seemed loud, and told myself the flutter under my ribs was indigestion from Mrs. Halloway's sample plate. Loneliness is a patient animal. It waits years inside contentment, quiet as a knot's ninth loop, and then someone smiles at you like a door opening and it starts pacing.

* * *

The rest of the night passed in inventory and self-deception, my two great competencies. I counted the till twice. I rewrapped salve pots that didn't need it. I thought about the stranger approximately four hundred times and permitted myself to think about them seriously maybe six.

Here is what I permitted myself to conclude: they were trouble. Not warden-trouble — wardens walk a particular way, hips weighted by authority, chins pre-loaded with accusation. This was a different trouble. Older. The trouble of someone carrying instructions they haven't finished reading. I knew that posture. I'd worn that posture into exile.

And here is what I concluded anyway, alone in my rented room above the stall, unbraiding my hair by candlelight: *same time next week.*

Three years I'd been careful. Three years of small weather, small lies, small life. I had a routine that fit me like the ninth loop fits the knot — snug, deliberate, unbreakable. I sold nonsense that worked to people who knew better. I watched the sun rise over a city that had agreed, collectively, to pretend, and I pretended along, and I had told myself so many times that this was peace that I'd nearly believed it.

Nearly. There's the wound in the whole arrangement. Contentment with a seam in it. Every night I closed the stall and felt the small relief of survival, and every morning I opened it again and felt the small absence of living, and I had learned to hold both at once the way you learn to carry hot dishes bare-handed: by never stopping moving.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off, thunder rolled — a soft, distant sound, impossible according to the glasshouse, impossible according to the season, and I lay in the dark and listened to it with my whole body the way a deaf woman listens to music, through the floor, through the bones.

Because here is rule zero, the rule underneath the other three, the one I've never said aloud: I miss it. I miss the storm. I miss being the thing the storm answers to. Whatever I gave up to be here — and there's a hole where that knowledge should be, a smooth scar of nothing in the shape of three lost months I've stopped asking questions about — whatever the price was, nobody told me it would include the sky.

The thunder rolled again, closer.

And beneath it — I sat up, heart going strange — beneath it, faint and unmistakable, came a sound like a whistle. Bone-made, high, wrong-pitched, the kind of note that isn't meant for human ears and finds them anyway. It came from the direction of the north gate. From the direction of the mountain.

It came, I realized with cold certainty and no evidence whatsoever, from him.

I lay back down. I stared at the ceiling. I performed the nightly litany of a careful woman: *you imagined it, you're lonely, loneliness makes music out of nothing.*

But I had sold him a luck-knot with nine loops, and he'd known what nine loops meant, and he'd chosen the knot that had been waiting, and somewhere north of the city thunder walked where no thunder should.

*Same time next week,* he'd said.

God help me — and there's no god in Greyhollow, only wardens — I was already planning what to stock.
