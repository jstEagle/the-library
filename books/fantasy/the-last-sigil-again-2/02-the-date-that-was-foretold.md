## 2. The Date That Was Foretold

There is a particular kind of danger in letting yourself be charmed, and I want it noted that I recognized the danger and walked into it anyway. Both hands. Eyes open. Like a woman stepping off a cliff because the view was nice.

Wren Vale had sent a note to my stall three days after the night market—a folded scrap of good paper, which in Greyhollow was practically a love letter, since paper that clean usually meant either money or trouble. *Dinner. The Gilded Kettle, seventh bell. I'll wear the coat you laughed at.* No signature, but the handwriting looped its letters like it was showing off, so there wasn't much mystery about the author.

I should have said no. I'd like the record to show that somewhere in my chest, a small sensible creature wearing a tiny warden's uniform raised a lantern and said *ma'am, this is a bad idea*, and I picked it up by the collar and set it on a high shelf and went to dig out my second-best dress.

The Gilded Kettle was the nicest lie in Greyhollow. It sat on Lantern Row pretending to be a respectable establishment, all copper lamps and polished oak, but everyone in the city knew it kept its best table behind a curtain for people who paid in coin they didn't want counted aloud. Exiles like me didn't get the curtain. We got the corner near the kitchen door, where the draft came through and the stew was honest. I arrived early, because arriving early meant choosing my seat with my back to a wall and both exits in view—old habits from a life I was supposed to have forgotten—and found Wren already there.

In the corner seat. Back to the wall. Both exits in view.

Our eyes met across the room, and Wren had the grace to look caught.

"Habit," they said when I slid into the opposite chair. "You too?"

"You first."

"Fair." They turned their cup a slow quarter-rotation on its saucer, then back again. "I traveled a lot before here. You learn where your spine wants to sit."

"And where does it want to sit tonight?"

Wren smiled, and I felt the smile land somewhere under my ribs and make itself at home without paying rent. "Apparently facing you."

*The thief comes smiling.*

I don't know why those words surfaced just then. They didn't exist for me yet—I hadn't read them, hadn't heard them, wouldn't find them for another two days in a book wrapped in oilcloth under a dealer's floorboards. But something in me flinched at that smile anyway, some animal ledger keeping accounts my mind couldn't read. I ignored it. I ordered the wine.

Here's what dinner with Wren Vale was like: easy. Offensively easy. Conversation with most people is rowing—you pull, they pull, you keep the boat straight between you. Talking to Wren was being carried downstream. They asked questions that weren't really about the answers but about the person answering. Where had I learned to knot charms so well? (My grandmother, I said, which was true, though not the whole truth; the knots were half craft and half weather-sense, and the weather-sense was the part that would get me hanged.) What did I miss most? (Rain that meant something. In Greyhollow the rain was just water doing its job, no opinion in it at all.) Did I ever think of leaving?

"No," I said, and then, because Wren's face did this thing—tilted, patient, like a held breath—"Maybe. Doesn't everyone?"

"Everyone thinks about leaving." They refilled my cup without asking, which should have been presumptuous and instead felt like being known. "Not everyone means it."

"Do you mean it?"

They looked at me for a long moment over the rim of their cup. The lamplight did something generous to their face—softened the sharpness at the cheekbone, warmed the gray of their eyes toward silver. "I mean everything I say," Wren said. "That's my problem."

"What's your other problem?"

"I don't say enough of them."

And then—the moment. I've turned it over so many times since that it's worn smooth as river glass, and every time I turn it I find a new angle of horror or tenderness, sometimes both at once. The stew had come and gone. The kitchen noise had settled into its late-evening drone. Wren leaned forward on their elbows, and the lamplight caught them just so, and they said:

"When you stop smiling, Astrid Whitlock, it looks like weather about to break."

I laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, delighted—because what a line, what an absurdly specific, absurdly intimate line, the kind of thing a person could only say if they'd been watching closely, and who doesn't want to be watched closely by someone with that mouth and those eyes?

"That's very poetic for someone buying luck-knots," I said.

"I'm told I'm full of poetry I can't account for." Something crossed their face then—quick, a shadow under water—and was gone before I could name it. "It comes out of me sideways. Like I'm quoting something I never read."

"Careful," I said, tapping the table. "In this city, unaccounted-for anything gets you a visit from the wardens."

"Then I'll account for it." They reached across the table—not grabbing, just resting their hand palm-up near mine, an offer rather than a claim. "Ask me anything. I'll give you the accounting."

And gods forgive me, I wanted to. I wanted to open them up like a clock and see every gear. But asking questions invites answers, and answers invite more questions, and somewhere down that road is the question *who are you really*, and I had spent three years making sure nobody asked *me* that. So instead I covered their hand with mine, and we stayed like that while the Kettle emptied around us, two liars holding hands over a table, each presumably convinced the other was the honest one.

When we parted at the corner of Rope Street—Wren walking backward a few steps, still facing me, still smiling, which I would remember later and hate myself for finding charming—I stood in the cold and let myself feel it. All of it. The warmth in the palms, the stupid lightness in the chest, the terrifying sense that the shelf in my head where I'd stored the sensible creature had developed a leak.

Three years in Greyhollow, I had been content. Or I had been *arranged* into something shaped like contentment, the way you arrange flowers: cut, placed, kept fresh in water that isn't yours. And in one dinner, Wren Vale had made contentment feel like a coat that no longer fit.

That was Tuesday.

*

Thursday night, the black-market dealer found me—or rather, I found him, which is how he preferred to tell it. He worked the far end of the night market, past the tallow-renderers' stalls, under a canvas awning painted with fish so faded they looked like ghosts of fish. Everything he sold was technically contraband and practically harmless: prayer cards from cities that had burned, dice carved from questionable bone, almanacs printed before the Warding with margins full of marginal handwriting.

He'd been saving something for me, he said. He said that every time. It was his way of doubling the price.

"A book," he said, producing a bundle of oilcloth from beneath his counter with the ceremony of a man revealing a relic. "Came in with a caravan from the eastern passes. Banned twice over—once by the old kingdom, once by the Wardens' Compact. Prophecy work. Pre-Warding."

"I sell salves," I said. "I buy salt. Books are above my station."

"Mm." He unwrapped a corner, and the smell came out—old vellum and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air in a cellar where nothing has lived for a long time. "But you looked at it. I saw you look."

I had looked. That was my mistake. With banned things, looking *is* touching, and touching is buying, and I knew this, and I looked anyway.

Because here is the thing about Greyhollow: it is a city built on forgetting. The Warding stripped the magic out of the stones sixty years ago, and the wardens strip the memory of magic out of the people, patiently, generation by generation. But forgetting leaves edges. A scar itches. A song you can't place hums in your sleep. And every exile in this city carries a hole in themselves shaped exactly like the life they lost. The dealers know it. They sell us our own missing pieces back at scandalous markup—prayer cards, folk songs, prophecy books. We buy because a scar that itches is at least proof you have skin.

"How much?" I said.

He named a figure that would have emptied my strongbox. I named a figure that wouldn't. We met in the middle, the way business between desperate people always meets in the middle, and I carried the book home wrapped in my shawl like a sick child.

*

My rooms above the dye-shop were small and smelled permanently of mordant and iron. I lit two candles, locked the door, and put the book on my table.

It was called *The Turning of the Wheel Under the Mountain*—no author, no printer's mark, the text copied in four different hands, which meant it had been passed along and added to for generations, each keeper writing their piece before handing the burden on. The early pages were the usual prophetic slurry: kings dying, rivers running backward, a winter that lasts until someone remembers summer. I skimmed, disappointed. Then I reached a page in the third hand, newer ink, tighter letters, and stopped.

*And in the age of ash and quiet skies, the storm shall hide itself in a city of salt and law, wearing a merchant's face, selling small charms against great weather.*

I read it again. Then a third time, moving my lips, the way you do when you're checking whether a thing is real.

Salt and law. Greyhollow is famous for exactly two exports: sea-salt and ordinances. Small charms against great weather. I sell luck-knots and weather-salves. My hands went cold in a distant, academic way, the way they go cold reading someone else's bad news.

I turned the page.

*A stranger comes to the hidden one, and the hidden one will not know them, for the thief comes smiling.*

The candle flame leaned. There was no draft. I checked—I always check—but there was no draft, and the flame leaned anyway, away from the book, the way a dog leans away from a door it doesn't like.

*The thief comes smiling.*

Word for word. Not the sense of it, not the spirit—*word for word*. *When you stop smiling, Astrid Whitlock, it looks like weather about to break.* No—that wasn't it, that wasn't the line, Wren hadn't said *thief*, hadn't said any of this. But I sat there with the phrase ringing in my skull and tried to reconstruct Thursday's dinner, tried to find the exact boundary between coincidence and quotation, and the boundary wasn't where I needed it to be. People don't quote books they haven't read. But books—banned books, prophetic books—sometimes seem to quote the future, and the future, apparently, had been having conversations with Wren Vale without consulting me.

I told myself: it's a common construction. Old prophecies are stock phrases stitched together. *Comes smiling* appears in a dozen traditions. Any charming stranger fits. Any hidden anything fits. Prophecy is a net with holes wide enough to drive a caravan through, and only fools catch themselves in it.

This was excellent reasoning. I made it thoroughly, and I believed it approximately none of the way down.

Because there was one more page. The last leaf, in the fourth hand—the tightest, oldest-seeming script, written by someone pressing hard, someone who wanted these words to survive. And beside the text, an illustration, ink and faded pigment, the colors gone strange with age: the red shifted to rust, the blue to a bruise-gray.

Two figures, walking into the dark mouth of a mountain. One wore traveling clothes and carried a staff tipped with a knot—I stared at that knot until my eyes watered, because I knew that knot, I *invented* that knot, or rather my hands had tied it ten thousand times into luck-charms without my ever deciding to, a pattern of three loops and a broken fourth that my grandmother certainly never taught me. The second figure walked half a step ahead, turning back, and even in the degraded illustration, even in rust-and-bruise ink, the face was unmistakable.

Gray eyes. Sharp cheekbone. Smiling.

I put the book down. I picked it up again, because putting it down felt like admitting what I'd seen, and I looked at the figure until the candle guttered, telling myself: *it's a woodcut, they're generic, faces in old illustrations are generic, anyone with gray eyes—*

Anyone with gray eyes. Anyone who buys charms neither of us needs. Anyone whose spine sits with its back to walls, whose poetry comes out sideways, who quotes the future without knowing it. Anyone who says *I mean everything I say, that's my problem,* when the problem might be precisely what they mean.

The sensible creature on its high shelf had gotten its lantern lit somehow and was screaming down at me, and I lay awake until dawn listening to it, doing arithmetic I didn't like. Because here was the sum I kept arriving at, no matter how I shuffled the numbers: either I had just bought a cursed book and infected my own imagination, or I had spent Tuesday evening holding hands with the villain of a century-old prophecy—and the villain had complimented my smile.

Outside, Greyhollow woke up lawful and magicless, bells ringing the hour, salt-carts rattling past. Ordinary. Safe. The whole city humming its little anti-magic tune, and me lying in it like a struck bell that refuses to stop vibrating.

I got up. I dressed. I told myself I would go to the market and sell salves and be a charm-seller and nothing else, and I almost believed it.

But the book sat on my table with its last page facing the ceiling, and every time I passed it, those two figures walked into the mountain again, one of them wearing my knot, one of them wearing Wren Vale's smile—and beneath the illustration, in that hard-pressing hand, the final line of the final page, which I had read a dozen times now and which read, simply:

*One enters. One leaves. The mountain keeps the difference.*

I blew out the candle. The figures stayed.
