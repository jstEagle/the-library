## 3. What the Law Forgets

There are three ways to cross the Sallow Bridge without being seen by a warden, and I have used all of them in the last week alone.

The first is the obvious one: don't be seen. This sounds like a joke until you've spent an evening learning Greyhollow's habits of attention. Wardens watch faces, not bodies. They watch for fear. Walk like a man late for dinner and you are furniture. The second way is the lantern queue on the bridge's underside — a maintenance ledge barely wide enough for a goat with regrets, running beneath the planks where the wardens' sightlines end because no one ever thought to look down at the river they cross every day. That is the thing about Greyhollow that took me longest to understand: it isn't a city without magic. It's a city that decided magic was someone else's problem, and then stopped looking at anything it didn't want to be a problem.

The third way is to be waved through by the wardens themselves, which requires either a bribe or a badge, and I have both, though only one of them is mine.

Tonight I take the ledge.

The river under the bridge is black and patient, smelling of tannin and old iron, and I move along it with my palm flat against wet stone while my boots find their footing by memory rather than courage. Above me, through the gaps between planks, lamplight falls in stripes across my shoulders. A warden's boots pass overhead — two sets, unhurried, mid-conversation about someone's wife and a debt involving a horse. I could tell them exactly which stable that horse came from. I know things about this city that its own law doesn't know, because I spent four months mapping what the law forgets: the blind corners, the bribed turnkeys, the cellars where charms change hands under the smell of pickled herring. Four months of walking Greyhollow's veins with a pencil stub and a head full of other people's orders.

And one month of something else. One month of Astrid Whitlock's laugh, which is the part I did not map, because there were no orders for that.

I drop from the ledge onto the far bank, brush silt from my knees, and walk into the Tanners' Quarter like any honest citizen heading home.

* * *

My rooms are above a dye-shop that smells permanently of vinegar and ruined wool, three flights up behind a door that sticks unless you lift the handle and curse it in a specific order. The landlady thinks I'm a surveyor's clerk. She thinks this because I told her so, and also because I once fixed her roof gutter, and in Greyhollow competence is more convincing than identity.

I light no lamp. Instead I go to the window, check the rooftops out of habit — nothing but cats and chimney smoke — and then take the whistle from around my neck.

It's bone. Of course it's bone. Everything below the mountain is bone; they can't help themselves, the way the sky-court can't help being weather and the Registry can't help being paperwork made flesh. It sits warm against my sternum even now, warmer than it should, the way a hand rests on you when it wants reassurance that you're still there. I hold it in my fist and I do not blow it yet.

Because here is my confession, made to a dark room and a river I can't see: I have been standing at this window every night for eleven days deciding whether to report tonight's meeting honestly.

Tonight's meeting. Dinner. Astrid had a crumb of bread at the corner of their mouth for ten minutes and I said nothing, because telling them would have meant looking directly at their mouth, and I am apparently not strong enough for that. They talked about the night market the way some people talk about a lover they're trying to quit — complaining about the rent on their stall, the damp getting into the salves, the neighbor who plays the fiddle badly at all hours — and underneath every complaint was this fierce, stubborn tenderness, the tenderness of someone who has decided to love a place precisely because it will never love them back. They sold luck to people who think luck is a superstition. They sold weather in a bottle to a city that outlawed weather. And every charm they tied was a small act of treason wrapped in twine, and they thought nobody noticed.

I noticed. Noticing is my job. It's the only part of my job I've ever been good at.

I raise the whistle. Lower it. Raise it again.

Somewhere below, the fiddler starts up, badly, right on schedule. I press my forehead against the cool glass and think about how Astrid looked when the candle guttered — not frightened, never frightened, just attentive, the way they get when a storm is still over the horizon but already in their teeth. They'd said, *You ask questions like a man taking inventory.* And I'd laughed, because it was funny, and because laughing kept me from answering.

What would I have said? *Yes. I'm taking inventory. Your favorite color. The scar on your left thumb. The way you flinch at thunder — wait, especially the way you flinch at thunder, that one's going in the report.*

I blow the whistle. Softly. Three notes, the third bent flat, the signal that says *report follows, nothing urgent.* The sound doesn't carry the way sound should. It goes somewhere else entirely, the way letters fall into wells.

Then I speak to the dark above the rooftops, quietly, in the trade-tongue of the deep court, which tastes like cold stone and old promises.

"The bearer is cooperative. Trusts me. Progressing on schedule." A pause. "No complications."

That last part is a lie. The complications are considerable and I have given one of them dinner.

The silence that answers is long enough to make my skin crawl. When the voice comes, it doesn't come through my ears so much as through the bones of my skull, which is, I suppose, appropriate.

*"Schedule,"* it repeats, in a tone like frost forming. *"The eclipse is in nineteen days. You have had a month and produced a dinner date."*

"A month has produced trust. Trust produces compliance. Compliance produces—" I stop myself before I say *the prophecy*, because saying it aloud makes it heavier. "It proceeds. I'll deliver."

*"See that you do, heirling. The Registry does not forgive delay, and it forgets favors faster than debts."*

The pressure lifts. The whistle goes cold in my hand, and I stand at the window feeling like a man who has just lied to his own reflection.

Heirling. They still call me that. Nineteen days, and then I walk into the mountain as something other than a courier, and everything I've been pretending to be gets filed away in the bone halls along with everyone else's forgotten years. There's a version of me waiting down there that I've never met — the heir who was taken up as an infant and raised as a tool, taught the surface world the way you teach a hunting bird: thoroughly, affectionately, and always with a tether.

Except nobody warned the falconers that the bird might like the mouse.

I put the whistle away. I lie down on my narrow bed in my false life in my borrowed city, and I make myself say it plainly, because plainness is the only discipline I have left:

I like them. More than the mission allows. More than is safe, more than is wise, more than the schedule permits. And in nineteen days, either I hand them over to a court that files souls like receipts, or I don't — and I have no idea which of those I'm capable of, and that uncertainty frightens me more than the Registry ever could.

Sleep takes its time. When it comes, I dream of a bridge with no underside.

* * *

Morning finds me doing something unforgivable: enjoying myself.

Greyhollow wakes slowly and dishonestly, shopkeepers opening shutters with the enthusiasm of men digging graves, gulls arguing over the fish market's leavings. I buy two meat pies from the woman on Cordwainer Row who puts too much pepper in them, which is why I buy from her, and I eat one leaning against a wall in the sun like a person with nowhere urgent to be. Which is true, until noon.

At noon I have a job. Not the mission — a smaller, meaner piece of work, the kind the Registry farms out to keep its tools oiled. There's a bookdealer off Ropewalk Lane who sells banned material out of a cellar behind his legitimate stock, and among his inventory, according to last week's intelligence, is a volume of prophecy. Old. Illustrated. The kind of thing that shouldn't exist above ground at all, let alone be priced at six silver and shelved next to treatises on drainage.

My instructions regarding it were simple: confirm it exists, confirm no copies, and leave it in place. Unwatched books are bait. The Registry doesn't burn prophecies; it uses them. Somewhere below the mountain, clerks with voices like frost are betting on which desperate soul walks into that cellar and buys their future.

So naturally, at half past noon, I descend into Marrow's cellar to confirm the book exists, and find myself standing in front of it wanting to buy it.

It's smaller than I expected. Calfskin gone soft with handling, spine cracked, title rubbed to ghosts. *The Turning of the Ninth Age,* and beneath it in faded gilt, *with figures.* I open it with the care you'd use on a sleeping animal.

The illustrations are woodcuts, crude and strange, ink gone brown with age. Cities burning. A mountain with doors. And then — I turn a page and my stomach drops through the floor of the world —

Two figures, entering the mountain side by side. One crowned in storm-light. One smiling.

*The thief comes smiling.*

I read that line in Astrid's voice, over candlelight, three nights ago, word for word, and I had laughed and asked where they got such a strange turn of phrase, and they'd shrugged and said it felt like something remembered. Felt like something remembered. They quoted a banned prophecy over dinner like a line from a song, and I sat there holding my cup and thinking: *they're closer to the text than I am, and I've had it memorized since childhood.*

"Wren Vale." Marrow's voice, dry as old glue, from the top of the stairs. "Didn't expect you till Thursday."

"Changed my route." I close the book gently, as if gentleness could retroactively excuse me. "Just browsing."

"You've been browsing in my banned section for twenty minutes. Nobody browses the banned section. They come for a thing." He descends, wiping his hands on an apron stained forty colors, and gives me the look of a man who has sold secrets long enough to price honesty accurately. "You want it. Six silver."

"I'm not buying it."

"Everyone says that." He leans against a shelf. "Here's what I know about you, Wren Vale. You've been in my cellar four times. First time you bought nothing and memorized my shelves. Second time you asked after prophecy traffic, casual as you please, like a man asking after the weather. Third time you bought a luck-knot from the storm-caller's stall on your way here, which was sweet, and stupid, and noted by at least two people who sell information for a living." He lets that sit. "You're not a surveyor's clerk."

My heart is doing something unprofessional. "And what am I?"

"Honestly? Haven't decided. That's usually my business, deciding what people are. But you—" he taps the book's cover without opening it, "—you keep coming back to this one like it owes you money. Or like you owe it. So. Six silver, and we never discuss it again. Or you leave empty-handed, and I wonder aloud, in public, about the charming young man who keeps visiting forbidden books. Your call."

I should leave. My instructions are explicit: leave it in place. Bait stays bait. If I remove it, the Registry loses its hook, and if the Registry loses its hook, the clerks lose their patience, and I have seen what the clerks look like when they lose patience. There is a room beneath the mountain where the walls remember screaming, and I was shown it on my first day home, as orientation.

But Astrid is going to find this book. I realize it with total certainty, the way you realize rain is coming — not from evidence but from the ache in an old wound. They quoted it once; they'll hunt it. They'll come down these stairs within days, maybe hours, hungry for the shape of their own future, and Marrow will sell it to them gladly, and they'll open to the final page, the one I haven't turned to yet, the one I refuse to turn to—

—and see themselves walking into the mountain beside me. And see which one of us the picture leaves alone.

Unless they never find it. Unless it's sitting on my windowsill instead, where I can decide what they learn and when, and soften it, and stand next to them when they read it, and say the true thing I should have said weeks ago: *it's me, it's been me the whole time, and I hate the page as much as you will.*

That's not protection. I know that's not protection. That's theft dressed up as mercy, the oldest trick in the Registry's ledger, and I learned it at their knee.

"Five silver," I hear myself say, "and you never saw me."

Marrow smiles with all the warmth of a ledger. "Six. And I never saw anyone."

I pay him. I wrap the book in brown paper, tuck it inside my coat against my ribs, where it lies against the whistle like two halves of a sentence I can't finish. On the stairs, Marrow calls after me, mild as milk:

"Tell your storm-caller the fiddle player's moved stalls. East row now. She'll want to know."

He knows. Of course he knows. In this city, the law forgets everything except what it can sell.

* * *

I spend the evening walking.

Not toward anywhere — away from everywhere, circuit after circuit of the city walls, the book a small hard weight against my chest, the whistle a smaller colder one. The sun goes down over Greyhollow and the lamps come up in their grudging way, and I try to compose the report I'll send tonight, the one where I confess that the bait is now in my coat and the mission is now compromised by a variable the Registry didn't plan for, namely: I have started to want things.

Wanting is not permitted below the mountain. Wanting is what souls do, and souls are inventory. I was raised on that arithmetic. Desire is a leak; plug it.

But here's what four months of Astrid Whitlock has done to my arithmetic: they sell luck to a city that forbids it, and every knot they tie says *you deserve good things* in a language the law can't arrest. They flinch at thunder and won't say why. They laughed tonight — yesterday, at dinner, whatever tonight is now — with their whole body, like laughing was a place they lived. And I sat across from them wearing a face I built for a purpose, and I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *if the prophecy needs one of us to leave alone, let it be me.*

Which is treason of a very specific flavor. Because the page shows what it shows. The page has always shown what it shows. And I have known, since I was a child reciting the ninth age in a classroom carved from femurs, exactly how the figure enters the mountain and which figure the artist drew walking out.

I climb to my rooms. I set the book on the table and stare at it for a long time, the brown paper catching the last grey light. Then, because cowardice deserves at least accuracy, I open it to the final page.

Two figures. Side by side. The mountain's mouth yawning behind them.

One leaves alone.

I won't tell you which one the woodcut shows walking out. Not because I don't know. Because I've known for twenty years, and knowing has never once changed it, and tomorrow — or soon, soon — Astrid is going to come looking for this book, and I am going to have to decide whether they find it on Marrow's shelf, where fate left it, or in my rooms, where I hid it like a man hiding a diagnosis.

The whistle warms against my skin. Report time. Three notes, bent flat.

I look at the book. I look at the window. Nineteen days.

Outside, far off toward the market district, the sky flickers — a heat-lightning shimmer, there and gone, the kind of thing a magicless city explains away as eyesight or wine. But I know storm-caller weather when I see it, and I know whose stall sits at the east end of the night market.

They're restless too. Good, whispers the part of me that is still a tool. Restless bearers move on schedule.

And the rest of me — the rest of me picks up the book, wraps it tighter, and wonders how long I can stand between a person and their own story before the story notices I'm in the way.
