## 4. The Book with Their Face In It

I want it noted, for the record I am currently keeping in my own head because no court in this city would accept it as evidence, that I did not sleep that night.

I lay in my narrow bed above the shop with the shutters closed and the rain doing its honest, magicless work against the glass, and I replayed dinner like a merchant counting coins she suspects are shaved. *The thief comes smiling.* Wren had said it over the wine, laughing at their own joke about the luck-knots, and I had laughed too, because Wren Vale had a laugh you wanted to be near, warm and slightly surprised at itself, like a fire catching in a room everyone thought was cold.

And then at midnight I sat up in bed and said it out loud to the dark, and the dark said nothing back, which is how I knew it was bad. My own magic has always answered me when I talk to it — a little static on the tongue, a taste of ozone, a hum under the floorboards. The dark just stayed dark. Which meant this wasn't magic talking. This was memory.

I have a scar where a memory should be. Three years old, smooth as river stone, sitting right behind my eyes where something used to live. Most days I don't touch it. That night I pressed on it like a bruise until dawn came up grey, and then I got dressed and went to find Quill.

* * *

Quill's shop doesn't have a sign. It has a door with a knock pattern, a different one every week, and if you knock wrong he opens it a crack, sees your face, and closes it again with the finality of a man shutting a coffin. I knocked right — three slow, two fast, pause, one — and heard him shuffling before I saw the light.

"Astrid Whitlock." He squinted at me through the gap. "You look terrible."

"I need a book."

"Everyone needs a book. That's why I sell them."

"I need *that* book."

He looked at me for a long moment, the way a man looks at weather he doesn't trust. Then he opened the door.

Inside, his shop smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sourness of paper that has been hidden from authorities for a long time. Quill is a dealer in banned things — mostly minor heresies, almanacs printed before the Purification, ballads about the Night of Broken Thunder that get you fined if you're caught humming them. He'd sold me the prophecy book once before. Sold. And then, two weeks later, come to my stall white-faced and bought it back off me at triple price, saying wardens were sniffing around prophecy traffic, saying he couldn't have it traced to me of all people, saying — and this is the part I keep turning over like a stone — *you especially shouldn't be reading it, Whitlock.*

At the time I thought he meant: you're an exile, exiles don't get hobbies.

Now I wondered what else he might have meant.

"It burned," Quill said, pouring tea he didn't offer me.

"You don't burn anything. You once tried to sell me a candle stub you claimed was blessed by a saint who turned out not to exist."

"The saint existed. He was just very badly behaved." He set down the pot. "The book burned. Tragic. Paper is flammable."

"Quill."

"Astrid."

"I saw the illustration," I said quietly. "Before you took it back. The thief comes smiling. There was a picture."

His face did something complicated, the way a market does when the wardens turn down the wrong street — everything rearranging itself while pretending nothing happened. "Lots of pictures in that book. Old woodcuts. Ugly ones."

"One of them had a woman with storm-grey eyes standing next to a smiling man. Under a mountain." I leaned forward. "You knew. You looked at my face when you bought it back, and you *knew*, and you still charged me triple."

"That's called commerce." But his hands weren't steady on the teapot, and Quill's hands are always steady; they've picked locks inside warden stations. "It's called looking out for you, if we're being sentimental, which I'd rather we weren't."

"I'm being robbed of my own life by inches," I said, "and I'd like to stop it."

Silence. Rain on the roof. Somewhere below, the night market setting up, the rattle of stalls, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending it has no magic in it, which is the most magical thing Greyhollow does.

Quill sighed the sigh of a man losing money. "Third shelf. Behind the false almanac. If anyone asks, I sold you a cookbook."

* * *

I didn't take it home.

Home felt wrong for it — my little rooms with their careful mundanity, the salves and knots, the life built like a fence against exactly this kind of thing. Instead I climbed to the top of the tannery steps where the night watch never bothers because the smell keeps everyone honest, and I sat under the eaves with the rain coming down soft, and I opened *The Sigil and the Storm* — because that was its title, I remembered now, I had known it all along and let myself forget — and I read it properly this time, cover to cover, like a woman reading her own sentence.

It's an ugly book. Not the prose — the prose is the usual prophecy mush, all thrones and eclipses, written by someone who clearly believed adjectives were free. The ugliness is in the margins, in the woodcuts, in the way it reads less like a vision and more like *instructions*. A ledger wearing a poem's clothes.

The first pages are general. Courts beneath mountains. Debts paid in souls. The Registry — the Pale Registry, it names them, clerks of the deep who keep accounts of everything a person is and file it away like tax records. I read those pages with the detached interest of someone reading about foreign wars. Terrible, presumably. Far away. Not my business.

Then the middle sections narrowed. The mountain under Greyhollow — not *near*, not *beyond the hills*, but under, its roots going down past the cellars of the world into halls lit by bone. The city built on top of it without knowing, or knowing and choosing blindness, which amounts to the same thing in a place like this. The debt owed since before the Purification. The eclipse that comes due. And the figures moving through it all like chess pieces: the soul-bearer, marked by weather; the thief, who comes smiling; the heir who forgot their name.

I read that last one three times. *The heir who forgot their name.* It sounded like a tragedy. It sounded, now that I thought about Wren's easy laugh, their charm that never quite landed anywhere specific, their uncanny knowledge of which alleys the wardens skipped — it sounded like a job description.

But it was the last page that stopped my heart, and I mean that almost clinically; there was a moment where I genuinely had to put my hand flat on my chest and wait.

Two woodcuts on the facing page. The left: two figures walking down, into a mountain's opened mouth, torchlight carving them out of black. One tall, narrow-shouldered, hair escaping its braid in wind that shouldn't exist underground — drawn with a tilt of the head I have seen in mirrors my whole life, with eyes the engraver had hatched in grey, storm-grey, unmistakably, insultingly mine. Beside them, the second figure: smiling. Of course smiling. The smile rendered with real skill, warm and crooked and slightly surprised at itself, and I knew it the way you know your own handwriting, and I hated that I knew it, and some traitorous chamber behind my ribs ached even while the rest of me went cold.

Wren. Me. Entering the mountain together, drawn a hundred years before either of us was born, or at least before either of us could have been born — I checked the printing date, twice, because I wanted so badly to be wrong about it.

And the right-hand cut. The exit. One figure climbing out of the same mouth of stone, alone, small against the vast dark, and the other figure nowhere. Not dead, exactly — the engraver hadn't drawn death, hadn't drawn a grave or a fallen body or any of the iconography grief usually demands. Just absent. Left behind. Filed away, I thought, thinking of the Pale Registry and their accounts. A soul returned to inventory.

One leaves alone.

I sat there a long time. The rain kept up its work. Below me the market chattered, and somewhere in it, probably, Wren was buying roasted chestnuts from Marta and charming her into giving extra, and being charming at me tomorrow, and I sat under the eaves with a stolen book open on my knees and understood that I was not reading a prophecy.

I was reading a script. With casting already done.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about terror: it gets boring. Oh, the first minutes are magnificent — the cold flood, the ringing ears, the animal certainty that you must run, hide, burn the book, leave the city, change your name. I had all of it. I gave it a full quarter hour, generously. And then it began to repeat itself, the same images cycling, the same dread re-served cold, and underneath the repetition something else started up, quiet at first, the way my storms start — a pressure change, a wrongness in the air, a feeling like the sky holding its breath before it decides.

Anger.

Because here's what occurred to me, sitting in the rain-smell with my whole future engraved in ugly woodcut: whoever wrote this book, whoever cut these images, whoever filed my face and Wren's smile into a century-old ledger — they assumed I'd comply. That's the whole architecture of prophecy, isn't it? It's not prediction. It's *expectation*. Someone wrote down what they intended, dressed it as fate, and waited for the actors to show up and say their lines. The thief comes smiling because the thief was *sent*. The soul-bearer walks into the mountain because she was *sold* there, piece by piece, memory by memory, until the only self remaining was the one that fit the page.

They'd taken three years of my life already. I had the scar to prove it — the smooth place behind my eyes where something had been cut away, and I suddenly understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that the missing years weren't collateral damage. They were *edits*. Someone had redlined me. Cut the scenes where I might have refused.

Well.

I am, by trade, a maker of small charms. Luck-knots, weather-salves, little braided promises that do almost nothing and cost almost nothing, and I have spent three years telling myself this was humility, this was safety, this was a woman content with a small life. Sitting there, I recognized it for what it also was: practice. Years of hiding power in plain craft. Years of making the ordinary do extraordinary work without anyone noticing. If they wanted a soul-bearer who walked obediently into the dark because the book said so —

— they had made a serious error in their accounting. The Registry keeps ledgers. Fine. So do I. And every knot I ever tied, every salve I ever sold, every scrap of storm I caged behind mundane stitching went into an account they had never audited. I was not empty. I was *stockpiled*.

The pressure in the air changed. Above the tannery roofs, the clouds — honest, magicless clouds, the kind Greyhollow permits — rolled and thickened, and a single thread of lightning stitched them briefly together, far off, silent. Coincidence, probably. The city's wards eat weather-magic for breakfast; nothing of mine could reach that high, that far.

Probably.

I closed the book. I held it in both hands and seriously considered dropping it off the tannery roof into the flooded gutter, letting the ink run, unmaking the script the cheap way. It would have been satisfying. It would have accomplished nothing — you don't kill a prophecy by drowning one copy; the words are already loose, already in heads, already in the shape of a smile I can't stop wanting to see again tomorrow evening at seven o'clock, damn it all.

No. If there was a script, I needed to know all my lines so I could refuse them precisely. That's the difference between defiance and tantrum: defiance reads the whole contract first.

So I wrapped the book in oilcloth, tucked it inside my coat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and climbed down the tannery steps into the wet noise of the market. And I made myself a promise, there among the stalls and lanterns, with the stolen centuries pressing warm against my side:

Nobody casts me but me. Whatever waits under that mountain, whatever Wren Vale is — thief, heir, liar, the only person in three years whose company hasn't felt like holding my breath — they will learn that the woman in their woodcut does not take direction.

Tomorrow was our third date. Wren had suggested the noodle stall by the canal, casual as anything, easy as anything, *smiling as anything*, and I had said yes before the dread arrived, and now the dread lived in me rent-free and I was going anyway.

Not because the prophecy said we'd walk into the mountain together.

Because I wanted to hear what Wren said next — and watch, very carefully, for the moment the smile became a line delivery.

The rain thickened as I walked home, and though I told myself it was only weather, I swear the clouds moved the way they move when I'm angry, and I swear — I swear — one streetlamp flickered as I passed, just once, like a nod.
