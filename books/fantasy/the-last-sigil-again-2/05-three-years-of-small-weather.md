## 5. Three Years of Small Weather

There is a hole in me shaped like a night.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that if you run your finger along the inside of my memory the way you'd run it along a shelf of books, everything is there in order—childhood, apprenticeship, the years of learning to listen to clouds—and then somewhere between one autumn and another there's a gap where a book should be. The neighbors are intact. The dust rings are there, marking exactly how much space the missing thing occupied. And every so often my mind reaches for that space out of habit, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and comes away aching with nothing.

Three years ago, someone took a night out of my head.

This is what I know: I was a storm-caller. This is what everyone knows: on the Night of Broken Thunder, something happened over Greyhollow, and by morning the wardens had writs with my name spelled correctly for once, and I walked out of the only city I'd ever loved before they could walk me out. What I do not know—the thing I cannot know, because knowing it was surgically removed from me—is why.

I've had three years to make peace with that. I have made instead a kind of furniture out of not-knowing. A chair I sit in. A table I eat off. Some nights it's almost comfortable.

Almost.

* * *

Let me tell you about the weather I kept.

When the wardens stripped my storm-calling—or when I buried it, the records disagree and so does my body—they missed the small things. Or maybe they didn't miss them; maybe they simply couldn't be bothered, the way an executioner can't be bothered to sharpen his blade for anything less than a head. What remained in me after the purge was weather the way a child makes weather: breath-fog on cold glass, the static charge that makes your hair lift before lightning, the particular pressure behind your eyes that means rain in six hours, guaranteed or your money back.

So I sold charms. Luck-knots braided from grey market twine, each one carrying a whisper of genuine pressure-change, enough to turn a gambler's bad hand or keep a fisherman's nets dry one extra hour. Weather-salves rendered down from tallow and—this was the trick, the small dishonesty I permitted myself—condensed humidity I pulled from the air with two fingers behind my back while pretending to stir. The customers thought it was herbs. Herbs were legal. Herbs were boring. Herbs were exactly the camouflage a fugitive storm-caller needed, because nobody raids the stall of the woman who smells faintly of lavender and talks too much about her joints.

I say woman. I say a lot of things. Greyhollow never asked me to fill out paperwork, which is one of its virtues, and I chose my shape here the way I chose everything else: carefully, and with an exit in mind.

The night market taught me contentment. Or rather, it taught me the performance of contentment, which after enough repetitions becomes indistinguishable from the real thing, like a forged signature practiced until even the forger believes it. I had a stall between a candle-dipper who hummed hymns to no god in particular and a knife-sharpener who'd once, allegedly, shaved a warden's beard without waking him. I had regulars. Old Tomas bought a luck-knot every month for his grandson's exams and every month told me the same joke about examiners and their mothers. The twins who ran the pie cart saved me the burnt-corner pieces, knowing I liked them best, knowing I'd pretend not to know they were saving them.

It was a life. It was small as a coin and I held it up to the light every day and found no cracks, mostly because I'd stopped looking for cracks. You can grieve yourself to death over a missing night, or you can sell salves. I chose salves. Most days.

But here is what nobody tells you about amputation: the ghost limb doesn't hurt all the time. It hurts on schedule. Mine came at dusk, when the sky over Greyhollow did its nightly performance—the smog-blush, the first stars fighting through, the wind coming off the mountain smelling of stone and old snow—and every nerve I still owned would stand up and reach for a storm that wasn't mine anymore. Three years of that. Three years of standing at my stall at closing time with my hands flat on the wood, feeling the atmosphere roll overhead like a great animal I used to ride, and telling myself: this is enough. This is the deal. You don't get to remember why you made it, but you made it, and whatever it cost, you paid it, and the city is still standing.

Because that was the part I clung to. Whatever happened on the Night of Broken Thunder, Greyhollow survived it. Every person in the night market, every burnt pie corner, every terrible joke about examiners—all of it existed downstream of whatever I'd done, or failed to do, or been made to pay for. My missing night had purchased *this*. I built my whole peace on that foundation.

Foundations made of holes bear weight surprisingly well. Right up until someone asks you what's underneath.

* * *

The first year, I tried to reconstruct the night from outside sources. This is what exiles do; we're historians of our own erasure, working from fragments, testimony, and spite.

Marrow the ledger-keeper—a former court archivist turned black-market information broker, a man whose memory was reportedly perfect and whose personality was accordingly insufferable—sold me three documents relating to the Night of Broken Thunder. I paid more than I should have. He charged extra for the courtesy of not asking questions, and then asked questions anyway, because Marrow.

The first document was a warden incident report, heavily redacted. Black bars marched across it like a funeral procession. What remained: a date, a location (the eastern quarter, near the old aqueduct), a casualty figure of zero, and a note in the margin in some supervisor's cramped hand: *"Property damage consistent with Class IV atmospheric event. Source unresolved. Recommend continued observation."* Class IV. That's the classification for a storm that levels buildings. There were no leveled buildings. Zero casualties. A Class IV event that killed no one and broke nothing—which meant either the report was wrong, or the storm had been aimed, or the storm had been *caught*.

The second document was a fragment of a ballad. Ballads spring up around Greyhollow disasters the way mold springs up around damp, and the wardens burn them faster than they can be copied, which is how you know they're accurate. Someone had salvaged eight lines:

*And the sky came down like a creditor,*
*and the mountain opened wide its ledger,*
*and what was owed was paid that night*
*in something dearer far than weather.*

I read those lines perhaps four hundred times. *Something dearer far than weather.* Gold? No—Greyhollow had little and hid less. Blood? The casualty count said zero. Then what? What does a city pay when it can't pay in blood or coin, when the collector has already specified the currency?

The third document was a bill of sale. Unsigned. Undated except by inference. It named no buyer and no seller, described no goods, and consisted entirely of terms: *held in perpetuity until such time as the debt is discharged or the term expires; the bearer shall remain whole and functional; the asset shall not be damaged, divided, or displayed.* Asset. Whole and functional. Not damaged, divided, or *displayed*.

You can see how a person might read those words and feel cold without knowing why. I read them in Marrow's back room by the light of a lamp he charged me for, and I felt the way you feel when a stranger on the street uses your childhood nickname: nothing you can point to, everything you can't shake.

"Odd lot," Marrow said, watching me with his terrible archival eyes. "Most clients want to know what happened. You're buying documents about what *wasn't* lost."

"I don't follow."

"No," he said, "you wouldn't yet." He wrapped the papers in oilcloth with the tenderness of a man handling relics, which, in his trade, they were. "A word of professional courtesy, Whitlock. Ledgers balance. Always, eventually, in full. When yours comes due—and ledgers always come due—you'll want to know what you signed. Find the scar."

"What scar?"

"The ones who cut memories out—" he tapped his own temple, "—they're craftsmen, not artists. They leave seams. You'll dream around it. Pay attention to what your dreams refuse to touch."

I told him he was being dramatic. He charged me for the insult. Fair enough.

* * *

Here is what the dreams refuse to touch, in three years of faithful record-keeping:

They refuse to touch rain that falls upward. They refuse to touch a door of green glass that I apparently stood before—I know this because I wake with my palm burning as though I've pressed it flat against something. They refuse to touch a voice. Not words, just voice: a register, a cadence, something patient and vast and *clerkish*, the way a glacier might sound if glaciers did accounting. And they refuse, absolutely and completely, to touch whatever happens in the moment between my falling asleep and my beginning to dream. There's a corridor in every night, a stretch of dark I cross without crossing it, and on the other side I arrive already grieving.

That's the worst detail. Not the green door, not the upward rain. The grief. I wake up mourning, most nights, for something specific and absent, the way you wake from a dream of a dead parent with their laugh still warm in your ear—except I have no dead parent in this equation. I have a hole. And every morning the hole breaks my heart before I'm even awake enough to know what heartbreak is.

Grief for a self you can't fully mourn. That's the sentence I'd give it, if anyone ever asked. You mourn the person you were, but you can't mourn properly, because the thing that made them worth mourning—the choice they made, the courage or the cowardice or the desperate arithmetic of it—is gone. So you're left loving a stranger. Left defending a stranger's honor against your own suspicion. Left wondering, in the small hours, whether the person who owned that missing night was better than you or worse, braver or more afraid, and whether you'd recognize their reasons if you heard them, or whether they'd sound like madness.

Some mornings I hated them. Whoever-I-was. Hated them for walking out of Greyhollow without a fight, for leaving me a stall and a false name and a head full of seams. Some mornings I loved them beyond sense, the way you love a soldier who carried something heavy so you wouldn't have to, even if you never learn what the something was.

Mostly I just missed them. Is that mad? Missing yourself. Setting a place at the table for the version of you that got left behind in a night you'll never find again.

* * *

Second year. The scar spoke once.

Not in a dream—in the market. Mid-afternoon, unremarkable, a customer haggling over luck-knots, and above us a summer cloud went briefly, structurally wrong. Not dangerous. Just... arranged. Stacked in a pattern that had intention in it, the way a sentence has intention, and every hair on my arms stood up, and something under my sternum said—not in words, in *pressure*—*that shape. You know that shape. You made that shape once, bigger.*

Then it passed, and the cloud was a cloud, and the customer wanted to know if the knot worked on dice or only cards, and I answered him in a steady voice while my hands shook so badly I had to braid slow.

That night I sat up with a stub of candle and drew the cloud-shape from memory. I looked at it. I looked at it for hours. And I understood two things with absolute certainty, the way you understand your own name: first, that I had once called a storm large enough to need shapes like that; second, that I had done it *on purpose*, for a reason, and that the reason was good.

I can't prove the second thing. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the one luxury exile affords in bulk. Maybe I was a monster who aimed a Class IV event at the eastern quarter and got lucky. Maybe zero casualties was fortune, not skill. But the certainty lived in my body, in the marrow the ledger-keeper wasn't named for: whatever I did that night, I did it *to save something*. And then I paid. And then I asked—or someone asked for me—to forget the bill.

Who asks to forget their finest hour?

Someone who couldn't live with it. Someone who knew that remembering would rot them, that guilt or pride or grief would hollow them out faster than the loss itself. Someone who chose the hole over the knowledge, and condemned me—the current tenant—to live in the house they'd emptied.

I used to curse them for that. Then I stopped. Because lately, on the bad nights, when the pressure builds behind my eyes and the mountain looms black against the stars and something in me whispers *soon*, I catch myself thinking: maybe they weren't weak. Maybe they saw the whole shape of it—debt, collector, expiry—and understood that a person who remembers everything she traded will either go back for it or die trying, and either way the city pays.

Maybe forgetting wasn't surrender. Maybe it was the last spell I ever cast: a ward around my own heart, keyed to keep me small and safe and selling salves until the term ran out.

Except wards break. Wards always break. And Marrow said ledgers come due, and lately the dreams have been getting louder, the corridor of dark getting shorter, and twice now I've woken speaking a language I don't know in a voice that isn't quite mine, and the word I keep saying—it sounds like *eclipse*—

No. Let me not borrow trouble. Trouble will find me; it always knows the address.

* * *

Third year. Last month, in fact. Which brings me to the night everything I've just described stopped being a private arrangement between me and my missing self, and started becoming a problem with witnesses.

He came to my stall near closing, when the lanterns were low and the crowd had thinned to stragglers and ghosts. Charming, in the way that expensive things are charming—easy smile, easy posture, clothes that pretended to be poor the way my salves pretended to be herbs. He picked up a luck-knot, turned it over with fingers that were too careful, too deliberate, the fingers of someone cataloguing rather than browsing.

"And this one?" he said. "What's it for?"

"Luck," I said. "It says so on the sign. I stand behind my signage."

"You look like someone who needs it more than sells it."

And here's the thing I haven't told anyone, the thing I've been chewing on for weeks like gristle: when he smiled at me, the hole in my head *ached*. Not the usual dusk-ache, the phantom-limb pull toward weather. Something sharper. Specific. As if the missing night had leaned against its walls from the other side. As if somewhere in the sealed archive of me, a clerk heard his voice and sat up and reached for a file marked *him*, and found it—gone, of course, gone like everything else—but the reaching left a bruise.

I sold him the knot. I let him buy me dinner three nights later. I told myself it was loneliness, and it was, partly, loneliness dressed in its good coat. But it was also this: he was the first thing in three years that made the scar speak. And a person will follow any voice that promises to explain the wound—even a smiling one.

Especially a smiling one.

Gods forgive me. I liked him. I liked him before I knew what he was, and I suspect—I *fear*—that's going to matter enormously, very soon, in ways my missing night already knew about.

Because yesterday, at the black-market dealer's stall, wedged between a banned anatomy text and a cookbook for poachers, I found a book. Old. Hand-copied. A century of prophecy pressed between covers the wardens would burn on sight.

I bought it. I shouldn't have. I opened it anyway.

And on the page I fell open to—of all the pages, of all the centuries of verse—there was a line about a thief who comes smiling.

I went very cold, right there in the dealer's stall, with my new charm-buyer's face floating up out of my memory wearing exactly that expression.

The hole in my head ached like a tooth. Like a bell. Like a debt.

Tomorrow I'm seeing him again. Tomorrow I'll watch him smile and try to reconcile it with ink a hundred years old. And tomorrow night, if I can't—if the dread wins—I'm going to do something I swore I'd never do.

I'm going to open the book past the verse, to the illustrations, and find out whether prophecy remembered the face that my own head refuses to hold.
