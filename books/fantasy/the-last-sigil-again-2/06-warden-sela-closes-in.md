## 6. Warden Sela Closes In

I have been a warden of Greyhollow for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned three things about the city I keep: it lies to itself, it lies to me, and the only honest thing in it is the ledger.

Everything else — every confession, every permit, every smiling shopkeeper who swears on his mother's grave that he's never so much as sneezed sideways at magic — goes through the ledger eventually. Numbers don't perform. Numbers don't flatter. When a man claims he sold forty candles last month and his supplier invoices him for sixty, I don't need a scryer. I need arithmetic.

So when the night-market charm-sellers' tallies started climbing in a pattern that made no sense, I did what I always do. I pulled the ledgers.

And then I sat very still for a long time, because the numbers were lying to me in a way that only magic can.

* * *

Here is what should happen to luck-knots and weather-salves in a city like ours: nothing. Greyhollow is proud of its emptiness. We scrubbed the arcane out of our streets three generations ago, or we tell ourselves we did, and since the Night of Broken Thunder — since the purge that followed it — the wardens have kept the city's magic-free status the way other cities keep their cathedrals. Swept. Guarded. Worshipped at.

A luck-knot is a knot with a story attached. A weather-salve is beeswax and camphor with a prayer folded into the label. They sell because people want to believe they're buying something, not because they do anything. I know this. I've confiscated enough of them to fill a warehouse, and not one of those knots has ever untangled my paperwork.

But in the last six weeks, charm-sales in the Lantern Row market had tripled. Tripled, and not uniformly. Not the way sales climb when a festival comes or a rumor spreads. The increase moved. It drifted north along the row week by week, stall by stall, the way a front moves across a valley — and if you drew the spike on a map of Greyhollow and connected the points, you got a line.

Weather moves in lines. Superstition doesn't.

I put the map down and told myself I was being ridiculous. Then I put it back up, because nineteen years of this work has taught me that the moment I call myself ridiculous is usually the moment I'm right.

* * *

"You think a storm-caller survived," said Warden Pellik. He said it the way men say things they hope will be disproven, leaning in my office doorway with his coat half-on, clearly hoping I'd laugh so he could go home to dinner.

"I think something survived."

"The purge was thorough, Chief. We burned the registries, we burned the—"

"We burned paper." I turned the map toward him. "Storms aren't paper."

He looked at it longer than he wanted to. Pellik is a good second-in-command, which mostly means he disagrees with me in the right order: first privately, then loudly, then obediently. "Even if you're right. Even if some spark of one of them lived through it. Three years, Sela. What's it doing? Selling salves?"

"Waiting," I said. "That's the part that worries me. Magic in hiding doesn't spend. It hoards. And then one day it spends all at once, and we remember why we built the walls in the first place."

Pellik rubbed his jaw. Outside my window, Greyhollow was doing its evening performance of itself — lamplighters, cart-wheels, somebody's terrible fiddle — a city pretending very hard that beneath its cobbles there was nothing but more city.

"If we raid the market on a suspicion," he said, "we'll have every guild-master in Greyhollow howling about harassment before dawn. Charm-sellers are licensed. Registered. They pay their fees and mind their stalls. You'd be turning the whole row against us over a line on a map."

"Yes," I agreed. "It'll be terrible. Do it anyway."

He sighed the sigh of a man whose dinner has just died of natural causes. "You want the raid quiet?"

"No," I said. "I want it loud. Loud makes liars stumble."

* * *

The night market at Lantern Row is the closest thing Greyhollow has to honesty. Everyone there is selling something they don't quite believe in, and everyone buying knows it, and the transaction is understood on both sides to be a small shared fiction. A knot for luck. A salve for rain. A copper for the pleasure of pretending the world can be bargained with. I've always found it less cynical than most of the city's commerce, which pretends otherwise while robbing you.

We came in at the change of watch, when the crowds are thickest and the sellers too busy to signal each other. Twelve wardens, plain coats under the cloaks, spreading down the row like oil on water. My orders were simple: licenses checked, stock inspected, and above all — eyes open. I wasn't hunting charms. Charms are kindling. I was hunting the fire that had walked past them.

The first four stalls were nothing. Knots, salves, dried lavender bundled into little protective fists, an old man selling "thunder-jars" that were, on inspection, jars. He was offended when I opened one, and I confess I enjoyed offending him; there's a particular satisfaction in confiscating an empty jar from a fraud, a small clean justice that requires no doubt whatsoever.

Doubt is the occupational disease of my work, and I guard against it the way sailors guard against scurvy. Regular doses of certainty. Confiscations. Ledgers. Because here is the truth about being the chief investigator of a magic-free city: the job only works if you never once wonder whether the emptiness you're enforcing is real.

The Night of Broken Thunder taught us what happens when it isn't empty. A storm-caller — one of the exiled court's people, the histories say, though the histories were rewritten so many times even I don't trust them — called weather down onto Greyhollow. Half the eastern district drowned or burned. The city answered with fire of its own, the legal kind, and we burned out every practitioner, every sympathizer, every book that so much as rhymed about clouds. I was a junior warden then. I held the lanterns. I told myself it was necessary, and the telling worked, mostly, on nights when I didn't sleep.

Three years of telling. You get good at it. Like any craft.

It was at the fifth stall that the evening stopped being routine.

* * *

The stall belonged to no one remarkable — a narrow table, a canvas awning beaded with drizzle, a woman with grey-streaked hair selling charms with the weary competence of someone who'd rather be doing anything else. Her license was current. Her stock was standard. But her tally sheet, when I asked for it, showed sales climbing in exactly the pattern I'd mapped, and her hands —

Her hands were wrong. That's the only word I have. She handled her own merchandise like a woman handling a snake she was fond of. Every knot she sold, she touched twice: once for the customer, and once — quick, almost invisible — for herself, thumb pressing the center of the knot the way you'd check a pulse.

I bought a luck-knot from her. Paid double, smiled, made small talk about the weather, which in Greyhollow is always a safe topic because it's always bad. And when I got two stalls away and turned back under the pretext of forgetting my change, I watched her take the coin I'd given her and hold it flat on her palm, watching it, the way you'd watch a cup of tea for ripples.

She felt me coming before she saw me. I'm sure of it now, whatever I told myself then. She packed her table with the speed of long practice and melted into the crowd, and I let her go, because chasing a charm-seller through a night market teaches you nothing except how fast charm-sellers can run.

But I stood where she'd stood, and I took out the luck-knot, and I did something I hadn't done since I was a girl in my grandmother's house, back before the purge, back when superstition was still allowed to be sweet.

I held it up to the lamplight and looked at it honestly.

The knot was beautiful. That was the first wrong thing. Market knots are quick and ugly, tied by the hundred. This one had been worked slowly, deliberately, each pass of the cord placed with the care of someone writing — and when I say writing, I mean it. Under the lamplight, at the right angle, the knot wasn't a knot. It was script. A binding-script, the old kind, the kind we burned the manuals for, coiled into a loop no bigger than my fist.

I couldn't read it. Nobody in Greyhollow could read it; that was rather the point of burning the manuals. But I knew what I was looking at the way you know a counterfeit coin — not by understanding the engraving but by feeling the weight of it in your hand and knowing it's too true.

Someone in my magic-free city was tying working charms and selling them for coppers to customers who couldn't feel the difference.

Which raised a question I didn't like at all: why would a real practitioner sell real magic to people who'd never know? Real magic is expensive. Real magic is dangerous. You don't spend it on luck-knots for farmers.

Unless you weren't spending it. Unless you were scattering it — seeding the market, laying down little anchors of working craft along a line through the city, the way sappers lay charges along a wall. Small weather. Harmless weather. Weather building toward something.

Or — and this thought arrived later, on the walk home, uninvited, and refused to leave — unless the seller wasn't the practitioner at all. Unless the seller was a fence. Unless somewhere upstream of her, someone was making these things without meaning to. Someone whose hands wrote binding-script into a knot the way other people hummed. Someone who maybe didn't know what they were doing, because someone had taken the knowing out of them, and the skill remained like a scar where a memory used to be.

I don't know where that thought came from. I've wondered since whether the knot whispered it to me, which is a thought I'm not proud of, and which I have not entered into any official record.

Officially, what I recorded was this: one stall in violation, vendor fled, sample confiscated for analysis. Unofficially, what I did was go home, lock my door, light a candle, and sit up until dawn holding a piece of string that hummed faintly against my skin, like a struck bell still ringing somewhere far below hearing.

* * *

The analysis came back inconclusive, which is warden language for "it did things and we won't say what." Our testing methods are deliberately crude — we're not equipped to measure magic, only to fail to find it — but the knot failed to be found so thoroughly that it circled back around to damning. It didn't react to the detection reagents because, as far as the reagents could tell, there was nothing there. And yet the knot weighed slightly more than it should have. And yet, left overnight in the sealed strongroom beside the confiscated thunder-jars, it had untied itself by morning and retied itself in a different pattern, and the different pattern, when I finally found someone old enough and drunk enough to squint at it, meant — loosely, crudely, in the grammar of a dead tradition —

*Come down.*

Or possibly *come home.* Old script is ambiguous about directions. Everything underground is both.

I sat in my office with that translation for most of an afternoon, feeling the certainty I'd carried for nineteen years shift under me like river ice.

Because here is the thing nobody says aloud about the purge, about the Night of Broken Thunder, about the whole shining project of a magic-free city: we never proved the storm-caller died. We burned the registries, we burned the practitioners, we burned the books — and in the ash of all that burning, we never produced a body. The histories say the storm-caller perished in the floods they called down, swept away by their own weather, and the histories are very confident about this, confident in the way of documents written by victors who needed the ending tidy.

Tidy endings are for ledgers. I've been reading ledgers for nineteen years, and I know a balanced column when I see one, and I also know what it looks like when someone has made the numbers balance by hand.

What if the storm-caller didn't die? What if the city, in its mercy or its fear or its shame, did something worse than killing them? What if they were stripped — memory cut away, name cut away, power bound down under layers of mundane craft — and turned loose into the population like a coin rubbed blank? Alive. Free. Obligated to no debt, owing no punishment, because the punishment had already been collected from somewhere else.

From whom? Collected how? Paid to whom?

I pushed the questions away. They were the wrong questions for a warden. The right question was simpler: where does the line on my map end?

It ended at the mountain road. Of course it did. The line ran north through Lantern Row, up through the tanners' quarter, past the old gate, and out along the pilgrims' path toward the mountain that looms over Greyhollow like an unasked question. The mountain nobody goes into. The mountain the founding charters specifically forbid anyone from entering, in language so old and so absolute that generations of wardens have enforced the prohibition without ever asking what's up there that needs guarding from us — or us from it.

I spread the map out one more time and looked at that terminus for a long time.

Then I sent for Pellik, and I gave him orders that made him look at me the way you look at a colleague who has begun speaking to furniture.

"Doubles on the mountain gate," I said. "Discreet ones. And pull the visitor logs from every inn and lodging-house along the north roads for the past two months. I want names, faces, and reasons for travel."

"For the mountain?" Pellik said. "Chief, nobody goes to the mountain. There's nothing there."

"That's what worries me," I said. "Lately everything I can't find is in one direction."

* * *

I raided the market again three nights later. Full warrant this time, loud, as promised — twelve wardens turned into thirty, licenses checked, stalls overturned in the legal and ceremonial sense, the whole ugly theater of enforcement performed at volume for the benefit of the crowd and the one person I hoped was watching.

She wasn't there. The grey-haired woman with the wrong hands had vanished from Lantern Row entirely, and so had her stall's registration, and so, when I checked, had any record that she'd ever existed. Her landlord remembered her vaguely. Her neighboring sellers remembered her less. It was as if the city itself had decided to forget her, smoothing her out the way water smooths a stone, and I stood in the middle of the market with my warrant going soft in the drizzle, feeling for the first time in my career like the intruder.

Around me the market resumed, aggrieved but resilient, selling its knots and salves and little fists of lavender to buyers who wanted, as always, to believe the world could be bargained with.

And I understood, standing there, that I had spent nineteen years believing the same thing in a warden's coat. That the world could be bargained with. That if we enforced the emptiness hard enough, the emptiness would hold. That behind the walls of our clean grey city there was nothing but more city, all the way down.

But the knot in my evidence locker had retied itself again. Third pattern now. And the third pattern, my drunk translator informed me over his fourth cup, meant — loosely, crudely, in the grammar of the dead —

*Too late.*

Old script is ambiguous about most things, he told me. But not about time. Time, in the old grammar, is always exact.

I went back to the office and pulled the eclipse tables off the shelf. The next eclipse fell thirty-one days out, and I sat looking at that number for a long while, listening to the rain start up against the window, thinking about lines on maps and storms that move in them, and about a city that lies to itself so well it has forgotten which parts are lies.

Thirty-one days.

Somewhere in my city, something that survived a purge was counting down, and it had just run out of patience with small weather.
