## 7. Run Smiling

**Astrid**

The trouble with falling for someone in Greyhollow is that you have to do it quietly, in a city that treats affection the way it treats magic: tolerated in small doses, taxed heavily, and banned outright after dark.

I was thinking something along those unhelpful lines when Wren took my hand crossing Tanner's Bridge, and I thought it even harder when my pulse did a thing no charm of mine had ever managed to fake.

"Your hands are cold," Wren said, and didn't let go. "Storm-cold."

"Bad circulation."

"Mm." They said *mm* the way other people said *I know exactly what you're lying about and find it charming*. Three dates in, and I still couldn't get a clean read on them. Most people were weather to me — low pressure here, a squall there — but Wren read like glass. Smooth, transparent, and impossible to grip when things got rough.

We'd gone to the lantern festival on Salt Row, which meant crowds, which meant wardens, which meant I should have stayed home with my salves and my loneliness like a sensible exile. But Wren had smiled at me across the market stall that morning and said, "Come see the lights with me. You sell little pieces of weather; you should at least meet some sky that isn't for sale."

So here we were. And here — I realized with the slow, cold slide of recognition I'd been avoiding all evening — *here* came trouble too.

Wardens travel in threes in Greyhollow: one to cast the net, one to hold the writ, one to watch the watchers. These three moved through the festival crowd the way oil moves through water, and every ordinary body between me and them suddenly looked very thin.

"Wren." I kept my voice light. "How do you feel about leaving early?"

"I feel wonderful about it." They turned, casual as anything, and their eyes did a quick sweep of the crowd — not the panicked sweep of a civilian, but the practiced sweep of someone counting exits. "Back way?"

"Back way."

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what they don't tell you about assignments: sometimes the person you're collecting gets under your skin before you can finish the paperwork.

Astrid Whitlock walked fast through the alleys off Salt Row, pulling me by the hand like I needed leading, and I let them believe I did. Every few steps they touched a doorframe, a drainpipe, a hanging chain — small gestures disguised as absent habits, but I knew what they were. Listening. Reading the city's mood through its metal the way I'd been taught to read it through its people.

They were better at this than they should have been. That was, after all, the entire problem.

"The wardens aren't following us," Astrid said, without looking back.

"No," I agreed.

"You're not even surprised they exist."

"I grew up near cities," I said, which was true in the way that saying you grew up near an ocean is true when you were raised by the tide itself.

They stopped under the eave of a shuttered dye-house and finally looked at me, and I braced myself, because Astrid Whitlock had a look I'd only seen described in old Registry files — the look of a storm deciding whether to be polite.

"Astrid—"

"Three dates," they said. "You've never once asked what I did before charms."

"You told me. Farming, briefly. Weather, professionally."

"Nobody *professionally* does weather anymore. It's banned. It's purged. There was a night three years ago—" They stopped themselves. Their jaw worked. "You know what, forget it. The wardens weren't following us because of me. They turned toward us *when you whistled*. Back on the bridge. That little tune you did while I was buying candied ginger."

My stomach dropped through the street, through the mountain beneath the street, all the way down to the court that had raised me and would, if I failed, take everything Astrid had left.

"It was just a tune," I said.

It wasn't. It was a status report. *Target mobile, suspicion rising, request guidance.* The bone whistle against my sternum had hummed back the answer before the last note faded: *Continue. Do not lose them.*

And now two wardens rounded the corner behind us, and one more ahead, and the alley that had been our exit became our box.

* * *

**Astrid**

Three wardens. A dead-end alley. My magic caged so deep under my skin it had almost forgotten how to be anything but ache.

In three years in Greyhollow I'd learned exactly one defensive trick worth the name: how to be boring. How to stand very still and think gray thoughts and smell like nothing more interesting than beeswax and lavender. I reached for that trick now.

Warden Sela — I knew her face from posters and from nightmares; chief investigator, the woman who'd made charm-dealing a capital offense in practice if not on paper — stepped into the mouth of the alley with her net-caster flanking her. She held up a strip of parchment. Even at this distance I could see the sigil inked on it, and worse, I could see it *reacting*, shivering toward me like a compass needle finding north.

"Astrid Vane," she said. Alias number four, the one I'd used for luck-knots. "Purveyor of unauthorized talismans. Suspected survivor of the Broken Thunder purge." Her voice was flat, procedural, and underneath it ran something almost like regret. "Come quietly and this stays administrative."

"And if I don't?" My mouth, traitor that it was, kept talking while my brain ran the arithmetic. No route. No storm. One stranger whose hand I was still holding, for reasons I could no longer pretend were casual.

"Then it stops being administrative," Sela said.

And Wren — sweet, glassy, unreadable Wren — laughed.

It was a terrible moment to laugh. It was such a bad moment to laugh that both wardens hesitated, and hesitation, in my experience, is where all miracles keep their coats.

"Sela Cress," Wren said pleasantly, stepping half in front of me. "Chief Investigator. Third generation. Your grandmother helped write the Warding Accords — did you know she consulted with the Registry on the wording?"

Sela went very still. "That information is classified above your station."

"I know," Wren said. "It's classified above yours too."

Then they whistled again — that same little tune, three notes up, one note down — and the alley answered.

Not metaphorically. The walls answered. The ward-stones mortared between the bricks lit bone-white, and every anti-magic sigil in Greyhollow's proudest defenses bent like reeds, and I felt the cage around my own power crack open by one careful inch, like a window unlatched from outside.

Because that's what it was. Someone had opened a window. Someone had just *unlocked* a warded city like a man unlocking his own garden gate.

"What are you," I breathed.

"Later," Wren said. "Run now."

* * *

**Wren**

I hated using the whistle openly. The Registry forbade it inside warded cities precisely because it revealed things — and now Astrid was looking at me with the expression of someone doing long division and arriving at an answer they despised.

But the alternative was watching wardens put iron bands on their wrists, and iron doesn't just suppress storm-callers. It *erases* them, slowly, cell by cell, until the Registry collects a husk and calls it protocol. I'd read the file. I'd read every file. That was supposed to make this easier. Knowing someone completely was supposed to make collecting them feel like clerical work.

Instead I was dragging them through a drainage culvert behind the dye-house while they catalogued every impossible thing I'd done, and I could hear the list compiling behind their eyes.

The culvert let out past the tanneries, where the stink covered any scent-tracking and the noise covered everything else. We climbed the embankment to the old rail path, and there, finally, with Greyhollow smoking gently below us and no pursuit yet — Sela would regroup; she was thorough, not hasty — Astrid rounded on me.

"No," they said. "No more later. Later has been stacking up for three dates and it's now taller than both of us."

"Astrid—"

"You unlocked the wards. You spoke to Sela like you sign her pay. You whistle and stone listens." Their hands were shaking — with fear or fury or the unfamiliar taste of freedom leaking through that cracked-open window in their chest, I couldn't tell. "The night we met, you bought a luck-knot. Nobody in Greyhollow needs luck-knots unless they're hiding something, and you bought it *smiling*. So here's what happens now. You tell me the truth, or you tell it to the wardens, because I will walk down that hill and hand them your name myself."

There it was. The storm under the calm. Three years of dammed-up weather looking for somewhere to land, and landing on me.

Good, some honest part of me thought. You should land on me. Half of this is mine.

"The court beneath the mountain," I said.

They blinked. "...What?"

"You've heard stories. Everyone's heard stories — the Undercourt, the Pale Registry, souls kept like coins in a vault. Greyhollow's founders built the city's wards as a deal with them. Magic stays out, the court stays down, everyone pretends neither is real." I watched their face go carefully blank, the way people do when a story touches a scar they don't want examined. "The stories are true. And Astrid — the court holds something of yours. Something they've held for three years. Something they're going to collect soon whether either of us likes it or not."

The wind off the mountain moved through the grass between us. Somewhere below, a warden's signal-horn sounded once, then twice.

"That's not possible," Astrid said, very quietly. "Everything I am is standing right here. I'd know if something of mine was missing."

"Would you?" I said, and hated myself even as I said it, because I'd seen the ledger entry, seen the line item written in the Registry's patient silver script: *One (1) soul, storm-marked. Memory excision included in fee.* "How many years ago did you arrive in Greyhollow? What happened the week before? Can you tell me, honestly, or is there just — a smooth place, where the answer should be?"

I watched them reach for it. Watched them hit the wall. Watched their whole understanding of themselves tilt, slowly, the way a ship tilts when it starts taking water.

"There's a scar," they whispered. "Right there. Like a tooth that got pulled. I always thought — I told myself it was the exile. That exile takes things."

"Exile doesn't take things," I said. "*Sales* take things."

The second horn blew, closer. Down in the city, lights were moving along the embankment road — Sela, regrouping after all, thorough as winter.

Astrid looked at me for a long time. Fear, fury, betrayal, curiosity — all of it churning — and underneath, barely visible, the thing that scared me most: hope. Ugly, stubborn, half-drowned hope, the kind that survives three years in a magic-free city selling lies to strangers.

"If you're lying to me," they said, "the court beneath the mountain won't get the chance to collect whatever's mine. Because I'll spend it all right here, on you, and it will hurt."

"I'd deserve it," I said.

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be *true*." I held out my hand. "The eclipse is in nineteen days. Everything I can tell you, I'll tell you — but some of it only makes sense below, and we can't stay above. Not with Sela hunting you, not with the collection date coming. Down is the only direction left, Astrid. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry it's me showing you the door."

They stared at my hand. Then at me. Then, briefly, at the sky, as if consulting it — and I remembered, absurdly, that they sold weather for a living, that they might genuinely expect the sky to have an opinion.

"The mountain," they said flatly. "You want me to walk into the mountain with the person who whistled me into a warden trap, based on a hole in my head and your word."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll tell you the rest anyway, and stay anyway, and we'll both be collected at the eclipse, and it'll have been lovely knowing you." I tried for a smile. "Mostly the middle part."

Something cracked in their expression — not trust, not yet, but the ground trust gets planted in. They took my hand. Their grip was storm-warm now, the cage in their chest still open that one inch I'd given them, power humming against my palm like a wire under tension.

"One rule," Astrid said. "From here on, you don't whistle unless I ask you to."

"Deal."

"And Wren?" Their eyes were very bright, and very tired, and fixed on the black shoulder of the mountain rising against the stars. "Whatever you're not telling me — whatever *half* this is — I'll figure it out. Storms always find the low ground."

I believed them. That was the worst part. I believed them completely, and I knew that by the time they found the bottom of the truth, they'd find the part of it with my name on it — heir, collector, the smiling thief of the prophecy — and I had nineteen days to decide which of us that page was really about.

Below us, Greyhollow's lamps went out one by one, curfew rolling across the city like a closing hand.

Above us, the mountain waited with its bone-lit halls and its patient ledgers.

And beside me, holding my hand hard enough to bruise, the soul I'd been sent to collect started walking toward its own vault — and asked me, of all things, if I was hungry, because treason apparently worked up an appetite.

"Yeah," I said. "Yeah, I'm starving."
