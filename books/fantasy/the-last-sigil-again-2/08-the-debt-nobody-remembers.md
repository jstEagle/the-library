## 8. The Debt Nobody Remembers

Marrow kept his shop above a tannery in the Sump, which was the kind of joke Greyhollow made about itself and then pretended not to notice. The stairs smelled like every bad decision ever made by someone who'd once had magic and lost it, and at the top there was a door with no handle that opened for people it liked.

It had always opened for me.

I knocked anyway. Old habits. Old courtesies. The door swung inward on its own, slow as a judge deciding whether to bother with you.

"Astrid Whitlock," said a voice from the dark, warm as dust and twice as dry. "Three years, and you finally come up my stairs. Either you're desperate or you're dying."

"Can't I just be visiting?"

The lamp caught. Marrow sat behind his ledger-desk like a toadstool that had learned bookkeeping—round, pale, patient, with ink stains up both forearms and spectacles thick enough to read the small print on fate itself. He'd been the exile community's unofficial archivist since before I arrived: debts, aliases, grudges, who owed whom what and in which currency. If you wanted something remembered in a city built on forgetting, you came to Marrow.

"Visiting." He tasted the word like wine he suspected of being vinegar. "Sit down, storm-child."

"I have a name now. Calla."

"Mm." He wrote nothing down, which from Marrow meant he'd already memorized it forever. "And how is Calla finding the city?"

"Charming. Damp. Full of wardens lately."

"Full of wardens *always*. Lately they've been *looking*." He folded his hands over his ledger. "So. Which is it? Desperate or dying?"

I sat. The chair knew me too—it creaked in exactly the place my guilt lived.

* * *

Here's what I'd done between Wren's dinner and Marrow's door, in case you're wondering why a person who has spent three years building a life out of small weather would suddenly tear a hole in it.

I'd gone home. I'd slept—or lain in bed performing a convincing imitation of sleep while my brain replayed the evening in forensic detail. The way Wren's fingers had paused over the luck-knots before choosing one. The exact cadence of *"the thief comes smiling"*—and no, I hadn't imagined it, because I'd made myself say it aloud three times in my kitchen, matching the rhythm against my memory until the two locked together like teeth.

Then I'd gone back to the black-market dealer's stall at dawn, before the market woke, and asked him where the book had come from.

He'd told me a name. The name had led me through three favors I owed and didn't enjoy spending, to a cellar under a chandler's shop, to a woman called Vetch who dealt in banned texts the way other people dealt in stolen silver. Vetch had looked me over and said, "You want the *Vigil of the Deep*? Everyone wants it eventually. That's the trouble with prophecies—they're patient."

She'd let me read it there, standing, under a lamp that buzzed like it disapproved.

The line was there. Word for word. *The thief comes smiling, and the marked soul follows gladly.* And beside it an illustration so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood: two figures walking toward a mountain's mouth, rendered in the flat, certain style of artists who believed they were recording rather than imagining.

One figure carried a lantern. One figure smiled.

The smiling figure had been drawn with a collar of feathers. Wren wore a scarf like that. I'd noticed it at dinner and thought *charming.*

I'd bought the book with money I needed for rent. Vetch had watched me tuck it into my coat and said, "You're in it, aren't you?" And when I didn't answer: "They always are, the ones who buy it. Funny thing—" she'd leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—nobody's ever in the prophecy who isn't already owed to somebody."

I'd walked home the long way, past the wardens' garrison, past the great iron bell they rang during purges, and I'd thought about that word. *Owed.*

There was a scar in me. Not on me—in me. A smooth place where memory should have been, like a riverbed where the water had been diverted. Three years ago I'd woken up in a cart headed east with papers that said *exiled* and a head full of holes, and everyone—the wardens, the exile registry, even Marrow—had assumed the storm-caller had burned her own memories fleeing justice. Guilty people did that. It was practically etiquette.

But I'd never questioned it hard enough. Because questioning it meant sitting down in front of the hole and asking: *what was taken, and who took it, and what did they leave as payment?*

Wren had said one more thing at dinner. I'd filed it away as flirtation at the time. Now it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

*"Everything in this city is a transaction, Calla. Even kindness. Especially kindness."*

Desperate or dying. Marrow had guessed right. Both.

* * *

"You remember the Broken Thunder," I said to Marrow.

His spectacles caught the lamplight and went white, twin coins hiding his eyes. "Everyone remembers the Broken Thunder. Half the city remembers it wrong, but everyone remembers it."

"Tell me your version."

"Why?"

"Because mine has a hole in it the size of a night sky."

For a long moment the only sound was the tannery below us, vats turning, the whole building breathing chemical breath. Then Marrow closed his ledger—not to stop work but to signal that real work was beginning—and took off his spectacles, which I had never once seen him do.

"On the third night of Frostmoon, three years ago," he said, "the sky over Greyhollow tore open. Not weather. Weather doesn't scream. This screamed. Every warden ward in the city lit at once and then failed at once, which should have been impossible, because the wards weren't built to fail. The Registry—" he said the word carefully, tasting it, watching my face for a reaction I didn't yet know to give, "—the deep courts, whatever you want to call them, had been promised something. And the promise broke."

"The Registry," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry around the syllables. "That's a story we tell children. The clerks under the mountain who weigh souls."

"Yes. Well. Children's stories are usually invoices that got softened in the telling." He put his spectacles back on. "Something came calling on its debt that night. And something above ground answered with a storm instead. You know what happened after. The storm broke the calling, broke the wards, broke eleven buildings and forty-one lives. The city survived. The wardens needed someone to blame, and the only storm-caller in Greyhollow conveniently had amnesia and couldn't argue."

"That's the official version. Exile as punishment."

"It's *a* version." Marrow reached beneath his desk and drew up a second ledger—older, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, chained shut with a lock that had no keyhole, only a sigil pressed into the metal. I recognized the sigil. I'd seen it an hour before dawn, inked onto the last page of a banned book: a mountain drawn as a closed eye.

My hands started shaking before my mind agreed to.

"Marrow. What is that?"

"This is the Debt Ledger." He said it the way priests say names of gods they don't worship. "Every transaction between the surface and the Undercourt for the last two hundred years. Who sold, who bought, what was paid, what was held in escrow. The exiles think I keep it because I'm sentimental." His smile was thin and old. "I keep it because somebody has to remember what everyone else was made to forget."

"How do you even have—"

"Does it matter?"

"It might matter enormously, actually."

"It doesn't." He set the ledger down between us. The lock-sigil faced me like a small closed eye of its own. "What matters is that you came here tonight asking about a hole in your memory, and I happen to know exactly which page the hole is on. So I'll ask you once, Astrid Whitlock, and I want you to answer honestly, because the honest answer changes everything and the dishonest answer will get us both collected. Are you ready to be reframed?"

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Marrow said softly, "that you believe you were punished. And I believe you were purchased. Those are very different lives to have lived. Ready?"

The chair creaked under me. Somewhere below, the tannery vats turned and turned, making something soft out of something that used to be alive. That felt relevant.

"I'm ready," I said, which was a lie, but the brave kind.

* * *

Marrow unchained the ledger with a word I didn't catch—he spoke it backward, or inside-out, or in the grammar of a language that predated either—and opened it to a page near the end. He turned it toward me.

I read it three times. My eyes kept sliding off it, the way eyes slide off a wound.

**LOT 411. ONE (1) SOUL, STORM-CALLED, WHOLE AND UNSPLIT.**
**SELLER: THE BEARER (NAME SEALED BY REQUEST OF BEARER).**
**PURPOSE OF SALE: CANCELLATION OF OPEN CLAIM AGAINST CITY OF GREYHOLLOW (SEE LOT 409, NIGHT OF BROKEN THUNDER).**
**TERMS: SOUL HELD IN TRUST BY THE PALE REGISTRY UNTIL NEXT TOTAL ECLIPSE FOLLOWING DATE OF SALE.**
**AT ECLIPSE: COLLECTION.**

Below that, in smaller script, an addendum that made my vision blur:

**BEARER HAS REQUESTED FULL MEMORY OF TRANSACTION BE CUT AND HELD WITH THE SOUL. MEMORY TO BE RETURNED AT COLLECTION. NOTE FROM REGISTRY CLERK: Bearer insisted. Clerk's remark: unusual. Most sellers beg to remember. This one begged to forget.**

The lamp buzzed. The tannery breathed. Somewhere far above, ordinary clouds crossed an ordinary moon, and neither of them knew anything.

"They spared the city," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it belonged to a stranger I was politely interviewing. "The storm. It wasn't—I wasn't attacking Greyhollow. I was defending it. From them."

"You were paying them off," Marrow said. "With yourself. The storm broke their claim on the city, and the claim had to go somewhere, and you signed it onto your own soul. That's what storm-callers could always do, back when there were any. Redirect. Take a debt aimed at ten thousand people and carry it alone." He tapped the page gently, reverently, like the paper might bruise. "Forty-one died in the Broken Thunder. It should have been all of us. Somebody bought the rest of the city its morning, and the wardens rewarded her with exile and a burned name."

"And the memory—"

"You cut it yourself. Read it again if you don't believe me. *Begged to forget.*" Marrow's voice gentled in a way that was worse than cruelty. "You didn't want to live knowing what you'd traded. So you paid twice. Once with the soul, once with the knowing."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply reported for duty without consulting headquarters, and suddenly I was across the room with my palms flat against the wall, breathing like a woman surfacing from deep water, staring at the tannery-stained plaster as if it might explain anything.

Exile wasn't punishment. Exile was *delivery.* They hadn't thrown me out of the city—they'd moved the merchandise to a safe location and left instructions. Three years of luck-knots and weather-salves and careful, careful smallness. Three years of loneliness I'd worn like a coat because I thought it was penance. It was packaging.

And Wren—

Oh, stars. *Wren.* The charm they'd bought that first night, the one neither of us should have needed. The dinner. *The thief comes smiling.* The feather collar. The way they'd looked at me like I was a package they were waiting to sign for.

"Next eclipse," I said to the wall. "When is the next eclipse?"

"Nineteen days," Marrow said. "Frostmoon's end. The Registry holds your soul in trust until then, and then they collect. That's the standard contract. Though—" and here his voice developed a careful, terrible hesitation, the hesitation of a man reading fine print he wished he'd never learned to read, "—there's a clause."

"Of course there's a clause. There's always a clause."

"Lot 411 specifies collection conditions. Turn to the back of the addendum."

I turned back to the desk. My hands had stopped shaking, which frightened me more than the shaking had—some part of me had already gone cold and efficient, a clerk of my own catastrophe.

At the bottom of the page, in script so fine it was nearly embroidery:

**COLLECTION SHALL BE EFFECTED BY THE COURTY HERALD, WHO COMES SMILING.**

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly, nothing like laughter at all.

"He already bought the charm," I said. "First night. He stood at my stall and bought a luck-knot, and I thought—stars help me, I thought I'd finally gotten lucky."

"Astrid." Marrow's voice was very quiet. "How much of the prophecy book did you read?"

I hadn't told him about the book. I hadn't told anyone. But Marrow had spent thirty years remembering things for people who weren't allowed to, and apparently that included the things they didn't say.

"The last page shows two figures going into the mountain," I said. "One comes out."

"Then listen to me, and listen well, because this is the only free advice I have ever given anyone." He rose, and for the first time I saw how old he was, how long he'd sat in this room above the tannery keeping accounts for the forgotten. "Prophecies record. They don't command. The Registry believes otherwise—that's the whole architecture of their power, making people live inside their own paperwork—but a ledger is only a story told by the winning side. Your soul is not a signature. Whatever they wrote you down as, you are still the one who redirected a god's claim onto herself to save a city that exiled her for it. People like that don't walk into mountains on schedule."

"Nineteen days," I said.

"Nineteen days," he agreed. "And the herald is already here, and he's already smiling, and I'd wager everything in this ledger that he's falling for you faster than his contract allows." He closed the Debt Ledger and chained it shut. "Which means you have one advantage nobody in that court has planned for."

"What's that?"

"You." I must have looked skeptical, because he snorted. "Think, girl. They sent a herald to collect a soul. Fine. Standard procedure. But they also let that soul *cut out its own memory*—and the Registry keeps that memory in trust alongside the soul, returned only at collection. Do you understand what that means? The truth about what you did, what you sacrificed, what you *are*—it's sitting in a vault under that mountain, and the moment they bring you down to collect it, you get it back. All of it. Every reason you had. Every ounce of what you were willing to pay."

I stared at him.

"They're not collecting a debtor, Astrid. They're escorting the most dangerous thing they own directly to the one place she can take it back."

The lamp guttered. Below us the vats turned, patient as ever, and outside the window Greyhollow lay under its ordinary clouds, nine days of ordinary left, give or take.

I picked up my coat.

"Where are you going?" Marrow asked.

"To find the herald," I said. "Before the herald finds me. And Marrow—if a charming stranger comes up your stairs asking after a storm-caller named Calla—"

"I'll remember nothing," he said, and smiled, and for once it wasn't dry at all. "It's what I'm best at."
