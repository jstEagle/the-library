## 9. Down Is the Only Direction

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, exactly like competence. I know this because I've been practicing it for years.

The mountain gate was three days' walk north of Greyhollow, through the Ashfen, where the ground had stopped pretending to be anything other than what it was: a graveyard that hadn't finished deciding what to be grave and what to be ash. The wardens never came here. Too far from the wards, too close to old stories they'd rather pretend were dead. Which meant, for three days, Astrid Whitlock and I were the only two people in the world, and I got to pretend we were just two travelers sharing a road instead of what we actually were: a thief delivering stolen goods to their rightful owner.

I was the goods, in case that wasn't clear. Or rather, I was the delivery mechanism. The soul-bearer walks beside me, smiling at my jokes, trusting me with her life in increments small enough not to notice — an extra ration here, a hand at a stream crossing there — and every increment is a coin I'm spending against myself.

*Deliver the bearer before eclipse or forfeit your claim.* That's what the whistle said, when it spoke, which was rarely and only at night and always in the voice of someone I couldn't quite remember loving. *Forfeit your claim, heir, and the Registry will find another way down.* They always said *heir* like it was a title. It felt more like a leash measured to fit before I grew into it.

"You're doing the face," Astrid said.

We'd stopped to make camp in the lee of a fallen standing stone, one of the old border-markers from back when the fen belonged to nobody worth conquering. She crouched over the fire pit she was building, arranging sticks with the same fussy precision she brought to knotwork, and she didn't look up when she said it, which somehow made it worse.

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing arithmetic." She struck flint. Sparks caught on the second try; she'd gotten good at mundane fire in three years of exile, good at everything mundane, so good you'd never guess there was a storm sleeping behind her sternum. "You get this look like you're counting something and losing."

"I don't lose," I said.

"Everyone loses. That's the whole point of counting." She fed the flame a twist of dry grass, and the light climbed her face, and I had the thought — the treacherous, mission-endangering thought I'd been having for days now — that I would rather watch her do ordinary things than do extraordinary ones anywhere else. "So. What are we walking toward? You've been vague for two days. Vaguer than usual, even."

"The mountain gate."

"I gathered that much, Wren. I can see the mountain. It's been getting bigger all day like it's eavesdropping." She sat back on her heels. "What's *in* it?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying like a stone in my boot since Greyhollow, since the market, since the moment I bought a luck-knot I didn't need because buying it meant talking to her. Chapter seven of the mission briefing had been very clear about disclosure protocols: reveal only as much as obedience requires. The Registry wrote its orders the way lawyers write contracts — every word load-bearing, every silence deliberate.

But the fire was warm and her face was lit gold and the truth sat in my chest like swallowed glass, and I heard myself say:

"Something of yours."

Her hands went still.

"You said that already," she said quietly. "In the alley, after Sela's people cornered us. Half an answer. I let you keep half because we were running." Her eyes came up now, storm-grey and level, and I understood suddenly why the Registry feared storm-callers, why they'd spent a century writing policies about them. It wasn't the lightning. It was being *seen* by someone who could see. "I'm not running anymore. Finish the sentence."

I fed the fire a stick I didn't need to feed it. Bought myself three seconds.

"The court beneath the mountain keeps things," I said. "Souls. Debts. Memories that people paid away. There's a ledger, and ledgers have entries, and somewhere in that ledger—" I made myself hold her gaze "—there's an entry with your name on it. Three years old. What it says, exactly, I don't know. But you've been walking around with a hole in you, haven't you. A scar where something should be."

I expected anger. I'd rehearsed for anger. Anger I could manage; anger was weather, and I knew how to wait out weather.

What I got was worse. She nodded. Slowly, like I'd confirmed something she'd suspected for years but hadn't dared say aloud, because saying it aloud made it real.

"The Night of Broken Thunder," she said. "Everyone thinks I called the storm out of malice. The wardens think it. The exile writs say it. And I remember—" her voice did something complicated "—I remember *pieces*. Rain. Screaming. My hands full of lightning. And then nothing, and then Greyhollow, and then three years of selling charms to people who think magic is a bedtime story." She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd ever heard, because it was almost funny to her. "I used to lie awake trying to find the shape of what was missing. You can't mourn a thing if you don't know what it is. You just ache in a room you can't enter."

"Astrid—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. Not sharp. Just final. "Don't apologize yet. Apologize when I know whether you should." She looked at the fire. "How do you know all this? Truly. Not the half-answer version. Because no street-charm seller knows the Undercourt's filing system, Wren Vale. I've been watching you for weeks. You walk like someone who's been *taught*. Taught by people who live underground."

The whistle was in my coat pocket, six inches from my hand, warm as a pulse. All I had to do was change the subject. Deflect. Smile — I was good at smiling, the prophecy itself said the thief comes smiling, and I'd resented that line ever since I read it because it reduced me to a technique.

Instead I said: "Because I was born there."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the fen, wind moved through dead trees with a sound like pages turning.

"Not Greyhollow," I went on, because once the door was open it wouldn't stop opening. "Not anywhere above ground. Below. In the halls with the bone-lights. I was raised above — sent up young, given a name, given a face people trust. Given assignments." I pulled the whistle out and set it on the flat stone between us, and watched her look at it: white, curved, small, utterly innocent unless you knew what bone it was carved from and whose. "This tells me what to do. It has been telling me what to do since before I could read. And right now it wants me to walk you through that gate before the eclipse, and I am supposed to believe that when it's done, everything I've been promised will finally be mine."

"And do you?" she asked. "Believe it?"

"That's the arithmetic," I said. "That's the count I keep losing."

She picked up the whistle. Turned it over in her fingers. I let her — God help me, I let her, some part of me hoping she'd snap it in half and solve everything, most of me knowing she wouldn't, because it wasn't hers to break any more than I was.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's always warm. It knows who holds it."

She set it back down with the care you give a sleeping animal. Then she looked at me across the fire, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to settle something in hers.

"Three questions," she said. "Honest answers, and I walk through that gate with you tomorrow. Lie, and I'll show you what's left of the storm, ledger entry or no ledger entry. Fair?"

"Fair."

"One." She leaned forward. "When you bought that charm the first night — the luck-knot neither of us needed — was that the assignment?"

"Yes."

Her jaw tightened. I kept going before she could decide what that meant.

"But staying after the assignment was finished wasn't. The brief said make contact, establish trust, begin the descent. It did not say take her to dinner twice a week for a month. It did not say learn how she takes her tea. It did not say—" my voice cracked and I hated it "—it did not require any of the rest of it. That part I did on my own. That part I did wrong, by every rule I was raised under, and I'd do it wrong again."

She was quiet a long moment. "Two. The prophecy. You've read it?"

"I've read it."

"Then you know how it ends. One enters, one leaves alone. Which one leaves, Wren?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the others, the one I'd been circling since chapter four of a banned book, the page I'd looked at so many times the ink had started to feel like a wound. Two figures entering the mountain. One leaving alone. The illustration gave faces to both figures inside. The figure outside was hooded.

"I don't know," I said.

"Liar."

"Honest liar," I said. "I genuinely don't know which of us the book means. The Registry believes it's you — the soul returns alone to its owner, or rather the owner leaves alone without it. Their whole plan depends on you walking out empty while the contract completes. But prophecies are written by clerks, and clerks are precise, and nowhere does it actually *say*—"

"Three," she said, cutting through me, and her voice had gone soft in a way that was infinitely more dangerous than angry. "If I asked you to turn around right now. Walk away. Take me east, past the fen, past the wards, somewhere the whistle's reach ends — would you?"

The fire burned. The mountain stood against the stars, blacker than the sky around it, patient as a debt.

Every order I had ever been given said no. Every promise the Registry had dangled since childhood — legitimacy, a name that was really mine, a place in the deep courts, *belonging* — all of it balanced on the word no. Say yes to her question and I lost everything except the one thing I'd never been allowed to want.

"Yes," I said.

It hung between us. She searched my face for the trick in it, found none, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — the half inch a person lowers when they've decided to be tired near you, which is its own kind of trust.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. We go through the gate. Together. And Wren — if I find out down there that you've known more than you've told me, that you've been steering me toward something—"

"I have been," I said. "Steering you toward something. Just maybe not the thing I was ordered to."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Sleep. I'll take first watch. And Wren?" I paused halfway to my bedroll. "Whatever's in that ledger with my name — when we get it back, you're helping me read it. Every line. No more half-answers."

"No more half-answers," I agreed, and lay down, and stared at the underside of the world, and did not sleep.

* * *

I woke to grey light and the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet, which meant either the weather was turning or Astrid was dreaming hard. With storm-callers, those were often the same event.

She was standing at the edge of camp facing the mountain, arms crossed, hair moving in a wind that touched no one else. When she heard me sit up she didn't turn around.

"There's a road," she said. "Under the heather. Old paving. You can see it where the ground's worn through."

"The Gate Road," I said. "It hasn't been used openly in a hundred years. The Registry closed it after the last purge. They prefer their visitors arrive confused and desperate."

"How comforting." Still she didn't turn. "Wren. Last night you said yes to my question. I believed you. I still believe you. But I want you to understand what you said yes *to*." Now she turned, and her eyes had lightning in them, literally, a faint flicker behind the grey that I'd seen only once before, in the alley, when Sela's wardens had nearly had us. "Whatever's in that mountain with my name on it — whoever I was before, whatever I traded — when I walk out of there, I walk out *whole*. Or I don't walk out. Those are the terms I'm setting, and I'm telling you now so you can decide whose side you're standing on before we're underground and it's too late for anyone to be neutral."

"I decided whose side I'm standing on a month ago," I said. "Over tea. You stir it counterclockwise. Nobody does that. I decided right then that whatever the mission was, I was off-script."

A beat. Then, wryly, despite everything: "Counterclockwise. You fell for me over *stirring direction*."

"I'm a simple creature."

"You're a liar and a spy and possibly royalty of some horrible underground bureaucracy," she said, shouldering her pack, "and you fell for me over stirring direction." She started toward the worn paving stones glinting through the heather, then paused. "Come on, then. Down is the only direction."

The Gate Road ran straight as an argument toward the mountain's northern face, where the rock split around a door tall as a cathedral and older than any kingdom I'd learned about above ground. As we walked, the heather thinned, the air thickened, and the light changed — went from morning-gold to something paler, sourceless, the color of the bone-lit halls bleeding upward through stone.

The whistle warmed in my pocket. Not speaking yet. But warming. Getting its words ready.

At the threshold, Astrid stopped and looked up at the door, and I watched her square her shoulders the way she did before markets, before warden patrols, before every day of a three-year exile she hadn't deserved and couldn't remember.

"Together," she said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted an answer anyway.

"Together," I said.

And the mountain opened, and we stepped out of the daylight, and behind us — far behind, at the fen's edge — I heard, faint and unmistakable, the sound of horses. Many horses. Moving fast.

Sela had found the road.
