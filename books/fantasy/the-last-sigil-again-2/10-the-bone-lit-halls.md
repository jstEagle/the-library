## 10. The Bone-Lit Halls

The gate behind us sealed itself with a sound like a book closing on a finger.

I felt it in my teeth more than heard it—a soft, final *thunk* that said, quite politely, *no refunds*. Wren didn't look back. I did, because I'm the sort of person who checks whether the door is locked even when they're the one locking it, and what I saw was stone grown smooth over where a doorway had been. No seam. No handle. Just mountain, wearing its original face again.

"Charming hospitality," I said, because my voice was the only thing I owned down here, and I intended to use it.

"They're efficient," Wren said. Their torch—bone-white flame, no smoke, no heat I could feel—threw their shadow long against the descending stair. "Try not to insult anyone. The clerks keep records of insults. Centuries of them."

"Wonderful. I'll add that to the list of things I'm not allowed to do, right under 'have a soul without paperwork.'"

Wren's mouth did something complicated. Not quite a smile. More like a smile's ghost, come back to haunt the place it used to live.

The stair went down the way grief goes down: gradually at first, then all at once. The walls changed as we descended. Up near the gate they'd been honest granite, cold and indifferent. A hundred steps later they'd begun to sweat mineral light, veins of pale gold running through the dark like something alive had been pressed flat into the rock and hadn't entirely stopped moving. Three hundred steps after that—and I counted, because counting kept me from thinking—the walls were bone.

Not decorated with bone. Not carved to resemble it. Bone. Great smooth arcs of it, fused and polished, ribbing the corridor overhead like the inside of some creature so vast the mind declined to finish picturing it. The light came from within it, a low warm glow, the color of candlelight through skin.

"Is the mountain—" I started.

"Don't," Wren said quietly.

"Dead?"

"*Don't.*"

So I didn't ask. But I put my palm against the wall anyway, just briefly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you've been told not to wake, and beneath the bone I felt—not a pulse exactly. More like weather. Pressure changes. The mountain breathing in a register too slow for lungs.

And underneath that, faint as a word spoken through a floor: singing. Hundreds of voices, none of them using words I knew, all of them using a melody I recognized in my spine before my brain caught up.

It was the tune I hummed while working. The luck-knot song. My mother's song, probably, though I couldn't have told you where I learned it.

I took my hand off the wall very quickly.

* * *

The first gallery opened around us without warning—one moment corridor, the next a cavern so large the torchlight gave up halfway to the ceiling and simply admitted defeat. And it was full of lights.

Lanterns. That was my first thought. Thousands of lanterns hung at every height, drifting or anchored, gold and white and one startling shade of green like lightning through leaves. They swayed in a current I couldn't feel. Some pulsed slowly, patient as sleeping breath. Others flickered fast, frantic, like moths against glass.

Then the nearest one drifted close enough, and I saw that it wasn't a lantern.

It was a face.

Or rather—it had been. It was a memory now, worn into a shape, the way a riverbed is shaped by water that isn't there anymore. An old woman's laugh, suspended mid-sound, glowing. Around her orbited smaller fragments: hands kneading bread. A dog, young then old then young again. A door painted blue, repainted blue, repainted blue across forty years of springs.

"She sold this?" I asked. My voice came out wrong. Smaller.

"She sold everything," Wren said. "Some people sell one memory to pay a debt. Some sell a whole life and keep only enough to walk out the door." They nodded at the old woman's laugh. "She kept the walking-out. She comes back sometimes. Stands there. The clerks let her look."

"Do you bring her?"

"I did. Once." Wren adjusted the torch. Their face did the complicated thing again, harder this time. "Before."

*Before.* Before what? Before me, presumably. Before the mission. I filed the question away with all the others I was keeping in the growing drawer labeled THINGS WREN WON'T ANSWER, a drawer that was getting heavy enough to need reinforcement.

We walked through the gallery and the memories noticed us.

That's the part I wasn't prepared for. I'd expected them to be inert—archived, shelved, dead stock. But as we passed, they turned. Slowly, the way flowers turn toward a window. A memory of a wedding lifted its music toward Wren and held it out like an offering. A fragment of someone's first snowfall drifted closer to me and burst, gently, into the smell of woodsmoke and cinnamon, and for half a heartbeat I was seven years old in a kitchen I have never seen, and someone was laughing, and I knew—I *knew*—that kitchen, and then it was gone and I was standing in a cavern full of other people's souls with wet eyes and no explanation.

"That one wasn't yours," Wren said softly.

"How would you know?"

"Because yours are locked deeper. Come on."

But I lingered. I'm not proud of it. I drifted toward a cluster of fragments near the gallery's edge—memories that flickered wrong, jagged, strobing between images like something trying to hold two shapes at once. And when I got close, I saw why.

They were exile memories. I knew it the way you know a face in a crowd from your own hometown. The Night of Broken Thunder had scattered storm-callers across half the continent, and every one of us who'd survived had walked out of our old life carrying gaps. I'd spent three years assuming my missing year was unique, private, a wound custom-fitted to me alone.

There were hundreds of these. Thousands.

One fragment showed a man I didn't know standing in a flooded square, arms raised, calling rain off a burning city—then cut to the same man signing a ledger with a hand that shook—then cut to nothing, a hole shaped like six years, and the hole *hurt* to look at, the way a socket hurts.

Another showed a woman trading a memory of her daughter's name. Just the name. You could see the name leaving her, a small bright thread pulled out of her chest like a splinter made of light, and the clerk taking it—you could see the clerks here, pale and tall and courteous as winter—and the woman walking away already reaching for a daughter she could no longer identify.

I stepped back. Bumped into Wren. Stepped back from that too.

"This isn't a court," I said. "This is a warehouse."

"It's both." Wren's voice had gone careful, the way you talk near a frightened animal, except the animal was me. "The Registry doesn't think of it as theft. Nothing is taken. Everything is purchased. There's always a signature."

"There's always a signature," I repeated. "Right. Because the knife feels better if the victim signs for it."

"Astrid."

"No, tell me—these people, the woman with the daughter—did they know? When they signed? Did they understand what it would be like to have a hole where a person used to be?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the singing in the walls seemed louder.

"Some did," they finally said. "Some understood perfectly. That's the part the Registry finds most useful. People will trade anything to stop hurting. Grief, guilt, love. Especially love. Love debts the fastest." They looked at me then, and the torchlight made their eyes the same gold as the memory-lanterns, and something in my chest turned over and presented itself for sale. "You'd be amazed what people sign away to avoid feeling one specific thing."

"And which thing did I sign away?" I asked. "Since apparently everyone else's holes are on display and mine is the featured attraction. Which memory did Astrid Whitlock put in the shop window?"

Wren opened their mouth—

—and the gallery changed.

* * *

It happened all at once, the way the stair had become the cavern. One moment the thousand lanterns were turning toward us like curious sunflowers; the next, every light in the gallery swung to face me, and the singing in the bones rose half a tone, and the air pressure dropped the way it does before weather.

I know weather. This was weather.

"What did you do?" Wren said sharply—but they weren't looking at me, they were looking *up*, at the dark above the lantern-field, where something vast was paying attention.

"I didn't do anything."

"You're doing something right now. Your sigil—"

"My what?"

They grabbed my wrist. And there, on the inside of my forearm, where for three years there had been nothing but a pale patch of skin I'd assumed was a birthmark fading—there was a mark. Ink-dark, intricate, coiled like a knot of storm seen from very far away. I'd never seen it before in my life. It was *mine*, the way my handwriting is mine, and I had never seen it before in my life, and both of those facts were true simultaneously, and the space between them was where I lived now.

"That's been there since the gate," Wren said. "It woke up when we crossed the threshold. It's a binding sigil. Registry work. It means—"

Above us, the dark answered before they could finish.

A single lantern detached from the field and drifted down. Not a fragment. A whole memory, intact, dense, heavy with itself. It descended past us with the unhurried grace of something that knows it has been sent, and it stopped at eye level, and it unfolded.

And I was home.

Not Greyhollow-home. Real home. The storm-calls' citadel on the cliffs, banners cracking in salt wind, the training yard where I'd broken my arm twice and my teacher's patience daily. My mother stood in the memory's center with her back to me, and she turned around and said something, and although the memory had no sound I read her lips easily, because I'd spent my whole childhood reading her lips through gale-force wind:

*Astrid. Run.*

Then the memory folded, politely, like a letter being returned to sender, and drifted back up into the dark, and the whole gallery exhaled, and the singing resumed, and Wren's grip on my wrist had gone white-knuckled.

"That wasn't random," they said. "Memories don't present themselves. Something *showed* you that."

"The clerks?"

"The clerks don't tease." Wren stared up into the dark, and for the first time since I'd met them—since the market, since dinner, since the wardens, since the gate—they looked genuinely afraid. Not of the dark. Of whatever was negotiating with it. "Something down there wants you to know what's coming. Or wants you angry. Anger burns brighter than fear. Easier to track by."

"Track me for what?"

Wren didn't answer, which was itself an answer, the way silence usually is.

We stood there in the bone-light, among the thousands of sold and stored and shelved lives, and I became aware of a sound I'd been hearing without hearing it, underneath the singing, underneath everything: a ticking. Vast, slow, distributed through the whole mountain like a heartbeat that had learned arithmetic.

An eclipse clock. Counting down to something.

"Eclipse is in four days," Wren said quietly. "Whatever they're tracking me toward, they want it done by then."

"And if it isn't?"

Wren finally looked at me. And I saw it plainly, then—all the things they'd been not-saying since the night market, stacked up behind their eyes like the lanterns above us, each one a debt with my name somewhere in the fine print.

"If it isn't," they said, "the contract defaults. And defaulted contracts don't expire, Astrid. They *collect*."

Far below us, deep in the singing dark, something began to climb.
