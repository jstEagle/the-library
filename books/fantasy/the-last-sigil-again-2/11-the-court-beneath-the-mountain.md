## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The clerks of the Pale Registry did not look up when we entered.

I want you to understand what that means. Wren and I had just walked—exhausted, singed at the edges, trailed by the faint smell of Sela's smoke bombs and three days of mountain dark—into the audience hall of the court that owned my soul, and not one of them glanced up from their ledgers. It was the most bureaucratic insult I have ever received, and I once had a landlord in Greyhollow who billed me for rain damage during a drought I caused.

The hall itself was beautiful in the way that tax documents are beautiful: flawless, symmetrical, and utterly without mercy for the reader. Columns of white bone, polished to a wet shine, rose into a ceiling that wasn't there—the vaults simply faded upward into a darkness that felt less like absence and more like *deferred*. Between the columns stretched desks of the same pale material, and behind each desk sat a clerk: tall, grey-robed, faces smooth as unmarked paper. Not blank, exactly. Blank implies something was removed. These looked like they'd never been written on at all.

And above us—this is the part that made my hands shake, and I am not a person whose hands shake over architecture—the lanterns.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Hanging from nothing on cords of woven hair, each one a soft gold-white glow with a shape inside it. Some pulsed slow as sleeping breath. Some flickered fast, frantic, like moths against glass. And every few moments, one would drift—a whole soul-lantern gliding across the hall to a clerk's desk, where it would be opened like a letter, read, and resealed.

"They're reading mail," I whispered.

"They're reading *people*," Wren said quietly. "Same thing, down here."

* * *

We were kept waiting for an hour. Of course we were. You don't build a bureaucracy this old without learning that the waiting is part of the sentence.

Wren stood beside me the way they always did—close enough to be company, far enough to be deniable—but I noticed their eyes moving constantly. Counting exits. Counting clerks. Counting lanterns with the particular fixed attention of someone doing arithmetic they hate.

"You've been here before," I said. Not a question. We'd passed that threshold somewhere around the gallery of flickering memories, back when I watched a dead exile's last birthday play out in a lantern's glow and understood exactly what was being done with mine.

"I told you. Everyone here is from somewhere."

"That's not an answer, Vale."

"It's the answer I have." They said it lightly, but their hand found my sleeve and stayed there, and I let it, because I am weak and because their fingers were cold and because I had decided—I think around chapter nine of this disaster—that whatever Wren was hiding, they were hiding it *with* me rather than *from* me. Ask me again after tonight. My convictions have been known to weather.

Finally, the central desk—the tallest, oldest clerk, robes gone the color of fog—lifted its head. Its face was smooth except for two shallow impressions where eyes might go, and into those impressions, slowly, like wax accepting a seal, features pressed themselves: a mouth, a nose, eyes the grey of ledger ink. It was making a face *for my benefit*. That was somehow worse than having none.

"Astrid Whitlock," it said. The voice was dry paper turning. "Storm-caller. Account holder."

"Account holder," I repeated. "That's what I am to you."

"You are a line item with excellent posture." The clerk gestured, and a second desk slid aside—not pushed, just *rearranged*, as though the room considered furniture a suggestion—to reveal two chairs of bone facing the great desk. "Be seated. Audits are conducted standing or sitting, never lying down. We find lying down encourages narrative embellishment."

I sat. Wren didn't. Wren remained standing behind my right shoulder like a bodyguard, which was either devotion or positioning, and I genuinely could not tell anymore, and neither, I suspected, could they.

* * *

The clerk opened a ledger the size of a door.

"The account," it began, "was established three years past, on the night designated in surface records as the Night of Broken Thunder. Assets tendered: one soul, entire, undivided, storm-attuned. Payment received in full. Purpose of transaction—" it turned the page with one long finger— "the sparing of the city Greyhollow from punitive weather action following unauthorized atmospheric intervention by the account holder."

I knew this. I'd learned it from Marrow's ledgers, from the scar in my memory, from the vault upstairs where I'd watched myself kneel in the rain and sign myself away so that a city full of people who feared me wouldn't burn for what I'd done. Knowing it again in this room, in this voice, still landed like a fist. Some facts are doors you have to walk through fresh every time.

"The term of the agreement," the clerk continued, "is until the next total eclipse, at which point the asset transfers permanently into Registry custody. Remaining interval: six days."

"Six days," I said. "Generous of you to schedule the apocalypse around everyone's calendars."

"The eclipse is not scheduled by us." Something flickered across the borrowed face—amusement, maybe, if amusement could be embalmed. "The eclipse is scheduled by the sky. We simply keep our paperwork current."

"And if I want to cancel? Close the account?"

The clerk closed the ledger. Gently. Reverently. The way a priest closes a scripture, or a con man closes a trap.

"Then we arrive at the reason for this audience," it said. "You will have noticed, account holder, that your descent has been... facilitated."

"Oh, I noticed."

"You were expected." The clerk folded its hands. "A prophecy circulated through the surface world a century ago. Banned, burned, smuggled—you know how surface institutions treat accurate records. The Registry does not ban books. The Registry *writes* them, occasionally, and then enjoys watching what surfaces do with them." It tilted its head. "You brought a copy. Page forty-one. May I?"

I hadn't brought it. But Wren had, tucked inside their coat since Marrow's shop, and now Wren produced it and set it on the desk, and I felt the floor tilt slightly under my certainty about whose idea that had been.

The clerk turned to the final page without looking at it. It didn't need to. "Two figures enter the mountain," it recited, in a bored monotone that suggested it had recited this many times, to many souls, and found the results uniformly satisfactory. "One leaves alone. The courts below have interpreted this verse in various ways across the centuries. Ours is the simplest reading, and therefore correct: the figure who leaves carries the soul. The figure who remains *is* the payment."

My mouth went dry. "So the soul comes back to me—"

"No." The clerk raised one finger. "Let us be precise, because precision is the only kindness we offer. If the prophecy completes—if two enter and one leaves—the *debt is discharged*. Not paid. Discharged. Annulled. The contract dissolves, the soul returns to its original owner, and the remaining party assumes the obligation in perpetuity." The grey eyes settled on me with terrible gentleness. "Someone must hold the debt, account holder. Debts do not vanish. They only change hands."

"And the other interpretation?" Wren asked. Their voice was very even. Even voices are loud, if you know how to listen.

"The other interpretation," the clerk said, "is that no one leaves, and the Registry collects both accounts at eclipse. Also acceptable. Less elegant. We are fond of elegance."

* * *

I stood up. Sitting felt wrong; my legs wanted options.

"Let me make sure I have this straight," I said. "Three years ago, I sold my soul to save a city. Fine. Terrible bargain, my choice, I own it—literally, apparently, I own all of it except the part of me that makes choices, which is in escrow. Now you're telling me the only way out isn't paying the debt back. There's no paying it back. There's only *transferring* it. To him." I jerked a thumb at Wren without looking. "Or letting the clock run out and losing everything anyway."

"The Registry admires a clear summary."

"And you built the prophecy. A hundred years ago. Before I was born. Before *he* was—" I stopped. Turned. Looked at Wren properly for the first time since we'd entered the hall.

Wren was looking at the lanterns. At one lantern in particular, high near the ceiling, burning steady and gold while others around it guttered. Their face had gone carefully, professionally blank—which, I realized with a chill, was a skill you learn in exactly one place, and I was standing in it.

"Wren," I said. "What did you give them?"

"Later," Wren said.

"There is no later! There's six days and a room full of soul-lanterns and a prophecy with my face on the last page—"

"*Later,*" Wren said again, and this time it cracked, just slightly, down the middle, and the clerk observed both of us with the patient satisfaction of a spider watching two flies agree on a flight path.

* * *

The clerk produced tea.

This is not a joke. From a drawer in a bone desk, in a court beneath a mountain, it produced a porcelain pot and two cups, and poured with the precise ceremony of someone who had done this ten thousand times and enjoyed it perhaps twice. The tea smelled of nothing at all. That should have been my first warning. Nothing down here smells like anything except memory, and this tea was determined to have none.

"Drink," said the clerk. "Audits proceed better hydrated."

I didn't touch it. Wren didn't either. The clerk sipped its own cup with visible contentment, then set it down and laced its long fingers together.

"You are wondering why we tell you any of this. Why the trap is displayed so openly." The grey eyes gleamed. "Because, account holder, a trap only works on the intelligent if they believe they've seen through it. You are already planning. I can hear it—you have a storm in your skull and it is drafting contingencies. Perhaps you refuse the terms and fight. Perhaps you flee and seek some third path. Perhaps you sacrifice yourself nobly and send your companion away alone, thus fulfilling the prophecy *and* preserving the soul, and consider yourself clever."

I blinked. Because—yes. That had been the plan forming. That exact plan, word for word, in the time it took to pour tea.

"We know," the clerk said softly, "because we wrote the kind of person who tries it. We have been collecting souls for longer than your language has had a future tense. Every hero believes they are the exception to the ledger. The ledger is patient. The ledger has *seen*."

"Then why bother offering terms at all?"

"Because sometimes," the clerk said, and here the borrowed face did something almost human—it smiled, thinly, like frost forming—"the exception arrives. And we would very much like to be holding the pen when she does."

It turned the great ledger around and pushed it across the desk. On the open page, in ink that shimmered faintly with trapped light, was a line item I recognized from Marrow's description, from nightmares, from the vault upstairs where my own missing year had played out in miniature:

*Asset: A. WHITLOCK. Status: HELD. Transfer conditions per Prophecy 7-C.*

Below it, a signature line. Blank.

"One signature," the clerk said, "and the terms are formalized. Refuse, and the default terms apply at eclipse: full collection, no discharge, no exceptions. Sign, and you buy six days of certainty and a fighting chance at the elegant outcome." It slid a bone pen across the desk after the ledger. "Take your time. Though I note the eclipse does not."

* * *

I looked at the line. I looked at Wren, still guarding my shoulder, still watching that one steady lantern near the ceiling like it owed them money—or like they owed it something worse.

Here is what nobody tells you about traps: the elegant ones aren't built to catch you. They're built so that every way out looks like a door, and every door opens onto the same room. Fight, and the prophecy wins. Run, and the eclipse wins. Sacrifice yourself nobly, and the ledger wins with a smile, because the ledger has never once lost a hero—it has only ever *acquired* one ahead of schedule.

But the clerk had said something, in all that velvet cruelty, that snagged in me like a hook.

*We would very much like to be holding the pen when she does.*

When the exception arrives. As if it hadn't yet. As if, in all the centuries of collected souls and fulfilled prophecies and heroes marched politely into ledgers, no one had ever actually torn the page.

They were afraid. Underneath the tea and the bone and the borrowed face, under all that patience—the Registry was afraid of something. Maybe of me. Maybe of the storm I'd spent three years folding smaller and smaller into the space behind my ribs. Maybe of what happens when a soul refuses to be a line item.

Or maybe—my eyes drifted up to that single steady lantern, the one Wren couldn't stop watching—maybe of whoever was burning up there, quiet and gold, in a hall full of flicker.

I reached for the pen.

Wren's hand closed over mine. Cold fingers. Steady. And when I looked up, Wren shook their head once—slowly—and said, in a voice pitched only for me, low enough that the clerks' smooth faces didn't so much as twitch:

"Not yet. First, I need to tell you what I gave them. And Astrid—" their throat worked, "—you need to hear it from me before you hear it from *them*, because the Registry only tells the truth in ways that hurt."

Behind the desk, the clerk refilled its untouched teacup and waited, with the serenity of an institution that had already read this scene, filed it, cross-referenced it, and shelved it under a name I hadn't earned the right to see.

The lanterns swayed overhead, hundreds of small held breaths, and somewhere above us the sky was counting down to an eclipse.

Six days.

Wren took a breath that sounded like the beginning of a confession, or an ending, or both.
