## 12. Heir of the Deep

I was six years old when a clerk with a face like folded paper told me I had been born twice.

The first time, in the dark beneath the mountain, to a mother whose name was struck from every ledger the moment my lungs filled. The second time—more important, more *useful*—on a hillside above Greyhollow, in the arms of a childless goatherd who would be paid in years he didn't know he'd sold.

"You are our heir," the clerk said, "and heirs have duties."

I thought duty meant inheriting something. A throne of bone. A hall of lanterns that were people. Instead I inherited an errand: grow up in the sun, learn how the surface folk love, and one day bring home the soul they'd bought.

That's what I was doing when I fell for Astrid Whitlock. The errand, I mean. Falling was not on the assignment sheet.

* * *

The Registry kept me waiting three days after we arrived at the gate, which I recognized as a tactic even before I understood its purpose. Let the soul-bearer sit in the antechambers, watching sold memories flicker past like moths. Let them grow hungry, tired, pliable. Meanwhile, summon the instrument separately. Soften it. Remind it what it's for.

"Instrument," by the way, is the word they used for me. In writing. I've seen the ledgers. *The heir-instrument Wren Vale has performed adequately.* Adequately. Three decades of being charming on command, and the best grade I ever got was *adequate*.

They took Astrid down the eastern gallery to the vaults—"to see what is owed," a clerk said, in the tone of someone offering a tour—and took me west, to the Registry proper, where the clerks of souls keep their cold little offices and their colder little ledgers.

I knew the way. That's the joke nobody laughs at. I knew the way home better than I'd ever known the goat track above Greyhollow.

A junior clerk named Vessel—clerks take names like that, nouns with the warmth boiled out of them—walked me through corridors lit by bone-lamps, each lamp humming faintly with somebody's stolen year. I passed one that flickered amber and smelled, absurdly, of rain on hot stone. I thought of Astrid's palms. I kept walking.

"Your collection went smoothly," Vessel said.

"It went," I said.

"The soul-bearer trusts you?"

"Astrid." I said it deliberately, the way you'd set a knife on a table between you. "And yes. Enough to walk into a mountain on my word alone."

"Good." Vessel made a note. Clerks make notes the way other creatures breathe; I'm fairly sure some of them would keep writing while drowning. "The Registry is pleased. After the eclipse, your service will conclude, and your standing will be restored. Full inheritance. The Deep Hall. Your mother's name unstruck from the record."

There it was. Thirty years dangled on a hook, and they'd finally told me what the bait cost.

"And Astrid?" I asked. "After the eclipse?"

Vessel looked at me with those paper-fold eyes. "Terms were offered. The soul-bearer will choose."

"They always choose?" I said. "In your experience? Given a choice between their own soul and a stranger they've known two months?"

"The terms are elegant," Vessel said, with something that in a human might have been pride. "Elegant terms resolve themselves."

I should tell you what the terms were, because I only learned them later, and because you need to understand that when I say the trap was beautiful, I mean it the way you'd mean it about a spiderweb in morning light—gorgeous, and built entirely out of someone else's ruin.

Take back the soul, forget the thief forever. Or keep the memories, and surrender everything at eclipse.

Either way, the prophecy completes. One enters the mountain. One leaves alone. Either way, I win my inheritance, and Astrid loses.

Unless, of course, the terms broke. Unless the soul-bearer refused both doors. The clerks had considered this, naturally. The Registry considers everything. Which is why, walking behind Vessel through the bone-lit halls, I heard the thing I wasn't meant to hear.

* * *

It happened because Vessel stopped to gossip.

Clerks don't gossip—they *reconcile discrepancies*—but the effect is identical. Two of them met at a junction ahead of us, and Vessel paused, and I lingered half a step back in the amber lamplight, and their voices carried the way voices do in halls built to hold echoes of souls.

"The eastern vault is prepared," said the first clerk, whose name was Quill or Spine or something equally affectionate. "The soul-bearer views the memory tonight."

"And then?"

"Then the choice is presented. If they take the soul and forget, clean resolution. If they refuse—"

"If they refuse," Vessel said, "the heir delivers them at eclipse regardless. As written."

"As written," the first clerk agreed. "Though there remains the matter of the heir's attachment. It has been noted."

My chest did something complicated. I stood very still among the lamps.

"Noted," Vessel said carefully. "And addressed. The attachment is useful. Attachment makes the delivery gentle. A struggling soul frays. A willing one comes whole."

"And if the heir objects to the terms? If the heir develops... preferences?"

A pause. In that pause I felt thirty years of my life rearrange themselves, the way furniture shifts in a house you suddenly realize isn't yours.

"The heir's preferences are irrelevant," the first clerk said. "If the soul-bearer refuses both terms, the sigil holds regardless. The debt does not require consent. It requires only the eclipse." A dry sound, the clerkly equivalent of a chuckle. "We let them believe there is a choice. Belief keeps them docile. And if the heir becomes a complication—the heir can be unmade as easily as made. We have done it before. Ask the heir about their childhood sometime. Ask what they remember of before the hillside."

I hadn't moved. I want credit for that. Every nerve in me wanted to step around that corner and put my hands on paper folds and squeeze, but I had spent my whole life learning to perform calm, and for once the performance and the truth agreed.

Because here is what I understood, standing in the lamplight that smelled of someone else's rain: the terms were never terms. There was no door where Astrid walked free. There was only a longer corridor and a shorter one, and at the end of both, the Registry holding a piece of Astrid Whitlock forever, and me holding a title I'd sell in a heartbeat if anyone were fool enough to buy.

And underneath that—the thing that cracked me open like an egg—

*I remembered nothing of before the hillside.*

Thirty years I'd assumed the fog around my earliest years was ordinary. Childhoods are foggy. Everyone says so. But clerks don't leave things to chance, and I had been *made*, twice, and the second making had required erasing the first. Whatever I was beneath the mountain, whoever my mother was, whatever name I'd worn before Vale—it hadn't been lost. It had been *filed*.

Somewhere in this building there was a shelf with my beginning on it.

"Vessel," said the first clerk. "You're needed in the tally room."

Footsteps receded. Vessel touched my elbow. "This way, heir. The Registry wishes to review your final instructions."

I followed. I smiled. I have never hated a smile so much as I hated mine in that corridor, bone-light flickering over it like weather.

* * *

The final instructions took an hour and contained perhaps four sentences of actual information: present the soul-bearer at the eclipse altar; stand in the marked circle; speak the closing words of the old prophecy when prompted. The rest was ceremony—clerks explaining clauses to me as if clause-explanation were a gift, as if elegance were something you could hand a person instead of a cage built out of grammar.

I nodded in the right places. I asked one question, casual as a card trick:

"When my service concludes—you said my mother's name gets unstruck. Which name would that be? I ask because I'd like to know what I'm inheriting."

Vessel consulted a ledger. Of course Vessel consulted a ledger. "The record shows the heir's lineal designation was sealed at the second birth. Unsealing occurs upon completion."

"So I won't know until it's done."

"The Registry rewards finished work, not curiosity."

There it was again. Finished work. Deliver Astrid, get a name. Deliver Astrid, get a mother. My entire selfhood held hostage behind a deadline measured in moonlight.

I thanked Vessel. I bowed, actually—I bowed, low and graceful, thirty years of training folding me like laundry—and walked out of the Registry proper with my heart going like a hammer in a bell tower.

I had until the eclipse. Hours, maybe less. And in those hours I had exactly one decision to make, except it wasn't one decision, it was all of them at once, stacked like plates:

Duty or love. Name or no-name. The self they built or the self I might, against all odds, still get to choose.

* * *

I found the eastern gallery by memory I didn't know I had. That's a strange sentence to write, but it's true—I'd never been shown the vaults, and my feet knew the turns anyway, left at the lamp that hummed in B-flat, down the stair worn concave by centuries of clerical shoes. Some part of me had been carved in these halls before it was carved anywhere else. Walking them felt less like trespass than like returning to a house I'd burned down on purpose.

Astrid was sitting on the floor.

Not standing reverently before the viewing glass as the clerks surely intended. Sitting cross-legged on cold stone, sleeves pushed up, staring at the vault door with the expression I'd first seen across a night-market stall—that look of someone measuring exactly how much trouble they're willing to cause.

"They left me alone with my own soul," Astrid said, without turning around. "Behind that door. You'd think they'd have better security."

"You could break in."

"I could break in," Astrid agreed. "But I haven't decided if I want to know yet. There's a version of me that walks out of here having never opened that door, and honestly, Wren, some nights that version sounds well-rested."

I sat down beside them. The stone was cold through my clothes. Somewhere below us, the eclipse was winding itself toward us like a fuse, and I had perhaps hours, and every word I chose now was load-bearing.

"There's something I have to tell you," I said. "And I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything. Not because I deserve the hearing. Because you deserve the whole shape of it."

Astrid turned. Storm-grey eyes, the color of the sky right before it decides. Three years of exile had taught those eyes to read weather in faces, and I felt myself being read, front to back, ledger to ledger.

"You're scaring me," Astrid said quietly. "Which takes effort. I'm already in a mountain full of lantern-people."

"The night we met," I said, "wasn't an accident. The charm you sold me—I was sent to buy it. The dinner. The second dinner. The walk along the wall where you almost told me about the thunder. All of it was assigned. I was raised above ground for one purpose: to find you, to be someone you'd trust, and to walk you down here before the eclipse. I'm not a surface-folk who stumbled into a prophecy, Astrid. I'm the prophecy's delivery boy. I'm—" and here my voice did something I hated, something small, "—I'm theirs. I was born theirs. Twice."

Silence. The lamps hummed. Somewhere far off, a clerk's pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

Astrid didn't move away. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger governs the things that saved me: Astrid Whitlock, offered the sight of what I truly was, did not move away.

"Keep going," they said.

"I overheard the clerks. The terms they're offering you—the soul-and-forgetting, or memories-and-surrender—they're not real choices. If you refuse both, they take the sigil anyway at eclipse. Consent was never part of the design. They just need you docile until the moon goes dark." I made myself say the last part, made myself carry it the rest of the way. "And if I become a problem, they'll erase me the way they erased my own childhood. That's the inheritance, Astrid. That's the throne. I get a name I've never heard in exchange for handing them the person I—"

I stopped. The word was right there. It had been right there for weeks, sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"The person you what?" Astrid said. Not cruel. Just precise. They'd earned precision.

"The person I love," I said. "Who I was sent to harvest. Who I'd burn this whole court down to keep, if burning were enough. And I don't know how to be both things anymore, the heir and the—the man who sat across from you eating terrible stew and wishing the evening wouldn't end. I don't know if both of us can fit in one body. But I know which one I'd rather die as. And I know they're planning to keep you regardless of what you choose, and I know the eclipse is coming, and I am asking you—not as the heir, not as the instrument—as the man from the night market: help me break this."

Astrid looked at me for a long time. Down in the vault behind them, something glowed softly, patient as a banked fire: three years of a life, waiting to be remembered.

"You lied to me from the first minute," Astrid said at last.

"Yes."

"Everything—the charm, the dinners, the run from Sela's wardens—all of it scripted."

"All of it."

"And the wardens cornered us and you threw lightning off your hands like you'd been born to it, and you told me the court held something of mine, and you gave me just enough truth to keep me walking downward."

"Yes."

Astrid exhaled slowly. "Okay," they said. "Then here's what I know that you apparently don't, Wren Vale, heir of whatever-the-deep-is. Prophecies are contracts, and I've read enough bad contracts to know they only bind the people who sign them in good faith. So we're going to do something no one down here has planned for. We're going to fulfill every letter of their prophecy and none of its spirit. One enters the mountain." Their mouth curved, sharp and bright, the exact expression I'd fallen for across a market stall, back when I believed falling was part of the assignment. "Fine. Both of us entered. Let them explain that to their ledgers."

"And the leaving alone?" I said.

Astrid stood, and pulled me up after them, and turned to face the vault door where their missing years glowed like a held breath.

"We'll deal with that page when we get to it," they said. "First—open my door. I want my three years back. And then I want a word with the clerks about the meaning of *choice*."

Beyond the walls, deep in the stone, I felt the mountain begin to count down toward the eclipse. And for the first time since I was six years old, standing in the dark listening to a paper-faced clerk tell me what I was for—

I had no idea what I was for.

It felt, I realized, like freedom.
