## 13. The Missing Year

The clerks of the Pale Registry did not walk so much as *proceed*, the way a verdict proceeds. They glided down the bone-lit corridor ahead of us with ledgers floating at their elbows like well-trained gulls, and I followed because following was the only thing left to do that didn't involve screaming.

Wren walked beside me. Close enough that our sleeves touched. Not speaking.

That was the part I kept noticing—the not-speaking. In Greyhollow, Wren had talked the way rain talks, constantly and about everything: the salt on the bread, the crooked shutters on the tanner's row, whether pigeons held grudges. Now they walked in silence like someone carrying something breakable up a long staircase, and every step of it was mine.

"You don't have to come," I said, for the fourth time since we'd left the audience chamber.

"I do," Wren said, for the fourth time. Their voice did something complicated on the word *do*. "You don't have to look at it alone."

"Look at what? You won't tell me."

"Because it's yours." They finally turned their head. The bone-light of the halls did unkind things to faces—made everyone look like an apology—but on Wren it just made them look young. Younger than me, somehow, though I'd never asked and they'd never said. "Whatever's behind that door belongs to you. Nobody else gets to describe it first. That's not mercy, Astrid. That's just... ownership."

"Charming philosophy from a court that owns souls by contract."

"Yes," Wren said quietly. "Well. I'm working on that."

The clerk leading us—Registry-clerk number whatever, its face smooth as a ledger page—paused before a door of black wood set into bone. It carried no handle. Instead there was a sigil burned into the grain, and the sigil was *familiar* in the way a scar is familiar: you forget it's there until you catch it in a mirror, and then you remember it has been on your body all along.

I knew that mark. I'd been drawing it absently in the margins of my ledger book for three years without knowing why. Little spirals and a broken line through the middle, over and over, until Marrow the ledger-keeper once joked that I was either cursed or bored.

Turns out the answer was *both*, and also *neither*.

"This is the vault of the asset designated Whitlock," the clerk said. Its voice had no more warmth than arithmetic. "Contained herein: one (1) continuous memory, approximately eleven months in duration, excised at owner request. One (1) soul, bound under sigil, pending collection at eclipse. Viewing of the memory is permitted per clause nine. Reclamation is not." A pause, as if consulting something invisible. "The Registry reminds the asset that what is viewed cannot be unviewed."

"The Registry," I said, "talks about me like I'm a shipment of wool."

"The Registry," said the clerk, "has shipped wool with more sentiment attached."

I almost liked it. That's the horror of the Undercourt—it keeps being almost funny right up until it isn't.

"Astrid." Wren's hand found mine. Their palm was warm, which felt obscene down here, where everything glowed cold as moonlight on snow. "Clause nine or no clause nine—you can walk away. We can go back up. Find another way. There are always other ways; it just takes longer and hurts differently."

"There isn't another way," I said. "There's an eclipse in four days and a sigil on my soul and a prophecy with my face in it. There's this door."

I pressed my palm to the sigil.

It opened the way memory opens—not all at once, but in a rush, like water finding the crack it always meant to find.

---

Inside, there was no room. Or rather, the room was irrelevant—a bare sphere of bone with nothing in it but a single lantern of pale flame, and beneath it, hanging in the air like laundry on an unseen line, a ribbon of light.

Eleven months long. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

"That's it?" My voice came out small. "That's my missing year?"

"It is the excision," the clerk said. It had followed us in, which I hadn't authorized, but apparently souls' vaults don't do authorization. "To view: touch the thread. To withdraw: touch the thread and let go. The Registry recommends a chair."

"The Registry recommends—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Three years ago somebody cut a year out of my head, and your recommendation is *furniture*?"

"We also recommend against viewing immediately after meals."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Then they took both my hands, and the humor went out of the air entirely.

"When you see it," they said, "remember that whoever you were then—whatever they did, whatever they chose—they were doing it with the whole sky falling on them. Judge them gently. Judge yourself gently. That's all I'll say."

"You know what's in here."

"I know some of it." Their thumbs moved over my knuckles, back and forth, a nervous metronome. "Not all. Please believe that if I knew all of it, I would have told you everything weeks ago, consequences be damned."

"And I'd have run," I said. "And we both know it."

They didn't deny it. That was its own kind of honesty.

I touched the thread.

---

Thunder.

Not remembered thunder—*actual* thunder, arriving in my chest like a guest who doesn't knock. Rain sideways, the taste of ozone and copper, and I am standing on the wall above Greyhollow's east gate with my hands full of sky, and I am *screaming*—

No. Wait. Earlier. The thread unspooled backward, obedient to some instinct I didn't know I had, and gave me the beginning.

The beginning was a drought.

Greyhollow, three years ago, and I was already in exile already hiding—I just hadn't used those words yet. I sold charms at the night market even then, luck-knots and weather-salves, and I was careful the way only a person with something to lose is careful. Magic-free city. Wardens on every corner with their brass instruments and their righteous certainty. Sela—gods, that was *Sela* walking the beat below my stall, younger then, harder now—Sela checking my salves twice and finding nothing but beeswax and honest craft.

The drought had gone eight months. Wells dropping. The river a rumor. And then the fires started—upcountry first, then closer, until one night the whole eastern horizon was a red seam and the wind changed and brought the smell of burning barley into the market, and everyone stood very still in the smoke-haze, smelling their own future.

I remember—I *remember*, it was mine again, all of it mine—that night I climbed the wall with a stolen storm in my chest.

Where had I gotten it? The thread showed me: months of work. Catching weather the way other people caught fish, patiently, a little at a time. A thunderhead hooked off a mountain range two hundred miles away and reeled in slow across the sky, disguised, folded small, hidden inside ordinary cloud cover so no warden's instrument would sing. Eight months of smuggling a storm toward a city that had outlawed exactly what I was.

Because the fires were coming, and the city would burn, and the wardens' anti-magic wards—great gray domes of suppression over every district—would do nothing against fire except keep the citizens from dousing it with anything better than buckets.

So I broke the wards. Mine to break—I knew their weave, I'd spent three years mapping every blind spot in Greyhollow's armor, the same knowledge Wren navigated so easily, the same knowledge I'd assumed was just exile-craft. I unpicked the suppression dome over the east districts like threading a needle, and I stood on the wall, and I opened my hands, and I let eight months of smuggled sky out of its cage.

The storm hit the fires like judgment. Rain in sheets, in walls, in oceans. Lightning splitting the burning ridge so the flames had nowhere left to climb. I called it and called it and called it until my voice was gone and my nose bled and my hands shook so badly I couldn't have charmed a kitten, and the fires died, and Greyhollow slept through its own salvation.

And then morning came, and the wardens found a broken dome, and a city that had outlawed magic woke up soaked and saved by it, and the question wasn't *who saved us*—the question was *what heretic broke the law that saved us.*

I could have run. The thread showed me that too, showed me standing on the wall at dawn with escape routes blooming in my mind like weeds—north road, coast road, Marrow's smugglers' tunnels, any of them. I was fast. I was trained. I'd have made it.

But the wardens were already dragging people out of the east district. Families from the streets nearest the wall. Because when the Registry's instruments traced the broken dome—and oh, they traced it *fast*, faster than I'd imagined possible, because the Registry had been listening to Greyhollow all along, feeding the wardens their certainty, using the city as an instrument panel for hunting people like me—when the trace came back, it came back pointing at the east district generally, at *my neighbors* specifically. Everyone who'd ever bought a charm from the quiet woman with the weather-salves. Everyone whose door I'd knocked on. Sela had a list of forty names and warrants for six, and she was reading them aloud in the square with the dawn behind her.

Forty names. Six arrests. And a court beneath the mountain that had noticed the whole performance and found it *interesting*.

That was the part that made my knees go—the thread showed me the moment I understood I wasn't the only one watching that night. Down at the base of the wall, in the shadow where the bone-gate yawned, something pale and patient had been observing my storm the way a merchant observes a harvest. And it had sent up an offer, written in frost on the stone beside my hand:

*One soul, freely given, stays the Registry's hand. Forty names, withdrawn. The city burns for nothing—or burns for no one. Choose.*

And I—past-me, whole-me, the me with the missing year—stood there in the ruin of the storm with rain still dripping off my chin, and I read it twice, and I thought about forty doors being kicked in, and I thought about Sela reading names in the dawn light, and I did the arithmetic that nobody should ever have to do.

One soul against forty.

Except I made it worse than that. Better. I don't know. The thread showed me negotiating—*negotiating*, past-me had gall—standing at the frost-written gate while the Pale Registry's clerks unfolded around me like paper, and I said: fine. Take the soul. But I want terms.

*Name them,* said the clerks, who clearly enjoyed this part.

"One," I said. "The forty names stay dead paper. Forever. No future tracing, no future collection, no *we've reconsidered.*"

*Granted.*

"Two. The city keeps its rain. What I called, it keeps—the wells fill, the river runs, and no warden ever learns it wasn't natural."

*Granted. Though the chief investigator will wonder,* said a clerk, with something like appreciation. *She will wonder for years. It will cost her sleep. We find this efficient.*

"Three." And here my voice shook, and the thread preserved the shaking, preserved it perfectly, cruelly. "Take the year too. All of it. The storm, the choice, the deal—all of it. Cut it out. Because I know myself, and if I carry this—if I wake tomorrow knowing what I traded and who I traded it to—I'll spend every day trying to buy it back, and I'll fail, and I'll regret, and regret is just the debt collecting interest. I want to live the life I'm purchasing without having to watch myself purchase it. Cut it clean. Let her"—and past-me gestured at herself, at me, at the woman who would wake up tomorrow with a hole in her head shaped like heroism—"let her be innocent. Let her think exile was punishment instead of price. She'll be happier."

*You understand,* said the eldest clerk, and its voice was almost gentle, which was the worst thing yet, *that this makes you the author of your own forgetting. We will merely hold the pen.*

"I understand," said past-me. "Do it."

And the last thing the missing year contained was the sensation of the cut itself—which was not pain, exactly. It was *relief.* That's what I'd asked for and that's what I got: eleven months of terror and courage lifting out of me like a weight I'd forgotten I carried, and the world going soft and blank and kind, and my last coherent thought before the hole closed over was:

*I hope she sells good charms. I hope she finds someone who makes her laugh.*

---

I came out of the thread on my knees on the bone floor, and Wren was holding me, had been holding me for gods knew how long, and I was crying in a way that had no dignity in it whatsoever.

"She's happy," I gasped. "Past-me. The version of me that made the deal—her last thought was hoping I'd be *happy*. She paid a soul and a year and her last wish was that I wouldn't have to know."

"Astrid—"

"I spent three years being *grateful*, Wren. Grateful for the exile, grateful for the little life, the charms, the market, the—grateful for *you*. I thought I was a criminal serving out a sentence I couldn't remember committing. I built a whole personality on top of it. And the truth is—" I laughed, wet and horrible. "The truth is I bought every second of it. I bought the rain. I bought forty strangers' safety. I bought my own ignorance at retail price. Exile wasn't my punishment. It was my *purchase*, and I chose it, and then I chose to forget choosing it, which means the person who's been living my life for three years is a fiction I wrote on purpose—"

"You're not a fiction."

"I'm the *receipt*," I said. "I'm what the purchase left behind."

Wren took my face in both hands and made me look at them, and down here in the bone-light their eyes were the color of deep water, and steady, steadier than anything else in this mountain.

"Listen to me," they said. "You called a storm to save a city that had outlawed you. You traded your soul so that no one else would pay for your courage. And then—*then*—you asked to forget, not because you were ashamed, but because you wanted the life you'd bought to actually be lived. Do you hear what that is? Every clerk in this mountain would call that weakness. Every warden above ground would call it crime. I call it the most expensive act of love I've ever seen, and I grew up *here*."

"You grew up—" But that thread could wait. Everything could wait. I put my forehead against theirs. "The eclipse is in four days. They're going to collect. And now I know what I'm losing, and it's worse, because before tonight I was only losing a soul. Now I'm losing the proof that I was ever worth one."

"No," Wren said, fierce and quiet. "You're not losing anything. Not if I have anything to say about it. And it turns out—" a breath, a crack in the fierceness, "—it turns out I have considerably more to say about it than either of us knew."

Behind us, the clerk cleared its nonexistent throat.

"The viewing period has concluded," it said. "The Registry notes the asset's distress and reminds all parties that clause nine does not extend to grief counseling. Additionally—" and here the clerk's flat voice did something almost apologetic, which frightened me more than anything else in the vault had, "—the Registry has become aware that the heir is present in the vault during a restricted viewing. This deviation from protocol has been logged. The Registry thanks you both for your cooperation and advises you that the audience chamber wishes to see the heir. Alone. Immediately."

Wren went very still against me.

"Heir," I repeated.

"Walk with me to the door," Wren said, and their voice was the voice of a person stepping off a cliff and trying to make it sound like a stroll, "and I'll explain on the way. Some of it. As much as—Astrid, whatever I say in the next hundred steps, please remember the vault. Remember what you just learned about yourself. People can be assigned terrible jobs and still choose, at the end, to do the right thing. You proved it. Three years ago, on a wall, in the rain."

"What," I said, as the clerk swept the door open and the bone corridors waited beyond, cold and patient and lit like the inside of a skull, "did they assign *you*?"
